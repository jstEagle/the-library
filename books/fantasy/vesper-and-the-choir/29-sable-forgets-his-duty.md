## 29. Sable Forgets His Duty

The song found him at the tree line.

Sable had been running for two days—through the whiteout pass, down the frozen riverbed, across the high moor where the wind stripped the breath from his lungs and gave nothing back—and the whole time, the Cantor's hymn had been following him like a hound that never tired. He could feel it in his teeth. A low, patient thrumming, the sound of a great machine turning over somewhere beneath the mountains, and every verse of it was a knife with his name on the handle.

*Your name,* the song said, in the Cantor's voice, in the voice of every master he had ever served. *You have no name. You are a note in the Choir. Come home. Come home and be emptied.*

He had been a note in the Choir for thirty years. It should have been easy to obey.

Instead he thought about Emrys Thackeray's face when she'd told him she was going back for him—the sheer, infuriating stubbornness of it, the way she'd said *you're not a note, you're a nuisance* and then kissed him like an argument she intended to win.

"Keep talking," he wheezed to the empty air, because talking helped, talking kept the shape of himself intact. "I'm listening."

The dead courier, who had been pacing him overhead in a manner best described as spectral smugness, drifted down through the branches. "You're talking to me now? Wonderful. Last week it was 'stop following me, ghost.' People grow."

"You're the only company I have."

"That," said the courier, "is the saddest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once attended my own funeral."

Sable laughed—a broken, gasping thing—and kept running.

* * *

The Vesper Gate did not look like a gate.

That had been his first thought, weeks ago, when he'd stood on the far side of it with orders in his pocket and frost in his boots: *this is what we've been guarding? This is what the Grand Cantor sings himself hoarse about?*

It looked like a wound in the mountain. Two doorways stood side by side in a cliff face that had no business containing them—one of pale horn, one of ivory, both taller than any cathedral door Sable had ever seen, both humming faintly, like struck bells that refused to finish ringing. The stone around them was scarred and melted, as if the mountain itself had once tried to close its eyes and could not.

And above them, written in letters of frost that never melted and never quite resolved into anything readable, was the riddle.

He hadn't read it then. Reading it would have meant thinking about it, and thinking about it would have meant doubting, and doubting was the first sin the Choir taught you to cut out of yourself, along with your mother's voice and the smell of bread and whatever your name had been before.

Now he crested the ridge above the gate with the hymn chewing at his heels, and he saw Maren standing between the doors with her father's compass held out before her like a lantern, and he saw Emrys—

Emrys, alive, whole, her hands still blackened with the soot of the sanctum, turning toward him with an expression that started as relief and curdled instantly into alarm.

"You're being followed," she called up.

"I'm aware!"

"There's a lot of them!"

"I'm *aware*, Emrys!"

Behind him, pouring out of the pass like grey water finding its level, came the hunters. Six of them. Then nine. Their robes made no sound. Their feet made no prints in the snow. They did not run—they simply advanced, unhurried, the way winter advances, and their mouths were open and singing, and the song they sang was the Cantor's counter-hymn, the one built from the archive key's stolen harmonics, the one designed to do exactly one thing:

Unwrite Emrys Thackeray.

He could hear it working. That was the horror of it—it wasn't loud, it wasn't violent, it was *persuasive*. Each verse reached into the world and gently suggested that there had never been anyone named Emrys at all. He watched it take the snow around her, which stopped holding her footprints. He watched it take the light, which began to slide past her shoulders as though she were made of smoke. He watched Maren frown, glance sideways, and say, in a small confused voice, "Wait. Who am I waiting for?"

"No," Sable breathed. "No, no—"

The binding rose in him to meet the hymn, old and deep, laid down when he was seven years old and weeping in a Choir dormitory while they ate his childhood out of him verse by verse. *You belong to the song. You go where the song sends you. You forget what the song tells you to forget.* Thirty years of obedience, worn smooth as river stone.

The nearest hunter raised one grey hand—not toward Emrys, but toward *him*. An invitation. A summons. *Come home, little note.*

And Sable stood on the ridge with the wind screaming and the hymn climbing and the woman he loved dissolving fifty yards below him, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man falling, that there was only one weapon left in the world that could cut through the Cantor's song.

It wasn't a sword. It wasn't the archive key. It wasn't any of the clever things they'd bled for across half a country.

It was her name.

* * *

Here is what the Choir teaches its children about names, on the night they take yours: a name is a hook, and hooks can be used against you. Better to be nameless. Better to be a note, a robe, a function. The Grand Cantor had built his entire order on that principle, and the Cantor's counter-song had swept the name *Emrys Thackeray* out of every living mind from Thornfell to the gate—out of innkeepers, out of mothers, out of Maren, who had walked beside her for a hundred miles and now stood squinting at empty air.

But a name is also a thread. And Sable had spent thirty years pretending he had no threads, and he was done pretending.

The hunters were twenty paces away. Fifteen. Their song swelled, and Emrys flickered like a candle in a draft, and Maren said, louder now, frightened, "There's someone here, isn't there? There's someone here and I can't—why can't I—"

Sable filled his ruined lungs.

He did not sing. Singing was their language; singing was how they'd bound him, and anything sung could be un-sung, folded back into their harmony. Instead he shouted—raw, ugly, human, the way a man shouts across a storm, the way a man shouts when he has nothing left but his voice.

"*EMRYS!*"

The word went out across the snow like a thrown stone.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The hymn faltered, puzzled, a machine encountering a part it didn't recognize. The hunters tilted their heads in eerie unison.

Then the name landed.

It landed in Maren first—he saw it happen, saw her eyes snap wide and fill, saw her hand fly to her mouth as a hundred miles of shared road came flooding back through the hole the hymn had torn. "*Emrys!*" she cried, whirling, grabbing the smoke-shaped woman by the arm, and where Maren's fingers closed, Emrys solidified, remembered, *held*.

It landed in the snow, which remembered her footprints all the way back down the pass.

It landed in the gate itself. Both doors rang—a deep, astonished chord, horn and ivory sounding together for perhaps the first time in forty years—and the frost-written riddle above them shivered, rearranged, and for one impossible moment Sable could almost read it.

And it landed in the hunters.

That was the part he hadn't planned. That was the part no one could have planned. Because the binding in each of those grey robes was the same binding in him—laid down in childhood, maintained by song, absolute—and a name spoken aloud, freely, in defiance, was a crack in it. Not a big crack. A hairline. But hairlines travel.

The lead hunter stumbled. Just one step, just one stutter in that gliding advance, and beneath the hood Sable heard something he had never heard in thirty years of service: a singer, hesitating.

"Say it again!" Maren screamed at him, laughing and sobbing at once, holding Emrys's arm like a woman hauling a drowning swimmer ashore. "Whatever you just did—do it again!"

So he did. He came down off the ridge at a dead run, shouting her name with every stride—"Emrys! Emrys! EMRYS!"—each repetition another hammer-blow against thirty years of silence, and it hurt, it *hurt*, the binding fighting back, driving needles of forgetting through his skull, whispering *you don't know that name, you've never known that name, sit down, little note, be quiet, be empty*—

"You know what?" he snarled at his own skull, at the Cantor, at the whole grey architecture of his life. "No."

He reached them. He reached *her*. And Emrys Thackeray—half-unwritten, soot-blackened, furious with fear—grabbed the front of his coat and said, "You absolute idiot, I told you to stay hidden until dark."

"You went back for me first," he said. "We're both idiots. It's very romantic."

"It is *not*—"

The hunters were coming again. But slower now. And one of them—just one, at the rear—had stopped entirely, hood tipped back, and Sable caught a glimpse of a young face underneath, wet-eyed, bewildered, mouthing something silently to herself over and over. Testing the shape of a word she wasn't supposed to know.

A name. Her own, maybe. Or someone else's.

"The binding's cracking," Sable said. "One name won't finish it. But—"

"But if everyone says hers," Emrys finished, and he watched the idea catch fire in her the way it always did, fast and slightly terrifying, "and then their own, and then each other's—"

"—then the Cantor's song has nowhere left to stand."

Maren was already moving, cupping her hands, bellowing toward the gate, toward the crowd of dead that had gathered beyond it in their hundreds—lucid, patient, waiting dead—"HEY! GHOSTS! THE WOMAN'S NAME IS EMRYS! SPREAD IT AROUND!"

The dead, who had spent forty years being forgotten by everyone, took to the task with what could only be described as relish.

* * *

Afterward, Sable would remember the next hour as the strangest battle he had ever witnessed, mostly because no one fought.

The hunters advanced, and the names met them. Emrys's name, passed mouth to mouth among the dead like a cup of water. Then, as the hairline cracks spread, other names—singers breaking surface inside their own robes, gasping out syllables they hadn't spoken since childhood. One hunter tore back his hood and said, "My name is Aldous," in the wondering tone of a man reading his own tombstone, and sat down in the snow and did not get up. Another simply turned and walked away into the pass, singing under her breath—not the Cantor's hymn but something small and tuneless and hers.

Three held. Three kept singing, grey and relentless, backing away step by step as the names pressed in, until they reached the lip of the pass and the lead hunter looked directly at Sable across the snow.

And spoke—not in song, but in the Cantor's own borrowed voice, carried through the hunter's mouth like a hand through a glove.

"Clever, little note." The voice was old and smooth and terribly tired. "But you've only proven my point. Look what one name cost you. Look what it will cost them all. Come to the gate tomorrow. Come and hear my offer. Bring the bone-carver." A pause, and something almost like gentleness entered the voice, which was somehow worse than menace. "Bring her. She will want to hear it too."

The hunter collapsed. The song stopped. The snow held very still.

"Well," said the courier's ghost, materializing beside Sable with his arms crossed. "That was ominous. I give it four out of ten. Villains should really learn to monologue less and gloat more."

"He knows we're here," Emrys said quietly. She was solid again—fully solid, her hand still gripping Sable's coat, though neither of them had let go. "He knew we'd come. He's been waiting for us this whole time."

"Yes," Sable said. His heart was still hammering, but underneath the fear something else had settled into place, calm and certain, like a bone set properly at last. "And tomorrow we find out why."

Emrys looked up at him. Snow was catching in her hair, and the horn door behind her hummed its unfinished chord, and she was the most real thing in a world that had spent a week trying to erase her.

"Sable," she said. "You said my name and it brought me back. Out of the song itself." Her grip tightened. "What did it cost you?"

He considered lying. He was, after all, trained in it.

"Nothing yet," he said honestly, which was worse. "Ask me tomorrow."

Above the gates, the frost-written riddle shifted once more—and this time, for three full seconds, every letter stood clear and legible in the dying light, addressed not to the dead, not to the living, but to whoever had dared to speak a stolen name aloud:

*WHAT WOULD YOU KEEP?*

Then the wind took the letters apart, and far below them, down in the roots of the mountain, something vast and ancient began, very softly, to sing back.
