## 30. The Grand Cantor

The Vesper Gate did not look like a gate.

Emrys had imagined doors—great ones, iron-banded or carved from the ribs of mountains—but what stood at the head of the valley was a wound in the air itself, a seam of pale light stitched between two standing stones. The horn door and the ivory door flanked it, each tall as a chapel arch, each humming faintly, like struck bells that had not yet decided to stop.

Maren stood before them with her father's compass open in both hands, and when she saw Emrys come down the last slope with Sable limping beside her, she did something Emrys had not seen her do in all their weeks together.

She smiled without calculating anything first.

"You're late," Maren said. "I've been here two days. I ate my boots' laces out of boredom. They were terrible."

"You ate—" Emrys stopped, breathless, laughing despite everything. "You're impossible."

"I'm thorough." Maren's eyes went to Sable, to the grey robe he'd torn the badges from, to the raw red line around his throat where the Choir's binding had burned itself out when he spoke her name. Her smile gentled. "You brought him."

"He insisted."

"I did not insist," Sable said. "I merely declined to die anywhere else."

It was the closest thing to a declaration either of them had managed aloud, and Emrys felt it settle into her chest like a warm stone. There would be time for declarations later, if there was a later. She looked past them both at the seam of light, and the laughter went out of her the way heat leaves a forge when the bellows stop.

Because the dead were waiting there.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They stood in the snowfield before the gate in ranks so deep that the farthest were only a grey suggestion against the grey sky, and every one of them was looking at her. She could feel their attention the way you feel sun through glass—not painful, but inescapable. Some of them wore the clothes of Vellichor: high-collared coats, embroidered sashes, boots made for cobbled streets that no longer existed. Some wore nothing but the shapes they'd died in. A child near the front held a wooden horse by one leg, patiently, the way children hold things when they have been told to wait.

"They've been singing," Maren said quietly. "All night. Not words I know. The compass spins toward them and then toward the gate and then back again, like it can't make up its mind which is the country."

Emrys listened. Beneath the wind she caught it—the dead's song, thin and patient, rising and falling like breathing. It was the same melody the bone whistle had played. The same one that had been following her since Thornfell, half-heard, half-remembered.

Vellichor's true name, sung by everyone who had ever been sorry to leave it.

"We should rest before—" Sable began.

"No," said a voice from behind the ivory door. "I'm afraid we shouldn't. Rest is for people with time, and time, I regret to say, has run out for all of us."

* * *

He came around the door the way fog comes off a lake—no footsteps, no warning, just suddenly present. He was tall and spare, wrapped in robes the colour of old ash, and his face was kind in the way that well-made masks are kind: symmetrical, serene, and entirely concerned with being looked at.

Emrys knew him at once. You could not spend your life listening to the dead and mistake this man for living. He sounded like a choir loft—like forty voices agreeing, badly, to be one voice.

"The Grand Cantor," Sable said, and moved—instinctively, stupidly, wonderfully—to stand between the man and Emrys.

"Sable." The Cantor smiled at him with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse than anger. "My wayward note. Do you know, in three hundred years of training singers, you are the first to go flat on purpose? It takes a certain courage, deliberate discord. Or a certain foolishness. History has never been good at telling those apart."

"Let us pass," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We're done running."

"Oh, I know. That's rather the point of gates—they're where running stops." He clasped his hands before him and regarded her with an expression of frank, unhurried interest, the way a jeweler looks at a stone he intends to cut. "Emrys Thackeray. Bone-carver. Listener. The woman who walked roads only the dead remember, paid a tollhouse with her own mother's face, and taught a chorus of angry corpses to sing instead of scream. Do you know how long it's been since anyone surprised me?"

"Longer than you've been alive," Emrys said. "Because you haven't been alive for a very long time."

Something flickered across the mask. Not anger. Recognition—the look of a man who has been carrying a secret so long he's forgotten its weight until someone else touches it.

"No," he agreed softly. "I haven't."

And he reached up, and took off his face.

Not literally—though with memory-eaters, Emrys thought wildly, who could say—but nearly. The serenity fell away like a dropped cloak, and beneath it was a face worn hollow by centuries: handsome once, proud once, now simply *tired*, tired in the marrow, tired the way only the guilty are tired. His eyes were dark and wet and terribly human.

"My name," he said, "was Aldous Vane. And before I was the Choir's first singer, I was the last king of Vellichor."

The snowfield went silent. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath. Behind Emrys, she heard Maren's sharp intake—the sound of a cartographer's daughter hearing the name of the man whose signature sat, faded, in the corner of a map her father had died protecting.

"The plague came in my ninth year of reign," the Cantor—Aldous—went on, walking slowly along the front of the gathered dead as though reviewing troops. "You've read the archive. You know what it did. What I *did*. I signed the bargain. I gave my country to the gate so that the sickness would die with the borders. Forty years ago, give or take a decade—I lost track; eternity is poorly lit." He paused by the child with the wooden horse and, for just a moment, his composure cracked like river ice. "These are mine. All of them. Every soul I sold. They waited for me to join them, and instead I built the Choir, because someone had to keep the door shut. Because if the door opened and the bargain proved worthless, then I hadn't saved anyone. I'd only buried them twice."

"The plague came back anyway," Emrys said quietly. "We found the records. It crossed the erasure like it wasn't even there. Your sacrifice bought nothing."

"Yes." He said it simply, without defense, and that simplicity was more damning than any argument. "I learned that eleven years ago. I have spent eleven years knowing that I unmade a nation for nothing, and eleven years deciding what to do about it." He turned to face her fully. "Which brings me to you."

* * *

He gestured, and the ivory door began to glow.

Light poured from its edges like honey from a split comb, and within that light Emrys saw—she couldn't help seeing—everything. Vellichor whole. Streets she'd never walked, fountains she'd never heard, a harbor town called Vesper with white sails and a market that smelled of oranges and tar. She saw libraries and festivals and a bridge with carved lions, and she saw people, thousands of them, living lives that had been folded up like letters and never sent.

"One door restores it," Aldous said. "Every street, every name, every grave made right. The dead cross over into peace. The country breathes again." He let her look. He was not cruel about it; he let her look for a long time. "There is a price. There is always a price; the gate is honest that way. Restoration requires fuel, and the only fuel a nation's return burns is memory. Every living mind that holds any fragment of Vellichor must surrender it. The song will be forgotten by everyone who ever hummed it. Maren's archive will be blank paper. And you—" his voice gentled, horribly, "—you, who have given up so much already, would give the last of yourself. You would walk into your own country a stranger, with no memory of why you came, carrying nothing but the carving tools in your hands and a feeling you couldn't name."

"And him?" Emrys asked. Her voice was rough. "Sable. He was born here. He got his childhood back. Would he lose it again?"

"For the second time, yes."

Sable said nothing. She felt him beside her, felt the effort of his silence, and loved him for it—for not arguing, for not begging, for letting her choose with clear eyes.

"And what do you get?" she asked the Cantor. "If I open it. If everyone forgets. What does the last king get?"

"Oblivion," he said, and smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on a face that still remembered how to be royal. "Which is the only mercy left to me. I would finally be allowed to forget myself. Don't you understand, carver? I built the Choir because I could not bear to remember. I have eaten ten thousand memories trying to digest one. If the door opens—even if I pay the same price as everyone else, even if I forget my guilt along with my kingdom—that is not my punishment. That is my *reward*."

He stepped closer. This close, she could see that his hands trembled.

"But I am an old coward, Emrys Thackeray, and old cowards learn one trick above all others: we find someone braver and hide behind them. So here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and I will make it honestly, because I am done lying—it is the one virtue of being damned." He drew a breath, and the light of the ivory door bent toward him like wheat toward wind. "Open the door, and rule what comes through it. Not me. *You.* The gate needs a keeper—a voice to hold the restored country's name, the way you hold the names of the dead. You have the gift. You have the grief. You have walked every road and paid every toll. Let Vellichor live again under a queen who actually cares whether its people are remembered—and I will take your place in the dark. I will be the price. I will carry the forgetting so you don't have to. You lose nothing. You gain a nation."

The snowfield held its breath.

Behind her, the ranks of the dead stirred. The child with the wooden horse took a step forward. An old woman in a high-collared coat pressed her hands together as if in prayer. They wanted it—Emrys could feel their wanting rolling off them like heat off a kiln. *Say yes*, the dead whispered, in forty years of accumulated voices. *Say yes and we can go home.*

And oh, it was sweet, the offer. That was the horror of it. It wasn't a devil's bargain, twisted and obvious. It was *kind*. It solved everything. It cost her almost nothing—her memories of a country she'd never known, traded away like a coat she'd never worn, and in exchange: a kingdom, a purpose, the dead at peace, Sable's childhood returned—

Wait.

Her mind snagged on it, the way her thumbnail always caught on a flaw in the bone.

"You said I'd lose my memories of Vellichor," she said slowly. "But I barely have any. Fragments. Glimpses on the ghost-road. So the price for *me* is small. But him—" she nodded at Sable, without looking, because looking would be too much, "—he has a whole childhood back. Real days. His mother's kitchen. And you're asking him to lose it *again*, while I stand there wearing a crown built out of what he paid." She finally turned to Sable. "Is that what you want?"

Sable met her eyes. He looked, she thought, like a man reading a letter he'd written to himself long ago and finding it full of things he no longer believed.

"When the binding broke," he said quietly, "and I remembered—do you know what I remembered most? Not the kitchens. Not the harbor. I remembered being six years old and hiding under a table during an air raid drill, and a girl next door holding my hand and telling me the drills were just the country practicing its heartbeat." His mouth quirked. "I don't even know her name anymore. But Emrys—if the price of a whole country coming back is that I trade one memory of a table for a lifetime of streets, I'd sign in blood. I'd sign twice."

"There," Aldous said softly. "You see? Even he agrees."

"That's not what I said," Emrys answered, and her voice came out low and dangerous, the voice she used on ghosts who lied to themselves. "That's what *he* said. About *his* memory. You're very good at arithmetic, Cantor—you counted our losses and balanced them nicely. But you left something off the ledger." She stepped forward, into the light of the ivory door, and the dead went utterly still. "You said the gate needs a keeper. A voice to hold the name. You didn't ask what the *gate* wants. In forty years of locking this door, did you ever once listen to it?"

For the first time, the last king of Vellichor looked uncertain.

"The gate doesn't want," he said. "Gates open and close. That's all."

"Everything wants," Emrys said. "I've never met a dead thing that didn't. And I have been listening to this valley since the pass, and the gate has been singing back, and I'll tell you what I hear." She raised her hand, and from the seam of pale light between the stones, faint and clear, the melody rose to meet her—the true name of Vellichor, patient as snowfall. "It doesn't want a queen. It doesn't want walls or borders or a restored state with a mint and a navy. It's been *singing* for forty years, Aldous Vane. Countries don't sing. Songs do. I think your bargain broke something, and I think the gate has spent four decades trying to tell you the repair isn't restoration." She turned to Maren, whose eyes were huge and wet. "What did your father write on the map's margin? The part you wouldn't show me until the tollhouse?"

Maren swallowed. "'A country is not its borders,'" she recited, voice shaking. "'It is what its people cannot stop humming.'"

"So before I answer your generous offer," Emrys said, turning back to the Cantor, "before I touch either of these doors, I'm going to do the one thing you never did in forty years of guarding them."

"Which is?"

"Ask the gate what *it* would keep."

The seam of light between the stones brightened. The horn door shivered. And somewhere beneath the snowfield, beneath the roots of the mountain, something vast and old and patient drew a breath to answer her.

Aldous Vane's hand shot out—not toward Emrys, but toward the ivory door, as if to steady it, or himself.

"Don't," he said, and for the first time in three hundred years, the Grand Cantor sounded afraid.
