## 32. What Emrys Would Keep

The horn door was smaller than Emrys had expected.

She had imagined something vast — a cathedral mouth, a wall of carved ivory stretching up into the clouds — but the Vesper Gate was modest, almost domestic. Two doors set into a cliff face that had no business holding doors at all: one of white ivory, gleaming even under the grey mountain sky, and one of dark horn, worn smooth as a river stone. Between them, letters crawled in the dead tongue, rearranging themselves like ants deciding on a new path.

"It's the size of my mother's pantry door," Maren said, from where she sat wrapped in two blankets against the rocks. She had stopped recognizing Emrys three days ago; she had started again an hour ago, when Emrys had pressed the compass into her hands and watched her father's handwriting spark something loose in her memory. "All this walking. All this dying, apparently, for a door you could fit a wardrobe through."

"Doors are like that," said the ghost of the courier, who had followed them the whole way and whom only Emrys could hear. He drifted near the ivory panel with the wariness of a man who had once been turned away from somewhere important. "The important ones never announce themselves. My grandmother's cottage had a door like a castle gate and nothing worth guarding inside but jam."

"Hush," Emrys murmured, out of habit, before remembering that Maren could not hear him and Sable — Sable was watching her lips move with an expression she was beginning to know very well. It said: *you're talking to him again.* It also said, underneath: *I don't mind.*

The Cantor was coming. They all felt it — a pressure behind the ears, like weather rolling in off a sea none of them had seen. The counter-song he had been composing for weeks now moved through the valleys ahead of him, and it had taken things. The innkeeper at the last village had looked straight through Emrys as though she were a draft under a door. A shepherd boy had asked Maren why she was traveling alone. Even the dead along the road had grown quieter, thinned out, as if the song were eating them too.

But here, at the threshold, the song faltered. Emrys could feel it — a hesitation in the air, the way a choir falters when the soloist steps forward.

"The gate is listening," Sable said quietly. He stood between her and the road they'd come by, half-turned toward it, the stance of a man who had spent his life as a door himself and knew what one looked like. His grey robe was torn at the hem, filthy with snow, and he had never looked less like a member of the Choir or more like someone she might have met in a smuggler's tavern and liked immediately. "It's been listening since we crossed the boundary. Everything here listens."

"Then let's give it something worth hearing," Emrys said, and stepped forward.

* * *

The riddle was already written when she reached the doors. The crawling letters stilled, arranged themselves, and became words she understood without knowing how — the way she understood the complaints of the dead, the way she understood bone.

*WHAT WOULD YOU KEEP?*

And beneath it, smaller, almost gentle:

*AND WHAT WOULD YOU GIVE?*

Emrys had expected this. Maren had told her about it, weeping, on the night the gate had turned her away — *it asks what you would keep, and I said Vellichor, I said everything, I said I would keep all of it, every street and every song, and it looked at me like I was a child who had asked to keep the ocean in a cup.* Maren had spent twenty years collecting wrong memories of a country she'd never seen, hoarding them like a dragon of paper and grief, and when the moment came she had answered honestly, and honesty had not been enough.

Emrys understood now why.

She thought of the ivory door. Of course she did. Behind it lay a country restored — whole, real, breathing, its streets un-erased, its people returned to the world's ledger. Every living memory of Vellichor would be consumed to pay for it, including hers, including Sable's, including Maren's precious archive of wrongness. The country would live and no one would remember wanting it back. No one would remember there had ever been anything to want.

The dead crowded close behind her. She could feel them the way you feel a crowd at your back in a narrow street — hundreds of them, thousands, the people of Vellichor who had waited forty years in the thin places of the world for someone to open a door. They were not threatening. That was the terrible part. They were *hoping*, and hope has more weight than any threat.

*Open the ivory door,* they whispered. *Open it and live there. Open it and forget us, but open it.*

"You don't have to answer them," Sable said softly. He had come to stand beside her, not behind her, not in front of her. Beside. "That's their grief talking. Grief always wants the big gesture. It wants the fire and the sacrifice and the beautiful ending." He paused. "I would know. I served grief for fifteen years and called it duty."

"What would you keep?" Emrys asked him. "If it asked you."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved down the pass and stirred the hem of his ruined robe.

"I gave everything away once," he said. "Every memory, every name, every face. I thought that was strength. The Choir taught us that emptiness was purity." He looked at her then, and his eyes — which had held hers across a market stall in Thornfell, impossibly, memorably, when every other Choir gaze slid off her like rain off wax — were steady. "I would keep the market stall. The first time you looked back at me. I didn't know yet what it meant. I just knew the world had a shape suddenly, and the shape was you turning around."

"That's a terrible answer," Emrys said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "The gate will hate it."

"The gate," said Sable, "is made of horn and ivory and forty years of a king's guilt. I think it's lonely for honest answers."

* * *

She thought about her mother.

Not her mother's face — that was gone, paid to the Tollhouse of Names on a road only the dead remembered, and some payments could not be reversed. She had learned that at the shrine, kneeling among cold stones, offering everything she had left and hearing the old dead voice say *no, little carver, that debt is paid, that coin is spent.* For months she had carried the absence like a stone in her chest. Her mother's hands she still had — the way they'd moved when carving, the way they'd smelled of juniper oil. Her mother's laugh she still had. But the face, the particular face, the thing you'd know in a crowd of ten thousand —

Gone. And she had grieved it, and then she had gone on, because that was what Emrys Thackeray did. She stayed invisible. She kept moving. She never got involved.

Except that she had gotten involved so thoroughly, so catastrophically, that she was now standing at the last door in the world being asked by a country whether it could please, please exist again.

"I can't give you what you're asking," she told the dead, aloud, in the ordinary voice she used for widows and ghosts and men bleeding out against her workshop door. "I know you want me to say yes. I know you've waited. And I know—" her throat tightened— "I know what it is to be forgotten. I'm being forgotten right now, mile by mile, by everyone I ever met. The innkeeper. The widow whose charm I carved. Probably the widow herself by now. There's a version of this where I open the ivory door and vanish into a country that loves me and doesn't know my name, and honestly, some nights, that sounded like peace."

The dead were silent. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"But I've learned something on your roads," Emrys went on. "I learned it from a tollhouse and a shrine and a woman who collected wrong memories for twenty years because loving something lost was the only country she had. You don't get to buy a home with other people's forgetting. That's not restoration. That's just a bigger erasure, with better manners." She touched the ivory door — lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you mean no harm. "Your king tried this. He traded a whole nation to escape his own guilt, and the plague came anyway, and all it bought was silence. I won't make his trade prettier. I won't make it kinder. It's the same trade."

*WHAT WOULD YOU KEEP?* the gate asked again, patient as bone.

Emrys laughed — a wet, unsteady sound, but a laugh.

"Not the country," she said. "I'm sorry. Not the crown — gods, definitely not the crown, I've seen what crowns do to people, I watched one crumble a man to dust not three days ago. Not the map, not the archive, not even the mountains I've walked until my boots gave out." She turned. Sable stood where she'd left him, and Maren had risen from her blankets and come closer, the compass clutched in both hands, watching with the fierce attention of a woman who had bet her whole life on this answer.

"I would keep the man beside me," Emrys said. "Who gave up everything twice — once to the Choir, once for me — and would do it again, and tells me so at the worst possible moments, usually while I'm crying. I would keep the woman who waited, who answered the gate wrong and got turned away and *came back anyway*, because that's what love looks like when it's stubborn. I would keep the ghost who complained about his own funeral and died getting a map to strangers because someone had to carry it. I would keep the widow's charm and the smell of juniper oil and my mother's hands, even though I can't keep her face." Her voice steadied. "Small things. That's my answer. Small bones. I'm a bone-carver. I work small. Give me the small things and I'll make them sing."

*AND WHAT WOULD YOU GIVE?*

"Everything else," Emrys said simply. "Whatever's left over. Take it. I was invisible for thirty years; I know how to be light."

* * *

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then the horn door — the plain, dark, river-smoothed horn door that nobody had looked at twice — began to glow.

Not brightly. Warmly. The way a workshop glows at dusk when the lamp is lit and the day's carving is going well. Light seeped out from under the door like warmth from an oven, and the letters between the doors dissolved, and Emrys heard — felt — the gate exhale.

*SMALL THINGS,* it said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole, like the last note of a lullaby. *FORTY YEARS OF KINGS AND CANTORS AND CHOIRS, AND THE FIRST HONEST ANSWER IS SMALL THINGS.*

"It's mocking us," Maren whispered.

"It's *relieved*," said the courier's ghost, and for once he sounded awed instead of grumbling. "Doors know, carver. Doors know what people really want. Everyone who ever stood here wanted the ivory. Wanted it all back, whole, huge, theirs. You're the first one who wanted something she could hold."

The horn door swung inward.

Beyond it was not a country. Emrys had braced herself for a country — streets, towers, the capital city that wasn't there. Instead there was a darkness full of voices. Thousands upon thousands of them, layered like sediment, like strata of song, and beneath the voices a vast patience, and beneath the patience something that had been waiting so long it had nearly become the waiting itself.

Vellichor. Not the state. Not the borders. The *song* of it — the thing that had existed before the king and would exist after the dust, the thing a country really is when you strip away the maps and the taxes and the names on doors.

It leaned toward her the way the dead leaned toward her, all her life, wanting to be heard.

"Oh," Emrys breathed. "Oh, you're *music*."

Beside her, Sable made a small sound — half gasp, half sob. He was born here. He had been stolen from here twice, once as a child and once from his own memory, and standing before the opened door he heard, faintly, a fragment of a lullaby he couldn't place and couldn't stop weeping over.

Maren had both hands over her mouth. Through her fingers, muffled and trembling, she was humming. Something she didn't know she knew. Something her father had whittled into a compass and a map and a daughter's bedtime hums, hoping it would survive.

The dead pressed close at Emrys's back, and their hope changed key — from begging to something brighter, something that sounded, unmistakably, like the first bar of a hymn.

"They want you to sing," Sable said. His hand found hers. His grip was cold and certain. "Emrys. They want *you*."

Emrys Thackeray, who had spent her life staying invisible, who had carved mourning charms for strangers and guided contraband through passes no living map remembered, who had paid her mother's face for a road and her own name for a chance — stepped to the threshold of the horn door, filled her lungs with the cold clean air of a country that did not exist, and opened her mouth.

The first note left her, and the mountain itself leaned in to listen.
