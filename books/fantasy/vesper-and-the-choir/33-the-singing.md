## 33. The Singing

The horn door stood open, and behind it was not a country.

Emrys had expected—she would admit this later, to Sable, over tea she no longer had to carve charms to afford—a landscape. Hills. A city with its towers restored, its bells ringing, its markets loud with the commerce of the returned. She had braced herself for a nation, whole and breathing, demanding to be ruled.

Instead there was light. Not sunlight exactly, though it had that quality—the particular gold of late afternoon in autumn, when everything looks like it's being remembered fondly. The light came from everywhere and belonged to nowhere, and it moved. It flowed through the doorway like water through a weir, slow and patient and endless.

And in the light were the dead.

They had been gathering for days, she realized now. Every ghost who had walked beside her on roads only they remembered, every voice she'd heard through bone and stone and river-water, every murmuring relic in every smuggler's stall from Thornfell to the sea—they were all here, pressed up against the threshold like travelers at a dock, waiting for a ship that had been forty years delayed.

The man from the tollhouse stood nearest. He had taken her mother's face as payment, and he had never once apologized for it, because the dead don't apologize; they only wait. Now he looked at her with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: weary competence masking loneliness.

"Well?" he said. "Are you going to stand there all day?"

* * *

Behind her, the false door—the ivory one—was still humming.

She could feel it the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. It sang of restoration. Of Vellichor reborn, stone by stone, name by name, paid for out of the pockets of the living. Every memory of the lost country, devoured to fuel its return. Every person who had ever hummed a fragment of a Vellichori lullaby without knowing why would fall silent forever. Every child like the ones Maren had interviewed, clutching their "wrong" memories like contraband sweets, would wake up empty-handed.

And Emrys would be its queen. That had been the Cantor's offer, made with dust already gathering in the corners of his mouth: *Rule what you resurrect.* A throne built on the forgetting of everyone she loved.

Sable stood beside her. He had not let go of her hand since the sanctum, since the ritual chamber where she'd found him mid-unbinding, his stolen childhood pouring back into him like water into a cracked vessel—and then, at the gate, poured deliberately back out again, all but one drop. One memory. Her. He had traded a homeland for a single afternoon in a workshop in Thornfell, and when she'd asked him if he was sure, he'd said, "I've had duty. I know what it weighs. I'd rather carry you."

He hadn't let go since. His hand was cold. Half-remembered men are always cold, she'd learned.

"The dead are waiting," he said quietly.

"I know."

"Maren's waiting too." Maren stood further back, at the edge of the gate's threshold, where the riddle had turned her away. She wasn't weeping anymore. She was watching with the fierce, hungry attention of a cartographer's daughter seeing the territory itself, not just the map—and Emrys understood suddenly that being turned away might have been the kindest thing the gate had ever done for her. You cannot enter the thing you love and still map it.

"Whatever you're going to do," Maren called, "do it loudly. I want to write down every note."

Emrys almost laughed. Almost. What stopped her was the weight of the moment pressing down like snow-load on a roof—the sense that everything, absolutely everything, balanced on what she did next.

Because here was the truth she had carried up the mountain, through the whiteout, past the tollhouse, through the city that wasn't there: the ivory door would work. That was the horror of it. It would restore Vellichor perfectly, completely, at the cost of every living memory of it—which meant the restored country would rise into a world that had already forgotten why it mattered. A nation of ghosts ruling the living's blank spots. And the Cantor knew it. Had always known it. That was why he'd engineered the erasure—not to save anyone from plague, but to escape the guilt of a king whose bargain had bought nothing. A kingdom restored by forgetting is just a kingdom with better lighting.

And the horn door? The horn door asked only one question: *What would you keep?*

She had answered. Not the country. Not the crown. The man beside her. The woman who waited. The small, stubborn, specific loves that no bargain should ever be allowed to touch.

The horn door had opened.

But an open door is not an answer. It is only permission.

* * *

The dead pressed closer.

There were so many of them. She had spent her whole life hearing them one at a time—one complaining merchant, one grieving soldier, one widow's husband grumbling about the eulogy—and she had thought of them as singular, separate, each with their own small bone-carved voice. But gathered here at the threshold of their own erased home, they were a weather system. They were a sea deciding whether to be tide or wave.

"We want to go home," said a woman in a dress style Emrys had never seen outside of Maren's sketches.

"There is no home," said another, gently, the way you correct a child. "That's the whole point."

"There could be." This from the courier—the first ghost, the dying man who had pressed the oilskin map into her hands in the storm. He had stayed with her longer than any ghost had a right to, unable to cross until his errand was done, and somewhere along those remembered roads his errand had become something larger. "The door is open. Take the ivory door, carver. Give us back our walls."

"And take everyone's memories to pay for them," Emrys said.

"They're only memories."

Sable's hand tightened around hers. "No," he said, to the courier, with a half-man's terrible authority. "They're never only anything."

The crowd of dead murmured. Some of them agreed with the courier—of course they did; they had lost everything and grief makes economists of us all, eager to spend whatever currency isn't ours. Others looked at Emrys with something worse than hope: expectation. She was the bone-carver. She heard them. In their long decades of silence, she was the closest thing to a representative they had ever had.

"You could give us back our names," said the tollhouse man. "Just the names. Let the country stay dead but say our names again."

"The names come with the country," Emrys said. "You can't carve a charm and keep the bone unbroken."

"Then break it," someone shouted from the back, and the murmur swelled toward a roar, and Emrys felt the old familiar terror—not of the dead, never of the dead, but of being the only living person in a room full of need—

And then she did the thing she had done her entire life, the thing that had kept her invisible and alive and alone through forty years of erasure and thirty years before that of listening.

She listened.

Under the roar, under the demands and the grief and the desperate arithmetic of the lost, there was another sound. Faint. Persistent. The dead weren't only shouting. They were humming.

A fragment of melody, passed from mouth to mouth like a secret sweet. A lullaby, maybe, or a work song, or a drinking tune—something so ordinary that it had survived the erasure precisely because no one thought it worth erasing. Mothers had sung it to children in countries that had never heard of Vellichor, and the children had grown and sung it to their children, and the song had traveled on while the country that birthed it dissolved like sugar in rain.

It was the same melody the child would bring her, years later, carved into a bone whistle. But she didn't know that yet. She only knew that the dead were humming it, and that when they hummed it, they stopped arguing, and that the sound of ten thousand ghosts humming a lullaby was the most heartbreaking and beautiful thing she had ever heard.

"Sable," she said. "Do you hear that?"

He cocked his head. Then his face changed—the face of a man remembering something he'd sworn away, reaching for a homeland and finding only one memory where it used to be. "I—" His voice caught. "I think my mother sang that. I think—I can't see her face, but I can feel the tune. Like a scar that itches."

"That's all that's left of her," Emrys said. "Of all of them. Not the walls. Not the borders. The tune." She turned back to the crowd of dead, and her heart was hammering, and her hands—the hands that had carved ten thousand charms, that had coaxed voices out of femur and rib and river-stone, that had unleashed a chorus of angry dead against hunters in the snow—her hands were steady.

"You want your country back," she said, and her voice carried the way it carried through bone, clear and low and impossible to ignore. "I can't give you that. The ivory door would eat the living to feed you, and you'd rise into a world that couldn't remember why you mattered, and within a generation you'd be the Choir all over again—enforcing your own resurrection, punishing anyone who forgot differently. I've met that future. He's standing over there turning into dust, and he whispers thank-you in his sleep."

The Cantor—what was left of him, a grey shape at the edge of the light, more apology than man—did not deny it.

"But," Emrys said, and here she did laugh, short and startled, because the idea had been sitting in her chest since the humming started and it was either laugh or weep, "I'm a carver. And I'll tell you the first thing they teach you about bone."

She held up her hands.

"You can't put it back together once it's broken. Everyone learns that the hard way. Glue fails. Splints crack. What's done is done." She let that land, let the dead's faces fall, let the silence gather. "But bone has one property nothing else has. Burn it, grind it, bury it for a century—it remembers its shape. The pattern's in it deeper than the breaking. And a good carver doesn't try to rebuild the animal. She listens to what the bone wants to become, and she helps it become that. A whistle. A charm. A song."

Maren, at the threshold, had gone very still, her pencil hovering.

"You're not a country anymore," Emrys told the dead. "You can't be. The bargain saw to that, and no door undoes a bargain, it only moves the bill. But you're not nothing either. You're a pattern too deep for the breaking. Forty years of erasure and you survived—in lullabies, in wrong memories, in a smuggler's map drawn by a man who loved you enough to die for it. So I'm not going to open the ivory door. And I'm not going to close the horn one."

She stepped forward, to the very lip of the threshold, where the golden light touched her boots.

"I'm going to sing."

* * *

Later—much later, when travelers came seeking the sung borders and Maren's maps hung in every inn from Thornfell to the coast—there would be arguments about what actually happened at the Vesper Gate. Scholars would write treatises. The reformed Choir, waking with empty hands and open eyes, would contribute confused testimony. Maren would publish three conflicting accounts on purpose, because she said a mystery with one answer dies.

But Emrys knew, and Sable knew, and the dead knew, and this is what happened:

She opened her mouth, and she sang the true name of Vellichor.

Not spoke it. Names spoken are commands; names sung are invitations. She didn't know the full name—no living person did—but she didn't need to. She had spent her life learning how the dead speak: in fragments, in refrains, in the parts of songs that stick. She took the lullaby the dead were humming and she gave it words, the words from the archive of ash, the words from Maren's collected wrong-memories, the words the courier had died whispering—*Vesper, Vesper, evening-song, the last light before the dark*—and she wove them together the way she wove a charm, patiently, listening for what the material wanted to be.

And the country answered.

Not rose. Not rebuilt. *Answered.* The golden light began to move in rhythm, and the rhythm was her rhythm, and the hills that weren't there rippled like a struck string. The dead felt it first—they went silent, every one, ten thousand mouths closing at once—and then they began, hesitantly, to sing along. The courier's voice joined hers, rough and off-key and weeping. The tollhouse man. The woman in the unfashionable dress. Voice after voice after voice, finding the melody, finding the harmony, finding the parts of the song that had been waiting forty years for someone to sing them.

And as they sang, they crossed over.

That was the miracle of it, the thing that made even the Cantor lift his ruined face: the song was the crossing. There was no other side, no restored nation waiting behind the door. There was only the song, and the song lived in memory—the living memory of the world, in Maren's pencil, in Sable's single kept afternoon, in every child who would ever hum a tune they couldn't name—and a country that lives in memory cannot be conquered, cannot be bargained away, cannot be erased, because you cannot burn a song out of every throat at once. Someone is always humming.

The dead didn't go to Vellichor. Vellichor went with them—into the singing, into the space between notes, into the place where a mother's voice lives after the mother is gone. They crossed over the way a note crosses into silence: not ending, resolving.

Emrys sang until her voice gave out, and then she sang harmony, and then she simply stood in the golden light with her mouth closed and tears running freely down her face while a nation finished becoming music around her.

The last to cross was the courier. He paused at the threshold—or what had been the threshold; the doors were gone now, both of them, horn and ivory dissolved into the song like salt into soup—and he looked at her with his tired ghost's eyes.

"Not bad," he said. "For a bone-carver."

"Go on," she managed. "Before I charge you a toll."

He laughed—a real laugh, the first she'd ever heard from him—and stepped into the music, and was gone, and the mountainside where the gate had stood was just a mountainside, wind-bitten and ordinary, with evening coming on.

Vesper. Evening-song. The last light before the dark, which is also, if you wait, the first light of whatever comes next.

* * *

They sat on the mountainside until full dark, the three of them, passing a skin of wine Maren had carried up the mountain for a celebration she'd imagined very differently.

"So," Maren said at last, pencil already moving in the blue dusk, sketching by feel. "When I publish this, the official version is going to be that the gate opened onto a field of eternal light and a choir of angels sang the nation into heaven. Yes? Something dignified."

"The official version," Emrys said, leaning into Sable's shoulder, "is going to be that a bone-carver with no formal training stood at the door of a lost country and sang it a lullaby until it agreed to stop haunting people."

"That's worse than what I said."

"That's what happened."

Maren snorted and kept sketching. "People will hum it, you know. The song. Within a year, they'll be humming it in taverns and calling it an old folk tune, and no one will know why it makes them homesick for somewhere they've never been." She looked up, and in the starlight her face was young and fierce and finally, finally at peace with the country she could map but never enter. "That's better than walls. Walls fall down."

"That's the idea," Emrys said.

Sable said nothing. He rarely did, in these hours—half a man, newly made, still learning the weight of the one memory he'd chosen to keep. But his arm was around her, and his hand had found hers again in the dark, and when the wind came down off the pass it carried, faintly, unmistakably, a few bars of melody that no one was singing.

Emrys listened to it the way she listened to everything: fully, patiently, with her whole attention.

And underneath the wind, underneath the fading song, underneath the ordinary night-sounds of an ordinary mountain, she heard—very far away, very faint, but getting closer—the sound of hoofbeats on the road below, and a voice calling a word she knew:

*"Vesper! Is there anyone at Vesper?"*

She sat up.

Someone was coming up the mountain. Someone who knew the gate's name—and the gate no longer existed to tell them.
