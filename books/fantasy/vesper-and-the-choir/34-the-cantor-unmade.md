## 34. The Cantor Unmade

Sable had spent his whole life learning to sing silence.

That was the first thing they taught you in the Choir—not the hymns, not the harmonies that unmade memory like wet paper, but the silence underneath them. *A singer who cannot hold a rest,* the Grand Cantor had told him, twenty years and one stolen childhood ago, *is a singer who cannot steal.* Sable had been very good at rests. He had held one for most of his life: the rest where his past should have been, the rest where his name's origin should have been, the long patient rest where love should have been until a bone-carver with ink-stained fingers walked into it and refused to leave.

Now, standing at the Vesper Gate with the last note of Emrys's song still trembling in the stone, he understood that he had never once sung silence at all. He had only been silent inside someone else's song.

And that song was coming apart.

It began in the Cantor's hands.

The old man stood before the horn door—no, not the horn door; the horn door was open now, and what lay beyond it was not a country but a melody, vast and golden and going somewhere—and the Grand Cantor of the Choir of Vellichor's Silence raised his arms as if to conduct the dawn itself, and nothing came.

No counter-hymn. No erasure. The great engine of forgetting that had run for forty years on stolen centuries simply declined to answer him, the way a well declines when the aquifer has moved on.

"Sing," the Cantor said. Not to Sable. To himself. His voice, which could once flatten mountains into maps and maps into blank parchment, cracked on the second syllable like an old plate dropped on stone.

He tried again. What came out was not a hymn. It was—Sable leaned forward despite himself—it was a lullaby. A child's lullaby, in a language Sable recognized without knowing how, the way you recognize your own face in a window at night. Vellichori. The tongue of the country that wasn't. The Cantor sang four bars of a bedtime song about a moon-boat crossing a river of sheep-clouds, and then he stopped, because he was weeping too hard to continue.

"You were there," Sable said quietly. "You remember it."

"I never forgot it." The Cantor did not turn around. "That was my punishment. My reward. I built an order whose entire sacrament was forgetting, and I kept the one memory I could not bear to lose." He laughed—a small, dreadful sound. "Do you know what it is to be the only man in the world who remembers a country? Forty years, boy. Forty years of singing everyone else empty while I sat alone at night with an entire nation folded up inside me like a letter no one would read."

Emrys stepped forward. Sable felt her before he saw her move—that gravity she had, the way the dead leaned toward her and the living unconsciously turned their shoulders. She looked at the Cantor the way she looked at bones before carving: assessing where the truth lived in him.

"You were the king," she said. It was not a question. Maren had worked it out days ago from the archive seals; Sable had confirmed it in the sanctum, mid-rescue, when the half-restored memories finally made sense of the throne room he'd dreamed about all his life.

"I was the king," the Cantor agreed. "And when the plague came—when I watched my city die by hundreds every morning, when the physicians said *there is no cure, Majesty, there is only bargaining*—I found the bargain. Give the country away. Fold it into a song and bury the song. Let the world forget us so thoroughly that even grief cannot find the address." His hands lowered slowly, like something landing. "The plague was supposed to die with the memory of us. Plague needs hosts, and hosts need names."

"But it didn't," Emrys said. "It came back anyway."

"It came back anyway." He said it the way other men said *I killed my own son.* "Forty years. Do you know how many mornings I woke and thought, *today I will tell them, today I will undo it, today I will let the world remember and hang me for it*? And every morning the order sang, and the silence held, and I told myself the bargain still might work if I only enforced it faithfully enough." He turned at last, and Sable flinched.

Not because the Cantor was frightening. Because he wasn't. Without the stolen centuries holding him upright, he was simply old. Small. A thin man in grey robes gone soft at the seams, with white hair escaping from under his hood and eyes the color of rain through a dirty window. Somewhere under all those eaten years was a boy from a moon-boat lullaby, and the boy had been waiting a very long time to be let out.

"The gate took them back," the Cantor said, wonderingly. He flexed his fingers as though counting them. "All of them. Every century I swallowed. Every name I ate. It—" He swayed. "Oh. Oh, there are so many. I had forgotten how heavy they'd be. How *many* people I was."

Sable moved without deciding to. He caught the old man's arm as he buckled, and the weight of him was nothing, less than nothing, a bundle of sticks in a robe.

"Don't," the Cantor whispered. "Don't hold me up. Let me—" But he didn't finish the sentence either way, and Sable held on.

* * *

Dying, it turned out, took longer than Sable expected, and was stranger than anything the Choir had ever taught him to fear.

They got the Cantor sitting against the base of the gate, where the stone hummed faintly with the residue of Emrys's song, and the humming seemed to ease him, the way a hand on the chest eases a frightened animal. Maren knelt nearby with her father's compass open on her knee—the needle spinning lazily now, pointing nowhere, its work done—and watched the last king of Vellichor with an expression Sable couldn't read. Grief for a man she'd hated an hour ago. Something like that. Grief is rarely tidy about its appointments.

The dust started at his fingertips.

There was no better word for it. The stolen years were leaving him, but not violently—they were going home, streaming off him in threads too fine to see, and where they left, he crumbled. Grey dust, fine as flour, lifting off his hands in the mountain wind and drifting toward the open gate as if summoned.

"It doesn't hurt," the Cantor reported, with the mild surprise of a man noticing the weather. "I want that noted. Someone should write it down. All those singers we sent to die for the order—I told them it would hurt. I told them oblivion was agony, to keep them obedient." He coughed, and the cough produced a little puff of dust, absurdly, like a snuffed candle. "It doesn't hurt at all. It feels like putting down bags I forgot I was carrying."

"What happens to them?" Emrys asked softly. "The ones you took. The centuries."

"Gone home, I think. Or gone wherever songs go." He smiled, and for one moment the smile was young—startlingly, heartbreakingly young, a king's son smiling at a festival forty years dead. "Maybe they'll be somebody's memory of a summer day. Maybe they're already in the song. Maybe somewhere right now a shepherd in Thornfell is humming a tune he doesn't know and feeling inexplicably happy about sheep." He laughed, which cost him some of his collarbone, judging by the way his robe sagged. "Forgive me. Dying makes me whimsical. It's the lightness."

"You engineered the death of a nation," Maren said. Her voice shook. "You lied to your own order. You fed children to the silence and called it initiation. My father spent his whole life branded a madman for remembering what you erased."

"Yes," the Cantor said simply. "All true. I have been waiting forty years for someone to say it to my face, and now that you have, I find I agree with you completely. Isn't that the worst part? That the villain always agrees?" He looked at her, and his dusty eyes were terribly clear. "Your father was the bravest man in this story, Maren Cartwright. Tell his map I'm sorry. Tell him—" the wind took a handful of him away mid-sentence, and he gathered himself with visible effort, "—tell him the borders were more beautiful than I deserved."

Maren wiped her face with her sleeve, fiercely, like a girl annoyed at rain. "I'll tell him myself. He's alive. He's in Lowmark. He thinks I'm collecting herbs."

The Cantor's laugh turned into something else, and for a moment Sable thought the end had come—but no, it was weeping, plain human weeping, the kind the Choir trained out of you in your first year. "Alive," the old man repeated. "Good. Good. One thing I didn't break beyond mending."

Then he looked at Sable.

Sable had rehearsed this moment, insofar as anyone rehearses watching a monster die. He had a speech prepared, actually—he'd composed it during the flight from the sanctum, polished it in the passes, tightened it at the gate. It began *You stole my childhood* and ended *and I will spend the rest of my life making sure no one ever does that to another child again*, and it was a good speech, and he had earned it.

What came out instead was: "Was I happy? Before. In Vellichor. Was I—was there anything good?"

The Cantor's expression went soft and ancient. "You used to sing," he said. "Badly. In the temple choir, the children's one—you stood in the back row and you sang every note wrong with tremendous confidence, and the choirmaster despaired, and your mother laughed until she cried every festival day." He reached up with a hand that was mostly memory now and gripped Sable's wrist. "You were not a sad child, Sable. Whatever else they took—whatever you get back or don't—know that you began in joy. They can't unring that bell. Joy is stubborn. It waits."

Sable nodded, because speech had temporarily left the country.

"And you," the Cantor said to Emrys. "Bone-carver. Gate-singer. The woman who found the third door nobody built." He was fading faster now, his outline going soft at the edges, dust rising off him in slow spirals that caught the light and glittered. "I offered you a kingdom this morning. You turned it down for two people and a song. Do you know how long I've waited for someone to make the arithmetic come out that way?"

"Was it a test?" Emrys asked.

"Everything is a test. Almost everything is also a trap. Very few things are both, and none of them are doors." He closed his eyes. The dust was thick around him now, golden in the light from the open gate, and Sable thought—with the part of him that would forever hear the world in music—that the old man was dissolving into harmony, note by note, going home into the song he'd buried. "Thank you," the Grand Cantor of the Choir whispered. "For ending it properly. For singing instead of conquering. For letting me put it down."

His lips kept moving after there was no voice. Sable, who could read lips in six languages and had once transcribed a confession across a battlefield, caught the last of it:

*—the moon-boat, mother, I'm coming, I kept it, I kept it all this time—*

And then there was a drift of gold dust settling against the gate's foundations, and a grey robe empty as a shed skin, and the wind off the high snowfields carrying, very faintly, four bars of a lullaby about sheep-clouds.

Nobody spoke for a while. The dead, who had crowded the valley all day—lucid, patient, waiting for their country to decide what it was—began, one by one, to turn toward the open gate. Sable watched an old woman take a farmer's hand. Watched a soldier in Vellichori blue straighten his coat out of habit before walking into the light. Watched, with his throat tight, a small boy chase a dog that had waited for him a very long time.

"They're crossing," Emrys murmured. "All of them. The song holds them now. They don't have to haunt anymore."

"Where does it go?" Maren asked. "The song. After today."

"Everywhere songs go," Emrys said. "Into throats. Into kitchens. Into children who hum without knowing why." She smiled, tired and luminous. "Countries die. Songs don't, if you feed them."

* * *

The binding broke at dusk.

Sable felt it go the way you feel a tooth finally come loose after weeks of worry—one clean, shocking release, and then a strange nakedness where the pressure used to live. He was standing watch at the mouth of the pass, half-listening to Maren argue with Emrys about whether they could reasonably stop for dinner (Maren was pro-dinner; Maren was always pro-dinner; Maren had once suggested they detour three hours for a legendary meat pie), when the Choir's chain simply fell off his heart.

He staggered. Caught himself on a boulder. And then, all around him, across the whole breadth of the mountains, he felt—heard—knew—hundreds of others doing exactly the same thing.

Grey robes in a hundred towns and garrisons and way-stations, stopping mid-task. Singers frozen over their instruments. Wardens dropping their ledgers. The entire vast machinery of forgetting grinding to a halt not with a crash but with a sigh, like a choir reaching the final rest and discovering, for the first time, that the silence afterward belonged to them.

*Empty hands,* Sable thought. *Open eyes.*

He knew what was happening in those moments, because he had lived the before-version of it. Men and women waking with no orders in their heads for the first time in decades. Some would weep. Some would wander. Some would sit down in the road and try to remember their own mothers and find, where the memory should be, a smooth white absence shaped like a mother. The Choir had eaten its members' pasts as initiation; the Cantor's death returned the stolen centuries to the song, but nothing returned the small personal thefts. Those were gone. Those were gone forever, and every grey robe in the world was about to learn how to be a person from scratch.

Some of them deserve pity, Sable thought. Some of them deserve a trial. Most of them, God help them, deserve breakfast.

He turned back toward camp and stopped.

Because down the pass, stumbling through the blue evening snow, came grey robes. Three of them. Ex-singers, ex-hunters—Sable recognized the walk of Choir field training even stripped of its purpose. They were moving toward the gate the way iron filings move toward a lodestone, drawn by the rumor of what had happened here, and they were singing.

Badly.

One of them was attempting the Vellichor song from memory—half-heard fragments, wrong intervals, words invented to fill gaps—and the other two had joined in, and the result was a catastrophe of harmony that would have gotten all three of them flogged in the old days, and Sable stood in the snow and listened to the worst singing in the history of the world and felt tears freeze on his cheeks, because it was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard. Nobody had taught them. Nobody was conducting. They were just three hollowed-out people trying to fill themselves with the only thing they'd found that echoed.

Emrys appeared beside him. She followed his gaze, listened for a moment, and winced. "Oh, that's dreadful."

"That's the future," Sable said.

"Mm. The future needs a choirmaster." She glanced up at him, and the corner of her mouth did the thing it did. "Someone patient. Someone who knows all the rests."

"I don't know any of the notes."

"Neither do they. You'll be perfect together." She bumped her shoulder against his arm. "Also, Maren has won the dinner argument by default, because she's already cooking, and whatever she's making smells like a decision we've already made."

They walked back toward the fire, and behind them the three grey figures crested the pass and stopped, uncertain, at the edge of the firelight—empty-handed, wide-eyed, humming.

But Sable slept badly that night, and near dawn he woke with a question sitting on his chest like a cat.

He crawled out of the tent into the cold pink light and found Emrys already awake, feeding the fire, the courier's ghost dozing against a stone nearby in the manner of ghosts who had finally finished their errands and discovered napping.

"When you answered the gate," Sable said. "What you'd keep. You said—the man beside you and the woman who waited."

"I did."

"And the gate opened." He crouched beside her, watching the flames. "Gates don't open for free, Emrys. I was Choir. I know how bargains are priced. Everything costs." He made himself say it. "What did it take from you?"

Emrys was quiet for a long moment. She stirred the fire. She looked, briefly, at the ghost, who had opened one eye with the frank interest of a man who intended to repeat everything he heard.

"That," she said at last, "is a very good question. And I think—" she reached into her coat and drew out the map, the oilskin worn soft as cloth now, the vanished roads faded to almost nothing, "—I think we'd better look at what I'm still carrying, and what I'm not. Because this morning I tried to remember my mother's voice, Sable. The one I had left. The one I paid the tollhouse to keep a shadow of." Her hand trembled, just slightly, around the map. "And I can't find it anywhere."

The fire crackled. The ghost sat up.

Down the valley, faint and clear, someone was humming.
