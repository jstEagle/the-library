## 35. Aftermath: The Grey Robes

Maren found the first one in her father's old map room, which was not a map room anymore but a kitchen, because kitchens were more useful and grief was a poor interior decorator.

He was standing at the window with his grey robe still on, looking out at the valley of Lowmark as though he had never seen a valley before. Which, Maren realized, watching him for a long moment from the doorway, he possibly hadn't. Not in any way that stayed.

"You're in my house," she said, not unkindly.

He turned. His face was the face of a man who had been asked to describe himself and had come up with nothing. "Am I?" he said. "I don't— I woke up on your road. There was snow. I think there was snow." He looked down at his sleeves as if they might contain an explanation. They did not. Grey wool, frayed at the cuffs, smelling faintly of incense and cold stone. "I don't know why I'm dressed like this."

"Do you know your name?"

He considered this with the seriousness of a scholar examining a difficult text. "No," he said finally, and then, with a small, startled laugh that seemed to surprise him more than it surprised her: "Isn't that funny? I know how to read. I know what a hinge is. I know that bread rises if you're patient. But I don't know my name."

"It'll come back," Maren said. "Some of it. The parts worth keeping."

She didn't tell him that she'd spent three days after the gate opened sitting up at night wondering whether that was true, or whether she'd only decided to believe it because the alternative was too bleak to carry. She didn't tell him that she'd seen four others like him since midwinter — grey-robed wanderers drifting down out of the high passes like leaves after a late frost, empty-handed and empty-eyed, blinking at farmhouses they had once stood outside of in the dark, listening, taking notes for masters whose names no longer meant anything even to themselves.

She told him none of that. She said, instead: "There's soup. It's my mother's recipe, except I've never actually tasted my mother's cooking, so really it's my best guess at a recipe, which makes it more of a hypothesis than a soup."

The man blinked. Then he laughed again — properly this time, startled out of him — and Maren felt something in her chest loosen by a single degree.

"That's the spirit," she said. "Come sit down before you fall down."

* * *

His name came back on the third day. Aldous. He wept when it arrived, quietly, into his soup, and Maren pretended very hard to be interested in the fire so he could do it with some dignity.

"That's the strange part," he said afterward, turning his spoon over and over. "I expected to feel *empty*. Everyone warned us — well. I suppose someone must have warned us once. That everything taken would leave a hole." He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't feel like a hole. It feels like a room that's been swept. Like I'm waiting for furniture."

"The Choir took your memories as initiation," Maren said. She'd learned to say it plainly. Plainness was kinder than tiptoeing. "Your past. Your family, probably. Your childhood, certainly."

"I assumed." He looked at his hands. "And you're telling me this because...?"

"Because you deserve to know what was done to you. And because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because the people in these valleys remember grey robes coming in the night. They remember questions they weren't allowed to know they'd been asked. Neighbors who vanished. My father spent forty years collecting 'wrong' memories because the Choir made sure every right one was contraband." She met his eyes. "You're going to walk into villages where people have every reason to hate what you were."

Aldous absorbed this. Then, to her lasting astonishment, he nodded and said, "I'd like to apologize to them. If I can't remember doing it, the least I can do is stand there while they remember."

Maren turned back to the fire so he wouldn't see her face do whatever it was doing.

That was the thing nobody had prepared her for — not Emrys, not the archive, none of it. She had expected the disbanded Choir to scatter like cockroaches, or worse, to reorganize; she had lain awake imagining grey robes regrouping in some mountain fastness, humming fragments of the counter-song, waiting. Instead they wandered. They wandered like pilgrims who'd lost the destination but kept the walking. And the terrible, inconvenient truth of them was that most of them had been *children* when the Choir took them. Orphans, Sable had said. Recruits with nothing left to steal. The Choir hadn't built its order out of monsters. It had built it out of emptiness, and filled the emptiness with duty, and called the result devotion.

Breaking a cycle, Maren was learning, mostly consisted of feeding people soup and refusing to look away.

* * *

Not everyone in Lowmark agreed with her approach.

The miller's son, Tam — who had been a boy of nine when a grey-robed singer had passed through and smoothed away his grandmother's memory of her own sister — spat at Aldous's feet in the market square and had to be physically restrained by two farmers who mostly wanted to get back to their haggling.

"He's one of *them*," Tam shouted. "Look at the robe! You think they forgot what they did? You think forgetting is a *punishment*? It's an escape!"

"No," Maren said, stepping between them before Aldous could answer, because she'd watched him try to answer twice already and each time his mouth opened and closed like a man reading a letter that kept blanking. "Forgetting isn't his punishment. Forgetting was his *wages*. He was paid in nothing for forty years of work. And now he's got nothing, and you've got your anger, and only one of those can build anything."

"Easy for you to say—"

"It's not," Maren said, and something in her voice made him stop. "My father died believing his life's work was madness. Every memory he collected, every 'wrong' story he wrote down — the Choir erased the people who told them to him. One by one. He kept a list of names in the back of his atlas. Forty-one names. People he loved who stopped knowing him." She held Tam's gaze. "I have every reason to hate them. I'm choosing not to. Not because they deserve it. Because I do."

Tam stared at her for a long moment. Then he spat again — nearer Aldous's boots than before, which in the arithmetic of Lowmark constituted progress — and stalked off to argue about flour prices, which was the traditional Lowmark method of processing emotion.

Aldous exhaled. "Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Fix the fence on the north pasture. Old Wenna will forgive you anything if you fix that fence; it's been leaning since the war she doesn't remember either."

* * *

Word traveled, the way word does. By early spring there were eleven of them at Lowmark — grey robes washed and mended and, in two cases, cut down and resewn into perfectly respectable coats, because Wenna had opinions about waste. They worked. That was the other thing nobody had predicted: they worked like people making up for lost time, because they were. They knew how to read, all of them, and how to keep accounts, and how to move quietly through mountains, and several other skills that Maren chose not to examine too closely. What they didn't know was who they'd been, and so they became, before her eyes, whoever the valley needed.

One of them — a broad, silent woman they called Brann because she answered to it — turned out to have hands that remembered surgery no one had taught her. She set the cooper's broken arm in March and delivered the shepherd's twins in April, and by May the shepherd's wife was leaving her fresh cheese on the doorstep and calling her "our Brann" in a tone that permitted no argument.

"They gave us away," Brann said once, sitting beside Maren on the mill wall, watching the wheel turn. "Everything. And now these people are giving us new things. Names. Work. A place at the table." She shook her head. "I don't understand it. We were the ones who came in the night."

"My father had a theory about that," Maren said. "He said memory isn't a possession. It's a debt. Everything anyone remembers about you is something you owe them back." She shrugged. "Maybe mercy is just the interest."

Brann looked at her sideways. "Was he always like this? Your father?"

"Oh, worse. He once explained the hydrology of the valley to me using bread rolls at dinner. I was twenty-three." Maren smiled, and found, to her surprise, that it didn't hurt the way it used to. "I miss him like a limb. But I got his habit of explaining things, so he's not entirely gone."

"No," Brann agreed gravely. "You're very gone-adjacent."

Maren laughed until she had to hold onto the mill wall, and Brann looked pleased with herself in the way of a woman discovering humor late in life and finding it excellent value.

* * *

Sable came in June.

She saw him from the map room window — walking up the cart road with a pack on his shoulder and no grey robe anywhere, just a plain brown traveler's coat, and beside him, arguing about the grade of the road with the passion of a professional, Emrys Thackeray.

Maren was down the stairs and out the door before her boots were properly on.

"You're supposed to be in Thornfell," she said, hugging Emrys hard enough to elicit a wheeze. "Rebuilding your workshop. Being mysterious. Carving things ominously."

"I was," Emrys said. "Then the dead started asking me to run errands, and it turns out errands have destinations, and one of the destinations was here." She pulled back and studied Maren's face with those bone-carver's eyes that missed nothing. "You look better. Rested. Fed."

"I'm running a halfway house for former memory-eaters. It's very calming."

"So I hear." Emrys glanced past her at the village — at Aldous splitting wood for Wenna, at Brann scolding the cooper's children away from the wheel, at the grey coat hanging on Wenna's line like a flag of a country that no longer existed and was, somehow, better off. "Eleven of them?"

"Twelve now. One arrived last week. He cries a lot, but he's learning carpentry, so we're calling it even."

"And they stay? Nobody runs them off?"

"Somebody tried." Maren thought of Tam, who now played cards with Aldous every market day and cheated him cheerfully and had never once mentioned the spitting. "Mostly they stay. Turns out it's hard to hate a man who fixed your fence."

Emrys smiled — that small, weathered smile that Maren had learned meant the smile was real precisely because it was small. "That's the whole secret, isn't it. Of everything. It's just fences."

Behind them, Sable had stopped at the gate, and Maren saw him take in the village — the ordinary, sunlit, unforgiven-and-anyway-forgiven village — with an expression she couldn't read. He'd grown up here once, or somewhere like it, before the Choir. Before Vellichor was taken from him twice. He'd paid the gate nearly everything to keep one memory of Emrys, and the rest of him was still a swept room, waiting for furniture.

"Well?" Maren asked him. "Is it strange? Coming back?"

"Yes," Sable said. Then, after a moment: "But it's a good strange. Like hearing a song you don't know and finding you already know the words."

Emrys looked at him sharply. "Where did you hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"*Never mind.*"

* * *

They stayed three days. On the second evening, the whole odd household ended up in the kitchen — Maren, Emrys, Sable, Aldous, Brann, and the crying carpenter, whose name had come back that afternoon (Pietr) and who was celebrating by not crying, mostly — eating a stew that Brann had made and that everyone politely did not compare to the hypothesis-soup of the previous winter.

It was Pietr who asked the question, in the careful way of a man testing ice.

"The others," he said. "The ones still wandering. There are hundreds of us, aren't there? Scattered across the mountains. Some of them won't find valleys like this. Some of them will find villages that remember what the robes did." He turned his bowl slowly. "What happens to them?"

Nobody answered immediately. The fire popped. Outside, dusk was coming down blue and soft over Lowmark, and somewhere Wenna was yelling at a goat, and the world was continuing in its stubborn, ordinary way.

"There's a thing I've been thinking about," Maren said at last. She got up and fetched her father's atlas — the great battered one, the life's work, the book of wrong memories — and laid it open on the table. "My father mapped roads that officially never existed. Roads only the dead remembered. And lately—" she glanced at Emrys, who nodded slightly, "—lately the dead have been *singing*. Fragments of a song about a country that used to be here. Travelers come through humming bits of it without knowing what they're humming. And I keep thinking: somebody should map that. Somebody should go out and find the scattered ones, and the singing places, and write it all down before it drifts."

"You want to make an atlas of a song," Sable said.

"I want to make an atlas of *everything my father couldn't put in his maps*," Maren said. "The roads, the singers, the grey robes looking for somewhere to put down their swept-out rooms. All of it." She looked around the table. "And I'm not doing it alone. That part's non-negotiable. I've done alone. Alone is where obsession lives."

Emrys was already smiling. "You're asking."

"I'm asking."

"I carve bones, Maren. I hear dead people complain about their funerals. I am not a natural traveling companion."

"You walked across an entire erased country and sang its name into a door."

"After which I retired!"

"Retired," Sable repeated mildly, "is a strong word for someone the dead keep sending on errands."

Emrys glared at him. He gazed back with the serene expression of a man who had paid a nation's worth of memories for the privilege of loving her and intended to enjoy every remaining scrap of himself. Maren watched them and felt the ache and the warmth of it together, the way you feel sun through a window in winter.

Pietr cleared his throat. "I can't come," he said. "I've got a roof to finish for the schoolhouse. But—" he hesitated, then straightened, "—when you pass through the eastern passes, there's a hospice at Coldbrook. Run by a woman named Ilsette. She takes in wanderers. She'll feed anyone who works. Tell her Pietr sent you, and mind the third stair, it's rotten."

"See?" Maren said, spreading her hands. "The network builds itself. One fence at a time."

Brann snorted. Aldous laughed. Outside, the last light went gold and then grey over the valley, and far off in the mountains — so faint it might have been wind, might have been water, might have been nothing at all — someone was humming.

Emrys tilted her head, listening. Her smile faded into something quieter and deeper.

"What is it?" Maren asked.

"A new verse," Emrys said softly. "There's a new verse in the song. It wasn't there last month." She looked around the table, at the grey coats on the line and the stew going cold and the mapless mapmaker's daughter with ink already staining her fingers. "Vellichor's still writing itself. And I think—" she paused, and Maren saw her choose the words with a carver's care, "—I think it wants to tell us where to go next."

Maren was already reaching for a fresh page.
