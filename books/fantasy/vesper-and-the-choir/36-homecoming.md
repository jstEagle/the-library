## 36. Homecoming

The road down into Thornfell had not changed, which was the first cruelty of it.

Emrys had expected ruin. She had half hoped for it, if she was honest—some visible scar to match the ones she carried, a burned stretch of hillside or a collapsed bridge that would say *the world broke while you were gone and here is the proof*. Instead the pass opened the way it always had, granite shoulders leaning together, the pines combed sideways by wind, and below it all the town sat in its bowl of grey stone like a dog asleep by a fire, utterly indifferent to what she had done at the edge of the world.

She stopped where the road bent above the rooftops and let herself look.

Smoke from chimneys. The tanner's yard with its racks of hides. The crooked bell tower of the Wayhouse chapel, still leaning east as though listening for something. Somewhere down there a widow named Hetta Brannock was probably arguing with her goat, and a dead man named Aldous Brannock was probably complaining about his funeral, and neither of them knew her name.

Neither of them *could* know her name. That was the shape of the bargain she had sung into the Vesper Gate: Vellichor lives, but only as song; and the woman who sang it is remembered by no one. She had known the cost going in. She had said the words with her whole chest, with Sable's hand in hers, and she would say them again.

Knowing the cost did not stop the cost from hurting.

"You're stalling," said Aldous.

He drifted up out of the charm at her throat—the mourning charm she had carved for his widow eleven years ago, which he had never entirely vacated, on the grounds that his funeral had been an insult and he intended to supervise all future arrangements personally. He was translucent in the morning light, faintly annoyed, as always.

"I'm taking in the view," Emrys said.

"You're standing on a road like a lost mule. I've seen mules do it. They stand there deciding whether the load is worth the bridge." He floated around her to peer down at the town. "Huh. They repainted the Wayhouse."

"They repaint the Wayhouse every few years. It's the damp."

"Still an improvement. That green was a crime against architecture." He settled beside her shoulder like a cold draft wearing a personality. "Nervous?"

"No."

"Liar. You've got your carving face on. The one you make when you've cut too deep and you're pretending you meant to."

Emrys touched her own cheek, caught herself doing it, and put her hand down. Eleven years of being read by a dead man. It was, she reflected, exactly the sort of intimacy she'd spent her whole life avoiding, and now she couldn't imagine walking into a town without him muttering commentary over her shoulder.

"It's strange," she admitted. "I'm coming home to people who have never met me. My stall is empty. My workshop—" She stopped. She had burned it herself, four years ago, on the night she left. Four years? Five? Time past the gate moved strangely, like water through fingers. "My workshop is ash. And whoever owns that ground now doesn't know why there's a foundation shaped like my life."

"So build it again," Aldous said, with the brutal simplicity of the dead. "You've rebuilt harder things. You rebuilt a country. Out of a *song*, no less. I heard it. We all heard it. Frankly it could use another verse, but the sentiment was sound."

* * *

Thornfell received her the way it received every stranger: with suspicion, then commerce, then grudging tolerance, in that order.

The first person to speak to her was a boy selling roasted chestnuts outside the market gate, who looked her up and down with the frank appraisal of youth and decided she was nobody important. "Chestnut?" he offered. "Or are you one of them pilgrims? We get pilgrims now. Weird ones. They hum."

"Hum," Emrys repeated.

"All the time. Walking through humming bits of songs nobody knows. Drives the chapel choir mad. Old Cantor Ferrow says they're singing heresy, but he says everything's heresy except the collection plate." The boy shrugged. "Chestnut?"

She bought six and ate them walking, and the warmth of them in her hands steadied something. So. The song had reached Thornfell. Travelers hummed fragments of Vellichor without knowing what they hummed, the way people whistled tunes their grandmothers had sung them. A country that lived as music, passed mouth to mouth, unowned and unerased. Maren would be insufferably pleased. Maren was always insufferably pleased; it was one of her best qualities.

The market square itself was exactly as she'd left it, which was to say arranged in a pattern that made sense to absolutely no one who hadn't grown up navigating it. She found her old pitch by instinct—a corner near the well where the morning sun hit the stone and vendors' voices didn't carry, ideal for private conversations with the bereaved—and stood there feeling like a ghost haunting her own absence.

A woman was selling soap there now. Good soap, by the smell. Lavender and something sharper underneath.

"That's a fine spot," Emrys said, because she couldn't help herself. "You'll want to watch the drainage from the well in spring. Water pools right about there." She pointed. "Ruins your stock by mid-April."

The woman frowned. "How'd you know that?"

"Lucky guess."

It wasn't a guess. She had ruined her own stock by mid-April three years running before she'd figured out the trick of raised pallets, and apparently some knowledge lived deeper than memory, in the feet, in the hands, in the body that had walked these stones for twenty years before anyone sang anything into any gates.

That was the thing she was learning, these first weeks back: the erasure took names and faces and histories, but it had not taken her craft. Her fingers still knew how a piece of bone wanted to be carved before her mind did. Her ears still caught the dead murmuring under the town's noise like a second current beneath a river. Whatever the gate had made of her, it had not made her less of a bone-carver.

If anything—

Well. If anything, the dead were louder now. Or perhaps not louder. Closer. As though the song had thinned whatever veil separated the living from the dead, or as though the dead, having finally crossed over in peace at the Vesper Gate, had told their friends downstream that the ferry ran both ways now, more or less, if you knew the carver.

* * *

She found lodging at the Drowned Goose, which had been called the Drowned Goose when she left and would presumably be called the Drowned Goose when the mountains themselves wore down to gravel. The innkeeper was a broad, cheerful man named Petch whom she had never seen before in her life, and who nonetheless kept glancing at her like a man trying to remember where he'd set down his pipe.

"Passing through?" he asked, sliding her a bowl of barley stew.

"Staying," she said. "I'm a carver. Bone and horn. Looking to set up shop."

"Carver!" His face brightened. "We could use one. Old Master Corwin died—oh, must be five years back—and nobody's taken up the trade. There's folk been waiting on charms ever since. Mourning pieces, mostly." He lowered his voice, embarrassed by his own superstition and committed to it anyway. "Between us, the ones Corwin carved, they worked. Don't ask me how. Widow Tamsin swears hers whispers to her. Thought she was cracked till my own mother's charm—I swear on the Goose's good name, it *hummed* at me once. Right at the anniversary. Like it knew."

Emrys kept her spoon moving and her face politely blank. Inside her coat, Aldous made a sound suspiciously like suppressed laughter.

"Folk say," Petch went on, warming, "that since the pilgrims started coming through, the charms work better. Stronger. Like something woke up in the hills." He shook his head at himself. "Listen to me. You'd think I'd had the Goose's own ale. But it's true, isn't it? Something's different. Can't say what. Like a word stuck on the tip of the tongue, the whole valley's got it."

"I wouldn't know," Emrys said.

"No," Petch agreed pleasantly, and refilled her bowl without being asked, because innkeepers know a returning regular when they see one even if they don't know why. "More stew, stranger. First one's on the house. Call it a welcome."

* * *

The foundations were still there.

Of course they were. Ash weathers, stone endures; she had built the original footings herself, deep and dry-set, the work of a woman who intended to stay invisible forever and had unknowingly built to last. Weeds had colonized the floor. Someone had piled firewood against the north wall's remains at some point and then abandoned it. A goat regarded her from atop the old chimney stump with the flat insolence of its kind.

"Well," said Aldous, drifting through the doorway that wasn't there anymore. "Cozy."

"Shut up."

"There's a goat in your parlor."

"She can stay. Goats keep the weeds down." Emrys walked the perimeter slowly, laying her palm on the stones. Here was where the workbench had stood, by the window facing east for the morning light. Here was the hearth, whose chimney the goat had claimed. Here was the corner where she had hidden the map inside a hollowed femur while grey-robed figures walked the market asking questions no one remembered being asked. She knelt and pressed her hand flat to that particular stone and waited, half expecting the fear to come back.

What came instead was quieter and stranger: grief, yes, but braided with something that felt almost like gratitude. This stone had held. This place had waited. Towns forget people constantly—it was practically their trade—but places remember differently. The mountain had watched her leave and had kept her footprint in the shape of its stones.

She stood, dusted her knees, and went to find a mason.

* * *

Rebuilding took the summer, and it took the town.

That was the unexpected part. She had planned to hire labor quietly, pay fairly, keep her head down—the old doctrine of invisibility, worn soft as an old glove. But invisibility turned out to be a skill that atrophied, or perhaps one she simply no longer fit inside. When the mason's apprentice dropped a lintel and Emrys caught it one-handed, pivoting with a carver's economy that saved both the stone and the boy's foot, the story went around the market by supper. When she re-cut the widow Tamsin's cracked charm—for free, because the crack was letting the poor woman's husband leak out like heat from a bad window—and the widow burst into tears of relief because suddenly the whispering sounded *kind* again instead of frightened, the story went around twice.

By midsummer, people brought her things without being asked. A wheel of cheese appeared on her doorstep with no note. The chestnut boy—his name was Pip, and he had appointed himself her unofficial herald—announced her opening day to half the county. Petch sent over ale "for the workers," which was generous of him given that the workers were two masons, one apprentice, and a woman carving lintel ornaments nobody had commissioned because the doorframe looked lonely.

"You realize," Aldous observed one evening, as they sat on the new doorstep watching the light go amber over the roofs, "you've become popular. You, the woman whose entire life philosophy was 'be furniture.' You're practically a civic monument."

"It's temporary," Emrys said. "They'll get used to me and go back to ignoring me."

"Mm. And how does that prospect sit? Be honest. You're terrible at honest, but practice makes less terrible."

She considered it. The truth was that being unknown had changed flavor. Before, anonymity had been armor—every forgotten face a door closed against the Choir. Now the Choir was broken, its singers scattered awake with empty hands, and anonymity was just... a room she happened to live in. She could walk the market unbowed and unburdened. Nobody's eyes slid off her because of an erasure hymn; they slid off her because she was a stranger, and strangers became neighbors, given time and good work and the occasional free charm.

"I think," she said slowly, "that I spent so long hiding that I forgot hiding isn't the same as living. I'm not hiding anymore. I'm just... new here. Same as any newcomer." She turned the thought over, testing it. "It's not nothing, being new. New means you get to choose what people learn about you. I never had that. Everyone in this town knew me as Thackeray the bone-carver, keeps to herself, don't ask about her mother. Now I can be—" She gestured vaguely at the fresh mortar, the straight walls, the evening. "Whoever I decide."

"A woman who talks to ghosts," Aldous supplied helpfully.

"That part's non-negotiable, yes."

"Pity. It's my favorite part."

* * *

The dead came to her the way family comes to a kitchen: without knocking, in ones and twos, assuming their welcome.

She noticed it properly on the first night in the finished workshop. She had hung her tools, oiled the bench, lit one lamp, and sat down simply to be in the room, when she realized the silence wasn't silent. It was populated. Not the desperate, clinging dead she had known all her life—the ones who gripped their relics like drowning men—but a loose, easy congregation, drifting through the walls on whatever business the dead conduct, pausing to look in at her window the way neighbors pause at a lit doorway.

An old shepherd nodded to her from the corner, the way one nods to a familiar tradeswoman. Two children chased each other through the rafters, laughing in the thin way children laugh after. A woman in the dress of forty years ago leaned on the doorframe and said, "You're the one who sang them across. My sister went with the rest. She wrote to tell me." Dead don't write; she meant it in the way the dead mean such things, dream to dream, stone to stone. "Thank you."

Emrys found, to her considerable surprise, that she could not speak. She managed a nod. The woman smiled and dissolved, and Emrys sat alone with her lamp and her full throat and understood that something had reversed itself in her life's ledger. All her years in Thornfell, the living had tolerated her and the dead had needed her. Now the living were learning her name and the dead treated her like kin come home.

Both, she supposed, were true. She had crossed the country of death and back. Of course its citizens knew her. Of course they pulled up chairs.

"You're crying," Aldous said, appearing with terrible timing.

"I am not. It's lamp smoke."

"We both know the lamp's clean."

"We also both know you were a terrible husband and Hetta's better off, so perhaps we should each keep our observations to ourselves."

He laughed—that bark of his, delighted despite himself—and settled onto the bench beside her, cold as a cellar and comfortable as an old coat. Outside, the town murmured toward sleep. Somewhere a mother hummed a fragment of a melody no one had taught her, four notes rising like a stairway, and Emrys closed her eyes and let the song settle over the workshop like snowfall, gentle and unhurried, filling in the shape of everything she'd lost with everything she'd made.

* * *

Autumn came early to the high passes, as it always did, and with it came travelers.

Mostly they were what Petch had promised: pilgrims, odd and humming, drawn along roads they couldn't explain wanting to walk. Emrys sold them charms and let them hum. But on a raw grey afternoon with the first sleet ticking against the new windows, the door opened on a figure that stopped her heart for one full beat before her mind caught up with her fear.

Not a grey robe. Just a traveler in a plain brown cloak, travel-worn, snow in his dark hair, standing in her doorway with the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech for four hundred miles and immediately forgotten all of it.

"Sable," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because her hands had gone white on the carving knife.

"You remember me." He said it like a man confirming a miracle he'd been afraid to hope for. Then, wincing: "Of course you remember me. You remember everyone. That's rather the point of you."

"The gate," she said carefully. "The gate takes payment. For what it gives." She had wondered, all these months, in the quiet hours, what he had paid. She had watched him step through the horn door behind her and emerge unchanged—or seeming unchanged—and she had been too afraid to ask, because prices at that door were never small.

Sable stepped inside, dripping, and looked around the workshop—at the tools, the lamp, the lintel ornaments, the small domestic empire she had rebuilt from ash—and something in his face eased, the way a knot eases.

"I paid," he said, "and I'd pay it again. But it seems I've forgotten what I paid with, and there's a hole in me shaped like a childhood, and I've walked four hundred miles hoping you'd know why I can't stop smiling whenever someone hums." He pulled something from inside his cloak and set it on her bench between them: a small bone whistle, worn smooth, older than either of them.

Emrys looked at it, and then at him, and felt the beginning of an answer move through her like weather.
