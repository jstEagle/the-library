## 37. One Memory Kept

The trouble with remembering someone no one else could remember, Sable discovered in the weeks after the gate, was that you became her sole witness.

"You're doing it again," Emrys said.

They were sitting on the rebuilt porch of her workshop in Thornfell—rebuilt by his hands, mostly, because she had a gift for directing and an allergy to holding anything level—and she was carving a small bone bird with the particular frown she wore when she was listening to two conversations at once. The living one and the other one.

"Doing what again?"

"Watching me like I'm going to evaporate."

"I'm watching you like you might drop the knife," he said. "You're carving toward your thumb."

"My thumb and I have an arrangement." But she turned the knife around, and he felt something in his chest loosen that he refused to examine too closely, because examining things too closely had been the whole architecture of his old life, and he was retired from it.

He was retired from a great many things. From grey robes. From singing in unison. From the particular silence of a man who has been emptied so thoroughly he mistook the emptiness for peace. What remained was this: a porch, a woman, a valley full of people who looked at him with polite blankness when he mentioned her name, and one memory, held alone, like a coal carried in cupped hands across a long winter.

He had not told her yet what it cost.

* * *

It came out the way most true things come out between them: sideways, over supper, provoked by Maren.

Maren had taken the room above the cooper's shop and filled it with paper until the floorboards groaned. She arrived at Emrys's table now with ink-stained fingers and the expression of a woman who had just won an argument with geography itself.

"The eastern passes," she announced, spreading a map across the soup bowls without apology. "I've redrawn them from my father's notes. And there's a discrepancy." She tapped a spot where two lines crossed. "Sable, you walked us through here. You knew the fork before I did. You said—" her brow furrowed, retrieving— "you said the left-hand path smelled like cedar smoke and someone's baking. You said it like you'd *been* there."

Emrys went still in the way she did when a ghost spoke to her. Sable put down his spoon.

"It's gone," he said quietly.

"What's gone?" Emrys asked.

"The smell. The memory of it." He turned his hand over on the table, palm up, as if the answer might be written there. "When we fled the sanctum, half-restored, I told you what I remembered. The house with blue shutters. My mother's voice counting in the old tongue. The festival where they floated candles down the Vell. All of it—the childhood the Choir took from me, given back." He met her eyes. "I lied about how much of it came back."

The silence stretched. Maren, wisely, began rolling up her map.

"How much?" Emrys asked.

"All of it. For about an hour." He smiled, and it hurt, and he let her see that it hurt, because lying to her even kindly had stopped being possible somewhere on the empty road. "Then the Cantor's song caught up to us, and everyone forgot you, and I understood that I wouldn't. That I *couldn't*. So at the gate, when it asked what I would give—I gave the childhood back. All of it. Every candle on the river. In exchange for keeping one thing." He reached across the table and touched her wrist, light as a man touching a relic he was afraid to crack. "You."

Emrys didn't cry. He'd learned that about her; grief moved through her like weather through a mountain pass, visible and enormous and ultimately quiet. She sat with it for a moment, jaw tight, and then said, "You gave up your homeland twice. Once to the Choir, once to me."

"Once to the Choir against my will," he corrected. "Once to me, by mine. There's a difference. It's the whole difference."

"That's a terrible bargain."

"It's the best trade I've ever made, and I once traded a stolen horse for a boat that sank."

That startled a laugh out of her—short, wet, real—and Maren muttered something about needing to check her ink pots and escaped through the door with the map under her arm, leaving them alone with the soup and the truth.

"You could have kept both," Emrys said softly. "The gate would have—"

"The gate would have taken something else. Gates always take. I've read enough of Maren's father's notes to know the arithmetic never favors the traveler." He shrugged. "And besides. I've been a man with nothing but duty. I've been a man with everything and no one to hold it with. This—" he gestured at the small warm room, the widow's charm hanging by the door that still glowed faintly when the dead passed by outside, the woman across the table who was looking at him like he was the impossible one— "this is more than either."

"You don't even know what you lost," she said. "You can't miss it properly."

"No," he agreed. "But I can see what it bought. That's better than missing. Missing is just grief wearing a nicer coat."

She laughed again, and then she got up, came around the table, and sat in his lap like it was the most ordinary thing in the world, which after everything, perhaps it was. She smelled of bone dust and woodsmoke and the rosemary she stubbornly grew despite the altitude.

"When you describe it," she said into his shoulder, "the childhood. When you described it on the road. Was any of it real?"

"All of it. That's what made it worth paying."

"And now?"

"Now it lives in the song." He tilted his head toward the window, toward the mountains, where on certain evenings—if you were quiet, if you didn't try—you could hear it. Travelers hummed it in the passes without knowing why. Children skipped rope to its rhythm in villages that had never heard of Vellichor. The country was gone and everywhere, which was, he suspected, a better fate than being a nation with borders, since nations got conquered and songs only got forgotten, and even forgetting, these days, seemed to be losing ground. "Somewhere in it there's a boy floating a candle down a river. He doesn't need me to remember him. He needs someone to sing."

* * *

The dead, at least, approved of him now.

This was new. In his Choir years, the dead had regarded him the way sheep regard wolves—with a patient, collective dread. Now, when he walked through Thornfell's churchyard on errands, they murmured to him. Not words, exactly. More like the feeling of words, the way warmth comes off a stone after sunset. Old Marta, buried forty years beneath the yew, liked him particularly; Emrys claimed it was because he'd fixed Marta's leaning headstone without being asked, and Sable suspected the dead simply appreciated a man who listened, having spent decades surrounded by the living who didn't.

"They say you brood," Emrys told him one evening, appearing beside him at the churchyard wall with two cups of cider.

"I don't brood. I think in a standing position."

"For an hour."

"It was a complicated hour."

She handed him a cup and leaned against the wall beside him, shoulder to shoulder. Below them the town went about its dusk: lamps lit, dogs argued with, the smith's hammer winding down. Nobody here knew him. A few months ago that would have been the old ache, the hollow place where a self should be. Now it felt almost like freedom—he was nobody's singer, nobody's instrument. He was a man with a hammer and a garden plot and one perfect memory, and the memory was walking beside him drinking cider.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Honestly. Not the answer you think I want."

He considered it honestly. He turned the question over the way she turned bones, feeling for the grain.

"Sometimes I reach for it," he said. "The childhood. Like reaching for a stair that isn't there. Especially at night, especially in autumn, when the light does that thing—" he waved at the amber slanting through the yew— "and some part of me says *you know this light, you've known this light*, and then nothing comes." He took a sip of cider. "And every single time, right behind the nothing, there's this." He looked at her. "So no. I'd pay it again. Twice. The gate can invoice me."

"You're insufferable when you're certain."

"I'm rarely certain. About you I'm certain. It's the one luxury I allow myself."

She bumped her shoulder against his. Above the yew, the first stars were coming out, and faintly—so faintly it might have been wind, but they both knew better—the song moved through the branches, carrying a river and candles and a boy who no longer needed remembering, because he had become music instead.

* * *

Winter came early that year, as it always did to Thornfell, and with it came Maren's cartographers' ink and a growing pile of letters.

They arrived by courier, which was itself remarkable—couriers had nearly died out during the erasure years, when half the roads led to places that officially weren't there. Now they came stumbling down from the passes weekly, frostbitten and excited, carrying letters addressed in a dozen hands: scholars, pilgrims, the merely curious, all asking after the sung borders. Asking after *her*, though none of them could have said who "her" was. They wrote to the bone-carver of Thornfell because travelers reported that the song sounded clearest near her workshop, and because a compass sold in a valley village pointed, persistently and uselessly, toward her door.

"She'll want to publish the lot of them," Sable said, sorting the mail on the kitchen table while Emrys carved. "Maren. She's already talking about an atlas. An *atlas of the sung country*. She asked me yesterday whether the eastern fork smelled like cedar, for verification purposes, and I had to tell her I was the wrong man to ask, and she looked at me like I'd broken her heart all over again."

"You could tell her what you told me."

"I could." He set down a letter, frowned at it. "But there's a difference between giving a memory away and spending it. Hers is for the atlas. Mine was for you. I'd rather keep the accounting clean."

Emrys looked up from her carving. The bird she was working on—a wren, this time, commissioned by the schoolteacher for reasons involving a deceased cat and a guilty conscience—caught the lamplight along its wing.

"You know what the strange part is?" she said. "Everyone forgets me. The whole valley. The innkeeper smiles at me like I'm a stranger every morning and offers me the same room at the same price, and I pay it, and we do the same dance. And none of it touches me anymore. Because there's one person in the world who walks in and says, 'You're doing it again,' and means the knife, and means the watching, and means all of it." She set down the wren. "Do you understand what you are, Sable? You're not the man who remembers me. You're the proof I happened."

He crossed the room. Outside, snow was starting, fat and unhurried, the kind of snow that meant business later but was content tonight merely to decorate. He took her face in his hands, careful of the bone dust, careful of everything, the way he had learned to be careful of the things that mattered.

"Then I'll keep saying it," he said. "'You're doing it again.' Every day. Until we're old and it becomes our whole conversation, and the neighbors—who won't know either of us—wonder why the two odd carvers laugh so much."

"That's a terrible future."

"It's the best trade I've ever been offered."

She kissed him, and the wren on the table, half-finished, seemed to listen, and somewhere far off in the white dark the song went on singing a country that no longer needed borders because it had a melody instead—and in the morning, Maren would arrive with the first proofs of her atlas and a letter bearing a seal neither of them recognized, wax pressed with the image of a door.
