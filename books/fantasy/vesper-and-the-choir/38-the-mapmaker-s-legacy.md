## 38. The Mapmaker's Legacy

The printing press in Lowmark had a stutter.

Every fourth impression it hesitated, gears grinding like an old man clearing his throat, and then it spat the page out with a shudder that shook ink from the rollers and spattered Maren's sleeve. She had been told by three different printers that this was simply how presses were. She had begun to suspect they were all lying to her, or perhaps that she was cursed to be surrounded by machinery with personality.

"Again," she said, and fed the plate through one more time.

The press groaned, hesitated on cue, and delivered.

Maren held the page up to the window light of the print shop and read her father's name for the hundredth time that morning: *Surveys of the Thornfell Approaches, Being the Collected Maps of Aldous Vane, Cartographer.* Below it, in smaller type: *with annotations by his daughter.*

Her father had died believing his life's work was worthless. That was the part that still caught in her throat, two years on. He had spent forty years drawing maps of roads that weren't there—roads that dissolved under surveyors' boots, passes that appeared on no chart but his, a border that ran through the mountains like a scar through skin, marking nothing at all. The Guild of Cartographers had struck him from their rolls. His patrons had withdrawn. Her mother had left, eventually, taking Maren's brothers to a city where maps described things that existed.

And now here was his work, set in type, bound in good calfskin, selling briskly enough that the printer had already asked—twice, with increasing eagerness—whether there might be a second volume.

"There will be," Maren told him now, wiping ink from her fingers. "When I've finished the annotations."

"You said that last month."

"I said it last month because it was true last month." She gathered the fresh sheets into a stack, squaring their edges with the fussiness her father would have recognized and despaired of. "It's also true today. It's simply true more slowly than either of us would like."

The printer, a round man named Hobb whose forearms were permanently grey to the elbow, laughed and went back to his typesetting. Maren carried the stack upstairs to the room she rented above the shop, which smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the mountain herbs she still rubbed into her boots out of habit she couldn't explain and didn't try to.

She sat down at the desk by the window and looked at what she'd been avoiding looking at for a week: the final annotation, the one for the map her father had drawn last, in the year before he died. It showed a valley with no name, a road with no destination, and at its center—a small, careful circle, drawn in ink so old it had gone brown—the words *Vesper Gate?* followed by a question mark her father had pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through.

He had never known if the gate was real. He had spent his whole life not knowing, and he had drawn the question mark anyway, because a cartographer's job, he used to say, was not to know things. It was to write down where the not-knowing began.

*Here,* his maps said, in forty years of careful hand. *Here is where I stopped being able to see. Go and look for yourselves.*

They were looking. That was the thing Maren hadn't expected, the thing that still surprised her every time she came down to the shop's front room and found the display copies thinned out, the thing Hobb reported with the delight of a man watching money grow: people were buying the maps. Not scholars, or not only scholars. Pilgrims. Wanderers. Young couples who wanted to walk a road that wasn't on any other chart. Old soldiers who claimed—and this was the strange part, the part Maren wrote down in her journal every night—that when they stood on certain ridgelines at certain hours, they could hear something. Singing. Faint. In a language none of them spoke.

Humming it, sometimes, without knowing what they hummed.

* * *

The first traveler to come back and tell her about it arrived in autumn, a lean woman with windburnt cheeks and a walking staff worn smooth at the grip. She found Maren in the shop's front room, re-inking the display copy for the dozenth time, and stood in the doorway turning her hat in her hands until Maren looked up.

"You're the mapmaker's daughter."

"I'm the annotator's daughter," Maren said. "My father was the mapmaker. What can I do for you?"

"You drew the notes in the margins. About the singing." The woman came forward and laid her staff against the counter with the care of someone laying down a weapon she hoped not to need again. "I walked the high road past the Tollhouse ruins. Third week of Highsummer. And I—" She stopped, started again. "I don't know how to say it properly."

"Try badly," Maren suggested. "Badly usually gets closer."

That earned a wet, startled laugh. The woman wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "I heard it. The song. Just a few bars, over and over, coming up out of the valley like smoke. And I found myself humming along before I knew I was doing it. A tune I'd never learned. Words I didn't know." She fumbled in her pack and produced a scrap of paper, soft with handling. "I wrote down what I could remember of the sound. Not the words—I couldn't catch the words—but the shape of it. My daughter does music. She read it and went white as milk and said it was in a mode nobody uses anymore. Said it was *old*, older than anything in her books."

Maren took the paper. The notation was clumsy, the work of an amateur, but the shape of it—the rise and fall, the way the line bent back on itself like a path doubling through a pass—made something tighten behind her ribs.

She knew that shape. She had heard Emrys sing it once, standing at the gate with the dawn coming up behind her, her voice cracking and steady at once, giving a country away to the air.

"It's real, then," the woman said, watching her face. "I half thought I'd dreamed it. Half hoped I had, truthfully. It was—" She searched. "It was like being remembered by something enormous. Like walking past a house where someone inside knows your name."

"That's very close to how it feels," Maren said quietly. "Would you write it down? All of it? Where you walked, what hour, what the weather was?"

The woman blinked. "You want my account?"

"I want everyone's account. That's rather the point of the book." Maren was already pulling fresh paper toward her. "My father mapped the borders of a country that had been erased. He did it alone, and everyone told him he was mad, and he died with a question mark on his finest map. I intend to finish the job differently. There are more of us now. We'll map it in chorus."

* * *

Winter brought the letters.

Hobb's wife took to sorting them, since they arrived faster than Maren could answer, bundled in twine and smelling of a dozen different post towns. A shepherd from the eastern slopes who swore his flock grew restless at dusk in the high valley, turning toward the border line as if called to supper. A choir mistress from a cathedral town three weeks south who had caught one of her choristers humming the fragment and traced it, appalled and fascinated, to a grandmother who "had never learned a note of music in her life." A boy of maybe twelve who wrote in enormous careful letters to ask whether it was true that you could stand on the Vesper ridge and hear your own name, and whether it cost anything, and whether his sister—who had died last spring—might be somewhere in the song.

Maren answered that last one herself, sitting up late with the lamp turned low.

*I don't know,* she wrote, because lying to children seemed a poor way to honor the dead. *But I can tell you what I believe, which is that the song keeps everything given to it freely, and gives it back changed but not diminished. Your sister is not in the song the way a stone is in a wall. She is in it the way a note is in a melody—which is to say, she is still sounding.*

She read it over four times, decided it was as true as she could make it, and sent it.

The strangest letters were the ones from former Choir members. They came without signatures, mostly, on cheap paper, written in hands that trembled or pressed too hard. *I woke in a field near Marrowdale with empty hands and no orders,* one began. *I have been walking toward the mountains ever since. I don't know why. I think I may have done something there once that I should be sorry for. If I meet anyone from that country, tell me—is there a way to be sorry properly? Is there a place to put it?*

Maren sat with that letter for a long evening. She thought about writing back *no*, about the tollhouse and the memories she had paid and could never buy again, about her mother's face gone from her mind like a coin dropped down a well. She had earned the right to a hard answer.

But she thought also of Sable, who had woken with empty hands once himself, long ago, and been handed duty instead of mercy, and look what that had built.

*Come to Lowmark in spring,* she finally wrote. *We are compiling accounts. Yours counts too. Especially yours. Grief needs a map as much as anything else does, and we have paper to spare.*

* * *

In the spring, Emrys came to visit.

She arrived unannounced, as she always did, appearing in the shop's doorway on a market morning with sawdust on her sleeves and a carved wooden box under her arm, and Hobb greeted her by name with the baffled cheer of a man who had known her for years and could not, if pressed, have said when she'd first walked in.

"How is she?" Maren asked, before hello.

"Stubborn. Talented. Convinced the new apprentice is going to ruin the workshop and secretly delighted by him." Emrys set the box on the counter. "She sends these. For the travelers. She says if they're going to walk the border humming, they might as well hum in tune."

Inside the box, nested in wool, lay bone whistles. A dozen of them, each one small enough to close in a fist, each carved from a different piece—sheep bone, deer antler, one of pale ivory-looking material Maren didn't ask about. When Maren lifted the first to her lips and blew, the fragment came out clear and sweet and instantly, horribly familiar: the shape of the song, the rise and fall, the bend-back-like-a-path. Her eyes stung before she'd finished the phrase.

"She's distributing them through me?" Maren managed.

"She says you're better at the part where you talk to strangers. Also she says—" Emrys's mouth twitched, the way it always did before she relayed one of her own messages secondhand, as though embarrassed to be caught caring. "She says the country isn't a place anymore, it's a tune, and tunes spread best mouth to mouth. Or whistle to whistle. She says your father would have understood: a map is just a song you can hold still."

Maren had to turn away and pretend great interest in the display shelf for a moment.

"He'd have hated it," she said at last, voice mostly level. "Then spent ten years mapping every place the whistles had traveled, and died happy."

"That's what she said too. Nearly word for word." Emrys leaned on the counter. "There's something else. The dead have started asking after you. The ones who crossed at the gate—they talk, on the far side, apparently, or near enough. Word travels even there. They call you the Mapmaker's Daughter like it's a title. Like it's an office." She tilted her head. "How does that sit?"

Maren considered it honestly, the way she tried to consider everything now, having spent too many years considering things dishonestly—or rather, obsessively, which was a kind of dishonesty. Obsession was love with the windows painted shut.

"It sits strangely," she said. "For a long time I wanted to restore Vellichor. Put it back. Walls and borders and a name on charts. I wanted it so badly I would have broken myself on the door of it." She touched the whistle, still warm from her breath. "This is better. This is what my father actually did, I see it now—he never restored anything. He just kept writing *here is where the not-knowing begins*, and left the door open for whoever came after. That's a legacy. Countries fall. Questions travel."

Emrys smiled, slow and real. "You should put that in the book."

"I intend to steal it shamelessly."

"Do. Everything true belongs to everyone; that's rather the point of the whole arrangement."

* * *

Summer, and the second volume went to press.

Maren had titled it before she'd written half of it, the way her father used to title things, claiming the name made the work behave: *The Sung Borders: An Account of Vellichor, Gathered from Those Who Hear It.* Inside were the shepherd's letter and the choir mistress's findings and the boy's enormous careful handwriting reproduced in facsimile, with his permission and his mother's. Inside were eleven separate transcriptions of the fragment, each slightly different, none wrong. Inside was a page Maren had argued with Hobb about for a week, a single sentence set alone in the middle of otherwise blank paper:

*A country that survives as a song cannot be conquered, only forgotten—and it can be relearned.*

Hobb had wanted it cut. Too grand, he said. Too much like a sermon. Maren had stood in the print shop with ink on her sleeves and her chin up and said, "My father was struck from the Guild rolls for telling the truth too carefully. I intend to err in the opposite direction," and Hobb had thrown up his hands and set the line, muttering about cartographers' daughters, every one of them born stubborn.

The press stuttered on the fourth impression. The page came out anyway.

On the day the second volume shipped, Maren walked up out of Lowmark alone, following her father's oldest map to the ridge above the nameless valley. Evening was coming on, gold going to violet along the peaks, and she sat down on the warm stone with the book in her lap and let the quiet gather.

Then, faintly, up out of the valley—smoke-thin, patient—it came. The fragment. The bend-back shape. And beneath it, woven through it, voices she could almost distinguish: a courier who had died at a door in a storm; a neighbor erased from his own wife's memory; a woman who had paid her mother's face to a tollhouse and sung anyway; thousands upon thousands, given freely, still sounding.

And threaded among them, new, unmistakable, a bar of melody she had never heard before but knew at once, the way you know your own name spoken in a crowd—

A child's voice. Clear, curious, asking a question in a language no one living spoke, and being answered.

Maren sat very still until the light failed, her heart hammering a rhythm she hadn't felt since the gate. Then she opened the book to the blank page at the back, licked her pencil, and wrote, in her father's careful style, in the margin of the margin:

*New verse heard this evening, Vesper ridge, sundown. Origin unknown. Possibly recent. Possibly a child.*

*Here is where the not-knowing begins. Go and look for yourselves.*

Far below in the valley, the song paused—just for a moment, just long enough to notice—and then, softly, it began again, and this time it sounded very much like it was learning her name.
