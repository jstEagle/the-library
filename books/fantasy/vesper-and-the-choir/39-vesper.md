## 39. Vesper

The winters had gotten into Emrys's hands the way rust gets into a hinge—slowly, then all at once. Some mornings her fingers took an hour to remember they were fingers, and she would sit by the stove flexing them like a woman trying to recall the words to a song she'd known all her life.

On the morning the child came, her hands were behaving beautifully.

She was working on a mourning charm for old Tam Wren's wife—a small thing, a wren carved from the knucklebone of a sheep, because Tam had insisted his wife would want something that could sit on a windowsill and look out at the garden she'd never finished weeding. The dead man himself had been hanging around the workshop for three days now, offering unsolicited opinions.

"Smaller beak," he said, peering over her shoulder in the way ghosts did, which was to say not at all and entirely at once. "She always said my beak was too big."

"You were a handsome man, Tam."

"I was a man with a nose like a hatchet, and she loved it, which is why you'll make the beak small and dignified." He sniffed. Ghosts couldn't smell anything, but they kept the habit. "What is that smell?"

"Bread," said Emrys. "I baked yesterday."

"You baked *last week*."

"Bread keeps."

"That's not bread anymore, that's geology."

Emrys smiled and kept carving. This was the shape her life had settled into, these forty-odd years since the gate: a workshop on the old foundations in Thornfell, a town that did not know her name but knew her work, a steady traffic of the dead who treated her parlor like a tavern with better acoustics. No one living remembered Emrys Thackeray. But everyone remembered the bone-carver up the crooked lane whose charms actually worked, who never asked your business, who left bread out on her step for travelers even when the bread had achieved geological significance.

Sable remembered her. That was the miracle she'd stopped questioning around year ten and started simply building her days around, the way you build a house around a hearth.

He was in the back room sorting bone stock, humming. He hummed constantly now. He didn't know what he was humming. He'd paid the Vesper Gate every memory of his childhood in Vellichor—the city he'd been born in, the mother whose face he'd finally recovered only to surrender again—and what remained was a melody with no name attached, rising out of him at odd hours like water finding its level.

"What's the tune?" she'd asked him once, early on.

"Don't know," he'd said, and his voice had gone strange and soft. "But I know how it ends."

* * *

The knock came just past midday, when the light through the workshop window went honey-colored and the mountains behind Thornfell turned the shade of old pewter.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind of knocks made by a fist the size of a pear.

Emrys opened the door onto wind and the smell of coming snow, and there stood a child—eight years old, perhaps nine, wrapped in a coat that had belonged to someone considerably larger, cheeks scoured red by the pass road. A girl. She had mud to her knees and a look on her face that Emrys recognized from mirrors: the particular stubbornness of someone who has decided that the world will simply have to make room.

"You're the carver," the girl said. Not a question.

"People say so."

"They say your charms work." The girl's chin came up. "They say other carvers' charms are just bones."

"Other carvers might take exception."

"Do they know where you live?"

"No," Emrys admitted. "That's rather the point of me."

The girl nodded as if this confirmed everything, and then she dug into the enormous coat and produced, cradled in both mittened hands like an egg she'd been carrying across three mountain passes, a whistle.

It was bone. Old bone, yellowed to the color of candlelight, worn smooth at the mouthpiece where a mouth had pressed it ten thousand times. Emrys knew before she touched it. Her hands knew first—they went hot the way they always did near the true relics, the ones still holding their dead—and her breath left her in a small white cloud that seemed, for a moment, to hang between them like a held note.

"Where," she said, and had to start again. "Where did you get this?"

"It came to us." The girl shifted her weight. "Well. It came to my gran. She said it arrived the winter I was born, sitting on our doorstep one morning with no footprints around it. Snow all smooth, and this whistle in the middle of it, warm as a fresh egg. Gran said that was witch-work and to throw it in the river." The girl's eyes narrowed with the ferocity of the young defending their treasures. "I didn't throw it in the river."

"Wise girl."

"But it doesn't do anything. It's just a whistle that won't play. I've tried for years. My brother tried. He tried so hard he got dizzy and fell in the trough." A pause. "He says you can have it if you can make it play. He says nobody can make it play. He's very annoying."

Emrys reached out.

The moment her bare fingers closed around the bone, the world went quiet—not silent, quiet, the difference being that silence is empty and quiet is full. She heard the workshop sounds fall away, heard Tam Wren's ghost stop complaining about bread, heard Sable's humming in the back room slow and deepen until it met—

Met the whistle.

The melody rose out of Sable's mouth and out of the bone at the same time, two streams finding one river, and Emrys stood in her doorway with snow beginning to fall and understood, with the calm of a woman who has spent her whole life listening to the dead, exactly what she was holding.

It was Vellichor. Not the memory of it. Not the grief of it. The country itself—or what the country had become when she'd sung its name into the horn gate all those years ago. A song instead of a state. And songs needed somewhere to live.

* * *

She brought the girl inside, sat her by the stove, fed her soup that had no geological qualities whatsoever, and listened.

Her name was Isolde, though everyone called her Sol, "because Isolde takes too long to shout." She was from a valley village two passes east, a place Emrys had passed through a dozen times without stopping. Her gran raised goats. Her brother fell in troughs. And lately—this was why she'd come, though it took two bowls of soup and considerable coaxing—lately things had been happening in the valley.

"The song," Sol said. She said it the way children say *the dragon*, with capital letters you could hear. "People hum it. They don't mean to. Da caught himself humming it while he was mending the fence and got angry, because he doesn't know any songs from anywhere, he says, we're mountain people, we don't sing. But he hums it. Everyone does. And sometimes—" she leaned forward, dropping her voice to the register children reserve for the truly important— "sometimes when people hum it together, the valley smells different. Like apples. Nobody grows apples. There's no apple trees for fifty miles. But the whole valley smells like apples, and Gran says it's witch-work, but she says that about weather too."

Emrys looked at the whistle in her palm.

Forty years. Forty years of the song spreading the way songs spread—mouth to mouth, mother to child, hummed over fences and cradles and graves, changing a little in every throat, keeping its shape the way rivers keep their shape by never being the same water twice. Maren had published the maps; travelers walked the sung borders and came home with tunes stuck in their heads they couldn't account for. Emrys had thought that was enough. That the country lived now as folklore, harmless and immortal, belonging to everyone and no one.

But the whistle had come to a doorstep with no footprints. Warm as a fresh egg.

And it wouldn't play for anyone.

"Sol," she said carefully. "Has anyone else tried to make it play? Anyone older? Your gran?"

"Gran won't touch it. She says it looks at her." Sol shrugged, unbothered. "Does it look at people?"

"Sometimes bones do that." Emrys turned the whistle over. On its underside, nearly worn away, there was a mark—a maker's mark, a small stylized gate with two doors. She knew that mark. She'd seen it burned into crates in a smuggler's cache forty years ago, on the map's oilskin wrapping, on the seal of a country that no longer existed.

The dead courier's ghost had told her once, in the early weeks, that Vellichor had always chosen its keepers strangely. Not kings. Not priests. "Doorkeepers," he'd called them, and laughed at some private joke. "A country built on a bargain needs someone to hold the door."

She hadn't thought much about it at the time. There had been rather a lot else going on, including a man she'd later marry standing in the snow pretending not to remember her while remembering her with every line of his face.

Now she sat in her warm workshop with a child's whistle singing silently in her hand, and the understanding arrived the way dawn arrives—not suddenly, but all at once anyway.

The song needed a keeper. Not a queen. Not a Cantor with stolen centuries and a guilty crown. Someone who listened. Someone who had already given the country the only gift it had ever asked for: her own name, freely, so that it could survive as music instead of devouring memory. The gate had taken her name from every living mind except one. And in exchange—she'd never thought of it as an exchange, but bargains were bargains, and Vellichor had been a country built on one—it had left something with her. Or left her with something.

A door to hold.

"Well?" Sol demanded. "Can you make it play or can't you?"

Emrys looked at the girl—at the stubborn chin, the mud to the knees, the treasure defended against grandmothers and brothers alike—and felt something turn over in her chest, the way soil turns in spring.

"Not today," she said. "Today it's tired, and so am I, and my bread is a geological specimen. But you'll stay the night, because it's snowing and your gran would skin me. And tomorrow—" she weighed the whistle in her palm, feeling it hum against her skin like a sleeping bird— "tomorrow I'll teach you the song properly. All the verses. Even the ones people forget."

Sol's eyes went wide. "You know it?"

"I've known it longer than you've been alive, little doorkeeper."

"I'm not a doorkeeper."

"Mm," said Emrys, and said nothing more, because some things a person has to grow into, like coats and callings.

* * *

That evening Sable found them by the stove, Sol already asleep in the good chair with the whistle clutched to her chest, and Emrys teaching herself—in silence, breath only, no sound yet—the fingering of an instrument she somehow already knew.

Sable crouched beside her chair. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way she found unfairly distinguished, and his eyes, when they found hers, still did the thing they had done the first day outside her stall: saw her. Only her. Always her.

"It woke up," he said quietly.

"It's been awake for years. Waiting."

"For what?"

"For someone to answer." She looked at the sleeping child, at the small fists around the bone. "Sable. Do you regret it? What you paid? You never got to keep the childhood. Your mother's face. Any of it."

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, the snow came down soft and thick over Thornfell, over the mountains, over the invisible borders of a country that smelled like apples in valleys where no apples grew.

"I kept the ending," he said at last. "You told me once—I asked what the tune was, and I said I knew how it ended." He took her hand, the one not hovering over the whistle holes. "The ending is this. This kitchen. That kid. You. I'd pay it again tomorrow, and the day after, and every day until my hands give out."

Emrys Thackeray—who had no name in any living mouth but his—leaned her head against his shoulder and breathed.

And in the child's sleep-loosened grip, the bone whistle grew warm as a fresh egg, and if either of them had been listening closely—if they had not been so busy being, for once, alive and present and unafraid—they might have heard it: a single clear note, testing itself against the dark, like a door opening somewhere very far away and very near.

Like a country, clearing its throat to sing.

Tam Wren's ghost drifted through the wall, took one look at the whistle, and went pale in the way only the dead can.

"Oh," he said faintly. "Oh, *her*. She wants a word with you, carver. Says it's time you learned the last verse."
