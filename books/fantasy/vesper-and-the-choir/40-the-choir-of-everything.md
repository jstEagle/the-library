## 40. The Choir of Everything

The trouble with growing old in a house built on bones—your own foundations, this time, laid honest and square over the ashes of the old workshop—was that the dead got chatty in the evenings.

Emrys Thackeray had made her peace with this decades ago. She sat now in her doorway with a blanket over her knees and a half-carved sparrow in her lap, watching the sun go down behind Thornfell's eastern peaks, and she listened to the evening crowd gather.

"You're sitting too far from the fire," said the dead man who had been a shepherd in life and remained a fussbudget in death. "Your joints will seize."

"My joints will seize regardless," Emrys said. "It's called being ninety-one, Aldric. Everything seizes eventually. Doors, clocks, knees."

"Then at least let me warm your hands." He drifted closer, translucent as breath on glass, and though he could no more warm her hands than he could knit them a scarf, the gesture was the point. It had always been the point. The living touched to say *I am here*; the dead hovered to say it too, in the only grammar left to them.

Behind him came the others, as they did every dusk. The smuggler woman who'd frozen in the high pass forty years back and had opinions about everything since. The courier—the first ghost, the one whose map had unmade her quiet life and remade it into something larger—still wearing his ruined coat, still apologizing for it. A handful of children who had never once frightened her, because children's ghosts only ever wanted someone to listen to their riddles. And beneath all of them, fainter than any of them but present as marrow: the great low hum of Vellichor, the sung country, threading through the mountains like water under stone.

Forty years since the gate. Forty years since she had stood before two doors—one ivory, one horn—with a dying king's dust on her boots and every dead soul in an erased nation pressing at her back, begging her to bring them home the easy way. The way that would have eaten every living memory of them, including hers, including Sable's, including the widow's charm still hanging around some grandmother's neck three valleys over.

She had chosen the hard way instead. She had sung.

And the song had held.

"Maren's cart was on the road this morning," the courier ghost reported, settling beside her chair with the air of a man delivering dispatches. "Headed south. She waved at your window."

"She always waves at my window. She could wave at my door like a person with legs."

"She says climbing your steps is like ascending a small mountain, and she's mapped actual mountains."

"Maren has mapped everything except humility," Emrys said fondly. The mapmaker's daughter was seventy herself now, white-haired and sharp as a compass needle, and she still came through Thornfell twice a year with new editions of the maps that had once been contraband. Travelers carried them now openly, folded into saddlebags, tracing the dotted borders of a country that existed nowhere except in song—and humming, always humming, fragments they couldn't name. That had been the miracle nobody planned. You could erase a nation from records and memory both, but you couldn't stop people from whistling.

The sparrow in her lap needed only its wing feathers. Her hands were slower now; the knuckles had gone knotted as tree roots, and on cold mornings the right one ached where she'd broken it falling out of a Choir sanctum window at fifty-three, an incident Sable still brought up whenever he wanted to win an argument. But the knife still knew its work. Bone remembered. That had been the whole of her gift from the beginning, before she'd understood anything else about it: bone remembers, and if you listened closely enough, it would tell you what it remembered.

She set the last feather-cut with a fine grating whisper and held the little bird up to the fading light.

"Oh, that's good," said one of the ghost children, orbiting her shoulder. "Is it for the whistle girl?"

"It might be."

The whistle girl. Ilse, nine years old, gap-toothed, fearless, the daughter of the innkeeper down the road—the one who stared straight through Emrys for the first six months after the counter-song unwrote her, then one morning blinked and said, "You know, I feel like I've known you my whole life," which was as close to remembering as anyone got. Ilse had come up the mountain four years ago carrying a bone whistle she'd found in a streambed, and when she put it to her lips, the Vellichor song had come out whole, every verse, in a voice that should not have known it.

Emrys had understood then what the country had done. It hadn't just become a song. It had gone looking for keepers. And somewhere in the vast gentle arithmetic of what survived erasure, it had chosen the bone-carver who had given it a voice at the gate.

Ilse came up most evenings now. Emrys was teaching her to carve—not relics, not yet, the girl wasn't ready to hear what the dead said through them—but simple things. Buttons. Birds. Patience.

"There she is," Aldric announced, and there she was indeed, puffing up the path with her satchel bouncing, red-faced from the climb, waving with both arms as though Emrys might otherwise miss the entire spectacle of her arrival.

"You're late," Emrys said.

"I'm punctual. Your mountain is steep." Ilse dropped onto the step beside her chair, caught her breath, and peered at the sparrow. "Oh, it's lovely. Who's it for?"

"The widow Brannock. Her husband died in the spring flood."

"He's here, isn't he? The husband?"

"He's been here all afternoon complaining that the eulogy was too long and the ale ran out too soon." Emrys lowered her voice with theatrical gravity. "Between us, he may have a point about the ale."

The ghost husband, hovering sheepishly near the woodpile, had the grace to look embarrassed. "There was barely a barrel," he protested. "Barely a barrel, and forty mourners."

"I'll tell her to order two next time," Emrys promised, and the man settled, mollified, the way the dead did when someone finally took their complaints seriously. That was the work, in the end. Not charms, not carvings, not even the gift itself. Just listening. Forty years of listening, and she had learned that most souls didn't want rescuing or avenging or restoring. They wanted what everyone wanted: to be heard, once, properly, by someone who wasn't in a hurry.

The light went amber, then rose. Down in the valley, lamps began to kindle one by one, and the first voices rose with them—the evening song, the one that had spread through the Thornfell towns without anyone deciding to sing it. A market tune, mostly. A lullaby underneath. And threaded through both, unmistakable if you knew how to listen, the deep bright spine of Vellichor's true name, which Emrys had sung into the horn door while the world held its breath.

Nobody in the valley knew what they were singing. That was the beauty of it. The song lived in them the way rivers live in stone—shaping without asking, persisting without permission. Scholars came sometimes, from universities Emrys had never heard of, asking about the "regional melody tradition," and Maren delighted in giving them lectures that raised more questions than they answered, and Emrys served tea and said nothing and enjoyed herself immensely.

"They're starting early tonight," Ilse said, tilting her head toward the valley. "Listen—old Tam's already going, and he usually waits for the lamplighter."

Emrys listened. Tam's cracked tenor floated up the hillside, joined by the baker's wife, then the ferryman, then a scatter of children's voices chasing each other through the lanes. And beneath it, around it, through it, the others: the dead rising out of the dusk like mist off a lake, hundreds of them now, thousands, gathering on the mountainside the way they gathered every evening, because the song was a door and every dusk it opened a crack, and every evening they came to stand in the light of it.

Not trapped anymore. Not begging. Just... present. Neighbors, almost. The living sang and the dead harmonized, and neither group entirely knew about the other, and it worked anyway, because music had never required anyone to agree on the terms.

"Sing with me," Ilse said. She always asked, though Emrys always did.

"I'm old, girl. My singing frightens cats."

"Your singing is the whole reason any of this exists, and you know it, and I'm not letting you hide behind false modesty on a nice evening." Ilse took her hand—small fingers around knotted ones—and pulled until Emrys, laughing despite herself, found her feet.

They walked out together onto the flat stone before the workshop, the stone Sable had hauled up the mountain himself thirty years ago because Emrys had mentioned, exactly once, that she missed having a place to stand and watch the sky. The dead made room for them. They always did. Aldric bowed, absurdly formal; the courier pressed a hand to his ruined chest; the ghost children curtsied to Ilse, who curtsied back, because she couldn't see them but she'd grown up on Emrys's stories and knew her manners.

And then the song swelled up from the valley, and Emrys opened her mouth and added her voice to it.

Her voice was a ruin these days—raspy, thin, prone to cracking on the high notes—but the song didn't care. Songs are patient. They wait inside you until you're ready, and then they use whatever you have left. She sang the market tune, and the lullaby beneath it, and then, as the dusk deepened to violet and the first stars pricked through, the true verses. The ones with the name in them.

*Vesper,* she sang, and the mountain seemed to lean in. *Vesper, vesper, the door made of evening, the country made of song—*

The dead sang with her. The living sang below, unknowing. And Sable—

Sable came out of the workshop with two cups of the terrible chicory he refused to stop brewing, stopped when he saw them singing, and stood there in the doorway with the steam rising past his face, and waited, the way he had waited for forty years, with the patience of a man who had once given up everything twice and considered it, on balance, a fair trade.

He was grey now too. They were grey together, two old people on a mountaintop, and he still looked at her some mornings across the breakfast table as though checking she was real, and she still caught him doing it, and neither of them ever mentioned it, because some things are better left as rituals than explained.

The song reached the place it always reached—the long descending phrase, the one like a door swinging shut, gently, on a room full of sleeping people—and then it turned, as it always turned, toward the final line. The naming line. The line the whole song existed to carry.

And Emrys, who had sung this song every evening for forty years, heard something she had never heard in it before.

Her own name.

It came woven into the melody so naturally that she stumbled mid-verse, certain she'd imagined it. *Emrys.* Sung by a hundred dead voices in harmony, tucked between the notes like a seed in bread. *Emrys Thackeray, the keeper, the listener, the one who stayed.*

"What?" Ilse whispered, gripping her arm. "What is it?"

But Emrys couldn't answer. The song was moving through her now, and the dead were turning toward her—all of them, the whole mountainside of them, Aldric and the courier and the fussing shepherd and the frozen smuggler and the children and the nameless thousands of Vellichor, every soul she had ever carved for or sung to or led home—and they were singing her name the way she had once sung theirs. Not as a record. Not as a monument. As a welcome. The way you say the name of someone arriving.

Forty years she had spent hearing everyone else. Every grief, every grievance, every unfinished sentence the dead carried. She had been the ear of the mountains, the door-holder, the one who listened so others could be heard. And she had told herself, all this time, that was enough—that being the listener was its own reward, that a life spent on other people's voices was a life fully spent.

She had believed it, mostly.

But oh, to hear your own name in the song. To learn that the listening had been heard. That somewhere in the great weaving of the sung country and the waking world, her thread had been noticed, and named, and kept.

Tears ran down into the creases of her face, and she let them, and she sang her own name back to them, and the mountains rang with it.

Sable set down the cups—he always set them down carefully, even now, even with miracles occurring—and crossed the stone and put his arms around her from behind, his chin resting on her hair, and he didn't ask what was happening because he had learned, over forty years, that she would tell him when the telling could survive words.

"They're singing me," she managed.

"I know." His voice was rough. "I've heard it for weeks. I was waiting to see if you'd catch it."

"You—" She twisted to glare up at him, tear-streaked and furious and laughing. "You let me miss my own name for *weeks?*"

"You were busy carving sparrows." He kissed the top of her head. "I didn't want to rush you. Some things you have to hear when you're ready."

Below them, the valley lights burned steady. Above them, the stars thickened. The song wound down to embers, and the dead began to drift back into the dusk—not departing, never departing, just folding themselves away like letters returned to their drawer—and Ilse sat down on the flat stone with her whistle and played the last phrase alone, soft, for practice, while the night came on.

Emrys stood in Sable's arms a while longer, listening to the silence after the song, which was not silent at all but full of the hum of Vellichor, patient as groundwater, content as a cat.

"One day," she said quietly, "that song will be sung and I won't be here to hear it."

"I know."

"And the country will need a keeper."

"It has one." He nodded toward the step, where Ilse sat with the bone whistle glinting in the starlight, absently turning it over in her fingers, frowning at it the way one frowns at a thing that has begun, very slowly, to speak.

Emrys watched the girl a long moment. Then she smiled, and leaned back against the man who had kept one memory of her against the whole weight of forgetting, and let the evening hold her, and thought: *not yet, but soon, and it will be in good hands.*

From the whistle, faint as breath, the next verse began—and it was one Emrys had never taught anyone, because she had never known it herself.

* * *

Ilse looked up, eyes wide. "Emrys," she said. "This song has words I've never heard before. And they're—" she hesitated, cheeks going pink in the dark— "they're about you. About what happens *next.*"

Emrys Thackeray, ninety-one years old, keeper of the sung country, leaned forward with her heart hammering like a girl's, and said, "Play it again, child. All of it. And this time—I think we'd best write it down."
