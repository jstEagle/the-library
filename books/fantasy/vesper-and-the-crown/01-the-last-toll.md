## 1. The Last Toll

The mule knew the way better than Rafael did, and that was the whole trouble with the world now.

He let her pick her footing along the shelf of rock above the Graywater gorge, one hand loose on the lead rope, the other resting on the strap of his pack as though he were merely adjusting it. He was not adjusting it. Beneath his palm, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the false bottom where a Vesper runner had once carried royal dispatches and church relics and, on one famous occasion, a fugitive archbishop disguised as a case of altar wine, lay forty pounds of raw opium gum pressed into bricks stamped with a merchant's mark out of Sallowmere.

It was not the kind of cargo that made a man proud. It was not the kind of cargo that made a man anything at all, really, which Rafael supposed was the point. The great cargoes were gone. The passes had been mapped, taxed, garrisoned, and paved, and what remained of the trade that had once kept three kingdoms honest ran on contraband medicine and smuggled salt and the small desperate vices of people who could no longer afford to be particular.

Four of us, he thought. Once there had been two hundred.

Once there had been bells.

Every Vesper station in the Thornfells had kept a bell at its door, cast from melted-down tax ledgers — the Guild's one piece of theater that Rafael had always found genuinely moving rather than merely sentimental. When a runner came home safe, the bell rang once. When a runner came home dead, the bell rang twice, and every station within earshot rang twice after, so that the news traveled down the mountains faster than any rider could carry it, and by the time the widow opened her door there was already someone standing on her step with bread and coin and no explanation required.

Rafael had heard the double-toll eleven times in his life. He had rung it himself four times. The last time had been for Bren, two winters past, caught in an avalanche slide above Kestrel Notch, and the bell at Hollow Waystation had cracked with the second stroke — old bronze, old grief — and they had never had the money to recast it. So now when someone died, the bell made a sound like a word spoken through broken teeth, and Rafael had privately decided that this was more honest anyway.

Three stations left. Four members. One of them dying.

He checked the sky without breaking stride. Clouds building white and fat over the western peaks, which meant snow by dark if the wind turned, which meant he wanted to be below the treeline before the wind turned. The opium would keep. Mules kept. Men were the perishable cargo, and he had been careless with himself lately — sleeping in waystations that should have been abandoned, taking trails that should have been retired, moving through the mountains with the particular recklessness of a man who had stopped entirely believing anyone was watching.

That was the other trouble with the world now. Nobody was watching. The Vesper Guild had spent two hundred years being watched — hunted by crowns, cursed by priests, romanticized by ballad-singers who got every detail wrong — and now the passes had their tollgates and their garrison roads and their imperial survey markers painted in cheerful yellow, and the Guild had been permitted the final indignity of becoming irrelevant. No one hunted them anymore. No one needed to. You did not hunt a ghost that had stopped haunting.

Rafael found, some mornings, that he missed even the hunting. At least it had meant they mattered.

* * *

The waystation at Hollow sat in a fold of the mountain like a stone cupped in a stone hand, roof sagging, chimney leaning, the cracked bell hanging in its frame beside the door with a bird's nest wedged into the crown of it. Rafael smelled woodsmoke before he saw the light in the window, and his chest did the thing it always did now, the small painful lift, because smoke meant Odile was awake, and Odile awake was a thing he had learned to count like coins.

She was at the long table in the main room, wrapped in two blankets despite the fire, sorting the post. That was what she called it — the post — though it was really just the trickle of coded letters and half-burned notes that still came to them from old contacts who hadn't quite managed to forget the Guild existed. Her hands shook. They had shaken for a year, but in the last month the shaking had changed character, become less a tremor and more a negotiation between her will and her body, and she lost more of those negotiations every week.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"Wind on the high shelf. I gave the mule her head."

"The mule has more sense than you. Sit. Eat. There's barley soup and it's terrible."

"It's always terrible."

"I make it terrible on purpose," Odile said, "so you'll appreciate the good days." She pushed a bowl across the table, and Rafael sat and ate, and for a while neither of them spoke, because they were both people who understood that silence between competent people was not empty but full — full of the wind checking the shutters, full of the fire settling, full of everything that didn't need saying yet.

Odile Vesper-Hand had run the Guild for thirty-one years. She had been beautiful once, in the way that weathered things are beautiful, and now she was mostly angles and gray braid and a voice like gravel poured slowly. She had trained Rafael from the age of nine, when his mother had handed him over at the Lowmark ferry with a bundle of clothes and the words *he reads too much, make him useful*, and Odile had looked him over the way she looked over cargo — weighing, assessing, finding the hidden seam — and said, *Can you keep a secret?* And nine-year-old Rafael had said yes, and she had said, *Wrong answer. Nobody can. The trick is choosing which secret is worth the cost of trying,* and he had loved her from that moment with the fierce, unspoken devotion of children adopted by institutions.

"Forty pounds of Sallowmere gum," he reported. "Delivered to the Widow Anser's barn, third lockbox, payment taken. She says the garrison doctor's been buying through her since midsummer, so we can probably raise the price."

"Raise it ten percent, no more. Greed is how the old Guilds died." She sorted another letter into the pile she kept for burning. "Anything on the road?"

"Survey crew working up toward Kestrel Notch. Imperial yellow paint, new markers. They're mapping a cart road through the pass."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes — not surprise; nothing surprised her anymore — something older and wearier, like watching a grave being measured. "Through the pass," she repeated. "Through our pass."

"Roads don't belong to anyone, Master."

"Roads belong to whoever remembers them," Odile said. "That has always been the entire business. Kings build roads and forget them. We remember them. Take away the remembering and we're just vagrants with good boots." She gathered the letters to burn, then paused, hands trembling around the bundle, and looked at him across the table with an expression he couldn't read and didn't like. "How is your mother?"

"She died six years ago, Master."

"Yes," Odile said. "I know. I'm asking how you are, boy. I'm old. My metaphors have died too."

Rafael looked down at his soup. "I'm fine."

"You're fine," she agreed, in the tone of a woman filing a lie in the correct drawer. "Eat your terrible soup."

* * *

He dreamed of bells that night, and woke before dawn with the dream still ringing in his teeth.

In the dream the whole mountain range was hung with bells, every station lit and manned, runners coming and going along the high trails like blood through a living body, and the double-toll was sounding somewhere far off, and in the dream he was running toward it, running to be the one who brought bread and coin to the door, and he could never arrive. He woke in the loft of the stable with frost on the blanket and the sound of Odile coughing rising through the floorboards — that deep wet tearing cough that had started in autumn and had not, as far as Rafael could tell, any intention of ever stopping.

There was a physic in Lowmark. There were physicks everywhere; the empire had seen to that, tidy little licensed physicks in tidy little licensed towns. Odile refused them all. *A Vesper master does not put herself in an imperial ledger,* she said, as though the ledger cared, as though anyone in the gray-wigged bureaucracy of the Thornfell Protectorate had given the Vesper Guild a single thought in twenty years. But Rafael understood it wasn't really about ledgers. It was about dignity, the last cargo she carried, and he did not press, because pressing her was like pressing on a bruise she'd decided to keep.

He came down to find her at the table again, before first light, writing. Her hand had steadied itself against the page with the sheer tyranny of long habit.

"You should be asleep," he said.

"You should stop telling me what I should do. It wastes both our mornings." She finished the line, sanded it, folded the paper. "I'm making the winter assignments. You'll take the southern circuit — Lowmark, Ferrow Bridge, the river stations. Tam has the north. Wren stays here with me."

Wren was nineteen, quick and silent, the closest thing the Guild had to an apprentice now, and Rafael had watched Odile teaching her all autumn with an attention that went beyond curriculum. Odile was not teaching Wren routes. She was teaching her the things that couldn't be written: how to read a stranger's boots, how to listen to a room, how to be forgotten on purpose. She was pouring thirty-one years through a funnel the width of one girl, and both of them knew the hourglass was running low, and neither of them said it.

"And after winter?" Rafael asked.

"After winter," Odile said, "we'll see what winter leaves."

* * *

The southern circuit took him eight days, and it was on the eighth day, coming back up the valley toward Hollow with an empty pack and a purse of fees, that he saw the smoke over Lowmark and felt the first cold finger of the future touch the back of his neck.

Smoke alone meant nothing. Lowmark was a river town; river towns burned things constantly. But this smoke was wrong — too black, too concentrated, rising from the riverside quarter in a column that stood perfectly straight in the still air, and beneath it, faint with distance, he could hear shouting. Not panic-shouting. Organized shouting. The shouting of men with orders.

Rafael stood on the ridge trail with his empty pack and his purse of fees and did not go down.

This was the discipline, the oldest discipline, drilled into him since childhood: *a runner never runs toward smoke.* The Guild survived two centuries of war and purge because its people understood that curiosity was a luxury purchased with other people's lives. Whatever was happening in Lowmark, the correct response was to turn east, take the Hollow Road, and be a rumor by nightfall.

He turned east. He told himself the tightness in his chest was only the climb.

But that evening, warming himself at a fire in a shepherd's hut two valleys over, he heard the news from a drover who'd come through Lowmark at noon: gray-cloaked riders at the ferry, asking questions. A warrant read aloud at the market cross. Inquisitors, the drover said, spitting into the fire. Crown inquisitors, in the valley, asking after a dead man on the high trails — a traveler, fallen ill or wounded, seen in the company of a local man on the switchback above the gorge two days past.

Two days past. Rafael went very still, feeding sticks to the fire, keeping his face stupid and interested in the way Odile had taught him, the face of a man with nothing behind his eyes but weather and prices.

He had taken the switchback above the gorge two days past. He remembered it clearly — the narrow ledge, the drop to the Graywater churning below, the mule's ears swiveling at the echo. And he remembered, now that the memory was asked for, the thing he had registered and dismissed: a figure far up the trail ahead of him, a man walking badly, lurching against the rock wall, who had waved him off or perhaps waved for help, Rafael hadn't stopped to look, because stopping for strangers on high trails was how runners died, because the discipline said move, move, move—

The drover was still talking. "...they hung the ferryman this morning, poor devil, right off his own jetty. Collaborating, they said. With what, nobody knows. Asking after some map, some say. A map." He laughed uncomfortably. "A map. What king burns a town over a map?"

Rafael looked at the fire and thought about the man on the switchback, walking badly, waving. Thought about how close he had passed to him. How close the inquisitors' questions were passing now, up the valleys, along the trails, closing like a hand.

"A dangerous one," he said quietly, and the drover looked at him, and Rafael smiled his stupid smile and asked about the price of wool.

* * *

He reached Hollow Waystation at moonrise, having run the last six miles of trail in the dark at a pace no careful runner would use, and found the yard full of horses.

Not their horses. Theirs were shaggy mountain stock, small and sure-footed. These were tall, deep-chested, shod in the imperial pattern, and they stood in the lean-to with the patient menace of animals accustomed to being fed by soldiers. The waystation windows were lit. Through them, silhouetted against the firelight, Rafael could see figures moving — gray cloaks, the cut of official hoods.

His stomach dropped through the mountain.

They had found Hollow. However they'd done it — the ferryman's confession under torture, the drover's gossip, some ledger of old suspicions dusted off in some provincial archive — they had found the station, and Odile was inside, and Wren was inside, and the cracked bell hung silent on its post because Rafael had come in from the east, downwind, quiet, unseen.

He crouched in the shadow of the woodpile with his heart slamming and forced himself to think, forced the fear down into the box where runners kept it, and counted. Four riders visible. Probably six. Inquisitors traveled heavy with escort but light in number — that much he knew from the old stories, and the old stories had been right about everything else.

And then the door opened, and Odile came out.

She walked slowly, wrapped in her blankets, leaning on her stick, and she stopped in front of the tallest of the gray figures and Rafael saw her look up at him — up, because she was small and had never once in her life seemed small — and though he could not hear the words across the yard, he could see the shape of the exchange. The inquisitor speaking. Odile answering. A document held out; Odile not taking it. A long moment. Then the inquisitor gestured, and his riders began to search the station, methodically, tearing at the walls Rafael had helped mend, and Odile stood in the yard and watched them with her chin up while the cold killed her by inches.

They found nothing. Of course they found nothing; there was nothing to find; the Guild had spent two hundred years learning how to hide things from exactly this. Near midnight the riders mounted and rode west down the valley toward Lowmark, and the door closed, and the light in the window burned on.

Rafael stayed in the woodpile's shadow until his knees ached, and then he went in.

Odile was sitting at the table. She looked smaller than he had ever seen her, the search having taken something out of her that the coughing hadn't touched. On the table before her, laid flat, was a letter — not one of her coded circulars but a single sheet of plain paper, and beside it, wrapped in wax-stained linen, a scroll case sealed with a blob of green wax stamped with a device Rafael did not recognize: a crown, inverted, sinking into a wave.

"They weren't looking for us," Odile said. Her voice was steady, but her hands, around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, were shaking worse than he had ever seen them shake. "Did you understand that? Twenty years they've ignored us, and now they tear my house apart, and hang a ferryman, and it isn't about us at all. It's about *that.*"

"What is it?"

"A dead man's map," Odile said. "Found on the high trail two days ago, by a shepherd, before the inquisitors got here. He brought it to me, because the shepherd's mother was Guild, once, and some habits outlive the Guild that taught them." She looked up at him, and her eyes in the firelight were the eyes of a woman reading her own ending. "I know this seal, Rafael. I've seen it twice in my life, and both times I prayed I'd never see it again. It's pre-War work. Royal sealwork, two hundred years old, from before the Thornfell War, from before the execution — from the court of the king they drowned in his own throne room."

Rafael stood in the doorway of his home with the cold still on him and the smoke of Lowmark still in his nose, and heard himself ask the only question that mattered.

"The map leads where?"

Odile pushed the scroll case across the table toward him, slow, like a woman pushing her last coin to the center of a losing game.

"To the Sunken Crown," she said. "And now, boy, whether we like it or not — so do we."
