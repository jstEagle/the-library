## 2. A Stranger on the Switchback

The switchback above the Widow's Tooth was the kind of trail that made even the mountain goats reconsider their choices. Rafael Whitlock had walked it in fog, in sleet, once with a broken rib and a sack of contraband brandy digging into his side like a second knife. He knew every loose stone by name, every place where the path narrowed to a hand's width of crumbling shale with nothing below but four hundred feet of gray air and older bones.

He was thinking about none of that now. He was thinking about Odile's cough.

It had changed over the winter. That was the thing Rafael couldn't stop turning over, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. A cough could be many things—a cold, a pipe habit, the damp of the waystation at Hollowmere where she'd slept for thirty years—but this one had a rhythm to it now, a wet, deliberate architecture, as though her lungs were being taken apart piece by piece by something patient and thorough. She'd waved it off, of course. Odile waved off everything: avalanches, tax collectors, the slow death of the world they'd been born into. But Rafael had carried her tea up the stairs last week and heard her breathing afterward, the long rattling silence between breaths, and had stood outside her door in the dark feeling something he refused to name.

Four of them left. That was the Vesper Guild. Four people, one ledger, and a network of roads that no longer led anywhere anyone wanted to go.

Rafael climbed anyway, because climbing was what remained. The morning was sharp and clean, the kind of alpine light that made distances lie—made the far peaks look close enough to touch and the next switchback look impossibly far. Below him the valley held its thin ribbon of river, and beyond that, small as a child's toy, the town of Lowmark with its ferry crossing and its market smoke rising straight up in the windless air. Somewhere down there, a customs officer was stamping papers. Somewhere down there, the Crown's roads ran flat and safe and toll-paid, and nobody needed a smuggler for anything anymore except the ones who couldn't afford to be seen, and those were fewer every year.

His pack rode light today. Salt from the lowland pans, a bolt of dye-cloth, letters for the hermit at Saint Verane's who paid in old coin he shouldn't have had. Honest work, or near enough. The Guild's last honest work.

He smelled the blood before he saw the man.

It came down the trail faintly, iron-sweet under the pine resin, and Rafael stopped mid-stride with the instinct of his trade—not fear exactly, but the sudden total attention of an animal that has heard something wrong in the forest. His hand went to the knife at his hip without consulting him. The wind shifted. The smell strengthened.

The man lay crumpled against the outer wall of the third switchback, half in the scree, half on the path itself, as if he'd been walking and simply been switched off. He wore traveling clothes that had once been good—dark wool, a riding coat with the shoulders cut for a sword-belt—and they were soaked through at the side, dark fabric gone black and glistening. One arm was flung out across the stone. His face was turned toward the sky, gray-lipped, eyes half-open and fixed on nothing, and he was maybe thirty yards away, which meant Rafael had been smelling him for a quarter mile without knowing.

"Hello," Rafael said, because you said hello. Because nine times in ten it was a drunk, or a fool caught out overnight, or a pilgrim with more faith than boots. "Can you hear me?"

The man's chest moved. Barely. A flutter, like a bird trapped under cloth.

Rafael approached the way he'd approach anything on a mountain trail that might still be dangerous—angled, unhurried, watching the hands. The hands were empty. The right one was a ruin, though: torn knuckles, nails split, the fingers blackened at the tips with frost and something else, something sticky dried in the creases. Ink, Rafael thought absurdly. Or wax.

Up close the wound told its own story. A blade, low on the left side, angled upward—the kind of cut that didn't bleed you out fast so much as fill you slowly, from the inside, while you walked. And he had walked. There were drag-marks in the scree behind him, ten feet of them, then twenty, a staggering line written in displaced stone all the way back around the shoulder of the ridge. He'd been climbing the switchbacks wounded. Alone. In the dark, maybe, or near enough to it.

"You're a long way from anywhere," Rafael said, kneeling. He pulled the flask from his pack, the good one, Odile's plum brandy, and got an arm behind the man's shoulders to lift him enough to drink. The body under the coat was furnace-hot and shaking. "Easy. Small sips."

The man's eyes found him then. They were brown, ordinary brown, and full of a terror so complete and so controlled that Rafael felt it pass into him like a current. This was not a man afraid of dying. This was a man afraid of not finishing something first.

"The road," the stranger rasped. His voice was wrecked, gravel and water. "You're Guild."

It wasn't a question, and Rafael's spine went cold in the bright morning sun. Nobody knew that. Nobody was supposed to know that. The Vesper badge was a story grandmothers told; the roads were just roads unless you knew how to read them, and Rafael's clothes were shepherd-plain, his pack unmarked.

"You've got me confused," Rafael said carefully.

"I don't." A bubble of red moved at the corner of the man's mouth. His hand came up—it took visible effort, the whole arm trembling—and closed on Rafael's wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong. "Listen to me. Listen. They'll come asking. Gray cloaks. Don't tell them—" A cough tore through him, deep and terrible, and when it passed he was weaker, his head lolling, but the grip stayed. "Don't tell them I spoke. Say you found me dead. Promise me."

"Who did this to you?"

"Promise." The word came out almost gentle, which was somehow worse than if he'd screamed it.

Rafael looked down at the dying man's face—at the ordinary brown eyes, the ink-stained fingers, the frostbitten ruin of his hand—and heard himself say, "I promise."

Something in the stranger eased. Not peace, exactly. More like a man setting down a load one-handed, knowing he'd have to pick it up again. His grip loosened, slid, and then tightened once more, and this time it wasn't Rafael's wrist he was holding. It was something pressed into Rafael's palm, folded small and hard, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with handling.

A map. Rafael knew the weight and shape of a rolled map the way a priest knows a psalter. But this one was heavy for its size, and stiff, and when the oilcloth parted in his hand he saw the edge of fine paper beneath a seal of dark red wax, stamped with a device he didn't recognize—a crown, inverted, its points driven downward like roots.

"Keep it off the roads," the stranger whispered. "Keep it buried. Whatever they offer. Whatever they—" His breath hitched, caught, held. His eyes had gone past Rafael now, up at the sky, seeing something else entirely. "The Crown remembers," he said. "God forgive us. The Crown remembers."

And then, between one breath and the next, he didn't.

Rafael knelt there on the switchback with a dead man's warmth still soaking into his knee and a sealed map burning a hole through his glove, and the wind moved the pines, and somewhere below, small and indifferent, Lowmark's market bell rang the hour.

* * *

He did the things you do. He checked for breath twice, though he already knew. He closed the man's eyes, which felt less like kindness than like forgery. He searched the body quickly, professionally, hating himself for how professional it was—no purse worth the name, no papers, no token of any house or order. Only a plain ring worn smooth, and inside the collar of his shirt, stitched flat against the skin, a scrap of linen embroidered with a single letter: **C**.

Corvin, then, maybe. A name for a grave.

Rafael buried him shallow, because the ground above the treeline gave you two choices: shallow or not at all. He stacked stones over the spot, working fast, glancing down the trail too often, and the whole time his mind kept circling the same three facts like crows over carrion. *One:* a man had bled out on a Vesper road, which hadn't happened in Rafael's lifetime, because the roads were secret and the secrets held. *Two:* the man had known what Rafael was on sight, which meant someone had told him, which meant the telling had happened recently and somewhere close. *Three:* Rafael was now carrying a thing a man had died to move, sealed with a crown stamped upside-down, and had promised a corpse to lie about it.

The map went into the inner pocket of his coat, against his chest, where it sat like a second heart. Cold at first. Then, as he climbed, strangely not.

He made the high traverse by early afternoon and dropped down through the pine shadow toward Hollowmere, the Guild's last waystation—a squat stone house built into the hillside so thoroughly that from three hundred yards it was just another slope, just another face of the mountain wearing its scree like a hood. Odile would know what the seal was. Odile knew everything pre-War; she'd been raised by people who remembered it firsthand, and she kept that knowledge the way misers keep coin, in the dark, counted nightly.

He was still a mile out, following the stream-cut that hid his approach, when he saw the horses.

Two of them, gray, tied at the waystation's lower gate with the neat, insulting precision of military tack. And standing in the yard, talking to Tam—the youngest of them, nineteen, loyal as a dog and half as suspicious—were two figures in travel-stained cloaks of a gray so deliberate it wasn't a color but a statement. Their hoods were back. Their faces were pleasant. One of them was smiling.

Rafael stopped in the shadow of the pines and did not move.

Inquisitors. He'd seen them twice in his life, both times at a distance, both times in towns where afterward something was different—quieter, or emptier. The gray warrant, Odile called it. Authority to ask any question by any means. They traveled in pairs, they never announced themselves in advance, and they asked questions no traveler should need to ask, because travelers wanted directions and inquisitors wanted *people*.

He watched the smiling one say something to Tam, watch Tam laugh nervously and shake his head, watch the smile stay fixed like paint. Then the other one—taller, a woman, her hair bound back with severe practicality—turned her head, slowly, and looked directly at the tree line.

Not at Rafael. At the trees. As if reading them.

Rafael withdrew the way he'd come, silent, heart hammering against the oilcloth-wrapped weight over his ribs, and circled wide through the rocks until he reached the back of the waystation, where the old smuggler's door opened out of the hillside behind a curtain of ivy. It was barred from within. He knocked the Guild knock—two, pause, three—and heard the bar scrape, and Odile's face appeared in the gap, older and grayer than it had been a week ago, and her eyes went from his face to his coat pocket and back with a single terrible fluency.

"They're here for you," she whispered. "Or for what you're carrying. Same thing, likely." She pulled him inside and threw the bar. "How long since the trail?"

"This morning. He died in my arms. Odile, he knew the roads, he knew the badge, he—"

"Hush." Her hand closed on his arm, thin as a bird's leg and hard as a trap. From the front room came the sound of the door knocker, polite and patient, and Tam's footsteps going to answer it, and Rafael realized with a lurch that the inquisitors weren't leaving. They were settling in. The woman's voice drifted through the stone, mild and courteous, asking whether the household had seen anyone on the high trails these past days, anyone hurt, anyone hurrying.

Questions no traveler should ask.

Odile looked at him across the dimness of the passage, and Rafael watched forty years of command hold steady on her face while something underneath it—the cough, the winter, the knowledge—cracked quietly through. She held out her hand.

"Give it to me," she said. "Whatever he gave you. Give it to me, and go out the back, and don't stop until the river."

Rafael's hand rose halfway to his coat. Stopped.

Because the dead man's voice was still in his ears, gentle and ruined: *keep it buried. Whatever they offer.* And because Odile's eyes, for the first time in all the years he'd known her, looked afraid.

Outside, the polite knocking came again.
