## 4. The Gray Warrant

The river came into view an hour before the town did, a gray ribbon unspooling between hills the color of old pewter, and Serah Vane watched it from the window of the post-chaise with the particular attention she gave to all things: complete, unhurried, and without affection.

Lowmark was a ferry town. That was why she had come by road instead of riding straight through the pass; that was why the chaise stopped at the crest of the last hill and let her look. Towns like this lived and died by their crossings—by who passed over the water, who paid for it, who remembered it. A town built on crossings learned to see everything and report nothing. It was, in her experience, the second most dangerous kind of place in the world.

The first kind was a place where someone had something to hide.

She unfolded the warrant again, though she knew its contents to the letter. She always read documents twice before acting on them, and then once more at the moment of use, because precision was not a habit but a discipline, and disciplines eroded the moment one decided they were unnecessary. The parchment was heavy stock, gray-edged, stamped with the double seal of the Office of Inquiry: the crown above, the open eye below. The text was brief. Gray warrants always were. Brevity was part of their function.

*Let it be known that Serah Vane, Inquisitor of the Third Chair, carries full authority within the province of Thornfell to seize, detain, question, compel, and if need be execute, in pursuit of matters touching the security of the realm. No local office may countermand her. No appeal lies against her acts.*

Any means. The words were older than she was, older than her superiors, written in an age when the Office had understood something modern clerks preferred to forget: that law was not a net. A net caught what blundered into it. Law, properly wielded, was a blade—and a blade did not apologize to what it cut.

Vane folded the warrant away and knocked once on the roof of the chaise.

* * *

Lowmark smelled of wet wood, fish scales, and tar. The riverside quarter clung to the bank like something half-drowned, houses on stilts leaning together for comfort, walkways slick with morning mist off the water. The upper town, where the merchants and the magistrate lived, sat higher up the slope behind a palisade that had never once been tested, and looked down on the river district the way comfortable people always look down on the people who make their comfort possible.

Vane did not go to the magistrate first.

This surprised her escort—a clerk named Pellis, a thin young man with ink-stained fingers and the nervous competence of someone promoted past his judgment—but she did not explain herself, because explaining was a habit of people who needed agreement. She went instead to the ferry landing itself, stood on the worn planks while the mist burned off the water, and watched.

Watching was the whole of the craft, though no one believed it. Interrogators thought the work began when the questions began. It did not. It began here, alone, looking at a thing until it told you what it was. The ferry landing told her a great deal in twenty minutes. It told her the ferry ran twice daily across the river and once along it, upriver to the mill landing. It told her the ferryman was a widower—the single chair on his porch, the one coat mended at both elbows, the grave-goods niche beside the door holding a woman's dried flowers gone to dust. It told her he drank, but not yet badly: two empty bottles in the scrap bin, spaced days apart by the weathering of the labels. And it told her he was afraid, because a man who is not afraid sweeps his porch in the evening and leaves the broom leaning by the door overnight, and this man's broom was inside, behind a locked door, where a broom is only kept by someone who has begun to imagine intruders.

Someone had already spoken to him. Or frightened him. Or both.

"Pellis," she said. "The magistrate's name."

"Ostrec, ma'am. Harl Ostrec. He's held the seat eleven years, by the provincial rolls."

"Eleven years." She turned from the water. "Then he knows everyone on this river by their debts. We'll see him after. First, the ferryman."

* * *

Tam Brell, ferryman of Lowmark, was sixty if he was fifty-five, with hands like knotted rope and the flat, guarded eyes of a man who had spent thirty years taking strangers across water and had long ago stopped wondering about any of them. He met her on his porch because she did not ask him to come out—he simply found her there when he opened his door, standing with her hands folded, her gray traveling cloak beaded with mist, and the instinct to run died in him before it finished being born. Running from an inquisitor was a confession with legs.

"Master Brell." Her voice was level, pleasant, almost warm. "Thank you for receiving me."

"You didn't give me much choice, lady." His accent was river-flat, vowels shortened by generations of wind off the water. "You're the third one to come asking questions this week."

That was interesting. Vane let the interest show—not all of it, a measured quantity, the way one lets light under a door. "The third."

"Two men in plain coats, day before yesterday. Said they were tax assessors." He spat carefully over the rail, into the reeds. "Assessors don't ask about passengers. They ask about cargo. These asked about a man who crossed Tuesday last, and about whether anyone walked the north switchback trail, and about—" he hesitated, and she watched the hesitation the way a hawk watches a field mouse's pause, "—about whether I'd seen anything dead or dying on the high paths."

"And had you?"

Brell's jaw worked. Here it was, then. The moment every interrogation turned on, the hinge point, invisible to the subject and load-bearing as a keystone. He could lie. Most did, at first. But lying to an inquisitor was a debt, and debts compounded, and somewhere in the flat river-country of his mind Tam Brell knew it.

"I saw a man carrying another man," he said finally. "Tuesday. Near dusk. Coming down off the north trail, along the ridge path. Carrying him like—" his hands made a shape in the air, awkward, "—like you carry a brother. Over the shoulders. The carried one wasn't moving. I called out, asked if they wanted the ferry, and the walking one said no. Said the crossing wasn't safe." A bitter sound, almost a laugh. "My crossing. Not safe. Thirty years I've run that boat."

"What did the walking man look like?"

"Young. Twenty, maybe twenty-five. Dark hair, cut short and growing out uneven, like he'd done it himself with a knife. Guild clothes—or what used to be guild clothes, the old mountain style, waxed canvas, patched too well to be poor. Patched *careful*, if you take my meaning." He rubbed his jaw. "Boots resoled twice. Rope burns on his palms. Climber. And he had that look."

"What look?"

"The look of a man doing arithmetic," Brell said. "Counting exits. I've ferried smugglers, deserters, runaway brides, one bishop in disguise. They all do the same sums. He was doing them while he talked to me."

Vane nodded slowly. "And the man he carried?"

"Never saw his face. Hood up, or head down. But the walking one laid him in the lee of the ferry shed while he bought bread and a skin of wine at Marda's stall—I saw that much—and when he shifted him, the carried man's arm hung down, and there was blood on the sleeve. Old blood, browned. And a ring." Brell frowned, dredging the memory. "A signet. Wrong hand for a poor man. Black stone in it."

"Did you tell the tax assessors all of this?"

"I told them some." His eyes slid sideways, and she let the silence do its work, three seconds, five, seven, until the silence grew heavier than the truth. "I told them enough to send them up the north trail. I didn't tell them about the ring, or about him coming back Wednesday morning and paying me triple fare to take him across at dawn, before the regular run. Alone. He came back across the river, lady, and he was alone, and he paid with a silver piece cut clean through the middle, and he said—" Brell stopped.

"Said what, Master Brell?"

"He said, 'If anyone asks, you never saw me.' And then he looked at me the way you look at a dog you might have to put down, and he said, 'But if they hurt you anyway, tell them everything. Don't die for me. I'm not worth it.'" The ferryman's voice had gone rough. "Thirty years on this water. Nobody ever said that to me before. Everybody wants you to lie for them. He's the first one ever told me not to."

Vane stood very still on the porch, and inside the stillness, she was working. A man who tells witnesses to betray him. Either the purest innocence she had encountered in nine years of service, or a sophistication so deep it circled back around to look like innocence. Both were possible. Both were dangerous. The Office taught that guilt wore a hundred faces and innocence only one, but the Office, in her private opinion, had never actually listened to anyone; it had only ever extracted from them.

"The assessors," she said. "Plain coats. Describe them."

"One was big. Soldier-built, close-cropped, moved like a drill yard. The other was small, soft, quick-eyed, did all the talking. They had a wagon with a false floor—I saw them load it, and nothing they put in it weighed what it should have."

Inquisitors' auxiliaries. Or worse—Office irregulars, the ones who worked ahead of warrants rather than behind them. So the machinery was already moving on multiple tracks, which meant someone above her was either careless or competing with her, and neither possibility improved her temper. She filed it. Filing things was most of survival in the Office.

"Master Brell," she said, "you are going to answer my questions completely now, and you are going to understand something first, so that your answers cost you nothing but breath." She drew the gray warrant from her cloak and held it where he could see the seals, and watched his face drain to the color of the river fog. "This authorizes me to do anything at all in the course of my inquiry. Do you understand what *anything* means? It means that if I decide tomorrow that you withheld something today, I can hang you from your own ferry beam and no court in the province will so much as open a file. It also means"—and here she softened her voice, and the softness was more frightening than the threat had been, because it sounded genuine, and it was—"that I have no need of cruelty. Cruelty is what people use when they lack authority. I have authority. So you will gain nothing from me by suffering, and lose nothing by honesty. Honesty is simply faster. Do we understand each other?"

"Aye," Brell whispered.

"Good. Now. The young man. When he returned Wednesday, did he say where he'd been? Did he mention a name? Odile? Corvin? The word *guild*? The word *crown*?"

"No names. He asked about the pass roads—which ones were watched, which weren't. Asked if the old toll stations were still manned. I told him nobody's manned the tolls since the war money dried up." Brell paused. "He laughed at that. Bad laugh. Like it hurt."

*The roads still matter to him,* Vane thought. *Even now. Even running.* She set the observation beside the others, in the ordered dark where she kept such things.

"Last question, Master Brell, and attend to it carefully. If the young man comes back through Lowmark—if he crosses your river again—what will you do?"

Brell looked at his own hands for a long moment. Then he looked up, and there was something in his face that was not fear anymore, or not only fear—something older and stonier, the thing that lived underneath fear in river people and mountain people and everyone the law had ever rolled over.

"I'll do what I did before, lady. I'll take his fare and keep my mouth shut till someone makes me open it. And when you make me—" he met her eyes, "—I'll tell you true, same as I'm telling you now. That's all I've got. It's not much. But it's mine."

Vane considered him for a moment longer than she needed to.

"That," she said quietly, "is considerably more than most people have." She left a coin on the porch rail—not payment, which would have insulted them both, but witness-money, the formal token that made his words testimony and bound him to them. "You are now a person of record in a matter of state. Guard yourself accordingly."

* * *

Magistrate Ostrec received her in a room too warm and too carpeted, with wine already poured, and confirmed in forty minutes what the porch had taken twenty to teach her: that Lowmark's official memory was a commodity, priced and sold. Yes, a stranger had died on the high trail—hunters found the body two days ago, stripped of anything valuable, buried shallow by the coroner's man. No, no inquiry had been opened; the dead man carried no papers. Yes, the two "assessors" had visited, and yes, Ostrec had lent them the town's rider to carry a message east. To whom?

"To the provincial archive annex at Candlemere," Ostrec said, sweating gently. "They had letters of introduction. Very fine letters."

Letters of introduction from an office that did not exist. Vane thanked him, declined the wine, and stood for a moment at his window looking down at the river, where the ferry was making its midday crossing, small and patient, carrying its anonymous cargo of lives.

Two maps of the board, then. Her warrant, slow and lawful and absolute. And whatever those two men served, moving faster, moving first, stripping bodies and sending messages east. The Office believed competition was corruption. Vane believed competition was information: someone, somewhere, was afraid of exactly what she was afraid of, and fear mapped the truth better than any dossier.

Pellis appeared at her elbow with her cloak. "Where to next, ma'am?"

Vane watched the ferry reach the far bank. Somewhere beyond those eastern ridges, a young man with knife-cut hair and rope-burned palms was doing his arithmetic, counting exits, carrying a dead man's secret the way he'd carried the dead man himself—over the shoulder, careful, too heavy.

"The north trail," she said. "Bring the spades. I want to see what our assessors left behind when they robbed that grave."

Because they had robbed it—she was certain of that now, certain the way one is certain of arithmetic. And if they had taken papers, they had missed something. People who rob graves in haste always miss something.

And Serah Vane had never once, in nine years, been in a hurry.
