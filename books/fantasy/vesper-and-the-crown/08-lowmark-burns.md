## 8. Lowmark Burns

Serah Vane had burned three towns in her career, and she had learned something on each occasion that the Gray Warrant never taught: fire was honest in a way that interrogation was not. A man on the rack would tell you anything—everything, nothing, whatever stopped the pain. But a town set alight told you the truth of itself. It showed you which doors were barred from within. Which cellars held more than turnips. Which neighbors ran toward each other's houses and which ran alone for the hills.

Lowmark was telling her its truths now.

She stood on the ferry landing with her hands clasped behind her back, gray cloak stirring in the heat-draft, and watched the riverside quarter catch. The buildings there were old—older than the province, some of them, their foundations laid when Thornfell still belonged to no crown but its own—and they burned slowly, sullenly, the way old things do. Smoke rolled up the valley walls in a gray tide, and above it the morning sun hung pale as a coin rubbed blind.

"Quarter's clearing, Inquisitor," said Brannoc, her lieutenant, at her shoulder. He was a big man with a soft voice, which people always mistook for kindness until they saw his work. "Forty-one souls out so far. Six resisting."

"Resisting how?"

"One threw a fish-knife at Tallow. Another tried to swim the river with his strongbox."

"And?"

"Drowned, most like. The strongbox is on its way to you."

Vane did not answer at once. She watched a woman in a green shawl drag a trunk into the street, then abandon it, then go back for it, then stand over it weeping while the roof behind her began to glow. The woman was not looking at her burning house. She was looking down the road, north, toward the pass—the way a stranger had come down that road eleven days ago, bleeding, and been carried the rest of the way by a young man with river-mud on his boots.

The ferryman had described him. Young, dark-haired, quick-eyed, moved like someone who knew every stone in the valley. The ferryman had also lied about it, twice, before Vane's methods loosened his tongue. She remembered the sound he'd made when the truth finally came out of him—not a confession so much as a surrender, the way a door gives when its hinges are pulled.

"The map," she said quietly, to no one, to the smoke. "Where is the map."

* * *

Three days earlier, she had arrived in Lowmark with a gray warrant folded inside her coat and six inquisitors at her back, and the town had received her the way towns always did: with bread, with wine, with too many smiles. The reeve had met her at the bridge with a speech about Lowmark's loyalty to the Crown, and Vane had listened with her head tilted, the way one listens to a song whose words one already knows are false.

"You've had a death in your valley," she'd said when he finished. "A stranger. Found dead on the high trail near the switchbacks. Carried down by one of your own."

The reeve's face had done something small and fast—a flicker, gone in half a heartbeat, but Vane had spent twenty years learning to read faces the way scholars read manuscripts, and she caught it. Grief? No. Guilt. The guilt of a man who has already decided what story he will tell and fears you know another.

"A traveler," the reeve said. "Fell. Bad weather on the heights. We buried him."

"Show me the grave."

They walked to it together through a cold drizzle, the whole town pretending not to watch from windows and doorways. The grave was neat. Too neat. Fresh-turned earth, a marker of planed wood with careful lettering. Vane crouched beside it and pressed two fingers into the soil, and the soil came away wrong—too loose, too shallow, turned recently from below.

"Dig," she said.

"Inquisitor—" the reeve began.

"Dig him up, or I will have your town dig him up and bury you with him. The warrant permits either."

They dug. And what came out of that grave was not the stranger. It was a sack of stones wrapped in a burial shroud, and beneath it, deeper, hastily hidden, a bundle of papers: rebel tracts, Ashen Circle seals, correspondence between Lowmark's ferryman and parties unknown in the eastern hills. The town had found a dying man carrying treason and had chosen—consciously, collectively, in the way small places do—to hide him rather than hand him to the law.

Or rather, to hide what he carried. The body itself was gone. Moved. And the young man who had carried him down from the trail was gone too, vanished into the passes the way water vanishes into limestone.

That night Vane had sat in the ferryman's boathouse with the man himself, an old river-rat named Colm with hands like knotted rope, and she had asked him questions in a voice so gentle he wept before she touched him at all. He told her about the stranger. About the young man—*Whitlock, they call him Rafe, he's Guild, or what's left of the Guild*—about the wax-sealed packet passed hand to hand on the landing like a christening. He told her about the seal, and this was the thing that made Vane stop pacing and sit down: the stranger had made him swear, if ever anyone came asking, to say the seal was black wax with a thorn sigil.

"He made me swear to lie about it," Colm had whispered, blood at the corner of his mouth, eyes pleading. "Said if folk knew it was royal red, plain royal red with the old crest, they'd—"

"—they'd know what he'd stolen," Vane finished. "And who would come for it."

She had left him alive that night. She remembered thinking, with a detachment that even then struck her as strange, that she ought not to have. The warrant authorized any means. The warrant did not authorize mercy, exactly—but it did not forbid it, and mercy was the one indulgence Serah Vane allowed herself, sparingly, the way a miser allows himself one coin spent foolishly per year.

She was regretting it now. Because Colm had talked since then—to someone. Word had reached the eastern hills; riders had been seen; and yesterday one of her outriders had found a dead crow nailed to the ferry post with an ash-leaf in its beak, the Ashen Circle's little signature, their way of saying *we know you're here.*

Two hunts, one quarry. The thought did not frighten her. It focused her, the way a whetstone focuses an edge.

* * *

By midmorning the riverside quarter was fully given to flame, and Vane walked its edges with her hands still clasped behind her, cataloguing.

This was the part of the work that the Ministry's manuals called *pressure application* and that the people it happened to called other things. You did not burn a town because you believed the map was in it. You burned a town because the map was *not* in it, and you wanted everyone within a hundred miles to understand what concealment cost. Fire was a sentence written in a language every reader understood without schooling. Somewhere in those hills, a young man with mud on his boots would hear that Lowmark's riverside was ash, and he would do the arithmetic. One quarter. Forty families. For a roll of wax-sealed paper. And he would ask himself whether he could carry that number, and whichever way he answered, he would move—and moving men leave tracks.

"Inquisitor." Brannoc again, at her elbow, his voice lowered. "The ferryman. What would you have us do with him?"

Colm stood where they'd put him, at the base of the ferry post, wrists bound behind him, flanked by two of her men. Around him, at a distance no one had ordered but everyone maintained, the displaced of Lowmark watched—wet-eyed, silent, huddled under the eaves of the granary with their salvaged belongings. Among them Vane saw the woman in the green shawl, and she saw something in the woman's face that she had seen before, in other towns, and always recognized too late to matter: not fear. Hatred, taking root.

"He passed messages for the Circle," Brannoc said. "He concealed evidence. He lied to a warranted officer of the Crown. Under the statute—"

"I know the statute, Lieutenant. I wrote the annotation they teach it from."

Brannoc waited. That was the thing about Brannoc; he never pushed. He simply stood adjacent to your decision like a wall you might lean on, and let you discover that leaning felt like falling.

Vane looked at the old man. His knees were going; she could see it, the fine tremor in his legs, an old man's body betraying him the way old bodies do. He was looking at the river. Not at her—at the river, at the brown water sliding past the landing where he'd worked fifty years, where he'd ferried her across herself on her first day in the valley, chatting amiably about the trout, lying to her with every second breath and she'd known it and let him think he'd gotten away with it because she wanted to see how far he'd run.

How far he'd run was to the eastern hills, with word of a red seal and a dead archivist, and now there were ash-leaves on her posts and riders in her periphery, and the quarry she'd spent eleven days cornering had slipped into terrain she did not control.

Justice, she thought. This is what justice looks like when it leaves the capital. It looks like an old man at a post and a crowd learning what the law weighs.

"Hang him," she said.

Brannoc nodded, unsurprised, and gave the order in his soft voice, and the machinery of it moved around her—ropes fetched, the ferry beam tested, Colm lifted bodily because his legs would no longer hold the pretense of standing. Vane remained where she was. She did not look away. That was her discipline, the one she was proudest of and the one that frightened her subordinates most: she watched everything the Crown did in her name, every hanging, every burning, every broken man carried out of her questioning rooms. If it was to be done, it would be done in front of her eyes, and she would carry the sight of it, all of it, stacked like cordwood, until the day she died.

Colm said something at the end. The crowd was too far and the river too loud, and only the man holding the rope heard it, and later, when Vane asked him, he wouldn't repeat it. She suspected it was a curse. She suspected, less comfortably, that it was a message—for her, or for whoever the old man still believed was listening from the hills.

The beam groaned. The crowd made a sound like a single indrawn breath and then nothing at all, and the silence afterward was worse than any screaming Vane had ever heard, because screaming meant people still believed someone might listen.

She turned back to the fire.

* * *

It was Brannoc who saw the figure first, near dusk, when the riverside quarter had burned down to its bones and the smoke had thinned enough to show the ridgeline north of the valley.

"There," he said. "Inquisitor. On the shoulder of the crag. Watching."

Vane took the spyglass from her saddlebag and raised it, and the world leapt close: black rock, gray smoke, and against them a single human shape, small with distance, motionless. Young. Dark-haired. Standing in the open with no attempt at cover, which told her more than a week of tracking could have. Men who hid were running. Men who stood in the open on a ridgeline above a burning town were *learning* something, and what they learned determined everything they did next.

She held the glass on him for a long time. She had the strangest sensation, looking at that distant figure, that he was looking back—that across four miles of smoke and evening air, their attention had met and locked, two instruments tuned to the same frequency. She thought about the report in her dispatch case. *Subject observed. Subject located.* She thought about how easy it would be, with another hour of light, to put riders into the high passes and make this whole campaign end tonight.

She lowered the glass.

"No," she said.

"Inquisitor?"

"He wants us to chase him. Look at where he's standing—open ground, single line of retreat, no pack. That's a lure or a farewell, and I don't gamble on which." She handed the glass back to Brannoc and looked north one last time with her naked eye, at the smudge of a figure on the ridge, already fading as the light died. "Post the reward descriptions in every market from here to the coast. Whitlock. Rafael Whitlock. And send a rider to the Ministry—I want the archives opened on the Vesper Guild. All of it. Everything back to the founding."

"The founding, Inquisitor? That's two hundred years of—"

"That's exactly why I want it." She turned away from the ridge, from the smoke, from the green-shawled woman's hatred and the swinging beam and the arithmetic of forty families, and she found that her hands, clasped behind her back, had gone very cold. "Our dead stranger carried a royal seal two centuries out of use, and a smuggler's apprentice carried him down a mountain, and a rebel cell is circling this valley like gulls after a wake. Whatever the Sunken Crown is, Lieutenant, it isn't treasure. Treasure doesn't make everyone who touches it lie."

Behind her, Lowmark's riverside settled into embers with a sound like slow applause.

On the ridgeline, the figure was gone. But Vane stood a moment longer in the cooling dark, looking at the empty place where he had been, and for the first time in twenty years of service she felt the warrant inside her coat not as authority but as weight—as though the paper itself were watching her, waiting to see what she would become before this was finished.

Then she gave the order to march, and the valley, and the fire, and the dead man on the beam, were all behind her—except none of them were, and she knew it, and the knowing went with her up the north road like a passenger who had paid for the whole journey.

* * *

Far above, on the shoulder of the crag, Rafael Whitlock stood until the last inquisitor torchlight dropped below the tree line, and then he turned to Odile, who leaned on his arm with blood seeping slow and dark through the bandage at her side, and said, in a voice scraped raw by smoke:

"She'll open the Guild archives. Everything. Odile—there are things in those archives, aren't there. Things you haven't told me."

Odile looked down at the burning valley for a long moment, and when she answered, her voice was quieter than the wind.

"Worse than things, boy. There's a name."
