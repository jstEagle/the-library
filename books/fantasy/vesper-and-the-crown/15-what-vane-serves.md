## 15. What Vane Serves

The order came at dusk, written in the Provost's own hand and sealed with the gray wax that meant no appeal, no review, no mercy.

*Execute the smuggler. Seal the archive. Burn what cannot be carried. Report by courier within three days.*

Serah Vane read it twice by lamplight in the monastery's upper scriptorium, where the Ashen Circle's heresy lay catalogued in rows around her like a patient disease. Below, through the broken floor of the nave, she could hear her inquisitors moving among the shelves—boots on stone, the scrape of crates, the methodical industry of an empire erasing a room. Somewhere down there, too, was Rafael Whitlock, wrists bound with cord, sitting against a pillar under the guard of Brother Cassel, who had once wept at his own induction oath and had never wept since.

Vane set the order down and looked at her hands.

They were steady. They were always steady. Twenty-two years in the Inquisition, from the reeking cells of Lowmark to the frozen assizes of the northern circuit, and her hands had never shaken—not when she'd signed the ferryman's death warrant, not when she'd watched him swing from the customs bridge while the river ran brown beneath him. She had told herself then, as she told herself now, that the law was a blade, and a blade did not tremble. A blade that trembled cut its bearer.

So why, she wondered, did the Provost's handwriting look different tonight?

It was the same hand it had always been—precise, upright, economical. The same hand that had authorized Lowmark. The same hand that had signed the gray warrant pressed into her palm two years ago with the words *any means, Serah, because the alternative is worse*. She had believed him then. She believed him now. That was the trouble. Belief was the one thing they could never take from her, and tonight it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because she had seen the archive.

She had spent four hours in it before the order arrived, and she had done what the Inquisition trained her to do: read everything. The Ashen Circle had been hoarding more than maps. There were tax rolls from the last years of the old king's reign, requisition ledgers from the northern provinces, grain tallies that showed entire districts stripped to nothing in the winter of the Great Famine—stripped *by royal order*, the seals still intact, the king's own cipher pressed into the wax beside the words *let them eat bark*. There were letters from provincial governors begging for relief and receiving, in reply, orders to raise levies for a war the provinces themselves were starving through. And there—wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the deepest shelf—a surgeon's field report from Thornfell itself, dated the year before the king's execution, describing the pacification of the northern valleys in language so clinical it might have been about clearing brush.

*Villages of approximately forty souls. Resistance negligible after the first example. Recommend continuation through spring.*

Two centuries. Two centuries of songs about the martyr king, the shepherd of the people, the crown torn from a righteous brow by tyrant usurpers—and the rebels sang it, and the Crown tolerated it, and both sides built their altars on the same grave. And all the while, in sealed rooms in the capital, men like the Provost had known. Had known and filed it away. Had stamped it *restricted* and locked it in vaults and gone on burning villages of their own in the map's name, because the legend was useful, and truth was not.

Vane had served the Crown for twenty-two years because she believed the Crown was the only thing standing between the mountain folk and the fire. Order against chaos. Law against the knife. She had watched what happened when law failed—she had grown up in a river town where the magistrates were bought and the strong did as they pleased, and she had sworn, at fourteen, over her father's body, that she would be on the side of the rope rather than the side of the mob.

And now the rope belonged to the mob's masters, and the law had known about the massacres for decades, and the Provost wanted her to kill a smuggler who had risked his life to carry the proof into daylight.

*Execute the smuggler.*

She picked up the lamp and went downstairs.

* * *

Rafael Whitlock looked smaller than Vane remembered from the ridgeline above Lowmark—the day the riverside quarter burned and she had stood in the smoke telling herself it was necessary. He sat slumped against the pillar, dark-haired, hollow-eyed, the cord cutting red lines into his wrists. His coat was scorched along one sleeve from the archive's collapse tunnel; he'd led them to the second map through passages no imperial survey had ever charted, and he'd done it without being asked, without bargaining, which had bothered Vane more than any bargain would have.

Cassel straightened when he saw her. "Provost's courier came through the north postern," he said quietly. "I take it—"

"You take it I'll handle it."

"Serah." He lowered his voice. "I've known you eleven years. You've never once asked to handle a sentence alone."

"That's why I'm asking now."

He studied her face for a long moment, and whatever he saw there made him uneasy, but he was Cassel, and Cassel obeyed. He unhooked the lantern from the pillar and walked back up the nave, leaving them alone among the heretic shelves.

Vane crouched, drew her belt knife, and cut the cord from Rafael's wrists. Then she put the point of the knife against his throat instead, because she was not yet ready to know what she was going to do, and the knife kept the question honest.

Whitlock didn't flinch. That, too, bothered her. Most men flinched.

"You've read it," he said. "The stone. The letters. All of it."

"I've read it."

"And the order is to kill me and seal it."

"The order is to kill you and seal it."

"So why am I still breathing?"

Vane turned the knife slowly, watching the lamplight run along the edge. "Because I'm deciding whether you're worth the treason."

"I'm not." He said it flatly, without self-pity. "I'm a smuggler with a dead guild and a head full of another man's ghosts. I'm not worth anything. But the stone down there—" he nodded toward the depths of the monastery, toward the vault where the carved confession waited in the dark, "—that's worth ten thousand of me. And you know it. That's why your hands are doing that thing they're doing."

Vane looked down. Her hands were perfectly still.

"They're not doing anything," she said.

"No," Whitlock agreed softly. "But you want them to."

Silence filled the nave, the kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. Above them the broken roof let in a slice of evening sky, going violet at the edges, and a single star stood in it like a nail.

"My father was hanged by a magistrate who took bribes," Vane heard herself say. She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way blood comes out—because something underneath had been cut. "I joined the Inquisition because I thought the law was the opposite of that man. I have burned towns, Whitlock. I hanged a ferryman whose worst crime was rowing you across a river. I told myself every time: the alternative is worse. Chaos is worse. The fire is worse. And now I find out the men who sign my warrants have been sitting on proof that the whole war—the whole *war*—was built on a lie they chose to keep, because the lie was cheaper than the truth." She paused. "Tell me what the alternative is. Tell me, because I genuinely no longer know."

Whitlock was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough, tired, and utterly without performance.

"There isn't one," he said. "That's the thing nobody wants to hear. There's no clean side. My guild hid that crown for two hundred years thinking we were protecting the world from a relic. Turns out we were protecting the world from a *confession*, and we didn't even know it. Odile died for it. A stranger died carrying it. Marrow will raise an army on the pretty half of the story, and your Provost will burn anyone who tells the ugly half, and everyone in between just wants the roads open and their children fed." He shrugged, and the motion pulled the raw skin of his wrists, and he winced. "All I know is this: a lie that needs killing to survive isn't order. It's just a longer fire."

Vane stood. She sheathed the knife.

Then she took the gray warrant from inside her coat, held it over the lamp flame, and watched the Provost's seal curl and blacken into ash that drifted down between them like dirty snow.

"Tactical decision," she said, and her voice was steady, because her voice would always be steady even if her hands learned to shake. "You're more useful alive. A dead smuggler is a closed file. A live one is a thread I can pull whenever I need to. Remember that, Whitlock—that's the only reason you're walking out of here. Not sentiment. Tactics."

"Of course," said Rafael Whitlock gravely. "Tactics."

"Don't make me regret—" She stopped. Started again. "The west postern gate is unguarded until the third watch. Cassel thinks I'm interrogating you. Take both maps. Take the road ledger if you have it. Get ahead of Marrow's army, because I've read his dispatches and he'll reach the old capital within the fortnight, and when he does, he'll put a crown on his own head before the ink on his proclamation dries."

"And you? What will you report?"

Vane looked at the ash of the warrant on the floor, and at the shelves of forbidden truth rising around them in the dark, and at the star nailed in the broken roof.

"I'll report the archive sealed," she said. "Every word true. Sealed." She met his eyes. "Sealed behind my seal, with my garrison, under my authority—which means, Whitlock, that from tonight, the truth has a jailer who's read it. Go. Before I remember what I serve."

He went—up the aisle, quick and silent as the smuggler he was, pausing once at the shattered doors to look back. She expected gratitude, or a quip, or some final piece of smuggler's philosophy. Instead he simply nodded to her, the way one professional acknowledges another across a dangerous table, and was gone into the blue dusk.

Vane stood alone in the nave for a long time.

Then she climbed to the scriptorium, took up the Provost's order, and began composing her report in the precise, upright hand she had learned to trust more than any living soul: *Archive located. Heretical materials secured. The smuggler was executed at dusk.* She wrote the lie in perfect form, and beneath it, in a private cipher only she and the Provost shared, she added a line that was also true, though he would not understand it for weeks:

*What we buried is not what we were told we buried. Come see for yourself—or send someone you trust more than you trust me.*

She blotted the page, sealed it, and gave it to the courier with instructions to ride through the night.

Below her, the archive waited in the dark, sealed behind her word. Ahead of her, somewhere east, an army marched on an empty throne carrying a crown and a lie. And somewhere between them, a smuggler ran through the mountains with two maps and a dead man's memories, carrying the only honest thing left in Thornfell.

Vane banked the lamps, checked her garrison's watch rotations, and allowed herself—for the first time in twenty-two years—to admit that she no longer knew which side of the rope she was on.

Only that, come morning, she intended to find out.
