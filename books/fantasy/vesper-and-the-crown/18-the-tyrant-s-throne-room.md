## 18. The Tyrant's Throne Room

The last of Corvin's memories spent itself like a candle guttering in a draft, and Rafael descended into the dark alone.

That was the cruelty of it. For weeks the dead man had walked beside them—his hands on their hands, his fear in their chest, his knowledge arriving unbidden like weather—and now, at the very threshold of everything he had died to protect, the bond thinned to almost nothing. Rafael felt him the way one feels a word on the tip of the tongue: present, insistent, and just out of reach.

*Left at the third pillar,* Corvin's voice murmured, faint as breath on a window. *Mind the water.*

Rafael minded the water.

It came down through the ruined capital in threads and ropes, finding every crack the centuries had opened in the old palace's bones, gathering in black pools that reflected nothing. The smuggler's roads from Odile's ledger had brought them through the mountain's underbelly—three days of crawling through passages no royal surveyor had ever charted, passages the Vesper Guild had kept secret for two hundred years precisely so that no one would ever do what Rafael was doing now. The ledger's routes ended here, at a seam in the rock where the stone had been cut by hands rather than water, and beyond it lay the Sunken Crown.

Rafael stood at the seam with a shuttered lantern and listened.

Above, muffled by a hundred feet of earth and ruin, Marrow's army was making camp. The Ashen Circle had come to the old capital in numbers that had shocked even Dain Marrow's own officers—ten thousand pilgrims, soldiers, opportunists, and true believers, singing the restoration hymns around fires that turned the night sky the color of an open wound. Rafael had watched them arrive from a ridgeline that morning, Corvin's fading memory supplying names for the banners: the Broken Wheel, the Nine Stars, the Weeping King in gold thread. All of it built on a story. All of it waiting for a throne.

And somewhere below all of it, in the dark where Rafael now stood, was whatever truth the Crown actually held.

*Third pillar,* the ghost said again, softer. *Then down.*

* * *

The passage beyond the seam was royal work, and it was wrong in ways Rafael could not immediately name. The walls were dressed stone, fitted without mortar, each block bearing the chisel-marks of craftsmen who had expected their work to be seen. But there were no reliefs. No heraldry. No inscription anywhere in the long descent—not so much as a mason's mark. Two hundred years ago this palace had been the most ornamented building in the world; travelers' accounts spoke of corridors where every surface sang with the king's victories. This stair had been stripped bare, or never dressed at all, and the silence of the walls pressed against Rafael like deep water.

Corvin would have known why. Rafael reached for him and caught only fragments—a smell of ink, a woman's voice saying *you can't bury what's written in stone, Corvin, you can only decide who gets to read it*—and then nothing, the memory dissolving before its meaning arrived.

The air changed. It grew colder, then warmer, then strange—a mineral sweetness, like rain on hot slate. The lantern flame leaned sideways in a current of moving air, which meant openings ahead, which meant the great vaulted spaces beneath the ruins where, according to every account Rafael had ever heard, the floodwaters of the War had drowned the palace entire.

The accounts were wrong. Or they had been made wrong.

The stair opened onto a gallery, and the gallery opened onto darkness so complete that the lantern seemed to shrink inside it. Rafael raised the light and understood, slowly, what they were looking at: a hall vast enough to swallow the whole of Lowmark, its ceiling lost above the reach of the beam, its floor dry as bone. Dry. The flood that had "drowned" the Sunken Crown—the flood every history book named as God's own verdict on the executed king—had never touched this place. Someone had drained it. Someone had kept it dry. Someone had maintained, in secret, across two centuries, a lie about water.

The Vesper Guild's first ledgers, Odile had once said, were written in a hand none of her predecessors could read. Rafael thought of that now, standing in the impossible dryness, and felt the shape of the whole conspiracy settle over them like a coat that had always been theirs to wear. The Guild hadn't guarded treasure. The Guild had guarded a room.

At the far end of the hall, doors.

They were bronze, taller than three men, and unlike the naked walls behind, they were covered—chased from edge to edge with figures. Rafael crossed the floor slowly, boots silent on dust that had never been swept because no one was ever meant to walk here, and held the lantern up to the metal.

The figures were not victories.

They were columns of people. Hundreds of them, thousands, rendered small and precise, marching in lines that wrapped around the doors and continued—Rafael followed them—onto the walls flanking the entrance, where the bronze gave way to carved stone and the columns of people became something else. They became ditches. They became pits. They became, carved with a realism that made Rafael's stomach turn two hundred years after the chisel cooled, bodies laid in rows by other bodies holding shovels.

Northern faces. The carver had given them northern faces—the high cheekbones, the braided hair, the distinctive collars of the Thornfell provinces that had risen in rebellion and been put down, according to every schoolbook in the kingdom, by a righteous king defending his realm against foreign agents.

There were no foreign agents anywhere in the carving. There was only the king's own army, and the king's own people, and the ditches.

Beneath the relief, in letters cut deep enough to be read by touch, ran a single line of old script. Corvin surfaced one last time—not a memory this time but something like a hand on Rafael's shoulder, steadying them—and translated it in a voice that was almost his own:

*I wrote it so that no one could say I did not know what I did.*

* * *

The doors were not locked. They had not been locked in two hundred years, because locks invite force, and whoever built this place had wanted no force used here—only reading. They swung inward at Rafael's touch on hinges that had been oiled within living memory, and the lantern light went ahead of them into the throne room of Aldric the Martyr, the Weeping King, the Father of the Restoration.

It was smaller than Rafael expected. That was the first thing. After the vastness of the hall, the chamber beyond was almost intimate—a round room perhaps forty feet across, domed, its acoustics perfect, so that Rafael's own breathing came back to them softly from every direction. No gold. No jewels. The throne itself sat at the center on a low dais, plain oak, its gilt long since peeled away, and it was not elevated on steps as thrones are but set level with the floor, as if the room itself refused to raise anyone above anyone else.

Every inch of wall was covered in writing.

Not decoration. Not scripture. Writing—dense, deliberate, carved in the same deep letters as the line above the doors, running in courses from floor to dome like the rings of a tree. Rafael walked the perimeter with the lantern and read, and as they read, the awful architecture of the place assembled itself in their mind: this was not a confession hidden away. This was a confession *built*. A man had ordered this room constructed, had commissioned its carvers, had dictated its text, and had then sealed it beneath a lie about a flood, and Rafael understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that the king had wanted it found. Someday. By someone. He had simply lacked the courage to say it aloud while he lived, and so he had said it in stone instead, and then let two centuries of martyrs be built on top of his voice.

The opening passage stood at shoulder height beside the door, and Rafael read it twice, lips moving, while the lantern trembled in their grip.

*I am Aldric, second of that name, and I write this in the forty-first year of my reign, being in health of body and full possession of my mind, so that no flatterer may soften it after me. What follows is mine. I did it. Let no priest absolve me, no chronicler excuse me, and no heir inherit the comfort of my innocence.*

Then the courses of text began, and they did not soften.

He described the northern grain requisitions of his thirty-third year, when the harvest failed and he ordered the quotas enforced anyway because the southern lords' loyalty was purchased with northern bread. He described the winter that followed. He did not use euphemisms. He listed the towns—Harrowmere, Cold Bracken, the nine villages of the Vell—gave numbers that made Rafael sit down on the cold floor with their back against the carved wall, numbers that historians had disputed for generations because no record survived, and here was the record, cut in granite by the order of the only man who knew. Eleven thousand. He had counted. He had made his stewards count, and he had checked their arithmetic, and he had written the total here because, he said, *a number unwritten is a number that can become anything. Written, it can only ever be eleven thousand.*

He described the executions that sparked the Thornfell rising—not as tyranny's excess but as policy, calculated, minuted, signed. He described the war itself, the burning of the passes, the things done in the name of order that he recorded with a precision that read, at times, like a man taking inventory of his own damnation. And near the end, in a hand the carvers had rendered slightly larger, slightly deeper, as if the words themselves needed more room:

*When they take me, they will make me a saint, because saints are useful and dead men cannot object. My enemies will make me a devil, because devils are also useful. Both will be lies, and both will serve men who never saw Harrowmere. I cannot prevent the lies. I can only leave this, in the dark, for whoever comes asking. If you have found this room, then someone kept the way for you. Thank them. They will have paid for it.*

Rafael thought of Odile. Of four members, of a dying woman in a waystation, of two hundred years of smugglers who had never known what they carried, carrying it anyway.

They knelt there a long time. The lantern burned low. Somewhere overhead, faint as a heartbeat through the stone, the army sang.

* * *

It was the silence that finally moved them—the realization that Corvin had gone entirely quiet, that for the first time since the switchback Rafael's head held only their own thoughts. The bond had spent itself completely, here, at the end of the road it had been keeping. Rafael felt the absence like a missing tooth, the tongue returning again and again to the gap.

But the work remained. Corvin had stolen a map, betrayed his cell, crossed half the kingdom wounded and hunted, and died whispering *the Crown remembers*—and the Crown remembered exactly this. Not glory. Not a relic to rally behind. A room of numbers. Rafael took out the charcoal and the sheets of rag paper Odile had packed for them years ago, against a need neither of them had imagined, and began to copy.

They worked by method, the way the Guild taught: headings, sections, cross-references, the totals boxed and initialed. The massacres first, town by town, number by number. Then the war. Then the passages about the lies to come—those especially, because those were the ones Marrow's army would need to hear in the king's own voice. Rafael copied until their fingers were black, until the lantern had burned down to its last finger of oil, until the dome above rang faintly with a sound that was not the army's singing.

Boots. Many boots, on stone, far above, descending through galleries that should have been impassable.

Rafael snuffed the lantern and stood in the perfect dark, listening, and understood with terrible simplicity how it had happened: the incomplete map. Marrow had the duplicate, and the duplicate had been good enough to find the mountain, and ten thousand men with picks and faith could open any door that patience could not. Corvin's bond had died too soon to warn them—or perhaps, Rafael thought, with the strange calm of exhaustion, it had died having done all it ever could. The rest was Rafael's alone to carry.

Light bloomed in the great hall beyond the bronze doors—torchlight, dozens of torches, throwing the carved ditches and the carved dead into leaping shadow. Voices echoed, awed and hungry. And above them all, pitched to carry, a voice Rafael knew from a hundred rebel broadsheets, warm and certain and utterly sincere:

"Careful with the doors! Careful, brothers—what waits beyond them belongs to all of us!"

Dain Marrow had come to claim his crown, and between him and the truth stood one smuggler, a sheaf of charcoal copies, and a room full of stone that could hang him.
