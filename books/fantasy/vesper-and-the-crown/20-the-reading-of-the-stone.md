## 20. The Reading of the Stone

Serah Vane had given orders for executions before. She had signed them, supervised them, and once—early in her career, in a coastal town whose name she had trained herself to forget—carried one out personally when the local magistrate's nerve failed. She knew the sound a crowd made when it watched a death: the held breath, the small wet collective flinch, the silence afterward that was never really silence but the sound of ten thousand people deciding not to think.

What she heard now, standing at the mouth of the Sunken Crown's approach with forty inquisitors at her back and torchlight rolling over an army, was something else entirely.

Marrow's voice still hung in the air. *A traitor of the old Guild.* He stood on the steps of the buried palace with a crown of black iron raised above his head, and Rafael Whitlock knelt before him between two soldiers, a knife resting against the smuggler's throat, and the army roared its approval like a tide coming in.

Vane's hand was already raised. The order was simple. Forty crossbows. A volley into the front ranks, a second into the confusion, and by dawn the Ashen Circle would be a stain on the mountain and the Crown's authority would be restored by the oldest argument there was. Her superiors had written the warrant themselves. *Any means necessary.* She had carried gray warrants for eleven years and had never once hesitated to use them.

*Report the archive sealed,* her orders said. *Execute the bearer.*

She looked at Rafael Whitlock kneeling in the dirt, and what she saw was not a smuggler or a traitor but a person who had run through the dark for weeks carrying something heavier than a map—a person who had come down into the earth alone, ahead of an army, for reasons Vane still did not fully understand. And behind her, in the archive beneath the monastery, lay the letters she had found with her own hands: her superiors' correspondence, decades of it, discussing the northern massacres in the dry language of administrative inconvenience. *The Thornfell question.* *The provincial settlements.* They had known. The Crown had known since before Vane was born, and it had built its peace on the grave of the truth, and it had sent her here to keep the grave shut.

Her hand stayed raised. The forty crossbows stayed leveled. And Vane stood in the space between two commands—the one on the paper in her coat and the one rising in her own chest—and discovered, with a strange clinical detachment, that she did not know which one she was going to obey.

* * *

Rafael's knees ached against cold stone. The knife at their throat was warm from the soldier's grip, and Marrow's shadow fell across them, long and triumphant in the torchlight.

"You could have been part of this," Marrow said, and his voice carried the way voices carry in vast underground spaces, multiplied by stone until it seemed to come from everywhere. "The Guild kept secrets for tyrants. You could have helped us bury them forever."

*He believes it,* Rafael thought, and the thought was almost pity. Corvin's memories were nearly gone now—spent like a candle burned to its socket—but one last echo surfaced, faint as breath on glass: *Dain was always best at believing what he needed.* Rafael lifted their eyes to the army, ten thousand faces lit orange and wavering, faces that had marched for days on a promise, faces that wanted so badly for the dead king to be a martyr because if he was a martyr then their suffering had meaning, their dead had died for something, the war had been worth its cost.

They wanted the lie. Rafael understood that now. Understanding it did not make the lie less deadly; it made it more dangerous, because a lie people need is armored against every blade except one.

"The king you're crowning," Rafael called out, voice cracking on the first word, steadying on the second, "massacred his own provinces. I've seen his confession. It's carved in the stone behind him—in his own throne room, in his own hand. He wrote it himself. He ordered the burnings at Harrowmere. He starved the northern valleys. He executed four thousand prisoners at Cael Ford and lied about it for two hundred years."

The roar faltered. Not stopped—faltered, the way a fire falters when a door opens somewhere and lets in a draft.

"Liar!" someone screamed, and others took it up, and Marrow smiled down at Rafael with genuine sadness, the smile of a man watching a condemned prisoner waste his last breath.

"Search the throne room," Marrow said, magnanimous, turning to his officers. "Let them look. There is nothing there but dust and the bones of a better age."

And that was when the torches changed.

It began at the edges of the crowd, where the light came from a different direction than it should have—from the pass above, descending in a column, forty flames moving in formation, and beneath them the gray cloaks of the Inquisition catching the firelight like the scales of some enormous waking serpent.

The army's roar turned to something lower and more animal. Soldiers reached for weapons. Officers shouted. Marrow's smile vanished.

"Stand fast," he thundered. "It's a trick of the Crown—they come to take your relic, your proof, your king—"

But Vane was no longer listening to Marrow. She was walking forward, alone, out ahead of her inquisitors, into the open ground between the army and the buried palace, and her heart was beating hard and slow, each pulse distinct, the way it beat before battles she expected to lose.

*Any means necessary.* She had always read those words as permission. Standing here, she understood they had also been a warning.

* * *

Vane had planned the assault on the march up the mountain. Volley positions, withdrawal routes, the choke point at the pass where forty crossbows could hold ten thousand men indefinitely. It was a good plan. It would have worked. It would also have produced a massacre in a confined space, inquisitor bolts and rebel steel and trampling bodies, and whatever truth survived the night would be buried under ten thousand corpses and a legend twice as strong as the one they'd come to kill.

So instead she walked forward with her hands empty and her voice trained to carry, and she did the single most frightening thing she had done in eleven years of service: she trusted strangers.

"Inquisitor Serah Vane," she announced, pitching her voice into the vastness of the cavern approach. "By warrant of the Crown." She let the word *Crown* land, let the army bristle, and then she drove the knife in. "And I am here to tell you that your leader is right about one thing. There is proof in this place. Proof carved in stone by the dead king's own command. I have read it. And it condemns him."

The word ran through the crowd like current through water. *Condemns.* Not the smuggler's accusation—not a traitor's desperate claim—but the Inquisition's own seal set upon it.

"You lie," Marrow spat. "The Crown has spent two centuries hiding his glory—"

"The Crown has spent two centuries hiding his crimes." Vane drew the folded papers from her coat—the copies she had made in the archive, the letters, the reports—and held them high. "I found these in my own superiors' vaults. Orders covering the massacres. Reports of the burnings at Harrowmere, filed and forgotten. My masters have known the truth for sixty years and buried it deeper than this palace, because the martyr-king was useful to them, just as he is useful to you." She turned, slowly, so that more of the army could see her face. "Both sides have fed you a ghost. Tonight you can meet the man who actually lived."

"Seize her," Marrow said.

No one moved. That was the moment Vane understood the ground had shifted beneath all of them: an officer three ranks back lowered his spear, not in surrender but in attention, the way a man lowers his weapon to listen.

"Read it, then," the officer said.

"Read it," said another voice. Then another. Then a hundred, then a thousand, the demand rolling and compounding until it was not a demand but a tide, and Dain Marrow stood on the steps of a dead king's palace shouting for order into a storm that no longer answered to him.

Vane looked back at her inquisitors. Forty faces, forty torches. She had trained most of them. She saw doubt in some, fear in others, and in a few—Lieutenant Cass among them, the youngest—a kind of terrible hope.

"Cass," Vane said. "Bring the lights forward. All of them. If we're going to do this, we do it where everyone can see."

* * *

They went down into the throne room together—Vane, Cass, and, after a moment's hesitation that cost him everything, Rafael, released by soldiers who no longer cared to hold a prisoner when a revelation was walking past them. Marrow raged on the steps above, but his voice was thinning, losing the acoustics of authority, becoming merely a man shouting in a hole in the ground.

The throne room opened around them like the ribcage of something immense. Pillars rose into darkness. And there, as Rafael had described it, the far wall gleamed pale where the torchlight touched it—covered floor to vault in carved script, deep-cut, deliberate, the work of months or years.

"He commissioned it himself," Rafael said quietly. "Before the end. He knew what was coming. He wanted it recorded in the one place no one would ever look—the truth, sealed under his own legend."

Vane approached the wall. The characters were archaic, formal court-script, but legible. She read the opening lines silently, and her throat closed.

*I, Aldric Thornfell, fourth of that name, King of the Sundered Realms, being in my right mind and near my death, do set down here what I have done, so that no hand may launder it...*

"Light," she said. "More light."

They brought the torches close, and the wall blazed, and Vane began to read aloud.

Her voice filled the chamber and poured out through its mouths and galleries, and the army pressed inward to hear, officers and footmen and camp followers, ten thousand people going silent in a way Vane had never heard a crowd go silent—not the held-breath silence of an execution but the silence of a congregation.

*"In the winter of the famine I ordered the granaries of the north sealed, though I knew the harvest had failed..."*

*"At Harrowmere I gave the command for the fires. Four hundred souls. The record will say plague. Let this stone say otherwise..."*

*"At Cael Ford I drowned the prisoners rather than feed them. I signed the order with my own hand and slept well that night, which frightens me more than the act itself..."*

She read for an hour. Her voice grew hoarse and she did not stop. Cass took over when she could not continue, then another inquisitor, then—Vane saw it happen and did nothing to prevent it—a gray-bearded sergeant of the Ashen Circle itself, climbing to the wall with a torch in a shaking hand, reading the tyrant's words in a soldier's flat accent while tears cut tracks through the grime on his face.

Above them, on the steps, Dain Marrow had gone very quiet.

That frightened Vane more than his raging had. She climbed back up into the open air and found him standing alone, his iron crown still in his hands, his army dissolved into knots of men arguing, weeping, staring at nothing. His officers had formed a loose ring around him—not guarding him. Containing him.

"It changes nothing," Marrow said, but his voice had lost its music. "The realm still needs a crown. The people still need—"

"They need the truth," Vane said. "You had it in your grasp and you threw it away because a martyr was more useful than a monster. You didn't want justice, Marrow. You wanted a coronation." She looked at the ring of officers. "This man stole a dead tyrant's legend and sold it to you as salvation. What you do with him now is not law. The law brought crossbows tonight. This—" she gestured at the glowing mouth of the throne room, at the crowd still gathered around the reading, "—this is something older than law."

The officers looked at one another. No sword was drawn. No blow was struck. Instead the gray-bearded sergeant who had read the wall climbed the last steps, took the iron crown gently from Marrow's unresisting hands, and set it down on the stone at the threshold of the palace—as an offering, or an accusation, or simply a thing that belonged to no one living.

Marrow did not fight when they bound his wrists. That was the strangest part, Vane thought later. He wept, quietly, like a man waking from a fever, and let himself be led.

* * *

Near midnight the fires were banked and the army sat in exhausted clusters across the mountainside, and Vane found Rafael sitting apart on a fallen pillar, looking down at the torchlit sprawl with the hollow expression of someone who has carried something a very long way and finally set it down.

"You disobeyed your orders," Rafael said without looking up.

"I'm aware."

"They'll hang you for it. Or worse."

"Probably." Vane sat down on the pillar beside them. Below, the entrance to the throne room glowed like a hearth; people were still drifting in and out of it, touching the wall, as if to confirm the words were real. "I spent eleven years believing the law was the thing that stood between people and the dark. Tonight the law stood outside with drawn crossbows, and the truth did the work." She exhaled slowly. "I don't know what I serve now. But I know it isn't the men who wrote my warrant."

Rafael was quiet for a long time. When they finally spoke, their voice was rough. "There are two maps. Copies of the route to this place. One belongs to the rebels' network—one of Marrow's people may still hold it. And the original is with me." Their hand went to their coat. "As long as these exist, someone else will come. Someone worse, or luckier, or more patient. Ten thousand people heard the truth tonight, but paper outlives testimony. Paper gets found by the wrong hands."

Vane turned to look at them, and understanding arrived cold and complete. "What are you going to do?"

Rafael stood. Down in the glow of the throne-room entrance, a child was tracing the carved letters with one finger, learning the shape of a dead tyrant's confession by touch.

"What Odile told me to do from the beginning," Rafael said. "What Corvin stole the map to make possible. What should have happened on a switchback trail a month ago." They looked at Vane, and there was no apology in their face—only a weary, immovable resolve. "I'm going to need a fire, Inquisitor. And I'd take it as a courtesy if you'd watch me light it."
