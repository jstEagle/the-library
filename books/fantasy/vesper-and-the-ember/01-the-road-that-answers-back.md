## 1. The Road That Answers Back

The grimoire weighed less than August had expected. That was the first betrayal of the night — after three years of dusting Magister Corvane's private shelves, after memorizing which floorboards sang and which stair groaned, after all of it — the book that would hang them came away from its chain like a bird leaving a perch. Light as a loaf of stolen bread. Warm, too, though the tower library had been cold enough to frost the windows, and the leather cover held a heat against their palms that no hearth could explain.

August did not stop to wonder about it. Wonder was a luxury for people whose masters were still asleep.

They went down the servants' stair with their boots in one hand and the book pressed to their chest with the other arm, bare feet finding the cold stone by memory. Third step from the landing: skip it. The turn at the second floor: hug the wall, where the stone was worn smooth and silent. Corvane slept on the fourth floor behind a door warded with seven seals, and August had spent two years copying those seals into a hidden notebook until they could draw them blindfolded, until the wards had become as legible as a letter from home — not that August had ever received a letter from home. There was no home to send one.

That thought should have hurt more than it did. It only felt like a key turning.

At the ground floor, the night porter dozed over his dice, and August slipped past him close enough to smell the ale on his breath, out through the postern gate whose lock they had been quietly rusting with vinegar for a month, and down into the terraced gardens where the master's herbs grew in neat, obedient rows. Beyond the wall, the drop to the ravine road was black and loud with water.

August stood at the edge for one breath, two, the grimoire's warmth beating against their ribs like a second heart.

Then they jumped.

* * *

The smuggler's road was not on any map Corvane owned, which was precisely why August took it. They had found it mentioned once, in a marginal note in a seized text about mountain trade routes — *the Thornfell cut, used by those who prefer no receipts* — and had spent a winter's worth of sleepless nights committing every detail to memory. It wound along the ravine's shoulder, narrow as a scar, sometimes barely wider than a goat's certainty, and it climbed northeast through country so broken and steep that even the Conclave's surveyors had apparently given up on it.

August climbed through the dark hours and the grey ones, boots laced now, breath smoking, the book strapped beneath their coat where it refused to cool. By midmorning the ravine had widened into a valley stitched with terraces gone wild, and the air changed — thinned, sweetened with pine and something else, something mineral and faintly sweet like struck flint. Ahead, half-hidden in a fold of the mountains, smoke rose from chimneys. Thornfell.

They had meant to pass around it. Smugglers' roads existed to avoid villages; villages had eyes, and eyes made reports, and reports traveled to men like Corvane faster than any horse. But the path forked at a cairn older than any of the houses below, and the left-hand way — the way the margin-note said continued toward the high passes — was blocked by a rockfall, fresh enough that the broken faces of the stones were still pale, still weeping grit. Someone had come through here recently. Recently and heavily, with tools or with weight, and August stood looking at the rubble for a long moment, doing arithmetic about pursuit, and chose the right-hand way down toward the smoke.

Hunger decided as much as anything. Three days of hard walking on a satchel of hard bread will make a coward of anyone's caution.

Thornfell was small and crooked and beautiful in the way of things that have stopped caring whether anyone approves of them. Houses leaned together like conspirators. Goats occupied the main street with the confidence of landowners. A woman splitting wood outside a smithy looked up at August, took in the road-dust and the hollow cheeks and the coat worn wrong for the season, and went back to her work without a word — which August understood, with a jolt, was a kind of welcome. Smugglers' roads ended in places like this. Places that asked nothing.

An inn called the Drowned Bell sold them stew and a corner table and no questions, in that order. August ate slowly, the grimoire a warm weight against their spine even through the layers, and listened.

They had learned listening in Corvane's house the way other children learned prayers. And what the inn murmured, between the clink of bowls and the pop of the peat fire, was strange.

"—third night running," an old man was saying to the innkeeper, low but not low enough. "Marta swears it. She prayed at the old shrine up the fell — you know how she is since the fever took her boy's strength — and she says the air *answered* her. Spoke."

"The wind spoke," the innkeeper said. "Wind does that up there."

"Not like this. She says it knew her name. Knew the boy's name." A pause, the sound of a cup set down carefully. "And Tam's girl, the one with the burned hands? Marta prayed for her too, same night, and the girl's hands closed over clean. You've seen them. Smooth as a bride's."

"Scars don't vanish."

"No," the old man agreed, agreeably. "They don't."

August kept their eyes on their stew and their breathing even, but something had begun to walk slowly up and down the back of their neck. Because they knew what the Conclave taught, had copied it out a hundred times in a fair hand for a hundred documents: *the gods died in the Ember Purge, four centuries past; their shrines are monuments to error; prayer addressed to them is addressed to silence.* Corvane had made August transcribe the histories of the Purge twice, once for the archive and once — this was the odd part, the part August had never stopped chewing on — once for himself alone, in a cipher August had never been given the key to.

A god answering prayer was impossible. A god answering prayer in a village three days' walk from the master's tower was a catastrophe.

August paid for the stew and a heel of cheese and asked, casually, the direction of the old shrine. The innkeeper pointed with his chin up the fell, behind the village, where the pines thickened and the light went green and dim. "If you're going to pray, pray quiet," he said. "Conclave rode through last spring asking after god-touched folk. We keep our heads down."

"I always do," August said, and discovered they meant it.

* * *

The path to the shrine climbed through trees so old and close that afternoon became evening long before the sky agreed to it. The light came down in coins and slats. Moss swallowed the stones of the path, then the stones of a wall, then — abruptly — the shrine itself.

It was smaller than August expected. A circle of standing stones, hip-high, worn round as river pebbles, around a hollow paved with slate. In the center, a shallow basin of black metal on a pedestal, holding rainwater and dead leaves and, when August leaned close, the ghost of old ash that no weather would wash away. No idol. No image. Whatever had been worshipped here had been worshipped without a face.

Someone had been here recently. The leaves had been cleared from the basin's rim, and a knot of dried flowers lay against the pedestal — meadowsweet and yarrow, tied with red thread, offerings laid by hands that still believed in something.

August should have turned back. Every instinct Corvane had trained into them said: *observe, record, withdraw.* Instead they stood in the ring of stones as the last light failed, and heard — distantly, down the fell — a woman's voice rising thin on the wind. Praying. The words were too far off to catch, but the shape of them was unmistakable, the rise and fall of someone pleading with the dark.

And the dark answered.

Not wind. Not echo. Echo repeats; this *replied*. The air in the ring of stones grew warm — August felt it against their face like an opened oven door — and a voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the basin and the stones and the space between August's own heartbeat, a voice so frayed and faint it was mostly silence, the way a candle is mostly dark and yet is entirely light:

"*—no. Oh, no, I can't, I haven't the strength, I—*"

The distant prayer faltered. The voice in the stones seemed to gather itself, the way a starving man gathers himself to stand.

"*Little mother. Little mother, listen. Your boy. Bring him up at moonrise, bring him where the air is warm — I cannot go to him, do you understand, I cannot leave this place — but bring him, and I will spend what I have. I will spend it gladly. Only — only hurry. I am so low. I am so low, and if I gutter before you come—*"

The voice broke. Not into silence exactly — into something worse, a sound like a fire settling into its own ash, like something trying very hard not to be afraid and failing.

August's knees had gone strange. They realized they were gripping one of the standing stones, both hands, the way a drowning person grips a spar. The grimoire against their back had grown hot enough to feel distinctly now, hot as a sleeping animal, and when August pulled it out with shaking hands, the sigil stamped into the leather cover — a flame within a circle, a mark they had catalogued a dozen times as *decorative* — was glowing. Faintly. Rhythmically. Like a pulse.

Like an answer.

"Who are you?" August whispered, and hated how young their voice sounded in the empty ring.

The warmth turned toward them. There was no other way to describe it — the attention of the air swung around like a lamp being lifted, and August felt *seen*, thoroughly and terribly seen, seen down to the stolen book and the stolen self and the three-day hunger and the name they had not spoken aloud in years.

"*Oh,*" said the voice, and it sounded astonished, and exhausted, and — August would remember this, would carry this moment like a coal in the chest for the rest of their life — *relieved.* "*You're a reader. You're one of his readers. Oh, thank the dark and the deep — child, child, listen to me, because I am Vesper, and I am the last of my kind, and I have been dying in this valley for four hundred years.*"

The glow of the sigil flickered, steadied.

"*And I think you are carrying my heart home.*"

Down in Thornfell, the first lamps were being lit, small and gold and oblivious. Up on the fell, the moon was rising, and somewhere below the shrine a woman was climbing through the pines with her fevered son on her back, hurrying, because a dying god had promised her a miracle.

August Ravensworth stood in the ring of stones, holding a stolen book that beat like a second heart, and understood that the world was larger and stranger and more terrible than anything Magister Corvane had ever let them believe —

and that Corvane, who missed nothing, who had raised them beside these books for reasons August was only beginning to suspect, would already know it was gone.
