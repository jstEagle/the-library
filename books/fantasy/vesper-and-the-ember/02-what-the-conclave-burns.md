## 2. What the Conclave Burns

The road into Thornfell smelled wrong.

Wren Hale had walked it a hundred times in her life — down from the high passes with salt and smuggled brandy, up from the lowlands with wool and lies. The road had always smelled of pine resin and cold stone, of her mother's woodsmoke drifting down from the village like an invitation. Now it smelled of ash and something sweeter underneath, cloying, like burnt sugar or scorched hair, and Wren stopped at the bend where the pines thinned and stared at what had been done to her home.

Thornfell sat in its bowl of mountains the way it always had, huddled against the weather, thatched roofs silvered with age. But above it, where the shrine to the old gods had stood for as long as anyone could remember — a mossy ring of standing stones that children were told were just rocks, nothing more, because everyone knew the gods were dead — there was smoke. A column of it, black and greasy, climbing into the grey sky like a finger raised in accusation.

And on the village green, where the autumn market should have been setting up, stood white tents trimmed in gold, and men in ash-grey robes moved between the houses with the unhurried confidence of people who owned everything they touched.

Ashen Conclave.

Wren's hand went to the knife at her hip before her mind caught up with her fear. She was three days home from a run along the coast — three days too long, as it turned out. She'd left her mother well and complaining about her knees. She'd left old Tam Whitlow arguing about the price of eels. She'd left a village that believed, the way all mountain villages believed, that they were too small and too poor and too far from anything for history to bother with them.

History had found them anyway.

She didn't go straight in. That was the first lesson of smuggling: never walk into a place until you know how you'll walk out. Instead she circled wide through the birch woods above the village, moving the way her father had taught her before he vanished into a Conclave prison and came back as a name on a list — heel to toe, weight low, letting the trees take her shape. From the ridge behind Widow Marsh's goat shed she could see the whole green, and what she saw made her mouth go dry.

They had built a scaffold. Not a hanging scaffold — a *questioning* one, which was worse. A frame of black timber with straps and pulleys, and beside it a brazier full of coals that glowed even in daylight, fed by something that wasn't ordinary charcoal. Conclave fire. God-fire, the old stories called it, though the priests insisted there was no such thing anymore, that the last god had guttered out four centuries ago and good riddance.

A line of villagers waited beside the scaffold. Farmers. A miller's wife. Tam Whitlow, his eel-cart abandoned, his hands shaking so badly Wren could see it from the ridge. An inquisitor moved down the line with a ledger under his arm, asking questions she couldn't hear, marking answers with a scratch of quill. Behind him, two acolytes held a boy of maybe ten by the arms, and the boy was crying without sound, the way children cry when they've been taught already that loud grief brings worse things.

Wren pressed herself flat against the cold earth and made herself breathe.

*They're asking about the prayers.*

Everyone in Thornfell knew. You couldn't not know. Three weeks ago, old Marta Kell had prayed at the standing stones for rain for her beans, and it had rained — not the thin mountain drizzle but a real, generous rain, warm as June, falling only on her field while the next valley stayed dry. Then the Cooper girl's fever broke after her grandmother sang to the stones. Then the landslide above the east pasture stopped, *stopped*, mid-fall, boulders hanging in the air long enough for the shepherd's son to crawl out from under them, and then settle gently, gently, like a parent laying down a sleeping child.

The gods were dead. Everyone knew the gods were dead. And yet something in Thornfell had begun to answer.

Wren had laughed about it with her crew, down in the lowland taverns. *Mountain folk and their ghosts.* She hadn't prayed at the stones herself. She wished now, with a ferocity that surprised her, that she had.

* * *

They took Tam Whitlow to the scaffold at midday.

Wren watched from thirty yards, hidden in the goat shed with its door cracked open, having come down in the hour when the inquisitors took their meal — an hour she'd spent in the Marshes' cellar with Widow Marsh pressing a heel of bread into her hands and whispering, voice cracking, *they burned the Kells' house last night, girl, burned it with Marta inside, said she was god-touched, said the answering was her doing.* The widow's hands had been steady but her eyes weren't. *You have to go. You have to get out and tell someone—*

Tell someone who? The Conclave *was* the someone. The Conclave was law and scripture and fire from the capital down to the smallest village, and if the Conclave had decided that answered prayers were heresy, then every soul in Thornfell was already guilty.

So Wren hid in a goat shed and watched them strap her neighbor to the black timber, and hated herself for hiding, and hid anyway, because a dead witness saved no one.

The inquisitor was young, which surprised her. She'd expected some scarred fanatic, but this one had a soft scholar's face and gentle hands, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing she'd ever seen. He spoke to Tam quietly, almost kindly, adjusting the straps himself, checking the buckles the way a man checks a horse's girth.

"Master Whitlow." His voice carried in the still air. "Three witnesses say you were heard praying at the standing stones on the night of the harvest moon. What did you ask for?"

Tam's jaw set. "Rain for my brother's barley. Same as I've asked every year since we were boys."

"And did it come?"

"Rains come when rains come."

The inquisitor sighed, a small sorrowful sound, and nodded to the brazier. An acolyte lifted out a coal with iron tongs — a coal that burned white instead of orange, that gave off no smoke, that hummed faintly like a struck bell — and held it near Tam's bare forearm without touching him. Just near. And Tam screamed, screamed as though his arm were already in the flame, though his skin showed nothing, not yet, not a mark.

"The ember does not burn the flesh," the inquisitor said, still gentle. "It burns the lie. Confession cools it. You know our doctrine, Master Whitlow. Truth is the only shelter." He leaned close. "What did you hear, when your prayer was answered?"

Wren realized she had drawn her knife. She realized she was gripping the shed door hard enough to splinter it. She made herself let go. There were nine of them on the green. Nine, plus the ones she couldn't see, plus the whole weight of an empire behind them, and she was one woman with a knife and a head full of fury that would get her strung up beside Tam before she cut a single throat.

"Nothing," Tam sobbed. "I heard nothing—"

The coal came closer. The humming rose. And Tam Whitlow, who had taught Wren to gut fish when she was six, who had never in his life broken a promise or a law worth breaking, broke.

"It spoke!" The words tore out of him like something ripped. "It said — it said *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I don't mean to be heard* — it sounded frightened, saints preserve me, it sounded like something *dying*, and I ran, I ran home and I didn't pray again—" He collapsed against the straps, weeping. "It begged me not to tell. It said they'd come. It said *when they come, it will already be too late for me*—"

The green went very quiet. Even the inquisitor's quill had stopped.

Then he smiled — a small, satisfied, scholarly smile — and wrote in his ledger, and said, "Thank you, Master Whitlow. Your honesty will be noted." And to his acolytes: "Mark this household. God-touched. It goes on the list for cleansing."

*Cleansing.* Wren knew what that word meant now. She'd seen the Kells' chimney still standing over its own ashes on her way down the ridge.

* * *

Dusk came, and with it the thing Wren would carry for the rest of her life.

They cleansed Tam Whitlow's household at sundown. They marched Tam and his wife and their grown daughter out to the road and made them watch, and the inquisitor read from a book in a language that hurt to listen to, and then they put god-fire to the house Wren had known since childhood, with its crooked door and its smell of pipe smoke and eels, and it went up all at once — not slowly, not the way honest fire eats a house board by board, but *all at once*, white light swallowing the walls, the roof, the years, gone in a single silent bloom like a star falling upward.

No one screamed. That was the worst of it. The Conclave fire made no sound at all, and the family stood on the road holding each other and watching forty years of their lives become light, and the inquisitors stood around the blaze with their heads bowed as if in prayer, and Wren lay in the bracken above the village with her face in the dirt and her teeth clenched shut so hard her jaw ached, and understood that she was looking at the future of every house in Thornfell unless someone did something.

Her mother's house among them.

When the inquisitors withdrew to their tents for the night vigil, Wren moved.

She told herself it was strategy. Get proof, get leverage, get something to trade on the roads where smugglers talked and Conclave seals opened doors that knives couldn't. But strategy was a coat she threw over the truth, and the truth was simpler: they had taken Tam's confession and thanked him and burned him anyway, and something in Wren Hale had gone past anger into a cold, clear country on the other side of it, and in that country there was only one road forward.

The inquisitor's tent was the large one, gold-trimmed, at the center of the camp. Two sentries outside, backs to the wind. Wren circled to the rear, where the tent wall met the storage wagons, and found what she'd hoped for: a gap where the canvas sagged away from a wheel hub, breathing cold air in and lamplight out.

Inside, the inquisitor slept in a cot like any man. His ledger lay on the camp table, and beside it — laid out with ritual care on a square of white cloth — the instruments of his office. A seal the size of her palm, heavy lead stamped with the Conclave's burning-eye sigil, the thing that turned a smuggler's word into imperial writ. A vial of the white fire, humming faintly. And papers. Orders, half-written reports, and one letter bearing a capital seal that made her blood slow:

*...the source has been located. Magister Corvane is dispatched with authority to secure the vessel entire. Let nothing of the ember remain unharvested. The villages are expendable; the god is not.*

*A god.* Her hand shook as she copied the words into memory, letter by letter, the way her father had taught her to memorize manifests. They knew. They had known since the first answered prayer, and they hadn't come to stamp out heresy — heresy was an excuse. They had come to *take it*, whatever dying thing was answering in the dark, and burn every soul that stood between them and it.

Wren took the seal. She took the letter too, folding it into her coat against her heart. At the tent flap she paused, one breath, looking back at the sleeping inquisitor's soft, gentle face, and felt the cold country inside her settle into permanence, like a stone finding its bed at the bottom of a river.

Then she slipped out into the dark, past the sentries, past the smoking ruin of the Whitlows' house, past her mother's shuttered window where a candle still burned — *stay hidden, Mum, stay dark, I'll come back for you* — and up out of the village toward the high passes, toward the standing stones, toward the frightened dying thing that had begged a fishmonger not to tell.

Because someone had to answer *it*, now. Someone had to hear.

The rumors were already running through the lowland taverns like spring meltwater: a stranger seen at the shrine, a hedge-witch's apprentice with a stolen book, asking questions about the old gods. Smugglers' talk, mostly. But Wren had learned long ago that smugglers' talk was the truest news in the empire.

If there was someone else asking questions — someone the ember might trust — then Wren Hale intended to find them.

And between her and the shrine, the mountain road wound upward through the dark, quiet as a held breath, as if the whole world were waiting to see what she would do next.
