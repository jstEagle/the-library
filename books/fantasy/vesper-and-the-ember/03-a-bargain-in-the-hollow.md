## 3. A Bargain in the Hollow

August came back to the shrine the way a dog returns to a place where it was once kicked — slowly, flinching at every shadow, unable to stay away.

The smuggler's road wound down through Thornfell's pines in switchbacks so steep that the village below seemed to hang from the mountainside like laundry on a line. August had spent two days in the loft of a tannery, sleeping badly and reading worse, because the grimoire did not want to be read. It resisted like a mule. Pages that had been legible by candlelight went blank when looked at directly; marginalia rearranged themselves into accusations; once, a diagram of the nine spheres of divine emanation had quietly become a drawing of an eye, and August had thrown the book across the loft and not touched it for six hours.

But it always came back to the same passage. The one about fire.

*The gods were not slain,* the old hand wrote, in ink gone brown as dried blood. *Slaying is quick and honest. The founders of the Ashen Conclave built their engines and their liturgies to do a slower thing: to cage, to siphon, to feed upon. What burns in every Conclave lantern is not oil. It is remnant. It is them.*

August had read it eleven times. Eleven times, the words had refused to become less true.

So: the shrine. The voice that called itself Vesper. August told themselves it was for answers — that they needed to know if the grimoire lied, if the weak and frightened thing in the mountain hollow was a god or a trap or a Conclave lure dressed in divinity. But there was another reason, smaller and more shameful, that August carried down the switchbacks with the grimoire strapped beneath their coat like a stolen heart.

The voice had sounded lonely.

* * *

The hollow opened out of the forest without warning, the way secrets do. One moment August was pushing through fir branches heavy with last night's rain; the next, the trees simply gave up, ringed back on all sides as though something enormous had once sat down here and never gotten up. In the center stood the shrine — a low dome of black stone furred with moss, older than any architecture August knew, its doorway a narrow slit that swallowed light rather than reflecting it. The air inside the hollow tasted of struck flint and snowmelt. Somewhere, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

And someone was already there.

A girl crouched at the shrine's threshold, hood back, dark hair cropped short and practical as a hedge sheared before winter. She had a knife out — not brandished, just held, the way other people held opinions — and she was feeding strips of dried fish into the darkness of the doorway one at a time, which made no sense whatsoever, unless you thought gods ate fish, which apparently they might.

"Don't," she said, without turning around. "I heard you crashing through the trees since the second switchback. You walk like a landslide."

"I walk like someone who doesn't want to be followed."

"Then you're bad at both." She rose, turning, and August got their first proper look at her: sharp-faced, sun-browned, with eyes the flat grey-green of river slate and a scar splitting one eyebrow. A smuggler, or August was blind. Everything about her said *borders crossed, taxes evaded, questions not asked.* "You're the one who found it first. The Thornfell folk talk. A stranger asked the shrine a question and the shrine answered." Her gaze dropped to the shape of the grimoire under August's coat. "And you've been carrying that around like it's going to hatch."

"It might," August said. "It has opinions."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. Then it hardened again. "Wren Hale."

"August Ravensworth." They didn't offer a hand. Neither did she. Two people deciding simultaneously how much of themselves to spend.

"You're not Conclave," Wren said. It wasn't a question, but August answered it anyway.

"No."

"Prove it's worth believing. Everyone says no. The inquisitors burned the Aldous family's house on Tuesday, and Master Aldous said *no* right up until his roof caught." Her voice stayed level, but August heard the thing underneath it, the way you hear water under ice. "So. Why are you here?"

"The same reason you are, I'd guess." August unslung the grimoire and held it where she could see it — not offering, showing. "Answers. I found this shrine three nights ago. I asked it a question and something answered me. Something that said its name was Vesper. And everything I've read since tells me that name should be impossible, and everything I've felt since tells me it isn't." They hesitated, then paid out more truth than they'd planned, the way you do when someone else is holding a knife and the truth is the only coin they'll take. "My master taught me the hedge-craft. She died in the spring. I took this book off her shelf the night after her funeral, and I've been running ever since. From her tower, from—" *from what, exactly?* The thought snagged, as it always did. From what? Corvane's reach, yes, but why did running feel like following? "From people I don't fully understand yet."

Wren studied them for a long moment. Rain dripped off the fir branches. Somewhere in the shrine's throat, faintly, something breathed.

"You first," she said finally. "You go in, ask your question. If it answers you the way it answered me, we'll talk business."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you're a fraud or a fool, and either way you can walk back up the mountain and I finish my conversation alone."

* * *

Inside, the shrine was colder than the day and darker than the night, and Vesper was weaker than it had been three days ago. August felt it immediately — the presence that had filled the hollow like woodsmoke now barely smoldered, a coal cupped in dying hands.

*You came back,* the voice said. It was still beautiful, still layered like a chord played on many instruments, but the layers were thinning. August could hear, underneath the music, something almost human in its frailty. *I hoped. Hoping is expensive. I did it anyway.*

"What's happening to you?" August knelt at the edge of the ember-light — a small red glow deep in the stone, no bigger than a fist now, where before it had lit the whole chamber.

*The village prays less. Fear does that. When the Conclave came, the faithful grew quiet — quiet prayers are thin prayers.* A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: *You have read the book.*

"Yes."

*Then you know what I am. What remains. My siblings are lanterns now. Their fire lights Conclave halls and heats the baths of men who never learned our names. I escaped the harvest because I was small, and stubborn, and my mountain loved me enough to hide me.* The ember pulsed, dimmed, pulsed. *I am the last unbanked flame, little scholar. And I am going out.*

"There's a way to save you." August heard their own voice shake. "In the grimoire. A shrine at Mount Cinder's heart, where—"

*I know what lies at Cinder's heart.* The chord turned strange, unreadable. *Knowing is not the same as affording. Ask me nothing more of that today. Today, ask me what you came to ask.*

August closed their eyes. "Is the grimoire true? Did the Conclave cage the gods and steal their fire?"

*Child.* The word was vast and tired and kind. *Your mentor's book is a candle beside the truth, and the truth is a bonfire, and the Conclave has spent four hundred years convincing the world the ash is all there ever was. Yes. Every lantern in their towers is a prison. Every hymn they sing is a lock turning. And every prayer that reaches me is a crack in the wall — and I am so hungry, and I am so ashamed of how hungry I am, and I would rather gutters out than take what they take.*

When August crawled back out into the grey daylight, Wren was waiting with her knife still in hand and her arms crossed, and whatever she saw in August's face made her uncross them.

"Well?"

"It's real. It's dying. And it's telling the truth." August sat down hard on the wet stone, the grimoire heavy in their lap. "Which means the Conclave isn't just tyrants. They're — thieves on a scale that makes tyranny look like petty crime. Four hundred years of empire, built on stolen divinity, and they'll burn every village in these mountains to keep anyone from finding out."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "I watched them burn the Aldouses' house," she said. "Old man Aldous confessed to hearing Vesper in his prayers. He confessed to anything they wanted by the end. They burned him anyway, because the confession wasn't the point. The fear was the point." She looked at the shrine door. "I came here to find out if the god was worth the burning. Now you're telling me it's worth more than that. That it's worth *everything*, if the book's right."

"The book's right. Vesper confirmed it."

"Gods can lie."

"Not this one. Not anymore. Lying takes strength it doesn't have." August met her slate-grey eyes. "You know the mountain paths. Real ones — smuggler routes, the ways that don't appear on any Conclave map. The rebirth shrine is at Mount Cinder's heart. I can't get there without you."

"And what do I get?"

"A god that isn't owned by the Conclave. Or dead. Pick whichever matters more to you."

"That's not payment, that's a sermon."

"Fine." August pulled the grimoire around and opened it to the pages they'd marked — the diagrams, the founder's names, the ledger of imprisoned flames written in a hand that had trembled even four centuries ago. "Payment is truth. You want to hurt the people who burned the Aldouses' house? This tells you where they keep what they stole. Every engine, every lantern-vault, every prison-liturgy. Knowledge like this doesn't just save one god, Wren. It could break their whole empire's spine."

Wren looked at the pages for a long time. Rain ticked against the fir branches. Inside the shrine, the ember breathed its small, stubborn breaths.

"You'll slow me down," she said at last. "You walk like a landslide."

"I'll learn."

"And if the Conclave comes for us — and they will, because a stolen grimoire and a talking shrine are exactly the sort of thing they send inquisitors for — you follow my orders on the road. No arguments, no heroics, no reading aloud from that book at campfires like some street preacher."

"Agreed."

"And if it comes to choosing between the god and one of us—" She stopped herself. Looked away. "Never mind. We'll see when we get there."

They shook on it, briefly, her grip calloused and strong, and August felt the strange vertigo of alliance — two strangers lashing their boats together in open water, each half-convinced the other's vessel was already leaking.

* * *

They were preparing to leave — Wren kicking dirt over her fire-ring, August wrapping the grimoire in oilcloth — when the ember's voice reached them both, faint as heat off a dying stove, spilling out through the shrine's narrow door.

*Little scholar. Little thief of roads. One gift, freely given, because bargains should be sealed in honesty:* a pause, long enough that August thought the god had guttered out entirely. *You are already followed. Not soldiers. Worse. Someone who knows your face, August Ravensworth, and knows it well. He rides under a white banner and he carries a ledger, and he has been writing your name in it for years.*

August went cold from the scalp down. "Who?"

The ember flickered. Dimmed. When it spoke again, the voice was thinner, frayed at the edges, spending itself like a miser's last coins.

*I cannot see him clearly. Faith is my only eye, and he has none. But the mountain remembers his boots, and the mountain says he has walked these paths before — long ago, when you were small. The mountain says he helped choose the road you would run down.*

Wren's hand had gone to her knife. "Conclave?"

"Worse," August heard themselves say, though they couldn't have explained why. Some memory stirred — a tower room, a voice reading lessons, hands adjusting their collar before an examination — and slipped away before it could show its face. "I think it's someone who used to be family."

Above the hollow, far up through the pines, a crow lifted off a dead branch and flew south, fast, like something carrying news.
