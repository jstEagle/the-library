## 4. The Defector

Brother Ilen had prayed every morning of his life, and for twenty-nine years the prayers had gone nowhere. He had never minded. Prayer, the Conclave taught, was not a conversation but a discipline — a whetting stone for the soul, a way of holding one's own heart up to the light and finding it wanting. You did not pray to be answered. You prayed to be made smaller, and therefore purer.

Then, on the ninth night of the march through the Thornfell highlands, something answered him back.

He had been kneeling in his cell-tent with the standard issue of a column acolyte: a slate of grey ashstone, a stylus of bone, the Litany of Embers inked across his knuckles in the old script that no one living could read aloud without a license. The tent was cold enough that his breath made small ghosts. Outside, the inquisition column creaked and muttered — sixty wagons, two hundred sworn brothers, and the iron thing they called the reliquary engine, which traveled under oilcloth like a corpse too heavy to bury.

*Ashen Father, who burned first and brightest,* Ilen recited, *take my unworthiness as fuel—*

*I'm still here.*

Ilen stopped. The voice was not in the tent. It was not in the wind. It was somewhere behind his sternum, faint as heat off a dying stove, and it sounded — this was the part he could not stop thinking about, all through the terrible days that followed — it sounded *afraid*.

*Who's there?* he asked, silently, feeling foolish.

*A god,* said the voice, with a small, cracked laugh. *Or what's left of one. Don't tell anyone. Please. They'll come for me.*

He should have reported it at once. That was the discipline; that was the whole architecture of his life. A brother who heard voices was either mad or touched, and both were processed the same way: confession, examination, and if the voice proved genuine — if it proved to be one of the *old names*, the dead gods the Conclave had buried in its founding centuries — then the brother was remanded to the Office of Harvest, where the touched were kept comfortable and drained slowly, their faith rendered down like tallow.

Ilen knelt on the cold boards until his knees ached and did not report it.

Instead he asked, *Why me?*

*Because you mean it,* the voice said. *The others pray like men counting coins. You pray like a man apologizing.*

---

For six nights he listened. The ember-god — Vesper, it named itself, and the name rang some half-buried bell in the liturgy, a saint demoted, a name scraped from the calendar but not from the stones — told him things in a whisper that frayed at the edges. It told him about Thornfell. About households marked with ash-crosses on their doors. About the interrogations conducted in the tithe-barn, where the screaming was muffled by design, because Magister Corvane believed fear carried better when it had to be imagined.

And it told him about the engine.

*They don't want to kill me,* Vesper said. *Killing is waste. They want to put me in the reliquary engine and burn me slow, the way you'd bank a fire to last the winter. Every prayer in the empire would pass through me first. Every miracle would be theirs to ration. That's what the founders built it for. That's what it did to my brothers and sisters.*

Ilen thought of the oilcloth shape in the wagon-train's heart, and the brothers who walked beside it with the particular reverence soldiers reserve for siege equipment.

*Why are you telling me?*

*Because there's a boy,* Vesper said. *A runaway. He has my last ember. He doesn't know what he's carrying, not really. And there's a woman — sharp as a fishhook, don't underestimate her. They're going to try for Mount Cinder.* A pause, and the voice guttered like a candle in draft. *Corvane knows the road. Corvane has always known the road. He taught it to the boy himself, years ago, before he sold him.*

The word *sold* went through Ilen like a nail.

*You have to warn them,* Vesper whispered. *You're the only one inside who can. And Ilen — brother — when you go, go quickly. Because Corvane is coming, and Corvane does not leave loose threads.*

---

On the seventh night, Ilen deserted.

He did it badly, by any professional measure. He left his ashstone slate on his cot with the Litany unfinished, which would be read as either madness or message, and he took nothing but his travel cloak, a skin of water, and the route-book he was not authorized to carry — the column's marching orders, copied in his own neat hand, because a lifetime of obedience leaves a man unable to destroy paper.

He went out through the latrine trench at the rear of the camp, where the sentries' lanterns didn't reach, and he walked into the pines with the snow squeaking under his boots and his heart going like a trapped bird. Behind him, the column's watch-fires made an orange stain on the low clouds, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, he could feel the engine humming. He had never let himself hear it before. Now he could not stop hearing it: a vast, patient appetite, idling.

Vesper's presence faded as he climbed — the god's reach was short now, a hand's span of warmth in a cold world — but it left him with a direction, pressed into his mind like a seal into wax: *northwest, past the Split Rock, where the smugglers' road crosses the frozen falls.*

He walked all night. Twice he heard horns behind him and went flat against a trunk with snow soaking through his cloak, and twice the horns proved to be hunting wolves, or wind, or his own pulse. Toward dawn he crossed a ridge and saw, far below in a white bowl of valley, the frozen falls of the Aldwater, hung like a curtain of dirty glass, and beside them the black seam of the old smugglers' road threading north toward Mount Cinder.

And on the road, small with distance: two figures, and between them a lantern.

---

He caught them at midmorning, and it went the way he might have guessed it would go.

The woman heard him first — of course she did; she moved like a cat that had been kicked too often to trust quiet — and by the time he stumbled into speaking distance she had a knife out and the boy behind her, and the boy had the lantern clutched to his chest like a child.

"Brother," Wren Hale said, and her voice could have chilled wine. "You're a long way from your bonfire."

"I've left it." He raised his empty hands, palms out, fingers spread so she could see the litany-ink on his knuckles. "My name is Ilen. I was acolyte-third to the inquisition column at Thornfell. I've come to warn you."

"Warn us." She circled him slowly, knife low, weight forward. "That's new. Usually your people just burn."

"Wren." The boy — August Ravensworth; Ilen knew the name from the column's warrant lists, knew it the way you know a scar you didn't earn — August's voice was thin. "The lantern. Ask the lantern."

Wren's eyes didn't leave Ilen, but something in her stance shifted, a hair's breadth. "Ask it what?"

"It knows him," August said. "It's been — it said someone was coming. Someone scared."

Ilen felt the truth of it land in his own chest a moment later, a faint warmth behind the sternum, familiar now, homecoming-sad. *Hello again,* Vesper murmured, so softly only he seemed to hear it. *Thank you. Oh, thank you.*

"He's telling the truth," August breathed. "Wren. He's telling the truth."

"The truth about what?" Wren snapped. "People tell the truth all the time right up until they sell you. Talk fast, brother."

So Ilen talked fast. He told them about the engine under its oilcloth, about the harvest-rations and the Office of Harvest, about the households in Thornfell with ash on their doors. He saw August flinch at that, and press a hand flat against the lantern glass, and he understood that the boy loved whatever burned in there, and that loving it was going to cost him something enormous. Ilen had seen enough of the Conclave's work to know that everything it touched was priced in blood.

Then he told them the rest, and watched the color leave August's face.

"The column isn't following the main road anymore. Two nights ago the scouts were recalled and the pace changed — we turned east, onto the shepherd tracks. There's a route-book now, sealed, magister's eyes only. But I copied the orders before I ran." He drew the folded pages from inside his cloak and held them out flat, the way you offer meat to a strange dog. "The Conclave has found the approach to the rebirth shrine. The old pilgrim stair on Cinder's western flank. They've known about it for months."

"That's impossible," Wren said. "Nobody knows that stair. My own kin don't know that stair."

"Somebody sold it," Ilen said quietly. "Or somebody remembered it. Because there's more." He made himself say it plainly, because kindness delayed was cruelty compounded. "The Conclave hasn't dispatched a pursuit column. They dispatched one man. Magister Corvane. With a harvest-warrant and a picked team of twelve, moving light and fast, ahead of the main force. He's expected to bring back the ember alive." He looked at August. "And the vessel. The warrant lists you by name, August Ravensworth. Not as a fugitive. As *property*. Recovered assets of Magister Corvane, to be returned to his custody."

August had gone very still. "Returned," he repeated. "I wasn't — I ran away. Three years ago. I ran, he didn't—"

"Brother Ilen." Wren's voice had dropped into something soft and dangerous. "What exactly does the Conclave think this boy is?"

"A vessel," Ilen said. "God-touched. Raised to purpose." He hesitated, hating the next words, saying them anyway. "There's a doctrine in the Office of Harvest. *The unloved vessel burns cleanest.* A sacrifice means less when there's less to grieve. If the records are true—" he nodded at August, at the boy's stricken face — "then your master didn't lose you three years ago. He was preparing you. Everything you remember may have been arranged."

The silence after that was very long. Snow ticked down through the pines. In the lantern, the flame bent toward August's hands like a plant toward sun, and August stared at it as though seeing it for the first time — as though wondering, perhaps, whether even this had been planted in him.

"Well," Wren said at last, into the quiet, and her voice was rougher than before. "Then we'd better make sure he's got something worth grieving for by the time we get there."

She took the route-pages from Ilen's hand. She did not take her eyes off him while she did it.

"You know the Conclave's roads," she said. "Their waypoints, their supply caches, how fast a picked team moves in snow."

"Better than anyone in the column," Ilen said. "I plotted half the marches myself."

"And you expect us to believe a sworn brother just — walks away from all of it. For a voice in his head."

"No," Ilen said. "I expect you to believe a man walked away because he finally got an answer to thirty years of prayers, and the answer was *please help me*, and he discovered he couldn't live in a church that says no to that." He held her gaze. "I don't ask you to trust me. I ask you to use me. Point me at the worst road and make me walk it first."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled — a thin, wintry thing. "Careful, brother. That's the most honest thing I've heard out of a Conclave collar. I might start liking you."

Behind them, August spoke, barely above the wind. He was looking northwest, up the white shoulder of the valley, toward the pass they would have to take.

"If Corvane is ahead of us," he said, "and moving light — then he isn't behind us at all. He's between us and the mountain."

No one answered. Far off, beyond the frozen falls, a single hawk turned in the cold air — or something shaped like a hawk, circling once, twice, and then flying hard and straight toward the pass, as though carrying a message.

Ilen felt the ember-god stir faintly against his ribs, one last flicker of warmth, and the words it left him with were not comfort.

*He's already on the road,* Vesper whispered. *Run.*
