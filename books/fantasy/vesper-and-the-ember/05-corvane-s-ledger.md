## 5. Corvane's Ledger

The waystation crouched at the shoulder of the mountain like something that had crawled there to die.

It had been a tollhouse once, Wren said — back when the smugglers' road carried more than rumor and fear — and it still wore the bones of its old purpose: a stone archway split by frost, a stable roof fallen in on itself like a cupped hand, walls furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Snow had drifted against the eastern side in a smooth white ramp, and the wind came off the peaks in long, low moans that made the whole structure seem to breathe.

"We'll stop here," Wren said, not asking. She was rubbing her gloved hands together, and even through the wool August could hear the chafe of cold-cracked skin. "One night. The pass beyond is suicide after dark, and the lantern—" she nodded at the iron-caged ember swinging from August's pack, dimmer than it had been three days ago, "—needs rest same as we do."

Inside, the waystation was one long room with a hearth at either end and a loft above, reached by stairs that groaned like penitents. Someone had left things behind when the tolls stopped: a rusted kettle, three stools of different heights, a wall of pegs hung with the ghosts of cloaks. The air smelled of stone and mouse and the faint mineral tang of snowmelt seeping through the mortar.

August set the lantern on the hearthstone and knelt before it. Through the soot-darkened glass, Vesper's ember pulsed — slow now, a heartbeat with too much night between beats.

"You should eat," August murmured, though they had nothing to give but warmth. They cupped their palms around the cage until their fingers ached with heat. It was a ritual now, three nights running: feeding the god what little a body could spare. In return, Vesper gave them light enough to read by, and sometimes — when the wind dropped — a voice like ash settling.

*You are kind,* the ember had said last night. *Kindness is expensive. Stop spending it on me.*

*Brother Ilen* had taken first watch outside, pacing the perimeter with his Conclave-trained thoroughness, checking sightlines toward the pass as though Magister Corvane might materialize out of the snowfall at any moment. He probably would, Ilen kept saying. The Conclave did not lose things. It *retrieved* them.

August went looking for firewood instead.

That was how they found the ledger.

It was tucked behind the eastern hearth, wedged into a gap between stones where someone had thought it hidden — or where someone had thought it safe. A book bound in grey leather gone green at the edges, its spine cracked, its pages swollen and shrunken by decades of damp. August pulled it free expecting a toll-keeper's accounts: sacks of barley, fees for mule and man, the small arithmetic of an ordinary life.

The first third was exactly that. Neat columns in a careful hand. *Fifthmonth — two carts, one priest (unmarked), toll paid in salt.* August smiled despite themselves, reading the dead stranger's tidy numbers, hearing in them the voice of a person who had counted everything because counting was how you kept the mountain from taking more than its share.

Then the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. On one page the toll-keeper's patient figures; on the next, a hand August knew better than their own reflection. A narrow, precise script, each letter built like a piece of architecture, every descender ruled as if against an invisible line. August had spent six years copying that hand under a tutor's correction. Six years of *again, Ravensworth, your loops are sloppy* until their own writing became a pale imitation of it.

Magister Corvane's hand. In a ledger hidden behind a hearth on a smuggler's road, dated eleven years ago.

* * *

August did not remember sitting down. But they were on the floor when they finally made themselves read past the shock, knees drawn up, the ledger open across them like something wounded.

The entries were not tolls.

*Entry 41. Subject shows no spontaneous sensitivity. Prayers offered nightly produce no resonance. Consider intensifying isolation protocol.*

*Entry 44. Subject asked today why the village children do not visit. Maintained cover: "your mentor prefers solitude." Cover holds. Subject's attachment to guardian deepens as instructed. Attachment is useful; grief will be leverage later.*

*Entry 49. Guardian reports subject found the locked cabinet. Contents relocated. Guardian administered corrective. Subject does not suspect. Subject is remarkably trusting. This is the patron's chief requirement.*

*Entry 57. Subject dreamed of fire again. Third instance this season. Patron notified. Payment released. Continue observation. God-touched blood will tell eventually, if we are patient. Ignorance must be maintained at all costs — a vessel that knows what it carries will refuse the vessel's work.*

The words swam. August read entry 57 four times, and each time the letters rearranged themselves into the same terrible sentence, and each time some part of them stood up and walked away from it, refusing.

*Guardian reports subject found the locked cabinet.*

Maren. Old Maren, who had smelled of juniper smoke and beeswax, whose hands were cracked like riverbed clay, who had taught August to read the grimoire's marginalia by candlelight and laughed — that particular laugh, head tipped back, delighted — whenever August got something gloriously wrong. Maren, who had died two winters ago and whose grave August had dug alone with a borrowed spade while the hedge-row stood bare and judging.

*Maren had been reporting on them.*

No. Not Maren. Maren had been *paid*. There was a difference, some cold clerk in August's chest insisted. A difference that mattered. Except there wasn't, not really, not when you laid the entries end to end and watched the shape of them: a child purchased, isolated, groomed toward attachment with a handler, raised in deliberate ignorance of what ran in their own blood — *god-touched*, the ledger said, as casually as it might say *bay mare, sound teeth* — all of it arranged, funded, *directed* by Corvane for a patron whose name never appeared, only the flat recurring word: *patron*. *Patron notified. Patron satisfied. The patron's requirements are met.*

And then, near the end, the entry August would have given anything never to have read:

*Entry 63. Guardian aging. Recommend replacement within two years. Subject's attachment transfers easily — note the speed with which they bonded to guardian after removal from mother. When the time comes, the runaway can be staged. A flight into the mountains serves us better than a death: a fugitive apprentice carrying the grimoire will draw the god-touched out of hiding, and the Conclave's interest provides excellent cover for retrieval. Let the subject believe they stole something. Belief in one's own transgression makes a person easier to steer than belief in anyone else.*

*Let the subject believe they stole something.*

August's hands were shaking so badly the pages rattled like winter branches.

They had stolen the grimoire. They remembered stealing it — the weight of it under their shirt, Maren's body already cooling in the next room, the terror and the strange exhilaration of the open door. That memory was the load-bearing wall of everything they were. *I ran. I chose. I am here because I fled.*

But the ledger said the door had been left unlocked. The ledger said the flight had been *staged*, a stage-direction in a play written before August could write their own name. And beneath that revelation, worse, quieter, coiling up through the floorboards of the self: *if that memory is false, what else?*

The laugh. Was the laugh real? The juniper smell, the cracked hands, the lessons by candlelight — real, or administered? *Corrective*, the ledger said, for whatever happened when August found the locked cabinet. What had the corrective been? August searched their memory and found only a soft blank place, a night they'd never been able to account for, a morning when Maren had been unusually gentle and August's wrist had ached without reason. They had filed it away years ago as a dream. Now it sat up in the dark of them and showed its face.

Even the love curdled under examination. Had Maren loved them at all, or had Maren simply performed attachment according to specification — *subject's attachment to guardian deepens as instructed* — until the performance wore grooves deep enough to feel true? And if a handler's performed love could be indistinguishable from real love, then what was August's love in return worth? What had it ever been worth?

Outside, the wind rose and fell. The lantern on the hearth pulsed its slow, starving pulse. Somewhere above, snow slid from the ruined stable roof with a sound like a held breath released.

August became aware, distantly, that they were crying — not the clean grief of Maren's funeral, which had at least been *theirs*, but something uglier, a grief with no object, mourning a childhood that may never have existed. They wiped their face with the heel of a hand and kept reading, because that was the one thing Corvane had genuinely taught them: finish the text. Know your enemy's whole argument before you answer it.

The final entries were recent. Within the month.

*Entry 112. Subject located. Travels with a Hale smuggler and the defector Ilen. Carries the ember. As anticipated, the god has chosen its vessel — or rather, the vessel we prepared has drawn the god as a lamp draws a moth. The patron's patience is rewarded.*

*Entry 113. Do not harm the subject. The vessel must reach the shrine intact. Everything the subject loves has been arranged to make the final payment cheap. A vessel with nothing to lose surrenders everything willingly.*

*A vessel with nothing to lose surrenders everything willingly.*

August closed the ledger. The sound of it — soft, final, like a coffin lid — seemed very loud in the empty room.

So that was the design. Not the tower, not the lessons, not even the flight. All of it, every warm moment and every cold one, had been construction: a person built to be spent. Raised unloved so the sacrifice would be cheap. And the cruelest cut of it, the cut that went deepest, was that Corvane had been right about the raw material. Because August sat there in the ruin of their own history and could not find, anywhere in the rubble, a single memory they still trusted. Every tenderness was suspect. Every kindness had a ledger-entry behind it. Even this grief — was it grief, or was it the response the architect had planned for, the keystone set in place twenty years ago, waiting?

*The past cannot be trusted,* they thought, and the thought had Vesper's cadence, ash settling on ash.

The lantern flickered. Faintly, through the glass, the ember stirred — and whether it was the god's weakness or the god's attention, August felt the change in the room's pressure, the way air leans toward an open wound.

*Little thief,* Vesper whispered, so quietly it might have been the wind in the broken chimney. *Your heart is making a sound I have not heard since the world was young. Come. Tell me what you found.*

August looked down at the ledger in their lap, at Corvane's beautiful architectural hand, and understood suddenly, completely, why the book had been left behind the hearth of a waystation on the one road a fleeing apprentice would take.

It hadn't been hidden.

It had been *planted* — a message, a leash, a reminder of ownership, waiting eleven years for them to walk this exact stretch of mountain. Even their despair had been scheduled.

And below the last entry, in fresh ink, in that same ruled and patient hand, a single line August had missed:

*I know you are reading this, August. Bring me the ember, and I will tell you which of your memories were real.*

* * *

From the doorway, snow swirling in around Ilen's boots, came the acolyte's voice, tight with alarm: "Ravensworth — lights on the pass. Torch-line, half a mile, moving fast." He stopped. His eyes went from August's tear-streaked face to the ledger lying open on the floor, and whatever he saw there made him go pale. "He's not waiting for the shrine," Ilen said. "Corvane's already here."
