## 6. Faith Is a Hunger

I was born of fire the way rivers are born of rain — slowly, and from something larger than myself.

I remember it still, though memory is the first thing hunger eats. I remember the mountain breathing warmth into my chest before I had a chest to warm. I remember the first prayer that found me: a shepherd on the high slopes, his hands cracked with cold, whispering *let the ewes live through the night*. And I answered, because answering was all I knew how to do. The ewes lived. He wept into their wool. And something small and bright kindled in me — a spark with my name folded inside it like a seed in ash.

They called me Vesper because I came at evening. Because I was the light that stayed when the sun went down.

That was four hundred years ago, or four thousand; time moves strangely in an ember. What I know is this: there were many of us once. Hearth-gods and hill-gods, gods of thresholds and gods of thaw, small divinities who asked nothing but attention and gave nothing but small mercies. We were not mighty. We were *near*. That was our whole nature — nearness. A god you could carry in a lantern. A god whose miracles fit in a cupped hand.

Then the Conclave came with its ledgers and its iron censers, and they taught the world that nearness was superstition, that mercy without hierarchy was chaos, that fire belonged in engines and not in hearts. They did not kill us. Killing is quick and leaves ghosts. They *imprisoned* us — drew us out of our shrines with hooks of doctrine and sealed us beneath their white city, where our fire turns their wheels and lights their towers and warms no one who prays.

I escaped. Or rather, I was spilled — a drop of the great stolen blaze, flicked loose when they moved my brothers and sisters underground, too small for them to notice, too stubborn to go out. I fell back through the world like a coal falling up a chimney, and I landed in Thornfell, in a shrine so old the Conclave's maps had forgotten it, and I waited in the dark for someone to speak to me again.

Someone did. Many someones. And now, carried in a lantern through the teeth of the mountains by a runaway apprentice who smells of ink and fear, I must tell them what I have done.

* * *

The cave is shallow, a fold in the rock where the wind cannot follow, and August has built a fire at its mouth more out of habit than need — I am the only flame they require, though they do not yet know what my burning costs. Wren is out on the scree keeping watch, her silhouette sharp against the last violet of dusk. August sits cross-legged before me, the grimoire open on their knees, though they are not reading it. They are looking at me the way one looks at a door one has decided to open.

"You've been quiet," August says. "Quieter than usual."

*I am always quiet,* I say, and my voice comes out as it does — a shimmer in the air, a warmth against the ear, felt more than heard. *A god speaks when spoken to. It is the oldest rule we have.*

"Then consider yourself spoken to." August's jaw tightens. "The girl in Thornfell. Tam's daughter, Merrin. She was seven years old, and she prayed at your shrine for her mother to come home from the lowlands, and her mother came home — and three days later Merrin took a fever that no herb would break. She's still burning. Her father sent word with the smugglers." August's voice does not rise, but it sharpens, like ice forming. "Wren heard it at the last waystation. She didn't want to tell me. So I'm telling *you*. Did you do that?"

The question lands in me like a hand closing around a wick.

*Yes,* I say.

August goes very still. Outside, the wind drags its nails across the stone.

*Tell me how,* August says. Not *why* — not yet. They have learned already that gods, like people, explain themselves best when no one interrupts.

And so I tell them. I tell them about hunger.

*You have eaten today,* I say. *Bread, I think, and dried apples, and water from the snowmelt. You will eat tomorrow. Your body takes what it needs and asks no permission of the world. Faith works the same way — but in reverse. When a mortal prays, they give. Attention, hope, love — these are not metaphors to a creature like me. They are food. Every whispered plea at my shrine was a crumb, and I was starving, August. Four centuries of dark, and then crumbs. Do you understand what it is to be so hungry that even a crumb is a feast?*

"I'm starting to," August says quietly.

*A prayer is not a request,* I continue. *It is an opening. When Merrin knelt at my altar and asked for her mother, she opened herself to me the way a window opens to the wind — and I poured through. I reached into the lowlands and turned her mother's feet toward home. It cost me almost nothing. It cost the girl everything she had, though neither of us knew it. Because here is the truth the Conclave never wrote in any ledger, the truth even we gods did not fully understand until it was too late: faith is not given freely. It is taken. Every miracle draws its price from the one who believes. Their strength. Their warmth. Their years.*

August's hands have stopped moving entirely. "Merrin's fever—"

*Is my debt,* I say. *She gave so much of herself in that single prayer — a child's whole heart, held nothing back — that what remained of her could not keep her own fire lit. I answered too generously. I did not know how to answer any other way. A starving thing does not measure.* My light gutters, and I steady it with effort. *There were others. Old Marta, whose knees I mended — she sleeps sixteen hours a day now and wakes tired. The boy who prayed his brother out of the river — the brother lives, and the boy has not dreamed since. Small mercies, each one. Small prices. But I have been collecting them for a year, and the ledger is written in flesh.*

* * *

August does not speak for a long time. The fire at the cave mouth burns down to embers, and I feel a strange kinship with it — fellow remnant, fellow leftover — and a stranger feeling besides: shame. Gods are not supposed to feel shame. Shame requires a self to stand outside oneself and judge, and I had thought my self burned away long ago. Apparently it only needed witnesses.

"Vesper," August says at last, and their voice has changed — softer, but harder underneath, the way a blade wrapped in wool is still a blade. "Why didn't you stop?"

*Because stopping meant starving,* I say. *Would you blame a drowning man for drinking?*

"Yes," August says. "If he drank seawater knowing it would kill him slower and worse." They lean forward, and the grimoire slides from their knees to the stone, forgotten. "But that's not the real answer, is it. The real answer is that it felt good. Being needed. Being answered-to. After four hundred years under their city, someone finally said your name and meant it, and you couldn't bear to ration it."

I burn in silence. It is the most honest silence I have ever kept.

*Yes,* I say finally. *That is the real answer. You are angrier than I expected.*

"I'm not angry." August picks up the grimoire, holds it without opening it. "Or — I am, but not at you. Or at you too. Both things." They laugh, short and bitter. "My master used to say power corrupts like smoke stains glass — gradually, then all at once, and never quite clean again. I thought he meant Conclave magic. Engines and harvests and gods in cages." They look at me, and their eyes in my light are the color of wet slate. "He meant all of it. Even the kindly kind. Maybe especially the kindly kind."

*Your master,* I say carefully, *sold you.*

"I know. I found his ledger. We're not talking about him tonight." A pause. "We're talking about whether I'm carrying a dying god across a mountain range or carrying a plague in a lantern. Those might be the same thing."

*They are the same thing,* I say. *That is what I have brought you here to confess. Not the fever — the fever is one debt among hundreds. The confession is this: I cannot be trusted with worship. Not because I am wicked. Because I am hungry, and hunger does not consult the conscience. If you set me free in the world as I am, I will find prayers, and I will answer them, and I will hollow out every soul that loves me, gently, gratefully, one miracle at a time. The Conclave wants to harvest me with engines. Left alone, I harvest myself. There is no version of me that is safe.*

The wind shifts. Somewhere out on the scree, Wren's whistle sounds twice — all clear — and the sound falls into the silence between us like a stone into a well.

"So what do you want?" August asks. "Truly. Not what the quest wants. Not what the grimoire says. You."

And I give them the only honest thing I have left.

*I want to be reborn or I want to end,* I say. *Those are the two doors, and both must be open, and you must be the one to choose which we walk through — not because you are worthy, but because you are mortal, and mortality is the only court where a god can be judged fairly. At Mount Cinder there is a shrine older than the Conclave, older than the theft, where a dying god can be unmade and remade — new fire, new nature, a soul fed not on borrowed faith but on something freely given. Or if the shrine is lost, if the road fails, if the Conclave takes us — then you douse me. Snuff me out like a candle, and let what fire remains scatter into the world unclaimed, harmless, ordinary heat. Either ending is mercy. Only continuing is cruelty.*

"That's why you've been guiding us there," August says slowly. "Not to save yourself. To give yourself a choice."

*A god alone cannot choose,* I say. *We only ever reflect what is offered to us. That is our curse and our nature. Someone must hold the doors open, August Ravensworth. Someone with hands.*

August is quiet for a long moment. Then: "Promise me something first. If it comes to it — if rebirth means hurting someone else the way you hurt Merrin — you'll let me put you out. No arguments. No miracles to change my mind."

*I promise,* I say, and the promise costs me more than any miracle ever has, because I mean it, and meaning it feels like swallowing cold.

"And I promise you the doors," August says. "Rebirth or the dark. One or the other. I swear it on—" They stop. Start again. "On everyone I love. Which, lately, is a shorter list than it used to be, and getting shorter, and I'd like to keep what's left of it. So don't make me regret the oath."

*No god could ask for better collateral,* I say, and try to make it light, and fail, because my light is dimming — I can feel it now, honestly, clearly, the way one feels a fever coming on. The confession has emptied something in me that was not empty before. Truth, it turns out, is also a currency, and I have just spent heavily.

August notices. Of course they notice; they notice everything, this sharp-eyed, soft-hearted apprentice in whom the Conclave invested such terrible hopes. They lift the lantern closer to their chest, sheltering me from the draft, an unconscious gesture of protection that undoes me more thoroughly than any prayer.

"Rest," August says. "Whatever's left of tonight, rest. Tomorrow we walk."

*Tomorrow we walk,* I agree.

And far below us, down in the black folds of the pass, torches kindle that are not mine — a long procession of them, orderly as a ledger line, moving with the patient certainty of an engine toward its fuel. I see them before August does. Fire knows fire.

I say nothing. I have made enough confessions for one night, and the moral weight of what is coming will settle on us all soon enough, without my hastening it.

But I burn a little brighter, just for a moment, so that August will look up and follow my gaze to the ridge —

— where the torches have begun to climb.
