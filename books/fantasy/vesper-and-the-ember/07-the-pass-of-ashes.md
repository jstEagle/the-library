## 7. The Pass of Ashes

The mountain had been trying to kill them for three days, and Wren Hale was starting to take it personally.

Snow came sideways off the pass in long white lashes, stinging her cheeks raw, filling the hollows of the world until up and down became suggestions rather than facts. She walked at the front because she always walked at the front — smuggler's habit, and a superstition: whatever the road meant to take from you, it took first from whoever led. Behind her came August, hunched under a borrowed cloak with the grimoire strapped against their chest like a second heart, and behind August, carrying the lantern, walked Brother Ilen.

The lantern swung gently at his hip, and inside it the ember burned low and gold, a coal no bigger than a thumbnail that had once been a god.

"Tell me again," Ilen said, for perhaps the ninth time since dawn, "why we could not have taken the valley road."

"Because the valley road has eyes on it," Wren said, not turning around. "Because Magister Corvane knows every mile of it the way you know your own prayers. Because up here there's nothing to burn and nowhere to build a pyre." She spat into the wind, which immediately threw it back in her face. "And because my mother's maps say this pass connects to the old shrine stair, and my mother's maps have never once lied to me."

"They've never once been *right* on time, either," August muttered.

Wren allowed herself a thin smile. That was the thing about August Ravensworth — three weeks on the road and they still apologized to the weather when it turned against them, still said *thank you* to innkeeps who cheated them, still flinched at loud voices like a dog that had learned what boots were for. But they hadn't quit. Whatever Corvane had done to them, whatever lies he'd fed them across all those years in his tower, he had failed to teach them surrender. Wren found herself thinking about that more than she should. About the set of August's shoulders when they were afraid. About how their hand had found hers in the dark of the last waystation, briefly, wordlessly, before snatching back as if burned.

She put the thought away where she kept all her dangerous cargo.

The ember flickered, and Vesper spoke — a voice like wind through a cold hearth, so quiet they had to stop walking to hear it.

"The snow is wrong here."

Wren crouched by the lantern. Up close, the god looked less like fire than like a memory of fire, its light trembling at the edges as though it might forget itself at any moment. "Wrong how?"

"It does not fall. It is *placed*." A pause, the ember dimming and brightening like a slow breath. "There is a will in this pass that is not ours."

Ilen went very still. He had gone very still like that ever since the pass began narrowing, and Wren had told herself it was just an acolyte's nerves, a Conclave man missing his incense and his bells. But now she watched him turn slowly, scanning the white walls of the defile above them, and she saw the color drain out of his face beneath the windburn.

"Wren," he said quietly. "How wide is this pass at its narrowest?"

"Fifty paces. Maybe forty."

"And how many men would it take to close it?"

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. Above them, at the lip of the eastern ridge, something moved — a shadow where no shadow should be, then another, and then the snow itself seemed to stand up, figures rising out of drifts where they'd lain buried and waiting, grey-cloaked, ash-marked, their breath pluming in disciplined unison.

Hunters. A dozen of them at least, and below, at the mouth of the pass they'd just come through, more emerging from the rocks like teeth from a jaw.

"*Down!*" Ilen shouted, and the world came apart.

* * *

The first arrow took the lantern handle out of Ilen's grip — not the lantern, Wren realized later, never the lantern; they wanted the god whole — and it spun away into a drift, ember guttering, and Vesper cried out, a sound so small and so old that Wren felt it in her back teeth.

Then everything was motion and noise. August dragging the lantern from the snow, cradling it, shouting something Wren couldn't hear over the wind. Hunters sliding down the slope in controlled falls, blades already drawn, moving with the terrible economy of people who had done this many times and enjoyed it only moderately. And Ilen — Ilen stepping *forward*, past her, past August, putting himself between them and the closing line of grey cloaks with nothing but a pilgrim's staff and the training the Conclave had given him and which he was about to spend against them.

"Ilen!" Wren grabbed his sleeve. "There's a scree chute, two hundred yards north, we can all—"

"No." He said it gently, which was somehow worse. His eyes found hers, and in them was none of the prickly, pedantic acolyte who had argued with her about theology for three weeks, who corrected her pronunciation of dead languages, who blushed when she caught him watching her laugh. There was only a man doing arithmetic he hated. "They're not here for me, Wren. They're here for the ember, and they'll chase it to the ends of the world unless someone makes stopping it cost more than they'll pay." He pulled free of her grip. "Someone has to hold the line."

"You'll die."

"I'm a defector," he said, and smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen. "I died the day I walked out of that column. Everything since is a gift I didn't expect." He turned, raised his staff, and called out in the high liturgical voice of the Ashen Conclave — words Wren half-knew, an old rite of challenge, one voice standing surety against many.

Then he ran at them, and Wren learned what a man who had spent his life among books could do when he finally believed in something.

* * *

August hauled her north by the arm, toward the scree chute, and Wren let herself be hauled because the alternative was turning around, and if she turned around she would stop, and if she stopped they would both die for nothing.

Behind them: shouting, the ring of steel on wood, a hunter screaming. The wind tore the sounds apart and scattered them.

"He'll be captured, not killed," August gasped, stumbling beside her. "Corvane wants — he'll want to question him — Wren, *listen*, he'll be taken alive—"

"You don't know that."

"I know Corvane." Something dark crossed August's face, the curdled knowledge of chapter five, of the ledger, of the years that had been a lie. "He doesn't waste things. Not people. Not pain. He'll keep Ilen breathing because Ilen is useful." Their voice cracked on the word. "That's the best I can give you. It isn't much."

"It isn't anything," Wren said, but she climbed.

The scree chute was a throat of broken stone choked with snow, near-vertical, the kind of route her mother had loved precisely because no sane person would use it. Wren went first, finding holds by feel, fingers screaming, and above her the wind suddenly *changed* — it went warm.

She looked back.

The lantern in August's pack was blazing. Not guttering — *blazing*, gold light pouring through the seams of the shuttered tin, and where the light fell the snow ahead of them softened, slid, parted, opening a path up the chute as though the mountain itself had been asked politely to move. Vesper was spending itself. Burning years, decades, whatever a dying god counted its life in, to buy three fugitives a hundred feet of climbable stone.

"*Stop it,*" Wren shouted up at August. "Tell it to stop! It can't afford—"

"I've told it! It won't—" August's face was wet, and not from snowmelt. "*Vesper, please—*"

The warmth pulsed once, almost tenderly, and in Wren's skull, soft as a hand on a fevered brow, the god said: *I have hoarded so long. Let me be generous, once, while it is still mine to give.*

Then the light snapped back down to a coal, smaller than before, dimmer, and the cold rushed in like a debt collector, and they climbed the rest of the chute in darkness and silence.

* * *

They came out on the shoulder of the mountain as the sun went down, a vast white saddle under a sky the color of a bruise healing. Below and behind, far down in the pass, torches moved in a slow, orderly pattern — hunters sweeping, securing, and one knot of them gathered around a single kneeling figure who was, even now, even at this distance, sitting with his spine straight as a candle.

Alive. Captured. Being marched west, toward the column, toward Corvane.

Wren stood in the wind and watched until the torchlight swallowed him, and she did not cry, because crying froze on your face at this altitude and because she had promised herself at fourteen, over her father's body, that she would never again watch something being taken without memorizing exactly what it cost. So she memorized. The angle of his shoulders. The grey cloaks around him. The way the snow held his footprints going west, each one a small dark wound in the white.

"We go back," she said at last. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Tonight. Now. Before they reach the column."

"With what?" August stood beside her, the lantern held against their chest, and their exhaustion had a glassy, feverish edge. "Twelve hunters, Wren. A Conclave magister behind them. Two of us and a god who just burned most of what's left of itself to get us up a hill." They lifted the lantern. Inside, the ember was barely visible now, a red thread in the tin dark, pulsing slow as a failing heart. "Vesper can't fight. Vesper can barely *speak*. If we go back, we lose everything — Ilen *and* the ember *and* the shrine, and then Corvane harvests a god and feeds it to the empire, and every village that ever whispered a prayer burns for it."

"I know the arithmetic," Wren said. "I hate the arithmetic."

"I know." August's hand found hers in the dark, and this time it did not snatch back. "I hate it too."

They stood there while the light died completely, two small warm shapes on a cold white shoulder of the world, and Wren understood, with the clarity that grief sometimes grants, that this was the real shape of the road — not the map, not the plan, but this: the constant weighing of what you could afford to lose, and the discovery, every time, that the answer was *more than you had*.

Below them, far to the west, a new light kindled in the pass. Not torchlight. Something colder, whiter, arranged in a perfect geometric pattern that no campfire ever made — and even at this distance Wren felt it against her skin like the pressure before lightning.

August made a small sound.

"What?" Wren said. "What is it?"

"That's the harvest-engine," August whispered. "It's already here. Wren — it's already *here*."
