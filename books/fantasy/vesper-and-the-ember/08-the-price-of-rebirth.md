## 8. The Price of Rebirth

The shrine had no door.

That was the first thing August understood about the place the maps called Mount Cinder's heart: you did not enter it so much as it permitted you. The threshold was a seam in the mountain itself, a fissure tall as a cathedral arch, its edges fused smooth like glass poured and cooled a thousand years ago. Warm air breathed out of it, slow and tidal, smelling of woodsmoke and rain on hot stone — the smell, August realized with a lurch, of every hearth they had ever known. Of Master Ellery's cottage on winter mornings. Of the tower kitchen where Corvane had let them warm their hands exactly once, as if granting a favor.

Behind them, the pass they had crossed lay silent under new snow. No sign of pursuit. That silence frightened August more than hoofbeats would have. Corvane did not lose trails. Corvane *allowed* them, the way a fisherman allows line.

"Say something," Wren said quietly. She stood at August's shoulder, frost in her dark hair, her hand resting on the knife she hadn't stopped touching since Ilen fell. "You've been staring at that rock for a full minute."

"It's not rock." August lifted the lantern. Inside, the ember pulsed — dimmer now, its light gone from gold to the deep bruised orange of a coal at midnight. Vesper had spent nearly everything shielding them in the Pass of Ashes. When the god spoke, its voice came thin as paper through the cold. *"Closer,"* it whispered. *"Please. It is very dark here, and I am very small."*

August stepped across the seam.

* * *

Inside, the mountain opened into a hollow vast enough to hold weather. Stalactites hung like frozen chandeliers, and every surface glittered faintly with veins of the same glassy substance as the threshold — as though the whole chamber had once been fire and had been caught mid-flow and told to stop. In the center of the floor rose a dais of black stone, and on the dais, a shallow basin shaped like an open hand.

Old carvings covered the walls. August raised the lantern and walked them slowly, reading by the light of a dying god.

Gods, hundreds of them, rendered in profile: figures crowned with flames, with rivers, with antlers and wheat-sheaves and storms. And around them, always, smaller figures — mortals, kneeling, arms raised, and between each mortal and each god ran a carved thread, a filament connecting heart to heart. The old faith, rendered plainly: not worship as submission, but worship as circulation. Faith flowing out; grace flowing back.

Then the frieze turned a corner, and the story changed.

August stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into them.

The next panel showed the same gods, but their threads were being cut. Figures in gray robes — robed figures whose faces had been deliberately chiseled away, all of them, centuries ago, by hands that wanted no one to know who they had been — stood among the kneeling crowds holding long hooked instruments. They cut the filaments one by one and gathered them into vessels, and the gods in the carving grew dimmer panel by panel, their crowns guttering, until the final panel showed only the gray-robed figures standing triumphant over empty thrones, holding up a single flame between them like a stolen jewel.

"The Conclave," Wren breathed. "Their founders. This is what they did."

"This is what they *were*." August's voice sounded strange in the hollow, flattened by stone. "Ellery's grimoire wasn't a spellbook. It was a ledger. A record of theft." They had known this since the hollow, since the night they'd shown Wren the ciphered pages — the names of ninety-one gods, each followed by a date and a location and a word that August had translated weeks ago and not dared say aloud: *harvested*. But knowing the history was different from standing inside it, inside the wound itself, with the last living scrap of everything stolen trembling in a lantern at their hip.

Vesper's light flickered toward the carvings. *"I remember this wall,"* the god said softly. *"I helped carve it. Before. When there were enough of us to hold a chisel steady."* A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: *"There is one more panel, child. Around the far side. We saved the truth for the end, because truth is heavy and should be carried last."*

August found the final panel alone. Wren had gone back to watch the threshold — she would not sit with her back to an open door, not after the pass — and August was almost grateful for the solitude, because what they read there, they read twice before their legs agreed to hold them.

It was not a picture. It was an instruction, carved in the old liturgical script that Ellery had taught them letter by letter over seven years of candlelit lessons, praising them for a quick mind, for careful hands, for a memory like a steel trap.

*To wake a harvested god, feed it a soul unspent.*

*A mortal must kneel at the basin and give the ember its dearest treasure: every memory it holds of those it loves. Face, voice, touch, name. The raw material of divinity is love, and love is made of remembering. What is given cannot be kept. What is given does not return.*

*Choose one who loves much, or the rebirth will be small and quick to die.*

August read it a third time. The words did not change. The mountain's breath moved warm against their face, patient as a tide, and somewhere behind them the lantern guttered, and Vesper said nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

They thought, absurdly, of the smell of bread. Ellery's kitchen. The way the old woman had hummed off-key when she stirred the pot, and how she'd ruffle August's hair with flour-dusted fingers, and how she'd said — on the last morning, the morning August fled the tower with the grimoire strapped beneath their coat — *you are worth more than what they'll use you for, little wren-of-my-heart.* They held that memory up against the carved instruction and felt it begin, already, to loosen. To become a thing they might set down.

Grief arrived first as anger, which was familiar; anger had been August's native language since the tower. But beneath the anger was something colder and more precise, assembling itself out of fragments they'd spent years refusing to look at directly.

Corvane had taken them in at six years old, after the plague took Thornfell's low quarter. Corvane, who never embraced them. Who taught them languages and star-charts and the grammar of prayer, but never once asked what they dreamed of. Who arranged their education with the meticulousness of a man building a specific instrument for a specific purpose. Who had, according to the ledger pages they'd found in the waystation — pages tracking a child named Ravensworth across eleven years, annotated in that narrow, patient hand — *sold* them, before they could even write their own name, to a patron who required a vessel of particular qualities.

And here were the qualities, carved in stone older than any empire: someone who loved much. Someone whose memories would be rich enough to feed a god. Someone raised just loved enough to have a harvest, and just unloved enough that no one would come looking when the harvest was taken.

*I am worth more than what they'll use you for,* Ellery had said. But Ellery herself had bought them from that patron, had raised them in a cottage full of warmth precisely because warmth was what the recipe called for. Had they been loved, or *cultivated*? Was there a difference? Could there be, when the gardener knew from the beginning what the flower was for?

August slid down the wall until they were sitting, the lantern cradled in their lap like a sick bird. The stone was warm. Everything here was warm. It felt like mockery.

"I can hear you thinking from here," Wren said. She had come back without August noticing — she moved like that, smuggler-quiet — and she lowered herself onto the floor beside them, shoulder to shoulder, offering nothing else. That was Wren's gift: she never demanded a wound be shown. She simply sat near it.

"The price," August said. Their voice cracked on the second word and they hated it. "The rebirth needs memories. All of them. Everyone I—" They couldn't finish. They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "Everyone I love, Wren. Gone. Not dead — worse than dead. Dead people stay remembered. This is just *gone*, like it never happened, like Ellery never hummed, like you never—"

They stopped. The sentence had been heading somewhere dangerous.

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was roughened but level. "In the Hollow, you told me the Conclave stole the gods' fire and built an empire on it. You said somebody had to carry it back or every village that ever prayed gets burned for it. Thornfell. My mum's grave is in Thornfell." She turned to look at them, and in the ember-light her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "I'm not going to tell you it's fine. It isn't fine. It's the worst bargain I've ever heard, and I've heard some rotten ones. But don't you dare decide you're cheap, August Ravensworth. Whatever Corvane built you to be, he doesn't get to have decided what you're worth. That's yours."

From the lantern, very softly, Vesper said: *"I do not want your memories, child. Understand that. I have watched mortals for three thousand years and I have never once envied anything but this — that you get to keep each other. If you give this, it must be because you choose it. A gift given from despair makes a starving god."*

"And if I don't?" August whispered. "If I keep myself?"

*"Then I die here, small and dark, and the Conclave finds another way to make fire, and the burning continues. Or perhaps something worse learns to grow in the dark I leave behind."* The ember pulsed once, slow, like a tired heartbeat. *"I will not lie to you. There is no version of this where no one pays. There is only the question of who chooses the coin."*

August looked down at their hands — Ellery's training in them, Corvane's shaping, Wren's bandage wrapped around the left palm from the pass. A vessel. An instrument. Eleven years of annotations in a patient gray hand.

But also: a choice. The one thing the ledger had never accounted for.

They were still sitting there, the instruction glowing behind their eyes and the ember breathing in their lap, when Wren went rigid beside them, head tilting toward the threshold.

"Snow's crunching," she said. "Lots of boots. Coming slow." She drew her knife. "They're not even hurrying."

Because they didn't need to hurry. Because the trap had been sprung years ago, in a plague-orphanage, in a cottage chosen like an ingredient, and everyone had simply been walking along the inside of it ever since.

Far down the tunnel, torchlight bloomed against the ice-glass walls — dozens of torches, and among them a light that was not torchlight at all: the cold white glare of the Conclave's harvest-engine, humming like a hive of metal wasps. And rising above the boot-sounds, unhurried, unhurried, unhurried, came a voice August had obeyed for eleven years, echoing warm and reasonable off the walls of the god's own tomb:

"August. Come out, child. Let's not make this difficult — we both know how it ends."
