## 9. Emberfall

The mountain heard them coming before anyone saw them.

August felt it first as a tremor in the lantern — the ember shivering against its glass like a moth against a window. Then the sound arrived, rolling up the switchbacks from the valley below: the groan of iron wheels on stone, the creak of harnesses, the low chanting of a hundred throats keeping cadence.

"They brought it," Ilen had said, three days ago, before the pass took him. "The whole engine. They don't send an engine for one runaway."

Now August stood at the threshold of the shrine and watched the harvest-engine come around the shoulder of Mount Cinder like a wound being opened.

It was worse than the grimoire's diagrams had promised. A cathedral on wheels — that was the only honest way to describe it. Iron ribs arched over a central furnace of black glass, and within that glass something moved, something hungry, something that drank light rather than gave it. Chains hung from its flanks, and from each chain swung censers of consecrated ash, and the smoke they poured did not rise but *fell*, pooling along the ground like water finding its level, smothering the alpine flowers, graying the snow.

Behind it marched the column: acolytes in ash-gray robes, inquisitors with their faces masked in porcelain, and at their center, bound hand and foot but walking anyway because Corvane never wasted a prisoner's legs, Brother Ilen.

Even at this distance August could see the blood on his collar.

Beside them, Wren made a sound low in her throat, half word, half animal.

"Ilen," she said. Just the name. As if saying it were a kind of prayer, and prayers were dangerous things now, prayers were how gods got hold of you.

"He's alive," August said. "Corvane needs him alive. He needs all of us alive." They touched the grimoire through their coat, felt its weight like a second heart. "That's the whole point of me, isn't it? A vessel kept unloved so the sacrifice would be cheap."

Wren turned to look at them, and her eyes were bright and furious and afraid. "You're not cheap."

"No," August said, and found, to their surprise, that they believed it. "I'm not."

The ember pulsed in the lantern, once, twice — a heartbeat quickening. And beneath the wind and the wheel-groan and the chanting, so faint it might have been imagination, August heard Vesper's voice, thin as paper:

*They are here. Little thief. The door is open. Choose quickly.*

* * *

Magister Corvane climbed the last stretch of road on foot, which surprised August. He had always been a man of carriages and towers, of distance and leverage. But here he came up the switchbacks himself, boots crunching frost, his gray coat immaculate despite the ash-fall, his face bare of the porcelain mask the other inquisitors wore. He wanted August to see him. That was the message. *You know me. You know what I am to you.*

He stopped ten paces away. Behind him the engine idled, its black furnace churning, and August felt it pulling at them even now — a faint tug behind the eyes, like a hook set in memory itself.

"August." Corvane said their name gently, the way he'd said it across a decade of lessons. *August, the conjugation is wrong. August, you've bled on the page again. August, magic is precision, and precision is love.* "You look well. The mountain air suits you better than my tower ever did. I regret that."

"You regret nothing," August said. "You sold me before I could walk. You raised me in a cage and called it kindness."

"I raised you in *safety*." Corvane spread his hands, and for a moment — a terrible moment — he sounded sincere. "Do you know what the Conclave would have done with a god-touched child, if I hadn't claimed you first? Do you know what the patrons wanted? I bought you years. I taught you to read, to think, to survive. Everything you are, you are because I built you."

"Everything I am," August said, "is standing between you and that door."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — not hurt, exactly. Recalculation. He was always recalculating; August could almost see the ledger turning behind his eyes, columns of cost and yield.

"Then let us speak plainly, as I taught you." He gestured, and two inquisitors dragged Ilen forward and forced him to his knees in the trampled snow. Ilen's face was swollen, one eye shut, but when he saw Wren at the shrine's threshold his mouth twisted into something that was trying very hard to be a smile. "Your old life, August. Your books, your laboratory, your name cleared — no one hunts a runaway who never ran. The Conclave will declare this whole affair a heresy of Thornfell's making. You will want for nothing. You will be *safe*. All you must do is place the lantern in my hands, and walk away, and live."

"And the village?" Wren's voice cracked across the snow like a whip. "Thornfell. The households already burned. The neighbors already broken on your racks. Do they get to walk away and live?"

Corvane didn't even turn his head. "Heresy is a fire, girl. Fires require fuel."

"There it is," Wren said softly. "There's the whole Conclave, in one sentence."

"August." Corvane stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and this was the cruelest thing he'd done yet, because he used the tone from all those late nights in the tower, the mentor's tone, the almost-father's tone. "You have read the final page. You know what that door costs. Memories, August. Everyone you love, poured out like wine. And we both know—" his gaze flicked, deliberate and surgical, to Wren, to the lantern, back— "*we both know you have so little worth losing.*"

The words went in like a blade finding an old scar. August felt the truth of them, the engineered truth, the truth Corvane had spent seventeen years carving into them: *unloved, unlovable, cheap.*

And then they felt the ember answer.

Warmth bloomed up through their ribs, and Vesper's voice came not faint now but fierce, a hearth roaring up against a winter night:

*Lies taste of ash, little thief. You know this. You have carried me long enough to know.*

August lifted their chin. "You're right," they said to Corvane. "I was raised to have nothing worth losing. So I built it myself. On the road. Out of smugglers and deserters and a dying god who talks too much." They held the lantern out — not toward Corvane, but toward the shrine's dark doorway. "You can't buy back what you never owned."

For the first time in August's memory, Magister Corvane looked genuinely surprised.

Then he sighed, and stepped back, and said, "Take them."

* * *

The engine woke like a beast scenting blood.

Its furnace flared, and the falling ash reversed itself, rising in a black blizzard, and the chains began to swing, each censer ringing a note that scraped against the bones. The inquisitors advanced in ranks, porcelain masks blank as eggs, wands of consecrated iron leveled. And above it all Corvane's voice rang out, liturgical and cold: *"By ash and order, by silence and law — what burned shall stay burned!"*

What happened next, August would later remember in fragments, the way one remembers a storm.

Wren moved first. She always moved first; it was her defining feature, the thing August loved most and feared most about her. She hurled herself sideways off the shrine's threshold, rolled down into the rocks below the path, and came up with a signal-horn already at her lips — she'd carried it since the pass, she confessed later, since the moment Ilen fell, hoping without admitting she hoped.

Three notes, ragged and wild, echoed off Mount Cinder's face.

And the mountain answered.

They came out of the high scree like ghosts given bodies: lean figures in patched leathers, faces scarred by wind, carrying hooks and hatchets and slingshots and, absurdly, one enormous cooking pot wielded as a shield. Smugglers. Wren's kin — the network of cut paths and hidden ledgers she'd grown up inside, the family of the roads that no empire had ever managed to map or burn.

"*HALE!*" roared a woman with a beard braided into two gray ropes, and it was less a greeting than a battle cry.

The lines collided where the path narrowed, and the narrowness was everything — twenty Conclave soldiers could not bring their numbers to bear on forty smugglers who knew every stone. Arrows sang down from heights the inquisitors hadn't thought to watch. A censer chain was cut and its holy ash spilled harmlessly into the snow, hissing.

Through the chaos, Ilen fought.

Later, Wren would describe it with something like awe: a man with bound wrists and a shattered eye, throwing himself backward off his knees into the legs of his captors, screaming — not words, at first, just sound, and then words: "*Vesper!* You're real, you're *real*, I heard you, I heard you in the dark—" An acolyte raised an iron wand over him. Wren was thirty feet away, buried in a tangle of masked bodies. It was the bearded woman, Brannock, who threw the hatchet.

It took the wand, not the man. The acolyte hesitated — one heartbeat of doubt, doctrine cracking against the impossible evidence of a god answering a prayer — and in that heartbeat Ilen rolled free, and Wren reached him, and hauled him up, and for one suspended moment they stood pressed together amid the whirl of the battle, his blood on her hands, neither of them saying anything at all.

"Go," she finally choked. "Get to the door. We'll hold them."

But the engine was not done.

It had been waiting, patient as doctrine, and now it opened. The black furnace split along seams August hadn't seen, and from within it came a sound like a choir played backward, and the harvest began. August felt it instantly — a raking pull through the skull, memories tearing loose like leaves in a gale. Around them, smugglers staggered mid-fight, forgetting which way home lay. Wren cried out and clutched her head. Even Corvane's own acolytes dropped, noses bleeding, emptied a little.

The engine drank indiscriminately. Doctrine did not care whose faith it consumed.

And the lantern — the lantern guttered.

August saw it happen. Saw the ember shrink, dim, curl inward like a hand closing. Vesper was spending itself, pouring out what remained, wrapping the battlefield in a thin warm shield that blunted the engine's pull — *mine*, the god was whispering, *they are mine, these small faithful furious people, mine to shield* — and every second of shielding was a second closer to going out forever.

*Little thief,* Vesper gasped. *The door. While there is still enough of me left to be born.*

August ran.

Up the frozen steps, past the threshold stones carved with names worn smooth by centuries, into the shrine's throat — and stopped, because Corvane was there.

Of course he was. He had never sent others to do what he could do himself. He stood between August and the altar, and he had abandoned rhetoric entirely; there was only the iron wand in his hand and the arithmetic in his eyes.

"You will destroy yourself," he said, "for a god. For a *smuggler*. For nothing."

"For something," August said. "That's the difference between us. You were born full and have been emptying ever since. I was born empty, and I filled myself up." They raised the lantern between them, and its dying light caught them both. "You taught me precision, Master. Here is the most precise thing I know:"

They dropped the lantern.

Glass burst on the ancient stone. Fire leapt — not up, but *out*, a bloom of ember-light that washed the shrine gold — and in that light August seized the grimoire, tore free the final page, the page of price, and spoke the words they had read until they knew them better than their own name.

The harvest-engine screamed outside. Whether it was Corvane's machine breaking or the mountain itself objecting, August couldn't tell — they only knew that the black furnace *shattered*, that the engine's ribs burst outward like a chest failing to contain its heart, that the stolen fire of dead gods came home at last in a river of light that poured up the steps, past the fighting, past the kneeling, past everything, into the open door.

Into August.

The last thing they saw clearly was Wren's face — terror and fury and love all fighting for the same inches — and the last thing they thought, as their memories rose like sparks off a bonfire, was: *worth it, whatever remains, worth it.*

Then the fire took them, and the door closed, and Mount Cinder held its breath.

* * *

Outside, the snow fell soft onto broken iron.

Brannock helped Wren bind Ilen's ribs by the light of a smuggler's cookfire, while around them the surviving inquisitors knelt weaponless in the drifts, masks removed, faces stunned and young. Somewhere down the valley, the Conclave's column was dissolving into rumor.

The shrine door stayed shut through the night, through the grey dawn, through the long white morning.

And then, as the sun crested Cinder's shoulder, it opened — and light walked out.

Not the starving flicker of the lantern-ember. Something taller, steadier, wearing a shape like a person made of hearthlight, with August's silhouette at its center like a figure seen through flame. It paused beside Wren. It inclined its head, courteous and new.

And August — changed, emptied, *beginning* — smiled at her with the ghost of recognition, and reached out one hand as if remembering, halfway, where it was meant to go.
