## 10. What Remains of August Ravensworth

The frost came early that year, and with it, the fires.

Wren Hale stood in the doorway of the rebuilt Hale house — her mother's house, though her mother had been three years dead and the roof had been open to the sky for two of them — and watched the hearth-flame settle into the stones like a bird folding its wings. It burned without wood. It burned without smoke. It burned the color of late evening, all violet and ember-gold, and when you looked at it too long you felt the strange, aching certainty that someone, somewhere, was glad you existed.

"Stop staring at it," August said from the table. "It gets smug."

"It's a god."

"It's a *houseguest*." August said it dryly, but their hand had gone, without their noticing, to the place below their collarbone where the last of the old ember had gone into them and come out again changed. Wren had learned to read that gesture the way she'd once read weather off the mountain: it meant August was reaching for something that wasn't there anymore.

Three weeks since Emberfall. Three weeks since the harvest-engine came apart in a shriek of white fire and Corvane's column broke against smugglers and villagers and one defector acolyte who fought like a man settling accounts. Three weeks since August Ravensworth walked up the shrine steps alone and did not walk back down as the same person.

Wren crossed the room and sat across from them. On the table between them lay the morning's news, carried up from the lowlands by her cousin Tam: the Conclave's Grand Synod had excommunicated three provincial chapters for "doctrinal deviation," which everyone understood to mean those chapters had started keeping hearth-flames of their own. Two inquisitor-lords had been found dead, their seals broken and laid on their own altars. The empire's fire, hoarded for four hundred years behind doctrine and iron, was leaking out through ten thousand village chimneys, and no edict could call it home.

"They're calling it the Scattering now," Wren said. "In Thornfell they're saying Vesper chose it. That the god looked at the throne of heaven and turned it down."

August turned the page without reading it. "Do you think it's true?"

"I think I've met the god." Wren nodded toward the hearth. "I think it would rather warm a room than rule it."

* * *

Vesper still visited.

Not the starving thing from the lantern — that creature had gone into the rebirth like a moth into flame, spent down to nothing, and what rose from Mount Cinder's heart was something else entirely. Larger and smaller at once. It no longer needed to be carried; it simply *was*, wherever a hearth was kept, wherever someone lit a candle for a neighbor or left bread on a sick man's sill. But it kept a particular watch over this house, over this valley, and sometimes in the hour before dawn it gathered itself into a shape at the edge of the firelight — a suggestion of wings made of banked coals, an eye like the last coal in a dying grate — and it spoke.

It spoke gently. That was the worst of it, some nights. The old Vesper had been desperate, hungry, half-mad with starvation. This one had the terrible calm of a thing that had died once already and found nothing on the other side worth fearing.

"You reach for her in your sleep," Vesper said that night, when August had gone up to bed and Wren sat alone with the fire. Its voice came from everywhere the light touched. "Every night. Your hand goes out across the blanket, searching."

"Does he know?"

"He knows his hand does it. He doesn't know why." A pause, and the flames bent low, almost apologetic. "I could give it back to him, Wren. A fragment. A name, a face, one true memory. I hold everything he fed me — every laugh, every kindness, every ordinary morning. It is not lost. It is *mine* now, which means it is offered."

Wren stared into the violet-gold. Her chest felt tight in a way she refused to examine. "And what would it cost him?"

"That is the question you always ask." Something like warmth moved through the room. "Nothing. Nothing but the learning of it. To be handed your own heart back after you'd made peace with its absence — that is not a small thing. Some wounds are cleaner than their healing."

"Then don't." Wren heard her own voice go hard, smuggler-hard, the voice that closed deals and doors. "Don't give it back. Let him grow new ones. Let him—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He reaches for me in his sleep and doesn't know my name. And he stays anyway. Every day he chooses this house, these people, without knowing why. That's not a wound, Vesper. That's the truest thing anyone's ever done for me."

The fire said nothing for a long time. When it spoke again, it sounded almost human. Almost young.

"When I was dying in that lantern, I thought faith was food. Something taken." The embers shifted. "He taught me otherwise without meaning to. Faith is what remains when memory burns away. You are teaching me that too."

* * *

Brother Ilen arrived on the fourth week, walking up the smuggler's road with a pack on his back and no robes at all, just a wool coat and a face that had lost the look of a man carrying a verdict. He'd been south, to the Conclave's heart, and come back with news that tasted like iron.

"The Synod's split clean down the middle," he told them, sitting at August's table, eating like a man who'd learned hunger was real. "Half of them want to declare the Scattering a plague and burn every village that keeps a flame. The other half have started praying to Vesper in secret and hoping nobody checks. Corvane's alive — barely. They pulled him out of the mountain with his mind full of white fire, and now he sits in a cell reciting the names of gods that died before he was born." Ilen set down his cup. "There are those who want him questioned. There are others who want him forgotten. And there are more every week who stop asking about either and just... light their hearths."

"And the harvest-engine?" August asked. Their voice was careful. Wren knew they were doing it — building sentences slowly, checking each word against a ledger of things they were supposed to feel. Grief for a master who had never loved them. Relief at a danger ended. The memories had been taken, but the habits of feeling remained, like a scar that aches in cold weather.

"Gone. The design burned with it. Though—" Ilen hesitated, and Wren saw him choose honesty over comfort, which was new for him, and cost him. "Though there are rumors. Men who worked on the engine, scattered now, selling what they remember. If the Conclave falls far enough, someone will try to build another."

"Then we'll break that one too," Wren said.

August looked at her then — really looked, the way they'd been looking more and more lately, like a man reading a letter in a language he'd almost recovered. "You say *we*," they said. "You keep saying *we*."

"You got a problem with that?"

"No." August's mouth did something complicated, half a smile and half a wince. "I just wish I knew why it feels like coming home."

* * *

That night Wren woke to find August standing at the window, wrapped in a blanket, watching the snow come down through the light of the hearth-flame that had followed them upstairs — because it did that now, it went where August went, patient as a dog, devoted as a debt.

"Bad dream?" she asked.

"I don't know if it counts as a dream if you can't remember it." August didn't turn around. "But my hand was out again. Reaching. And this time—" They lifted the hand, studied it in the firelight. "This time there was a name in my mouth when I woke. Half of one. *Ren.* Just that. Like the rest of the word burned off."

Wren's heart did something she'd have denied under torture. "Could be anything," she managed. "Could be a wren. Stupid little birds, always flying into windows."

"Mm." August turned. In the violet light their face was unreadable and completely readable at once. "The god says I gave something up at the shrine. Everything I loved. It says it's keeping it safe." A pause. "It says I can have it back whenever I ask."

"And?"

"And I asked it what I'd be getting." August came to sit on the edge of the bed, elbows on knees, hands hanging — the hands of a person deciding something. "It said: a life. People. A road through mountains with a girl who argued with me the entire way and never once left. It said the memories are good ones. It said they're *mine*."

The snow ticked against the window. Downstairs, the hearth-flame breathed.

"So what did you tell it?" Wren whispered.

"I told it I'd think about it." August looked up, and there was something raw and steady in their face, something that had survived the burning of everything and chosen to keep growing anyway. "Because here's what I figured out, Ren — and don't ask how I know your name starts that way, I just do — whoever I was before, he paid everything he had so that something kind could be born. That was his whole self, spent in one act. And I'm what's left. So maybe the best thing I can do isn't take the old life back." Their hand crossed the space between the chairs and found hers, deliberate this time, awake this time, choosing. "Maybe the best thing is to build one worth the price."

Outside, the storm deepened. Somewhere south, an empire cracked along its seams, and god-fire spread from chimney to chimney faster than any inquisitor could follow. And in a small house above Thornfell, a woman who had never believed in anything held the hand of a person who had given up everything to believe for her, while a young god — reborn, unbound, and free — kept its gentle watch over them both, and waited, without hurry, to see what they would make of the morning.
