## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The dead of Vesper did not shout. They murmured—like rain on a roof, like a conversation in the next room you couldn't quite make out—and after twenty-six years of it, Tamsin Halloran had learned to work with the sound the way other people worked with a hearth: close enough for warmth, far enough not to burn.

They sat at the workbench by the eastern window, where the morning light came down the valley clean and gray, and carved a shinbone with a burin older than they were. The bone belonged to Old Marta Cade, who had died eleven days ago of her heart simply deciding it was finished, and who had spent her last hour instructing Tamsin on exactly what to put on it. *Not my name first,* she'd said, breath rattling like a loose shutter. *My name last. Put the garden first. I was a gardener longer than I was anything else.*

So Tamsin carved a row of bean flowers along the length of the bone, each petal no bigger than a grain of barley, and beneath them, in the tight looping script that only bone-carvers learned and only the dead could read, the story of her life as she had told it. When the carving was done and the bone set into the cairn above her grave, she would be able to find herself again. That was the whole point. That was all of it.

*Careful with the third petal,* Marta's voice said through the bone, faint as breath on glass. *I never liked that one.*

"The third petal is fine," Tamsin said to the empty workshop.

*I never liked it,* the bone insisted, mildly, the way the dead insisted on everything. They had no urgency left. That was the mercy of dying in Vesper. Whatever else went out of you, so did the hurry.

Tamsin adjusted the third petal anyway. It cost nothing to be kind to the dead, and in this valley, kindness to the dead was most of the economy.

Outside, the morning was doing what Vesper mornings did: lying about how warm it would be. Frost still held the shadows under the pear trees. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, unhurried columns because there was no wind—the valley never had wind, not really, not since before anyone living had been born. The ridgeline stood around them on every side like cupped hands, dark with pine, and at the top of it, invisible but always present, the ward hummed.

Everyone in Vesper could feel the hum if they paid attention. It wasn't sound, exactly. It was more like the pressure behind your ears when you dive too deep in the millpond, a fullness, a *no*. Magic does not cross the ridge line. It had not crossed it in living memory. The elders said it had been that way since the founding, since the valley's first families had fled something terrible and paid—nobody knew what—to be forgotten by it. The ward kept magic out. It also, though nobody said this part aloud very often, kept everything else out too. No traders came up the pass anymore. No pilgrims. No letters. The world beyond the ridge had become something like a story people told children to make them eat their supper, and Vesper had settled into its own company the way an old woman settles into a chair she can no longer get out of.

Tamsin had never minded. Why would they? Everything they loved was here: the workshop with its smell of chalk and marrow-oil, the rows of memorial cairns climbing the hillside like a stone orchard, the murmur of the dead keeping them company from dawn until sleep took them under. They were the valley's bone-carver, the fourth in an unbroken line, and if their life was small, it was small the way a seed is small. Full. Waiting.

They were halfway through the fifth petal when the knock came.

Nobody knocked on the workshop door before noon. The dead didn't knock at all—they just got louder—and the living knew better than to interrupt a carving mid-line, because a broken line meant a confused ghost, and a confused ghost meant weeks of quiet complaints drifting through the cairns like smoke through a keyhole.

But the knock came again, three raps, apologetic.

"Door's open," Tamsin called, not looking up.

It was Pell Wicker, the sexton, and he was wearing the face he wore for funerals, which was bad, because Pell wore his funeral face even for funerals half the time. He was a round man made rounder by forty years of carrying other people's grief, and he stood in the doorway now wringing his hat like it owed him money.

"You're going to want to come see this," he said.

"If it's another groundhog in the Cade plot—"

"It's not a groundhog."

Something in his voice made Tamsin set down the burin. It made a small bright click against the bench, and through the half-carved bone, Marta murmured, *What is it? What's happened?* and Tamsin shushed her gently, the way you'd shush a child at a sickbed.

"Pell," they said. "What happened?"

He looked over his shoulder at the street, at the frost and the pear trees and the smoke rising straight as plumb lines, and then back at Tamsin, and he said the sentence that would undo the next year of Tamsin's life and, in the end, remake the whole shape of it:

"There's a new grave in the west field."

* * *

The west field was old burial ground, older than the cairns on the hill, older than the chapel, older probably than the ward itself if you believed the stories. Nobody buried anyone there anymore. The plots had filled three generations ago, and the ground had gone soft and mossy and settled, and the dead in it had long ago worn their voices down to almost nothing—a hush, a tide-sound, the way a room sounds years after a party. Tamsin walked through it sometimes just to feel that hush. It was the closest thing the valley had to silence.

Now there was a wound in it.

The grave sat near the field's northern edge, where the ground began to rise toward the ridge, and it was wrong in every particular. Wrong first because it existed at all—fresh-turned earth, dark and wet, thrown up in a rough mound with no cairn, no marker, no carved bone. Wrong second because it hadn't been dug by anyone in Vesper; the spadework was clumsy, frantic, the edges torn rather than cut. And wrong third—this was the one that stopped Tamsin's breath, standing at the edge of it with Pell hovering behind them—because the earth itself was disturbed in a way fresh digging shouldn't allow.

The grave had been dug *upward*. As if something had come out of the ground, and then, changing its mind, gone back in.

"That's what I thought too," Pell said quietly, watching Tamsin look. "First light today. Dog of mine wouldn't come within ten feet of it." He paused. "Neither would I, much."

"How deep?"

"I didn't dig. You're the one who talks to them. I just—" He gestured vaguely at the whole mossy field, at its centuries of hush. "I keep the ground."

Tamsin crouched at the grave's edge. This close, they could feel it: not the old tide-hush of the west field's dead, but something newer, rawer, a voice like a thread pulled taut. Not one of theirs. Not one of the valley's dead at all. The valley's dead sounded like home. This sounded like a stranger shouting through a wall.

And underneath the voice, underneath even the hum of the ward far up on the ridge, there was something else. A wrongness in the ground's own quiet. Tamsin had spent their whole life listening to the dead of Vesper, and the dead of Vesper were always *settled*. Even the freshest of them, even the ones who died angry or afraid, settled within days, smoothed out by whatever gentle mechanism the ward worked on the valley's soil. Grief here had a shelf life. That was the gift of the place, the thing the founders had supposedly bought with whatever terrible price the stories only hinted at.

This grave was not settled. This grave was *loud*.

"Pell," Tamsin said. "How long has this been here?"

"My dog started acting strange two nights ago. So. Two days, maybe three."

"And nobody saw anyone dig it?"

"Nobody's *come* here," Pell said, with the flat emphasis of a man stating the weather. "Nobody comes anywhere. Last soul up the pass was a fur trader, and that was—" he counted on his fingers, gave up, waved— "years. Before your grandmother's knee went bad. There's been no one, Tamsin. There's never anyone."

Tamsin looked at the mound of turned earth. Looked at the torn edges. Dug upward, then downward again. Something had climbed out of this grave—or into it—and either way, it had done it without crossing the ridge, without passing through any gate or ford or road, without being seen by a single living soul in a valley where everyone knew everyone's business down to the color of their nightshirts.

The ward hummed on the ridgeline, patient and total, the way it had hummed every day of Tamsin's life.

*Nothing gets in,* Tamsin thought. *That's the whole point of us. Nothing gets in.*

"Well," they said, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they felt. "I suppose we should meet our guest."

Pell made a noise like a hinge. "You're not going to—"

"I'm going to do my job, Pell." Tamsin rolled up their sleeves. The morning frost was melting off the grass, and the light was strengthening, ordinary and gray and kind. "Somebody's dead in my valley, unmarked and unsettled, and somebody put them there. Or something did. Either way, they deserve a bone with their name on it, same as anyone." They picked up the spade leaning against Pell's fence post—he had brought it, which told Tamsin he'd known all along how this would go. "Besides. If something walked out of the ground and walked back in, I'd rather know while it's staying in."

They dug.

The earth came away easily, too easily, wet and loose as if it had been turned recently and often. Three feet down the spade struck cloth. Then, beneath the cloth, the dull resistance of a body curled on its side, knees drawn up, in the posture of someone trying to take up as little room as possible. Tamsin cleared the soil from the shoulders, the head, careful as excavating an egg.

The dead man was young—not much older than Tamsin—with dark hair matted with grave-dirt and a leather case strapped across his chest under his coat, clutched against him with both arms even in death, even curled up, as if he'd held it all the way down. His boots were good boots, road boots, worn at the heels from hard miles. His coat was fine wool, city-cut, and it was soaked through with blood that had dried black along his left side, low, under the ribs.

"He didn't die of burying," Tamsin said softly. "He was stabbed. Days ago. And then he—" They stopped. Frowned. "Pell, he was walking. He was wounded, days old, fevered by the smell of it, and he *walked*, and he dug himself a grave in the west field, and lay down in it."

"Why would a man do that?"

Tamsin didn't answer, because they didn't know, and because at that moment they touched the dead man's bare wrist—just to move his arm, just to reach the strap of the case—and the voice came through.

Not a murmur. Not the settled tide-sound of the valley's dead. This was a grab, a drowning man's grip, a voice with all its hurry still in it, and it hit Tamsin like cold water:

*—find her, you have to find her, Maren, ash road, follow the ash road, tell her it's real, tell her the map is real, tell her the thirteenth moon, oh gods, the thing that answers when he prays, don't let it finish, don't let it—*

Tamsin snatched their hand back and sat down hard in the wet grass.

"Tamsin?" Pell's voice, far away. "What is it? What did you hear?"

The valley hummed. The pear trees dripped. Somewhere up on the ridge, the ward went on singing its one endless note, the note that had kept Vesper safe and asleep and forgotten for longer than anyone could remember, the note that nothing crossed.

Nothing crossed.

Tamsin looked down at the dead man in his ruined city coat, at the leather case strapped to his chest like a second heart, and felt, for the first time in their life, the floor of the world tilt a degree or two—just enough to notice. Just enough to make the stomach lurch.

"Pell," they said. "Get me my tools. And don't touch the case."

"Why? What's in the case?"

"I don't know yet." Tamsin swallowed. Through the dead man's skin, faintly, terribly, the voice was still talking—still saying the name, over and over, like a knock on a door. *Maren. Maren. Maren.* "But I think it's why he's dead. And I think—" they looked up at the ridgeline, at the dark pines, at the invisible humming wall that had defined every day of their life, "—I think he shouldn't be here at all."

The frost was gone off the grass now. The morning went on being ordinary. But the grave lay open between them, freshly turned, impossible, and the dead man inside it would not stop saying her name.
