## 3. What the Bones Said

The dead did not leave all at once.

That was the first thing they never told you about bone-work in Vesper—or maybe nobody knew, because nobody had ever carved a stranger before. The valley's dead were known dead: grandmothers and stillborn lambs, men who fell off the mill roof, women who walked into the winter river on purpose. Their voices faded from the bone the way warmth fades from a hearthstone, slowly, politely, leaving only the shape of what they'd been.

The courier's voice did not fade. It *insisted*.

Tamsin worked through the night with the workshop shutters closed and a single tallow candle burning, because the elders would come at dawn and they both knew it. The courier's remains lay on the long table under a linen sheet, and beside them, in a shallow dish of salt water to keep it honest, sat the first sliver of femur Tamsin had taken that morning—already carved, already speaking.

*"North by the split stone. North by the split stone. Tell her—"*

Then it broke apart, like a sentence dropped down a well, and started over.

*"North by the split stone."*

Tamsin set down the graver and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until they saw stars. Twenty-six years of carving memorial bones for people whose names they'd known since birth, and not once—not once—had a voice come through like this. Not once had a voice refused to settle.

It wasn't haunting. Tamsin wanted to be clear with themselves about that. Hauntings were grief that hadn't found its shape yet, and you could carve grief into quiet. This was something else. This was a message wearing a dead man's voice like a coat, held together by whatever he'd swallowed with his last breath—fear, or duty, or both—and it repeated because repetition was all it had left.

*"North by the split stone. Thirteen turns. Tell her—"*

"Tell her who?" Tamsin said aloud, and hated how thin their voice sounded in the empty workshop.

The bone answered the way bone always answered: *"Maren."*

There it was again. The name. It surfaced every fourth cycle or so, bobbing up out of the coordinates and the warnings like a drowned thing finally allowed to breathe. Maren. Tamsin turned the sliver in the candlelight. They had carved the letters into its face without quite deciding to—the old instinct, the one that said a name spoken is a name kept—and now the word stared back at them, small and certain as a nail driven into a doorframe.

They didn't know anyone named Maren.

But the map did. Or the map knew *of* her, anyway. Tamsin had unrolled the leather case twice more since the stranger died, each time telling themselves it was the last time, each time finding more than they wanted. The sigils along the road-lines weren't surveyor's marks. Tamsin had grown up three ridges away from the ward-stones; they knew ward-script the way farm children know weather. These were binding-marks. Royal ones, if the double-crown flourish meant anything, which it did, which was the whole problem.

A king's binding-work, drawn on a map carried by a dying man into a valley where magic could not cross the ridge line.

Into the one place in the world the crown could not follow.

Tamsin thought about that for a long time, sitting very still while the candle guttered. Outside, the wind came down off the ridge carrying the faint hum of the ward—that sound every Vesper child stopped hearing around age six, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Tonight Tamsin heard it clearly. Tonight it sounded less like a lullaby and more like a lock turning.

*"Thirteen turns,"* said the bone. *"Thirteen turns. He prays and it answers. Thirteen—"*

"I know," Tamsin whispered. "I heard him say it."

That was the worst part, maybe. That the voice in the bone matched the voice in the workshop, hour by hour, word for word. The courier had died saying these things, and now his death said them too, and there was no difference between the man and the message anymore except that one of them bled when you cut it.

Tamsin picked up the graver again and went back to work.

* * *

By the time the light came gray through the shutter cracks, Tamsin had carved four bones and learned four things.

First: the coordinates. *North by the split stone*, always north, always the split stone, and then numbers—three of them, in an order that never changed, spoken in the flat recitation of someone who has memorized something they don't understand. Tamsin wrote them on the underside of the workbench, where the wood was scarred anyway, in the shorthand carvers used for commission dates.

Second: *thirteen turns*. A moon count. Something ended—or began—in thirteen turnings of the moon, and the courier had spent his last strength making sure someone knew it.

Third: *he prays and it answers*. Tamsin turned this one over and over and could not make it harmless. Kings prayed the way everyone prayed—to saints, to the garden-gods, to nothing. But the courier hadn't said *he prays*. He'd said *it answers*, with the particular weight people in Vesper gave to words like *ward* and *ridge* and *never cross*. A prayer that got answered was just religion. An answer that needed a pronoun like *it*—

Fourth: *tell Maren*. And beneath the name, surfacing rarely, half-swallowed: *she'll know. She'll know what I—*

She. So Maren was a woman, or had been once, or was something that took the pronoun. She knew things. She knew what the courier had—done? Found? Been?

Tamsin laid the four bones in a row on the table next to the shrouded body and looked at them the way you look at a snare you've sprung on your own foot.

"You should have gone anywhere else," they told the dead man quietly. "Any village. Any road. You picked the valley where no one can help you."

But even as they said it, they knew it wasn't true. He hadn't picked Vesper despite the ward. He'd picked it *because* of the ward. Whatever hunted that map—and something had hunted it; Tamsin had seen the wounds, three days old and made by blades, not beasts—could not follow it past the ridge. The courier had run toward the silence the way a drowning man runs toward air.

Which meant the silence was complicit now. Which meant Tamsin was.

The knock came at the door just after sunrise. Two knocks, spaced evenly, patient. Elder Sable's knock. Tamsin had known it since childhood, back when Sable had been the one who taught them to hold a graver, back when the workshop had belonged to Tamsin's mother and the knock had meant bread and lessons instead of judgment.

Tamsin covered the bones with the linen, folded the map case into the false bottom of the salt chest—they'd done it an hour ago, hands steady, heart anything but—and opened the door.

Sable stood in the frost with two of the other elders behind her: old Coram, who smelled of peat smoke, and young Wrenna, who wasn't really young anymore but had been voted the title the way valleys vote everything, by tradition rather than accuracy. Three faces Tamsin had known all their life. Three faces that had never once had reason to look at Tamsin the way they were looking now.

"Tamsin." Sable's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "May we come in?"

"It's early for a commission."

"It's early for a stranger to die in your workshop, too."

Tamsin stepped aside. There was no point in anything else. The whole valley would have known by midmorning regardless—Wrenna's nephew ran the ford ferry, and the ferryman saw everything that crossed the water, including, apparently, a body wrapped in sailcloth being carried up from the river path at dusk.

The elders stood around the table. Sable drew back the linen herself, without asking, and looked down at the courier's face for a long moment. Whatever she saw there, she didn't share it.

"He crossed the ward," Coram said. It wasn't a question. Nothing crossed the ward. That was rather the point of it.

"He was dying in the shallows at the ford when I found him," Tamsin said. Every word measured out like grain during a bad winter. "He lived long enough to say a few things. Then he didn't."

"What things?"

"That he was sorry. That he was cold." Both true. Tamsin let the truths do the lying for them, and discovered, with a strange distant horror, how easy it was.

Sable's eyes came up. She had eyes the color of wet slate and forty years of reading carvers, and she read Tamsin now the way she'd read a badly cut bone—finding the flaw, the hidden crack, the place where the structure would fail.

"And he gave you nothing else? No token, no letter, no charge?"

"The charge was burial." Tamsin nodded at the bones under the linen. "Which I'm doing."

"The map, Tamsin."

The name landed in the room like a dropped stone. Tamsin kept their face still through sheer lifelong practice. *So Wrenna's nephew saw more than the body.*

"There was a map case," Tamsin said. "Waterlogged. The leather was ruined. I burned it with his other effects."

Coram made a small sound, disbelief or disgust. Wrenna looked at the cold hearth, where no fire had burned that morning, and said nothing, which was its own kind of testimony.

"Burned it," Sable repeated.

"Everything burns in Vesper, Elder. You taught me that."

Something moved behind Sable's slate eyes—hurt, maybe, or respect, or the weary recognition of a student surpassing the lesson in the worst possible way. She looked at the shrouded body again.

"A man crosses thirty years of sealed ward-line to die here," she said slowly, "carrying nothing but a ruined map, and asks a bone-carver to bury him. Do you know what that sounds like to me?"

"No, Elder."

"It sounds like the beginning of a story my mother told me to frighten me. The one where the valley stays safe right up until it doesn't." She straightened. "The council will want his bones interred in the common mound. Unmarked. Today."

"They're his bones," Tamsin said. "And he asked me."

"He was a stranger."

"He was a *man*." The words came out harder than Tamsin intended, and once out, they would not be called back. "He died alone in the cold with holes in him, in a valley full of people who owe their safety to walls he apparently believed in enough to die against. If the cost of those walls is that we can't give a dying man his last request, then I'd like someone to tell me exactly what the walls are *for*."

Silence. The ward hummed faintly beyond the shutters, indifferent. Coram's jaw worked. Wrenna had gone pale.

Sable studied Tamsin for a long, long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a woman stepping onto ice.

"You've never lied to me before, Tamsin Halloran. In twenty-six years. I want you to understand that I know that, and I'm choosing, this morning, to believe that you're telling me the truth now." She pulled the linen back over the courier's face. "Bury him where you like. Mark him if you must. But if anything else comes out of that river—if anything else comes *over* that ridge—you bring it to the council first. Before you carve it. Before you listen to it. Swear it."

And there it was. The trap inside the mercy. Because Tamsin had already carved him, already listened, already written coordinates under the workbench, and swearing otherwise would be a second lie stacked on the first like stones in a wall.

"I swear," Tamsin said, "that I'll bring the council anything that comes over the ridge."

Every word true. Every word arranged.

Sable held their gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, and the three of them filed out into the frost, and the door closed, and Tamsin stood in the quiet workshop listening to their own heartbeat and understanding, distantly, that something in them had just changed permanently and would not change back.

* * *

They buried the courier at noon, alone, on the south slope above the workshop where the ground was soft and the view ran out toward the ridge. Not the common mound. A grave of his own, dug deep, lined with river stones the way Vesper lined all its honored dead.

Before they lowered him, Tamsin placed the four carved bones in his hands.

It was a risk. Everything now was a risk. But the voice had been cycling slower all morning—*north by the split stone, thirteen turns, tell Maren*—winding down like a clock, and Tamsin had realized sometime around dawn what the tradition their mother never explained actually meant: you buried the dead with their words so the words could rest. The bones would go into the dark with him, and the message would go with the bones, and the valley would forget him by spring, and that was how peace worked here.

Tamsin stood over the filled grave for a long time afterward, dirt on their knees, the wind pushing at their back from the direction of the ridge.

"Maren," they said aloud, testing the name. It felt foreign in their mouth. A capital name. A road name.

*She'll know.*

Know what? Know where the split stone was. Know what thirteen turns meant. Know what the king prayed to, and why the answer needed hiding, and why a courier had bled out in a ford at the edge of the world to keep a piece of leather dry.

Tamsin looked down at the fresh-turned earth, then up at the ridge line, hazy blue against the afternoon sky, humming its endless note.

The valley had been enough for twenty-six years. The valley had been safety, and silence, and the slow kind work of remembering the dead one bone at a time. But the valley had also been a lie of a particular shape—a lie Tamsin had helped maintain, willingly, their whole life—and a man had died against its walls to hand them the truth of it, and now the truth was buried in the ground with his voice still ticking inside it like a stopped watch that wouldn't stop.

Beyond the ridge, somewhere north, there was a split stone. Somewhere there was a woman named Maren who would know.

Tamsin wiped their hands on their trousers, went inside, and began, quietly and without letting themselves think about it too hard, to pack.

* * *

That night, the voice woke them.

Not from the grave—Tamsin would have sworn to that, would swear it still. From the workbench. From the offcut of femur they'd missed, a curl of bone no longer than a finger, swept into the corner with the shavings. It had dried overnight, and drying, it spoke.

*"North by the split stone,"* it whispered, thin as thread. *"Tell Maren. Tell Maren. Tell—"*

And then, for the first time, something new. A fragment that had never surfaced before, worn almost to nothing, like a coin rubbed smooth:

*"...the ash road. She keeps the ash road..."*

Tamsin sat up in the dark, heart slamming, and reached for the knife they kept by the bed out of habit, and did not reach for it, because it was a dead man's voice, and dead men in Vesper deserved better than knives.

"The ash road," they breathed.

Outside, beyond the ridge, beyond the ward's low hum, the world went on being large and loud and full of things that answered prayers. And somewhere on a road made of ash, a woman named Maren was waiting for a message that had finally, impossibly, arrived.

Tamsin got up and lit the candle.
