## 4. Maren of the Ash Road

The trouble with living beyond the ridge, Maren had learned, was that everything wanted to kill you politely.

The frost came first each autumn, soft as an apology. Then the wolves, who circled her lean-to at night with the patience of creditors. Then the cold itself, which crept into her bones and stayed like a relative who'd overstayed a welcome by three winters. None of it was cruel. That was the insult of it. The world beyond Vesper's ridgeline simply did not care whether she lived or died, and after six years in the capital—where everyone cared very much, in the way that vultures care—she had come to find indifference almost restful.

Almost.

She crouched over her cookfire that evening, feeding it pine knots, and tried to remember what her sister's laugh sounded like. Six years was long enough that Sefa's voice had gone soft at the edges, worn smooth like a river stone. She could still see her face perfectly. It was the sound she was losing.

"Don't," she told herself, the way she did every night. "Don't start."

Starting was dangerous. Starting led to thinking about the tithe wagons, and the tithe wagons led to the palace steps, and the palace steps led to the night she'd stood in front of the Magistrate of Rites with her hands still smoking from a working she hadn't been able to stop, and the Magistrate had looked at her with something almost like pity and said, *Exile or the tithe house, hedge-witch. Choose.*

She'd chosen exile. Sefa hadn't been given the choice.

The fire popped. Somewhere down the slope, a wolf answered it.

Maren pulled her cloak tighter and reached for the bundle of dried nettle she'd gathered that morning—and that was when she felt it.

Not heard. Not saw. *Felt*, the way you feel a storm building behind mountains before the sky shows anything at all. A pressure along her wards, the tripwire charms she'd strung through the pines in a lazy spiral around her camp. Cheap magic, hedge magic, the kind that wouldn't stop a determined rabbit. But they were hers, tuned to her, and something had just brushed against all of them at once.

Something moving fast. Something dying.

She snuffed the fire with a pinch of ash and stood in the dark, listening.

The forest gave her nothing for a long moment—just wind, just the ordinary small machinery of the night. Then, downslope toward the old pilgrim road, a sound: not footsteps, exactly. The failure of footsteps. Someone stumbling, catching themselves on trees, falling, rising again with the stubborn idiocy of the nearly dead.

Maren knew better than to go toward trouble. Trouble beyond the ridge meant crown riders, or bounty men, or worse—the quiet ones in gray who didn't wear livery because livery could be traced. Six years of survival had taught her one lesson above all others: *be smoke*. Be smoke, be fog, be the smell of woodsmoke drifting the wrong direction. Never be found.

But the stumbling thing was coming up her slope anyway, straight toward her wards, and there was something wrong with its passage—a dragging quality, a hitching rhythm, like a heart skipping beats. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, she caught the taste of blood on the air. Old blood. Days old.

Whoever it was had been bleeding since before they crossed the ridge line.

That was impossible. Everyone knew the valley of Vesper kept the world out; fewer people remembered it kept its people in. Nothing crossed that ridge without either extraordinary luck or extraordinary purpose, and Maren had spent enough years in the capital's archives, back when she'd still been allowed near them, to know that luck was rarely extraordinary on purpose.

Purpose, though. Purpose she understood.

"Idiot," she muttered at herself, and went down the slope to meet it.

* * *

He collapsed at the edge of her ward-circle like a puppet whose strings had finally been cut, and Maren got her first good look at him by the light of a relit fire: young, maybe twenty-five, courier's leathers cut away from his left side where someone—or something—had opened him hip to ribs. The wound had been bound, badly, with field dressings gone brown and stiff. He smelled of rot beginning its slow work, of horse sweat, of road dust from places far south of here.

And under all of it, threaded through his leathers like a second skin, he smelled of the palace.

Maren had spent eleven years in Bellharbor before her exile. You didn't forget that smell. Incense and lye soap and the particular oiled wool the Royal Guard wore, and beneath it all, so faint most people never noticed it, the mineral tang of the deep wells beneath the citadel—the water they used in the binding rites. Every servant, every clerk, every courier who worked the palace grounds carried it home in their clothes like a curse.

This man had swum in it.

He was conscious, barely. His eyes tracked her as she knelt beside him, and whatever he saw in her face made him try to speak.

"Don't." Maren held up a hand. "Save it. I'm going to look at the wound, and you're going to lie there and not die while I do it. Can you manage that?"

His mouth twitched. Almost a smile. He managed a nod.

The wound was bad. Worse than bad—it was a killing wound that had refused to finish its work out of sheer spite, three days old at least, stitched in the field with thread that might once have been fishing line. She'd seen men survive such things. She'd seen more men not. But there was something else, when she peeled back the dressing, that made her go still.

The edges of the wound were gray.

Not pale. Gray, like ash, like the color leached out of a thing, and when she passed her palm above it—not touching, never touching—she felt the wrongness immediately. This wasn't infection. This was a *draining*. Something had fed on this man's vitality, and it had done so deliberately, precisely, leaving just enough of him alive to keep moving.

Just enough to keep running north.

Maren sat back on her heels and looked at his face properly for the first time. Young. Scared. And watching her with an expression she recognized from mirrors, from the worst nights of her own life: the look of someone carrying something too important to put down and too heavy to hold much longer.

"You're a palace courier," she said quietly.

A nod. A wince.

"And you've run a very long way to die on my doorstep."

His hand moved. Slowly, trembling, it crawled toward the satchel strap across his chest, and Maren watched him fight his own body for every inch. When his fingers finally closed on the buckle, he fumbled it open and drew out a leather map case—slim, scarred, sealed with wax stamped by a sigil she hadn't seen in six years and had hoped never to see again.

The royal seal. But wrong. The old seal showed the crowned stag of House Aldermere, antlers spread, dignified. This one showed the stag too—but the antlers had been redrawn, elongated, branching into something almost like roots. Like veins. Like the thing had grown since the last time anyone official had bothered to recut the stamp.

Maren's mouth went dry.

"I know what you are," the courier rasped. His voice was a ruin, gravel and wind. "Hedge-witch. Exiled. They said—" a wet cough that lasted too long "—they said you'd know binding-work when you saw it."

"They."

"The others. In the archive. The ones who still remember what the king used to be." His eyes were fever-bright now, fixed on hers with terrible urgency. "There isn't time. There's never been less time. Thirteen moons. The thirteenth turn completes it, and then—" another cough, longer, darker "—then it won't need him anymore. It'll have eaten its way out through his soul, and there won't be anything left to bind it *to*. Nothing left to hold it. Do you understand? The binding is the only cage. And the cage is about to become a door."

Maren's hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain night.

"What is?" she asked, though some part of her, the part that had felt the wrongness in his wound, already knew. "What won't need him anymore?"

The courier smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen in six years of hard living—that serene, exhausted smile of a man who has set down his burden at last.

"The thing that answers when he prays."

Then his eyes rolled back, and Maren stopped asking questions and started fighting for his life.

* * *

She fought until dawn, and lost.

It wasn't for lack of trying. She burned half her stores of willow bark and marrow-moss, drew the healing sigils until her fingers bled at the knuckles, sang the old mending-cant that her grandmother had taught her in a kitchen that smelled of bread and safe things. The gray in his wound retreated a finger's width, then stalled, then began creeping again, patient as winter. Whatever had fed on him had left too little behind. She was trying to mend a garment woven from threads that no longer existed.

Near the end, he surfaced from the fever one last time, and his eyes found hers with sudden, shocking clarity.

"The map," he whispered. "Take it. Not to the elders—they're kind, but they're *inside*, they can't leave, the valley makes them small. Take it to—" his breath hitched "—there's a bone-carver in Vesper. Tamsin Halloran. They hear the dead through their work. If anyone can read what I couldn't—"

"You want me to go into the valley," Maren said flatly. "The valley that hasn't let me within a mile of its ward line in six years. The valley whose elders would hand me to the first crown rider who asked."

"Not the elders." His hand found her wrist, grip feeble but insistent. "Halloran. Find Halloran. Tell them—" His gaze drifted past her shoulder, toward the ridge line black against the graying sky, and something in his face loosened. "Tell them the dead south of the ridge aren't resting. They're being *eaten*. Tell them the whisper they hear at night isn't the wind."

His grip failed. His chest rose once, twice, shallow as paper.

"Maren," he said.

She frowned. "What?"

"That's your name. Isn't it? Maren of the Ash Road." A ghost of the smile again. "They told me to find you too. After Halloran. They said you'd know the sigils." His eyes were closing. "I ran four hundred miles. Four hundred miles, and the last hundred I walked, because horses spook near the drained ones, and I thought—if I can just reach the ridge, if I can just get inside where it can't follow, where it can't *hear*—"

"Stop talking," Maren said. Her voice cracked on it. "Conserve your strength."

"There isn't any strength." He said it simply, without self-pity, and somehow that was worse than weeping. "There's just the message. So take the message. Take the map. And when you look at it—when you see what's written there—remember that I got it out. Remember that somebody tried."

His hand slipped from her wrist.

Maren sat with him through the last of it, holding his hand the way you'd hold a door shut against a gale, and when the sun cleared the ridge line and laid gold across the dead man's face, she closed his eyes and did the only other thing left to do.

She opened the map case.

* * *

The case held three things: a folded map drawn on hide so fine it might have been vellum, a scrap of paper covered in a cramped cipher, and a ring of office bearing the sigil of the Royal Archivists—the third-highest rank in the palace bureaucracy, carried by perhaps a dozen people in the whole kingdom.

Someone very senior had sent this man to die.

Maren spread the map across her knees in the morning light and forgot how to breathe.

At first glance it was a survey chart of the capital region—streets, districts, the great sweep of the palace complex rendered in the precise draftsmanship of the Royal Cartographers' Guild. But overlaid on the streets, in ink that shifted color when she tilted it, ran lines. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Converging from every corner of the kingdom toward a single point beneath the palace, marked with a symbol she knew intimately, horribly well.

The binding knot. The closed circle with nine tails. The sigil that appeared in the oldest rites, the ones from before the kingdom had a name, the ones the Archivists kept locked in vaults and pretended were decorative.

Except this knot had thirteen tails. Twelve drawn in full, and the thirteenth sketched in ghost-ink, waiting, unfinished.

Along each completed tail, in tiny script, ran dates. Maren read them with growing horror. Each date marked a tithe. Each tithe marked a harvest of magicians—Sefa's cohort, Sefa's year, Sefa herself somewhere in that ledger of names—and each harvest fed a line on the map, and each line fed the knot, and the knot fed—

She turned the map over with shaking hands.

On the reverse, in the same shifting ink, was a diagram of the palace under-chapel, and a single sentence in the Archivist's cipher, which she decoded slowly, letter by letter, because her mind kept refusing to simply read it:

*THE CAGE WAS BUILT TO HOLD IT. THE KING IS THE LOCK. WHEN THE THIRTEENTH TAIL CLOSES, THE LOCK OPENS FROM THE INSIDE.*

Maren sat very still for a long time.

The wolves had gone quiet. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath. She became aware, distantly, that she was crying—not with sobs, just leaking, steadily, the way a cracked jar leaks, and she let it happen because the alternative was screaming.

Six years. Six years of exile, of frost and wolves and losing her sister's laugh, and she had told herself a story to survive it: *the crown is corrupt, the crown is cruel, the tithes are greed, greed can be fought, greed has limits.* Greed had a shape you could hate. Greed had a face.

This had no face. This was older than faces. This was a hunger wearing a dynasty like a coat, growing fat on twelve generations of harvested magicians, and in twelve turns of the moon it would finish eating through the soul of King Aldous and step out into the world wearing his body like a glove.

And the courier—he'd run four hundred miles to reach the one place in the kingdom the thing couldn't follow. The valley of Vesper, where magic died at the ridge line, where the crown's scrying went blind, where a stolen map could be hidden from the hunger in the walls. He hadn't stumbled into Vesper.

He'd aimed for it.

Which meant the people who sent him—the archivists, the remnant, whoever still remembered what the king used to be—had chosen their messenger with the same precision. And they'd named two names. A bone-carver who heard the dead. And a disgraced hedge-witch living rough on the ash road, who would know binding-work when she saw it.

They hadn't just sent a message. They'd assembled a weapon, piece by piece, and mailed the parts to the one address the enemy couldn't watch.

Maren wiped her face with the heel of her hand. The tears stopped. What replaced them was colder and steadier and far more useful.

She looked east, toward the ridge line, where the valley of Vesper lay hidden behind its wall of silence. Somewhere down there was a bone-carver named Tamsin Halloran who had no idea what was coming for them. Somewhere south was a tithe house where her sister might still be alive, hollowed but breathing. And somewhere beneath the capital, a hungry god was counting down twelve moons to its freedom.

Twelve chances to stop it. Or twelve chances to fail.

Maren folded the map along its creases, tucked it inside her shirt against her skin, and began, methodically, to break camp. She buried the courier deep and marked the grave with a cairn and no name, because names could be scryed and she couldn't afford to give the enemy even that much of him. She took his ring of office. She doused her fire, pulled down her wards, and stood for one last moment in the clearing that had been her whole world for six years.

Then she shouldered her pack and started walking toward the ridge line, toward the valley, toward a stranger with bloody knuckles and a gift for hearing ghosts.

The frost crunched under her boots. The wolves watched her go from the treeline and, for once, did not follow.

Behind her, the ash road stretched empty to the horizon. Ahead, the mountains waited, patient as the thing in the walls.

Twelve moons, she thought. *Hold on, Sefa. I'm coming. And I'm bringing the end of the world with me—or the end of the thing that wants it.*

By nightfall, she could see the ward line shimmering faintly on the ridge ahead, a wall of silence older than the kingdom.

She only hoped the bone-carver would listen.
