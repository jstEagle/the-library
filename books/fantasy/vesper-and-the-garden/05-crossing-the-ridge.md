## 5. Crossing the Ridge

The map said the ward was weakest where the river left the valley.

Tamsin had read that line a dozen times by lamplight, tracing the courier's cramped notation with a fingertip gone gray from bone dust. *Where the water runs out, so does the king's leash.* It made a kind of sense. Magic couldn't cross the ridge line—everyone in Vesper knew that the way they knew winter came after harvest—but water wasn't magic. Water was just water. It fell on the high fells, gathered itself, and went looking for the sea, and whatever old bargain kept the valley sealed had apparently never thought to argue with it.

So: the river gap. The one place in thirty years Tamsin had ever considered leaving, and the only place the courier's map bothered to mark with anything other than distance.

They packed light, which was to say they packed like a liar. A knife. A roll of carving tools wrapped in oilcloth. Dried fish and hard bread in a sack that had once held chisels. The map case, strapped against their ribs where no honest search would look. And beneath it all, against their skin, three small bones they'd carved from the stranger's remains—the ones that still murmured when Tamsin held them too long. Coordinates. A warning. *Maren.*

They did not pack a note for the elders. Writing one would have been a second lie, and the first was already sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone.

*The map is lost,* Tamsin had told Elder Corrin two days ago, standing in the meeting house with their hands open at their sides, palms out, the way you stood when you wanted to be believed. *It burned with him. I put it in the pyre myself.*

That part was even true. They had put something in the pyre. Just not the map.

Corrin had looked at them a long moment with eyes gone pale as river ice, and Tamsin had understood, in that moment, that she hadn't believed them—and that she was going to let it go anyway. That was the thing about Vesper nobody told you until you were old enough to hear it: the valley forgot on purpose. Forgetting was a kindness the whole community performed together, generation after generation, until it looked like innocence.

Tamsin was done being innocent. The dead man had died holding their wrist, and his last word had been a name, and names were debts.

* * *

They left at the dark of the moon, because the map said so and because every instinct Tamsin owned said the same. No moon meant no silver on the water, no shine off the wet stones, nothing but the sound of the current and the smell of pine rot to steer by.

The workshop door they locked from outside, which felt absurd—who would steal memorial bones?—but their hands did it anyway, hands always knowing things before the rest of them did. Then down through the sleeping village, past the meeting house with its shuttered windows, past the long low mound of the communal barrow where generations of Vesper's dead lay in their carved resting places, whispering their small contented whispers through the bone walls.

Tamsin stopped there. Just for a moment.

The whispers were faint tonight, the way they always were this far from the workshop. In Vesper, the dead spoke only through worked bone—carved, polished, given shape and purpose—and only softly, like people talking in the next room. Tamsin had spent their whole life inside that murmur. It was the sound of home. It was, if they were honest, the sound of themselves.

*I'll come back,* they told the barrow, and didn't know if they were lying again, and hated how easy it was getting.

Then the river path, and the pines closing overhead, and the last lights of the village gone behind them like a held breath finally released.

* * *

The river gap was exactly what the map promised: a notch in the ridge where the water had spent ten thousand years arguing its way downhill, leaving a corridor of scree and stunted alder maybe twenty feet wide. On either side the ridgeline rose black against the stars, and Tamsin could feel it—not see, *feel*—the way you feel a wall at your back in a dark room. The ward. The hum everyone in Vesper grew up inside without ever noticing, the constant subsonic note under everything, the reason the valley's bones whispered instead of sang.

They had never heard silence before. They realized that now, standing at the mouth of the gap. Even here, even this close to the edge, the hum was there, familiar as their own pulse.

Beyond it was something else.

Tamsin sat down on a wet stone and ate half the bread and tried not to think about Elder Corrin's ice-pale eyes, or the grave that shouldn't have existed, or the weight of a dying man's grip. They thought about all of it anyway. Then they got up, and walked into the gap.

The hum thinned as they climbed. That was the strangest part—how gradually it went. Like wading out of warm water into cool. Ten steps and it was quieter. Twenty and it was a memory. At fifty steps Tamsin stopped, heart slamming, and turned around, and saw the valley spread below them in the starlight: the dark smudge of the village, the thread of the river, the barrow, the workshop with its locked door. Small. Containable. A bowl of quiet with a lid on it.

And above it all, invisible but utterly present, the ward itself—a shimmer they could suddenly perceive now that they stood outside its edge, a skin of dimness stretched over the whole valley like a drumhead, humming its one flat note.

*We live under a lid,* Tamsin thought. *My whole life. Under a lid, and I never once looked up.*

They turned north, toward the world, and took one more step.

Magic hit them like the river in spring melt.

There was no other way to say it, no gentler comparison, and later Tamsin would try to find one and fail. The world beyond the ridge was *full*. Not empty and waiting for magic the way Tamsin had always imagined the outside must be—barren, mute, a field unplanted. Full. Saturated. Every stone and stream and tree trunk thrumming with something alive and enormous and utterly indifferent to Tamsin Halloran, the way the sea is indifferent to a single fish.

They went to their knees in the scree.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was volume. Their whole life they had listened to the dead through a keyhole, and someone had just taken the door off its hinges. Power poured through the ground and up through their palms where they braced against the stone, through their knees, through the thin skin of their throat, and underneath it—

Underneath it, the dead.

Not whispers. Not the polite murmur of carved bone in a quiet valley. *Everywhere.* The air itself seemed crowded. Tamsin pressed their hands over their ears and it changed nothing, because the voices weren't coming through their ears; they were arriving fully formed behind the ears, behind the eyes, in the marrow. The dead of the outside world were not resting in neat barrows. They were *loose*, scattered through the land like seed, clinging to rivers and crossroads and the foundations of ruined houses, and every one of them was talking.

A woman's voice, repeating a child's name, over and over, from somewhere downslope. An old man grumbling about a debt in words worn smooth by centuries of repetition. Soldiers—dozens of soldiers, hundreds, a chorus of soldiers muttering drill commands and prayers and the names of towns none of them had ever reached. Something vast and slow speaking a language Tamsin didn't know, deep under the mountain's root, patient as geology. And threaded through it all, thin and sharp and everywhere at once, a sound like laughter played backward.

Tamsin vomited into the alder brush.

When they could breathe again, they knelt there shaking, and the voices kept on, indifferent, endless, a roar wearing the shapes of individual mouths. This was what the ward kept out, they understood then. Not just magic. *This.* The valley's silence wasn't poverty. It was mercy. Everyone in Vesper lived inside a soundproofed room and called it a cage, and Tamsin had spent two days calling it a cage too, and now—

Now they knew what the cage was for.

*Get up.* The thought arrived in their own voice, their own stubborn interior tone, and it cut through the roar the way a carving knife cuts through soft pine—one clean line of self amid the noise. *Get up, or die here on your knees, and then you'll be one of these voices forever, and you'll deserve it.*

They got up.

* * *

Walking helped. Walking gave the roar a rhythm to hang on, footfall after footfall, and Tamsin discovered—if discovery was the right word for something that felt like learning to swim in a flood—that the voices thinned near running water. Not vanished. Thinned. The river's voice was louder than theirs, somehow, or older, or simply less interested in being interrupted. So Tamsin followed the water downstream, away from the gap, away from the valley, hands jammed under their arms, jaw clenched, breathing through their nose like a woman in labor.

By the time the sky went gray in the east they had made perhaps six miles, and learned three things.

First: the dead clung to places, not people. Crossroads were loud. Old bridges were louder. A collapsed farmhouse they passed at dawn was deafening—generations of one family, still arguing about a fence line.

Second: unworked bone still carried them, faintly, the way a dry creek bed carries rain. Tamsin found a deer skull in the brush, and when they picked it up the nearest dead leaned toward it like moths toward a lamp, their voices sharpening, clarifying, almost grateful. *You can hear us. You can hear us.* They said it the way drowning people say *help*.

Third: they could not stop hearing. There was no off. There was no lid. Tamsin had crossed the ridge carrying the assumption—unexamined, arrogant—that their gift was portable, that they'd step outside the valley and be the same person in a bigger room. Instead they were a cup held under a waterfall, and the waterfall did not care that the cup was full.

Somewhere around midmorning, hollow-eyed and hoarse from a screaming fit they didn't remember starting, Tamsin sat down under an oak and took out the three bones from the dead courier.

And the roar—shifted.

It was subtle at first, like a change in wind direction. The general tumult receded, the way a crowd recedes when someone important enters the room, and the bones warmed in Tamsin's palm and the courier's voice rose out of them, clearer than it had ever been in the valley, no longer fragments but nearly whole:

*—the twelfth turn puts him at the capital, tell her the twelfth, tell Maren the sigils are binding-work, royal work, she'll know it, she was taught by the best of them before they—*

Static. Then, fainter, fraying at the edges like cloth worn through:

*—she'll hate me for sending it to strangers. Tell her I'm sorry. Tell her the ford at Ash Road, the witch at the ford, she never goes far from the ford—*

The voice guttered out. The bones went cold and ordinary in Tamsin's hand, just calcium and craft, and the great roar of the world's dead rushed back in to fill the silence he'd left.

But Tamsin was already moving.

Because the courier had been specific—he'd been *dying* specific, and the dead didn't waste breath—and because the map, when Tamsin wrestled it out of its case with shaking fingers, showed a ford marked on the Ash Road barely two days' walk north. A ford, and beside it, in the courier's cramped hand, a single symbol Tamsin now recognized from the margins of every page: a small circle crossed by a wand. The same sigil inked next to the word *Maren* on the map's outer flap.

The witch at the ford. She never goes far from the ford.

Tamsin stood, tucked the bones back against their skin, and looked north along the river, where the land opened into country they'd never seen—hills the color of hammered bronze, a road cutting through them like a scar, and far off, hazy with distance, a smudge on the horizon that might have been smoke or might have been the capital itself, wearing its king like a glove.

The dead roared on all sides, pressing close, hungry to be heard.

*Two days,* Tamsin thought, and started walking. *Hold it together for two days, and find the one person alive who knows what we're up against.*

Behind them, faint and fading, the valley's ward hummed its one flat note over the place where the dead still whispered politely, and Tamsin did not look back, because if they looked back they would hear their mother's grave calling their name, and they were not sure they had the strength to answer and keep walking.

Ahead, on the road, something moved. A figure, small with distance, bent over the ford's edge—gathering herbs, or washing something, or watching.

Watching.

Tamsin's hand found the bones at their ribs and squeezed, and the courier's last word rose in the roar like a lantern in fog:

*Maren.*
