## 6. The Hedge-Witch's Bargain

*Tamsin*

The map said *follow the ash road to the burned waystation, then north where the cairns go crooked.* The map did not say that the woman waiting there would look like she'd been carved from the same stubborn stone as the hills.

Tamsin found her at dusk, three days past the ridge line, standing in the doorway of a hut that leaned against a dead oak like it was afraid of falling over on its own. She was small, gray-streaked, and holding a knife in a way that suggested she'd held it many times before and had never once been sorry about it.

"You've been following me since the ford," Tamsin said.

"And you've been walking like a man being chased by his own shadow," the woman said. "Which is either very stupid or very honest. Put the satchel down. Slowly."

"I'm not—"

"The map case. On your hip. I can smell the wax seal from here." Her eyes were pale as river ice and just as warm. "I've been waiting eleven days for someone to come down that road carrying royal binding-work, and here you are. So either you stole it—and I'll congratulate you before I rob you—or someone gave it to you, which is worse."

Tamsin set the satchel down but didn't open it. "You know what it is."

"I know what the sigils are." The knife didn't waver. "I spent nine years in the capital learning to read them before they decided I read too well. Maren," she added, as if remembering that names were things people traded. "Maren of the Ash Road, if you're the sort who cares about names. Most people who come out this far aren't."

"Tamsin Halloran. From Vesper."

Something flickered across Maren's face—the first genuine expression Tamsin had seen on her. "Vesper. The valley where magic goes to die." She laughed, short and bitter. "Of course. Of course he sent it there. That clever, dying fool."

"He?" Tamsin's mouth went dry. "You knew the courier?"

"I knew *of* him. Corvin. He carried messages for people the crown wanted silenced, back when I still had a door with my name on it." Maren finally lowered the knife—not sheathed it, just lowered it, which Tamsin understood was the closest thing to trust she was going to get tonight. "He reached Vesper. He actually reached Vesper. Do you understand what that means? The valley's the one hole in the king's web. No scrying crosses the ridge. No tithe-men ride in. If you want to hide something from a god, you bury it under a ward that eats magic for breakfast."

"A god," Tamsin repeated. "Corvin said—before he died—he said the thing that answers when the king prays. I thought he was raving."

Maren looked at them for a long moment. Then she stepped back from the doorway and jerked her chin inward. "Get inside before the light goes. This conversation takes a fire and probably a drink, and you look like you haven't slept since you crossed the ridge. Which, incidentally—" her eyes narrowed, "—you shouldn't have been able to do. The ward doesn't open for just anyone."

"It opened for me." Tamsin hesitated at the threshold. "Or maybe it didn't notice me. In Vesper we say the valley forgets people. Maybe it forgot me harder than most."

"Maybe." Maren didn't sound convinced. "Or maybe whatever made that map wanted you out here, and arranged matters accordingly. Get inside."

The hut's single room smelled of ash, dried herbs, and something burnt long ago that had never quite left the walls. A cauldron. Bundles of yarrow and wormwood hanging from the rafters. A cot, a stool, a table scarred with candle-wax and older burns—circles within circles, half-scrubbed away. Ritual work. Tamsin had never seen real ritual work; in Vesper, carving bones for the dead was craft, not magic, because no magic could live there. But they recognized intent when they saw it scored into wood.

Maren built up the fire without asking permission and put water on to boil. Only when two chipped cups of nettle tea sat between them did she hold out her hand. "The map."

Tamsin's fingers closed around the satchel strap. Everything in them resisted. Three days of walking with this thing against their hip, three nights sleeping with one hand on it, and now a stranger with a knife and a burned-out life was asking for it like it was already hers.

"I don't give it to anyone," Tamsin said. "Corvin died putting it in my hands. His last breath went into it. Whatever it is, it's mine to carry now."

Maren's expression didn't change, but the temperature of the room did. "You're not from the capital, so let me educate you. That sigil work on the case—that's not decoration. That's a binding lattice. Royal-grade. It means whatever's inside isn't a message, it's a *key*, and keys are the only things kings are afraid of. Which means everyone who knows it exists wants it, and the ones who want it most can't be reasoned with, bargained with, or buried. You cannot carry that alone. You especially cannot carry it alone when you flinch like a kicked dog every time I move my hand near the table."

"I don't flinch."

"You absolutely flinch."

Tamsin stared into their tea. The truth was that they hadn't slept properly since the ford, that Corvin's voice still lived somewhere behind their sternum repeating *thirteenth moon* like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing, and that every hour alone on the road had made the weight heavier instead of lighter. They had crossed the ridge to find this woman. Arguing with her about whether they needed her was a lie told with extra steps.

"I need to reach the capital," Tamsin said. "Before the thirteenth moon turns. Corvin said the binding completes then—whatever the king bound himself to becomes permanent. And I need someone who can read this." They drew out the map at last, unrolling it on the table between the cups.

Maren went very still.

Tamsin watched her take it in: the inked roads, the sigils stitched along the borders like seams, and at the center, drawn in a hand that had shaken, the shape of the capital with a spiral driven through its heart like a nail.

"That's the under-chapel," Maren whispered. "That mark. Nobody outside the palace knows there's an under-chapel. I only know because I helped catalog confiscated grimoires for three years before they confiscated *me*." Her finger hovered over the spiral without touching it, the way you hover over a wound. "This is binding-work from the founding dynasty. Older than anything in the royal archive. Whoever drew this copied it from the original."

"Can you undo it?"

"Undo it?" Maren looked up, and for the first time Tamsin saw fear in her—not the sharp practical fear of a woman with a knife, but the deep old kind, the kind that has been living in a person for years and pays rent. "Tamsin Halloran. Do you know why I was exiled? Not arrested. Exiled—they put me outside the walls alive, which the crown does perhaps once a decade, because it's crueler than killing. I was exiled because I decoded a fragment of exactly this kind of lattice and wrote a paper saying the royal line wasn't blessed by the old gods. It was *hostage* to one. My sister Sefa defended me at my hearing. Two years later the tithe came for her anyway. Magicians get taken, remember. The crown calls it a tithe like it's grain."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a night bird called once and thought better of it.

"Sefa," Tamsin said slowly. "Corvin's voice—it stayed in the bone after I carved his remains. It repeats things. Coordinates. A warning. And a name." Their throat tightened. "Maren. It says your name. Over and over. *Tell Maren. Tell Maren.*"

For a moment Maren looked like a woman who had been struck. Then the look passed, packed away with the speed of long practice, and what remained was harder and more dangerous.

"Then Corvin knew about me," she said quietly. "Which means he knew Sefa too, or knew of her, or knew the tithe rolls. Which means this map was always meant to come through Vesper and out again to *me*. He didn't stumble into your valley, bone-carver. He ran there. And he died making sure it kept running." She pressed both palms flat against the table, as if steadying herself against a ship's deck. "All right. Here is my price, and it is not negotiable, and if you refuse it I will take the map off your body while you sleep, because I have done worse for less."

Tamsin lifted their chin. "Name it."

"You hear the dead." Maren's pale eyes pinned them. "Don't bother looking shocked—only a bone-hearer could cross that ward unnoticed, and only a bone-hearer would carve a stranger's remains and keep listening afterward. Vesper breeds them; it's the whole reason the founders warded the valley, though I suspect the elders have forgotten that part. So. You guide me to the capital with that map, and when we're inside the walls, you use your hearing to find my sister. Sefa Vane. Taken in the tithe of the Ember Year, nine years ago. The crown keeps its tithed magicians somewhere, drains them somehow—I've searched eight years and gotten nowhere, because the dead in the capital are ten thousand strong and screaming, and I can't pick one voice out of that storm. But you can. That's what you do. That's all you do."

"And if she's dead?"

"Then you find her body, and you carve her a bone, and you tell me what she says." Maren's voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture in stone. "Nine years, Tamsin. Nine years of not knowing. Dead I can grieve. Silence I cannot."

Tamsin looked at her—at the burned circles scrubbed half-away on the table, at the knife within reach, at the eight years of searching written in the lines around her eyes—and felt something in their chest shift and settle, like a bone finding its joint.

"I'll do it," they said. "But I have terms too. We go fast. The moon is turning whether we sleep or starve. And when this is done—if we live—you help me make sure Vesper's elders never get their hands on this map, and never learn what I did with it."

"Elders," Maren scoffed. "What did they do, ask nicely for it?"

"They demanded. I lied." Saying it aloud still felt strange, the first lie of their life sitting in them like a swallowed stone. "It won't be the last."

"No," Maren agreed, and something almost like approval crossed her face. "It never is." She stood, went to a chest beneath the cot, and began pulling things out: dried food, a coil of good rope, a second knife which she tossed onto the table in front of Tamsin. "We leave before dawn. There's a smuggler's track along the river that avoids the tithe checkpoints until the pilgrim road forks. After that we're in conscription country, so you'll want to practice looking boring."

She paused, her back to Tamsin, hands resting on the open chest.

"One more thing," she said, not turning around. "When you heard Corvin's voice in the bone. What did he sound like?"

Tamsin considered lying, decided they were done with that for one day. "Afraid," they said. "But more than afraid. Sorry. Like he was sorry he was handing it to someone young."

Maren nodded once, sharply, and closed the chest. "They always are," she said. "Sleep. Dawn comes early, and the road to the capital is long, and everything on it is hungry."

* * *

*Maren*

She didn't sleep. She never slept the night before a journey anymore—some old instinct from the capital, where the last night before anything was the night they came for you.

Instead she sat by the banked fire and watched the bone-carver breathe, and turned the problem over and over like a stone with a sharp edge.

They were young. That was the trouble. Twenty, twenty-two at most, with sawdust still under their fingernails and a face that did not yet know how to hide what it felt. Corvin had pressed a royal key into the hands of a child from a forgetting-valley and pointed them at the hungriest city in the world, and Maren was supposed to march them straight into it.

And yet.

She turned the map toward the firelight again and traced the spiral. Nine years she had lived with the shape of that lattice in her head, decoded from a confiscated fragment, the discovery that had cost her everything. She had written her paper believing someone in power would act on it—a minister, a magistrate, anyone. Instead they had exiled her and doubled the tithe quotas, and she had spent nearly a decade telling herself she'd been wrong, that it was ambition talking, that the crown was merely corrupt rather than *occupied.*

Now a dying courier had crawled into the one place the crown couldn't see and died passing the proof to a stranger, and the stranger had walked out of the impossible valley carrying it like it weighed nothing.

Either the gods had a sense of humor, or Corvin had planned better than any man she'd ever known. Possibly both.

"Maren." The bone-carver's voice, rough with half-sleep. "You should rest. I can take the watch."

"I don't watch," Maren said. "I wait. Different discipline." She fed a stick to the coals. "Go back to sleep. You'll want it. When we cross into conscription country you'll hear things, won't you. The recently dead. The conscripted ones."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. "Yes."

"And it hurts."

"It's loud," Tamsin said carefully. "In Vesper it's whispers. Out here it's—" They stopped, hunting for the word, and Maren watched them fail to find one.

"Screaming," Maren supplied. "I know. I grew up next to a battlefield memorial. My mother said the ground there never slept." She looked at the young face across the fire—open, exhausted, already carrying more ghosts than some graveyards—and felt the unfamiliar, unwelcome pull of something that might have been protectiveness. "Listen to me, Tamsin Halloran. In the capital, the dead are everywhere and none of them are quiet. If you try to hear them all, they will drown you, and I will have dragged you across half the world to die of kindness. So you'll learn to listen narrow. One voice at a time. Mine first, if you like. Practice on mine."

"I can't hear yours. You're not dead."

"Not yet," Maren agreed. "Give the crown time."

Tamsin almost smiled. It was small and crooked and gone in a second, but it was there, and Maren found herself absurdly glad of it.

Outside, the wind moved through the dead oak, and far to the south—too far to see, close enough to feel—the thirteenth moon was climbing toward fullness, one night at a time, patient as hunger.

Maren banked the fire, checked the knives, and began, silently, to pack.
