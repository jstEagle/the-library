## 7. Ashes on the Pilgrim Road

Three days past the ridge, Tamsin learned that the world outside Vesper was not quiet. It had never been quiet. It was a choir with no conductor, and they were standing in the middle of it.

They'd thought crossing the ward line would be the worst of it—the moment when the whisper of the valley's dead became the roar of everyone's dead, everywhere, all at once. Maren had warned them. *You'll want to claw your ears off. Don't. It passes.* It hadn't passed. It had only taught them to swim.

Now, walking the Pilgrim Road south toward the capital, Tamsin swam constantly. The dead were in the ditches and the hedgerows, in the old cairns and the unmarked mounds, in the foundations of bridges built over older bones. Most of them were old voices, worn smooth as river stones, saying nothing much anymore. *Cold,* said a woman who had died two hundred years ago under an oak that no longer stood. *Cold, cold,* said a soldier from a war whose name even Maren didn't know. They were weather. You could learn to walk through weather.

It was the new voices that stopped you.

* * *

The first village was called Harrow's End, though there was nothing left to end. They came over a rise at midday and saw it whole from a distance—thatch and timber, a well, a shrine with its little tin sun catching light—and then closer, and closer still, until the distance collapsed and it wasn't whole at all.

Every door stood open. Every door stood open, and behind every door there was either nothing or the evidence of leaving: a shoe by the threshold, a pot still on the hearth, a child's wooden horse in the road where it had been dropped. Weeds already in the grain fields, though it couldn't have been more than a season. Ash in the fire pits, rained on once and hardened into gray crusts.

"Conscription," Bram said. He said it the way you'd name a disease. "Third levy in six years. They take everyone between fifteen and fifty who can hold a spear or lift a stone." He walked the street with his shoulders up around his ears, a man revisiting a place he'd been trying to forget. "The tithe wagons come through after. Anyone who can't fight but can work magic goes to the tithe houses instead."

"And anyone who resists?" Maren asked.

Bram didn't answer. He didn't have to. Tamsin could hear the answer, because the answer was standing right beside them, in the well.

Tamsin stopped at the well's edge and gripped the stone lip, and the stone was cold under their hands, and the voice rising out of the dark below was not weather at all. It was fresh. It was days old, maybe weeks, and it was screaming without sound, the way the dead do, and what it was screaming was:

*I had a name. I had a name. Someone knew my name.*

Tamsin had spent their whole life listening to the dead of Vesper, and the dead of Vesper were never anonymous. That was the whole point of the valley, the whole point of the carving, the whole point of Tamsin's hands: every bone that went into the ground beneath the memorial garden had a name cut into it, a life told back to it, so that when the voice faded—and it always faded—it faded knowing itself. The valley forgot nothing. That was its kindness and its cage both.

But here, outside, the dead were dropped like the child's wooden horse. Left where they fell. No one carved for them. No one spoke their names back. And a dead person with no one to remember them didn't fade gently—they frayed. They came apart at the edges, and the fraying felt, to anyone who could hear it, like standing next to a wound that wouldn't close.

*Tamsin.* Maren's hand on their arm. "What is it?"

"There's someone in the well."

Maren went very still. Bram's hand moved to his knife out of pure habit, then away again, ashamed.

"How long?" Maren asked quietly.

"Weeks." Tamsin's own voice sounded far away to them. "Maybe more. They were—" They swallowed. The voice was pouring into them now, fragments, images without words: a rope, a torchlight, men in crown-gray, a daughter's face. "They were thrown. They refused the levy. They had a daughter."

"Names," Maren said. It wasn't a question; she understood. Hedge-witches buried their own dead too, in hedges and hollows, with words. She knew what an unnamed death did. "Do you have a name?"

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Tamsin would carry the rest of their life: they didn't. The dead couldn't give what they'd never been given. All they had was the shape of a life—a farmer's calluses, a fear of deep water, love like a lit window—and no name to hang it on.

"No," Tamsin said. "I don't."

* * *

That night they camped in the shrine, because it had a roof, and Tamsin sat awake while the others slept and listened to the village's dead murmur in the dark like people talking in another room. And somewhere in the small hours, without deciding to, they took out their knife and a deer femur from their pack—travel stock, blank, meant for practice—and began to carve.

They didn't carve a name. There was no name. They carved what they had: broad knuckles, a scar across the wrist-bone, the way this person had held things carefully, as if everything might break. They carved the refusal—that was in the bone too, once Tamsin listened long enough, the stubborn set of a spine that had said *no* to armed men. When dawn came, the femur was covered in fine lines, and Tamsin's eyes were burning, and the voice in the well was quieter. Not gone. Quieter. As if someone, finally, had turned to listen.

"Tamsin." Maren crouched across the cold hearth, watching them with her sharp hedge-witch eyes. "What did you do?"

"I don't know." Their hands ached. "I couldn't stop myself."

Maren took the bone, turned it in the gray light. Her expression did something complicated. "This is grave-work. Proper work. My grandmother did this for the plague years—carved the ones nobody else would touch. She said it was the only magic the crown never managed to tax." She handed it back. "It costs you, though. I can see it costing you."

"It's just listening."

"It's never just listening." She looked at them a long moment. "There are thousands of dead between here and the capital, Tamsin. Thousands of new ones. Levies, tithes, the roads themselves. If you stop for every voice—"

"I know."

"Do you? Because we have twelve nights, maybe fewer, and if you spend them all carving bones for strangers—"

"*I know.*" Louder than they meant. In the corner, Bram shifted in his sleep. Tamsin lowered their voice. "I know what it costs. I also know what it's like to be left in a well."

Maren held their gaze, then nodded slowly, once. "Then we make a rule. One bone a day. One. You carve for one voice each night, and in the morning we walk, and you don't look back. Agreed?"

Tamsin agreed. It lasted four days.

* * *

The second village had burned. The third hadn't been a village at all, just a waystation where a levy column had camped, and where three conscripts had died of fever and been buried in a pit without markers, and Tamsin lay awake hearing three young men ask each other, over and over, *does anyone know we're here, does anyone know we're here*, and got up in the dark and carved three bones by moonlight while Bram kept watch and pretended not to see.

On the fifth day they passed the column itself—or what was left of it. Crown-gray banners, drooping. Wagons. Maybe two hundred people walking in chains of twos and threes, guarded by soldiers who looked barely older than the people they guarded. Farm boys with scythes still callused into their hands. A woman with gray in her hair carrying a baby too young to march, which meant she'd be made to leave it somewhere, which meant—

Tamsin heard them before they saw them. Two hundred voices, most still alive, and the alive ones were almost worse than the dead, because the dead at least knew. The living ones were still hoping, and hope made a sound Tamsin had no defense against.

A boy near the road's edge stumbled, and a guard raised his baton, and Tamsin was moving before they knew it—two steps toward the road before Maren's grip closed on their arm like a shackle.

"Don't," she breathed.

"They're—"

"They're two hundred people and eight guards and we are four, and one of us is a deserter with a price on his head and one of us is an exile with her face on tithe-house lists. If you step onto that road, we all die on that road, and the map dies with us, and the thing wearing the king eats the world on schedule." Her fingers dug in. "I hate it too. Walk."

Tamsin walked. It was the hardest twenty paces of their life. Behind them, the baton came down, and a voice cried out, and Tamsin catalogued it—added it to the ledger of sounds they'd failed to answer—and kept walking, and hated themselves with a clean and efficient hatred.

That night they broke the rule. Not one bone. Five. Tamsin sat up carving until their fingers bled at the nail beds, giving names-back to five strangers: the boy with the baton-bruise, whose name was Dannel, who'd been taken from a mill; the gray-haired grandmother, Sef, who'd hidden her granddaughter in a root cellar and prayed the soldiers wouldn't sniff out the girl's small wild talent; three others from villages whose names Tamsin never learned. At some point Bram woke and sat nearby, silent, sharpening a knife he didn't need to sharpen, keeping watch over the carving the way you keep watch over a sickbed.

"You can't save them," he said finally. Not cruelly. Bram didn't seem capable of cruelty; guilt had burned it out of him. "I marched behind columns like that for six years. You can't save any of them by carving."

"I'm not saving them."

"Then what are you doing?"

Tamsin blew bone dust off the last line. "Refusing to be part of the forgetting. The king wants a country with no memory. That's how you keep people marching—no graves, no names, nothing to come home to. Every bone I carve is a record he can't burn."

Bram was quiet a long time. Then: "My commander used to say something like that. Before." He didn't finish. He didn't need to; Tamsin had carried the commander's apology to him on the second day, and Bram had wept like a breached dam, and neither of them ever mentioned it again.

* * *

On the eighth night, the rule broke entirely, because they reached a place where the road itself was wrong.

Maren felt it first—a wrongness in the hedge-magic, a flat spot, like a tongue finding a missing tooth. Then Tamsin heard it: silence. A hole in the roar of the dead, a mile long, exactly the shape of the road. No old voices. No new ones. Nothing.

"What does that?" Bram asked, hand on his knife.

"The crown," Maren said grimly. "Silence-work. They strip the dead out of places they want forgotten. Battlefields. Mass graves." She dismounted the cart horse and touched the dirt at the roadside, and her face went gray. "Or places where there were too many dead to leave lying around loose."

They walked the silent mile because there was no other road, and the silence pressed on Tamsin like deep water, and halfway through it Tamsin understood what the silence was made of, and stopped walking.

It wasn't empty. Silence-work didn't destroy the dead—it *packed* them. Compressed them, folded them down into the earth like linen into a chest, layer on layer on layer, hundreds deep, thousands, all along the road's verge where the mass graves ran. They were all still there. They were all still *saying their names*. Hundreds of voices, compressed to a single thread of sound, and the thread was fraying, and when it frayed completely, everything inside it would be gone—not faded, *gone*, unwitnessed, as if they had never lived at all.

"Tamsin." Maren's voice, very far away. "Tamsin, your nose is bleeding."

Tamsin knelt in the road. They didn't decide to. Their knees simply folded, and their hands found a stone, a piece of road-metal, anything, and they began to scratch lines into it, because there was no bone, no time, no possible way to carve for a thousand dead, and they did it anyway, they scratched and scratched, and the blood from their nose dripped onto the stone and the dead in the packed dark below rose toward the sound of their name being attempted like drowning people rising toward light.

*We're here,* the thread sang, fraying. *We're here, we're here, we're—*

"I know," Tamsin whispered. Blood on their teeth. "I know you're here. I'm writing it down."

Hands pulled them up. Bram, one arm around their ribs, hauling them off the road like a drunkard. Maren ahead, walking backward, watching the tree line. Somewhere a crow laughed.

"You can't carve a thousand dead," Bram said into their ear, half-carrying them. "No one can. Not even you."

"Then I'll be the one who tried."

He didn't argue. He just walked them out of the silence, and at the edge of it, where the old voices resumed their weather-murmur, Tamsin looked back once at the mile of quiet road, and made a promise to it that they suspected they'd die keeping.

* * *

They made camp that night in a ruined tithe-station, a squat stone building with iron rings in the walls, and Maren built no fire, and nobody spoke much. Tamsin's hands shook too badly to carve. They sat against the wall with their bleeding nose pinched, listening to the ordinary dead of the ordinary hills, and tried to feel like a person instead of a wound.

Sefa sat down beside them.

She rarely chose to sit near anyone; since the tithe house she flinched from touch and noise alike. But now she lowered herself to the stones at Tamsin's side, wordless as always, and after a moment she took Tamsin's scraped, trembling hand in both of hers and turned it palm-up, and studied it, and then traced one finger across the palm—slow, deliberate, a line like a carving stroke.

Then she looked up, and her hollowed eyes were wet.

*One at a time,* she was saying. *That's how my mother did the plague year. One at a time. It was enough. It has to be enough.*

"She's right," Maren said softly, from across the dark. She'd seen. She always saw. "One at a time, Tamsin. That's not failure. That's the whole craft."

Tamsin looked down at their ruined hands, at the sister holding them like something worth saving, at the deserter and the witch keeping watch in the doorway against a world that had done all this. Four people in a ruin, none of them family a month ago.

"One at a time," Tamsin said.

They slept, finally, and dreamed of nothing, which was a mercy.

They woke before dawn to Bram shaking their shoulder, his face lit orange from the ridge line to the east, where—far off, but unmistakable, a smudge against the stars—the capital's beacon towers had begun to burn.

All of them. At once.

"That's a summons," Bram said hoarsely. "Full mobilization. Something's spooked the crown." He looked at Tamsin, and his eyes were afraid in a way Tamsin had never seen. "They know we're coming."
