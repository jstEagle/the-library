## 8. The Deserter

Bram Vey had been following the witch and the bone-carver for six days, and in six days he had learned three things.

First: the witch slept with one hand in the fire, which was either a habit or a threat, and he hadn't decided which.

Second: the bone-carver—Tamsin, the witch called them, though they never seemed to answer to it happily—talked to their knife while they worked. Not to themselves. To the knife. Low, patient, like a man coaxing a mule across a flooded ford.

Third: neither of them was what they claimed to be, because nobody who was what they claimed to be traveled with a map case stamped with the royal sigil and didn't burn it on the first night.

Bram crouched in the bracken above the road and watched their camp through the last grey light of evening. Two figures. A small fire, banked low. The witch stirring something in a dented pot, and Tamsin sitting apart, turning a length of bone over in their hands the way other people turned worry-beads.

Crown agents. It had to be crown agents. Who else would be out here? The king's tithe wagons ran the pilgrim road twice a season, but those moved in columns of twelve with crossbows on the benches. This was different. This was quiet. Quiet was how the crown did its real work—he knew that better than most, because for nine years he *had been* the quiet work.

He touched the scar at his collarbone without meaning to. Old habit. The mark where the captain's blade had opened him when he'd said no.

*No.* One word. Nine years of service, and it came down to one word spoken in a burning village square, and everything after it—the running, the hunger, the winter he'd eaten bark off trees to stay alive—everything after it was just the price of that word.

And now two strangers were walking toward the capital carrying royal binding-work, and every instinct he had left said: *follow them, find out why, and if they're the crown's, put a knife in the dark between them.*

He'd told himself that for six days. He still hadn't done it. He wasn't sure anymore whether that was caution or cowardice, and not knowing scared him more than either answer.

The witch looked up suddenly. Straight at the bracken. Straight at him, or near enough that his spine went cold.

"Your soup's burning," she said—to the fire, apparently—and went back to stirring.

Bram let out a slow breath. Coincidence. Had to be.

It wasn't coincidence. He'd learn that soon enough.

* * *

They caught him two nights later, and it was embarrassing how easily they did it.

He'd gotten careless. That was the truth of it, and he made himself sit with the truth because lies were what had rotted the Guard from the inside out. He'd stopped sleeping properly around day four, and on the sixth night he dozed against an oak no more than three hundred paces off their camp, and when he woke there was a knife at his throat and the witch's knee on his chest and her face above his, lit orange by a torch Tamsin held.

"Well," she said. "Look what fell out of the tree."

"I'm not—" His voice cracked. He cleared his throat. "I'm not with them."

"With who?" the witch asked pleasantly.

"The crown."

"You've been following us for six days," she said. "Sleeping in our leavings. Eating what we don't finish—I found your boot prints at the waystation trough, friend, you drank out of the horse side." She pressed the knife a fraction closer. "That's not the behavior of a man who isn't with someone."

"I was making sure," Bram said. "You're carrying crown work. I saw the map case. I needed to know—"

"Needed to know what?"

Whether they were hunting him. Whether the deserter's brand on his file had finally grown legs. Whether the order he'd refused had come looking for its balance. But saying any of that aloud felt like handing them the knife handle-first.

So instead he said nothing, and Tamsin—the quiet one, the one with the strange flat valley accent and eyes that never quite settled—crouched down beside him and looked at him for a long moment.

"He's afraid," Tamsin said. Not to the witch. To the air, maybe. "Not of us."

"Everyone's afraid of us," the witch said. "I find it flattering."

"Not of us," Tamsin repeated, and then, softer, almost puzzled: "Of himself."

Bram's chest went tight. "You don't know a thing about me."

"No," Tamsin agreed. They tilted their head, listening to something Bram couldn't hear—which he'd noticed them doing before, always a half-second behind the world, as if conversation arrived to them by slow post. "But the road does."

"The road," the witch said flatly.

"People leave things on the road," Tamsin said. "Grief mostly. Sometimes worse." They stood, and looked down at Bram with something that wasn't pity, exactly. Pity would have been easier to bear. "We're not crown agents. We're going to the capital to stop the crown. You can believe that or not. Maren, let him up."

Maren—the witch had a name after all—didn't move. "He's been shadowing us for six days. Could be reporting positions. Could have signal whistles. Could be—"

"If he were crown," Tamsin said, "he'd have called for help by now. There's no one else out here. Listen."

And Bram, who had spent six days telling himself the same thing—that no one was coming, that no one ever came for deserters, that was rather the point of deserting—felt the words land somewhere under his ribs and stick.

Maren searched his face a moment longer, then got off him and sheathed her knife with a flick of disgust, as if the whole business bored her. "Fine. Truce. But you walk in front where we can see you, and if you so much as whistle, I'll turn your knees backward."

"That's fair," Bram said hoarsely.

"It is fair," she agreed. "I'm glad we're honest with each other."

Tamsin offered him a hand up. Their grip was cool and dry, a craftsman's grip, calloused in odd places. Up close Bram could see the fine bone dust in the creases of their knuckles, pale as flour.

"Bram Vey," he said, because it seemed like the thing to do, and immediately regretted it. A name was a thread. A name could be pulled.

"Tamsin." A pause. "You flinched."

"I didn't."

"You flinched when you said your name. Like it cost you." Tamsin's head tilted again, that listening tilt. "Vey. Vey. Why does the road know that name?"

"There's no road," Maren said, packing up camp with sharp efficient movements. "There's a madman and a superstition and me, and I want to be past the ford by dawn. Walk, deserter."

* * *

He walked in front, as ordered, and hated every step of it, and by the third hour had begun talking despite himself. That was the trick of walking in front, he realized too late—it turned you into a guide, and guides filled silence, and silence was where he kept the things that hurt.

He told them about the Royal Guard without meaning to. The white cloaks. The oath sworn on iron, not paper, because paper burned and iron remembered. The years of escort duty, tithe runs, keeping order in towns whose names he'd let blur. And then—because his mouth had apparently decided to hang itself—Ashford.

"Ashford?" Maren said, from behind him.

"Nothing."

"You said Ashford."

"It's nothing."

Tamsin had gone very quiet. Bram could feel it at his back, that listening quality, like standing near a door someone might open.

"There was a village," he said finally, because the alternative was carrying it another mile, and his shoulders had been carrying it since autumn and the straps had cut through long ago. "Ashford Hollow. We were sent to collect the tithe. Magicians, or suspected magicians. There was a list. Names on it." He watched the road. "One of the names was wrong. A clerk's error—a boy of eleven, no gift in him at all, I'd have staked my cloak on it. I said so. Captain Dree said the list was the list. I said the boy was a child. Dree said—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "Dree said take him or stand aside, and if I stood aside I'd stand with him. In the wagon. So I took him. I put the boy in the wagon myself. Because the alternative was dying next to him and the wagon going anyway with one more body in it."

Silence behind him. Hoofbeats of wind in dead grass.

"They burned the village anyway," Bram said. "After. For resistance that didn't happen. And I stood in the square in my white cloak and did nothing, because doing nothing was all I had left, and then that night I cut my cloak into rags and walked south and I haven't stopped walking since." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Nine years. All that training. And the best use I ever found for it was running."

"You didn't run," Tamsin said quietly.

"Deserters run."

"You stayed in the square until the burning was done. You counted the dead. I'd guess you know the number."

Bram stopped walking. "Forty-one," he said, before he could stop himself. "Forty-two with the boy."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "You know it because you stood there and took it in, all of it, because someone had to. That's not running. That's witness. It's a kind of work." A pause. "Though it's heavy work. No wonder you're tired."

Bram turned around. Tamsin was watching him with that unsettled gaze, and Maren was watching Tamsin with narrowed eyes, and the space between the three of them had gone strange and thin, like air before lightning.

"What are you?" Bram asked. "What do you hear?"

Tamsin opened their mouth—and closed it. Shook their head slowly. "Later," they said. "When I'm sure."

"Sure of what?"

"Sure I should say it out loud." They started walking again. "Because once I say it, you'll either hate us or need us, and I'd like one more night of not knowing which."

* * *

The night came cold and starless, and they made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, some old border ruin with moss eating its stones. Maren set wards with salt and muttered syllables; Bram pretended not to note their pattern, then noted it anyway, because old habits. Tamsin sat apart with their knife and a piece of antler, working.

Bram couldn't sleep. He'd known he wouldn't. He lay watching the firelight move on the tower stones and thinking about forty-two, and about the boy's name, which was Tammas, which was a cruelty the world had played on him without knowing it, because now every time the carver said their own name he'd hear the boy's.

He must have drifted, because when the voice came he thought at first he was dreaming.

"Bram Vey."

He sat up fast. The camp was still. Maren asleep sitting up, hand in the embers. Tamsin across the fire, knife resting, looking at him.

"You spoke," Bram said.

"I listened," Tamsin said. "And then I repeated. That's all I do. I'm a listener. Bones hold what the living leave in them, and I can hear it, if the bone's worked right. It's quieter in the valley where I'm from. Out here it's—" they gestured vaguely at the dark, at everything— "loud. But tonight, near the tower, there was a soldier's grave. Old. The marker stone fell years ago, but the bones are still there, under the moss, and they're worked—someone carved them, a long time ago, the old style. And one of them has been trying to talk since sundown."

Bram's mouth had gone dry. "Trying to say what?"

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Whatever they saw in his face made them decide.

"It says: *Bram Vey, Second Company, if you ever come this way.*" A breath. "It says: *the order was wrong and I knew it and I made you carry it because I was a coward, and I have been sorry every day since, and I am sorry now.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a fox screamed and quit.

"It says," Tamsin went on, softer, "*you were the only one who said no, and I have thought about that word every day of my death. Tell him it counted. Tell him it counted for more than he knows.*"

Bram didn't remember standing. He was on his feet with the fire between them, and his hands were shaking, and his eyes were hot and blind, and nine years of walking came up out of him all at once like a spearhead working free.

"Dree," he said. "Captain Dree. He's dead?"

"Two winters," Tamsin said. "Fever, I think. The bone doesn't say. The bone only says what it was left holding." They rose too, careful, unhurried, hands visible. "I can't make it say anything else. I can't even promise it's truly him and not just the shape of his regret. Bones hold what's poured into them. But whatever it is—it's been waiting a long time for someone to carry it, and I think it was waiting for you."

"He never apologized." Bram's voice came out wrecked. "Not once. Not when I bled for it, not after, not—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I didn't want it. That's the worst part. I told myself I didn't want it. And I have wanted nothing else for nine years."

"I know," Tamsin said simply. "It knows too. That's why it kept trying."

Bram laughed—or sobbed, it was hard to find the line in the dark—and sat back down hard, and put his face in his hands, and for the first time since a burning square in Ashford Hollow, he let himself shake.

He didn't know how long. When he surfaced, Maren was awake and watching with an expression that wasn't quite soft but had stopped being hostile. She handed him a tin cup of something bitter and hot without comment. It was the closest thing to kindness he'd been offered in years, and he took it like a sacrament.

"Why," he managed finally, to Tamsin. "Why tell me? You don't know me. By your own account you weren't sure of me an hour ago."

"I'm still not sure of you," Tamsin said. "But I'm sure of that message. And it seemed wrong to make you both wait." They picked up their knife again, turned the antler in the firelight. "Besides. We're going to the capital to unmake something the crown built. We'll need everyone who's ever said no to the crown and lived. That's a short list, Bram Vey. I'd like it to be shorter than one."

Bram looked into the cup. Steam curled off it, shapeless, rising.

"The thing you're going to unmake," he said slowly. "In the capital." He'd seen things in the palace he'd never told anyone. Things in the walls of the prayer chapel. The king alone at midnight, talking to nobody, his shadow moving a half-step behind his body like a man walking in shallow water. "You don't know what it is yet. Do you."

"No," Tamsin said. "Do you?"

Bram stared into the fire for a long time. Then he heard himself say, in the flat voice of a man stepping off a ledge:

"I've seen it."
