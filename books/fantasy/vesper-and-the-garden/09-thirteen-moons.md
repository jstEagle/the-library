## 9. Thirteen Moons

The waystation had been a shrine once. Maren could tell by the alcove in the back wall, the one some practical soul had bricked over and turned into a cupboard for lamp oil. Pilgrims had built it three hundred years ago to honor a saint whose name nobody remembered, and now it sold weak beer and weaker stew to carters on the Ash Road, and the saint's bones were probably under the floorboards holding up somebody's bed.

Maren sat at the corner table with the map spread out under her hands and pretended to be reading a prayer book she'd bought off the shelf by the door. The prayer book was mostly water damage. The map was mostly terror.

Tamsin sat across from her, carving. They always carved when they were nervous — small things, tokens, the kind of memorial bones Vesper folk hung on their doorposts. Tonight it was a sparrow, half-finished, turning slowly between their fingers while their knife whispered along its wing. Bram stood at the window with his arms crossed, watching the road both ways like a man who expected his past to come walking up it at any moment. Which, Maren supposed, was exactly what he expected.

Sefa slept on the bench beside her sister, curled small, her breathing shallow and even. She hadn't spoken since the tithe house. She hadn't let go of Maren's hand for more than a minute at a time, either, except just now, when sleep had finally pulled her under. Maren missed the weight of it already.

"Tell me again what you're looking for," Tamsin said quietly.

"Anything I missed." Maren turned the map ninety degrees. "The first time I read this thing, I saw roads. Sigils. A route from here to the capital with the binding-work marked along it like milestones. That's all I saw, because that's all I was looking for." She tapped the parchment's edge, where the courier's blood had stiffened into a brown crescent. "But royal binding-work isn't decoration. Every sigil is also a sentence. It's a language, not a picture. And languages have grammar."

"And you've found the grammar."

"I've found *a* grammar." She pulled the candle closer. "Maybe. If I'm right, then everything I told you before is wrong in one important way, and we need to know if I'm right tonight, because if I'm wrong we can keep walking at a sensible pace, and if I'm right—"

She stopped. The knife had gone still in Tamsin's hands.

"If you're right," Tamsin said, "then what?"

Maren looked down at the map instead of at them. There were things she'd learned to do with her face, in the capital, in the years before her exile — ways of going smooth and unreadable, a hedge against every room she walked into. She'd thought she'd left that habit behind on the Ash Road. Apparently not. Apparently it came back the moment the stakes got high enough.

"Maren," Tamsin said. Not unkindly. Just steady, the way they said most things.

"The sigils aren't milestones," Maren said. "They're a countdown."

* * *

She'd worked it out an hour past midnight, alone by the fire while the others slept, and she'd spent the hours since checking it, hating it, checking it again. Now she laid it out for them the way she'd lay out a diagnosis: plainly, in order, without flinching, because flinching never helped anyone.

"The binding that holds the god into the crown — the original one, the one made centuries ago — wasn't one working. It was thirteen. Thirteen anchors, set at intervals, each one tied to a turn of the moon. Whoever built it wanted it slow. Deliberate. Reversible, if someone caught it early enough." Her finger traced the outer ring of sigils, the ones she'd taken for ornament. "Each anchor has to be renewed. Fed. And the feeding schedule is written here, in the margins, in cipher — the same cipher the Royal Binders used when I studied under them, which is how I know, and which is also why I'm going to be very surprised if I die of old age."

Bram turned from the window. "Fed with what?"

"What do you think?" Maren didn't look up. "What does the crown harvest magicians for? What are the tithe houses *for*? We assumed power. Draining spellwork to fill the king's coffers and his guard's wards. But draining is just the first step. They're not storing it. They're spending it. Every magician the crown takes goes into keeping the thirteenth anchor fed, and the thirteenth anchor is the last one, and when it's full—"

"When it's full," Tamsin said, "the binding completes."

"The binding completes." Maren sat back. Her hands had gone cold; she pressed them flat on the table to warm them. "And here's the part I need you to hear clearly, because I got it wrong before and I'd rather be corrected by you than by reality. I told you the binding was what kept the god *in*. That finishing it would be the catastrophe. That's backwards. The binding isn't a cage around the god. It's a tether between the god and the king. Thirteen anchors, thirteen tethers, woven through Aldous's soul one moon-turn at a time. As long as the binding is incomplete, the god needs him. Needs a living human soul to pour itself through, needs his body to walk around in, needs his mouth to pray through. He's the vessel. Half-full, he's still a man with something inside him, and a man can be reached. Can beg. Can consent." She heard her own voice go flat and made herself lift it again. "When the thirteenth anchor fills, the vessel stops being necessary. The god will have eaten so much of him that there's nothing left to distinguish them. It won't wear the king anymore. It'll simply *be* the king, and there will be no Aldous left inside to save, and no rite on this earth that can cut them apart, because there won't be two things to separate."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the road, a night-bird called twice and went quiet.

"How long," Bram said. Not a question, really. His voice had the sound of a man asking for a wound count.

Maren did the arithmetic one more time, though she'd done it until the numbers were burned into the backs of her eyes. "The cipher gives the interval between feedings. Twenty-eight days, one per lunar turn, and the sequence started—" she counted backward through the marginal dates, the little hooked numerals she'd spent four hours cracking, "—twelve turns ago. The twelfth feeding happened eleven nights ago, at the last full moon. Which means the thirteenth anchor gets its final filling on the next full moon."

"Twelve nights," Tamsin said.

"Twelve nights. Give or take a few hours I can't calculate without an almanac, so let's not give or take anything. Twelve nights, and we are—" she measured the distance on the map with her thumb, the way she'd measured it a dozen times already as if hoping it would shrink, "—nine days' hard travel from the capital. If the weather holds. If the roads hold. If nobody stops us, follows us, or recognizes any of us, which, given the company, is a generous set of ifs."

Bram made a sound that might have been a laugh in a better life. "I'm the recognizable one."

"You're all recognizable. You're a deserter of the Royal Guard with a face like a wanted poster. Tamsin is carrying a dead courier's map that the crown has presumably been hunting since the moment he slipped the leash. And I am Maren of the Ash Road, exiled for binder-work done without license, and the queen regent's spymaster has my description memorized down to the scar on my chin." She touched the scar absently. "We are, collectively, the least anonymous rescue mission in the history of the kingdom."

"Rescue," Tamsin repeated. They'd picked up the sparrow again, but they weren't carving it. Just holding it, thumb moving over the unfinished wing. "You keep saying rescue. Like he's worth rescuing."

Maren looked at them across the candle flame. In the week since the ford, since the stranger died in their workshop with a king's name in his mouth, she'd watched Tamsin carry the map like a debt rather than a gift — grimly, resentfully, the way you carry a burden someone else dropped on you. She understood it. She'd carried plenty of debts that weren't hers. But she'd also seen what happened in the tithe house, when Tamsin knelt beside the drained magicians and carved names for them, one after another, unable to stop, unable to walk past a single hollowed body without giving it back its name. People like that didn't get to decide who deserved saving. People like that saved whoever was in front of them, because the alternative was becoming the kind of person who could walk past.

"He's a hostage," Maren said. "Aldous. Whatever he was when this started — and I don't know, maybe he was cruel, maybe he was weak, maybe he was just the wrong man crowned at the wrong time — he's been drowning for twelve turns of the moon with something eating him from the inside. You met him yet?"

"No."

"You will. And when you do, I want you to remember that the thing begging you might be the last scrap of him, and the thing offering you bargains might be everything else." She gathered the map's edges, rolled it, tied it. "Twelve nights. So here's what we know that we didn't know yesterday. One: we can't take the pilgrim road at a walker's pace anymore. Two: the tithe houses along our route are supply lines. Every magician they drain goes to the capital for the final feeding, which means the roads ahead are going to be busy with carts and guards, and it also means—"

She stopped. The thought arrived fully formed, ugly and useful, the way the best plans always were.

"It also means," Bram said slowly, watching her face, "that every tithe house we pass is a piece of the thirteenth anchor."

"Every drained magician is a measure of it. And the final feeding needs all of it delivered." Maren nodded. "Which means the crown will be moving its cargo carefully, heavily guarded, on a schedule. And it means that if we wanted to hurt the binding — really hurt it, buy ourselves more than twelve nights — we wouldn't need to reach the capital at all. We'd only need to reach one cart."

Silence around the table. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"That's a war," Bram said at last. "That's not a rescue, that's a war on the road, against the Royal Guard, with three fighters, one of whom is me."

"Two and a half," Maren said. "I throw a mean hex."

"You threw a hex at a locked door last week and it gave you a nosebleed."

"The door was warded."

"The door was *oak*, Maren."

Tamsin laughed — short, startled out of them, gone as fast as it came, but real. Maren found she'd been waiting for it without knowing. Found, too, that the sound settled something in her chest that had been rattling since midnight, since the numbers, since *twelve nights*.

"We don't decide tonight," she said. "Tonight we sleep, because tomorrow we move fast and tired people die stupidly. But tomorrow, when we hit the crossroads at Harrow Ford, we make a choice. Straight to the capital and gamble everything on reaching Aldous before the moon turns. Or—" she tapped the map where a small red mark sat two days east, a tithe house she'd noted weeks ago without knowing why, "—we pay the Ash Road a visit first."

Sefa stirred in her sleep. Without waking, her hand crept across the bench, found her sister's wrist, and closed around it. Maren went still, letting her hold on, and watched the fire, and did the arithmetic again even though the numbers never changed.

Twelve nights. Nine days' travel. Three days of margin, if nothing went wrong.

Something always went wrong.

* * *

She took the second watch, because Bram had the first and Tamsin refused to take one at all — claimed they couldn't sleep anyway, claimed the silence outside the valley was too loud, which Maren had stopped questioning somewhere around the third day of hearing them describe the world's dead as a roar. She sat by the shuttered window with the prayer book open on her knees for cover and the map's cipher running behind her eyes like a fever.

That was when she noticed the fourth line.

It was in the margin of the final segment, the capital approach, tucked under a fold she'd smoothed out a hundred times without reading closely — a short string of cipher, smaller than the rest, almost an afterthought. She'd catalogued it days ago as a delivery note. *Cargo to arrive complete.* Standard binder's phrasing.

Except it wasn't standard. She knew the standard phrasing. She'd been exiled partly for knowing it too well. This was older — the archaic conjugation, the one from the first-century binders, the ones who'd built the original thirteen anchors. And the archaic conjugation of *complete* didn't mean finished.

It meant *consummated*. Made whole by union. The word they'd used for weddings. For mergings.

For the moment a bound thing and its vessel ceased to be two.

Maren read it six times. Then she set the prayer book down, put her head in her hands, and breathed until the shaking stopped.

The god wasn't waiting for the thirteenth moon to become free. The thirteenth moon was when it would stop needing permission. Until then, every act of it — every order given through the king's mouth, every tithe collected, every magician drained — still required the thin fiction of a human hand. Still required Aldous. Which meant that somewhere in the palace, in whatever lucid scraps remained of him, the king could still say no. Could still refuse. Could still, if someone reached him, withhold the one thing no binding could take: consent.

The rite needed his voice. She'd known that abstractly. Now she knew it was the whole plan, the entire slender thread they were gambling nine days of hard road on — that a drowned man, at the bottom of his own possession, could still choose.

Footsteps behind her. Tamsin, wrapped in their blanket, ghost-pale in the dark.

"You felt it," Maren whispered. "Didn't you. Something changed."

Tamsin shook their head slowly. Then, after a moment, tilted it — the gesture they used when words failed, when the roar outside was too much to translate. They crossed the room, sat beside her, and held out their palm.

On it, the half-finished sparrow. Finished now, sometime in the dark hours. And beneath its feet, cut fine as hair, a row of tiny marks.

Thirteen of them.

The last one, freshly cut, still showing pale wood at the bottom of the groove.

Maren looked at it for a long time. Outside, far to the west, behind cloud, the moon was setting — fat, one night off full, pouring its thin light across the Ash Road and everything on it.

"Twelve nights," she said.

Tamsin closed their fist around the sparrow and nodded, and in the grey before dawn they began packing to move.
