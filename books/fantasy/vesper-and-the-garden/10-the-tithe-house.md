## 10. The Tithe House

The tithe house sat in a fold of hills like a wound that had learned to dress itself.

From the pilgrim road it looked almost gentle—a long stone building with a slate roof, whitewashed walls, a bell over the door that rang the hours. There were flower boxes under the windows. Someone had planted rosemary.

"That's for the smell," Maren said. She hadn't spoken much since we'd found the waystation and read what the map's third ring actually said. Her voice was flat as slate now, scraped down to nothing decorative. "Drained magicians. The process stinks. Like copper and burnt hair."

"You've seen one before," Bram said. Not a question.

"I escaped one." Maren adjusted the strap of her satchel for the fourth time since dawn. "Sefa didn't."

We lay in the gorse on the hillside above it, the three of us, watching the guards change shift at the gate. Two men out, two men in. Bored movements. The loose shoulders of soldiers who had never been shot at. I counted everything the way Bram had taught me—sight lines, lantern spacing, the dog run along the eastern fence, the kitchen door propped open with a brick because the latch was broken and nobody had fixed it.

Small cruelties of neglect. That was how you got in.

"The drain room will be below ground," Maren said. "Always is. You don't want to hear what comes out of them." She said *them* the way you'd say a word you'd bitten your tongue on too many times. "Tamsin. When we get down there—you'll hear things. Dead things, and worse than dead. If it gets to be too much—"

"It won't."

"Tamsin."

"I spent twenty-six years listening to my whole valley whisper through bone," I said. "I can hold a door open in my head. I've done it before."

I hadn't done it in a place like this. But she needed me to say it, so I said it, and she looked at me for a long moment with those ash-grey eyes that missed nothing, and then she nodded once, because she understood about lying to keep people moving.

Bram rolled onto his back and studied the sky. "Kitchen door at dusk. The guard rotation thins when they bring the supper cart. Tamsin walks in front looking simple—I'm sorry, but you have the face for it—and I walk behind looking like an escort. Maren stays outside with the dog and a length of sausage."

"I'm not staying outside."

"Maren." He said her name gently, which from Bram was rarer than gold. "If this goes wrong, someone has to be outside who knows where we went. And if Sefa's in there—" He stopped. Started again. "You'll want to burn it down. I know I would. Somebody has to stay cold enough to pull us both out."

Maren stared at him. Then at the tithe house, with its rosemary and its flower boxes and its bell ringing four o'clock into the empty hills.

"Half an hour," she said finally. "Then I come in after you whether you've found her or not."

"Fair," Bram said.

It wasn't fair. Half an hour wouldn't be enough for anything. We all knew it, and none of us said it, and the bell rang again, and we waited for dusk the way people wait for a tooth to be pulled.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of onions and something underneath the onions—the copper-and-burnt-hair smell, faint but present, soaked into the stones themselves. A cook glanced up at us, took in Bram's guard's coat—he'd kept it, all this time, folded at the bottom of his pack like a confession—and went back to her knives without a word. Whatever happened in this place, the staff had decided long ago not to see it.

"Tithe inspection," Bram said anyway, loud enough for anyone listening. "Crown business."

"Down the hall, past the stairs," the cook said to her cutting board. "Mind the third step. It's loose."

The hallway was clean. Scrubbed floors, fresh whitewash, little framed verses on the walls about service and gratitude. *The Gift Returns Tenfold.* *Blessed Are the Chosen Vessels.* My skin crawled just reading them, and I hadn't even heard anything yet.

Because I could hear something. Even here, even through the ward-dampened quiet I'd learned to expect from buildings, there was a sound underneath the sound. A hum. Not the valley's ward-hum, which was smooth as a held breath. This one stuttered. It caught and dragged, like a wheel turning through mud.

Like many voices trying to be one voice and failing.

"Third step," Bram murmured, stepping over it. He'd gone soldier-stiff the moment we crossed the threshold, spine straight, jaw set, every inch the man he used to be. It frightened me a little, how well it fit him. "Stairs down are guarded. One man. I know him—Corporal Ennett. Young. Scared. He'll stand aside for an inspector if I lean on him right."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then I lean harder."

The stairs were narrow and lit by oil lamps set in niches, and the hum grew louder with every step until it wasn't underneath anything anymore. It filled the stairwell. It filled my skull. I pressed my palm flat against the wall and felt it vibrating faintly, and beneath my hand the old plaster held a memory—not a death, nothing so clean, just years of fear soaked in like smoke into a ceiling.

I heard: *please, please, I didn't mean to, it was only a cantrip, I was only nine—*

Nine.

I took my hand off the wall and made myself stop touching things.

At the bottom, a young guard sat on a stool beside an iron door, picking at the stitching on his cuff. He stood up so fast the stool tipped. "Sergeant Vey?" His voice cracked on the name. "They said you deserted. They said you were—"

"They say a lot of things." Bram let his voice drop into that parade-ground register. "Inspection. Crown seal." He produced a wax stamp he'd apparently stolen or forged or both; I decided not to ask. "Open the door, Corporal."

Ennett looked at the seal. Looked at Bram's face. Looked at me, standing behind with my hands folded and my expression arranged into the bland patience of minor officialdom. I could hear his heart going like a rabbit's. I could hear, underneath it, the thought he was too disciplined to say aloud: *they say Sergeant Vey cut down two men at the chapel gate rather than follow orders, they say the order was to open the door to the thing in the walls.*

He opened the iron door.

"Thank you, Corporal," Bram said quietly. "You'll want to be somewhere else for the next half hour. Go check the east fence."

Ennett fled. I didn't blame him.

* * *

Beyond the iron door, the corridor sloped down into the earth, and the hum became a chorus, and the chorus became unbearable.

There were cells on either side. Doors of wood, not iron—wood, because iron would have interfered with the drawing, I realized later, though at the time I only knew that the doors were thin and the sounds came through them like water through cloth. Not screaming. Screaming would have been easier. What came through the doors was quieter than that. Moaning. Praying. In one cell, someone singing a lullaby in a language I didn't know, over and over, getting the words slightly more wrong each time, as if the song itself was being drained out of her note by note.

And everywhere, threaded through it all, the stuttering hum of magic being pulled out of living people and piped upward, stored, spent. I could feel it in my teeth. Outside the valley, where magic ran loud and wild through everything, these cells were the loudest places I had ever stood—loud with theft.

*Tamsin.* Maren's voice, remembered. *You'll hear things.*

I built the door in my head. The one I'd built crossing the ridge, the one that turned the roar of the world's dead into something I could carry. I built it narrower this time. I let in only what I needed: the direction of the strongest drawing, the layout of the dark.

Twelve cells. Eleven occupied. At the corridor's end, a larger room glowed with lamplight through a grille, and the hum came from there, thick and rhythmic, like breathing.

"Sefa would be in the main draw room," Maren had told me. "She was strong. They keep the strong ones closest to the apparatus. They work them hardest." Her hands had shaken when she said it. Everything else about her had been still.

Bram took the left side of the corridor. I took the right. We moved fast and quiet, and I did not look through the grilles in the wooden doors, because if I looked I would stop, and if I stopped we would lose the half hour, and Maren would come in burning.

But hearing isn't seeing. Hearing comes through walls whether you want it or not.

In the second cell, a boy—no, a man, grown, but small-voiced—was reciting multiplication tables to himself. *Seven times eight is fifty-six. Seven times nine is sixty-three.* Holding on to numbers because numbers couldn't be drained. In the fifth cell, silence, and then a woman's voice saying, very clearly, very calmly, as if reporting the weather: *I have forgotten my mother's face. I remember that I loved her. I cannot remember her face.* In the eighth—

In the eighth cell, someone was carving.

Scratching sounds. Fingernails or a button or a spoon handle against wood, deliberate, rhythmic. And through the scratch, faint as a thread pulled from a hem, a shape of intention: not words, not prayer. *Marks. Sigils. Writing on the inside of the door.* Writing that said, in the careful grammar of binding-work I'd been staring at on Maren's map for weeks:

*I am Sefa Ashroad. I am still here. Whoever finds this: I am still here.*

I stopped walking.

"Tamsin." Bram's hand closed on my shoulder. "Keep moving."

"She's marking the door." My own voice sounded far away. "Bram. She's writing binding-sigils on the inside of her door. Blind, by feel, with her fingernails. She's telling whoever comes that she's still *in there*—"

"Tamsin. Half an hour."

I tore myself away. But I marked the door. Eighth cell. I carved the number into my memory the way I carve names into bone, deep enough to survive anything.

* * *

The draw room hit us like weather.

It was a vaulted cellar, older than the building above it—older than anything, its stones black with age and something else, something rubbed into them by centuries of use. Lamps burned in iron brackets. In the center stood the apparatus: a great wheel of copper and glass and silver wire, turning slowly, driven by water somewhere below, and around the wheel, in chairs bolted to the floor, sat six figures.

Six hollowed people, wired to the machine with filaments that pulsed faintly gold. Their eyes were open. Their eyes saw nothing. With each turn of the wheel, light moved along the filaments—from them, into the wheel, up through a conduit in the ceiling, away. Spent somewhere. On wards, maybe. On the king's prayers. On the thing that answered when he prayed.

Two attendants in grey robes monitored the flow, making notes in ledgers. They looked up when we entered, and their faces held the mild annoyance of clerks interrupted.

"You're not scheduled until—" the taller one began.

Bram hit him. It was quick and professional and the attendant went down like a sack of meal. The other one backed against the wheel, hands raised, and I heard his heart hammering and his thoughts tumbling—*not the deserter, gods, it's the deserter, they'll say I helped, they'll put me in a chair—*

"We're taking one," Bram said, blade out, steady. "You're going to sit in that corner and think very hard about which side of this you want to be recorded on."

The attendant sat.

And I walked down the row of chairs, looking for her, hating every second of it, because I knew her only from Maren's descriptions—*taller than me, darker, a laugh like a door banging open, a birthmark on her left wrist shaped like a comma*—and the six figures in the chairs had stopped being people. They were pale shapes with open eyes. They were the hum given bodies.

Fourth chair. Birthmark, left wrist, shaped like a comma.

She was younger than Maren. Or she seemed younger—drained things seem young the way dried flowers do, preserved and diminished at once. Her hair had gone half white at the temples though her face said mid-twenties. Her fingers twitched in her lap, tracing sigils on air, always writing, always writing, the habit of the door carried into sleeplessness.

"Sefa," I said.

Nothing. The wheel turned. Light crawled along her filament, out of her, away.

I did what I do. It was instinct before it was decision—I knelt, took her hand, and pressed my ear to the bones of her wrist, and listened.

What was left of her was very small and very far down, like a coal buried under ash. But it was there. Around it, the harvested channels of her magic lay open and raw, and through those channels I could feel the wheel pulling, patient as tide. But underneath, in the marrow, in the last sealed room of herself: *Sefa Ashroad. I am still here. I am still here. Tell Mare. Tell Mare I am still—*

"I know," I whispered. "I know. I'm going to get you out."

I looked at the filament anchored to her arm. It entered the skin at the wrist, just above the birthmark, and the entry point was swollen and shiny with scar tissue—old scar tissue, years of it. The anchors weren't new. They'd been in her a long time.

"Bram. How do these come out?"

"You don't pull them." He'd crossed to the wheel and was studying it with the grim competence of a man reading siege plans. "There's a release valve on the chair base—crown issue, for transfers. Turn it counterclockwise and the filament retracts. Slowly. Fast retraction kills them—I've seen it." His jaw worked. "I've seen it done deliberately, when they decide a vessel's spent."

I found the valve. I turned it, slowly, slowly, watching the gold filament withdraw from her wrist like a splinter leaving flesh, inch by inch, while Sefa Ashroad sat unmoving and the coal of her flickered in my hearing.

The filament came free. The wheel stuttered—one voice fewer in its chorus—and somewhere overhead a bell began ringing, wrong-numbered, urgent.

No. Not the half-hour bell. The alarm.

"They know," Bram said. "Maren."

Above us, boots on stairs. Many boots.

I got Sefa's arm over my shoulder and lifted. She weighed almost nothing. That was the worst part—that she weighed almost nothing, that whatever the crown had fed into its machine for however many years had been drawn out of a body that used to be a person who laughed like a door banging open.

"Eighth cell," I said.

"What?"

"There's a man in the eighth cell carving help-signs on his door. We take him too."

"Tamsin, we can't—"

"We *can*." I shifted Sefa's weight. Rage had arrived in me, sudden and total, the rage of someone who has spent a life among the honored dead and just seen what the living do to their own. "The cells aren't warded against opening, Bram. Only against magic. And I—" I bared my teeth at him, and I am told it was not a kind sight. "I am very good at doors."

* * *

We should not have made it out. Later, Bram would insist we shouldn't have, running the arithmetic of guards and corridors and arriving always at the same answer: impossible.

Here is what happened. I opened the eighth cell and found a gaunt man with bleeding fingertips and eyes like a hawk's, and I said, "Can you walk?" and he said, "Who's asking?" and I said, "Someone who heard you," and something in his face broke open and he stood up. His name was Odo. He'd been in the tithe house eleven years. He could barely walk, and he walked.

We opened three more doors on the way because I could not not open them, and Bram stopped arguing after the second, and the four of them—Odo, the multiplication-tables man, the woman who had lost her mother's face, the lullaby singer—became our shield without meaning to. Because when the guards came down the stairs and saw seven prisoners flooding the corridor, they hesitated. You cannot cut down escapees in front of other escapees. Word travels. Even tithe houses fear word.

Bram fought twice, briefly, beautifully, using the flat of his blade. The lullaby singer, passing a fallen guard, paused and touched his forehead with two fingers, and the guard slept instead of waking, and she smiled for the first time in what her bones told me was six years.

Maren met us at the kitchen door with the dog straining at its rope and her eyes wild and her hands already smoking with half-formed spellwork, and she saw Sefa in my arms and every spell died in her palms at once.

"Mare." I didn't know what else to say. There was nothing else to say.

Sefa's head hung forward. Her eyes were open and empty and fixed on the middle distance, on some country inside herself where the crown had exiled her. Maren reached out with shaking hands and took her sister's face between them, and said her name, and said it again, and said it a third time in the old language, the road-language, the tongue of hedge-witches and ash roads.

Nothing.

And then—Sefa's hand moved. Found Maren's wrist. And gripped.

Not gently. Gripped the way a drowning person grips. Her fingers were ice and her grip was iron and her empty eyes stayed empty, but her hand said everything her mouth no longer could, and Maren made a sound I had never heard a human being make, grief and relief fused into one note, and folded around her sister, and the dog leaned against both of them, and the tithe house bell rang on behind us, ringing and ringing, calling guards to a corridor that was already empty.

We ran for the treeline. All of us. Seven prisoners, one deserter, one hedge-witch carrying her sister, one bone-carver from a valley that forgot the world.

Behind us, someone finally thought to set fire to the rosemary. I don't know if it was accident or mercy. The smoke followed us for a mile, sweet and bitter at once.

* * *

We made camp at midnight in a hollow of pines, too tired to post proper watches, too afraid to skip them entirely. The freed magicians slept in a heap like puppies, touching each other in their sleep, unable to bear space between their bodies. Odo sat apart, flexing his ruined fingers, staring at the fire.

Maren had not let go of Sefa's hand since the kitchen door. She sat holding it now, wrapped in Bram's cloak, and Sefa sat beside her, upright, obedient, vacant, her fingers curled in her sister's grip like a child's.

"She squeezed," Maren said to me, across the fire. Her voice was wrecked. "That means she's in there. That means she can come back. Doesn't it? People come back from this. You've heard them—the ones in the cells. Some of them still had themselves. Didn't they?"

"Some did," I said. It was true. It was also not the whole truth, and she knew it, and I watched her decide to take the true part and hold it like a coal against the wind.

"She wrote on the door," Maren said. "Eleven years, and she wrote on the door. *I am still here.*" She laughed, wet and terrible. "Stubborn little—she was always stubborn. When we were girls she'd argue with rain."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, the woman who had lost her mother's face began humming the lullaby singer's song, having caught it like a cold, and the lullaby singer hummed back, harmonizing, and the sound drifted down through the pines like something mended.

"Tamsin." Maren looked up. Firelight moved in her ash-grey eyes. "The map says twelve nights now. Thirteenth moon. And tonight I saw what the king spends his stored magic on—wards, armies, that *thing* answering his prayers. Twelve nights, and we're seven days' hard travel from the capital, and half our company can barely stand."

"I know."

"So tell me why you're smiling."

I looked at my hands. They were steady. For the first time since the courier died in my workshop, they were steady, because for the first time I understood exactly what we were fighting, and exactly what it cost, and exactly who was waiting for us at the end of it—a hollow king who begged strangers to end him.

"Because she's still here," I said. "And so are we. And the thing in the palace thinks nobody's coming."

Maren held my gaze for a moment. Then, slowly, terribly, she smiled too.

Up the slope, Odo spoke for the first time since his rescue, his voice rusted from disuse: "The capital's bells," he said. "When you get there. Don't listen to them."

"Why not?" Bram asked.

Odo fed a stick to the fire. "Because they're not bells anymore."
