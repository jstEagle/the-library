## 11. What Wears the Crown

Bram Vey had learned to sleep in thirds. A soldier's habit: one third for the body, one third for the watch, and the last third lying awake listening to everything he'd rather forget.

He was in his second third when he heard Tamsin scream.

Not a battle cry. Not pain, exactly. It was the sound of someone drowning on dry land, and it came from the lean-to where the bone-carver had bedded down at the edge of Maren's camp. Bram was moving before he'd finished deciding to move, knife already out of habit, boots silent on frost-stiff grass.

Tamsin sat up inside the shelter, hands pressed flat over their ears, eyes wide and white-rimmed in the dark. Their lips were moving. No sound came out.

"Tamsin." He crouched, kept his voice low and even, the way you talked down a spooked horse. "Tamsin. You're safe. Nothing's here."

Their eyes found him. Something in them focused, slowly, like a lens grinding into place. "It's so loud," they whispered. "The city. We're close enough now that I can hear it. Bram, there are *thousands*—"

"Then don't listen."

A laugh cracked out of them, wet and bitter. "You think it's a door I can close?"

"I think," Bram said, "you've been closing doors your whole life. You crossed a ward line that should have kept you in forever. You can do this too."

They stared at him. Then, slowly, their hands came down from their ears—not all the way, but enough. Enough to breathe.

"Stay," they said. It wasn't quite a question.

Bram looked back toward the fire, where Maren slept sitting up with her sister's hand in hers, and made the small arithmetic of trust that had been getting larger every day since the ford. "I'll stay till dawn."

He stayed longer than that.

* * *

It was three nights later, camped in the ruin of an old waystation with the capital's glow staining the horizon like a bruise, that Tamsin asked him the question he'd been dreading.

"You were Royal Guard," they said. They were working a piece of bone by firelight—a habit Bram had come to find almost peaceful, the little scraping sounds, until he remembered what the carving was *for*. "You served in the palace. You must have seen him. The king."

Bram fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch. "I saw him."

"And?"

"And what do you want me to say? That he's tall? That he has a crown?" The words came out harder than he intended. He softened them with effort. "I saw him eleven times in two years of service. Kings don't walk among their soldiers, Tamsin. They're weather. You know the king exists the way you know winter exists."

"But you saw something." Tamsin's voice was quiet, patient, the voice of someone who spent their life listening to things other people couldn't hear. "That night at the river, when I told you we were going to the capital, you went gray. You didn't argue. You just started walking faster. People who are only afraid of a city don't walk faster. People who are afraid of something *in* the city do."

Bram looked at the fire. At Maren, pretending not to listen while she pretended to mend Sefa's cloak. At Sefa herself, hollow-eyed, watching him with the terrible stillness of the drained.

He owed them. He knew that. He'd known it since the message from Commander Hale—the dead woman's apology that had cracked something open in him he'd sealed shut the day he deserted. If anyone had earned the truth about why he ran, it was the three people around this fire.

So he told them.

* * *

"It was my second year in the Guard," he said. "Winter. I'd been assigned to the night rotation in the inner palace—you start there when you've proven you can keep your mouth shut, which I could, then. Most of the rotation is boredom. Corridors, torches, the sound of the building settling. But the palace has a heart, did you know that? Every old building does. And the heart of the royal palace is the prayer chapel. Underneath the main floor. Down a stair nobody uses except the king."

"The chapel below," Maren said softly. Her needle had stopped moving.

"You've heard of it."

"I've heard *of* it. Hedge-witches trade stories the way soldiers trade rumors. Nobody who's been inside has ever talked."

"No," Bram said. "Nobody has." He turned his hands toward the fire. "Until now. Because I'm done keeping my mouth shut, and because if we're going to walk into that city, you deserve to know what's waiting under it.

"My post that winter included the corridor above the chapel stair. Midnight to fourth bell. And I want you to understand—I was a good soldier. I followed orders. I didn't ask questions. So when the king came up those stairs at odd hours, alone, no guards, no priests, I looked at the wall and let him pass. That was the job.

"But you hear things on night rotation. Not voices. Sounds. And the sounds that came up from that chapel weren't prayers.

"The first time, I thought it was the wind. A long, low sound, like breath drawn through a crack. Then the king's voice underneath it, thin and fast, the way a man talks when he's begging. I told myself: the king prays. Kings pray strangely. Kings pray alone at three in the morning in chapels without priests. Fine.

"The ninth time, I counted. Nine visits in four months. Always alone. Always after midnight. And always—" Bram paused, and the old cold climbed his arms the way it had climbed them in that corridor, "—always, when he came back up the stairs, he moved wrong."

"Wrong how?" Tamsin asked.

"Like this." Bram stood, took two steps away from the fire, and walked back—but he did it stiff-legged, precise, each footfall placed exactly where the last one had been, arms hanging a half-beat behind his stride. He let them watch for five paces before he stopped. "Like a man remembering how a body works. Like something wearing King Aldous the way I'm wearing this coat. And his shadow—" He sat back down. His mouth had gone dry. "His shadow came up the stairs a moment after he did. I watched it happen. The torchlight was steady. There was nothing to make a shadow lag. But it lagged. It came up the steps slow, like it was reluctant, like it was being *dragged*, and when it caught up to him it settled onto his heels and it was—"

He stopped.

"Say it," Maren said quietly.

"Wider than he was," Bram said. "For just a moment, before it fit itself back to his shape, his shadow was wider than a man should be. And it had edges. Fingers, maybe. Or roots."

Sefa made a sound. Small, wordless, but everyone turned. She was looking at Bram with an intensity that had nothing vacant in it, and her hand rose—trembling—and drew something in the air. A circle. Then lines through it, crossing, a lattice.

Maren went white. "Sefa. Sweetheart. Where did you see that?"

Sefa pointed down. At the ground. Then she pointed east, toward the capital, and pressed both hands flat over her own heart.

"She saw it in the tithe house," Maren translated, her voice gone thin. "In the magic they drained out of her. There was a shape in it. A binding lattice." She looked at Bram. "Keep going."

"There isn't much more, because there's where it ends for me." Bram leaned forward, elbows on knees. "The eleventh time the king visited the chapel, I broke protocol. I know why now—I think part of me already knew, even then, that whatever was down there would eventually stop needing the stairs. Would eventually come up. So instead of watching the wall, I watched the stairwell door as he descended. And I heard him pray. Clearly. Word for word.

"He said: '*I have held the door for forty years. My father held it thirty. How many generations must stand this post before You are satisfied?*'"

Silence, except the fire.

"And then something answered him." Bram's voice dropped. "I never heard it speak. What I heard was the walls. The whole chapel, the whole foundation of the palace, made a sound like a stomach after a long fast. And the king laughed. This awful, relieved, broken laugh, like a man whose captor finally acknowledged him. And he said, 'Soon. Yes. Soon.' And I left my post. I walked out of the palace, out of the barracks, out of my oath, and I didn't stop until the river.

"They called it desertion. Cowardice. Maybe it was. But I'll tell you what I knew in my bones that night, even without proof. Whatever is under that palace isn't the king's god. It's the king's *jailer*. And Aldous isn't its servant. He's its lock. Its doorpost. Generations of kings, propping shut a door with their own souls—and the thing behind the door has been whispering through the cracks the whole time, learning us, tasting us. The tithe houses. The conscriptions. The draining of magicians." He nodded at Sefa, at Maren, at all of it. "That's not policy. That's *feeding*. It's been eating its way outward for generations, one harvested witch, one emptied village at a time, and the thirteenth moon is when it gets to stop eating through a straw."

Maren had both arms around her sister now. "An old god," she said. "Of hunger. The stories call it different names. The Hollow Guest. The Patient Mouth. My grandmother swore the first Aldous bound it into the bloodline as a bargain—power for the dynasty, famine for everyone else, and a soul posted as sentry in perpetuity."

"That matches what I heard," Bram said. "Every word."

Tamsin hadn't spoken through any of it. Now they set down the bone they'd been carving, and Bram saw their face and understood, suddenly, what it cost them to be the one who listened for a living.

"The courier," Tamsin said. "The man who died in my workshop. He said the thing answers when the king prays. He said the binding completes on the thirteenth moon." They looked up, and their eyes were steady, and terrified, and resolved, all at once. "He wasn't carrying a warning to the capital. He was carrying instructions *out* of it. Someone in that palace is trying to unbind it—and they sent the map to the one valley in the world the crown can't watch. To someone who can hear what's written in bone."

"Then whoever sent it," Maren said slowly, "is still alive in there. Still fighting. And they're running out of moons."

Bram nodded toward the horizon, where the capital's glow pulsed faint against the dark sky—steady, rhythmic, almost like breathing.

Almost like hunger.

"Twelve nights," he said. "Then we stop talking about what wears the crown—and go meet it."
