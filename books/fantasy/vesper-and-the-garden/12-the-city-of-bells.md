## 12. The City of Bells

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

I thought at first it was the wind. A low moan off the plains, rising and falling, rising and falling. Then Maren stopped mid-stride on the pilgrim road, went pale as ash, and said, "That's not weather."

It wasn't.

It was bells. Hundreds of them, thousands, ringing in a city we couldn't yet see, and underneath the bronze I could hear something else—something that made my hands go cold around Bram's spare cloak where I'd been carrying it folded over my arm.

Voices.

In Vesper, the dead whispered. One or two at a time, patient as rain, murmuring through the bones I carved for them. I had spent my whole life learning to listen to that whisper, to sort one voice from another the way you sort threads by touch in the dark. It was my gift and my burden and, until a dying courier bled out on my workshop floor, the whole shape of my world.

The capital was not a whisper.

The capital was an ocean.

* * *

We crested the last hill at dusk, and there it was: Aurelmar, the City of Bells, spread across the river valley like spilled jewelry. Walls white in the fading light. Spires everywhere, each crowned with its own bell, and beyond them the palace on its crag, windows burning gold, the thirteenth moon's predecessor hanging fat and half-full above it like an open eye.

Twelve nights left. Maybe eleven, depending on how Maren counted the turns, and she'd been counting them wrong and correcting herself twice a day since the waystation.

"Gods," Bram said quietly. He hadn't said much since the tithe house. Since Sefa. He walked with his hand near his sword hilt even now, though we were still miles from any gate, and his eyes kept going to the girl slumped against Maren's side.

Sefa had not spoken since we pulled her from the draining racks. She ate when food was put in front of her. She slept when sleep came. She walked when Maren tugged her hand. But her eyes were flat as pond ice, and when anyone spoke to her directly, she looked through them as if they were made of smoke.

Maren had stopped trying to talk to her somewhere outside the tithe house. Now she just held her sister's hand and walked, and the holding was its own kind of conversation, and it broke my heart a little more each day.

"We camp here," Bram said. "Go in at dawn, with the market crowds."

"Maren," I said. "How far can you feel it? The binding-work?"

She didn't answer right away. Her thumb moved over Sefa's knuckles, back and forth, back and forth.

"It's like standing next to a furnace," she said finally. "Every step closer, the heat gets worse. By the time we're inside those walls—" She shook her head. "I don't know what happens to someone like me inside a city that loud. And you." She looked at me then, and I wished she hadn't. "Tamsin. You've heard dead villages. Dead roads. What do you think a city does to you?"

I had been asking myself the same question since the bells started. Because the bells weren't the problem. The bells were nothing. Underneath the bronze, under the moan rolling across the plains, the dead of Aurelmar were already talking to me, and they were not whispering.

They were screaming.

Not all of them. Some were just talking, the way the dead do—fragments, repetitions, the last thoughts worn smooth like river stones. *Cold. Where is my mother. The bread burned again. Cold.* But there were so many, and they layered, and they did not wait their turn the way valley dead waited their turn. They pressed. Centuries of them, packed into those walls like grain into a sack, and every bell-toll stirred them up, and every stir sent them washing over the plains toward me like a tide with my name on it.

"I'll manage," I said.

Maren's look said everything her mouth didn't.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, and we joined the queue at the River Gate among carters and fishwives and a funeral procession carrying a child's small coffin, and I made the mistake of listening to the procession.

The mother was walking behind the coffin with her hand flat on the wood, and the child inside was saying *it's dark, it's dark, why is it dark,* and I stopped walking. Just stopped, in the middle of the gate queue, while carts honked and guards shouted and Bram's grip closed hard on my shoulder.

"Tamsin." His voice, low, close to my ear. "Stay with me. Eyes on me."

"She's asking why it's dark," I said. My own voice sounded far away. "Bram, she's six years old and she's asking why it's dark, and her mother can't hear her, and I can, and I can't—"

"You can't carve a bone in the middle of a gate queue." He turned me gently, put himself between me and the procession. "Look at me. Not them. Me."

But it wasn't just the procession anymore. That was the thing I hadn't understood from the hills, couldn't have understood. Inside the walls, the city closed over my head like water. Every street had its dead. Every house. The cobbles themselves were laid over graves older than the kingdom, and beneath everything, deep down, something vast and slow turned over in its sleep—the old god's hunger radiating up through the foundations, through the palace crag, flavoring every voice with want.

*Hungry. Hungry. Hungry,* said the stones.

*Cold,* said the child in the coffin.

*Where is my mother,* said ten thousand voices at once, some of them mothers themselves, some of them children grown old and dead without ever finding anyone, all of them asking, all of them asking—

The noise went through me like a spear through water. I felt my knees go. Felt Bram catch my weight against his chest, felt him half-carry, half-drag me into the shadow of a doorway while the crowd flowed past us, indifferent, everyone in this city walking over voices every day of their lives and hearing none of them.

"Breathe," he was saying. "Tamsin. Breathe."

I couldn't. Breathing meant opening my mouth and the sound would pour in. I clamped my hands over my ears, which was useless—they weren't coming through my ears—and squeezed my eyes shut and felt myself going down and down into the roar, a stone sinking in a well of voices, and distantly I thought: *this is how it ends, drowned in a city, twelve nights too early, and the king will pray to his hungry god forever because a bone-carver from Vesper couldn't cross a square.*

Then something cold touched my wrist.

Small fingers. Chapped lips. A grip surprisingly strong.

I forced my eyes open. Sefa crouched in front of me, her flat pond-ice eyes fixed on mine, and she was watching me drown with an expression I hadn't seen on her face since the tithe house.

Recognition.

She knew this drowning. Whatever they'd done to her in that place, whatever they'd drained and broken, she had been somewhere like this—inside a noise too big to survive—and she had found a way through it, and now she was looking at me the way one swimmer looks at another floundering in the same sea.

Her fingers tightened on my wrist. Then she took my hand and pressed it flat against her own throat, against the pulse, and held it there, and looked at me.

*Feel this,* she was saying. *Only this.*

One beat. Another. Slow, steady, alive.

And here is the thing about the dead: they are many, but they are not now. They are always then. Every voice in that roaring dark belonged to a moment that had already ended—a death finished, a grief completed, a hunger long cold. But the pulse under my palm was happening *now*. It had no words. It had no past. It was just the simplest sentence a living body knows how to say, over and over: *here. Here. Here.*

I grabbed onto it like a drowning sailor grabs a rope.

Sefa kept her hand over mine and her eyes on mine, and slowly—slowly—she began to hum. No words. A single low note, matched to her heartbeat, and as she hummed she moved my hand from her throat to my own chest, pressing it flat over my heart, and nodded once, sharply, like a teacher confirming a correct answer.

*Yours. Feel yours. Only yours.*

The roar didn't stop. I want to be honest about that—it didn't stop, it never stopped, it would never stop for as long as I lived in this city. But it changed. Sefa's note gave it a floor to stand on, a rhythm to arrange itself around, and instead of ten thousand voices hitting me at once from every direction, they began to sort. To stream. Like debris caught in a current, flowing past me instead of through me.

*Here. Here. Here.*

I breathed. The first breath hurt; the second came easier.

"There you are," Bram said hoarsely. He was crouched beside us now, one hand on Sefa's shoulder, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "There you are."

Maren appeared at the edge of the doorway, wild-eyed, a knife half-drawn. When she saw us—saw her sister kneeling in the filth of a capital alleyway, humming, her hand on a stranger's heart—she went absolutely still.

"Sefa?" she whispered.

Sefa didn't look at her. Couldn't, maybe. But her humming changed key—softened, bent upward, into something that might, in a kinder world, have been called a lullaby. Their lullaby, from the shape of Maren's face. A song from before the tithe wagons, before the ash road, before any of it.

Maren slid down the wall until she was sitting in the dirt, and she cried without any sound at all, which I suspect is a skill you learn in exile, and Sefa hummed to both of us like we were the ones who needed saving.

Maybe we were.

* * *

We rented a room above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, because Bram said no one asks questions in the Tanners' Quarter and the stench covered our scents from anything tracking by nose or otherwise. Two coppers bought us a loft with a skylight, a crooked table, and a landlady who looked at Sefa's empty eyes and asked no questions at all, only pressed an extra heel of bread into Maren's hand on the stairs.

That first evening, Sefa taught me properly.

She couldn't speak, but she didn't need to. She sat me down at the crooked table, set a cup of water in front of me, and began. Tap the cup—*listen to the ring, let it finish*. Press my palms flat on the tabletop—*feel the wood, only the wood*. Hum with her, matching her note, low and steady—*find your own sound and hold it like a wall*.

She was building me a harbor, I realized. Out of nothing. Out of a cup of water and a crooked table and her own ruined voice. In the tithe house they must have drowned her the way the city was drowning me—magic harvested, sense overwhelmed, self dissolving—and she had survived by learning exactly this: how to anchor. How to find one true sensation and hold it while the flood went by.

Now she was handing me the rope that had saved her.

By the second night I could walk through a market square. By the third, a main street. The dead still streamed past me—*cold, hungry, where is my mother*—but they streamed now, ordered by the rhythm Sefa had given me, and I learned to read them the way you read a river from the bank: noting the eddies, the dangerous currents, the places where the flow ran thick with recent grief.

Because that was the other thing the city taught me. Under the ancient murmur, under the centuries of sediment, the recent dead were thick here. Thicker than the plague villages on the pilgrim road. People vanishing off the streets—beggars, foreigners, anyone the crown wouldn't miss—and ending up somewhere below the palace crag, their voices cut off mid-sentence, gone silent in a way that wasn't death's silence but something worse. A silence with edges.

"They're taking people," I told the others on the fourth night, my hands shaking around a cup of tea I'd forgotten to drink. "Down there. Under the palace. The dead near the crag just—stop. Mid-word. Like they're being *used*."

Bram's jaw tightened. "The under-chapel."

"You've heard of it?"

"I've heard things I prayed were drunk hallucinations." He looked at the skylight, at the half-moon climbing over the palace. "Eleven nights, Maren?"

"Ten," Maren said, and then, catching herself, "Ten and a half. Give or take."

Sefa, in the corner, had gone very still. She was looking at me—not through me, at me—with those pond-ice eyes, and her hand had risen halfway to her own throat.

Slowly, deliberately, she tapped her collarbone twice. Then pointed down. Toward the floor. Toward the crag.

"What is it?" Maren breathed. "Sefa. What is it, love?"

Sefa's mouth worked. Nothing came. But her eyes filled, and her finger stayed pointed at the ground, and the humming started again—that low, steady note—but this time it shook.

Whatever they did to her in the tithe house, I thought, was practice. A rehearsal. Somewhere under that crag, something older and hungrier was doing the same work on a whole city, on a whole kingdom, on a king.

And Sefa knew. However hollowed-out she was, some part of her remembered the sound of it.

She tapped her collarbone twice more, pointed down, and then—her hand trembling—she held up ten fingers.

Ten nights.

Then she folded one down.

Nine.
