## 13. An Audience Refused

The petition cost eleven silver pieces, and that was the first lie of the day.

Maren counted the coins into the clerk's soft palm at the Hall of Petitions and watched him slide them off the table without looking at them. Eleven silver for the privilege of standing in a marble corridor for six hours. The fee was supposed to be four. Everyone in line knew it. Nobody said anything, because the alternative was going home, and nobody in this line had a home worth going back to.

"Name," the clerk said.

"Maren Ostrewe."

His pen paused. Just a fraction—a hitch in the scratch of ink—and then it kept moving. But Maren had spent nine years learning to read the small betrayals of people's faces, the way a hedge-witch reads weather in the bend of grass, and she knew what she'd seen. He knew the name. He'd write it down anyway, because his job was to write things down, and somewhere upstairs there would be a man whose job was to read them.

Too late to turn back now. Sefa's hand was cold in hers, and Bram stood behind them both like a wall pretending to be furniture, and Tamsin—Tamsin was across the city with the stolen tithe-list, memorizing names. It had seemed wise last night, splitting up. This morning, with the clerk's pen still scratching, it felt like leaving half her body in another room.

"Purpose of petition."

"Warning to the Crown. Regarding the king's health." She'd rehearsed it a hundred ways. *Health* was the word that opened doors; *binding*, *entity*, *the thing that answers when he prays* were words that opened cells.

The clerk stamped the paper without reading past the second line. "Hall Three. You'll be called by midday."

They were called at dusk.

* * *

Hall Three was not a hall. It was a room the size of a generous closet, paneled in ash wood gone gray with age, containing one table, two chairs, and a woman in a gray dress who introduced herself as Undersecretary Vell and did not offer her hand.

"The petition states you have information concerning His Majesty's health," Vell said. She hadn't looked up from the paper. "You are not a physician."

"I'm not. I'm—"

"You were trained at the Collegium." Still not looking up. "Expelled. Nine years ago. The record says unlicensed practice, consorting with banned texts, and—" here she did look up, and her eyes were flat as coins "—unauthorized divination regarding the royal household."

Maren felt the floor tilt under her, very slightly, the way it did before a storm. Nine years. She'd been a girl of nineteen, asking one question too loudly about why the tithe lists never matched the census, and they'd taken her name and stitched it into a file, and the file had waited in some drawer all this time like a trap set for exactly this day.

"That was a long time ago," Maren said carefully.

"Names persist." Vell folded the petition in half, then in half again, precise as a surgeon. "Your companion. The large gentleman. He has the look of the Guard about him."

"He's my cousin."

"He has a Guardsman's stance and a deserter's boots." Vell set the folded paper down and laced her fingers on top of it. "Here is what will happen, Mistress Ostrewe. Your petition will be forwarded to the Office of Household Health, which does not exist, and reviewed by an officer who does not exist, and in six days you will receive a letter informing you that your concerns were noted. That is how petitions work. That is how everything works. You came here believing the palace is a machine that can be warned. It is not a machine. It is a body, and bodies do not listen to their own fever dreams."

"And if the fever is real?" Maren asked. "If the king is dying? If something is—"

"If." Vell smiled, and it was the worst thing Maren had seen all day, worse than the tithe house, worse than Sefa's hollow eyes, because it was patient. "You use that word as if it protects you. Let me tell you what I know, since you've paid eleven silver for honesty. I know that three weeks ago a courier named Dannel Fess left the capital carrying sealed correspondence and never arrived anywhere. I know that two days ago a hedge-witch of no particular consequence walked into my hall and used a name that has been sitting in a flagged file for nine years. And I know that Her Majesty the Queen Regent takes a personal interest in flagged files."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere beyond the door, a bell rang the hour, and Maren counted the strokes out of pure animal habit—seven—and thought: *twelve nights until the thirteenth moon, minus seven hours.*

"I'd like to withdraw my petition," Maren said.

"Too late. It's been noted." Vell rose, smoothing her gray dress. "You're free to go, of course. The palace has no authority to detain a petitioner." She reached the door and paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "The guards outside have no such limitation. They are not palace guards. Do walk quickly, Mistress Ostrewe. And do give my regards to whoever taught you to forge a Collegium seal on your travel papers. The work was good. Not good enough."

She left. The latch clicked. Bram moved before Maren finished breathing—had the door open, had Sefa's wrist in his grip, had assessed the corridor in one sweep.

"Two men at the far arch," he said quietly. "Gray cloaks, palace issue but wrong cut. They're waiting for us to pass them, not following. There's a service stair behind the paneling—I can smell the lamp oil they use down there. Can you open it?"

Maren could. It was, absurdly, the first useful thing her exile had ever bought her: nine years of picking apart other people's wards and locks because her own life had been picked apart first. She found the seam in the paneling, whispered the release that every Collegium child learned and every Collegium child was later forbidden, and the wall swung inward on a breath of cold air and tallow stink.

They went down into the dark.

* * *

The service stairs let out into a laundry court where washerwomen beat sheets against stone in the blue last light, and nobody looked at three more shadows slipping along the wall, because servants were invisible by design—the palace's oldest magic, older than any binding, the spell that made whole categories of people into furniture.

Maren kept hold of Sefa's hand and made herself think. Vell had known about Dannel Fess. Vell had known about the flagged file. Which meant either the undersecretary served the crown's ordinary paranoia—or she served the thing in the walls, and there was no longer any difference between the two. That was the lesson of the day, and it sat in Maren's chest like swallowed ice: *there is no one above us to appeal to.* The court wasn't deceived. The court was the deception. You couldn't warn a body about its own fever when the fever was doing the thinking.

"We can't go back to the safehouse," Bram said. They'd stopped in the mouth of an alley off Pilgrim Street, rain just starting, fine as sifted flour. "If Vell flagged me, she flagged the description. Aldric knows my habits. He'll have watched every place I ever slept in this city."

"Aldric?"

"My former sergeant." Bram's jaw worked. "He's thorough. It's why I liked him. It's why this is going to hurt."

"Bram." Maren stepped in front of him. Rain beaded on his cropped hair. "What are you planning?"

He didn't answer right away. Down the street, the evening bells began—not the hour bells but the smaller ones, the chapel bells of the low quarter, ringing the office called Compline, and the sound rolled over the rooftops in overlapping waves. In the valley, Tamsin had told her once, the dead whisper through bone. Here they drowned in bronze. Maren had lived in this city for twenty years and never once heard the bells properly; now, knowing what she knew, they sounded less like prayer and more like a countdown.

"When they take me," Bram said finally, "they'll want to do it quiet. A deserter dragged through the streets is a story, and stories spread. So it'll be an alley, or a doorway, or a cell below the Guard barracks with no paperwork." He flexed his hands. "I'm not letting it be quiet."

"Bram—"

"They need me alive to find you. That's my only value, and it expires the moment they have you instead. So I'm going to stand in the middle of Oath Square, in front of the Cenotaph, where every Guardsman in the district can see me, and I'm going to tell them exactly what I am." He was already unlacing his collar, pulling something from around his neck on a cord: a thin strip of vellum, rolled tight, wax-sealed, worn soft from nine years against his skin. His oath. Every soldier carried it from the day of swearing until the day of discharge or death. Deserters weren't stripped of theirs—they simply carried it forever, a debt that outlived the crime.

"You burn that," Maren said, "and there's no coming back. No pardon, no reinstatement, nothing. Ever."

"I know what it costs. I've had nine years to price it." For the first time since she'd known him, Bram smiled, and it transformed his face into something younger and more frightened and more certain than she'd expected. "Tamsin gave me a message from Commander Hale. Did I tell you that? *I should have refused sooner.* Nine years I thought I was the only coward in the Royal Guard. Turns out the bravest man I knew died wishing he'd been braver. I'm done being quiet, Maren. Quiet is how the thing in the walls eats."

* * *

Oath Square at night was all wet stone and lamplight, the Cenotaph rising black against the sky, names of dead kings chiseled up its spine. The evening crowd was thin—clerks hurrying home, a flower-seller packing up, two city watchmen warming themselves at a brazier who would remember this night for the rest of their lives.

Bram walked to the center of the square and stood where the lamplight was best, and Maren and Sefa pressed into an alcove across the way, close enough to see, far enough to run.

He held the oath up so the light caught it.

"My name is Bram Vey," he said, and his voice carried the way big voices carry, filling the square without effort. "Late of the Royal Guard, Third Company. Nine years ago I deserted my post, and I have regretted it every day since—but not tonight, and not for the reason you think." He struck flint from his pocket. Sparks jumped. "I deserted because I was ordered to stand guard outside a door and ask no questions about the sounds behind it. Tonight I'm telling you what's behind every door in this palace. The king is not sick. The king is *eaten*. There is a thing in the walls of the chapel that wears this kingdom like a coat, and it has been feeding on your sons and daughters through the tithe, and in twelve nights it finishes its meal—"

The flint caught. The oath flared, vellum curling gold at the edges, and Bram held it high, burning, and shouted over the crackle:

"—and I burn my oath because the crown it bound me to is already dead! I swore to protect the realm! *This* is me protecting the realm!"

Boots. Of course—boots, everywhere, from three directions at once, gray cloaks and city watch and, pushing through them all, a broad man with a sergeant's stride and a face like a closed fist. Aldric. He stopped ten feet from Bram and stared at the burning scrap of vellum as if it were a wound.

"Bram," Aldric said, and there was something awful in it—not hatred. Grief. "You stupid, brave bastard."

"Aldric."

"Put your wrists out."

"No."

Aldric sighed and nodded, and four men moved, and Bram did not make it easy—he took one down, spun another into the Cenotaph hard enough to ring the stone—but he was one against eight and he'd spent his strength on the speech, and Maren watched them take him at the knees, watched them pin him, watched Aldric kneel beside him and say something too low to hear. Bram laughed. Then they hauled him up between them, and his eyes found Maren's alcove across the square—one glance, half a heartbeat, deliberate as a signature—and he shook his head, almost imperceptibly. *Don't. Run.*

So they ran.

Sefa's hand in hers, rain thickening, Maren hauling them both down the pilgrim stairs and into the warren of the low quarter where the lamps were few and the alleys remembered her name. Behind them the square erupted—shouting, a whistle, the crowd waking up—but the alleys swallowed sound, and Maren had spent nine years learning these veins of the city the way Tamsin had learned bone.

It wasn't until they reached the aqueduct arch, breath ragged, that she let herself stop and feel it. Bram, taken. Their plan—warn the court, work from inside, find allies among the decent—burned on the Cenotaph steps with his oath. There was no inside. There was no above. There was only the thirteenth moon, twelve nights now, and whatever they were going to do, they would do it with stolen magic and their own hands.

Sefa tugged her sleeve. Maren looked down. Her sister was pointing, urgent, at the arch's shadowed underside—at the mortar between the ancient stones, where someone had scratched a mark years ago and years ago Maren had walked past it a hundred times without seeing.

It was a sigil. Binding-work. Royal.

And beneath it, fresh-cut, no more than a day old, in a hand Maren recognized because she'd seen it on the map's final page:

*Tamsin was here. The chapel below. Come alone if you must come.*

Maren put her fingers to the cut letters and felt, faintly, through the stone, something she had not felt since crossing the ridge: the dead, murmuring. And underneath their murmur, deeper, hungrier, patient as roots—

something answering back.
