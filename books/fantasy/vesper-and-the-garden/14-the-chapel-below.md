## 14. The Chapel Below

The map's last marking was not a place. It was a shape.

Tamsin had stared at it for three days as they moved through the capital's underbelly—through the tithe-house tunnels Sefa still knew by instinct, through cellars that smelled of tallow and older things—turning the leather case over in their hands until the sigils swam. Maren had finally taken it back with a sigh and held it to candlelight at an angle no sane person would try.

"It's a floor plan," she said. "Upside down."

"Of what?"

"A chapel." Her finger traced the lines. "But look—the walls are drawn twice. There's a chapel above, and something beneath it. A mirror." She looked up, and in the candle flame her eyes were flat and grim. "The under-chapel. I thought it was a story they told first-years to keep them out of the archive stacks. A binding site from the founding of the line. Everyone said it was torn out when the palace was built over it."

"Everyone," Bram said from the doorway, "also said the king talks to his dead mother every morning. So."

They went down on the night of the eleventh moon, when the palace above them rang with some feast or other and the guards were drunk enough to be slow. Sefa led. She could not speak—had not spoken since the tithe house—but she moved through the dark like water finding its level, one hand trailing the wall, reading stone the way Tamsin read bone. Twice she stopped, pressed her palm flat, and waited, and twice the distant tread of boots passed above them and faded.

Tamsin heard the dead the whole way down.

That was the thing about the capital no one had warned them about—not Maren, not even Bram, who had lived here half his life. The city was loud. Not with the living; the living were nothing, a murmur under everything, easy to ignore. It was the dead. They layered. Centuries of them, packed into the foundations like silt, and where Vesper's whispers had been a brook, this was a flood. Tamsin had learned, in the weeks since crossing the ridge, to hold the roar behind their teeth, to let it wash past without catching on any single voice. Sefa had taught them that—wordless lessons, hands shaping invisible currents, *let it move, don't drink it*.

But as they descended below the palace's lowest cellar, below the cisterns, below even the old guard-tunnels Bram swore he'd never known existed, the roar began to change.

It thinned. Then it stopped.

And in the silence that followed, something else began to sing.

* * *

Tamsin felt it before they heard it—a pressure behind the sternum, like the moment before a storm breaks. They stopped so abruptly that Maren walked into their back.

"What is it?"

"You can't hear that?"

"Hear what?" Maren's voice was careful. "There's nothing. That's the point. This deep, there shouldn't be anything at all."

"That's what I mean." Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "It's not the dead. The dead sound like—" They groped for it. Like people. Like grief, mostly, and sometimes love, and once in a great while forgiveness. What hummed up through the stone now sounded like none of those things. It sounded like appetite. A long, patient, bottomless wanting, sung in a register below hearing, felt in the teeth and the soft organs and the wet dark of the eye.

Bram had his hand on his knife, though there was nothing yet to cut. "Say the word and we go back."

"The word is *down*," Tamsin said, and hated how steady it came out. "We didn't crawl through a sewer to stop at the door."

Sefa was already moving.

The passage narrowed, then opened without warning into a vaulted space, and Tamsin understood at once why the map had been drawn upside down. This was the chapel above, mirrored. Same proportions. Same seven alcoves. But where the royal chapel gleamed with gilt and glass—and Bram had described it, the light, the choir loft, the king alone at prayer—this place was raw. Unfinished stone. And every surface of it, floor to vault, was carved bone.

Not decorated with bone. Carved *from* it. Femurs laid in courses like brickwork, ribs arched into ribs, skulls set into the keystones with their faces turned inward, all of them looking down at the center of the floor. Centuries of them. Thousands. Tamsin's skin prickled with the scale of it—the work of generations, done in secret, under the feet of kings.

"They never tore it out," Maren whispered. She had gone gray in the candlelight. "They built the palace on top of it. They've been feeding it ever since."

"Feeding it what?"

Maren didn't answer. She didn't have to. Tamsin could feel the answer rising through their boot-soles, through the bones of their legs, humming up the spine like a struck string.

*People.* Magicians, drained at the tithe houses. Soldiers sent to die at the borders. A kingdom's worth of small losses, funneled downward for two hundred years, keeping the hunger fat and patient and bound.

The singing grew louder as Tamsin crossed the threshold. Not louder exactly—*closer*, the way a voice is closer when it stops being ambient and starts being addressed to you. They froze halfway across the floor, between two rings of inlaid bone that formed concentric circles around an empty center, and the song bent toward them like grass toward sun.

"Oh no," Tamsin said softly. "Oh, it knows I'm listening."

"Tamsin." Maren's hand closed hard on their arm. "What do you mean, *listening*?"

"I mean it's not just singing. It's—" Tamsin closed their eyes, which did nothing, because this wasn't sight. It wasn't even quite hearing. It was the thing they'd been born doing, the thing Vesper's ward had muffled their whole life, turned inside out. In the valley, the dead spoke *through* bone, faintly, reluctantly, like voices through a wall. Here, the bone was the wall itself, miles thick, and on the other side of it something enormous had noticed the crack in the mortar.

*You hear me.*

The words arrived without language. Tamsin felt them as shapes: vast, slow, amused.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, because saying it aloud made it less true. "I hear *dead people*. You're not dead. Dead things end. You're just—"

*Hungry,* the god agreed, and the agreement itself was a kind of caress, cold and enormous. *Yes.*

Tamsin's knees buckled. Bram caught them under the arms.

"That's enough." His voice cracked across the chamber like a whip. He couldn't hear it—he couldn't possibly hear it—but he could see what it was doing. "Tamsin. Look at me. Whatever it's saying, it's lying. That's what it does. That's all it does."

*Tamsin,* Maren repeated, and then, sharper: "*Tamsin.*"

Tamsin surfaced. The chamber reassembled itself around them: candle-glow, bone-vault, Maren's white face, Sefa standing at the far ring with both hands pressed to the carved wall, her eyes shut, tears cutting clean lines through the grime on her cheeks.

"Sefa—" Maren started toward her.

"No." Sefa couldn't say it, but she could shake her head, violently, and pull her hands back, and press them against her own sternum as if holding something in. Maren stopped. Understanding moved across her face like a shadow.

"She can feel it too," Maren said. "Whatever they left in her. The harvested magic—it recognizes its—its landlord." She spat the word.

Tamsin got their feet under them and stood, shaking, and made themselves look at the center of the floor.

The innermost circle was empty. That was the wrongness of it, the thing their eye kept snagging on. Every binding Tamsin had ever seen—in stories, in the hedge-lore Maren muttered over the campfire—needed a core. An anchor. A name at the middle of the knot. This circle had a hollow where the heart should be, and the hollow was shaped, Tamsin realized with a slow, sick lurch, like a kneeling person.

Like a king.

"It's not anchored to the room," they said. Their voice came out strange, distant. "It's anchored to him. To the bloodline. The circles aren't a cage—they're a *channel*. Everything drains inward, through the rings, into whoever kneels here. And then up." They looked at the ceiling, at the palace sleeping above them, at the feast-hall noise and the drunk guards and the man in the prayer chapel talking to the walls. "Into the crown."

Maren was already moving along the outer ring, reading the carvings, her lips moving. "This is founding-era work. Pre-Aldous. Maybe pre-Aldous's grandfather." Her finger hovered over a line of sigils without touching. "The rite of unbinding would be recorded somewhere in here—they always record it, binders are vain, they want credit if anyone ever has to undo their masterpiece—"

"Maren."

"—and if we can find the release formula, we take it to the king, we get him down here, we—"

"*Maren.*" Tamsin's voice broke on the second syllable. "Look at the center. Tell me what you see."

She looked. Tamsin watched her see it: the kneeling-shaped hollow, the empty heart of the knot, the channel running upward through two hundred years of stone and dynasty.

"No," she said.

"The binding doesn't pass through the room. It passes through *him*. Through the blood. We can't unbind it from out here any more than you can untie a knot by pulling the rope tighter." Tamsin heard themselves speaking calmly, which was absurd, because their heart was slamming and the god's attention lay across their shoulders like a hand. "Someone has to stand in the circle. Inside the channel. Someone who can hear it well enough to find the seam."

Silence. Even the god's singing seemed to lean in.

"Then it's me," Maren said immediately. "I'm the only one here with actual training. Hedge-craft, yes, but I know binding theory, I know—"

"You're deaf to it." Tamsin kept their voice gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You'd be feeling your way blind in the dark, inside a god's throat. Sefa's magic is broken open—she'd burn through in a heartbeat, and it would take her with it. Bram can't hear anything at all." They swallowed. "I'm the only one who can hear the seam. You know I am."

"There has to be another way."

"There isn't. You know there isn't. You've known since the ford." Tamsin almost smiled. "You took me on because I could listen. Well. This is what listening costs."

Maren turned away sharply, and Tamsin let her turn away. Some griefs needed privacy even in company.

Bram hadn't spoken. When Tamsin glanced at him, he was staring at the innermost circle with an expression Tamsin hadn't seen on him before—not fear, not guilt, but the flat, working stillness of a soldier doing arithmetic.

"One problem," he said. "Even if we do this. Even if someone stands in the circle and cuts it loose. The channel runs through the king. You cut the god free, the backlash goes through him. Through the bloodline." He met Tamsin's eyes. "You're not just volunteering to stand in a fire. You're volunteering to make sure the king burns with it. Unless—"

"Unless he consents," Tamsin said quietly. "A binding held through a soul needs the soul's grip. If he lets go—if he *wants* to let go—the channel opens instead of snapping. The god comes loose clean. Or cleaner." They thought of the courier dying in their workshop, pressing the map into their hands with the last of his strength. *He reached Vesper deliberately,* Maren had said. The valley was the one place the crown couldn't track it. Someone, somewhere, had planned this whole road—had known it ended here, in this hollow-hearted room, with a question no one could answer except the man wearing the crown.

"We need Aldous," Tamsin said. "Alone. Lucid. Willing. And we need him in twelve days, before the thirteenth moon turns and the binding sets permanent and none of this matters anymore."

"And how," Bram asked, "exactly, does a bone-carver, a disgraced witch, and a deserter get a private audience with the most guarded man in the world?"

Above them, muffled by centuries of stone, the palace bells began to ring the midnight hour. And beneath the bells, beneath the silence, beneath everything, the god sang on—patient, vast, and suddenly, horribly, attentive—as if it had heard the question too, and found it funny.

*Come back,* it seemed to say, in shapes rather than words, in cold rather than sound. *Bring your friends. I have room for all of you.*

Tamsin walked out of the under-chapel with their spine straight and their hands shaking, and did not tell the others that part.
