## 15. Betrayal at the Bell Tower

Bram Vey had spent eleven years learning to read a city by its bells, and the capital rang wrong tonight.

He'd told himself he was scouting. That was the word he used, anyway—*scouting*, clean and soldierly, the kind of word that let you sleep. The truth was uglier: after the tithe house, after Sefa's hollow eyes and Maren's silence on the road south, Bram couldn't stand the sound of his own thoughts inside the group anymore. So when they'd made camp in the drowned quarter beneath the aqueduct, he'd volunteered for the night watch and then walked out of it, alone, into the city that had chewed him up and spat him out two years ago.

He told himself he was checking the bell tower routes. The plan—their mad, half-formed plan—depended on knowing which streets the watch patrolled before the thirteenth moon. Someone had to know. He was the one who'd served. It made sense.

It made sense right up until the moment a hand closed over his mouth from behind and a knee buckled the back of his own, and the cobbles came up fast to meet him.

He knew the grip before he knew the face. He knew it in his shoulders, in the way the arm across his throat locked—not to kill, but to hold, the restraint of a man trained to take prisoners, not corpses. There were only so many people alive who'd learned to hold Bram Vey exactly that way.

"Evening, Vey," said Sergeant Dain Corvas.

The lamplight found his face when Bram stopped struggling. Older. A new scar splitting his eyebrow. The Royal Guard black-and-silver sat on him like it had always sat there, unwrinkled, unbothered, as if desertion were a thing that happened to other men.

"Corvas." Bram's voice came out rough. "You always did like dramatic entrances."

"You always did like walking into them." Corvas whistled low, twice, and four shapes detached themselves from the shadows of the arcade—Guard, all of them, swords already drawn but held low. Professional. No shouting, no alarm. That was the part that turned Bram's blood cold. This wasn't an arrest. Arrests were loud. This was *collection*.

They hauled him up the bell tower stairs instead of down to the cells, which told him more than any interrogation could have. Nobody took a deserter upward unless someone above wanted to look at him personally.

* * *

The room at the top of the tower had once been the bell-keeper's quarters. Now it held a table, a lamp, a decanter of wine the color of garnets, and Captain Elira Voss.

Bram's stomach dropped through the floor and kept falling.

Voss had been his commander's second, back before. Back when Commander Hale still lived, back when Bram still believed the Guard was a thing you could serve with a straight spine. She'd written the report on his desertion herself—he knew because he'd stolen a copy of it during his first week running. He'd read it forty times. *Private Vey abandoned his post under circumstances suggesting cowardice.* Cowardice. As if he'd run because his legs failed him, and not because he'd been ordered to drag a twelve-year-old tithe-boy into the drain-chambers under the palace and had finally, finally said no.

"Bram." She poured two cups without asking. "Sit."

"I'll stand."

"You always were sentimental about furniture." She pushed a cup toward him anyway. "Drink or don't. I'm not going to poison you. If the crown wanted you dead, you'd have died at the ford outside Harrow's Mill, eight days ago. We've known where you were since the ash road."

The words landed like a slap. Eight days. They'd crossed the ash road nine days ago, Tamsin stopping every few miles to carve bones for strangers, Maren grumbling about the delay, Sefa riding silent in the cart. Nine days of thinking they were ghosts.

"You've been herding us," Bram said.

"We've been *observing* you. Herding implies effort." Voss sat, unhurried, and folded her hands. "Do you know what I admire about you, Vey? You're the only man I ever served with who actually meant it. All that talk about honor. Everyone else recited the oath like a prayer they didn't believe in. You believed it. Which is why this is going to be easy."

"Easy."

"Because you're not a traitor. You're a good soldier having a very bad year." She slid something across the table. A paper, sealed with the crown's sigil—and beneath the sigil, a second seal Bram had never seen before: a circle broken by a vertical line, pressed in wax so dark it looked like dried blood. "Full reinstatement. Sergeant's rank, backdated. Clean record. Pardon for Hale's business, if you want it. And a posting somewhere quiet, far from this city, where nobody will ever ask you about the last two years again."

Bram didn't touch it. "In exchange for?"

"Tamsin Halloran. The hedge-witch Maren. And whatever's left of the girl Sefa." Voss said the names lightly, like items on a supply manifest. "Deliver them to the under-croft of the chapel district before the thirteenth moon turns, walk away, and none of this—" she gestured at the warrant, at him, at the whole rotten architecture of his life since the drain-chambers, "—ever happened."

"And if I don't?"

"Then we take them anyway, and you hang beside them. Honestly, Vey, the arithmetic isn't complicated."

It wasn't. That was what frightened him. He looked at the pardon, at the clean record, at the words *full reinstatement* written in a clerk's careful hand, and felt the old hunger rise in him like tide—the hunger to be a soldier again, to belong to something, to stop being a man defined by the single worst and best decision of his life.

"What happens to them?" he asked. "If I do this. What happens to them, exactly?"

And Voss smiled, and the smile was the answer, and they both knew it.

"That's not your concern," she said gently. "That's the point of the pardon."

* * *

They gave him a cell for the night—which was itself a message. Not chains, not irons. A room with a lock on the *outside* and a window he could have opened, if opening it wouldn't have dropped him forty feet onto stone. They wanted him to feel that the door was a formality. That he was already theirs; the paperwork was just catching up.

He sat on the cot and did what he'd done his whole career: he thought like a soldier.

First: they'd known his location for eight days, which meant they'd likely tracked the others too, which meant the camp under the aqueduct might already be watched—or deliberately left alone until he led them somewhere better. Bait stayed fresh only if the trap wasn't sprung early.

Second: Voss had talked too much. Not carelessly—she never did anything carelessly—but she'd named the under-croft, named the deadline, named the terms. Interrogators didn't hand over operational details to a target unless the details were meant to travel. Either they expected him to accept and needed him informed, or they expected him to refuse and wanted the information loose in the hopes he'd run straight to his friends and lead them into the open.

Third, and worst: both could be true at once.

He lay back and stared at the ceiling and thought about Commander Hale. Dead two years now, officially killed by rebels on the north road. Bram had carried the guilt of it like a stone—Hale had died three weeks after Bram fled, and some rotten corner of Bram's heart had always whispered that if he'd stayed, if he'd kept his mouth shut and his spine bent, the old man would still be breathing.

Then, on the pilgrim road, a bone-carver with strange eyes had taken Bram's wrist and gone white-faced, and spoken in a voice that wasn't their own—a dry, tired voice that said: *Tell the boy it wasn't his fault. Tell him I saw the order coming and chose my hill. Tell him the boy was the best thing I grew.*

Bram hadn't slept properly since.

He got up. He tested the window. Forty feet, stone flags, certain death. He tested the door: locked, as promised, but the lock was old, and the tower was old, and whoever had refitted this room had cared about comfort more than masonry. The jamb was rotted where rain had gotten in, season after season, behind the new hinges.

Bram Vey had deserted the Royal Guard once by refusing an order. It turned out that doing it a second time was much easier. You got better at things with practice.

* * *

He went over the wall in the dead hours, down the rotted jamb and along the ledge and through the bell-keeper's old stair, past a sleeping guard whose wine Bram had liberally supplemented from Voss's own decanter. His hands shook the entire time. Not from fear—from clarity. It was the shaking of a man who has finally stopped pretending he has anything left to lose.

The city was quieter than he'd ever known it. Even the bells had gone still, as if the whole capital were holding its breath for the thirteenth moon. In the silence he could hear the palace, miles off, humming. Not bells. Not machinery. Something lower, hungrier, a sound he felt in his teeth—the thing in the walls, the thing Aldous prayed to, awake and feeding and close to full.

He ran.

He ran the drainage ways and the tanner's lanes, the routes he'd memorized as a guardsman and never forgotten, doubling back twice, changing his silhouette with a stolen cloak. By the grey hour before dawn he reached the aqueduct, and his heart was hammering for a reason that had nothing to do with the run.

The camp was there. Unburned. Untouched.

Tamsin sat up first—they slept light, always, the way people sleep when they've spent their lives listening for voices no one else can hear. Their eyes found him in the dark and went wide.

"Bram? You're supposed to be on—"

"They have me," he said. "They've had me for eight days. Voss. The Guard. They know everything—the route, the names, the moon. They offered me a pardon to sell you." He was talking too fast, breathless, and he forced himself to slow down, to say the rest plainly, because it mattered. "I need you to listen. The under-croft of the chapel district—that's where they want you delivered, before the thirteenth moon. Which means it's either where they're weakest or where they want you most, and I don't know which. And Tamsin—" he caught their shoulder, made them look at him, "—they let me go. Or I think they did. I think the door was meant to open. I think everything I just did, I did exactly the way they planned it."

Tamsin stared at him. Behind them, Maren was already awake, already moving, waking Sefa with a hand over her mouth, and Bram saw the witch's eyes flick from him to the darkness beyond the aqueduct and understand it all in a heartbeat.

"So they're following you," Maren said flatly. "Right now. We're burned."

"Or they're waiting to see if you run to the under-croft anyway." Bram's mouth tasted like ash. "I'm sorry. I thought—I thought I could get ahead of it. I thought warning you was worth—"

"It was worth it," Tamsin said quietly.

And then Bram heard it: boots. Not many. Six, maybe eight, moving with the disciplined spacing he knew in his bones, closing on the camp from two sides. They weren't even hurrying. They had all the time in the world, because the world belonged to them.

"There," he said. "East arch. Go. All of you, now—"

"You're coming," Tamsin said.

"No." He stepped away from them, toward the west side, toward the noise, pulling his hood up. "They're here for me. Let them find me. It buys you the east arch and thirty seconds, and thirty seconds is a life." He managed something that was almost a grin. "Besides. I've been captured once tonight already. I'm getting good at it."

"Bram—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, the thing he'd carried two years and finally set down: "Tell me later how it ends. I want to hear how it ends."

Tamsin's hand closed on his sleeve—one heartbeat, two—and then they were gone, into the east arch with Maren and Sefa, swallowed by the pre-dawn dark, and Bram turned to face the lanterns bobbing up the west path, and raised his empty hands, and smiled at Captain Elira Voss like a man who had just won something she would never find on any manifest.

"Well," he said, as the net came down over him. "Took you long enough."
