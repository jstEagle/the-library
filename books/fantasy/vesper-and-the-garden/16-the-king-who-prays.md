## 16. The King Who Prays

The smuggler's route into the palace went through the laundry.

That was Sefa's idea. She couldn't speak, but she could write now—slowly, in a hand that shook like a leaf in rain—and when Maren laid out the palace plans stolen from a tithe-house clerk, Sefa had tapped one finger on the service corridors beneath the east wing and then drawn, with terrible clarity, a picture of a cart full of sheets.

"The tithe houses feed the palace," Maren translated, her voice flat with something that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite grief. "Of course they do. The crown drains its magicians and launders its linens through the same doors."

So we went in as laundry.

Bram wore a guard's uniform he'd taken off a man two sizes too big for him, his beard shaved to nothing, looking younger and more miserable than I'd ever seen him. Maren went as a matron of the washing wards, gray-streaked hair pinned under a cap, carrying herself with the particular authority of a woman who has been ignored by important people her whole life and has decided to weaponize it. Sefa pushed the cart. I walked beside her with my head down, my hands raw from lye soap we'd rubbed on them that morning so they'd smell right, and the dead of the capital screamed overhead the whole way, and I held it, because Sefa had taught me how.

*Shape it*, she'd written. *Don't fight it. A river isn't a flood unless you stand in its way.*

So I stood sideways inside my own skull and let the voices pour past me, thousands of them, the city's dead stacked in its stones like sediment, and I kept my eyes on the back of Sefa's neck and counted her breaths and did not listen.

The laundry corridors smelled of steam and tallow and, underneath it, faintly, of the tithe houses—that sweet-rot smell of magic being pulled out of living people like marrow from bone. I knew it now. I would know it in my sleep for the rest of my life.

We turned where Sefa's map said to turn. We passed two guards who looked at Bram's uniform and looked away, because that was what guards did. And at the third stairwell down—the one that wasn't on any plan but was on the courier's map, marked in that same cramped royal hand—I stopped.

"This is me," I whispered.

Maren's hand closed hard around my wrist. "Tamsin—"

"You knew this was the plan."

"I knew you'd say that." Her eyes were wet and furious. "Twelve hours until the moon turns. If you're not at the bell tower by moonrise—"

"Then Bram comes after me, and if Bram can't, then you do, and if neither of you can—" I took a breath. "Then Vesper burns, and every valley like it, and everything the courier died for was for nothing. I'll be there, Maren. On my mother's bones, I'll be there."

She stared at me. Somewhere above us, a bell rang the hour, and the sound came down the stairwell like a stone dropped into a well.

"Your mother's bones," she repeated softly. "You people swear by your dead. Do you know how strange that is? Everywhere else, the dead are a debt. In your valley, they're a promise." She let go of my wrist. "Go. And Tamsin—if he's already gone, if there's nothing left of him but the thing wearing him—*run*. Don't be brave. Bravery is just a slower way to die."

Sefa caught my hand as I turned. She didn't write anything. She pressed her palm flat against mine, held it there a moment, and then let go, and somehow that said more than all her careful letters.

I went down alone.

* * *

The stairs were older than everything above them. I could feel it in the way the stone gave under my feet—not worn smooth by use but worn smooth by *time*, centuries of it, each step hollowed like a riverbed. The walls changed too. Down here they weren't dressed blocks but living rock, and carved into the living rock, over and over, were marks I recognized.

Binding-work. The same sigils Maren had traced on the courier's map, the same ones Sefa had flinched away from in the tithe house. But these were old. Ancient. The knife-strokes had weathered soft as sea glass.

And they were singing.

Not the way the dead sang—a murmur, a memory, a name held against forgetting. This was hunger given a melody. It came up through my boots and settled in my teeth, and my bone-hearing, which had spent three days learning to shape the city's roar into something bearable, bent toward it helplessly, the way a compass needle bends north.

*Empty*, the song said. *Empty empty empty. So long empty. Fill me fill me fill me.*

I pressed my hand against the wall and felt the ward-lines shiver under my palm like the flank of a sleeping animal. It knew I was there. Of course it did. It had probably known since I crossed the city wall.

I kept walking anyway.

The chapel at the bottom of the stairs was small. That was the first shock of it. I had imagined vaulted ceilings, a thousand candles, an altar big enough to sacrifice a kingdom on. Instead: a room no bigger than my workshop in Vesper, low-ceilinged, lit by three candles burning in dishes of black glass. No altar. Just a chair—a plain wooden chair, the kind a farmer might sit in after supper—and a kneeling rail worn pale by knees.

And a king.

He was smaller than his coins. That was my second thought, absurd and irreverent, and I clung to it because the alternative was to be afraid. On the currency stamped in the capital, King Aldous the Ninth had a jaw like a cliff and eyes like a hawk's. The man in the chair had a jaw like a man who had forgotten to eat, and eyes that were open and fixed on nothing, and hands folded in his lap with the terrible stillness of a thing that has been told not to move.

His shadow moved wrong.

Bram had said it and I hadn't understood, and now I did. The candlelight came from three directions, and the shadow should have wavered, tripled, softened. Instead it lay behind him in a single clean line, sharp as a cut, and while the king sat motionless, the shadow's shoulders rose and fell. Breathing. Out of time with the man it belonged to.

"Aldous," I said.

Nothing. The shadow breathed. The candles burned straight up, unnaturally straight, as if the air itself was afraid to move.

I took another step, and my boot scuffed the stone, and the sound of it—the small human sound of it—did something. The king's head turned. Not smoothly. In jerks, like a puppet finding its strings again, like a joint remembering it was a joint. His eyes found me and focused, and I watched focus happen, watched awareness come back into that face the way water comes back into a dried well—slowly, grudgingly, filling cracks.

"You're not mine," he said. His voice was rust and disuse. "You're not one of mine."

"No," I said. "My name is Tamsin Halloran. I'm from Vesper."

Something moved across his face. Not recognition—Vesper meant nothing to him, or less than nothing—but longing. The way a starving man looks at a window when someone else's bread is baking.

"Vesper," he breathed. "Where the magic doesn't reach. Where it's quiet." His hands unclenched, refolded. "I used to pray for a place like that. Before I understood what prayer cost."

I should have run then. Every instinct Maren had ever voiced, every warning Bram had ground out between clenched teeth, said: this is the trap, this is the mouth of the thing, walk away. But I had spent my life listening to the dead, and I knew the difference between a voice that lied and a voice that grieved, and this voice grieved. Whatever wore this man, whatever fed on him from the inside, the man himself was still in there. Starving in his own body. Praying in a room built like a cage.

"How long?" I asked. "How long have you been—"

"Lucid?" He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, a smile worn thin as paper over ruin. "Minutes. Sometimes an hour, if it's fed well. It feeds at the tithe houses, you know. Did you know? It drinks what they drain out of the children, and when it's drunk, it sleeps, and in its sleep I get—" he gestured at himself, at the chair, at the smallness of everything— "this. The scraps. The leavings."

The thirteenth moon. Twelve hours. "When the binding completes," I said carefully, "you won't get even this. Will you."

"No." He said it simply, without drama, the way a man says *it's raining*. "It won't need me anymore. It's spent sixty years learning to wear me. After the thirteenth turn it will wear itself." His eyes came up to mine, and they were clear, horribly clear, the eyes of a lucid man in a burning house. "You've seen the binding-work. You know what it is. My great-great-grandmother's grandfather made a bargain to win a war, and every heir since has paid the interest, and I am the last payment due."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you came down those stairs knowing what waits down here." He leaned forward, and the shadow behind him leaned too, a half-second late, and I saw him notice it and flinch. "No one comes down those stairs knowing. The court pretends this room doesn't exist. The guards pretend the corridor doesn't exist. You came anyway. Which means you have a plan, and the plan involves the binding, and I am the only person alive who can tell you the last piece of it."

My heart was going like a drum. "The rite needs consent," I said. "Maren found it in the old work. The original binding was sworn, not forced. To unmake it—"

"It must be unsworn." He nodded, slow. "By the blood that swore it. By the voice that holds the office. Yes." He rose from the chair, and it took him three tries, his legs shaking like a newborn foal's, and the shadow rose with him, lagging, straining, like a dog on a leash it had grown too strong for. "Do you know why it keeps me lucid at all? Why it leaves me these scraps of myself?"

"To steer," I said. "A king who signs decrees. An audience refused. A queen regent who looks the other way. It needs a functioning Aldous."

"It needs a *willing* one." His laugh was dry as ash. "That's the cruelty of it. The binding runs on consent, so it must keep me consenting. Every morning it shows me what happens if I refuse. Cities. My sister's grandchildren. It ate my brother that way, forty years ago—showed him a plague in a dream until he opened the door himself and let it take more of him than any king before." His voice cracked, and steadied. "I have signed things, Tamsin Halloran. I have prayed things. I have knelt at this rail and asked it for mercy and heard it laugh through my own mouth. And in twelve hours none of it will matter anymore, because it will not need my consent, and it will not need my voice, and it will not need—" he spread his arms, this hollowed man in a hollow room— "*me*."

"So give it," I said. "Give your consent. Now. To us."

The shadow stopped breathing.

I felt it happen—the way you feel a room go quiet when someone dangerous walks in. The candle flames leaned, all three together, toward the king, though there was no draft. And Aldous stood very still in the middle of his own body, a man holding a door shut from the inside, and said, in a whisper:

"It's listening. It always listens. Say nothing of the rite aloud—it cannot read minds, only mouths. Come here. Kneel at the rail as a penitent would. Quickly."

I crossed the room and knelt, and he knelt beside me, close enough that I could see the tremor in his hands, the pulse jumping in his throat. Up close he smelled of candle smoke and cold stone and, faintly, underneath, of the tithe houses. Sixty years of being drunk from.

"Listen," he murmured, lips barely moving. "The rite requires three voices. The hearer—you. The maker—the one who cuts. And the bound. Me. The words are in the old work your hedge-witch found; trust them, they're true, my family wrote them in their own blood across four generations. But words without will are nothing. It will offer you anything. Everything. It offered my brother our parents' ghosts. It will offer you your valley's dead, every voice you've ever carried, every name you've carved. Refuse it. However real it sounds. *Refuse it.*"

"I know," I whispered. "I've been warned."

"Warned isn't ready." His hand found mine on the rail—cold fingers, a king's signet ring loose on flesh gone thin. "Nothing is ready. I have been dying for sixty years and I am not ready. But I am *willing*, and willing is the coin this thing trades in, and tonight I spend all of it." He drew a breath, and I felt the room change, felt the walls lean in, felt the hunger-song in the stone rise to a frequency that set my teeth aching, because it knew, it *knew* what was about to be said—

"In the sight of the old dark and the new light," King Aldous said, and his voice rang off the living rock like a bell struck once, "I, Aldous the Ninth, of the blood that bound, do consent to the unbinding. Freely spoken. Fully meant. Let my word be the key."

The candles went out.

All three, at once, and in the dark the shadow screamed—I heard it, felt it, a sound like frost cracking across a lake, like a starved animal denied at the bowl—and the hunger-song in the walls rose to a shriek and then, slowly, sullenly, sank back down to its long muttering, cheated, watchful, waiting for the thirteenth moon to make consent irrelevant.

Light returned. Not candlelight—moonlight, impossibly, falling through a shaft in the ceiling I hadn't seen, silver-pale, and in it the king looked twenty years lighter and twenty years deader, a man who had finally put down a weight and discovered the weight had been holding him upright.

"It's done," he said. He was smiling. It was the first honest smile I'd seen on him, and it broke my heart. "Sixty years, and it's done. Whatever happens at the turning—whatever it does to stop you—it cannot unsay what I said. Consent, freely given, is the one coin it cannot counterfeit." He swayed. I caught his arm. "Take me to the chair. It will wake soon, and it must find me kneeling, empty, obedient. It must suspect nothing until the circle closes."

I got him seated. Folded his hands. Stepped back into the dark of the stairwell, where the binding-marks sang their hungry lullaby.

"Tamsin Halloran." His voice followed me, barely a breath. "In the silence afterward—when it's gone and your hearing goes with it, because it will, the rite takes its price from the hearer first—remember that some of us chose the cage so others wouldn't have to. And remember that you—" he paused, and the smile was still in his voice— "you came down the stairs. No one comes down the stairs."

I climbed toward the moonlight and the noise of the city and the twelve hours left in the world, and behind me the shadow began, softly, to breathe again.

At the top of the stairs, Bram was waiting in the dark with his sword drawn and blood on his sleeve, and the look on his face told me before he said a word.

"They know," he said. "Tamsin—they've known since the tithe house. And the bell tower's already surrounded."
