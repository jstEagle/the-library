## 17. Twelfth Night

The safehouse was a tanner's cellar two streets from the tithe house, and it smelled like it—brine and lime and old blood soaked into stone. Maren had chosen it for the smell, actually. Nobody wanted to linger near a tanner's cellar. Nobody asked questions.

She stood at the worktable with the map unrolled to its final panel, weighting the corners with candle stubs, a knife, and Bram's belt buckle, because she'd run out of dignified objects an hour ago.

Twelve nights had become one.

Tomorrow was the thirteenth turn of the moon, and the map's last sigils—the ones Sefa had finally been able to read once her magic started coming back in stuttering, unreliable floods—described the rite in language older than the capital itself. Maren had copied it out three times, checking each stroke against the original, because there would be no second attempt.

*The binding holds while the circle holds. The circle holds while a voice within it holds.*

A voice within it. Someone standing inside the ring while they cut a starving god loose from a king's soul.

Maren read the line again and felt the same cold slide down her spine that she'd felt the first time, and the tenth time, and every time between.

Behind her, the cellar was filling up with people who were about to volunteer for that.

* * *

They came down the stairs in ones and twos through the evening: the six magicians they'd pulled from the tithe house four nights ago, blinking and swaying like plants brought out of a dark room. Two of them still couldn't walk without help. One—a boy called Pell, maybe sixteen, with burn scars where the draining-circles touched his wrists—hadn't spoken since the rescue, but he'd come anyway, carried on the back of a woman named Odile who had earth-magic enough left in her to keep his heart steady when it faltered.

And Sefa.

Sefa came down last, on her own feet, which three weeks ago Maren would have wept to see. Her sister moved slowly, carefully, like someone relearning a body that had been borrowed by strangers. The crown's harvesters had drained her so deep that her magic came back wrong—flickering, surging, guttering out mid-spell. She could light a candle or crack a wall, and sometimes both when she meant neither.

But she could speak now. A few words at a time. Her first full sentence, five days ago, had been *I knew you'd come*, and Maren had gone into the privy afterward and cried until Bram knocked and asked if she needed anything and she'd said *no* in a voice that fooled no one.

Now Sefa crossed the cellar and stood beside her at the table, looking down at the map, and tapped the line Maren had been staring at.

*A voice within it,* her sister's finger said, tracing the old script. Then Sefa looked up at her, and her eyes were clear and steady and absolutely certain, and Maren understood what she was about to say before she said it.

"No," Maren said.

"You haven't heard me yet."

"I don't need to. No."

"Maren." Sefa's voice was still rough, still rationing itself, but there was iron under it. "My magic is broken. It comes and goes. I'm the least reliable person in this room. But I'm also the only one here who knows what it feels like inside those circles, because I've *been* inside them. I know how to hold still when something is eating you. That's not courage. That's practice."

"I'm not trading you back to them," Maren said. "I got you out. I am not putting you back in a circle."

"It isn't their circle."

"It's a circle."

Around them, the room had gone quiet. The freed magicians had stopped settling in. Bram stood by the door with his arms crossed, watching, and Tamsin sat on a crate in the corner with a piece of bone in their hands—they were always holding bone now, working it absently, a habit Maren suspected was the only thing keeping them sane in a city that roared with dead voices—and Tamsin was watching too, with that flat, patient attention that made Maren feel like a specimen under glass.

"Let's hear the plan," Bram said, into the silence. "All of it. Before anyone starts volunteering for anything."

Maren exhaled. "Fine. All of it."

* * *

She laid it out the way she'd rehearsed it, pointing at the map's final panel, at the diagram beneath the chapel floor that Sefa had rendered in careful ink.

"The under-chapel sits directly beneath the king's prayer chapel. The original binding was cut there four hundred years ago—seven magicians, according to the marginalia, and a king who gave his consent. That's the part everyone forgets." She tapped the oldest lines of script. "The binding isn't a cage built around the god. It's a knot tied between the god and the royal bloodline. You can't cut the god loose without cutting the king loose too, and you can't do either without someone inside the knot holding it open while the blade goes through."

"Hold it open how?" asked Odile.

"With your voice. With your magic. The circle has to stay *singing*—that's the word the text uses—while the severance is made. If the song stops, even for a breath, the knot closes on whatever's nearest. Which will be the person standing in it."

"And if the god gets free before the cut finishes?"

"Then it doesn't dissipate," Maren said. "It takes the nearest vessel. Which is the person standing in it."

Cheerful silence.

"So," Bram said. "We need someone who can sing a four-hundred-year-old binding-song without stopping, while a hungry god tries to bargain with them, in a hole under a palace full of soldiers. Anyone want to check the qualifications list?"

"We have the song," Maren said. "Sefa recovered most of it from the tithe house archives before we burned them. And Tamsin—" she hesitated, then made herself say it plainly, because they deserved plainness, "Tamsin can hear the god. Has been hearing it for days, they tell me. Under everything else. Like a bass note under the city's dead. That means Tamsin can tell us, moment to moment, whether the severance is working. Whether the knot is loosening. Whether we're cutting a rope or strangling ourselves."

Everyone looked at the corner.

Tamsin looked up from the bone in their hands. They didn't seem surprised. Maren wondered, not for the first time, how much of what happened around them they simply heard coming.

"I'll stand in the circle," Tamsin said.

"No," said four voices at once—Bram, Maren, Sefa, and Odile—and then, absurdly, weakly, from the pallet against the wall, Pell said it too, his first words in days, cracked and barely audible, and the whole room turned to look at him and he flinched but didn't take it back.

Tamsin almost smiled. "Thank you all. It's decided, though."

"It is *not*—" Maren began.

"It is." Tamsin set the bone down. It was a memorial piece, Maren saw—half-finished, a name taking shape in the grain. They'd been carving names for strangers all the way across the country, unable to stop, and Maren had watched the cost of it hollow them out night by night. "Listen to me. I need to say this once and then never again, because it's true and true things shouldn't be repeated like arguments."

The cellar went very quiet.

"I grew up in a valley where nothing changes. Where the dead whisper and the living keep their heads down and the ward keeps the world out, and I thought that was safety. It wasn't. It was a shelf. I was kept, like a jar." Their voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "Everything I've done since the courier died in my workshop has been me choosing. Choosing to leave, choosing to listen, choosing to carve names for people I never met. This is the last choice in the sequence. It's mine. And it's not a sacrifice, whatever you're all preparing to call it—it's arithmetic. I'm the only one who can hear whether this works. I'm the only one whose death won't break anyone's magic, or anyone's oath, or anyone's family."

"That's not—" Maren started.

"It's exactly arithmetic," Tamsin said, gently, terribly. "You have a sister who just learned to talk again. Bram has a regency to build. Odile has six people to shepherd out of this city when it's over. I have bones and a valley that forgot me the day I left. The math is not close."

The silence after that had weight. Somewhere above them, the tanner's dogs barked twice and went quiet.

It was Sefa who broke it. She crossed the room, took Tamsin's face in both hands—she'd stopped being gentle with people she loved, Maren had noticed; the tithe house had burned the patience out of her—and pressed her forehead to theirs.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said. "You have us."

Tamsin closed their eyes. For a moment they were just a tired person leaning into another tired person, and Maren had to look away at the ceiling because her throat had gone tight.

Then Tamsin stepped back, and the moment folded itself away, and they said, "Teach me the song."

* * *

They worked until the candles drowned in their own wax.

The binding-song was not a melody, exactly. It was closer to a shape you made with your voice and your magic together—a sustained chord of intent, four phrases cycling, each one keyed to one of the seven original anchors carved into the under-chapel's stones. Sefa sang it first, demonstrating, her broken magic flickering through the notes so that the candle flames bent toward her and away like grass in wind. Then she taught it phrase by phrase, and the freed magicians took it up, weaving their surviving scraps of power into the sound, and the cellar filled with a low, layered hum that made Maren's teeth ache and the lime-stained walls seem briefly translucent, as if the stone remembered being alive.

Tamsin did not sing. Tamsin listened, eyes shut, hands flat on the table, and after the third repetition they opened their eyes and said, "The third phrase is wrong. Not wrong—you have it right—but the god will fight you hardest there. It's the phrase that names hunger as *lack*. It hates being called a lack. It thinks it's a mouth."

"How do you know that?" Bram asked.

"Because it told me. Last night. While you were all sleeping." Tamsin said it the way other people reported the weather. "It's been talking for days. It offers things. It offered me my valley. It offered me the courier—I could hear him again, it said, properly, forever, if I walked away tomorrow and let the thirteenth moon turn."

"And?" Maren asked, though she already knew.

"It's a liar with excellent taste in gifts." Tamsin picked up the half-carved bone again. "Change the third phrase. Make it a question instead of a statement. Hunger hates questions. Questions make it think."

Sefa looked at Tamsin for a long moment, then nodded and began reworking the phrase, and Maren watched the two of them bend over the script together—her hollowed, flickering sister and this bone-hearing stranger from a valley Maren had never seen—and felt something in her chest that was too large and too painful to be called hope, so she didn't call it anything. She just let it sit there, heavy and warm, like a stone taken from a fire.

* * *

Past midnight, the others drifted onto pallets and bedrolls. Odile slept sitting up with Pell's head in her lap. The hum of the practiced song seemed to linger in the walls even after the voices stopped.

Maren stayed at the table, copying the final rite one last time, because her hands needed work or they'd start shaking.

Bram appeared beside her with two cups of something that claimed to be tea. He set one down without asking. They'd known each other long enough now for that.

"He'll do it," Bram said quietly. "In the morning, when we move. He'll just—walk into the circle like he's going to the market."

"I know."

"You could drug him. Odile could. She'd do it."

Maren looked up at him. "Would you? If it were your commander. If it were the man whose ghost apologized to you."

Bram was silent for a while. Above them, faintly, the city bells marked the hour—one, then two, then a third that belonged to no official hour, the way capital bells never quite agreed with each other.

"No," he admitted. "I'd hate him for it, though. Every day after. If there was an after."

"There'll be an after," Maren said. "That's the plan. The whole plan is *after*."

She gathered the copies, rolled the map, snuffed the candles one by one. In the last light she glanced at the corner where Tamsin lay on their bedroll, apparently asleep, the finished memorial bone tucked under their cheek like a child with a doll. The name carved into it, she saw, was not the courier's.

It was Tamsin's own.

Her stomach dropped. She crossed the room quietly, knelt, and eased the bone free. Tamsin's eyes opened immediately—not asleep at all, or not anymore.

"That's morbid," she whispered.

"It's honest." Their voice was soft, unhurt. "Whatever happens tomorrow, I want a marker. I've carved them for everyone else. It seemed rude to skip myself."

"If you live, I'm carving over it. I'll put something embarrassing. *Here lies Tamsin Halloran, terrible at arithmetic.*"

Something moved through Tamsin's face—quick, real, gone. The closest thing to laughter Maren had gotten out of them in days. "Deal," they whispered.

She tucked the blanket up around their shoulders like they were her sister, like they were hers, and went back to her own bedroll, and lay in the dark listening to the breathing of the strange small family the road had assembled, and hated the thirteenth moon with her whole body.

Outside, high over the tanner's yard, the moon rose fat and nearly full. One more night. One more turn, and the knot either came undone or closed forever around the thing wearing the crown—and the throat of whoever stood inside it.

Maren watched the moonlight crawl across the cellar floor until it reached Tamsin's bedroll, and she did not sleep at all.
