## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon rose fat and white over the City of Bells, and every bell in the city was silent.

Tamsin felt that silence like a held breath. For twelve nights the bells had rung at odd hours, cracked and arrhythmic, as if something beneath the streets were learning to play them. Tonight they hung still, and the dead pressed close—thousands of them, packed into the stone of the capital like seeds in frozen ground, all of them whispering the same word in a hundred different mouths: *soon. soon. soon.*

"Last chance to change your mind," Maren said. She stood at the edge of the under-chapel's threshold, chalk in one hand, her sister's hand in the other. Sefa had spoken three words since the tithe house—*hold, line, open*—and each one had cost her something visible, a flicker draining out of her eyes. But she'd insisted on coming. She'd drawn the binding circle herself, her chalk lines steady even when her body wasn't.

"No," Tamsin said. "There isn't."

Bram said nothing. He'd said nothing for an hour, standing guard at the stair with his sword drawn and his jaw set, watching the door they'd barricaded behind them. His former comrade's warning was already hours old: *the crown knows everything. They're coming.* The crown, tonight, was a thing wearing a king's face, and it would come too—but not until the moon reached its height. Tamsin could feel its attention like a hand pressed flat against the back of their neck.

They descended.

The under-chapel was older than the palace above it, older than the city, older than the kingdom whose name it no longer remembered. The walls were bone—not carved decoration but structural, femurs and ribs and skulls mortared into arches, centuries of the dead stacked into architecture. And they were singing. Not with voices. With hunger. A low, continuous note that Tamsin felt in their teeth, in the marrow of their own bones, a sound like an empty stomach the size of a cathedral.

The original binding circle was cut into the floor at the center, a ring of glyphs worn shallow by a thousand years of prayer. King Aldous knelt inside it, waiting. He looked smaller than he had in the smuggled audience—a hollowed man in ceremonial white, his crown set on a cushion beside him because he could no longer bear its weight. When he saw Tamsin, he smiled, and it was the smile of someone being released from a long illness.

"You came," he said. "I prayed you would."

"Don't pray," Maren said sharply. "Not here. Not tonight."

He bowed his head, chastened, and Tamsin's chest ached looking at him. This was the man from the courier's map, the man who'd bound his own soul as an anchor so the god couldn't slip free—and who'd been eaten slowly ever since, decade by decade, until there was barely enough of him left to beg. *End it,* he'd whispered through the grate. *Please. I am so tired of being a door.*

Tamsin stepped to the circle's edge. Sefa took up position at the northern point, Maren at the western, Bram—having abandoned the stair with a last look at the barricaded door—at the eastern. The stolen tithe-house magicians filled the southern arc, six of them, gaunt and flickering, holding hands because none of them trusted their strength alone.

And Tamsin stood at the center, beside the king, where the plan required someone to stand inside the binding and hold the line open while the god was cut loose.

"The rite is simple," Maren had said, twelve nights ago, in the way people say *simple* about things that will kill you. "The binding is a knot. We untie it. But a knot fights back, and this one has been fighting for a thousand years. Someone has to stand in the middle and keep the circle open while we work—keep it from snapping shut around us, keep the god from grabbing the first soul it touches. That person hears everything it says. That person feels everything it does. And when it comes loose—" she hadn't finished. She hadn't needed to.

Everyone had volunteered. Everyone. Even Sefa, wordless, had raised her hand. So Tamsin had lied to them, gently, the way they'd once lied to the valley elders about a map: agreed to draw lots, then palmed the marked stone. Some lies were kindnesses. Some were just necessary.

"Begin," Aldous said.

Maren began.

The chalk lines flared white. The magicians' voices rose in a chant that wasn't a language so much as a permission—the same words, Tamsin realized, that had been carved into these bones a millennium ago, spoken backward now, unmaking instead of making. The knot in the world began to loosen, and Tamsin heard it happen, because hearing was what they did.

It sounded like a scream stretched over centuries.

*No,* said the god.

It didn't speak aloud. It spoke into the bones of the room, into the bones of Tamsin's body, into the place where the dead lived in their head. It was vast and patient and starving, and its voice was the voice of every hunger Tamsin had ever felt, amplified past bearing.

*I know what you are,* it said. *Bone-listener. Valley-child. You have spent your whole life among whispers, and you are so tired of whispering. I can give you the whole choir.*

The circle shuddered. Tamsin planted their feet and held.

*Your valley,* the god murmured, silk over teeth. *Every dead soul Vesper ever buried, every voice you carved into bone and never heard finish its sentence—I will give them back to you. Whole. Loud. Forever. Your mother's mother's mother, telling you her name. The stranger who died in your workshop, telling you why he came. All of it. Everything the silence took from you.*

Tamsin's breath caught. Because that was the wound, wasn't it? A lifetime of half-heard messages, names repeated until they wore smooth, stories that ended mid-word when the bone cooled. They had built their whole self around listening, and listening had always been almost enough.

*And your lost years,* the god pressed, sensing the crack. *You crossed the ridge and left your valley behind, and part of you died at the ward line and stayed there. Come away from this circle. Walk out under the thirteenth moon, and I will give you back the life you would have had—the workshop, the commissions, the quiet mornings. No war. No dying kings. Just the garden and the graves and the gentle murmur of home.*

The chalk lines flickered. Somewhere to the west, Maren shouted something urgent. The god's attention bore down on Tamsin like deep water.

And Tamsin thought of the villages on the pilgrim road, emptied by conscription, the recent dead begging for names. Thought of Sefa's hollowed eyes and her grip on her sister's hand. Thought of Bram burning his oath in the square, of a king kneeling in the dark saying *I am so tired of being a door.*

Thought of what the god was offering, and understood it at last for what it was: not a gift. A trade. Their whole life, bought with everyone else's.

"No," Tamsin said aloud.

The word fell into the chapel like a stone into a well.

"I've heard the dead my whole life," Tamsin said, louder, to the god, to the room, to themselves. "And do you know what they ask for? Not more years. Not their old lives back. They ask for their *names*. To be remembered as they were, not kept as someone wants them. You're offering me a cage full of voices and calling it everything." They gripped the air at the circle's heart, feeling the binding strain against their palms like a living thing. "Everything isn't having. Everything is *holding*. And I'm holding."

The god screamed.

The knot came undone.

It happened all at once and forever: a thousand years of binding unraveling in the space between two heartbeats, the ancient glyphs blazing and shattering, the walls of bone howling, the king's soul rising out of him like smoke from a snuffed candle—pale, tattered, *free*—and the god lunging for it, for any of it, for the open line Tamsin was holding—

Tamsin held.

They felt it try to pour itself into them, a flood of endless appetite seeking a new anchor, and they stood in its path the way a valley stands against weather. Around them the circle blazed, Maren chanting until her voice broke, Sefa's hands throwing light she shouldn't have had left, Bram shouting words nobody heard, the magicians swaying like reeds. The god pushed. Tamsin held. It offered again—everything, everything, *everything*—and Tamsin answered with silence, with refusal, with the simple stubborn weight of a body that would not move.

And then the king's soul cleared the circle's edge, and there was nothing left to anchor to, and the god of hunger came apart.

It didn't die. Things that hungry don't die; they starve. It thinned and scattered, ripped into threads of cold and craving that streamed upward through the chapel ceiling, through the palace, through the silent bells, and out into the night sky where the thirteenth moon burned full—and there, without flesh or crown or anchor, it dissipated like frost in morning sun. Tamsin felt it go. Felt the last thread of it brush past them, almost tender, whispering *you could have had it all*—

And then the backlash hit.

It came through the bones. Of course it did. A thousand years of pressure releasing through the only structure in the room that had carried it, and Tamsin's hearing—the whisper of Vesper's graves, the roar of the road's dead, the choir of the capital—all of it was standing in the flood's path.

They had time for one thought: *so this is what it costs.*

Then the sound went out.

Not gradually. All at once, like a bell struck silent mid-ring. One moment the world was a roar of voices, dead and desperate and dear; the next it was—nothing. A silence so total it had texture, weight, a pressure against the eardrums of the soul. Tamsin staggered. Bram caught them. Their mouth was moving—they could feel it—and no sound of the dead came in, and no sense of the living either, that other hearing that had hummed beneath the first their whole life. Gone. Both. Forever. They knew it the way you know a severed limb is gone: completely, immediately, without appeal.

The chapel settled into ordinary darkness. The bone walls were just walls now. The glyphs on the floor were ash.

King Aldous lay in the center of the ruined circle, and he was breathing—barely—and when Tamsin knelt beside him, his eyes were clear for the first time. Human. Empty of everything except exhaustion and a fading gratitude. His lips moved.

Tamsin couldn't hear him.

They leaned close, close enough to feel breath, and read the shape of the words off his mouth the way you read a letter from someone far away: *thank you.* Then his eyes closed, and his breathing slowed, and slowed, and stopped—gently, like a man setting down a burden he'd carried too long—and the silence where the god had been was just silence now, clean and enormous and empty.

Tamsin knelt there in it, in the first true silence of their entire life, and found they could not tell if they were grieving or relieved or both.

"Tamsin." Maren's voice reached them from very far away, muffled, ordinary, a human voice arriving through human ears and no further. Her face appeared above theirs, streaked with chalk dust and tears. "Tamsin, can you hear me?"

Tamsin opened their mouth to say yes.

What came out instead, before they could stop it, was the truth:

"I can't hear them anymore." Their voice cracked down the middle. "Maren. I can't hear anyone."

Maren pulled them upright and held on, and Sefa pressed in from one side and Bram from the other, and the four of them knelt in the ashes of a thousand-year-old prison, holding each other in the wreckage of a miracle.

Above them, faintly, the first bell began to ring.

It rang wrong—cracked, wavering, human—and then another bell caught it, and another, and soon the whole City of Bells was ringing with a sound that had no magic in it at all, just people, waking, shouting, running into the streets to see why the sky felt lighter.

Tamsin sat in the noise they could barely feel and thought of the valley. Of the ridge line. Of the workshop and the garden they would plant, someday, over a stranger's grave.

But that was later. First there was a king's body to carry up into the light, and a city waking below, and Bram already moving toward the stair with his sword drawn, saying something urgent Tamsin had to watch his lips to catch:

*"Someone's coming."*
