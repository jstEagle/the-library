## 19. Aftermath of a Hollow Crown

Bram Vey had stood in a great many battles, and he had learned that the moment after the killing blow was always the strangest. The world did not end. It simply kept going, indifferent, while you stood there with your heart hammering and your hands empty and no idea what you were supposed to do next.

The thirteenth moon's light came down through the shattered roof of the under-chapel like water through a torn sail, and the god came apart in it.

He had expected screaming. He had expected the walls to bleed, the stones to weep, some grand catastrophe to mark the death of a thing that had worn a crown for three hundred years. Instead it went quietly. The hunger that had pressed against his skull for weeks—the pressure he'd only half-recognized until now, the way you don't notice a weight until it lifts—simply *unclenched*.

The god rose. That was the only word for it. It peeled up out of the binding circle like smoke off a doused fire, a vast gray shape with too many edges, and for one terrible heartbeat Bram thought it would turn on them all, starving and furious, and take what it could before the end.

Then Tamsin said, "No," in a voice that was not loud at all, and the god went up.

It dissipated over the city like frost in sun. Bram watched through the broken vault as the shape thinned, spread, caught the moonlight, and became nothing—wisps of gray drifting over the rooftops of the capital, over the bell towers, over the river, unraveling as it went. Somewhere below them a dog began to bark, ordinary and confused, and somewhere else a baby cried, and the city went on being a city, because that was what cities did.

Beside him, Maren made a sound he'd never heard from her—a small, cracked noise, half laugh and half sob—and grabbed Sefa's hand. Sefa was swaying on her feet, gray-faced, her stolen magic flickering weakly around her fingers like a guttering candle, but she was upright. She was upright and she squeezed back.

And Tamsin—

Tamsin Halloran stood in the center of the circle they had held open with their own body, and Bram watched them listen to something that wasn't there anymore.

"No," Tamsin said again, softer. Their hands came up, palms out, the way they did when they were listening to bone. "No, wait—"

Nothing answered. Bram knew it before Tamsin's face confirmed it. He had spent one night around their hearing and understood instinctively that it lived in their posture, in the tilt of their head, in the way their eyes went distant and focused at once. Now Tamsin stood very still, head tilted toward a silence so total it seemed to have mass, and their face did something Bram had no military vocabulary for.

"Tamsin." Maren moved toward them.

"I'm fine." Flat. Automatic. The voice of a soldier reporting a wound they hadn't looked at yet. "It worked. Did it work? Is it—"

"It's gone," Bram said. He was surprised by how steady his own voice was. "It's gone, Halloran. You did it."

Tamsin looked at their hands. Turned them over. Looked at the carved bones scattered inside the circle—the rite-bones, the ones they'd cut and marked over three sleepless days—and touched one with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder.

Nothing happened.

"Right," Tamsin said. And sat down abruptly on the chapel floor, among the bones, and put their face in their hands.

* * *

King Aldous IV died forty minutes after dawn, in a bed that smelled of lye and old incense, with a hedge-witch's poultice doing nothing and a bone-carver's hand resting on his forehead doing everything.

Bram stood at the foot of the bed and watched a man die human.

He had seen the king twice before, both times at distance—once at a review, once at a coronation anniversary parade where Aldous had sat his horse like a man carved from wood and smiled at crowds with a smile that never reached anywhere near his eyes. Bram had thought, in those years, that the king was simply cold. Distant. A ruler bred into marble.

Now he saw what three centuries of tenancy did to a house.

Aldous was old. Not the sixty-odd years his body should have carried, but genuinely old, ancient, as though every year the god had spent inside him had been drawn against his flesh like credit against a purse. His skin lay against his skull like wet paper. His eyes, when they opened, were pale and watery and—this was the part Bram couldn't stop looking at—*present*. There was someone home behind them. There had been no one home behind them for a very long time.

"The voices," the king whispered. His voice was ruin, gravel dragged through ash. "They've stopped."

"They have, Your Majesty," Maren said. She was seated at the bedside, professional despite everything, her hands steady as she checked the pulse at his wrist. "It's finished. It can't come back."

"It was singing." A tear slid sideways into the pillow. "All this time, I thought—I told myself it was prayer. That I was praying. But it was singing to me, and I sang back, and I—" The breath caught. "How many?"

No one answered. How many what—how many magicians drained through the tithe houses, how many villages conscripted hollow, how many years of a kingdom slowly eaten from the throne outward—there was no number large enough to be honest.

"Aldous." Tamsin leaned closer. They looked wrecked—hollow-eyed, gray-lipped, moving like a person carrying an injury no one could see—but their voice was gentle. "You gave your consent. You held the door open from the inside when it mattered. Whatever else is true, that's true too."

The king's gaze found Tamsin's and held it. Something passed between them that Bram, standing at the foot of the bed with his arms crossed like a guard at a hanging, felt he had no right to witness.

"Who are you?" Aldous whispered.

"Nobody," Tamsin said. "From Vesper."

"Vesper." The king smiled, and it was the first honest expression Bram had ever seen on a royal face, and it broke his heart. "The valley that forgets. I tried to forget it too. It wouldn't let me." His eyes drifted closed. Opened again. "There will be those who want the crown. Tell them—tell them it's only a hat. Tell them I wore it and there was never anything under it but fear."

He died an hour later, quietly, between one breath and the next, with the morning bells ringing out across the city—not the alarm peal, not the mourning toll, just the ordinary seven o'clock bells marking an ordinary hour, which struck Bram as exactly right. The city didn't know yet. The city would wake, and find its king dead and its sky clean, and it would take years to understand what had been lifted off it.

Bram understood already. He felt it in his shoulders, in his jaw, in the place behind his eyes where the pressure had lived since the day he'd fled the Royal Guard. For six years he had carried the knowledge of what he'd seen in the palace chapel—that the king talked to the walls, that the king's shadow moved wrong—and he had told himself he ran to save his own skin, and hated himself for it every single day.

He hadn't run from cowardice. He'd run from a thing too big to name, and now it was named, and dead, and dissolving over the river like frost in sun.

He went out into the corridor and was sick, quietly, into a drainage grate, and then washed his face at a pump and went back in, because someone had to make arrangements and none of the others knew how a royal household worked.

* * *

By noon, the palace was a snake pit.

Bram had predicted it, and it still went faster than he'd guessed. The queen regent—complicit or enchanted, they still didn't know which, and with the god gone it hardly mattered—was found in her chambers at midday, lucid for the first time in a decade, weeping and asking for her son. Her son had been dead eleven years; the thing wearing his face had attended state dinners in his place. She took the news badly, then strangely well, then asked to see the body, and when she did she thanked Tamsin Halloran with a formality that chilled Bram more than any threat.

Word spread the way word does. By evening there were factions.

Bram counted them from the doorway of the map room, arms crossed, watching men and women in fine clothes orbit each other like knives looking for throats. Lord Chancellor Deverell, who had signed the tithe orders and would claim he'd been enchanted. Marshal Okonkwo of the Royal Guard, who had commanded Bram's old company and whose loyalty Bram would trust exactly as far as he could throw the palace. The merchant consortium's representatives, already talking about stability and continuity and the tragic necessity of strong interim authority. Three separate cousins of the royal line, each with a genealogy scroll and a ready-made speech.

"They're going to eat each other," Maren said beside him. She had a cup of the palace's terrible wine and was drinking it like medicine. "Or worse, they'll agree to share."

"That's worse?"

"Mm. Shared power means nobody's responsible. Nobody's responsible means the tithe houses reopen within five years with new paperwork." She glanced at him. "You know this. You wore the uniform."

"I deserted the uniform."

"You burned it publicly in front of two hundred people. Very dramatic. Doesn't change that you know how this machine works better than anyone in this building who isn't currently scheming." She took another swallow. "Deverell's people approached Sefa an hour ago. Offered her a pardon and a position if she'd testify the regent acted alone."

Bram turned. "What did she say?"

Maren smiled, thin and fierce. "She wrote him a single word. I'll let you guess which one."

* * *

They came for Bram at dusk.

Not with blades—with deference, which was worse. Marshal Okonkwo herself, in full dress grays, flanked by two officers Bram had drunk with, trained with, bled with. She stopped three paces away and looked at him for a long moment, and Bram looked back, and neither of them saluted.

"Bram Vey." Her voice carried down the corridor. "Six years listed as a deserter. Warrant of execution outstanding. And tonight you're standing in the palace, unarrested, while the realm falls apart." She let that hang. "I'm offering you reinstatement. Full rank restored, record expunged, command of your own company. The council forming tomorrow needs soldiers who saw the truth early. You saw it earliest of anyone."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the warrant stands, and I arrest you here, and you hang beside whoever else the new order needs to hang to look decisive." She said it without malice, which was the most frightening thing about her. "I'd rather not. You were the best officer I ever fielded. But I need an answer, Vey, and I need it now, because there are four other factions making the same offer to four other people, and whichever of us consolidates first decides what this kingdom becomes."

Bram looked past her shoulder, down the long corridor, toward the room where Tamsin sat with the bones they could no longer hear. Toward Maren and Sefa, heads together over a list of tithe-house survivors. Toward the window, where the last gray wisps of the god were fading off the rooftops into an ordinary evening sky.

Power refused is power redeemed. He didn't know where the thought came from—some sermon, maybe, from a chaplain he'd stopped listening to years ago—but it arrived whole and settled in his chest like a stone finding its place at the bottom of a wall.

Here was the arithmetic he'd learned in twenty years of service: every faction in this palace wanted the same thing. Not justice, not reform—they wanted *legitimacy*, the appearance of continuity, a story to tell that kept them comfortable. And the fastest way to buy legitimacy was a deserter's public redemption: see, even the traitor kneels. His reinstatement would be worth more than a regiment. His hanging would be worth more still.

Either way, they wanted to spend him.

"Marshal," Bram said. "Do you know what was under the crown? Do you actually know, or do you know the version your faction agreed on this afternoon?"

Okonkwo's jaw tightened. "An old god. Bound to the royal line. We've read the witch's account."

"Read it." Bram nodded slowly. "I stood in the chapel and watched the king talk to the walls. I heard what it sounded like when something ancient chewed on a man's soul for three hundred years. And I'll tell you what I learned, Marshal, free of charge: the god didn't take the throne. It was *given* the throne, generation after generation, by people who found it convenient. People who signed papers. People who needed a hungry thing on the throne because a hungry throne keeps everyone too scared to ask questions." He stepped closer. "So no. No reinstatement. No company. No seat at your council. If you want my testimony about what I saw in that chapel, you'll have it, sworn and public, and you can build whatever you build on top of it. But I don't kneel to crowns anymore. Empty or otherwise."

For a long moment the corridor was silent except for the distant bells.

Then Okonkwo did something Bram had never seen her do. She exhaled, and her shoulders came down half an inch, and she looked—briefly, terribly—tired. "Four other factions," she repeated quietly. "Vey. Someone has to hold the center, or the merchants buy the Guard and we get a steward-king within the year, and everything those people died for in that chapel gets sold back to us at interest."

"Then hold it honestly. Put the tithe survivors on the council. Put the valley elders on it. Put people who were *drained*, not people who did the draining." He paused. "And put me on it as nobody. No rank. No command. Just a voice with a warrant over its head, so the whole realm can watch you decide whether you're building a kingdom or a newer cage."

Okonkwo stared at him. One of the officers behind her shifted, appalled. And then—slowly, reluctantly, like a drawbridge lowering—the marshal almost smiled.

"A council seat for a condemned deserter. Deverell will call it madness."

"Deverell signed the tithe orders. Let him call it whatever he likes from wherever he ends up." Bram finally, deliberately, offered his hand. "One condition. The warrant stays active until the council votes it dead in open session. I want the realm to watch you pardon me, not wave it away. Let them see that power can admit a mistake. Gods know they've never seen it before."

She took his hand. Her grip was iron and her eyes were wet and furious and relieved, all at once, and Bram understood that he had just done the hardest thing he'd ever done, harder than deserting, harder than holding the chapel door: he had been offered everything he'd lost, and he had said no.

"Tomorrow, then," Okonkwo said. "Council chamber. Bring your ghost stories."

"I'll bring witnesses instead. They're louder."

* * *

He found Tamsin on the palace roof an hour past midnight, sitting on the lead sheeting with their knees drawn up, looking out over a city that glittered and smoked and slept without knowing what it had lost and gained.

Bram lowered himself down beside them, joints complaining. For a while neither spoke. Below them, the capital murmured—carts, drunks, a night-watch whistle, the endless soft roar of a hundred thousand lives.

"Okonkwo told me," Tamsin said eventually. "About the council. About refusing her offer." A pause. "Why?"

Bram considered the question honestly, because Tamsin deserved honesty and because he'd been asking himself the same thing since sundown. "Because I spent six years thinking the bravest thing I ever did was run. Turns out running was easy. Running was just fear with good footwork." He watched a lantern move along the river road, small and stubborn in the dark. "Staying is hard. Staying and saying *no* when they offer you back everything—that's the first brave thing I've done since I left the chapel that night." He glanced sideways. "Your turn. Why are you up here instead of sleeping? You held a god's leash with your spine. You've earned unconsciousness."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. When they spoke, their voice was careful, the way people speak when they're describing a wound to a medic.

"It's so quiet," they said. "I keep reaching for it. Like a tongue for a missing tooth. Twenty-eight years of voices, Bram. My whole life, the dead were there—whispering in the valley, roaring out here—and now there's just..." They gestured at the city, at the vast humming silence of it. "Me. In here. Alone with my own thoughts for the first time since I was a child." A shaky breath. "I don't know how to be a person who doesn't hear them. I don't know who that is yet."

Bram thought about it. He was not a comfort-giver; his talents ran toward entrenchments and supply lines. But he had buried enough friends to know that grief didn't want fixing. It wanted company.

"Then don't figure it out tonight," he said. "Figure it out on the road. Figure it out with people around you who knew you when the voices were there, so when you catch yourself listening for them, somebody notices and hands you tea." He shrugged. "That's what a company is. That's what it was always supposed to be, before it got eaten by the crown. People who notice."

Tamsin huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Maren's going to make us plant things. I can feel it coming. She's been looking at me like I'm a garden that needs starting."

"She's been looking at *everyone* like that. Sefa's already been assigned herbs." Bram stretched his legs out, wincing. Below, the bells began the midnight count, twelve slow strokes rolling across the roofs. Twelve. The number that had chased them for a thousand miles, and now just a number, just an hour, just bells.

When the last stroke faded, Tamsin spoke again, quieter.

"There's something else. I found it in the king's papers this afternoon, going through them with Maren. A ledger. Tithe-house records—shipments, transfers." They turned to look at Bram, and in the dark their face was grim. "There's a facility the council doesn't know about. North. Past the Ash Road. It's not in any of Deverell's files, which means whoever built it wanted it forgotten." A beat. "There are names in the ledger, Bram. Forty-one names. And a note in the king's handwriting, dated nine years ago: *'Still feeding. Do not close.'*"

Bram felt the tiredness fall away from him like a dropped cloak.

The god was dead. The god was frost in sunlight, gone over the river, gone for good. But gods were only ever the hungry thing itself—and somewhere north, past the Ash Road, forty-one people might still be locked in a building built to feed it.

"When do we leave?" Bram said.

Tamsin almost smiled. "At dawn. I'll wake Maren. Try to talk Sefa into staying."

"She won't stay."

"I know," Tamsin said. "That's rather the point of us, isn't it?"

Below them the city slept on, and above them the stars came out clean for the first time in three hundred years, and Bram Vey—deserter, pardoned-in-waiting, councilman-of-nothing—sat on a palace roof with a bone-carver from a hidden valley and waited for the dawn that would start the next march north.
