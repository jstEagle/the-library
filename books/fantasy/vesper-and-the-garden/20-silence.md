## 20. Silence

The silence had a shape.

That was the first thing Tamsin learned about it, three days after the thirteenth moon. It wasn't empty the way a room is empty. It was full — pressing against their ears like deep water, like wool, like the moment before a sneeze that never comes. Their whole life they had lived beneath a whisper, the dead murmuring up through every piece of bone they touched, and now that whisper was gone and what remained was not quiet but *absence*, a hole in the world shaped exactly like everything they'd ever been.

They sat in the borrowed room above a chandler's shop in the City of Bells, turning a knife over in their hands.

Below, the city was still celebrating. Three days of bells, actual bells, rung by people who didn't know what else to do with a god dying overhead like frost in sunlight. The king was dead — human at the end, Bram said, his face slack and ordinary on the prayer chapel floor, a man who had been hostage in his own body for forty years. The regency council was already arguing about bread prices. The world, indifferent and alive, went on.

Tamsin could not hear a single one of its dead.

* * *

They had tried, of course. That first night, hollowed out and shaking, Maren still smelling of ozone and burnt circle-salt, Tamsin had pressed their palm flat against the chapel floor where centuries of carved bones sang with hunger — had sung, past tense — and felt nothing. Stone. Just stone. They had pressed until their arm ached, until Maren pried them gently back by the shoulders.

"It's done," she'd said. "You did it."

*I did it.* As if those were comforting words. As if Tamsin hadn't known, standing inside the binding circle with the god's voice pouring honey into their ears — *your valley, your dead, your mother's mother's mother, we kept them all, walk away and have them back* — as if they hadn't known exactly what holding the line would cost. The backlash had come through them like a bell struck too hard, and something inside had cracked, and when they woke the world was mute.

They had said yes anyway. They would say yes again. But knowing a price is fair doesn't stop it from hurting.

Now they turned the knife, and thought about work.

Because that was the thing no one understood yet, not even Maren, who grieved for them fiercely and wrongly: Tamsin Halloran had not lost a gift. They had lost a *practice*. Forty years of listening, of carving names into femurs and ribs so the dead of Vesper wouldn't drift nameless, of being the valley's quiet hinge between one side and the other. The hearing had been the instrument. The work was the hands.

The hands still worked.

There was one more bone to carve. There had been since the ridge line, if they were honest — since the courier died in their workshop with a map case crushed against his ribs and a name half-spoken. Tamsin had carved him then, in Vesper, before any of this, and heard his fragments: coordinates, warnings, *Maren*. But that memorial lay buried under impossible soil in an impossible grave, far behind the ward line, and the man deserved better than fragments. He deserved the whole rite, the full carving, the kind of bone you made for someone whose story you finally knew.

And Tamsin knew his story now. Every mile of it.

They had asked Bram, two days ago, whether anyone had claimed the courier's remains. Bram, who was learning to be a person again instead of an oath, had gone quiet and then said, "The palace keeps a charnel vault. Everything from the tithe houses, the executions, the unclaimed dead." A pause. "I know which door. I still know most of the doors."

So here they were, on the fourth morning, with a parcel wrapped in oilcloth on the table and a knife in their hand, and the parcel held a radius and an ulna and three finger bones, and Tamsin could pick them up and feel nothing at all except the weight of a man who had run himself to death carrying hope across a kingdom.

They unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

Maren found them there at dusk, the candles lit, the bones laid out in anatomical order the way Tamsin had laid out a thousand corpses' worth of pieces in Vesper without ever once thinking of it as strange.

"You're doing it tonight," she said. Not a question. She stood in the doorway with her ash-grey braid coming loose and her sleeves pushed up, and she looked at the bones the way she looked at all death now — closer than comfortable, refusing to look away.

"Tonight," Tamsin said. "While I can still remember what it's supposed to sound like."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." They kept their eyes on the radius. "You'll say it was worth it. It was worth it. Both things are true and I don't want to hear the first one tonight."

Maren crossed the room and sat down across the table from them, deliberately, the way she did everything — like planting her feet in a argument she intended to win. "Then I won't say anything. I'll just sit."

"You don't have to—"

"I watched my sister get hollowed out in a tithe house because I was too proud to go back for her." Her voice was level, which meant it was costing her. "You went into a binding circle for a stranger's soul. Sit with me while I sit with you. That's how this works. I read it in a book somewhere."

Despite everything — despite the water-press silence and the ache behind their eyes — Tamsin huffed something almost like a laugh.

A knock. Bram filled the doorway next, Sefa a step behind him, and Tamsin realized with a small cold start that they must have planned this, all three of them, some silent soldier's coordination. Bram held a bottle of plum wine and the expression of a man reporting for a duty he'd volunteered for and regretted immediately. Sefa just looked at Tamsin — looked, and then came forward and took the chair beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched.

Sefa, who hadn't spoken since the tithe house. Sefa, whose magic had been drained down to flickers, who communicated in grips and glances and, lately, in the way she arranged things — cups, cutlery, the little tin soldiers Bram bought her in the market — into patterns that meant *stay* or *go* or *I am here*.

She reached over and put her hand flat on the table, palm up. An offering. An instruction.

*Stay. I am here.*

Tamsin put their hand in hers.

"All right," they said, and picked up the knife. "All right."

* * *

The first cuts were the hardest, because the first cuts were where the voices used to come in.

Every carver has a ritual. In Vesper, Tamsin had always begun the same way: thumb to the bone's face, a breath, and then the listening — waiting for the dead to tell them where to cut, which memory lived closest to the surface, whether this person wanted to be remembered laughing or grieving or furious. The bone spoke first. The knife followed.

Now the knife led, and there was nothing to follow, and Tamsin's hands shook so badly on the first stroke that they gouged a line sideways across the radius like a scar.

They stopped. Breathed. Stared at the mistake.

"You can sand that out," Bram offered quietly.

"It's not the line." Tamsin set the knife down before they threw it. "It's — I keep waiting. You understand? Twenty years of muscle. My hands know how to listen. They're reaching for it and there's nothing there and it's like—" They searched for it. "It's like walking down stairs that aren't there. All night. Forever."

Sefa's grip tightened on their hand.

Then she let go, and did something none of them expected. She pulled the radius toward herself, took the knife from Tamsin's slack fingers, and made a single careful cut beside the ruined one — a long, clean groove, deliberate, following the grain of the bone.

She passed the knife back.

"What—" Maren started.

But Tamsin understood. Sefa couldn't speak, couldn't cast, couldn't do much of anything but endure and arrange and *mean* things with her body, and she had just told Tamsin the only thing worth telling: *the bone doesn't need your ears. It needs your hands. Cut.*

Tamsin picked up the knife and cut.

* * *

The hours went soft after that.

This was the part they had forgotten, or maybe never known, because the hearing had always been so loud in their life: that the work itself was holy even without the voices. The rhythm of blade on bone. The way a name emerged letter by letter, each stroke a small commitment you couldn't take back. The candle-wax smell and the wine going around the table and the four of them falling into a silence that was, for the first time since the thirteenth moon, actually *quiet* instead of absent — because it was shared, because it had people in it.

Bram talked, low, in his parade-ground murmur. He told stories about the courier — Veylan, his name had been, they'd finally gotten it out of the palace records — who had carried dispatches along the Ash Road for eleven years and once outrun a mudslide on horseback and complained about it for a month. "He owed me money," Bram said. "Three coppers. I'm putting it on the grave marker. *Veylan. Courier. Owed Bram Vey three coppers.*"

"He'd have hated that," Maren said.

"He'd have loved it. He'd have contested the debt at his own funeral."

Maren laughed — a real one, startled out of her — and then pressed her sleeve to her eyes and pretended she hadn't. Sefa drew shapes in spilled wine on the tabletop: a horse, badly. A road. Four small marks walking together, and then a fifth, off to the side, with wings or a satchel or both.

"That's him," Tamsin said, throat tight. "Yes."

They carved. The ulna took the story of the crossing — the ford, the fever, the last miles crawled on hands and knees because a dying man with a map will not stop, cannot stop, knows in his marrow that the message matters more than the messenger. The finger bones took the smaller truths: that he'd been afraid, that he'd been stubborn, that he'd chosen Vesper not at random but because everyone knew the valley forgot, and what better place to hide a map than inside the forgetting itself?

Tamsin carved all of it into the bone, stroke by stroke, and heard nothing, and it was enough, and it was unbearable, and both at once, all night long.

Somewhere past midnight they realized they were crying without having noticed starting, tears dropping onto the shavings, and nobody made a thing of it. Maren refilled their cup. Sefa leaned her head briefly against their shoulder, a weight and a warmth and then gone. Bram got up and fed the fire without being asked, the way he did everything, service as penance slowly becoming service as love.

* * *

Near dawn, Tamsin set the knife down for the last time.

The bones lay finished on the cloth, every face covered in fine script — the story of Veylan of the Ash Road, courier, runner of mudslides, carrier of hope, debtor of three coppers. Tamsin ran their thumb over the deepest line and waited, out of habit, out of twenty years of habit, for something to rise up and meet them.

Nothing rose. Nothing would ever rise again.

But their thumb knew what it was touching. Their hands knew what they had made. And sitting in the grey light with three people who had stayed — who had *chosen* to stay, who had coordinated it like a military operation, who were still here as the candles drowned themselves in their own wax — Tamsin found that the silence around the finished bone was not the same silence as the one in their head.

The one in their head was a wound.

This one was a room.

"I used to think the hearing was the point," they said. Their voice cracked; they let it. "The voices. Being the one who listened. Take that away and what's left, right? Just — a person with a knife." They turned the radius over, reading their own carving by the last candlelight. "But the listening was never the memorial. This is. The cutting. Somebody deciding you mattered and writing it down where it can't rot. I can still do that." A breath. "I can still do that."

Maren reached across and closed Tamsin's fingers around the bone. "Then you'll do it louder than anyone who ever heard a word," she said. "Louder than the dead. Louder than bells."

"The bells have been going for four days," Bram said. "Anything's louder than the bells."

Sefa smiled — actually smiled, crooked and tired and real — and Tamsin laughed, wet and rough, and the four of them sat in the wrecked candlelight while the city outside went on being saved and noisy and alive.

They buried Veylan at midmorning, in the chandler's garden, under a plum tree, with a marker that read COURIER. HE MADE IT. Bram stood at attention the whole time and then, alone, paid three coppers onto the fresh earth, and dared anyone to comment.

Afterward, on the chandler's steps, Maren squinted east at the road out of the city, where the hills went gold in the early light, and said, casually, in the voice she used when she'd been thinking about something for days:

"The tithe houses are emptying. Official order, regency council, very official. Hundreds of magicians with nowhere to go. No homes. No families — the crown saw to that." She examined her nails. "Some of them are children, Tamsin."

Bram grunted. "Roads north are safe now. First time in years."

"And Vesper," Maren went on, relentless, gentle, terrible, "has a lot of empty workshops. And a ward that's barely humming these days. If it hums at all."

Tamsin looked at the road. At the hills. At the three people beside them who had sat with a corpse's bones all night because sitting was the only rite that mattered.

"When do we leave?" they said.
