## 21. The Road Back to Vesper

Maren counted them at the city gates the morning they left: four. By the time they crossed the first ridge, they were seven.

It happened without ceremony, which was how most true things happened, in her experience. A girl named Odile who had been drained so long she'd forgotten her own surname fell into step behind Bram simply because he walked like someone who knew where he was going. A tithe-house boy called Pip—no other name offered, none asked—carried Sefa's pack when her arms shook. And a gray-faced man named Halloway, who had spent eleven years lighting candles with his mind for men who never learned to say thank you, asked Maren quietly if there was anywhere in the world that would take him.

"There's a valley," Maren said. "I've never been inside it. But I know someone who has."

She didn't say *Tamsin*. She didn't have to. The whole road home, Tamsin walked half a pace ahead of everyone, and every stray they gathered oriented on them like iron filings finding north. Maren watched it happen and thought: *this is what I was doing wrong all those years alone on the Ash Road.* She had built her exile like a fortress. Tamsin built theirs like an open door.

The road unwound westward through country still smelling of smoke. Word traveled faster than they did—the god was dead, or gone, or whatever you called a hunger with nothing left to eat—and villages that had emptied themselves rather than feed the crown's tithe were trickling back to their fields. In one such village, a woman pressed bread into their hands and refused payment. "You're the ones from the capital," she said, looking not at Bram's guard's build or Maren's hedge-witch scars but at Tamsin, standing quiet in the middle with bone dust still under their fingernails. "They said someone made the king human again."

"He died human," Tamsin said. "That's not the same as making him human."

"It's more than anyone else managed," the woman said, and gave them cheese besides.

Maren slept badly that night, as she had slept badly for years, but differently now. She kept waking to check that Sefa was breathing beside her. Her sister hadn't spoken since the tithe house—not a word, not a sound—but she had begun to hum. Tuneless, half-swallowed, mostly breath. The first night it had frightened Maren so badly she'd sat bolt upright in the dark. Now she found she could only sleep if she could hear it.

On the fourth day out of the capital, they were nine. A pair of brothers who had hidden their weather-sense for a decade came down out of the hills because rumor said there was a place forming, somewhere west, where magic wasn't a crime or a commodity. On the fifth day, a midwife whose hands could ease pain joined them at a crossroads without asking permission, just nodded at Tamsin and fell in.

"We're going to need a bigger house," Bram said, deadpan, and Maren laughed for the first time since the bell tower.

"Vesper will take one look at us and bar the gate," she said.

"Does Vesper have a gate?"

"No," Tamsin said, and something in their voice made Maren look over. They were gazing west, toward the ridgeline that had held the world out their whole life. "It never needed one."

* * *

The ward announced itself three miles out, the way it always had—a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper coins, the sense of walking into a room where a conversation stops. Except now it flickered. Maren felt it stutter against her own small magics like a hand gripping and losing its hold, grip and lose.

"That's new," Tamsin said.

"The god anchored it," Maren said. She had worked it out on the road, in the long nights, talking it through with Sefa's silence for company. "Whatever your elders bound into that ridge line, it drew from the same well. You cut the well dry."

"You're saying we broke our own wall."

"I'm saying walls are expensive." Maren watched Tamsin's face do the thing it did when they were hearing something they already knew. "Your valley bought its safety with the same coin the king used. It just got a better rate."

Tamsin didn't answer. But that night, camped within sight of the ridge, Maren saw them sitting apart with a piece of bone in their hands, turning it over and over, listening—for nothing, now, for the silence they carried like a stone—and Maren understood that Tamsin was grieving the ward too. It had been the last voice of the old world. Even a cage sings when you've lived in it long enough.

* * *

The elders met them at the ford.

Of course they did. Old Wenna stood in the middle of the crossing with her staff planted and six of the council flanking her, and behind them, Maren noted, a fair portion of the village, come to see the prodigal and the strangers both. Word had outrun them again. It always did.

"Tamsin Halloran," Wenna said. "You left in the night without leave. You return with—" her gaze swept the line of them, eleven now, twelve, strays stacked like cordwood, "—an army."

"A family," Tamsin said. "More or less."

"The valley cannot take them. The ward—"

"Is failing," Tamsin said. "You know it is. You've known since the moon turned. What does it feel like from inside, Wenna? Does it still hum?"

A silence. One of the younger elders shifted. Wenna's knuckles whitened on her staff.

"It stutters," she admitted, and the admission cost her visibly. "Some nights it doesn't sing at all. The bones have gone quiet in the memorial house. We thought—" her voice caught, and Maren saw it then, the fear under the rigidity: *the magic is dying, the protection is dying, everything we were is dying* — "we thought perhaps you had done something."

"I did," Tamsin said. "I unbound a god. Your ward drank from the same spring. I'm sorry. I'm not sorry."

"That's not an answer that keeps anyone safe."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "There isn't one anymore. That's the whole point."

Maren stepped forward before Wenna could rally. "Elder. I'm Maren of the Ash Road. Exiled from the capital for teaching children letters the crown hadn't approved. My sister was drained near to death in a tithe house. These people—" she gestured down the line, at Odile and Pip and Halloway and the rest, at the midwife holding a sleeping child not even hers, "—have nowhere. Not nowhere-to-be-allowed. Nowhere. The world outside is full of people like us now, and the crown that hunted us is a regency council and a prayer. You can turn us away at this ford. And in a year, when the ward fails entirely, you'll be turning away hundreds. Or you can let twelve of us in now, and find out what a valley looks like when it stops being a hiding place and starts being a home."

"And if we refuse?" Wenna said.

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Then they looked past her—at the villagers on the bank, at faces Maren couldn't read but Tamsin clearly could, had grown up reading, had carved memorials for their dead and heard their whispers through the bone.

"They won't refuse," Tamsin said quietly. "Will you, Wenna? Because I can't hear the dead anymore, but I remember what they always said. Every single one of them, at the end." They paused. "*Don't waste it.*"

Wenna's staff trembled. For a moment Maren thought the old woman would curse them all from the ford, ward or no ward, and they would make camp in the rain and decide in the morning whether to fight or walk away.

Then Sefa moved.

She stepped forward, past Maren, past everyone, and stood at the water's edge facing the elders. And she hummed—that tuneless, half-swallowed hum—and as she hummed, the water in the ford began to move. Not violently. Gently. Ripples spreading outward in rings, catching the late light, and above the water the air itself seemed to soften, the way frost softens when sun touches it. A small magic. A drained, broken, harvested magic, given back to the world for no purpose except beauty.

The villagers on the bank went very still. Someone gasped. A child laughed.

Sefa stopped. Turned. Looked at Maren with an expression that said, as clearly as speech ever had: *there. That's my argument.*

Wenna looked at the water for a long time. Then at Tamsin. Then, last, at Maren—and there was something in the old woman's face that Maren recognized, because she'd worn it herself on the Ash Road for fifteen years: the exhaustion of guarding a door that no longer kept anything out, and the terrible, liberating question of what you became once you set down the key.

"The memorial house has empty shelves," Wenna said at last, gruffly. "And the east field hasn't been planted in two seasons. And Halloran's mother's workshop is large enough for a school, if anyone's asking." She turned, staff striking the stones. "The council will meet tonight. Don't look triumphant, any of you. It's unseemly."

But her back, Maren thought, looked lighter than her words.

* * *

They crossed at dusk.

Maren felt the ward pass over her like weather—a shudder, a stutter, a whisper of the old copper taste—and then nothing. No wall. No hum. Just valley air, sweet with cut hay and woodsmoke, and the mountains rising dark around them, keeping nothing out anymore, holding nothing in.

Behind her, the strays came across the ford one by one. Odile, who cried silently the whole way and pretended she hadn't. Pip, whooping, splashing, getting glared at by two elders and not caring. Halloway, who stopped in the middle of the crossing and stood so long in the water that Bram had to go back for him, and when Bram reached him the old man was saying, over and over, "I didn't think there'd be anywhere. Eleven years, and I didn't think there'd be anywhere."

Bram put a hand on his shoulder and steered him ashore. "There's here," he said.

Tamsin came last, as Tamsin seemed to do everything—without announcement, without flourish. They paused at the center of the ford, where the stranger had once stumbled toward them out of an impossible grave, and Maren watched them look downstream, toward the workshop lights, toward the garden they'd left planted over a nameless man's bones.

Then they waded out, dripping, onto Vesper soil, and the whole ragged line of them stood together in the gathering dark while the valley elders argued in low voices and the villagers stared and the mountains kept their ancient, useless, beautiful silence.

"Well," Maren said. "Now what?"

Tamsin looked at the workshop. At the crowd. At the twelve lost souls behind them, and the empty shelves of the memorial house, and the east field lying fallow under the first stars.

"Now," Tamsin said, "we plant."
