## 22. Vesper and the Garden

The first frost of the season came on a morning when Tamsin was teaching a child to hold a chisel.

"Looser," they said, wrapping small fingers around the tool the way you'd hold a bird—firm enough that it couldn't fly off, gentle enough that it wouldn't break. "The bone already knows what it wants to be. You're not carving it. You're listening to it."

Pell, who was seven and missing two front teeth and had crossed half the Ash Road with her mother before finding her way to Vesper, frowned at the memorial bone in her hands. It was a rib bone from an ox, smooth and pale, destined for the cairn at the valley's mouth where the freed magicians who hadn't survived their rescue were honored.

"It doesn't say anything," Pell said.

"No," Tamsin said. "It doesn't."

This was the lesson now. Not *listen for the voice*—there was no voice, there would never be a voice again—but *listen anyway*. The difference was invisible from the outside. Inside, it was everything.

Tamsin had spent thirty years hearing the dead murmur through bone and river-stone and the roots of the birches along the ford. They had built a life on that hearing: the commissions, the memorials, the quiet conversations held in workshops while grieving families waited outside. When the god's backlash had torn through them under the thirteenth moon, it had taken all of it in a single white-hot instant, like a hand closing over a bell.

What remained was silence. A silence so complete it had its own texture, its own weather. Some days it was a wound. Most days now, it was a room.

"Tell me what it wants," Pell insisted, because children always did.

Tamsin took the bone back and turned it in the light. "It wants to be honest," they said. "That's all any of them want. To be remembered as they actually were—not better, not worse. So we carve true things. Her name was Odrys. She liked plum wine and she cheated at cards and she died so that other people could go free. All of it goes on the bone. Even the cheating."

Pell giggled, then caught herself, solemn again. "Even the bad parts?"

"*Especially* the bad parts. That's how you know it's love and not just grief."

Through the workshop's open door, Tamsin could see the yard where the school happened now—happened openly, in daylight, with elders watching from the meetinghouse steps instead of pretending not to see. Six students this season. Two of them valley-born, which would have been unthinkable a year ago, when bone-work was done behind shutters and spoken of only in the conditional tense. The ward still stood at the ridgeline, but it was thinner now, translucent, more suggestion than wall. Magic leaked through it like light through curtains. The dead didn't whisper here anymore—they'd never whispered here for Tamsin again—but the living felt freer. That was its own kind of hearing.

Maren appeared in the doorway with two cups of cider and the particular expression she wore when she'd been arguing with Elder Sorrel and had won but wasn't happy about it.

"Sorrel wants the school registered with the council," she said, handing over a cup. "Official rolls. Charter. A seal."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the last time someone put magic on official rolls, it ended up in a tithe house." Maren drank. "Then I told her yes, but on our terms. She's old enough to remember why hiding mattered and young enough to admit it doesn't anymore. It took her four hours, but she got there."

"You're good at that," Tamsin said. "Getting people there."

"I'm good at arguing. Getting people *there* is your job." Maren nodded toward the yard, where Bram was showing three of the older students how to fall properly—a soldier's skill repurposed, as most of his skills had been—and Sefa sat on the workshop step with a lap full of wool, teaching the youngest ones to dye it with madder root. Sefa's magic had come back in flickers, unpredictable as summer lightning, and she had decided, in her wordless way, that if it wouldn't behave it could at least be useful. The wool turned colors when she was calm. It turned *spectacular* colors when Bram lost an argument, which he did often, on purpose, everyone suspected.

"How is she today?" Tamsin asked.

Maren followed their gaze. Her sister's face, hollowed by three years in the tithe house, had filled out some. Her eyes still went distant sometimes, staring at nothing, and Maren never filled those silences anymore. She'd learned to sit inside them. "She laughed yesterday," Maren said. "At Bram. Actually laughed. First time since—" She stopped. Started again. "First time in a long time."

Tamsin drank their cider and let the moment be what it was. In the silence where the dead used to live, they had gotten very good at letting moments be what they were.

* * *

In the afternoon, Tamsin walked out past the cairns to the grave at the edge of the fallow field.

They had buried the courier there in the first week, before any of it—the map, the ridge, the road, the chapel below—when he was just a stranger who had died in their workshop holding a leather case. They'd carved his bones without knowing his name, and the bones had given them fragments instead: coordinates, a warning, *Maren*. Later, on the road, a tithe-house ledger had supplied the rest. Corvin Ashe. Courier, third rank. Twenty-six years old. Entered crown service at fifteen.

He had carried a map across a kingdom into the one valley the crown couldn't watch, knowing—or guessing, or hoping—that someone there might listen. He had been right. It had cost him everything and saved everything, and Tamsin had never once managed to feel that the arithmetic balanced.

The grave was a mound of dark earth gone soft with rain, marked by a single uncarved stone. Around it, the ground had been turned and fenced against rabbits, and it was ready.

Tamsin knelt with a basket of seed bulbs—snowdrops, crocus, and a hardy mountain tulip Maren had sworn would survive anything short of fire—and began to plant.

Their hands knew this work the way they used to know carving. Dig, place, cover. The soil was cold and smelled of coming winter. Above the ridgeline, the sky was doing something enormous and indifferent and beautiful, clouds stacking themselves against the blue like pages in a book no one would finish.

There was no voice in the ground. There was no voice anywhere. On the worst days of the first months, Tamsin had pressed their palms flat against the earth and begged it, silently, furiously, the way you beg a locked door. Nothing. Not even echo. The dead of Vesper slept soundless beneath their stones, and the dead of the whole wide world beyond the ridge had gone out like candles, and Tamsin had walked back down to the workshop each evening carrying that silence like a stone in the chest.

But silence, it turned out, was not empty. Silence was a room, and rooms could be furnished.

They furnished theirs slowly. With students. With Maren's terrible singing and Sefa's impossible wool and Bram's careful, over-explained kindnesses. With the smell of woodsmoke and the specific sound of Pell losing her temper at a difficult curve in the bone. With mornings like this one, cold and clear, kneeling over a stranger's grave making it into a garden.

Corvin Ashe had wanted to be heard. Tamsin couldn't hear him anymore. But they could grow things over him. They could make sure that every spring, the first flowers in the valley bloomed on the grave of the man who had started all of it, and that children walking to the school would pass them and ask, and someone would tell the story—the whole story, even the bad parts—and that was a kind of hearing too. Maybe the kind that lasted.

Dig, place, cover. The last bulb went into the earth as the light went long and gold across the field.

"There," Tamsin said aloud, to no one, to him. "That should do it."

And in the silence, which was a room, which was theirs, something settled. Not peace the way the stories promised it—peace as reward, peace as rest. This was quieter and harder than that. Peace as a thing you planted annually. Peace as work you chose every morning and would keep choosing, because the alternative was the cage, and the cage was comfortable, and comfort had nearly ended the world.

Survival's price had been paid in full. Standing up, brushing earth from their knees, watching the light fail gently over the garden-to-be, Tamsin found—to their genuine surprise, after everything—that it had been worth it.

* * *

They came looking for Tamsin at dusk, the way they always did, drawn by habit or worry or the simple gravity of a family that had learned to orbit.

Bram arrived first, ostensibly to fix the fence latch, actually to check on them; he still did that, checked, with the diligence of a man paying down a debt no one had asked him to owe. Then Pell, sent by her mother to fetch Tamsin for supper and dawdling shamelessly to look at the grave-garden. Then Maren and Sefa together, Maren talking about the charter terms and Sefa's hands flickering color in her lap like slow fireworks.

They stood around the turned earth in the last light, six people and a garden that didn't exist yet, and nobody said anything profound, because they had all stopped needing to.

"Snowdrops," Pell said, reading the empty seed packets Tamsin had left by the fence. "Those are the little white ones?"

"The first ones up," Tamsin said. "Before the frost's even done."

"Who's buried here?"

"A friend none of us ever met," Bram said, and Tamsin looked at him, and he shrugged, slightly embarrassed. "Seems fair. He did the hardest part alone."

Sefa touched the soil with two fingers. Where she touched it, faintly, impossibly, a thread of green shimmered and was gone—a promise, or a blessing, or just a magician's habit of leaving the world a little more alive than she found it. Maren put her arm around her sister and said nothing, eloquently.

They walked back to the workshop together as the stars came out over the ridge, and the ward hummed its thin old song above them, weaker every month, and no one minded. Behind them the garden slept in its dark bed. Ahead of them the windows glowed, and inside there was supper and noise and the ordinary miracle of people who had chosen each other.

Tamsin paused once at the door and looked back—at the field, the grave, the mountains going blue-black against the sky—and felt the silence where the voices used to be, and heard, in the way that mattered, everything.

Spring would come. The snowdrops would come up first, white as carved bone, on the grave of the man who had carried the map.

And the valley would remember.
