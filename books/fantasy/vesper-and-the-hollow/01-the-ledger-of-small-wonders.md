## 1. The Ledger of Small Wonders

The compass did not point north.

It pointed, as far as Ines Ashgrove could tell without touching it, toward whatever the person holding it wanted most. He had watched it through the glass of its containment jar for three hours now, from four different angles, and in that time it had swung toward the tea he wanted, the window when he thought about leaving, and—once, embarrassingly, for a full minute—the small locked drawer beneath his desk where he kept a letter he had never sent.

He had not touched it. That was the first rule, carved into the lintel of the Guild of Impossible Things in letters so old no one remembered who had cut them: *Catalogue, but do not covet.* The second rule was less formal and more strictly enforced: *Do not become part of the collection.*

Ines dipped his pen and wrote, in the small, precise hand that had won him his position twelve years ago:

*Item 4,417. Brass compass, housing cracked at the western seam. Needle of grey metal, unidentifiable alloy; does not respond to lodestone, prayer, or threat. Responds only to desire. Provenance: a fisherman's widow in Saltmere claims her husband traded his shadow for it and drowned within the year. Assessment: genuine. Danger: moderate to severe, depending on what the holder wants.*

He paused, considering the last line, then added:

*Recommend classification: Do Not Open.*

The guildhall around him went on with its evening noises. Somewhere above, the Archivists were arguing about whether a singing stone could be said to have an opinion. Somewhere below, in the Vault of Small Wonders, a door that led to Tuesday was being re-hinged by apprentices who would complain about it for a week. And here, in the Cataloguing Room, forty desks arranged in careful rows beneath lamps that burned without oil, Ines sat alone at the end of the row nearest the wall, because the wall was where you put the people who never caused trouble.

He liked the wall. He told himself he liked the wall.

"Still awake, Ashgrove?" Old Mistress Pellin shuffled past with a lantern she was not supposed to carry near the flammable exhibits. She was ninety if she was a day, and half-blind, and the only person in the building who reliably remembered his name without checking his badge.

"Finishing the compass," he said.

"The wanting-compass? Corvane wants it moved to Deep Storage tonight."

"It should stay in glass," Ines said. "If someone carries it through the halls, it'll point at everything they've ever been denied. There are people here—" he stopped himself, "—there are *things* here that shouldn't learn what we want."

Pellin snorted. "You say that like you're not one of them."

"I'm very well supplied with nothing," Ines said, and smiled, and it was almost true, and Pellin laughed and shuffled on, and the lie sat in his chest like a swallowed coin.

That was the trick of it, the craft of it, the thing he had gotten so good at that he sometimes forgot it was a performance: contentment. He had built it the way he built maps, line by line, border by border, until the shape held. A room of his own with a good lock. Tea at seven. Work precise enough to be praised and dull enough to be forgotten. Twelve years in the Guild of Impossible Things, surrounded by wonders beyond counting, and he had touched none of them—not one—and that was why he was safe, and invisible, and entirely, perfectly alone.

He looked at the compass again. It had stopped pointing at his desk drawer. Now it pointed, steadily, at the window. At the dark beyond the window. At the city below the guild quarter, where the lamplighters were making their rounds and nobody knew his name.

"What do you want?" he asked it quietly.

The needle trembled, as if embarrassed, and settled toward the drawer again.

Ines closed the ledger.

* * *

Deep Storage took two hours, because Deep Storage always took two hours. It occupied the guild's oldest cellars, floors of shelving descending past the level where the city's foundations ended and the guild's foundations began, which was a difference most people preferred not to think about. Ines signed the transit book, wheeled the jar on its trolley down the spiral ramp, and walked the aisles looking for shelf 9-C, reading labels as he went because he could not help it. Reading things was his whole nature. Labels, spines, plaques, warnings.

*Jarred thunder, vintage unknown.*
*A key that fits every door except the one it came from.*
*One (1) honest mirror, damaged.*
*The last word of a dead language, spoken into this bottle in 311; do not uncork.*

At shelf 9-C there was a gap exactly the size of the compass jar, as though the shelf had been waiting, and Ines set it in, adjusted it until the label faced outward, and stepped back.

And that was when he heard the humming.

It came from the far aisle, low and tuneless, the kind of sound a person makes when they think they're alone. Ines froze, hand on the trolley. No one else was scheduled for Deep Storage tonight. No one else ever volunteered for Deep Storage. The junior archivists said the cellars *listened*, which was superstition, but the senior archivists said worse things, which was experience.

He should have announced himself. That was protocol. Instead—because invisibility was not just his habit but his art—he stepped behind the shelf of bottled weather and listened.

Two voices now. One was the hummer's, a man's, dry and unhurried. The other was Master Corvane Dusk's, and hearing it down here made Ines's spine go cold, because Corvane Dusk did not come to Deep Storage. Corvane Dusk sent people to Deep Storage. The guildmaster existed, as far as Ines could tell, in a state of permanent upstairs, a figure of polished boots and quiet orders, seen at the heads of staircases, never at their bottoms.

"—and the payment cleared?" the dry voice asked.

"Cleared and buried," Corvane said. "Three accounts deep. You worry too much."

"I worry exactly the right amount. It's why I'm still alive." A pause, and the sound of something heavy being set on stone. "This is the last one from the Vesper lot?"

"The last. The buyer was specific."

"And the source?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters," the dry voice said, "if the source has family."

Silence, long enough that Ines found himself counting his own heartbeats. Then Corvane, softer: "Everyone has family. That's never once stopped us."

Something scraped—a ledger being closed, Ines thought, or a lid. Boots moved away up the ramp. The other man lingered, humming again, and passed within six feet of Ines's hiding place: a narrow figure in a courier's oilskin, hood back, face young and tired and marked under the left eye with a pale scar like a crescent moon. He carried a satchel flat against his chest the way soldiers carry wounds.

Then he was gone too, and Ines stood alone among the jars with his heart going too fast and a sentence lodged in him like a splinter.

*Everyone has family. That's never once stopped us.*

He told himself, walking back up the ramp, that he had misheard. That guild business was guild business, and the Guild of Impossible Things trafficked in impossible things, and impossible things had strange supply chains. That he had spent twelve years learning not to ask questions, and he was very good at it, and he would be good at it tonight too.

But when he reached his room and unlocked the small drawer beneath his desk—to check, merely to check, that the letter was still there—he found his hands were shaking.

The letter was there. It was always there. It was addressed in his own handwriting to an address he did not remember choosing, in a town he had no memory of visiting, to a name that meant nothing to him and everything, somehow, whenever he read it: *Mara.*

He had written it eleven years ago, the night after the fever, the night he'd woken screaming words in a language the guild's linguists couldn't place. He had never sent it. Sending it would mean admitting there was a before. Before the guild, before the fever, before the blank wall where his childhood should have been—there was supposed to be a life, and he had built his careful borders right up against the edge of it and called the empty country inside him *contentment.*

Ines put the letter away. Locked the drawer. Made his tea at the proper hour, though it was long past seven, and drank it standing at his window while the lamplighters finished their rounds below.

Map everything, he reminded himself. Touch nothing. Be no one.

It had worked for twelve years.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to ring the hour, and somewhere between one stroke and the next, Ines thought he heard—faint, absurd, impossible—someone whistling a tune he almost knew, in a key that hurt.

* * *

Morning came grey over the guild quarter, and with it the ordinary machinery of wonder. Ines arrived at the Cataloguing Room at the stroke of eight, hung his coat, sharpened his pens, and opened the day's intake list. Three items awaited assessment: a lamp that burned memories instead of oil (low priority, common forgery), a birdcage with no bird that nonetheless required feeding (medium priority, mildly alarming), and a box of teeth that whispered the names of their former owners (high priority, do not shake).

Ordinary. Manageable. Safe.

He was halfway through cataloguing the birdcage—it had begun, he noted with professional detachment, tapping its bars in a rhythm suspiciously close to a waltz—when Mistress Pellin appeared at the end of the row of desks, and something in her face stopped his pen mid-word.

"Ashgrove." Her voice was carefully casual, which from Pellin was louder than shouting. "Corvane wants you in his office. Ten o'clock."

"Me?" Ines heard his own voice come out pleasantly puzzled, and marveled distantly at how well-practiced the sound was. "Did I file something wrong?"

"He didn't say." Pellin lingered, lowering herself onto the corner of his desk, which she never did. Her eyes, milky with age, were fixed on him with terrible gentleness. "There was a courier come through the cellars last night, they're saying. After hours. Unregistered."

Ines kept his face still. He had practiced that too, all these years; it was the same skill as mapping, really—showing the surface of a thing and nothing more.

"I wouldn't know," he said. "I delivered the compass and came straight up."

"Mm," said Pellin. She patted his desk twice, rose, and shuffled away, and Ines sat looking at the birdcage, which had gone silent, as if it too were listening.

Ten o'clock. Corvane Dusk's office was on the fourth floor, behind a door of black oak with a silver plate that read simply *GUILDMASTER*. Ines had been inside twice in twelve years, both times with completed ledgers, both times for less than five minutes. He rehearsed the walk the way he'd rehearse a difficult survey: steady pace, easy shoulders, the face of a man with nothing hidden anywhere in him.

He was early. He stood outside the door for four minutes composing his breathing, and it was during those four minutes, through the gap where the corridor window overlooked the alley below, that he saw the courier.

The young man with the crescent scar. Oilskin satchel pressed to his chest. Running—not walking, running—up the alley toward the guild's side entrance, stumbling, leaving a dark thread of something on the cobbles behind him. Even from four floors up, even in the flat morning light, Ines knew what the thread was. He had catalogued enough blood in twelve years to know it at any distance.

The courier reached the door, clawed at it, failed to open it, and turned—slowly, the way turning goes when the body is already failing—and looked up.

Four floors. Grey light. Impossible distance.

And the man with the crescent scar looked directly at Ines Ashgrove, who had spent twelve years ensuring that no one ever looked directly at him, and raised one shaking hand, and mouthed words that Ines could not hear and understood anyway, the way you understand a map, instantly and completely, the whole territory at once:

*Thirteen turns.*

Then the alley door burst inward from the street beyond, and figures poured through it in grey livery that Ines did not recognize and recognized absolutely, the way you recognize a border drawn on a map of a country everyone swears doesn't exist—

—and the guildmaster's door opened behind him, warm and courteous, and Corvane Dusk said, "Master Ashgrove. Come in. We have a great deal to discuss."

Below, in the alley, the screaming started.

Ines did not move. His hand was already in his pocket, curled around the folded scrap of paper he had no memory of putting there, and when he drew it out with fingers that did not feel like his own, he found a strip of oilskin cloth, torn from a satchel, bearing a single line of ink in a hand that shook:

*a map of what was sold — find it before the thirteenth turn*

The compass in Deep Storage, he thought absurdly, would be pointing at him now.
