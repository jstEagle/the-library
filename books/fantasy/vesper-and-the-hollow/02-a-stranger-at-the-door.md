## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The rain came down on Copperlane Alley like a ledger being read aloud—drop by drop by drop, each one accounted for, none of them forgiven. Ines Ashgrove stood beneath the guild's eave with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and counted the ways he could get from the door to the chandlery without stepping in a puddle. There were four. He had mapped all of them years ago, back when mapping puddle-routes was the most dangerous thing he did.

He liked days like this. Days when the guild's business was slow, when the only marvels crossing his desk were minor ones—a lamp that burned regret instead of oil, a key that fit any lock but only opened doors that wanted opening. Small wonders. Catalogued, described, priced, and never once touched with anything but gloved hands and careful ink. That was the rule he'd built his life on, brick by patient brick: *map everything, touch nothing.* The world was full of beautiful, terrible things, and Ines Ashgrove intended to die of old age having admired every one of them from a very safe distance.

He was thirty-one years old, and he had been invisible for so long that he sometimes forgot it was a thing he'd chosen rather than something he simply was.

The bell above the guild's side door rang at half past the ninth hour, which was wrong, because no deliveries came at half past nine, and the bell itself had been broken since spring. Ines knew this because he kept the maintenance ledger too. He knew everything about the Guild of Impossible Things except how to be part of it.

He opened the door anyway. Curiosity was the one indulgence he permitted himself, and even then only in small, measured sips.

The alley was empty.

No—not empty. He corrected himself the way he corrected all his maps: precisely, immediately, without mercy for the wish behind the error. The alley was not empty. There was a shape against the far wall where the chandlery's gutter spilled its silver rope of rainwater, a shape that had not been there when he'd looked out an hour ago, because Ines Ashgrove noticed everything, and things that appeared between one glance and the next were exactly the sort of thing the guild paid him to notice.

"Hello?" he said, and hated how small his voice sounded against the rain.

The shape moved. It unfolded—one limb, then another—with the creaking deliberation of a man who had been holding himself together through sheer refusal. A courier's cloak, travel-stained the grey-brown of every road between here and the coast. A courier's boots, worn through at the heel. And beneath the hood, a face that made Ines take an involuntary step backward, because it was the face of a man who had already decided to die and was merely waiting for the paperwork to finish.

Blood. There was blood everywhere, thinned by rain to a pink wash that ran along the gutter seams like a map of some country Ines had never charted and never wanted to. It pooled around the man's knees. It dripped from his left hand, which was pressed flat against his ribs as though holding something in—or keeping something from getting out.

"You're the mapmaker." The voice was gravel dragged over glass. "They said—the guild keeps a mapmaker who doesn't touch anything. They said you're the one."

"I'm a cataloguer," Ines said automatically. "I don't—I'm not authorized to—"

The courier laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought up something dark that the rain carried away before Ines could decide whether to look at it. The man pushed off the wall and took two steps, three, and then his legs simply resigned their post, and Ines lunged forward on instinct and caught him under the arms, and the weight of a dying stranger settled against his chest like a debt coming due.

"No," the courier said, close to Ines's ear, urgent as a fire alarm. "Don't call them. Don't call anyone inside. Do you hear me? *Inside* is why I'm—" Another cough. His fingers found the front of Ines's coat and gripped it with the last of his strength, knuckles white as bone. "Thirteen turns. You have to count them. Thirteen turns of the moon, and then the door closes, and then it's too late for everyone whose name is written down."

"I don't understand what you—"

"They'll come for it. The collectors. They wear faces like everyone else, but they don't blink enough, watch for that, watch for—" His breath hitched. His grip tightened. And then, with a slowness that seemed almost ceremonial, he drew something from inside his coat—a folded square of paper, soft-edged with handling, dark at one corner where the blood had soaked through—and pressed it into Ines's hand.

Not handed. Pressed. Folded Ines's fingers closed over it himself, both of the courier's ruined hands wrapped around Ines's one, as if sealing a covenant.

"It has to be you," the man whispered. "It was always going to be you. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I waited so long to find you."

"Find me? Who are you? Who sent you?"

But the courier's eyes had gone past him, fixed on something over Ines's shoulder—the doorway, or nothing, or perhaps the particular quality of light that dying men see arriving—and his expression changed. Not to fear. To recognition. As if someone had just walked into the alley whom he'd been expecting all along.

"Thirteen turns," he said again, softer now, almost gentle, the way you'd remind a child of something important before sleep. "Count them. And when you remember your name—"

He didn't finish. His hands went slack. His weight went from heavy to absolute, and Ines, who had never touched anything in his life, held a dead man in the rain and did not let go for a long time.

* *

He should have called for help. He knew that. There was a procedure for this—a dead body in the guild quarter required notification of the wardens, a report filed in triplicate, a statement given to Master Corvane Dusk himself, who would appear with his grey gloves and his grey voice and ask questions in that soft way of his that felt less like inquiry and more like inventory. Ines had drafted such reports before. He knew the forms. Form 7-C, Sudden Death, Unnatural Circumstances Suspected. He could probably recite the field codes.

Instead he stood in the alley with the rain running off his nose and the folded paper burning a hole through his glove, and he did the single most out-of-character thing of his carefully unremarkable life.

He hid the body.

Not well—he was a cartographer, not a murderer—but well enough. The coal cellar door stood open twenty feet down the alley; the guild's porters wouldn't need it until morning. He dragged the courier inside, arranged him among the sacks with what dignity the situation allowed, closed the man's eyes with two fingers—*touched him*, touched a dead stranger's eyelids, and felt the transgression of it all the way down his spine—and shut the door.

Then he climbed the back stairs to his room, bolted the door, lit no lamps, and unfolded the map.

The paper was fine stock, vellum probably, expensive, the kind the guild used for commissions that mattered. It had been folded many times, along precise creases, by someone who understood that maps are meant to survive. Ines's fingers, trained by fifteen years of handling documents worth more than his life, smoothed it open on his desk with a tenderness that surprised him.

At first glance it was a mountain. Mount Vesper, unmistakably—the twin-horned silhouette that dominated every horizon for fifty miles, drawn here in fine black line work with a cartographer's precision that made Ines's professional heart ache with envy. But Vesper had been cut open. Longitudinal section, the way anatomists drew bodies: the mountain flayed to show what lived inside it.

And inside it was a court.

Ines had heard the stories the way everyone had—nursery tales dressed up as warnings. The Hollow Court beneath the mountain. The pale nobility who traded in souls the way merchants traded in spice. Every child in the guild quarter grew up knowing not to sign anything offered after midnight. It was folklore. It was the kind of thing the guild catalogued under *Mythical Geography, Unverified* and priced accordingly.

Except that whoever had drawn this map had drawn it from *inside*. The detail was intimate in a way no outside survey could achieve. Corridors rendered in confident strokes. Chambers labeled in a small, disciplined hand: *the Feasting Hall. The Gallery of Faces. The Vault of Quiet Things.* Stairways descending past the depth where any sane surveyor would have stopped and turned back. And at the mountain's heart, rendered in ink so red it might have been blood—it probably was blood, Ines realized, and his stomach turned—a single chamber marked with a symbol he didn't recognize: a circle, bisected, with thirteen radiating lines.

One line for each turn of the moon. Twelve of them had been crossed out.

His mouth went dry. He counted twice. Counted a third time, because counting was what he did, counting was the solid ground he stood on when everything else shook. Twelve strokes struck through. One remaining.

*Thirteen turns,* the courier had said. *You have to count them.*

And then Ines saw the contract.

It occupied the lower third of the sheet, written in that same small disciplined hand but in a different ink—iron-gall, brown-black, the ink of legal instruments. It was formatted the way guild contracts were formatted, which meant it was old, older than the current guild charter, older maybe than the guild itself. The language was archaic but the structure was legible, and Ines had spent his whole adult life reading the fine print of impossible transactions.

*Be it known to all parties, witnessed beneath stone and star, that the following soul is sold, whole and entire, unto the Court of the Hollow Mountain, in perpetuity, across all turns of all moons...*

There was a space for the soul's name. It had been filled in.

Ines read it once. Then he read it again, because his mind refused the information the first time, sliding off it the way water slides off oiled cloth. Then he sat down, because his legs informed him they were no longer interested in supporting his opinions.

The name on the contract was his own.

Not *Ines Ashgrove*. Something else—a child's name, a name in a script he half-recognized the way you half-recognize a face from a dream, letters that seemed to rearrange themselves whenever he tried to sound them out. But beneath the name, in the signature block, in a cramped childish hand he knew absolutely, instantly, the way you know your own reflection—

That was his handwriting. His signature. He signed his initials that exact way every day, forty times a day, on every intake form and catalogue entry: the first letter hooked, the second crossed with a flourish he'd never been able to train himself out of. Fifteen years of daily repetition lived in those two small marks.

He had signed a contract selling his soul to the Hollow Court beneath Mount Vesper.

He had no memory of it. He had no memory of ever standing before that mountain. He had no memory of any life before the guild took him in at—an age he'd never been able to fix, a childhood with edges worn smooth as sea glass, a past that had always felt slightly less detailed than other people's, like a map of a coastline surveyed from too far away. He had told himself for fifteen years that this was normal. That memory was simply like that. That everyone's childhood dissolved the same way.

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the guild went about its evening—footsteps in the corridor, the clink of the supper bell, someone laughing in the common room. The sounds of the only life Ines had. The sounds of a place that had raised him, fed him, taught him his trade, and never once, not in fifteen years, answered the question of where he had come from.

He looked at the twelfth crossed-out stroke. Then at the thirteenth, still open, still waiting.

Four weeks. Whatever bargain his signature had sealed, whatever part of him had been sold and shipped beneath the mountain like cargo in a jar, the invoice was coming due. The courier had died in the rain to deliver him thirty days' notice.

*When you remember your name—*

The sentence unfinished. The man dead before the end of it. And Ines sitting alone in the dark with a map of hell drawn in blood, realizing with a vertigo so complete it felt almost peaceful that the rule he'd built his life on—*map everything, touch nothing*—had just been broken in both directions at once. Something had touched him. Something had reached out of the deepest dark on the map of the world and put its finger on his name.

A knock at his door made him jump hard enough to knock the inkwell over.

"Ashgrove." The voice on the other side was soft, unhurried, and grey as winter slate. Corvane Dusk. "We've had word of a disturbance in the alley. You were seen at the side door. Open, please."

Ines looked down at the map spread across his desk, glowing pale in the dark like a wound. He thought about form 7-C. He thought about triplicate reports and grey gloves and questions asked softly. He thought about a dead man's hands folding his fingers closed over a secret.

For the first time in fifteen years, Ines Ashgrove lied.

"One moment, Master Dusk," he said, folding the map into his shirt, over his heart, where he could feel it ticking like a second pulse. "Just—give me one moment."
