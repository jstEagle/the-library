## 3. What the Guild Keeps

The map was still warm.

Ines felt it through his coat as he climbed the guild's back stairs, past the sleeping dormitory, past the room where the apprentices kept their contraband sweets and stolen star-charts, up to the narrow cell he had occupied for eleven years without once being asked to leave. He closed the door. He did not light a lamp. The window over the rooftops of the guild quarter let in enough moon to work by, and he had always been able to draw in the dark—that was the first thing they taught you here, before they taught you anything else: *the hand remembers what the eye is not permitted to see.*

He laid the courier's map on his desk and unfolded it with the care one gives a wounded bird.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Whoever had drawn it—drawn it in blood, in haste, in the last minutes of a life leaking out onto guild cobblestones—had possessed a master's line. The mountains rose in fine hatched strokes, and at their heart sat Mount Vesper, rendered not as stone but as something almost tender, its slopes shaded like the curve of a sleeping face. Thirteen circles ringed the summit, each one a moon, each one inked with a turn-count that had already begun its subtraction. Twelve remained.

And at the center, where a map should say *you are here*, it said instead: *a soul is here.*

Ines read the contract notation twice because the first time his eyes refused it. A sale, dated nineteen years past. A name signed in a child's unsteady hand. A price entered in the Hollow Court's ledger-script, which was not writing so much as a wound given grammar.

The signature was his own.

Not similar. Not an echo. It was his—the particular way he crossed his letters, the small hook on the terminal stroke that no teacher had ever managed to beat out of him. He had signed a thousand catalog entries with that hand. He was looking at it now, aged backward into childhood, at the bottom of a document selling something to the mountain.

*Thirteen turns,* the courier had said. And then nothing, forever.

Ines sat very still for a long time. Below him the guild slept the sleep of people who dealt in wonders and were therefore certain nothing could surprise them. He listened to the building breathe—pipes ticking, someone's dream-murmur through the wall—and understood, with the clarity of cold water, that everything he had built here was made of glass.

Map everything. Touch nothing. Be no one worth finding.

He had broken the second rule by catching the courier. He was about to break the third by keeping what the man had died to deliver.

His hands moved before his courage caught up. That was the mercy of craft: it did not require you to be brave, only busy. He took a fresh sheet of guild vellum and began to copy—not the soul-map itself, never that—but a decoy. A chart of the Underways' upper galleries, plausible, well-made, deliberately flawed in three places only he would know. If the map was taken from him, let them find this. Let them die politely in a gallery that didn't connect to anything.

Then he took the original and folded it along creases that turned it into something else entirely: a page of marginalia, a scrap of failed practice-work. He slid it inside the hollow spine of his oldest atlas—a book so unremarkable, so thoroughly catalogued, that no one had opened it in a decade. The guild kept ten thousand marvels behind wards and glass. Ines Ashgrove kept his miracle inside a book about drainage canals.

He washed the blood from his cuffs. He burned the courier's satchel-token in the lamp flame and watched the wax bubble black. When there was nothing left on him or near him that smelled of death, he lay down on his cot and stared at the ceiling and did not sleep.

Somewhere below, in the alley, men with shovels were doing quiet work. He tried not to think about whose job that was tonight, and whether it was usually done by someone who whistled.

* * *

Corvane Dusk did not sleep either, but then, Corvane considered sleep a concession to the body that he granted perhaps twice a week, like alms.

He stood at the window of the guildmaster's office—a room positioned, by old design, so that every approach to the Guild of Impossible Things could be observed from it—and watched the lanterns bobbing in the alley below. The removal crew worked slowly tonight. They always worked slowly when the dead was one of theirs.

A courier. Dead on their threshold. His throat opened with a precision Corvane had seen exactly once before, thirty years ago, on a mountainside he did not permit himself to remember.

"Report," he said, without turning.

Sister Halvene, the night registrar, stood in the doorway with her ledger hugged to her chest like a shield. "No pouch. No tokens. No identification. The body's marked—" she hesitated, "—marked *removed*. Someone's cut the guild sigil off his shoulder. And there's—" She stopped again. Sister Halvene had been registrar for twenty years and had learned that the pauses were often more informative than the words.

"There's what."

"Ink on the cobbles. A great deal of it. As though a map were drawn there and then—taken away."

Corvane turned. Lamplight caught the silver thread at his collar, the only ornament he permitted himself, and even that was less vanity than uniform: a single stitched line, running collar to hem, like a meridian. Like a border on a chart. *This far and no farther.*

"A map," he said. "Drawn in a dying man's blood, on my doorstep, and then removed by person or persons unknown." He smiled, which was a thing he did with his mouth alone. "How impossible. We should catalogue it."

"The watch will want—"

"The watch will want nothing, because the watch will hear nothing. A drunk fell in our alley. The guild expresses regret. There is soup for the family, if there is a family." He came around the desk. "There is always a family, Halvene. That is what makes lying expensive. You must feed all of them."

She withdrew. Corvane remained at the window a while longer, watching the shrouded shape carried out under canvas, and let himself think about Mount Vesper for precisely the length of time it took a candle to gutter—one breath, two—and then he put the mountain away in whatever sealed room of himself he kept such things, and went down to find out who among his cartographers had been standing in the alley at midnight.

He already knew. That was the trouble with invisible men: you noticed them precisely because they cast no shadow.

* * *

Morning came gray and procedural, as mornings in the guild always did, and Ines dressed himself in his ordinary face: the slightly stooped shoulders, the ink-stained fingers, the expression of a man perpetually calculating the cost of lamp-oil. He had worn this face so long it fit better than his own.

He was halfway through cataloguing a compass that pointed at regrets—he'd logged it yesterday; today it needed its ward-check renewed—when the floor of the Hall of Small Wonders went quiet in the specific way that meant Corvane Dusk had entered it.

Ines did not look up. Looking up was interest, and interest was a debt.

"Ashgrove."

He looked up. Refusal would have been louder than obedience.

Guildmaster Dusk crossed the hall unhurried, his boots making no sound at all on stone that ought to have announced him, and stopped at Ines's table. Up close, Corvane smelled of cold air and something faintly mineral, like the inside of a cave. His eyes were the pale gray of unfixed fog.

"You were in the alley last night."

"I take the air after late cataloguing, guildmaster."

"At midnight. Past it." Corvane picked up the compass from the table—picked it up, casually, the way a man might lift an apple—and Ines's stomach dropped, because the needle swung, quivered, and fixed on Corvane's chest. On the heart. Regrets, pointing home. The guildmaster regarded it with mild distaste and set it down. "You heard something. Saw something. A man died forty feet from your door, Ashgrove, and you are the only soul in this building whose habits place him within earshot."

"I heard a fall. I looked out. I saw a drunk in the dark." Ines heard his own voice and marveled at it: level, dry, apologetic. Eleven years of practicing invisibility, and it turned out the skill converted seamlessly to perjury. "I called down. He didn't answer. I assumed the watch would come, and I didn't want to—" he gestured vaguely, the gesture of a small man declining entanglement, "—get involved. I'm sorry. I should have reported it."

Silence. Corvane studied him the way he studied acquisitions: turning him over, checking for repairs, assessing whether the provenance was clean.

"You've never lied to me before," the guildmaster said at last, almost gently. "Eleven years. I have found it restful. Do you know how rare that is, in this place? Everyone in this guild lies—about provenance, about price, about what they saw in the deep places. But you. You lie only with your silences, and your silences are honest ones." He leaned in, and his voice dropped to something almost kind. "So understand what it costs me to believe that a drunk fell in my alley with his throat opened like a letter and his guild-mark peeled from his shoulder."

"I only know what I saw."

"Yes," said Corvane. "I imagine you do." He straightened. For a moment something moved behind the fog-gray eyes—not suspicion exactly; suspicion was too warm a word. Inventory. "The courier trade has been strange lately. Routes failing. Riders not arriving. Three weeks ago a whole post went silent on the Vesper road." He said the name lightly, tossing it out like a coin to see who would stoop for it.

Ines did not stoop. "Roads are always failing somewhere, guildmaster."

"Mm." Corvane turned to go, then paused. "Ashgrove. Your cell faces the north stair. Your window faces the alley. If anything else should... fall... where you can see it, you'll bring it to me. Whatever it is. Whoever asks you otherwise." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Whatever they promise you."

"I'm not promised things," Ines said. "I'm a cataloguer."

"Yes," said Corvane Dusk. "That is exactly what I once was, too."

He left. The hall resumed its noise around Ines like water closing over a dropped stone. Ines looked down at his hands and found them steady, which frightened him more than shaking would have—because they were steady the way they were steady over a dying courier, the way they were steady over any map, and he was beginning to suspect that his calm had never been peace at all. Only distance. And distance, he now understood, was just fear that had learned good posture.

For eleven years he had believed the guild was a shell he hid inside. Walking back to his cell, past the wards and the glass cases and the sleeping marvels, past the drainage-canal atlas waiting on its shelf with its folded secret, he felt the truth of it settle over him like snow:

The shell had a keeper. And the keeper had just told him, in the politest possible language, that he was already inside a cage whose dimensions Corvane knew by heart.

* * *

Corvane stood in the archive of acquisitions, alone, in the dark, and did not light the lamps.

He did not need them. He had catalogued this room himself, decades ago, shelf by shelf, and his memory kept better order than any ledger: third case, the compass that wept; seventh, the door that opened onto yesterday; and in the iron cabinet at the room's center, under triple seal, the guild's oldest and least discussed holding.

The Ledger of Precedents.

Every guild had one, though none admitted it. A record of transactions the Guild of Impossible Things preferred history to forget: wonders acquired through means that would not bear daylight, bargains struck on behalf of members who had not been asked, names entered into contracts by hands other than their own. Corvane had read it cover to cover at thirty years old, kneeling on this same floor, and had vomited afterward, and had risen the next morning and become guildmaster anyway, because someone had to hold the key, and he had reasoned—with the terrible arithmetic of ambitious young men—that a cage guarded by a man who hated it was safer than a cage guarded by a man who loved it.

Nineteen years ago, an entry had been made in that ledger. He had not made it. He had been in the northern provinces, chasing a rumor about a river that flowed uphill, and when he returned the entry was there, in another clerk's hand, and the boy involved was gone—sold, the entry said, in partial settlement of a debt the guild had owed the court beneath the mountain. A debt incurred before Corvane's tenure. A debt the guild had settled the way it settled everything: with someone else's self.

The boy's name had been entered and then struck through, struck so hard the pen had torn the page. Corvane had sometimes wondered, across nineteen years, whether the tearing had been shame or an attempt at erasure, and whether erasure ever truly worked, or whether it merely drove a name deeper, the way a splinter driven in past feeling becomes part of the flesh.

Last night, a courier had died on his doorstep carrying ink and secrets off the Vesper road. This morning, the most invisible man in his guild had lied to him for the first time in eleven years, and lied *well*, which was worse, because skill implied practice, and practice implied motive.

Corvane did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what small minds called the machinery of large ones working where they couldn't see it.

He unlocked the iron cabinet. He did not open the ledger—not yet. He rested his hand flat upon its cover, feeling the faint warmth of the thing, the way one feels heat through a door from a fire in the next room, and he thought about the struck-through name, and the torn page, and the boy who had come back years later with no memory and a talent for maps and a habit of standing just outside every circle of light.

*He doesn't know,* Corvane thought. *Or he knows and doesn't know he knows. Either way, the mountain has begun collecting.*

Outside, dawn came up over the guild quarter, gilding the weathervanes, touching the windows of a narrow cell where a cartographer slept badly beside a book about drainage canals.

Corvane closed the cabinet, locked it, and gave the order that set everything moving: double the watchers on Ashgrove. Quietly. And send word to the Vesper road—

—to ask the mountain, very courteously, what it wanted.

* * *

Ines woke past noon with the atlas's spine digging into his back—he had, apparently, dragged the book into bed sometime before dawn, the way a child drags a blanket, and had no memory of deciding to.

He lay still, listening to the guild's daytime sounds, and performed the morning inventory he had performed every day for eleven years: name, rank, duties, debts. Ines Ashgrove. Senior cataloguer. Compasses, keys, and class-three impossibles. Owed two months' dues, paid monthly, invisible.

Except that today the inventory snagged, snagged on the signature, on the child's hand that was his hand, and for the first time the list came back wrong. *Ines Ashgrove.* He said it silently, testing it, and it sounded—for one vertiginous half-second—like a name on a label over someone else's jar.

He got up. He washed. He ate standing. And when the knock came at his door—two soft raps, the guild's signal for *errand, not emergency*—he answered it wearing his ordinary face, and found a runner with a message slip, and read the slip, and felt the crack travel through the armor of his careful life like frost through glass:

*Senior Cataloguer Ashgrove — requisitioned, effective immediately, for survey detail, Vesper Road, departing tomorrow's first bell. By order of C. Dusk, Guildmaster.*

They were sending him to the mountain.

They were sending him home.

Ines looked up, and down the length of the corridor, at the far end, a watcher he had never noticed before noticed him back—then turned away, badly, toward the window.

*So,* Ines thought, folding the slip into quarters, into eighths, into something small enough to swallow. *Now everyone is mapping me.*

That night he would take out the atlas, unfold the dead man's map, and study the twelve remaining moons until the candle drowned. But standing in the corridor, in the ordinary gray light, with the requisition trembling in his hand, one thought rose above all the others, quiet and absolute as a plumb-line:

If the guild was going to walk him to the Hollow Court's door—then for the first time in his life, Ines Ashgrove intended to arrive knowing exactly where he was going. And he would need help to get there. There was a thief in the east wing who owed him a favor, and sang when she thought no one was listening, and had lost someone to the mountain besides.

He had never once, in eleven years, asked anyone in this guild for anything.

Tomorrow, that would have to change.
