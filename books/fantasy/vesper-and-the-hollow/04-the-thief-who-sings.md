## 4. The Thief Who Sings

Wren kept her knives the way other people kept secrets: close, oiled, and never shown to anyone who hadn't already earned a scar.

She was working the guild's upper galleries that evening, three floors above the Hall of Small Wonders, where the ceiling beams ran wide enough to walk and the dust lay thick enough to record every footstep that didn't belong to her. She liked it up here. The rafters smelled of cedar oil and old varnish, and from between them she could see everything—the clerks drifting below like pale fish, the glass cases glinting with their impossible inventory, the great brass orrery turning in slow silence at the center of the hall. From up here, the Guild of Impossible Things looked exactly like what it was: a vault with pretensions of scholarship.

She'd been lifting a duplicate key to the reliquary annex for two days now, waiting for the night clerk's circuit to settle into its predictable rhythm, when she heard someone climbing the maintenance ladder behind her.

Nobody climbed to the rafters. That was the point of them.

Her hand went to the knife at her hip without her deciding anything. She turned on the beam, balanced easy as breathing, and found herself looking down at Ines Ashgrove.

He stood at the top of the ladder with one hand white-knuckled around the rail, which was funny, because Ines had drawn half the maps of this building and could probably navigate the rafters blindfolded. He just never came up here. He never came anywhere unexpected. That was the whole architecture of him—Ines Ashgrove, the man built out of hallways and ledgers, who moved through the guild like ink through water, present everywhere and touching nothing.

"Ines," she said. "You're lost."

"I need to find you."

"You found me." She let the sentence sit there, sharp-edged. "That's not the same as needing me. You've spent six years making sure nobody needs anybody in this building."

He flinched. It was small—a tightening around the eyes—but she caught it, because catching things was her trade, and because she'd known Ines longer than he probably realized. He thought his invisibility was perfect. Nobody's invisibility was perfect. You just had to know where to look.

"Please, Wren." His voice was low, pitched under the hum of the orrery. "Not here. Somewhere no one maps."

*Somewhere no one maps.* The phrase landed strangely in her chest. Everyone in the guild talked about mapped and unmapped spaces the way sailors talked about weather—constantly, superstitiously, with one eye on the horizon. But Ines said it like a prayer, or an apology.

"The cistern house," she said finally. "Midnight bell. Come alone, come clean—if you're carrying anything that ticks, hums, or whispers, leave it in your room."

"It doesn't tick," he said. "It—" He stopped himself. "Midnight."

He went back down the ladder with the careful movements of a man carrying something breakable inside his ribs, and Wren watched him cross the gallery floor below, hugging the walls, and felt the first cold thread of unease slide down her spine.

Because Ines Ashgrove did not ask for help. In six years, through thefts and audits and the time the mirror cabinet had eaten an apprentice's reflection for a week, Ines had never once asked anyone in this guild for anything. He paid his debts before they were owed. He kept his ledger cleaner than the clerks'. And a man like that, coming to a thief—

A man like that was already drowning.

* * *

The cistern house sat at the guild quarter's eastern edge, a squat stone building over a reservoir nobody used since the aqueduct rerouted forty years ago. Water still pooled down there, black and perfectly still, holding reflections of nothing. Wren liked it for meetings because sound died in it. You could scream in the cistern house and the stones would drink it.

She arrived first, obviously. She lit no lamp; she knew the room by heart, every drip-channel and rusted ladder rung. When Ines came down the stairs she heard him counting them under his breath—he always counted—and she waited until he reached the bottom before striking a single match and touching it to the stub of candle she'd left on the reservoir's edge.

The light bloomed small and yellow between them. Ines looked worse than he had in the rafters. There were shadows under his eyes like bruises, and his hands wouldn't quite stay still.

"You owe me a favor," he said.

"I owe you *a* favor," Wren agreed. "Singular. Carefully specified. You got me out of Corvane's audit four years ago by misfiling my name onto a shipping manifest bound for the coast. I have repaid that favor twice over—I got you the good ink when the quartermaster cut your allotment, and I looked the other way when you were copying charts after hours, which is technically guild theft, so don't pretend you're the innocent one here."

"I'm not pretending anything." He reached into his coat. "I need you to look at something and tell me what it is. Not what it looks like. What it *is*."

He unfolded the thing on the flat stone beside the candle, and Wren leaned over it, and for a long moment she forgot how to breathe.

It was a map. Of course it was a map—everything Ines touched was a map eventually. But she had seen soul-charts once before, years ago, in a smuggler's strongbox in the Saltmarket, and she had burned that box and everything in it and drunk herself stupid afterward, and she had sworn she would never look at another one as long as she lived.

This one was finer than the smuggler's. Finer and worse. It was drawn on skin—not leather, *skin*, pale and faintly luminous, and the lines of it moved. Slowly, like things swimming under ice. A mountain dominated the chart, rendered in strokes of silver-gray, and beneath the mountain, in chambers drawn with terrible precision, small lights drifted like fish in a dark pond. Each light had a name written beside it in a script that hurt to read directly, the way the sun hurts.

At the bottom of the chart, in a border of thorned script, ran a line of text. Wren made herself read it.

*Sold to the Hollow Court beneath Mount Vesper. Thirteen turns of the waning moon. Payment received in full.*

"Where did you get this?" Her voice came out flat. Flat was better than shaking.

"A courier. Last night. He was dying in the alley behind the guild—someone had opened him up like a letter. He put this in my hands and said 'thirteen turns' and then he was gone." Ines's hands had gone still now, pressed flat against his knees, the way people go still when they're holding themselves together by force. "I need to know what it is, Wren. Truly. Not what the guild's bestiaries say. What it actually is."

"You know what it is." She heard the crack in her own voice and hated it. "You're a cartographer. Look at the medium. Look at the script. You know."

"I know what the guild says. The guild says the Hollow Court is a story the mountain towns tell to keep children off the passes. The guild says soul-trade is rumor. The guild says a lot of things." He looked up at her, and in the candlelight his eyes were very tired and very frightened and absolutely unblinking. "Tell me what's true."

Wren looked at the map. At the little drifting lights with their names beside them.

And she thought about Tam.

* * *

Her brother had been the singer. That was the joke everyone made—that the thief's family produced one honest talent and it wasn't hers. Tam sang the way water runs downhill, effortlessly, constantly, whether anyone wanted it or not. He sang hauling crates. He sang in the bath. He sang on the long road north when they took the caravan work because winter was coming and rent wasn't going to pay itself.

He was nineteen when they took the high road past Vesper. Nineteen and singing, some nonsense ballad about a miller's daughter, his breath steaming in the cold, when the collectors came down out of the treeline.

They didn't look like monsters. That was the thing nobody told you. They looked like undertakers dressed for a wedding—pale gray coats, faces like paper, courteous in a way that made her skin crawl worse than any snarl would have. One of them held up a document, sealed in black wax, and read out Tam's name in a voice like a key turning in a lock.

"We don't owe anyone," Tam said. "We don't owe anyone anything. Check your book again."

"They checked," Wren said now, to the candle, to the black water, to the girl she'd been standing in the snow doing nothing, *nothing*, while her brother's voice came apart mid-note. "There was a debt. Some contract signed before either of us was born—our grandmother's gambling markers, sold and resold until they ended up in that court's ledger. They showed us the chain of title. Every signature legitimate. Every transfer lawful. They were very polite about it."

"What happened?"

"He walked up the mountain with them. They didn't drag him. That was the worst part—they didn't have to. Something in the document made him want to go. I watched it happen. I watched my brother turn around and start walking, and partway up the trail he started singing again, except it wasn't his song anymore. It was something old and slow and wrong, and he was smiling while he sang it." Her hands had curled into fists without her permission. "I followed as far as the tree line. The mountain didn't want me. Only him. Only what was owed."

The candle flame bent. Somewhere in the dark of the cistern, water dripped into black water, patient as a clock.

"That's a soul-chart," she said. "Hollow Court work. The skin it's drawn on belonged to whoever brokered the sale—the court takes the broker's hide as their fee. The moving lines are the terms of the contract, still alive, still working. And those lights—" She touched the edge of the chart, not quite letting her finger cross onto the skin itself. Some instincts you didn't argue with. "Those are souls. Held beneath the mountain. Each one named, each one owned, each one burning down slowly to keep the court fed. The mountain doesn't sleep, Ines. It's fed. Thirteen turns means the contract completes in thirteen turnings of the waning moon, and whatever soul this chart tracks gets collected in full."

Ines was quiet for a long moment.

"And can they be gotten back?"

The question hung in the wet air. Wren laughed—it came out of her sideways, jagged, nothing like a laugh should be.

"Gotten *back*. Do you hear yourself? People spend their whole lives not saying that mountain's name. Caravans go around. Armies go around. The guild has a whole drawer of documents about Vesper and every single one is filed under fiction. And you're asking if we can walk in and take one back."

"Not we." His voice was barely there. "Me. I'm asking what I do, Wren. Because this chart—" He stopped. Started again. She watched him wrestle with it, watch the words fight him like live things. "The courier knew my name. He was looking for me specifically. And when I look at this map too long, I—" His hand rose halfway to his chest, then dropped. "Something in it knows me. I can't explain it better than that."

Wren studied him. Six years of watching Ines Ashgrove be the most carefully invisible man in the guild, and here he was in a dead cistern by candlelight, asking her to believe the Hollow Court had sent a dying courier to find *him*.

She should tell him to burn it. That was the sane answer. Burn the chart, scatter the ashes in running water, take a posting to the southern offices, and never say the word Vesper again. That was what she'd done with the smuggler's box. That was the only answer that kept a person whole.

But Tam had been nineteen, and he'd sung all the way up the mountain, and somewhere under that stone there was a little drifting light with his name beside it, and every year on his birthday Wren got drunk and told herself she'd done everything she could.

Every year it sounded less true.

"How many turns left?" she asked.

"Twelve. Maybe eleven. I haven't been able to count the moon properly since—" He shook his head. "I need someone who reads lunar reckonings. Properly. Priest-trained."

"Priest-trained priests don't help thieves." She chewed the inside of her cheek. "But there's a defrocked moon-priest drinks at the Drowned Bell. Oskar. Got thrown out of the Lunar Order for asking too many questions about the mountain calendars—which, incidentally, is the only interesting thing about him, so don't expect charm." She pulled a knife from her boot and began cleaning her nails with it, a habit she fell into whenever her hands wanted to shake. "Here's what this is, Ines, so we're clear. This isn't a rescue mission. This isn't a quest. This is me looking at a soul-chart and confirming it's real, which I've done, and you owing me a second favor, which you now do. Whatever you decide to do with twelve turns of moonlight is your funeral."

"Understood."

"Because I'm not going up that mountain. I want that said plainly. I lost enough to Vesper already."

"Plainly said." He began folding the chart with the tenderness of a man handling a wound. "Thank you, Wren. For telling me the truth. I know it cost you."

"It didn't cost me anything." It had cost her everything, and they both knew it, and the lie sat between them like a third person at the table.

He stood, gathered himself, moved toward the stairs. Then he paused, half-turned, candlelight catching the side of his face.

"Why did you really agree to meet me?" he asked. "You could have ignored the note. You could have told Corvane I was acting strange—you'd have earned points with him. Why help at all?"

Wren turned the knife over in her fingers and considered lying, and found she was too tired.

"Because four years ago you misfiled my name to save me, and you never asked why I needed saving, and you never told anyone, and you never held it over me. Not once." She shrugged, rough, uncomfortable. "People in this guild keep accounts, Ines. You kept mine clean for free. So." She flicked the knife closed. "Ask Oskar at the Drowned Bell. Tell him Wren sent you and that it's about Vesper, and watch his face go gray. And Ines—"

"Yes?"

"When the collectors come—and they'll come, the courier's death will have been noticed, the court doesn't lose documents gracefully—don't run for the gates. Everyone runs for the gates. Come find me instead. I know ways out of this quarter that aren't written down anywhere." She smiled, thin and sharp. "Except maybe in your head."

He looked at her for a moment longer, and something passed across his face that she couldn't name—surprise, maybe, or the beginning of trust, that fragile first stitch of a seam that might hold or might tear.

Then he counted his way up the stairs, and was gone.

Wren sat alone in the dark with the candle burning low, and after a while she did something she hadn't done in nine years. She sang. Quietly, barely voiced, just a few bars of a nonsense ballad about a miller's daughter, into the black water where the stones drank the sound.

"Twelve turns," she said to the dark. "Damn you, Tam. Damn you both."

Above ground, the waning moon cleared the rooftops of the guild quarter, one notch smaller than the night before—and far to the east, at the mouth of an alley that appeared on no map, something pale and patient unfolded itself from the shadows and began, courteously, to search.
