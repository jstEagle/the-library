## 5. Collectors at Moonrise

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Ines had lived in the guild quarter long enough to know its rhythms the way he knew the grain of his own desk—delivery carts at dawn, ledger clerks at midmorning, the night-soil men before first bell, and nothing, *nothing*, between second bell and sunrise except the cats and the drunkards. It was half past second bell when someone rapped three times on Wren's door: soft, precise, patient. The kind of knock that did not need to be loud because it already knew it would be answered.

He was still in her garret, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the soul-map spread between them and a stub of candle burning low. They had been arguing for an hour about what to do with it—Wren wanted to burn it; Ines could not make himself say yes—and the argument had gone quiet in that way arguments do when both people are too tired to keep pretending they're winning.

The knock came again. Three times. Soft. Patient.

Wren's head came up like a hawk's. She didn't speak. She put one finger to her lips, rose without a sound—Ines would never stop marveling at how she did that, how a woman made of noise could become silence when she chose—and drifted to the window, easing back the shutter a finger's width.

Then Ines heard it too. Not from the stairs. From the street below.

Boots. Many boots, moving in step. Not the arrhythmic shuffle of the watch or the clatter of a late reveler, but a single shared cadence, deliberate as a metronome, filling the whole length of Candlemaker's Row. And beneath it, fainter, something worse: a sound like paper being unfolded. Everywhere. From every doorway. A dry whisper of vellum opening, as if the street itself were being read.

"Collectors," Wren breathed. She turned, and in the candlelight her face had gone the color of old tallow. "That's Hollow Court work. I told you—I *told* you—the map's marked. Anything touched by the court carries their seal under the ink, and they can feel it, like a fisherman feels the line go taut." Her eyes dropped to the parchment between Ines's knees. "And you've been carrying it through the quarter for two days."

"I wrapped it in oilcloth—"

"Oilcloth keeps out rain. Not *them*." She crossed the room in three strides and hauled him up by the collar, which was a thing no one had done to Ines Ashgrove since he was nine years old and small enough to hide behind the guild's atlas cabinets. "Window or stairs, cartographer. Choose fast."

The boots stopped outside.

* * *

Later—in the long gray aftermath, when he would have time to be afraid properly—Ines would try to reconstruct those first minutes and find them mostly missing, worn away like footprints on a tidal flat. What remained was fragments:

Wren blowing out the candle so fast the smoke hadn't time to rise.

The door downstairs—not Wren's door, the building's front door—opening without a creak, which was wrong, because Ines knew that door, had catalogued its complaints in his mind the way he catalogued everything, and it *always* creaked.

A voice in the street, level and courteous, saying words that slid through the shuttered windows like water through cloth: *"By order of the Court Beneath Vesper. All residents will remain within. All residents will present themselves for reading. This is not a request. This is a receipt being collected."*

And Wren's hand closing around his wrist, her mouth against his ear, whispering with terrible calm: "Roof. Now. Don't look down, don't touch the iron, and whatever you hear behind us—don't stop walking."

They went up through the trapdoor into the dark above the garret, where the guild quarter's rooftops lay under a sky scoured clean by wind, the thirteen moons hanging in their slow wheel—eleven visible tonight, two still below the horizon, counting down. The tiles were slick with frost. Ines's breath tore out of him in white rags. Below, through gaps between chimneys, he saw them come out of the alley mouths.

There were nine of them, though he counted twice because his mind refused the number the first time. They wore gray that was not cloth-gray but something more like the gray of things forgotten—coats that seemed to drink the moonlight rather than reflect it, faces pale and pleasant and utterly smooth, as if memory itself had been sanded off them. Each carried a ledger bound in skin the color of candle wax, and each stood with a collector's stillness, the stillness of things that do not breathe unless breathing is required.

One of them looked up.

Not around. *Up.* Directly at the roofline where Ines crouched, as if the frost on the tiles had whispered to it, as if his warmth had bled down through the clay and been tasted.

"Two signatures unaccounted," the collector said mildly, to no one, to everyone. "One thief. One clerk." It opened its wax-pale ledger. "We'll begin with the clerk. Clerks come quietly."

"They really don't," Wren said—which was absurd, because she'd said it barely above a breath, and yet the collector's head tilted as if it had heard perfectly—and then she was moving, pulling Ines across the rooftops by the wrist while the world behind them unfolded like a book.

That was the only way he could describe it afterward. The alley behind Wren's building *unfolded*. The shadows peeled up off the cobblestones and stood, and where there had been four collectors there were now more, stepping out of darkness that should not have held them, and the sound of vellum turning filled the whole quarter, page after page after page, as if the night were a ledger and they were reading it aloud to find the line where two names were written.

Ines ran. He was not built for running—he was built for desks, for lamplight, for the small careful life—but terror is an excellent tutor, and his legs learned quickly. Wren moved ahead of him like water finding its level, across the gap over Cooper's Lane, along the spine of the chandler's roof, down a rain-gutter she trusted and he did not, always choosing before he could see why, always right.

"The tannery," she hissed. "There's a way through—"

"No." The word came out of him before thought, pure reflex, the reflex of twenty years of maps. "Tannery yard's a dead end after the third vat—they bricked the arch last spring. There's a way *over*—the drying racks hold if you cross at the posts, not the middle—but past that you want the rope-walk, not the lane, because the lane floods and the floodwater's caustic this time of year—"

She stared at him. Even running, even hunted, she stared at him with an expression he'd never seen on her face before, an expression he would spend a long time learning to name.

"You've mapped all this," she said. "You've never left the quarter. You've never *gone* anywhere. But you've mapped it all."

"It's what I do," Ines said, wretchedly. "I map everything. I just don't—" He ducked a guy-wire. "I don't *touch* any of it."

"Well," said Wren, and grinned, wild and white in the moonlight, "tonight you're touching."

* * *

They lost the first pair of collectors at the tannery, crossing the drying racks with the reek of lime and cured hides rising around them like a miasma with opinions. Ines felt the boards flex under his weight and heard, somewhere below, the wet heavy sound of a collector simply walking through the middle of the racks as if physics were a courtesy it had declined. He did not look back. Wren had told him not to look back, and for once in his careful, cowardly, meticulously ordered life, Ines Ashgrove obeyed.

They lost the second pair at the rope-walk, where the long shed's interior was hung with cords thick as a man's wrist, a forest of taut lines in the dark. Wren slipped through them like a needle through cloth. Ines followed the route he had drawn in his head a hundred times from the window of the guild's map room—third post, fourth cord, duck, turn, duck again—and heard behind him the collectors moving with perfect confidence directly into the cords, because they navigated by ink and contract and claim, and a rope-walk at midnight belonged to no ledger but the ropemaker's, and the ropemaker was asleep.

It was Ines who realized that, later. That the collectors were not clumsy. They were *blind* here. The Hollow Court owned souls and streets and names written down in its wax-pale books, but the space between claims—the unmapped, unowned, unclaimed dark—that was foreign country to them. They could unfold shadow and walk through walls, but they walked it like strangers, feeling along the edges of things, reading the world by what had been written about it.

And Ines Ashgrove had spent twenty years writing the world down in the only ledger the court could never seize: the one behind his eyes.

* * *

The third pair nearly had them at the well-square, and it was there that Ines understood the true shape of the night.

The square sat at the quarter's heart, ringed by arcades, and there was no roof-route across it—the buildings were too far apart, the jumps too long, and Wren took one look at the gap and swore in three languages. Below, the collectors had begun going door to door. Not breaking doors. *Knocking.* Politely. And the doors were opening, because people always open doors when the knocking is polite enough, and Ines watched the lamplighter's wife step out into the cold in her shawl and stand very still while a collector turned her face toward the moons and read her, slowly, the way one reads a document one has all the time in the world for.

"We can't cross," Wren said. "Open ground. They'll see—"

"They won't." Ines heard himself say it and was distantly astonished at the voice, which was steady, which was certain, which sounded nothing like the man who apologized to furniture when he bumped into it. "Look at the arcade on the north side. Third bay. There's a maintenance stair to the cistern gallery—it's not on any plan, the builders put it in during the drought year and then pretended it didn't exist because cistern access was supposed to be public and the guilds bribed the council to wall it off. The wall's fake. It swings. I've known about it for six years."

"How could you possibly have known—"

"Because the mortar's the wrong color, Wren. Because the hinge-shadow falls wrong at noon. Because I *look*." He was already moving, down the arcade's outer edge, keeping to the strip of darkness between the pillars' shadows where the moonlight couldn't quite decide. "I look at everything. I've spent my whole life looking at everything and touching none of it, and I told myself that was safety, but it turns out—" He found the third bay. Found the seam in the mortar. Pressed, the way he had always imagined pressing it, six years of imagining compressed into one shove. The wall swung inward on oiled hinges with a sigh, exhaling cold air that smelled of stone and deep water. "—it turns out it was practice."

For a moment Wren just looked at him. Behind them, across the square, another door opened; another neighbor stepped out to be read. The collector's voice drifted over, gentle as snowfall: *"You are not in trouble. You are only being confirmed."*

"Ines," Wren said quietly. "You just saved our lives with a wall."

"I saved our lives with a *map*," he said. "The wall was just where I happened to file it."

* * *

The cistern gallery ran beneath the quarter like a secret kept by the city from itself—a vaulted corridor of black brick above black water, lit by nothing, navigable only by touch and memory. Ines had never walked it. He had only ever drawn it, reconstructed from dripsound and draft and the angle of light through grates, and now his feet found the path his pencil had found years ago, along the ledge, left at the pillar with the mason's mark, right where the ledge widened, and every step landed true, and every true step rang in him like a bell.

This was the thing he had never let himself know. He had called it caution, called it cowardice, called it the sensible invisibility of a man who traded in wonders he never touched. But walking the dark with death unfolding itself through the streets above, guided flawlessly by twenty years of untouched observation, Ines finally understood what Corvane Dusk had seen in him all those years ago, what the guild had actually bought when it took in a friendless boy with a habit of staring.

His cartography was not a record. It was a power. He had simply never claimed it, because claiming things meant being seen, and being seen meant being taken.

Well. He was being taken now, one way or another. The court's collectors wanted the map in his coat, and the guild's masters would soon want it too, and somewhere beneath Mount Vesper a stranger's soul waited with Ines's own signature on its sale-contract, and there was no version of the coming days in which Ines Ashgrove remained a man whom nothing happened to.

So he might as well happen to the world instead.

"There's a grate ahead," he murmured, fingers trailing the black brick. "Opens into the dyer's yard on Saffron Street. From there—"

"From there we run," Wren finished. "And then?"

Ines paused. Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone and city, he could just hear it: the patient knocking, door after door, and the soft turning of pages.

"Then we find the ninth turn," he said. "Oskar. The moon-priest. If the map counts down by moons, we need someone who reads moons. And Wren—" His hand found hers in the dark, which was the second most reckless thing he had done all night, and the one that frightened him more. "Thank you. For not leaving me on the roof."

"Don't thank me yet," she said, but her grip tightened. "The collectors lost us. They won't stay lost. Things like that don't lose, they *wait*." She pulled him onward, toward the grate and the gray promise of dawn beyond it. "And Ines? Whatever else happens—we're going to need to get you out of the quarter entirely. Out of the city. Because there's one place in this world they'll never think to search for a guild clerk."

"Where?"

"A road," said Wren, "that isn't on any of your maps."

Ines smiled into the dark, and it felt strange on his face, like a door swinging open.

"Wren," he said, "there is no road that isn't on my maps."

* * *

They came up through the grate into the last hour before dawn, into a Saffron Street washed thin and blue with early light, and for one whole minute it seemed the night might release them. The collectors' knocking had fallen silent. The moons were setting. Somewhere a baker was lighting his ovens, and the smell of bread—ordinary, mortal, wonderful bread—drifted down the street like an apology.

Then Ines saw the figure standing at the end of the lane.

Not gray. Not wax-pale. A woman in traveling clothes, unremarkable, forgettable, standing beside a hired coach with its lamps still lit—as if she had been waiting all night, and had enjoyed it. She held a folded paper in one hand, and even at fifty paces Ines knew it for what it was: a chart. A guild chart. Drawn in familiar ink, in a hand he saw every day of his working life.

She raised the paper slightly, so the light caught it, and called down the empty street in a voice like a key turning in a lock:

"Master Ashgrove. Guildmaster Dusk wonders if you might come home."

Beside him, Wren's hand slid to the knife at her hip. Ahead, the coach door swung open onto darkness far deeper than a coach should hold.

And Ines, cartographer of everything, touched nothing, known by no one, realized that the map he carried had just become the least dangerous thing about him.
