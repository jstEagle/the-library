## 6. The Ninth Turn

Oskar Vane had been counting the moon's turns for eleven years, and he had never once been wrong.

That was the cruelty of it. They had stripped him of his vestments, burned his almanac in the courtyard of the Lunar Seminary, and struck his name from the rolls of the Ninefold Choir — but they could not strip the arithmetic out of his skull. He woke each morning knowing exactly where the moon stood in her long descent, the way a man who has lost his tongue still knows the shape of every word he can no longer say. Four hundred and six days since his disgrace. Two thousand and some since he had last been permitted to touch a proper ephemeris. And tonight, standing at the cracked window of his rented room above a tannery that stank of lime and old sorrow, he knew — with the flat, unarguable certainty of a falling stone — that the ninth turn had begun.

Four turns remained.

He had known this night would come eventually. He had hoped, in the cowardly way of the defrocked, that it would come after he was dead. The moon-priests did not read stars for farmers and sailors; that was the public face of the craft, the harmless half. The true work was older and darker: the Ninefold Choir kept the calendar of Mount Vesper, and the mountain kept its own calendar of debts. Thirteen turning moons made one full circuit of the Hollow Court's ledger. And when the thirteenth turn closed, the Court collected.

Oskar had spent three years inside the seminary's inner sanctum before he understood what the great lunar orrery in its vaulted heart actually *was*. Not an instrument. A clock. Not a clock. A countdown. The brass moons wheeling in their slow concentric orbits were not modeling the sky; they were modeling a contract, and every soul sold beneath Mount Vesper wound another spring within it. The mountain did not merely endure, as the pilgrims believed. The mountain *fed*. It drank memory the way roots drink rain, and the Choir's sacred duty — the duty they dressed up in hymns and silver vestments — was to tell the Hollow Court precisely when the next harvest would ripen.

He had said so aloud, once, at nineteen years old, in a voice still soft with belief. He remembered the silence afterward more clearly than anything else from those years: forty faces turning toward him in the lamplight, and High Cantor Ilvane looking at him not with anger but with something far worse — pity, and behind the pity, fear.

*You are not wrong,* she had told him later, alone, her hands folded like a woman at a funeral. *You are only early. Come back to me when you have learned what early costs.*

He had not gone back. He had run instead, and running had cost him everything anyway, which he supposed was the lesson.

Now the ninth turn was rising over the tannery roofs, swollen and yellow as a bruise, and Oskar Vane stood at his window with a cup of cold barley tea going untouched in his hand, and he waited. Because on the ninth turn, if the pattern held — and it always held; that was the whole horror of patterns — someone would come to him. Someone always came. A mother whose son had signed something in a fever. A widow whose husband's letters had stopped mid-sentence, the ink trailing off into nothing. People who had begun to suspect what the mountain ate, and who had heard, through channels that should not have existed, that there was a disgraced priest in the tanner's quarter who could still read the count.

The knock came at the third hour past moonrise. Two knocks, then a pause, then one — the old seminary signal for *I know what you were*, which meant either a former brother of the Choir or someone very well informed, and both possibilities made his hands go cold around the teacup.

He opened the door on a thin man with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a man who had spent years practicing how not to be looked at, and beside him a woman with a thief's easy shoulders and a knife visible at her hip purely as a courtesy.

"You're Oskar Vane," the man said. Not a question.

"I'm no one," Oskar said, which was the correct answer, the safe answer, the answer he had given a dozen desperate visitors across eleven years. "You've mistaken my door."

"We haven't," the woman said. She held something out flat on her palm: a folded square of paper, and even folded, even at arm's length, Oskar felt the hair rise along his forearms. He knew that paper. Everyone in the Choir's inner ranks knew that paper — vellum cured in the dark, faintly warm to the touch, warm the way skin is warm. Soul-map stock. The Court wrote its contracts on it, and the paper remembered.

"Where did you get this?" His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"A dying courier gave it to us," the ink-stained man said. "His name was Merrin Stave. He carried it out of the mountain. He said — he said *thirteen turns*, and then he died in an alley holding my wrist."

Oskar looked at the man properly then, and saw what he had missed in the first glance: not just practiced invisibility but *specific* invisibility, the invisibility of someone who had once been seen very clearly by something terrible and had never recovered. And under it, faint as a watermark, something else. Something that made Oskar's stomach drop.

"Come inside," he heard himself say. "Both of you. Quickly. Before the moon clears the chimney line."

* * *

The room was small and smelled of tallow and dried herbs, and every surface that could hold a chart did: lunar tables pinned three deep to the walls, their margins crowded with Oskar's corrections, because the official ephemerides lied — had always lied, he understood now, had been lying since before his grandfather's grandfather took vows — and only his own hand-figured numbers could be trusted. The thief, whose name was Wren and who moved through his room the way water moves through a wreck, examined the charts with frank appreciation. The other one — Ashgrove, he'd said, Ines Ashgrove, cartographer — stood with his back to the wall and watched Oskar the way a man watches a surgeon.

Oskar unfolded the map on his table and put his hands flat on either side of it, and made himself look.

It was worse than he had feared. It was worse than the thing he had feared, which had been a routine contract: one soul, one signature, one name entered into the mountain's hunger. Contracts he could bear. He had spent eleven years telling himself the harvests were small, that the Court took only what was freely sold, that the arithmetic balanced.

But this was not a contract. This was a *survey*. The map showed a soul rendered in the Court's own cartography — a human self drawn as territory, rivers of memory, mountains of grief, borders marked and measured — and across its face ran annotations in the Queen's spidery silver script. *Incomplete,* the annotations said. *Escaped. Fragment retained. Subject viable for reclamation.* And at the bottom, where a contract would bear its signature, there was instead a date, written in the old Choir notation that only three living people could read.

"The courier wasn't carrying a sale," Oskar said quietly. "He was carrying a warrant. This is a claim against a soul that got away." He looked up at Ashgrove. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Souls don't escape the Hollow Court. In four hundred years of recorded harvests, in all the ledgers I was permitted to read before they burned them, there is no record of a soul escaping. Except this one. Except yours."

"My—" Ashgrove started, and stopped, and Oskar watched the man's whole careful architecture of not-knowing tremble.

"Not yours now," Oskar said. "Yours *before*. Whatever name you had before you were Ines Ashgrove, the Court holds a fragment of it under Mount Vesper, and it has sent collectors to reclaim the rest. That is what this map is. That is why they're hunting you. You are not a witness, Master Ashgrove. You are *inventory*."

The silence that followed had weight. Wren had gone very still by the window, and when Oskar glanced at her he saw her jaw set in a way that had nothing to do with Ashgrove and everything to do with a brother, though he didn't yet know about the brother. Grief has a posture. He had seen it often enough.

"And the turns," Wren said finally. "The courier said thirteen turns. What does that mean? Give it to us plainly."

Oskar crossed to the wall of charts and pulled down the largest one — his masterwork, the chart he worked on nightly and hid beneath the floorboards daily, the true calendar of Mount Vesper that the Choir had forbidden and falsified. Thirteen concentric rings, thirteen moons, and inked into each ring, the events of the last full circuit: dates, harvests, names redacted by his own hand out of mercy.

"Every thirteen turns, the Court completes a cycle," he said. "And at the close of a cycle, the mountain must be paid. Not a soul here or there, bought from the desperate — a *tithe*. Enough to keep the Queen anchored and the halls lit and the nobles feasting on borrowed memory for another century. The turns aren't days. Each turn is a phase of the moon, roughly seven nights, and each one marks a stage of preparation. By the twelfth turn the roads below are open. By the thirteenth, the gate stands wide, and whatever the Court has claimed during the cycle comes due." He tapped the map on the table. "This warrant was issued late in the cycle. Which means your escaped fragment isn't just inventory anymore, Master Ashgrove. It's part of the tithe. The Court intends to present it — and you, and everyone bound to it — at the closing."

"How many turns remain?" Ashgrove asked. His voice was steady. Oskar gave him credit for that.

Oskar went to the window. The moon had cleared the chimney line now, and he read her face the way other men read letters from home — the libration, the terminator's angle, the faint darkening at the eastern limb that no farmer would notice and no false ephemeris would admit to.

"Tonight begins the ninth," he said. "Four remain. Twenty-eight nights, give or take, before the vault seals and the gate closes and everything claimed in this cycle belongs to the mountain forever."

* * *

They argued, afterward, in the low urgent way of people planning something they may not survive. Oskar let them argue and listened, and while he listened he watched Ashgrove's hands.

The hands troubled him. A cartographer's hands, yes — ink in the creases, a callus on the middle finger — but there were older calluses underneath, faded, in patterns Oskar almost recognized. He had seen hands like that once before, in the inner sanctum, on the arms of the acolytes who served the orrery directly. Hands marked by brass. Hands that had turned the wheels.

"You've been inside the mountain," Oskar said, when Wren had stepped out to see whether the street was clean.

Ashgrove didn't answer. But he didn't perform confusion either, and after eleven years of reading liars and frightened truth-tellers across a table, Oskar had learned that the absence of performance was itself a kind of confession.

"You don't remember," Oskar pressed gently. "But your hands do. That's how it works, down there. They don't take the whole self — they couldn't; a soul stripped bare dies, and dead souls have no flavor. They take the center and leave the rim. You walk away with your skills, your habits, your fears. Everything except the part that knows why." He paused. "I read the harvest ledgers for three years, Master Ashgrove. There was a boy, decades ago. Sold by a guild that needed a favor from the Court — the Guild of Impossible Things has always paid its debts in the foulest coin. The boy's contract failed. The extraction broke halfway. The fragment stayed below and the rest of him walked out into the world with a hole in the middle of him shaped like himself. The entry was flagged for reclamation. It was the only flagged entry in four centuries." He met Ashgrove's eyes. "The entry had no name. Just a number. But the warrant we're looking at — the courier's map — carries the same registry mark."

Ashgrove sat down slowly, like a man whose legs had received news ahead of the rest of him.

"Why tell me this?" he asked at last. "You don't know me. You owe me nothing. You could close the door and let the turns run out and no one would ever trouble you again."

It was a fair question, and Oskar took his time answering it, because the honest answer was one he had not spoken aloud in eleven years and it tasted strange on his tongue, like bread after a long fast.

"Because I helped build the clock," he said. "Not knowingly — I was young, I was devout, I thought I was keeping time for God's sake and the farmers'. But the harvests happened on my numbers. Every soul the Court collected in my years at the seminary was collected *on schedule*, and the schedule was mine. I told myself I didn't know. Then I learned, and I ran, and I told myself running was enough. Eleven years, Master Ashgrove. Eleven years of answering my door to mothers and widows and giving them the only thing I had left, which was the truth about the dates." He looked at the map on the table, at the warm vellum, at the registry mark that meant a broken boy walking around with a hole in his middle. "Running is not enough. I have known it was not enough for eleven years, and tonight the ninth turn rose, and you knocked, and I am done pretending that counting the debt is different from owing it."

Wren came back in from the stair with frost on her shoulders and grim news on her face: two figures at the corner of Tanner's Row, pale-coated, patient, not even pretending not to watch the door. Collectors. The Court had traced the courier's route faster than Oskar had estimated, which meant his estimates were wrong, which meant the schedule was compressing, which meant—

"Twenty-eight nights," Oskar said, pulling his old traveling cloak from behind the loose brick where it had hung unworn for a decade, gathering his hidden charts, his corrected ephemerides, the one true calendar of Mount Vesper. His hands shook, and he noted the shaking with distant interest, the way he noted all data. Fear was information. Fear said: *this matters.*

"Less, if they're already here," Wren said.

"Then we go tonight." He snuffed the lamp. "There is a road beneath the world, and I know its milestones, and I know where the gate of thirteen moons opens onto the mountain's throat. I cannot fight collectors and I cannot lie to queens. But I can read the count, and I can tell you, at every mile, exactly how much time the dead have left us." He paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the two of them — the thief and the mapmaker, the broken soul and the woman who had lost someone to the same hungry dark. "Understand what you're asking of me, and what I'm giving. If we do this, there is no coming back to rooms above tanneries. There is no door to hide behind. The moment I walk out that door with you, I am a priest of the Ninefold Choir again, defrocked and damned and *counting*."

"Good," Wren said, with a thief's crooked smile that did not reach her eyes. "We were short a priest."

Ashgrove said nothing. But he picked up the soul-map from the table, folded it with a cartographer's precise economy, and held it out to Oskar — the warrant, the claim, the survey of his own stolen self — as if to say: *you read the numbers; you carry the proof.*

Oskar took it. The vellum was warm as a sleeping child, and through it, faintly, he felt the pulse of the mountain counting too.

Outside, the ninth turn rode high and yellow over the tannery roofs, and somewhere beneath the world, four hundred feet of stone and older things, a gate began, very slowly, to think about opening.
