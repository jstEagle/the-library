## 7. Corvane's Offer

The summons came at the hour when the guild's lamps burned lowest, when even the night-clerks had surrendered to their ledgers and the corridors smelled of cold tallow and older secrets. A boy in guild gray—no older than fourteen, with the flat eyes of someone trained not to see what he carried messages about—appeared at my door without knocking and said only: "The Master will see you now."

I had known this moment would come. I had counted on it, the way a man counts the days until a storm he cannot stop. Three nights since the courier died in my arms in the alley behind the counting-house. Three nights since I folded a dead man's soul-map into the false binding of *A Treatise on Estuarine Sediments* and told Wren, through the gap in her window shutter, *not yet*. Three nights of lying by omission to every clerk who asked after my health, every appraiser who noticed my hands shaking over the compass work.

But knowing the storm was coming did nothing for the way it felt when it arrived.

I followed the boy down the spiral stair, past the Hall of Small Wonders where the impossible things slept in their glass cases—the compass that pointed at regret, the lantern that burned only in rooms where someone had once been happy—and I found myself cataloging them out of habit, the way a drowning man counts his breaths. *Item: one compass, brass, needle weeping. Item: one lantern, glass clouded, wick long cold.* If Corvane Dusk was going to end my life in that office, I wanted at least to leave it knowing exactly what I'd be leaving.

His door stood open. It always stood open; that was part of the theater. Corvane believed that a man who hid behind closed doors had something to hide, and so he conducted his empire from a room anyone could walk into, trusting that no one ever would.

"Ines Ashgrove." He didn't look up from the ledger before him. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with silver in his beard and ink permanently staining the first knuckle of his right hand, and he had the particular stillness of a spider who has decided the fly is already wrapped. "Sit. Pour yourself something. You look like a man who hasn't slept."

"I haven't."

"Honesty. Good. We'll want plenty of it." He turned the ledger around so I could read it. My own name sat at the top of the page in his careful hand, and beneath it, an entry I recognized as guild shorthand for *pending inquiry*: *Ashgrove, I. — courier incident, alley of the counting-house, third night past. Deceased male, unknown affiliation. Ashgrove first on scene. Ashgrove last seen with deceased's effects.*

"You wrote a report," Corvane said. "Very thorough. Very you. The man collapsed, you went to him, he said nothing intelligible, he died, you sent for the watch. Is that accurate?"

"Every word."

"Mm." He closed the ledger with a soft slap, like a door shutting on a small animal. "Then you won't mind explaining why the watch surgeon found the body stripped of everything but his boots. No coin purse. No papers. No satchel. Nothing a courier would carry. And no map."

My heart performed a slow, deliberate stumble, and I made myself breathe through it. *Map everything, touch nothing.* Ten years of that rule, ten years of being the most forgettable man in a building full of people who trafficked in the unforgettable, and here was Corvane Dusk looking at me the way he looked at an artifact whose provenance didn't match its case.

"He was robbed," I said. "Before I reached him, perhaps."

"Perhaps." Corvane rose, crossed to the window that overlooked the guild quarter's rooftops, and stood with his hands clasped behind his back. From somewhere below came the clink of the night-watch changing shifts, the ordinary music of a city that didn't know what its own guild kept in its cellars. "Do you know how long you've been with us, Ines?"

"Nineteen years. Since I was eleven."

"Eleven years old, half-frozen, pulled off a grain barge with no name anyone could pronounce and no family anyone could find. And do you know who signed the paper that took you in?" He glanced back at me. "I did. Your intake document is the first thing I ever countersigned as master. I remember it because the clerk wanted to turn you away—we had no budget for another mouth—and I said, *this one has cartographer's hands.* Do you know what I meant?"

"That I drew well."

"That you held the pencil the way a man holds a knife he doesn't yet know he'll need." He came back toward the desk but didn't sit, looming instead, which I understood was intentional. "Nineteen years, Ines. I have watched you become the finest surveyor this guild has produced in a century. I have also watched you make yourself smaller every single year. Fewer commissions. Fewer appearances. You map wonders and touch none of them. You are a ghost in your own house, and I let you be, because a ghost is useful and ghosts ask for nothing." He leaned forward, palms flat on the desk. "But ghosts don't bleed in alleys holding dying couriers, and they don't lie to me in reports, and they don't—" his voice dropped, almost gentle, which was worse than shouting— "*they don't disappear for three nights running and come back smelling of the Underways.*"

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft. Or perhaps there was, and it came from the space between us, from the thing Corvane already knew and was only now deciding whether to say.

"The map," he said. "The Hollow Court's work. A soul-chart. I'm going to ask you once, as the man who signed your intake paper, and then I'm going to ask you again as the Master of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I would very much prefer you answer the first asking."

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about fear, since I have had occasion to study it the way I study coastlines: it does not feel like running. Running is easy. Fear feels like standing very still in a room with a man who raised you, doing arithmetic. *If I give him the map, the collectors stop hunting me. If I give him the map, whatever the court wants done with that stranger's soul gets done. If I give him the map, I am safe, and safe is the thing I built nineteen years of walls to be.*

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, a question I hadn't let myself ask until that moment: *why does the Master of the Guild of Impossible Things know exactly what a Hollow Court soul-chart looks like?*

"I don't have it," I said.

Corvane sighed, and it was the sigh of a man watching a promising investment burn. "Ines."

"I don't have it *here*," I amended, because I had decided three nights ago that if I was going to break my life into pieces, I would at least choose which pieces. "And before you ask again—I'm not giving it to you. Whatever it is. Whoever it belongs to."

"It belongs to a dead man."

"It belongs to someone the court bought," I said. "That chart isn't a record, Master. I've studied it. It's a *deed*. And deeds can be contested."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. He sat down at last, heavily, and for a moment the spider became just an old man with ink-stained fingers, and that transformation frightened me more than anything else he'd done.

"Contested," he repeated. "You'd contest a contract with the Hollow Court. With a pencil and a good hand and nineteen years of mapping things you never touched." He laughed, short and without humor. "Do you know what the court does to people who contest?"

"I imagine I'm about to find out either way."

"No," Corvane said. "You're not. Because I am offering you the only exit that exists, and you're going to take it, because you are not a brave man, Ines—you're a careful one, and careful men know when a door is closing." He opened a drawer and set something on the desk between us.

It was a document. Guild seal, court seal, both pressed side by side into black wax. I knew the format instantly—it was the same format as the deed folded inside my treatise, the same columns, the same clauses written in that beautiful, terrible script the court used for its purchases. But this one was old. The wax had gone amber with age. And the name on the seller's line—

The name on the seller's line was *Guild of Impossible Things, by the hand of Corvane Dusk, apprentice-master.*

The room got very far away. The lamp flame stood perfectly still. I heard my own pulse in my ears like surf.

"What is this," I said. Not a question. I couldn't make it a question. Questions invite answers, and I suddenly did not want the answer the way a man doesn't want to look down.

"You asked what the guild keeps," Corvane said quietly. "Everyone asks eventually. They think it's the compasses and the lanterns and the doors that open onto nowhere. Those are inventory. Those are *shop goods*." He tapped the old document. "What the guild keeps is relationships. The court buys souls, Ines—has bought them for centuries, under Vesper and a dozen other mountains before it. And for most of those centuries, the guild has been its broker. We find the desperate ones. The debtors, the dying, the parents with too many mouths. We bring them to the table, we witness the signature, and in exchange the court leaves our quarter alone and pays us a tithe in things like that compass downstairs. Every wonder in this building was bought with somebody's name. That is the trade this guild was founded on, and it is the trade that kept you fed and clothed and taught for nineteen years."

I was looking at the seller's line. I couldn't stop looking at it. "Whose soul," I managed. "On this one."

Corvane was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading coordinates he wished were wrong.

"A boy. Eleven years old. Half-frozen, pulled off a grain barge, no name anyone could pronounce." His eyes met mine. "The court paid a remarkable price for him. Remarkable. And the strangest part—the part I have never understood, in all these years—is that the transaction *incomplete*. The court's records show the purchase, the payment, the sealing. But when the collectors came to take delivery, the boy's soul wasn't whole. Something had already come loose. Some piece of him was missing, and the court refused partial goods. They've been waiting for the balance ever since. And the boy—" he spread his hands, helpless, an old man's gesture, a father's gesture, and I hated him for how well he wore it— "the boy grew up with gaps in him he learned not to notice. Grew up afraid to touch the things he mapped. Grew up wanting, more than anything in the world, to be invisible. Tell me, Ines. Does any of that sound familiar?"

I don't remember standing. I remember the wall against my shoulder, cool plaster, and my own breath coming in pieces, and the treatise sitting in my rooms three floors up with a dead man's soul folded inside it like a letter that had finally, after twenty years, arrived.

"They sold me," I said. "When I was a child. You sold me."

"We *saved* you," Corvane said, and the word cracked down the middle as he said it, and I saw—for one blinding instant—that he believed it, that he had spent twenty years believing it, that a man can build an entire life on a sentence like that and never once hear how it sounds. "You were starving on a barge. Nobody wanted you. The court offered more for you than the guild's annual budget, and I negotiated them down to a fraction and kept you anyway. Kept you *here*, where their collectors couldn't touch you, because the contract was flawed, because your soul was incomplete, because some piece of you slipped loose before the ink dried and they've been hunting that missing piece for two decades." He stood, and his shadow fell across the desk, across the old amber-sealed deed, across me. "And now a courier dies in an alley carrying the other half of your own soul in his satchel, and the court's collectors tear the quarter apart looking for it, and you—*you*—are planning to carry it straight to their mountain. To hand them the balance. To complete the sale."

"To steal it back," I said. "It was never theirs. It was never *sold*. I was a child."

"The law of the court doesn't recognize children. It recognizes signatures." He rounded the desk, and I braced, but he only stopped close, close enough that I could smell the ink and the lamp oil and, beneath it, something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in a cellar. "Which brings me to the offer. Listen carefully, because I will only make it once, and because despite everything, I am trying to save your life."

He laid his hand flat on the desk, next to the old deed.

"Give me the map. Tonight. I will deliver it to the court myself, settle the account, close the file that has been open for twenty years. In exchange: the guild's full protection. Ward-signs on your door, collectors turned away at our borders, your name struck from every list the court maintains. You keep your position, your rooms, your work. You go back to mapping wonders and touching none of them, and in five years this will be a story you barely remember." His voice softened to almost nothing. "Or keep it. Run for the mountain with your thief and your mad priest. And when the thirteenth moon turns and the vault seals with you inside it, I will write your death into the guild ledger myself, and I will grieve you, Ines, I promise you that. I will grieve you honestly. But I will not follow you."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere below us, in the Hall of Small Wonders, I swear the compass that points at regret began, very faintly, to spin.

I thought about the boy on the barge. I thought about the eleven-year-old with cartographer's hands, carried into this building half-dead, and the paper signed over his head while he shivered, and the price entered in a ledger in a hand I had trusted for nineteen years. I thought about every wonder in every case downstairs, and what each one had cost, and whose name was written on the underside of every velvet cushion in every display box, names I had never once thought to check because checking would have meant seeing, and seeing would have meant the end of the quiet, careful, invisible life I had mistaken for safety.

They hadn't raised me. They had *warehoused* me. The most valuable item in the collection, shelved in plain sight, waiting for the buyers to come collect the balance.

"You negotiated them down," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, level and cold, like a line drawn across empty country. "For me. What did you keep the difference in?"

Corvane didn't answer. He didn't have to. I had spent nineteen years reading the fine print of impossible things, and his silence was the finest print of all.

"No," I said.

"Ines—"

"No." I pushed off the wall and walked to the door, and my legs held, which surprised me. At the threshold I stopped, because there was one more thing, and I found I needed to say it—not for him, but for the boy on the barge, who had never been asked anything at all. "You signed me away when I was eleven years old. So understand that I'm not refusing you tonight. I'm refusing you for him. Every year of it."

I stepped into the corridor and pulled the door shut behind me, and through the wood, muffled, I heard Corvane Dusk say, to no one, in the voice of a man setting a seal into wax:

"So be it. Wake the assessors. And send word to the mountain—we have a buyer's grievance to report."

I was on the stairs before the words finished landing, taking them three at a time, toward my rooms, toward the treatise, toward Wren's window and the gap in the shutter and the single word I would press through it:

*Now.*
