## 8. Road Beneath the World

The Underways did not appear on any map the Guild kept, and that was precisely why Wren trusted them.

She went first down the rusted ladder behind the tannery, testing each rung with her weight before committing, because a thief who had survived twenty-two years in Vesper's underbelly knew better than to trust anything she hadn't touched herself. Below her, Ines descended with his charts pressed flat against his chest like a man carrying scripture through a storm, and behind him came Oskar, muttering something in old lunar cant that was either a prayer or a complaint. With Oskar it was usually both.

"Rung," Wren said.

"I see it," Ines said.

"You're looking at your map."

"I can do both."

He couldn't. She heard his boot slip, heard Oskar catch him by the collar with the unbothered reflex of a man who had spent decades catching falling drunks outside temple doors. Then they were all three of them standing in the dark at the bottom of the world's throat, and Wren struck a match, and the Underways opened around them like the inside of some enormous sleeping animal.

The tunnel ran wider than a temple nave, its ceiling lost somewhere above the reach of her light. The walls were brick older than the city—older than the *idea* of the city, maybe—stained with mineral bloom in colors that had no names above ground: bruise-violet, liver-dark, a green like drowned copper. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of a thing that had been dripping since before anyone was born to be annoyed by it.

"Your charts," Oskar said. It wasn't quite a question.

Ines unrolled one by matchlight, and Wren leaned in despite herself. She'd seen guild maps before, the official ones, all compass roses and confident ink. These were different. These were *honest*. The lines wobbled where the tunnel wobbled. Where Ines hadn't been, he'd written *unknown* in a small, careful hand instead of inventing something pretty—and there were whole regions of *unknown*, entire cathedral-sized darknesses he'd left blank rather than fill with lies.

"Nobody maps this place," Wren said. "It's not on the ledgers."

"That's why I mapped it." Ines rolled the chart up again, quick, almost embarrassed, as if she'd caught him doing something shameful. "Ten years. Sundays. When the Guild thought I was cataloguing drainage."

Wren stared at him. Ten years. A decade of Sundays spent alone in the dark, drawing tunnels no one would ever pay him for, no one would ever even *see*, because the seeing wasn't the point. The mapping was the point. She thought about saying something about that and decided against it. Some things you didn't poke at. Not yet.

"Which way?" Oskar asked.

"West gallery runs under the river," Ines said. "Three hours to the first dry chamber. After that—" he tapped the blank space on the map, "—we're in the unknown."

"Charming," said Wren, and took the lead.

* * *

They walked for hours, and the walking became its own kind of country.

Wren had grown up in places like this—not these exact tunnels, but their cousins, the smugglers' warrens under Saltwharf where she'd learned to pick locks before she could write her own name. She knew how to move underground: weight on the balls of the feet, breath shallow, ears doing half the work of eyes. What she'd forgotten, in the years since, was the silence. Not emptiness-silence but *pressure*-silence, the way the dark above them seemed to lean down and listen.

Oskar broke it around the second hour, because Oskar was constitutionally incapable of silence for longer than a quarter hour.

"The ninth turn ended at moonrise," he said. "We have four turns left. Four nights, if we're lucky, and the moon doesn't care about luck."

"And after four turns?" Wren asked, though she already dreaded the answer.

"After four turns, whatever soul that map describes is fully the Court's. Sealed. Signed. Filed away in whatever vault they keep their quiet things." He walked with his staff clicking ahead of him like a blind man's cane, though his eyes were fine; he just liked the sound, he'd said, of knowing what was coming. "The courier bought the boy those turns with his life. I'd hate to waste them."

*The boy.* Wren glanced back at Ines, who was walking with his head down, lips moving faintly as though reciting distances to himself. She still hadn't told him why she'd agreed to come. She'd let him believe it was the favor—the old debt from the night she'd stolen the Provost's signet ring and needed a map of the Provost's house faster than any fence could sell her one. That was true, as far as it went. Debts were true as far as they went. But debts weren't the whole of why she'd looked at that dying courier's blood on the cobbles and felt the floor tilt under her.

Her brother Tam had gone up the mountain eleven years ago. Everyone said it gently, the way you tell someone their sailor drowned: *the mountain took him*. As if Vesper were weather. As if it had moods. She'd been sixteen and furious and she had hated the mountain the way you hate a god, which is to say helplessly, from below, without recourse.

Now here was a map that said the mountain *ate people*, deliberately, with paperwork. And here was a soft-spoken cartographer who'd never stolen so much as an apple, willing to walk into its mouth.

"Left fork," Ines said quietly, without breaking stride. "There's a collapse fifty paces up the right one. Old, but the rubble's unstable."

"How do you know?" Oskar asked.

"I mapped it. Six years ago." A pause. "I hope it hasn't gotten worse."

They took the left fork. Fifty paces later, Wren heard the right-hand passage groan behind them—a long, settling sigh of stone, like the mountain shifting in its sleep—and decided not to think too hard about what would have happened if Ines Ashgrove had been the kind of man who invented pretty things for his maps.

* * *

The dry chamber announced itself by smell first: cold stone and old smoke, the ghost of other travelers. It was a natural vault off the west gallery, high enough that her matchlight couldn't find the ceiling, floored with fine pale sand that someone had clearly camped on before them—there were old fire-scars, a ring of blackened pebbles, a niche carved into the wall where generations of passers-through had left offerings. Wren counted them by matchlight: a clay bead, a bird skull, a child's shoe worn through at the heel, a brass button polished bright by handling.

"People leave things," Oskar said, following her gaze. "For luck. Or for grief. Sometimes both."

"What's the shoe for?"

"Grief, I'd guess." He set his staff against the wall and lowered himself down with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. "Or hope. Hard to say which, with shoes."

They built a fire small and shielded, because Wren insisted—in ground she didn't know, you burned low or you burned noticed—and ate hard bread and dried plums and a wedge of cheese that Oskar produced from his pack with the smugness of a magician. Ines sat across the flames with his charts spread around him, re-inking tomorrow's route by firelight, and Wren watched him work and felt the question rising in her like water in a well.

"Why do you have these?" she asked. "Really. Ten years of Sundays. Nobody paid you. Nobody knew. Why?"

Ines's pen stopped.

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the galleries, water dripped its patient count.

"Because nobody else would," he said finally. "And because—" He turned the pen over in his fingers. "You spend your whole life being careful, Wren. Careful is a full-time occupation. You get so good at not being seen that eventually you stop seeing yourself. Mapping the Underways was..." He searched for the word. "Proof. That I existed somewhere, even if only in ink."

Wren knew about invisible lives. She'd built hers out of locked windows and false names, out of being whoever each job required and no one otherwise. She understood the arithmetic of it: safety purchased in installments of self, until you weren't sure how much of yourself was left unpaid.

"My brother went up the mountain," she said.

The words came out flatter than she meant them to. Eleven years of not saying them had compressed them into something dense and heavy, and now they landed on the sand between the three of them like a dropped coin.

Ines looked up. Oskar went very still, the way priests go still, which is to say attentively.

"Tam," she said. "Tamasin Vale. He was nineteen. He used to sing while he worked—drove our mother mad, drove the neighbors mad, sang constantly, badly, joyfully. And then one autumn he got it into his head that the mountain was hiring. They always said the Court paid well. Good wages, room and board, and you only had to go up and never come down." Her laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Our mother begged him. I begged him. He laughed and said he'd send for us when he was rich. And he walked up the switchback road with his pack on his shoulder singing some tavern song, and that was the last anyone saw of him."

The fire cracked a knot. Sparks rose toward a ceiling none of them could see.

"No body. No letter. No wages sent home. Just—nothing. And everyone said *the mountain took him*, like it was a rockslide, like it was fate." She stared into the flames. "Eleven years I believed it was fate. And then a dying man hands Ines a piece of paper and it turns out my brother was probably *filed*. Catalogued. Put in a jar somewhere by creatures who think souls are currency." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "So when you asked me to come, Ines—when you called in that stupid favor—I said yes because of the debt. But I'm here because if there's even a chance my brother's soul is sitting in that vault, then I want to be the one who opens it. Even if all I can do is open it and let him out and never know if he knows I came."

Silence. The good kind, the kind that holds a thing carefully instead of crushing it.

Oskar moved first. He reached over and put his hand, briefly, on top of hers—dry, warm, steady, the hand of a man who had held a lot of other people's griefs professionally and had never once become numb to them.

"The Court keeps everything," he said softly. "That's the horror of it, and also—if you look at it sideways—the mercy. Nothing given to the Hollow is ever destroyed. Only kept. Your brother may be more himself in that vault than he has been in eleven years anywhere else."

"And if he isn't?" Wren asked. "If keeping is just another word for suffering?"

"Then we open the jars anyway," Ines said.

Wren turned. He'd spoken quietly, without drama, the way he said most things, but there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—or rather, something she had, and hadn't recognized: not the careful mask, not the invisible man. Someone underneath, looking out.

"It's what we came to do," he said. "All of them. Every jar. Not just mine—ours. Yours. Everyone the Court ever wrote down." He hesitated, and then, as if paying out rope hand over hand: "I don't remember my childhood. Did I ever tell you that? Before the Guild found me—before Corvane—I have nothing. No mother's face. No name I chose. Just gaps, and the Guild's paperwork filling them in. So when I say every jar, Wren, I'm not being generous. I'm being selfish. I think one of them might be mine."

The fire settled. Wren found, to her surprise, that her eyes were wet, and that she didn't particularly mind these two seeing it. That was new. That was dangerous. That was, she suspected, what people meant by *family*—not the blood kind, which she'd lost, but the chosen kind, which you built in dry chambers under dead cities out of shared bread and shared wounds.

"Well," she said, rough-voiced, "then we'd better all come back. I refuse to have done my crying in front of an audience for nothing."

Oskar smiled. "I'll make a litany of it. Three names, three returns. It's poor theology but excellent motivation."

They banked the fire and took watches. Wren took first, sitting with her back to the warm stone, knife loose in her hand, watching the two of them sleep—Ines curled around his satchel of charts like a child around a comfort, Oskar flat on his back with his staff across his knees even in sleep, ready to click his way toward whatever came.

And it was in that quiet, in the drip-count dark, that she heard it.

At first she thought it was water. Then she thought it was wind moving through the galleries, though there was no wind underground. Then she stopped making excuses, because the sound had *words* in it.

Singing. Faint and far and threading up through the stone from somewhere deep beneath them, a voice singing a tavern song she hadn't heard in eleven years, sung badly, sung joyfully, sung exactly the way her brother used to sing it when he thought no one was listening.

Wren sat very still. The knife trembled in her grip. Down the western gallery, past the fork, past the collapse, deeper than any line on Ines's honest maps, the song wound on—and then, unbearably, it paused, the way a singer pauses between verses, listening to hear if anyone is singing along.
