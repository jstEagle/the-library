## 9. The Market of Borrowed Names

The Underways did not have weather, but they had moods.

For three days the tunnels had been sullen—dripping, narrow, lit only by Oskar's moon-lantern and the phosphorescent veins of stone that pulsed like slow heartbeats in the walls. Then, without warning, the passage opened its throat and breathed warmth at us, and I smelled cinnamon, hot iron, tallow, and something sweeter underneath that I could not name and immediately distrusted.

"Ah," Oskar said, in the tone of a man greeting an old sin. "The Bazaar."

Wren appeared at my shoulder as if she'd been summoned by the smell of trouble, which, knowing her, she had been. "That's not on your map."

"No," I admitted. "It isn't."

I had mapped eleven hundred miles of the Underways in the years before any of this began—back when mapping was a way of touching the world without being touched by it. Every fork, every flooded gallery, every shrine to forgotten gods scratched into the rock by pilgrims who had also, apparently, gotten lost. But there was a blank space on my chart where this bazaar now stood, and blank spaces on my maps were never accidents. They were warnings I had given myself and then chosen to forget.

"You knew it was here," Wren said. Not a question.

"I knew something was here." I folded the chart away before she could see how badly my hand shook. "The guild has a file. *The Market of Borrowed Names.* It's classified under Impossibilities, Subsection: Economies That Should Not Function. Nobody who filed the report came back to answer questions about it."

"Cheerful," Wren said, and grinned, because Wren grinned at things that would make a sane person weep. "I love shopping."

Oskar adjusted his lantern so its pale light fell forward instead of back. He had not slept well since the ninth turn; none of us had, but he wore his sleeplessness differently, like a priest wearing sackcloth. "Understand what you're walking into," he said. "This is not a place where coin matters. Coin is a promise, and promises are cheap down here. What they trade in is *proof*. Proof that you are someone. And proof, once sold, does not come home."

We stood at the mouth of the tunnel while the warm air moved over us, carrying voices—hundreds of them, layered, haggling, singing, weeping with laughter—and beneath the voices, faint as a pulse under a wrist, a sound like pages turning.

Somewhere in the dark behind us, very far away, Mount Vesper waited for the tenth turn of the moon. We had four turns left. Maybe five, if Oskar's arithmetic was generous, and Oskar's arithmetic was never generous.

"Well," said Wren, cracking her knuckles. "After you, cartographer."

* * *

The Market of Borrowed Names filled a cavern so vast that the ceiling was a rumor. Stalactites hung down like chandeliers, and someone—or something—had hung lanterns from them, hundreds of lanterns of colored glass, so that the light came down in broken rainbows and made every face look like several faces at once.

The stalls themselves were the first impossible thing. They were not built; they grew, or perhaps they had always been there, root-cabinets of black wood sprouting shelves, and on the shelves sat the merchandise.

Names.

Not written names—I expected that, some ledger or scroll. Actual names, kept like birds. Little cages of woven silver holding murmurs. Bottles of green glass with syllables swimming inside them like minnows. A tray of rings, each one humming with somebody's grandmother's name. A woman at one stall wore a necklace of small brass bells, and when she turned her head they didn't ring—they *spoke*, whispering fragments of herself: *-mira, -mira, call me Mira, please, someone remember me—*

"Don't listen too closely," Oskar murmured. "They want to be bought. It's the cruelest thing about this place. Everything here is eager."

I kept my eyes on the ground and my hands inside my coat, where the soul-map lay against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Rule one, the rule I had lived by since I was old enough to have rules: map everything, touch nothing. I catalogued as we walked—the layout of the stalls in concentric rings, the exits (three visible, two implied), the guards (not human; too tall, faces smooth as eggs), the currency counters who sat at brass desks weighing intangibles on scales of bone. I mapped it all in the secret ink of memory, and the mapping steadied me, the way it always had. A thing observed cannot entirely own you.

But the Market noticed us anyway.

It noticed us the way deep water notices a dropped stone—a ripple moving outward through the crowd, heads turning, vendors lifting their wares higher, calling softer. Not shouting their prices. Whispering them.

"Fresh name, fresh name, barely worn—"

"Young man, young man, I can hear yours from here, it's a good one, it's a *heavy* one—"

"Cartographer! Oh, cartographer, we know what you carry, we can smell the paper—"

Wren's knife was out before I registered that the last voice had come from directly beside us. Its owner was a stall-keeper shaped like a question mark—all elbows and curiosity—with eyes the color of spilled wine. "Peace, little thief," it said pleasantly. "I don't steal. I *appraise*. Free of charge, for travelers bound for the mountain." Its gaze slid to my chest, to the shape of the map beneath my coat, and its smile widened into something genuinely delighted. "Oh, that's a lovely cargo. That's a *dangerous* cargo. You'll never get through the Gate of Thirteen Moons carrying that, you know. The collectors will take it off your corpse and resell it here by winter."

"Then we'll be sure to be gone by autumn," Wren said flatly.

"But you'll need passage," the appraiser said, and here its voice changed, dropped, became almost kind, which was infinitely worse. "And passage past the Ninth Ring costs more than you have. More than any of you have. Unless—" it leaned close, and its breath smelled of old libraries, "—unless one of you has something *real* to trade. Something with weight. A name, for instance. A whole name, unencumbered, never traded before. Do you know what a virgin name fetches in this market? Passage. Protection. Anything. *Everything.*"

"I'm flattered," I said, "but I'm attached to it."

"Attached." The appraiser tasted the word. "Yes. That's what they all say. And then they remember what it costs to be known—to be *findable*—and they come back. They always come back. My stall has a waiting list, cartographer. Half the people in this cavern sold their names to escape someone hunting them. The other half sold them to escape themselves." It spread its knuckly hands. "Erasure is the only mercy that's ever on sale."

I told myself I wasn't listening.

I told myself that all the way across the third ring of stalls, past a cage where a child's name sobbed quietly to itself, past a fountain that dispensed nicknames instead of water, past a fat merchant asleep atop a chest labeled *FORMERLY DUKE OF SOMEWHERE, CONTENTS GENUINE.*

But the words went in anyway, the way splinters go in—small, painless at first, working deeper with every heartbeat.

Because here was the truth I carried under my coat along with the map: I had spent my whole life practicing erasure. I had simply done it slowly, politely, without a receipt. Invisible Ines Ashgrove, the guild's quiet pen, the man who drew wonders he never touched. If the Market was offering me the finished version of the life I'd already half-built—no name, no trail, no one able to find me, no one able to *lose* me again—

Again. Where had that word come from?

I stopped walking.

"Ines?" Oskar said.

The fountain was nearby. I could hear it trickling, and in the trickle, faintly, names dissolving. And I thought: if I had no name, the Hollow Court could not hold a contract on me. Whatever was tangled in my past—whatever the dying courier's map meant by that signature, whatever Corvane Dusk suspected about my missing years—the ledger would simply have nowhere to write. No handle to grab. I would slip through the Queen's fingers like water through a net.

And Wren would be safe. Oskar would be safe. Because they hunted *me*, and I would be no one, and no one is the one thing in all the worlds that cannot be collected.

It would cost me nothing. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. I had never used my name for anything but signing maps no one read. I had hoarded my solitude like a miser, and now the Market was offering fair trade: the hoard for the lives of the only two people who had ever walked beside me instead of past me.

"Ines." Wren's voice again, closer now. Sharper.

There was a desk at the center of the fourth ring. Brass, bone-scaled, exactly as I'd catalogued. Behind it sat a clerk with a face like smoothed wax and a pen that wrote without ink. An empty scale waited on the counter, one pan up, one pan down, patient as a gallows.

I was three steps away before I felt Wren's hand close around my sleeve.

* * *

Later, I would try to reconstruct those last three steps and fail. Memory is a map drawn after the territory has burned. But I remember the clerk's smile, gentle as anesthesia. I remember the empty pan rising slightly, as if it could already smell what would be placed in the other. I remember thinking, with perfect calm: *this is fine, this is tidy, this solves everything,* and noticing, distantly, that the thought did not feel like mine—that it felt like the Market's, poured into me through the warm air, through the whispering stalls, through the soft turning of ten thousand invisible pages.

"Ines Ashgrove." Wren said my full name out loud, in front of everyone, which in this place was either a battle cry or a blasphemy.

Several stalls went silent. Somewhere, a bottle of green glass rattled hopefully on its shelf.

She stepped between me and the desk. She was half a head shorter than me and she looked up at me the way you look up at a cliff you're deciding whether to climb or blow up.

"That's mine," I said. My voice sounded far away. "My choice."

"Sure." She didn't move. "Say your name, then."

"What?"

"Say it. Right now. Out loud. Tell me who you are, and why selling yourself is such a great bargain, and I'll step aside." Her grip tightened on my sleeve. "Go on. Who are you?"

"I'm—" The clerk's pen hovered. The scale waited. The warm air pressed in, full of whispers: *sell, sell, be free, be nothing, be safe.* "I'm the one keeping you alive. I'm the map. Without me you're both lost down here, and if I'm no one, they can't—"

"*Who are you?*" Wren said again, quieter, and that was worse. Quiet Wren was the real Wren; the grinning was just armor, the same as my silence was mine. "You're the man who stayed up three nights redrawing the Underway charts so my boots wouldn't find the flooded galleries. You're the man who gave Oskar the driest bedroll without him noticing. You're the man who held the courier's head in the alley and promised him—we all heard you promise him—you'd finish it. Nobody named *nobody* ever made a promise like that." Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway. "You don't get to erase yourself. I found you. That makes you *mine* to lose, and I don't lose things."

The whispers rose around us, urgent now, offended: *sell, sell, be free—*

"Wren," I said.

"There it is." She let go of my sleeve and stepped back, arms crossed, chin up, eyes bright and furious and wet. "There's the name. Costs nothing to say. Try it again sometime."

The clerk's smile had faded to something almost human, almost pitying. "Sentiment," it said, "is the most expensive commodity in this market. Are you certain you won't reconsider?"

"He's certain," Wren said.

I looked at the scale. At the empty pan, waiting to weigh a life. And I understood, finally, what the appraiser had shown me without meaning to: that I had been selling pieces of myself my entire life—not to this market, but to my own fear, one scrap of visibility at a time, until almost nothing remained but a signature on unread maps. The Market hadn't tempted me with anything new. It had simply offered to complete the transaction.

"No," I said, and my voice came back to me from somewhere deeper than usual. "No sale."

The clerk sighed, and the pen lowered, and somewhere in the cavern a thousand caged names exhaled in disappointment.

* * *

We bought passage honestly, or as honestly as anything works in the Market of Borrowed Names. Oskar, it turned out, still carried relics from his priesthood—consecrated moonlight sealed in glass, worthless above ground, priceless here, where proof of divinity was the rarest identity of all. Three vials bought us a guide's oath to the Ninth Ring and a boat on the underground river that ran cold and black beneath the bazaar, going where the mountain went.

The ferryman took payment without counting it and asked no names, which in this place was courtesy, not indifference.

We stood at the rail as the Market receded behind us, its rainbow light shrinking to a smear of stained glass in the dark, its whispers fading last of all—*come back, come back, everyone comes back*—until even that was swallowed by the sound of the river.

Wren leaned on the rail beside me. For a long time neither of us spoke. The water made its arguments against the hull.

"You stopped me," I said finally. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She was watching the dark ahead, where the river bent toward a glow that wasn't a glow so much as an absence of dark—pale, sourceless, wrong. "You wanted to disappear, Ines. For about ten steps back there, you *wanted* it. That doesn't just go away because I yelled at you."

"I know."

"And the next time it comes for you, I might not be standing close enough."

"I know," I said again.

She nodded slowly, satisfied, and bumped her shoulder against mine—an entire conversation, in Wren's language, about staying close enough.

Ahead, the pale light strengthened. Oskar came to stand at the bow, lantern raised, and I saw his face change as the light touched it—not fear exactly. Recognition. He crossed himself in the old lunar fashion, thumb to brow, thumb to heart, a gesture I'd seen him make only twice before: once at the ninth turn, and once over the courier's body.

"What is it?" I asked. "What's down there?"

"The river runs through the deep galleries," Oskar said quietly. "Past the places where the sold souls are kept. The echoes, we called them, in the old days. The ones the mountain couldn't fully digest." He looked back at me, and in the strange light his eyes seemed to hold their own small moons. "They sing, Ines. And they remember names better than the living do. Be ready to hear yours—from mouths that have no right to know it."

The river carried us forward into the pale.

And out of the darkness ahead, faint and clear and horribly young, a voice began to sing a lullaby I had not heard since before I could remember hearing it.
