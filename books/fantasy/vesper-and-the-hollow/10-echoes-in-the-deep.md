## 10. Echoes in the Deep

Oskar had spent nine years learning to listen to silence.

It was the first thing they taught a moon-priest at the Seminary of the Turning Veil: before you read the sky, learn what it sounds like when it says nothing. Silence had a texture. The silence of a temple at dawn was wool. The silence after a funeral was glass. And the silence of the deep places—the silence Oskar had been trained for and had fled from—was teeth.

He was thinking about teeth when he heard his name come out of the dark.

Not *Oskar*. Not even his old name, the one he'd burned with his vestments on the night he walked out of the Ninth Temple with the ledgers under his arm and the law at his back. This was something older, worn smooth as river stone, a name his mother might have used when he was small enough to be carried. It came from the left-hand tunnel, from a darkness so complete that his lantern seemed to make it darker by contrast, and it came in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

*Come home, little turner. The moons miss you.*

Oskar stopped walking. Ahead of him, Ines Ashgrove's lantern-light wavered—Ines had heard it too—and Wren's hand went to the knife at her hip with the ease of long habit.

"Don't answer," Wren said quietly.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to." She glanced back at him, and in the lantern glow her face was all sharp planes and watchful eyes. "You do that thing with your mouth. Priests do it. You start composing the reply."

Oskar closed his mouth. He hadn't realized he'd opened it.

They stood in a junction of the Underways where five tunnels met like the spokes of a wheel, and the ceiling arched above them into blackness that their light could not reach. Ines had his map out—not the soul-map, which stayed folded against his chest in its oilcloth wrapping, but one of his own, drawn in the careful hand that Oskar had come to think of as the steadiest thing in the world. Ink lines on good paper. Tunnels measured in paces, marked with water sources and collapse-risks and small annotations in a cramped script: *loose scree, third bend; echo-pool, do not drink; here the dark listens.*

That last one Oskar had taken for poetry when he first saw it. Three days into the Underways, he no longer did.

"Which way?" Wren asked.

Ines turned the map a quarter-rotation, checking it against the junction, against some interior compass of his own. "Left fork climbs toward the Second Gallery. Right goes down to the flooded levels. Straight—" He paused. His thumb rested on a stretch of blank paper where no line had yet been drawn. "Straight is unmapped."

"Then we don't go straight," Wren said.

"No," Ines agreed. But he kept looking at the blank space, and Oskar watched him look, and understood that for a man like Ines Ashgrove, an unmapped passage was not merely a danger. It was an insult. A hole in the world. And holes, Oskar had learned in his years among the moon-counters, were where things got in.

The voice came again, and this time it did not use Oskar's name.

It used Ines's.

*"Tam."*

One word. Soft as ash falling. And Oskar saw—in the way the lantern light trembled, in the way Ines's whole body went still with a stillness different from caution—that the word had landed somewhere deep and old and unguarded.

"What did it say?" Wren demanded. "What did it call you?"

"Nothing." Ines's voice was level. Too level. The levelness of a man standing on ice and listening to it talk. "It said nothing. Move. Left fork, quickly, before the echoes find our rhythm."

But Oskar had spent nine years reading the faces of penitents who came to the temples to sell their griefs by weight, and he knew a lie when it was wearing someone else's mouth. He said nothing. There would be time. There was always time in the dark; that was the trouble with the dark.

They took the left fork, climbing through a passage ribbed with pale stone like the throat of some enormous buried creature, and behind them, faint and patient, the darkness repeated the name it should not have known.

*Tam. Tam. Tam.*

Like a bell that would not finish ringing.

* * *

They made camp four hours later in a gallery where the stone opened into a rough dome and a thread of true water fell from a crack in the ceiling, cold and mineral-bright. Ines insisted on the water. He had a cartographer's memory for every spring he'd ever charted, and this one he'd mapped eleven years ago on a survey he claimed never to have taken. Oskar noticed these contradictions now. He collected them the way he'd once collected lunar omens: small signs that a larger pattern was turning beneath the surface of a life.

Wren took first watch, though Oskar suspected she never truly slept down here. Ines sat with his back to the wall, the soul-map's oilcloth bundle visible against his chest like a second heartbeat, and began to redraw the day's route from memory, because Ines redrew everything from memory, twice, as if paper could be made to hold what flesh could not.

Oskar built the fire small. Down here you built fires small, or you built them not at all, because light drew things and heat drew other things, and the Underways had been hollowed out by the Hollow Court's miners for longer than any living guild record admitted. He fed the flame a pinch of consecrated salt as he lit it—an old habit, half superstition, half science. Salt remembered being part of something whole. It made the fire burn a cleaner color, easier on the eyes, harder on the things that watched.

Then he sat across from Ines and said, "Tell me about Tam."

The pen stopped moving. For three full seconds the only sound was water falling and fire breathing.

"There is no Tam," Ines said.

"The dark disagrees."

"The dark says many things. That's rather the point of it." Ines looked up, and his face in the firelight was composed, courteous, closed—a door painted onto a wall. "You've been down here too long, priest. Start hearing names in echoes and next you'll be hearing sermons."

"I heard sermons for twenty years. I can distinguish them from ghosts." Oskar leaned forward. "I'm not your enemy, Ines. I'm not Corvane Dusk, sniffing at your pockets. I'm not the Court, hungry for what you carry. I am a man who read the lunar tables for the Hollow Court's bargain for six years before I understood what I was reading, and who has spent nine years since trying to un-read them. When I say the dark knows you, I am telling you as a professional."

Something moved behind Ines's eyes—quick, submerged, gone. A fish under winter ice.

"It called me a name I don't know," Ines said finally. "That's all. Echoes do that. They take sounds from travelers and give them back wrong. Every mapmaker knows it."

"Yes," Oskar said gently. "We do."

He let it lie there between them. He was good at letting things lie; it was a priest's skill, and it survived defrocking. Across the gallery, Wren sat with her knees drawn up and her knife across her thighs, watching them both with the expression of a woman counting exits. She had told him once, on the second night, about her brother—about the debt, the collector, the door that opened onto the mountain instead of the street. She had told it flatly, without tears, the way people tell things they have told themselves ten thousand times in the dark. But her hand had found her knife halfway through and stayed there, and Oskar had understood that the story was not finished being survived.

Now she said, "The echoes have been getting worse since the flooded levels. Closer together. More specific." Her eyes flicked to Ines. "More personal."

"They're following us," Oskar agreed. "Or rather—they're *reaching* for us. There's a difference. The Court doesn't send collectors into the deep galleries if it can help it; the collectors hate the echoes as much as we do. What you're hearing is residue. Runoff. Souls that leaked on the way down to the vaults, souls that were sold badly, souls that broke in transit." He held his hands out to the small fire. "A mountain full of stolen selves doesn't sit quietly, friends. It seeps. And seepage remembers."

"Remembers what?"

"Everything it touched." Oskar looked at Ines. "Everyone it belonged to."

The pen had not resumed moving. Ines stared at the half-drawn line on his page as though it were a rope thrown to a drowning man just beyond reach.

"In the temple," Oskar continued, quieter now, "we had a rite for the dying. We'd speak their name into their ear at the last, so the soul would know itself when it crossed over. Names are anchors, Ines. Not metaphor—mechanism. The Court's entire economy runs on that mechanism. They buy a soul by buying its name; the contract *is* the binding. Which means—" and here he made himself say it plainly, because kindness delayed was cruelty compounded— "which means that whatever that echo called you, it wasn't guessing. Residue only remembers what it actually touched."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the stone, water found water.

"I was raised by the Guild," Ines said at last. Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Foundling intake, autumn cohort, year of the Pale Comet. I have the papers. I've seen them."

"Papers can be written," Wren said. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way she said most things.

"So can maps," Ines shot back, and there it was—the crack, sudden and bright, a flash of something raw underneath all that composure. "So can ledgers. So can lunar tables, priest, and confessions, and brotherhoods, and every other comforting document people build their lives inside of. Paper says what paper is paid to say. I learned that before I learned my letters."

Silence. Wren's eyebrows had risen slightly. Even Oskar, who had known the man barely two weeks, understood he had just been shown something rare: Ines Ashgrove angry. Or afraid. With Ines, Oskar was beginning to suspect, the two ran in the same channel, like floodwater.

"I apologize," Oskar said. "That was clumsily done."

Ines exhaled slowly. The anger drained out of him the way it had come—all at once, leaving behind that terrible steadiness. "No. You're right to ask. If the Court has a claim on me, you both deserve to know it before it comes due." He looked down at his hands, ink-stained, steady, strangers to himself. "The problem is that I don't know it. I have a childhood the way you have a room described to you by someone who visited it once. Guild dormitory. Grey porridge. A matron who smelled of camphor. I remember lessons, and the feel of chalk, and the exact sound the archive doors make. I remember everything," he said, and his voice did something almost like wonder, almost like horror, "except anything that happened before the Guild found me. There's just—blankness. Smooth as fresh paper. I used to think everyone's beginning felt like that."

"And now?" Wren asked.

"Now I think beginnings don't feel like anything. Because you're *in* them." He tapped the oilcloth bundle over his heart. "And I think that whatever this map is, whatever the courier died carrying—it isn't a stranger's soul. It never was. I've known it since the alley. I've known it the way you know a face in a crowd that you have no business knowing."

Oskar nodded slowly. "The echo called you by a child's name. That suggests the residue knew you as a child. Which suggests you were near the Court's workings as a child. Which suggests—" He stopped himself. Some conclusions should be walked to, not pushed.

"Say it," Ines said. "You've earned the right to be blunt. You gave up a god for less."

Oskar smiled thinly at that. It still astonished him, how easily these two spoke of his ruin—as if it were a coat he'd simply taken off. Perhaps it was. "It suggests you weren't found by the Guild, Ines. You were *delivered*. Sold children pass through intermediaries; the Court uses brokers, and brokers use frontages, and a foundling intake in a guild quarter is exactly the kind of frontage that lets a purchase walk away clean. Someone bought you. Someone sold you. And somewhere between the sale and the delivery, something went wrong—because the Court does not lose property, and yet here you are, free, walking, unnamed in their ledger but named in the mouths of their dead."

"That's a theory."

"That's a reading of the tables," Oskar said. "Thirteen turns, the courier said. Thirteen is not a number the Court uses lightly. It's the number of the anchoring rite—the rite that binds a soul into the mountain's root-stone. The turns aren't a countdown to your friend's soul being consumed, Ines. They're a countdown to a *completion*. Something was sold that was never fully delivered. Half a soul makes a broken anchor. And the Court has spent however many years looking for the missing piece."

The fire had burned low. None of them moved to feed it.

"And the missing piece," Wren said quietly, "is walking around with a map against his chest and no memory of why it fits."

* * *

Sleep did not come for Oskar, which was usual, and did not come for Wren, which was habit, and came for Ines only in shallow, saltless doses, which was new. Oskar lay on his bedroll watching the ember-light breathe on the dome of the ceiling and listened to the Underways listen back.

He had heard echoes before. Everyone who worked the deep roads had. But there was a taxonomy to them, and he had never taught it to anyone outside the seminary, and he found himself teaching it now, silently, to keep his own fear organized:

There were dumb echoes, which were only stone repeating stone. There were mimic echoes, which repeated voices, sometimes days old, sometimes decades. There were lure echoes, which imitated the specific voices you loved best, and those you learned to walk past with your jaw clenched and your heart nailed shut.

And then there were the true ones. The residue. The leaked and broken souls that had soaked into the stone like wine into bread, and that did not imitate anything, because they were not performing. They were simply remembering, out loud, forever, the lives that had been taken from them.

The voice in the junction had been a true one. Oskar was certain of it. Mimics flattered; lures seduced; residue only grieved. And it had grieved a child's name at a grown man with ink on his fingers, and the grief in it had been the specific grief of something that had once been *responsible* for him. A nurse's cadence. A mother's, perhaps. Or—

Or a seller's. Oskar had read enough contracts to know that the worst bindings wore the softest voices.

Near midnight—or what he judged to be midnight, down here where midnight was a decision rather than a fact—Ines cried out in his sleep.

It was a small sound. Strangled early, the way people strangle such sounds when they have spent a lifetime practicing. But the Underways were made for carrying small sounds, and the gallery took it up and passed it outward, tunnel to tunnel, and for a moment the whole deep world around them seemed to lean in and listen.

And then, from very far away—from everywhere—came the answer.

Dozens of voices. Faint as breath on a window. Not mimics, not lures. Residue, waking in the stone like fish rising to a dropped crumb, all of them murmuring the same syllables, passing the name along the way a wave passes along a shore:

*Tam. Tam. Tam-as. Tamash.*

And underneath it, woven through it, older and colder, a voice that did not grieve at all—a voice with the smooth patience of a contract awaiting signature—said:

*We kept your half safe, little one. Come and see.*

Oskar sat up. Across the dead fire, Ines's eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling, wide and black and full of a vertigo that Oskar recognized from the mirror of his own worst nights: the vertigo of a man discovering that the floor of his self was thinner than he'd believed, and that beneath it there was another floor, and beneath that, a depth with no bottom drawn on any chart.

"I remember water," Ines whispered. He didn't seem to know he was speaking aloud. "Stone steps going down into water. And someone holding my hand too tight. And a bell—" His breath caught. "Priest. Why do I remember a bell?"

Oskar reached across the ashes and gripped the man's shoulder, because some anchors you had to be for each other when the paper failed.

"Because you were there," he said. "Whatever else is true—you were there. And tomorrow we keep walking, and we walk *toward* it, because the only way out of a half-life is through the place that cut you in two."

Above them, miles of stone and older dark, the thirteenth moon turned another degree toward its fullness. And in the tunnels all around their small brave fire, the dead went on whispering the name of a boy who had been sold, and had escaped, and had forgotten—and who was now, at last, beginning to remember.
