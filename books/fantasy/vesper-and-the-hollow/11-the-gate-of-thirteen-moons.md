## 11. The Gate of Thirteen Moons

The mountain announced itself long before we saw it.

For three days we had walked through a country where the light came from below—seams of pale gold running through the rock like veins in marble, so that Oskar's shadow fell upward and mine fell nowhere at all. Wren said it made her feel like a moth pinned to the underside of the world. I said nothing, because I was mapping.

That is what I do. That is what I have always done. When fear comes, I take out my kit and I draw, because a line on paper is a thing I can hold, and holding things has kept me alive longer than courage ever did.

So I mapped the upward shadows. I mapped the way the gold seams thickened as we climbed, braiding together like rivers gathering toward a sea. I mapped the silence, which was not empty but *attentive*, the way a room is attentive when someone stands behind the door listening for you to stop talking.

And then the tunnel opened its mouth, and there it was: Mount Vesper, seen from beneath, and the gate of thirteen moons.

* * *

It should not have been beautiful. Everything I knew about the place—the courier's blood drying on my hands, Oskar's whispered accounts of souls fed into stone like coal into a furnace—should have made me see only a wound in the world. But I am a cartographer before I am anything else, and beauty found me anyway, the way it always does, uninvited and slightly ashamed of itself.

The gate stood at the head of a valley that had no business existing this far underground. Black stone rose on either side in cliffs smooth as poured glass, and set into those cliffs were thirteen discs of moonstone, each the size of a millwheel, arranged in an ascending spiral toward a doorway tall enough to swallow a cathedral. Twelve of the discs were dark. The thirteenth, highest and nearest the door, glowed with a light like milk held up to a candle—and even as I watched, that light began to move, to *turn*, slow as the hour hand of a clock, and I understood with a lurch that I was watching the twelfth turn begin.

"Four turns left when we started," Oskar murmured beside me. He had his prayer-knife out, though he hadn't prayed since the Underways took his faith apart piece by piece. "One spent already. We're late."

"We're not late," Wren said. "We're exactly on time to be terrified. There's a difference." She was crouched at the edge of the valley's mouth, reading the ground the way she read locks—with her fingers, patiently, looking for the trap in everything. "No guards. You notice that? A gate like this, and nobody standing watch."

"That's because the gate doesn't need watching," Oskar said. "It needs *feeding*. Look at the threshold."

I looked. At first the doorway seemed merely deep—a corridor of darkness receding into the mountain's heart. Then my eyes adjusted, the way they adjust to a map drawn in fading ink, and I saw what he meant. The floor of the passage was strewn with things. Cups. Shoes. A child's wooden bird. Rings. Letters, hundreds of letters, their wax seals split like little mouths. All the small possessions people leave behind when they stop being people who own things.

"They don't guard the door," I heard myself say. My voice sounded very far away, as if it belonged to someone else whose map I was reading. "They let you carry your own surrender in. Anything you can't bear to lose, you set down at the threshold, and the mountain keeps it. And then—" I stopped.

"And then you're lighter," Wren finished softly. "Light enough to walk in and never come back out." She spat, delicately, into the dark. "My brother would have left his boots. He loved those stupid boots more than he loved anyone."

We stood a long moment in the upward-shining gloom, three small figures at the foot of a spiral of dead moons, and above us the twelfth disc turned, and turned, and turned.

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened next, because honesty is the one luxury I denied myself for thirty years and I find I cannot stop spending it now.

I wanted to turn around.

Not the way a coward wants to—not the hot, animal flinch. This was colder than that. This was arithmetic. I stood at the gate of the Hollow Court with one turn of the moon remaining after this one, carrying a soul-map folded against my heart, and I did the sums every traveler does at the border of a country that kills people, and the sums said: *turn back*. The sums said: you are a man who has survived by being no one, by touching nothing, by drawing doors on paper instead of walking through them, and here is a door you could simply not walk through, and no one would ever know, because knowing things is precisely what you have spent your life avoiding.

The map against my chest was warm. It had been warm since the alley, since the courier's hand closed over mine and he said *thirteen turns* with his last breath, and I had told myself the warmth was fever, or candle-heat, or imagination. But maps don't lie about temperature any more than they lie about distance, and the truth was that the stranger's soul knew where it was going, and it was pulling me like a tide.

"Ines." Wren touched my elbow. She had learned, somewhere in the weeks beneath the world, that I go deaf when I'm calculating—that the numbers build a wall around me and voices can't get through. She didn't shake me. She just put her hand where I could feel it and waited until the wall cracked. "Whatever you're adding up, add this too: I'm not leaving without you, and Oskar walks slower than either of us, so if you run, we'll be annoying about it for years."

"I wasn't going to run," I said.

"You were going to stand here until the moon finished turning," she said, "and then claim the decision had been taken out of your hands. I've watched you do it with smaller things. Which tavern to sleep in. Whether to tell me your middle name." A pause. "You still haven't told me your middle name."

"It's Ashgrove," I said. "Ines Ashgrove Ashgrove. My parents were not imaginative."

She laughed—the short, sharp laugh she deploys like a knife between ribs—and the sound broke the arithmetic open, and I could breathe again, and I hated how easily she could do that, and I loved it, and both of those facts went into the map I was building of myself, the one I'd been drafting since the alley, the one with no legend yet because I still didn't know what any of my symbols meant.

We started down the valley.

* * *

The collector was waiting for us at the third moonstone.

I say *waiting*, but that implies anticipation, and what I felt from him was worse: patience. He stood beneath the third dark disc with his hands folded, wearing a coat the color of wet slate, and he had no face—or rather, he had a face the way a house has a face, features arranged where features go, but nothing behind them doing the work of being alive. The Hollow Court's collectors. I had seen one once, through a guild window, across a crowded square, and had told myself I imagined the wrongness. I had not imagined it.

Wren's knives were out before I finished blinking. Oskar raised his prayer-knife and began, under his breath, a rite of warding that the moon-priests used against eclipses—which tells you something about what the Court was to them. An eclipse. A thing that eats light and calls it holy.

But the collector looked only at me.

"Ines Ashgrove," he said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a clerk, a schoolmaster, a man who sells insurance against disasters he has personally arranged. "Cartographer, second class. Twenty-two years of service to the Guild of Impossible Things. Commended twice for discretion. Never commended for anything else." He tilted his head, and the gesture was almost human, which made it obscene. "You've been difficult to find. Not because you hide well. Because you barely exist. Do you know how hard it is to serve papers on a man who has spent three decades making himself a rumor?"

"I'm here," I said. "Apparently I exist enough."

"Oh, you exist *magnificently* now. The moment you took that map, you became the most documented man in three kingdoms. The Guild has a file on you thicker than a hymnal. The Court has a ledger." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a mask fits—it covered everything and expressed nothing. "Which brings me to why I've been waiting. I have an offer. Not from the Queen—from the *ledger*. There is a distinction, and it matters."

"I don't make bargains with books," Wren said.

"The book makes bargains with you," the collector said, without looking at her. "That is rather the point of books."

He extended one hand, palm up, and light gathered in it—not the warm gold of the valley seams but a cold white, paper-colored, ink-colored. And in that light I saw images, or memories, or perhaps simply advertisements; I couldn't tell where the difference lay anymore.

I saw the guildhall at evening, lamplit, safe. I saw my old cell with its narrow bed and its shelf of atlases, each one annotated in my own careful hand. I saw Master Corvane Dusk at his desk, and in the vision—no, in the *offer*—his face was kind, the way it had been kind when I was nine years old and newly arrived and crying for a mother I could not remember. I saw my desk. My pens. My routine, whole and unbroken, stretching forward like a road with no forks in it.

And over all of it, written in the air in the ledger's own script, the terms:

*Return the map. Forget the courier. Forget the mountain. Resume your life as of the night the stranger died. No memory of Wren. No memory of Oskar. No debt collected, no collector sent. You will be as you were.*

As you were.

I stood there while the twelfth moon turned above us, and I will not pretend the offer didn't reach into me and grip something vital. Because what he was offering was not wealth or safety or power. He was offering me *myself*—the self I had built, plank by plank, out of routine and anonymity and the gentle lie that contentment and loneliness look identical from the outside. My old life. My invisible life. The life where nothing hurt because nothing touched.

"You would forget them," the collector said, gently, gesturing at Wren and Oskar as if they were furniture being itemized. "The thief who sings off-key when she thinks no one is listening. The priest who prays to a moon that abandoned him and keeps praying anyway. You would forget the campfires. You would forget the sound of your own name spoken by someone who means it." He paused. "Most men, offered this, weep with relief."

"Have many taken it?" Oskar asked quietly.

"At this gate? Eleven." The collector said it the way a fisherman counts fish. "The mountain prefers volunteers. Forced souls burn bitter."

"And the ones who refused?" Wren asked.

"They're inside," he said. "Or they're jars. The distinction is administrative."

Above us, the twelfth moonstone completed another fraction of its turn, and I felt the map against my chest pulse once, like a second heartbeat answering its own.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I record it plainly because I am still not certain it was bravery and not simply the exhaustion of a man tired of hiding.

I stepped forward. Past Wren's outflung arm. Past Oskar's half-finished warding. Into the cold white light of the offer, close enough to see that the visions of my old life had edges, and the edges were torn, and behind the torn edges there was nothing. No continuation. No future in that lamplit guildhall. The offer showed me a room, but every window in it was painted shut.

"You're showing me a map," I said to the collector. "A map of a life. And I've read enough maps to know the first rule: a map is not the territory. You're offering me the *drawing* of my life. The real one burned the night that courier bled out in the alley. There's no returning to it. There's only returning to a picture of it, and pictures fade, and when yours fades I'll be standing in a guildhall that doesn't remember me, holding pens I don't remember choosing, and the only person who'll know what I traded away will be the ledger. And ledgers," I said, "don't grieve."

The collector regarded me for a long moment. Somewhere far above, stone shifted. The twelfth moon turned.

"You've changed," he said at last, and there was something almost like respect in the flatness of his voice, the way there is sometimes respect in the voice of a lock being picked. "The file on you says you never refuse anything directly. You deflect. You delay. You vanish." He closed his hand, and the light died, and my old life died with it, all twenty-two years of it, and I found—to my genuine surprise—that I did not weep with relief, or with grief, or with anything at all except a strange clean emptiness, like a page after the ink has been scraped away. "The file will need updating."

"Add a footnote," Wren said, stepping up beside me, knives still bare. "'Accompanied.'"

"*Escorted*," Oskar said, on her other side. "There's a distinction, and it matters."

The collector looked at the three of us—at the shape we made together, which was, I realized, the first shape I had ever been part of that was larger than one—and inclined his masked head.

"Then hear the terms of refusal, cartographer, so you may weigh them with open eyes. Beyond this gate, the Court will know your name, your craft, and your face. Every door in the mountain will open for you, and none of them will open *outward*. The Queen does not bargain twice." He began to walk backward, toward the shadowed base of the cliffs, and the shadows received him the way water receives a dropped stone. "One turn remains. Thirteen hours, by the old reckoning. Spend them well."

And then he was gone, and the valley was just a valley again, black stone and upward light and the litter of surrendered lives scattered along the threshold.

* * *

We did not speak for a while. The twelfth moon turned. Somewhere in its turning, I understood that the collector had made a mistake, and that the mistake was mine to keep.

He had shown me my old life to tempt me. But I had spent thirty years inside that life, and I had never once seen it whole—I had only ever seen it from within, the way you cannot see the shape of a room you're standing in. For the first time, I had seen it from outside, complete, framed, *mapped*—and I had seen what it was. A beautiful drawing of a locked box.

"Your hands are shaking," Wren said.

"My hands always shake. It's the coffee."

"You hate coffee."

"I contain multitudes."

Oskar laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and the sound went up the valley and struck the thirteen moons, and I would swear on any atlas you name that the dark discs rang faintly in answer, like bells remembering they were bells.

We walked to the threshold. Wren knelt and picked up one of the letters from the scattered offerings, read the seal, and set it down again with a gentleness she would deny under torture. Oskar murmured the eclipse-rite's final line, which translates, roughly, *even the eaten light returns.* And I stood at the mouth of the Hollow Court with my family on either side of me and the stranger's soul burning against my heart, and I unfolded the map one last time before we went in.

The lines had changed.

They change sometimes; I've stopped questioning it. But this time the change was not subtle. The route I had charted—the careful, winding path through the court's outer halls that I had planned and re-planned for a hundred miles of walking—had been redrawn in ink that was not mine. The new line ran straight as a plumb-bob through every guardpost, every sealed door, every impossibility, direct as an arrow to the vault. And at its end, where I had marked the stranger's soul with a cartographer's neat symbol, the ink had formed itself into two words in a hand I almost recognized:

*Come home.*

Above us, the twelfth moonstone shuddered, brightened, and began its final rotation.

"One turn left," Oskar whispered.

Wren rolled her shoulders, checked her knives, and grinned at the dark doorway like it owed her money. "Then let's not be late."

I folded the map. I touched nothing at the threshold—let the mountain learn early that I travel light and leave nothing behind—and I stepped through the gate of thirteen moons, into the dark that had been waiting thirty years to say my name.
