## 12. Descent

The mountain swallowed them the way a throat swallows a coin.

One moment Ines stood at the Gate of Thirteen Moons, the collector's offer still sour on his tongue—*your old life back, cartographer, for the price of walking away*—and the next he was inside, and the gate behind him had become smooth stone, seamless as an egg. No seam. No hinge. No way back that the mountain did not choose to permit.

"Cheerful place," Wren murmured.

She moved like she'd been born in dark places, which perhaps she had; guild thieves learned their first steps in crawlspaces and chimney flues. She wore grey over grey, her hair bound back with soot-darkened cloth, and she carried her lockpicks the way other people carried rosaries. Oskar walked between them, his moon-priest's collar torn away weeks ago, but he still held his hands folded as if in prayer. Old habits. Or maybe not habit at all—the ninth turn had come and gone somewhere in the deep roads, and Oskar counted everything now, lips moving, fingers twitching through some lunar arithmetic only he could follow.

"Three turns left," he said quietly. "Maybe two, if the court cheats."

"The court always cheats," Wren said.

Ines said nothing. He was listening to the mountain.

It breathed. That was the first thing they never told you about Mount Vesper, because no one who mapped it had ever come back to say so: it *breathed*. The air moved in long slow tides, warm then cool, warm then cool, and beneath it ran a sound too low for ears—a hum, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the soft meat of the heart. Ines had spent his whole life listening to places. This one listened back.

He unslung his satchel and drew out the tools of his trade: charcoal stubs, a folding rule of whalebone, sheets of pressed vellum cut small enough to hide in a boot heel. He had promised himself, back in the guild quarter, that if he ever came here he would do the one thing he had sworn never to do.

He would map what he touched. He would touch what he mapped.

"Stay against my left shoulder," he whispered. "Both of you. I'll walk the walls. Walls remember being drawn, even when no one drew them."

"That doesn't mean anything," Wren said.

"It means everything," Oskar said softly. "It's why we brought him."

* * *

The halls of the Hollow Court were pale.

Not white—pale, the way skin is pale, the way old milk is pale, the way a face goes when the blood has been invited elsewhere. The corridors were ribbed like the inside of a shell, and light came from nowhere Ines could find: no lamps, no windows, just a diffuse glow rising out of the walls themselves, as though the stone remembered sunlight and was dreaming of it badly.

And the nobles feasted.

They sat at long tables of translucent bone-stone set into alcoves along the galleries, dozens of them, scores, dressed in garments that flowed like poured wax. Their faces were beautiful the way frost on glass is beautiful—precise, symmetrical, and empty of anything you could call warmth. Before each of them floated a bowl of nothing visible, and each noble leaned over the bowl and *inhaled*, long and slow, and as they inhaled, their color improved. A flush rose in their cheeks. Their eyes brightened. And somewhere in the city above, Ines understood, someone forgot the smell of their mother's kitchen, or the sound of their own child laughing, or the name of the street where they'd been happiest.

"They're eating memories," Oskar breathed. "Not souls. Not yet. Souls go to the vault. Memories are the vintage—they drink those fresh."

"How can you tell?" Wren asked.

"Because one of them is crying." Oskar nodded toward a noble three alcoves down, a tall figure with hair like spun smoke, tears sliding down its flawless face even as it smiled and drank. "That grief isn't theirs. It belongs to whoever they're tasting. The body remembers what the soul is made to forget."

Ines's hand shook as he sketched. He crouched in the shadow of a buttress shaped like a folded wing, vellum braced against his knee, and he drew fast and true: the gallery's curve, the alcoves, the sight-lines, the two archways out, the rhythm of the nobles' feeding—he timed it, four breaths in, one pause, four breaths in—and marked the blind spot in the patrol of silent servants who glided between tables carrying ewers of something that steamed cold.

Four breaths. One pause. He would move on the pause.

It was the work of his life, this. Years of cataloging wonders he never touched, years of standing at the edge of marvels with his pen moving and his feet planted safe—all of it had been training for this single hour, though he'd never known it. Every careful line, every measured distance, every night spent memorizing a map until it lived behind his eyes: all of it sharpened now into a blade.

Fear came with him. He didn't pretend otherwise. His heart hammered so hard he was sure the whole gallery could hear it, sure the pale faces would turn as one, sure the mountain would simply open beneath him and swallow him down to whatever furnace fed its breathing. But fear, he was discovering, was not the opposite of courage. Fear was the whetstone. Courage was what you honed on it.

He moved on the pause.

Twelve paces along the wall, hugging the ribbed stone, breath shallow, charcoal and vellum stowed, satchel cinched flat against his spine. Four breaths. Pause. Twelve more paces. At the second archway he flattened himself into an alcove abandoned by its noble—an empty chair, an empty bowl, and he did not let himself wonder whose memory had been eaten from that seat, or whether the eater had grown bored, or full—and waited through six cycles of the feeding rhythm before the servants' glide carried them past and away.

Wren followed his chalk marks exactly. He'd left tiny signs only she would read: a dash angled left meant hug the wall; a dot beside a seam meant wait; a small circle meant *there is a door here that does not wish to be found*. She found every one of them without a whisper of sound, and once, when a servant drifted too close, she pulled Oskar into a feeder-alcove and stood so still, so perfectly still, that the thing looked straight through them both.

Afterward Oskar's hands wouldn't stop trembling, and Wren held them between hers until they quieted.

* * *

The vault announced itself by silence.

Everywhere else in the mountain there was sound—the breathing tide, the low hum, the murmur of feasting, the whisper of servants' hemless robes. But as they descended a stair that spiraled inward like the chamber of a nautilus, the sounds fell away one by one, until Ines was walking inside a silence so complete he could hear his own blood.

"It's warded," Oskar whispered, and even his whisper seemed to arrive muffled, wrapped in wool. "Sound can't carry secrets away from here. Neither can anything else." He looked at Ines. "Your maps. Can you draw in silence?"

"I've drawn in worse."

The stair opened onto a landing, and the landing opened onto the vault antechamber, and Ines stopped breathing for a moment that lasted far too long.

Shelves. Shelves upon shelves upon shelves, curving away into gloom in ranks like the stacks of a great library, and on every shelf, jars. Preserved-fruit jars, kitchen-simple, absurdly domestic, each sealed with wax and wire, each holding a faint light the color of a candle seen through closed eyelids. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Some burned steady. Some guttered. Some had gone dark altogether, and those shelves were labeled with small tags in a script Ines recognized from the courier's soul-map: ledger-script, the writing of contracts.

At the far end of the antechamber, a desk. At the desk, a clerk.

It had been human once, perhaps. Now it was mostly posture—a stooped figure of grey parchment stretched over grey bones, with eyes like two drops of ink suspended in water. It wrote without looking at its page, quill scratching in the dead silence, and it did not look up as the three of them froze on the stair.

Ines did the bravest thing he had ever done, which was also the simplest: he kept walking.

Down the last steps, across the antechamber floor, past rank after rank of glowing jars, following a route he traced in his mind from the courier's map—the map that had started everything, the map that showed a soul sold beneath Mount Vesper with a signature on the contract that was *his*. Shelf 40. Row 9. The courier's soul, the missing half of himself, sat in a jar on a shelf like a jar of plums.

The clerk's quill scratched. The clerk did not look up. Perhaps the wardens of this place had never needed eyes for the living; perhaps the living were beneath its notice, the way a fisherman does not count the rain.

Ines reached shelf 40. Row 9. He found the tag—ledger-script, a string of numbers and a name that was almost his name, worn at the edges—and he lifted the jar from its shelf with both hands, and the light inside it flared at his touch like a startled bird.

The clerk stopped writing.

In the vast silence, the stopping was a thunderclap. Ink-drop eyes rose from the page. They fixed on Ines, and on the jar in his hands, and the clerk's mouth opened—it had a mouth, a thin dry line like a cut in paper—and it said, in a voice like a ledger falling open:

"That item is sold."

"No," Ines said. His voice cracked, then steadied. "It's *claimed*. There's a difference. Look again at the contract."

The clerk tilted its head, and Ines saw the terrible truth of the place even as terror flooded him: the clerk was already reaching for a bell on its desk, a small brass thing that would bring collectors, wardens, the whole pale machinery of the court down upon them—

—and behind him, soft as a moth landing, Wren's pick slipped into the wax seal of the nearest jar, and Oskar began, very quietly, to sing.

It was a moon-hymn, an old one, the kind sung at turning nights to mark the passage of time the court had stolen. The silence swallowed most of it. But not all. The jars began to flicker in time with the melody, thousands of small lights pulsing like a field of fireflies remembering dawn, and the clerk's ink-drop eyes wavered toward the sound, confused, hungry, *listening*—

—and Ines ran.

He ran with the jar clutched to his chest, its light spilling between his fingers, past the shelves, past the desk, toward Wren and Oskar and the spiral stair, and behind him the clerk's voice rose into a shriek of ledger-script, and somewhere far above, bells began to ring.

The thirteenth turn, Ines thought wildly, sprinting into the dark with his own soul beating against his ribs like a second heart. The vault seals at the thirteenth turn.

They had less time than anyone knew.
