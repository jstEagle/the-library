## 13. The Court Beneath

Wren had stolen crowns from sleeping kings and picked the lock of a prison built inside a single pearl, but she had never dressed for a crime before.

The gown they'd taken from a dead woman's trunk in the Underways was silver-grey, the color of moth wings, and it fit her the way lies fit—snug where it needed to be, loose enough to move in. Oskar had stitched a knife into the left sleeve with a moon-priest's patience, muttering blessings over the thread until Wren told him to stop wasting holy words on hemming. Ines stood by the mouth of the service tunnel in his borrowed courtier's coat, tugging at the collar like it was trying to strangle him, which, knowing this mountain, it might have been.

"You look fine," she said.

"I look *visible*."

"That's rather the point tonight." She stepped close and adjusted the mask on his face—a plain thing of dark lacquer, featureless as an unwritten page. "Remember the rules. You don't speak unless someone speaks first. You don't eat anything. You don't drink anything. And if anyone asks your name—"

"I don't have one," Ines said. It was supposed to be a joke. It came out flat and true, and all three of them went quiet for a moment, because none of them had quite gotten used to how much weight that sentence carried now.

"Give them 'Master Quill,'" Oskar said gently. "A borrowed name for a borrowed night. The market taught us that much."

"The market nearly sold you," Wren said.

"And yet I remain." Oskar smiled his ruined smile, the one that didn't reach the scar where his priesthood had been cut out of him. He wore his old vestments under a plain cloak, grey and silver to match theirs, the last scraps of authority he'd carried down nine turns of the moon. "The Queen's masque begins at the twelfth hour. The vault is beneath the feast hall—we know that from the maps. While every eye in the court watches the dancing, two guests slip away to steal back what was stolen. Simple."

"Nothing down here is simple," Wren said. "That's why we're the ones doing it."

She looked at Ines one more time—at the map folded against his chest, at the fear he wore so carefully under the lacquer mask—and felt the strange fierce ache she'd been carrying since the alley behind the guild, since the fire in the Underways, since the moment a man who mapped everything and touched nothing had reached out and touched *her*, asking for help. Family, Oskar called them. She hadn't had a word for it before. She still wasn't sure she did.

"Stay near the walls," she told him. "Count the doors. That's what you're good at."

"That's what I'm *only* good at."

"No," Wren said. "It isn't. But it'll do for tonight."

Then the tunnel breathed them downward, toward the music.

* * *

The Hollow Court did not look hollow. That was the first cruelty of it.

Wren had imagined bone and emptiness, a palace carved from absence. Instead the gates opened onto splendor: corridors of white stone veined with silver, chandeliers hung with what she sincerely hoped were crystals, tapestries depicting hunts and harvests in colors so rich they seemed to bleed. Everything was beautiful. Everything was *too* beautiful, the way fruit is too sweet when it's been soaked in something to hide the rot. The light came from no source she could find. It simply was, the way the cold was, the way the faint pressure against her skull was—the mountain leaning on her thoughts like a reader leaning on a closed book.

And the people. Pale nobles in masks of porcelain and glass, moving through the halls in slow processions, laughing at jokes Wren couldn't hear being made. Their laughter was the worst part. It sounded rehearsed. It sounded *practiced*, the way a phrase sounds when you've said it ten thousand times and forgotten what it means.

"They've been here a long time," Oskar murmured beside her. His voice was steady, but his knuckles were white around his cloak. "Some of these faces are on temple murals a thousand years old. They haven't aged a day. This is what the souls buy. Not just the mountain's life. *Theirs.*"

"Then let's ruin their evening," Wren said.

A herald in a mirror-bright mask announced them—or rather, announced the names Oskar had forged for them, names plucked from minor houses whose heirs had vanished generations ago and would not be missed by anyone who could complain. *Lady Mereth of the Grey Stair. Lord Aldous Vane. Brother Silence, envoy of the lunar orders.* The herald's voice echoed strangely, arriving at her ears a half-beat after his lips moved, and Wren understood: sound down here was a courtesy, not a law. The mountain permitted noise the way a jailer permits singing.

The feast hall opened before them like the inside of a bell.

It was vast beyond reason, tiered galleries rising into darkness, and at its center a floor of polished black stone where hundreds of masked dancers turned in patterns too intricate to be chance. Above them, suspended somehow without chains, thirteen great discs of pale metal turned slowly overhead—moons of silver and iron and one of dull red, each etched with lines and sigils. The twelfth moon hung lowest, and as Wren watched, it shuddered and began its slow rotation, and a ripple went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Every masked face tilted up. Every dancer paused mid-step.

*Twelve turns done,* Oskar breathed. *One remains.*

And then the crowd parted, the way water parts around a keel, and Wren saw the Hollow Queen.

She was smaller than Wren had expected. That was the trick of her, she realized—the stories made her a giantess, a horror, a thing of teeth and shadow, and instead here was a slender woman in a gown the color of deep water, wearing a mask of white glass with no features painted on it at all. No eyes. No mouth. Just smooth, blank glass, and somehow that was infinitely worse than any monster's face, because it meant whatever looked out from behind it had decided long ago that a face was more than it needed.

The Queen did not walk to the center of the hall. The hall arranged itself around her, dancers flowing aside, musicians shifting key without being asked. She stood in the middle of the black floor like a pin holding a map to a wall, and everything in the room—light, sound, breath—hung from her.

Wren had planned this part. She'd rehearsed it in the tunnels, in the dark, saying the words over and over until they lost their terror: *walk up to the most dangerous creature you will ever meet and ask her to dance.* It had sounded almost funny when she practiced it. It did not sound funny now.

But Ines was counting doors somewhere along the eastern wall, and Oskar was drifting toward the gallery stairs to watch the moons, and there was no one else. There had never been anyone else. That was the whole story of Wren's life: if it needed doing and no one would do it, then it was hers.

She crossed the floor.

The dancers made way for her without looking at her, which meant they were looking at her with every part of themselves except their eyes. The music thinned as she approached, instruments dropping away one by one until only a single high string remained, a note held so long it stopped being sound and became a kind of silence. Ten paces from the Queen, Wren stopped and bowed—not deep, not shallow, exactly as deep as a lady of a minor house should bow to a queen whose favor she sought. She'd stolen etiquette manuals once, for a job. She'd never been so grateful for burglary.

"Lady Mereth of the Grey Stair," the Queen said.

Her voice was not what Wren expected either. It was warm. It was *kind*. It was the voice of someone who has never once needed to raise it, because everything in the world already belongs to her.

"Your Majesty honors me," Wren said.

"I know who comes into my court. I know why they come." The blank glass mask tilted, considering. "You want something. Everyone who dances with me wants something. Shall we discover what it is?"

"I would be honored to dance, Your Majesty."

"Would you?" A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Or would you be *useful*? Honored is what people feel. Useful is what people are. In my experience, Lady Mereth, very few are both."

Wren kept her breathing even. Somewhere above, the twelfth moon groaned through another degree of its turn. Somewhere along the wall, Ines was counting doors, and she prayed to every god she'd ever robbed that he was fast.

"Honored," she said. "And useful. My mother taught me never to waste an opportunity."

"Your mother," the Queen said softly, "taught you to pick pockets. You learned the rest from a brother who is no longer available to teach you anything."

The floor did not tilt, but Wren's whole world did. Her hand wanted the knife in her sleeve; she kept it still, barely, the effort of it ringing through her arm like a struck bell.

"How—" she began.

"I know what is owed me," the Queen said. "Every debt in my ledger sings. Your brother's soul has been in my keeping for eleven years, Lady Mereth. He sits in my vault among the quiet things, and he is *content*. Would you like to know what contentment sounds like? It sounds like nothing at all." The blank mask came closer, and though it had no eyes, Wren felt herself seen down to the marrow. "You did not come here to dance. You came here as a distraction. So let us be honest with one another, you and I. Dance with me while your friends do their work—and I will consider, at the end, whether honesty earns them anything more than my attention."

The music swelled again, obedient, and the Queen extended one pale hand.

Every instinct Wren owned screamed at her to run. Instead she took the hand—it was cool as riverbed stone—and let the Hollow Queen of Mount Vesper draw her into the dance.

* * *

The Queen danced the way winter moves: inevitably, beautifully, without any haste at all. The other dancers fell into orbit around them, and Wren found herself turning through figures she'd never learned, her feet finding the steps as if the mountain were whispering them to her body directly. She hated how well it worked. She hated that some traitorous part of her *enjoyed* it—the sweep of the turn, the perfect pressure of the Queen's hand, the sense of moving inside a machine so vast and elegant that resistance seemed quaint.

"So," the Queen said, as they turned. "Tell me about the cartographer."

Wren's blood went to ice water. "I don't know what you mean."

"He walks along your eastern wall. He has counted forty-one doors, and he believes no one has noticed. He carries a folded paper against his heart the way a sinner carries scripture." The Queen laughed, low and pleasant, and the sound crawled down Wren's spine. "Do you know what I collect, Lady Mereth? Not souls. Souls are merely currency. I collect *names*. A name is the only thing a person owns outright. Kingdoms can be taken. Bodies can be broken. But a name, freely given or fairly bought—that is the root of a self. Pull it, and everything else comes loose."

They turned. The moons wheeled overhead. Wren counted her own heartbeats and made herself speak lightly, the way you talk to a bear you cannot outrun.

"And the courier? The one who died in my city? Was his name in your collection?"

"All my property returns to me eventually." A shrug, silk-smooth. "He stole something that was mine. Well—mine adjacent. A curious piece. Half a soul, sold long ago by a father who needed coin more than a son, and escaped my vault incomplete before the ink on the contract dried. Do you know how maddening that is, Lady Mereth? To hold a contract and not hold the soul? For thirty years I have kept collectors in every city, watching for that missing fragment. And three turns ago, it walked into my mountain wearing a cartographer's coat."

The dance slowed. Around them, the other couples were slowing too, the whole hall winding down like a clock running out.

"He doesn't remember," Wren said. It wasn't a question anymore; it was a plea, and she hated that the Queen heard it as one.

"No," the Queen agreed, almost tenderly. "We took that from him when he fled. His name, his childhood, the shape of the boy he was—all of it stayed in my vault with the shard of his soul. He built himself a new life out of what remained. Maps instead of memories. Distance instead of family. Oh, he thinks it was his own cleverness, his own caution. But a person is a pattern, Lady Mereth, and his pattern was drawn by my hand. He became invisible because I unmade him visible. He touches nothing because he was *taken*." The blank mask inclined. "And now he has come home to take himself back. Isn't that wonderful? Isn't it *funny*?"

"It's not funny," Wren said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because some things were worth spending. "You sold him. You broke him. He spent thirty years flinching from shadows he couldn't name, and you call it funny."

The Queen stopped dancing.

The music stopped with her. The whole hall stopped, hundreds of masked faces frozen mid-turn, and in that enormous silence Wren heard only two sounds: the groan of the twelfth moon completing its arc, and, far away, faint as a memory, the sound of a door opening where a door should not open.

The Queen's glass face caught the light, empty and shining.

"You love him," the Queen said, wonderingly. "How unexpected. How *expensive* that will be."

Then she raised her voice—not loud, but the way a bell is loud, the way a verdict is loud—and the sound filled every corner of the feast hall and every corridor beyond it:

"Ines Ashgrove."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor.

Across the hall, along the eastern wall, a figure in a dark lacquer mask went rigid. Even at that distance, even through the crowd, Wren saw it hit him like a physical blow—saw his hand press flat against the stone wall, saw Oskar lurch toward him from the gallery stairs.

"Yes," the Queen murmured, and her voice dropped back to that terrible warmth, meant now for Wren alone. "*That* is his name. The one his mother gave him. The one his father signed away. The one that has sat in my vault for thirty years, waiting." She released Wren's hand and stepped back, and the hall resumed around them as if nothing had happened, music and motion pouring back into the space she vacated. "Names are keys, Lady Mereth. Say one in the right ear, and locks begin to open. Watch."

Across the hall, Ines Ashgrove pulled off his mask.

Wren shoved through the dancers, elbowing past porcelain faces, shouting his name—his *other* name, the borrowed one, Master Quill, Master Quill—but he wasn't listening. He was staring at his own hands as if he'd never seen them before, and his lips were moving, shaping syllables she couldn't hear, and tears were cutting clean tracks down his face.

Behind him, where a service door had been, the wall stood open. And out of the dark beyond it, silent as spilled ink, came the collectors.
