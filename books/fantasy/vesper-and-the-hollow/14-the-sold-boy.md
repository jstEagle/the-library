## 14. The Sold Boy

The map would not stop humming.

Ines had felt charts whisper before—the Guild's archive was full of restless documents, maps that dreamed of places they'd been drawn from, inks that murmured in dead languages. But this one hummed against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and it had not stopped since they'd made camp in the hollow beneath the Eleventh Turn, where the Underways opened into a cavern so vast their lantern light died before it found the walls.

He sat apart from the fire, the soul-map unfolded across his knees. Wren slept with her back to a stone shaped like a sleeping dog, her knives within reach even in dreams. Oskar's bedroll was empty—he'd gone to keep vigil at the moon-shaft, reading the turns by the pale light that fell through it like water through a grate.

*Thirteen turns,* the courier had whispered. *It knows your name.*

Ines had spent his life believing he understood what that meant. A name on a contract. A debt. He had imagined himself a stranger to the Hollow Court, an innocent caught in someone else's bargain—a man who happened to be holding the wrong paper when the wrong people came looking.

The map disagreed.

He had redrawn it three times now, because that was what he did, because ink and vellum were the only language he had ever trusted. Each redrawing revealed more. The chart showed Mount Vesper in cross-section, its thirteen turning moons rendered as concentric rings descending toward a black heart. And at that heart—where any sane cartographer would mark the Queen's court, the vault, the engine of stolen souls—there was a small, precise notation in handwriting he knew.

His own.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. His. The particular way he crossed his sevens. The little hook he put on the terminal of a capital letter, a habit from a copybook he could not remember owning. He had checked it against his field journal, hands steady through long practice and shaking everywhere else.

He had written this map. Or some version of him had. And the map did not point to the vault.

It pointed home.

---

The memory did not come all at once. It came the way floodwater comes—not as a wave but as a rising, finding every crack, filling every low place he had built his life around avoiding.

He had been six years old. Perhaps seven. There had been a winter kitchen and a woman who sang off-key, and he had believed, with the whole ferocious certainty of small children, that she was his mother. Then there had been a man at the door with a ledger under his arm and snow melting on his shoulders, and the woman had not looked at Ines while she signed. She had looked at the coins. That detail arrived first, decades late: not her face, not her voice, but the coins, stacked in towers of five, and how her eyes moved across them like a starving woman across bread.

*One boy,* the man had said, writing. *Sound of body. Unclaimed name.*

Unclaimed name. Ines had not understood then. He understood now, sitting in the dark with a map of his own soul trembling in his lap: names were currency in the deep places, and a child's unclaimed name was worth more than a child, because a child grew and ate and wept, while a name only appreciated.

They had taken him down through the mountain's root, past gates of green stone, into a hall where the ceiling was hung with lanterns that burned without flame. Pale people had gathered to look at him the way the Guild's traders looked at new stock. One of them—a woman whose face he could not hold onto, whose beauty slid away from memory like water off glass—had crouched before him and asked him his name.

And he had told her. Of course he had told her. He was six, and she had smiled at him like springtime, and no one had ever taught him that a name given freely could be taken forever.

There had been a pen. There had been a book bound in something that was not leather. There had been the strange sensation—this was the part that had haunted the edges of every night since, the shape behind every dream of falling—that something was being drawn out of him along the line of the pen, thread by thread, like silk reeled from a cocoon. It had not hurt. That was the horror of it. It had felt like being *noticed*, deeply and completely, for the first time in his life, and he had leaned into the feeling the way children lean toward warmth.

Then the mountain had shuddered. Later—in another life, in the life of a grown man named Ines Ashgrove—he would learn that the turning of the thirteenth moon had faltered that night, that the great engine below had stuttered, that for eleven seconds the Hollow Court's grip on everything it held had gone slack.

Eleven seconds was enough for half a soul to run.

He remembered running. He remembered small bare feet on cold stone and a corridor that bent where corridors should not bend, and he remembered that he did not look back, because looking back was forbidden, though he could not remember who had forbidden it or why he obeyed. He remembered a door. He remembered light.

And then—nothing. A blank page. A gap in the world with his shape pressed into it, like a fossil in limestone.

The next thing the boy remembered—and here the memories braided together, child and man, two streams joining—was a road above ground, and a cartographer named Ashgrove finding him huddled in a milestone's shadow, and asking him his name.

*I don't have one,* the boy had said.

*Everyone has a name,* the old mapmaker had said, unwrapping his scarf and winding it around the boy's neck. *Yours is just lost. We'll give you a place-holder until you find it.* He had smiled. *Ashgrove. For now you'll be mine.*

A place-holder. Twenty years of his life, built on a placeholder. Every kindness he'd accepted cautiously, every friendship declined politely, every wonder catalogued and never touched—all of it suddenly legible. He had kept his head down because some buried part of him knew that heads-down men don't get asked questions. He had mapped everything and touched nothing because touching things invited questions about what else he might touch, what else might be touched in him. He had told himself it was contentment.

It had been a scar, walking around wearing his coat.

* * *

"Ines."

Wren's voice, quiet, from across the dead fire. He hadn't heard her wake. She was sitting up, watching him with the particular stillness she used when she thought sudden movement might spook him.

"You're crying," she said.

He touched his own cheek, surprised. "Am I."

"Since when do you answer questions with questions?"

"Since I found out I've been lying to myself for twenty years." He looked down at the map. "Wren. Come look at this. Tell me what you see."

She crossed the cavern floor without a sound, the way she did everything, and knelt beside him. Her eyes went over the chart—the rings of the moons, the descent, the black heart at the center. He watched her reach the same notation he had reached, watched her frown.

"That signature," she said slowly. "That's yours."

"Yes."

"But the map's older than you. Oskar said the vellum alone predates the last dynasty." She looked up, and he saw the moment understanding reached her, saw her face change the way faces change at funerals. "No," she said. "No, that's not possible."

"The courier," Ines said. His voice came out level, which cost him more than screaming would have. "The dying man in the alley. He didn't press a stranger's map into my hands, Wren. He pressed *mine*. Half of it, anyway." He turned the chart over, showed her the reverse, where a torn edge ran ragged as a wound. "This is the piece that escaped. This is the piece that ran, eleven seconds ahead of the dark, the night they sold me. Someone has been carrying it for twenty years, keeping it out of the Court's hands, moving it from courier to courier like a coal passed hand to hand so it wouldn't go out. And the last carrier ran out of road in an alley in the guild quarter, and found the one person in the city who could read it."

"Because the map wanted to come home," Wren whispered.

"Because the map *is* home." He laughed, and hated the sound. "I've spent my whole career mapping realms that shouldn't exist. Do you know why the Guild gave me that work? Impossible places, unmappable places? Because I was already an impossible place. A hole shaped like a person. You can't map a man who's missing half of himself—but apparently I'm the only one who can try."

Wren did something he did not expect. She took his hand. Her grip was rough, calloused, warm—the grip of a living person, and it anchored him better than any argument could have.

"My brother Tam went up the mountain nine years ago," she said. "Debts. Our family sold him the way your mother sold you, except nobody bought him back, and nobody carried his missing half around for twenty years keeping it safe." Her jaw tightened. "When we get into that vault, Ines—if there's a jar with Tam's name on it—I'm not leaving without it. But I need you to understand something first."

"What?"

"You're not going in there to steal back a stranger's soul anymore. You're going in to finish an escape you started when you were six years old." Her eyes were fierce in the lantern light. "Twenty years ago, half of you got out. Tomorrow, the rest of you follows. And when the Queen comes looking for what's hers—" his hand tightened in hers— "she's going to learn that nothing in that mountain is hers. Not you. Not Tam. Not one single jar on those shelves. They're *loans*, Ines. And loans get called."

Somewhere above them, faint as a struck bell, the twelfth moon completed its turn. The light through the moon-shaft changed color—from silver to a bruised violet—and Oskar came back from his vigil at a run, robes flying, his face gray.

"It's begun," the priest called. "The gate opens at the completion of the thirteenth turn. We have one turn left—one day, maybe less. And Ines—" He skidded to a halt, staring at the map between them, at Ines's tear-streaked face, and whatever he read there made him go still. "You've remembered."

"I've remembered."

Oskar closed his eyes briefly, the way men do before delivering wounds. "Then you should know what I read in the shaft tonight. The turns aren't just counting down to the gate opening." He opened his eyes. "They're counting down to a *renewal*. Every thirteen turns, the Court must feed the mountain fresh names, or the engine starves. And the ledger shows a debt outstanding, twenty years old, never collected." His voice dropped. "The Court never stopped looking for you, Ines. The collectors in the guild quarter weren't hunting a map. They were hunting the boy who ran. And the Queen has saved a place for you at her table—because as far as her books are concerned, you were never sold at all."

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well.

"You're not the thief," Oskar finished softly. "As far as Vesper remembers, you're the *purchase*. Still hers. Still waiting."

Ines looked down at the map—at his own handwriting pointing home through thirteen rings of dark—and understood, finally, what the courier's dying whisper had meant.

*Thirteen turns* hadn't been a warning.

It had been an invitation. And somewhere above them, behind gates of green stone, something ancient had just set a sixth place at its table, and was smiling.
