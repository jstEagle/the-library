## 15. Corvane's Betrayal

The masque was still spinning above them when the bells of the court began to toll wrong.

Ines felt it first in his teeth—a vibration too low for sound, a tremor in the mountain's bones. The music in the great hall faltered, recovered, and then died altogether, and in its place came a silence so complete that Ines could hear his own heart stumbling. He pressed himself against the cold basalt of a service corridor, Wren's shoulder warm against his, and watched the pale nobles drift past the grate like fish behind glass.

"They know," Wren breathed.

"They always knew," Ines said. "They just didn't care until now."

Oskar crouched at the corridor's end, his stolen livery—pale silk over a moon-priest's underrobe—already gray with mountain dust. He had his counting beads out, thumbing them without looking, the way he did when the turns were close. Four beads left on the string. Then three.

"Twelfth turn ends tonight," he murmured. "When it does, every door in this mountain becomes a mouth."

Ines had mapped their route back to the gate in his head a hundred times since the masque: down the Weeping Stair, across the Gallery of Names where the walls whispered the sold, through the antechamber where the collectors hung their coats of borrowed skin. It was a good map. It was, he thought grimly, the best map he'd ever drawn, because he'd drawn it knowing he would have to run along its lines with his life in his hands.

He did not expect the doors at the top of the Weeping Stair to already be open.

He did not expect Corvane Dusk to be standing in them.

* * *

Corvane looked exactly as he had the day Ines first signed the guild's ledger: tall, unhurried, dressed in the gray-and-silver of the Guild of Impossible Things as though the mountain's dark were merely an inconvenient weather. Two guild wardens flanked him, lanterns hooded, faces blank as unsold stock. Behind him, the stairwell breathed with the smell of deep stone and old wax.

"Ines." Corvane's voice carried the mild disappointment of a man finding a ledger entry misfiled. "You've made a terrible mess of things."

Wren's knife was out before Ines could blink. Oskar's beads went still.

"You followed us," Ines said. His voice came out flat and strange. "Through the Underways. Through the market. You've been behind us the whole descent."

"I've been *behind* you," Corvane agreed, "the way a signature is behind a contract. The guild has treaties older than your grandmother, boy. Treaties with the Court Beneath. Do you imagine we catalog wonders for the joy of it? We are *brokers*. And brokers keep their accounts balanced." He stepped forward, and the hooded lanterns swung, throwing his shadow long and thin down the stair. "Three souls, delivered intact, in exchange for the guild's privileges renewed for another century. Passage rights. Immunity from collection. Perhaps even a seat at the Queen's table."

"And if we refuse?" Wren asked, blade low and easy.

Corvane smiled the way he did at auctions, right before the price went up. "Then I take you anyway, and the guild loses nothing but a thief, a defrocked priest, and a cartographer who was never more than a pair of careful hands."

The words should have cut. Ines found, distantly, that they didn't—not anymore. Somewhere between the alley where a courier bled out against his knee and the vault he hadn't yet reached, Corvane Dusk had stopped being the center of Ines's world and become merely another landmark on the map. A thing to be charted. A thing to be gone around.

"You sold my memories," Ines said quietly. "Didn't you. Years ago. Before I ever signed your ledger. That's why you took me in—that's why the guild raised me. Not charity. *Storage*. You kept what was left of me where you could watch it."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—the first honest expression Ines had ever seen him wear. It might have been guilt. It might only have been surprise that the boy had finally done the arithmetic.

"The Court keeps excellent records," Corvane said. "Better than ours. Ask the Queen, if you live long enough."

"No," Ines said.

Corvane sighed. "Wardens."

And that was when Oskar moved.

* * *

Later—in whatever time came after, if anything came after—Ines would understand exactly what Oskar had done, and how long he must have been planning it. But in the moment there was only motion: the priest hurling his counting beads not at the wardens but at the hooded lanterns, and the beads *igniting*, each one a small white star of consecrated moonfire, and the corridor filling with light so pure that the pale nobles beyond the grate shrieked like scalded things.

"THE THIRTEENTH TURN IS COME!" Oskar roared, in the high liturgical voice of a man who had once commanded congregations. "THE MOUNTAIN KEEPS ITS ACCOUNTS! RUN, YOU FOOLS, *RUN!*"

Wren grabbed Ines's collar and hauled him backward down the stair as the wardens lunged. Corvane shouted something about contracts, about ruin, about names—and then the whole stairwell shuddered, because Oskar had done one thing more. Ines saw it in a flash of moonfire as they fell down the steps: the priest pressing both palms flat against the wall, fingers spread, mouthing the old lunar canticle, and the stone beneath his hands *softening*, rippling, becoming something that remembered being sky.

A turning-moon seal. The last craft of a disgraced priest, spent all at once, like a man emptying his purse into a beggar's bowl.

It would hold the stair for perhaps a minute. Perhaps less.

"WREN—" Ines gasped.

"I see it!" She was already dragging him off the stair at the third landing, shoulder-first through a crack in the wall he had marked on his second night in the mountain—a maintenance passage, unmapped by the court, mapped by him. They ran. Behind them the light of Oskar's fire guttered and flared and guttered, and the sound of Corvane's voice rose and broke against a wall of singing stone, and then the mountain itself seemed to inhale, and everything behind them went quiet in a way that quiet should never be.

* * *

**Oskar**

The seal held for forty-one seconds. Oskar counted every one, because counting was the last prayer he knew.

He had failed his order. He had failed his god—or his god had failed him; after thirty years he no longer troubled himself with the distinction. He had drunk in temple gutters and read fortunes for copper and told himself that the moons were just stones in the sky, that nothing turned, that nothing kept accounts. And then two fools and a map had come asking him to read the countdown to a soul-vault, and he had looked at their faces—her sharp and grieving, his careful and starving for something he couldn't name—and he had heard, for the first time in twenty years, the canticle rise in his own throat unbidden.

This, then. This was the office he had been ordained to. Not the high altar. Not the silver processions. A stairwell in a dead mountain, holding a door shut with his bare hands while a cartographer ran toward the only good thing left to do in the world.

The wardens tore at the seal and the seal sang back at them, and Oskar laughed, actually laughed, because the song was beautiful. The old canticles were true. All of them. Every verse he'd ever doubted came pouring out of the stone now, and the stone was his voice, and his voice was the stone, and somewhere above, the thirteenth turn began.

The last bead on his string went dark.

Corvane Dusk stood before him when the light finally failed, breathing hard, robes scorched, dignity in ruins. Behind him the wardens lowered their lanterns. Beyond them, up the stair, the Queen's collectors were already descending—drawn by the fire, drawn by the noise, drawn the way rot is drawn to a wound.

"You could have walked away," Corvane said. "I offered terms."

"You offered *prices*," Oskar said. His knees were giving. He let them. He knelt as he had never knelt at any altar, willingly, and found the posture fit him better than all the others ever had. "There's a difference, guildmaster. I'd hoped you'd learn it before the end. So did the ones whose souls you sold."

The collectors took him gently, almost tenderly, the way one handles a book that must not be damaged. Oskar did not struggle. He fixed his eyes on the crack in the wall—the little maintenance seam, hardly wider than a hand—and he thought: *they went through there. They're moving. They're still moving.*

Let the mountain have his body. Let it have his name, his orders, his failures, his voice. It could not have the forty-one seconds. Those belonged to Ines Ashgrove now, and to the thief who loved him in the fierce sideways way thieves love, and those seconds were running forward through the dark toward the vault, and they would arrive.

The moons had promised it. And the moons, Oskar knew at last, always kept their accounts.

* * *

Ines stopped running when the quiet reached them.

He didn't decide to stop. His legs simply refused, some animal wisdom in them understanding before his mind did that the silence behind them was not the absence of pursuit but the presence of an ending. He stood in the maintenance passage with his palm against the stone, feeling for vibrations the way he'd feel for a pulse, and there were none. No footsteps. No shouting. No singing stone.

Nothing.

"He held it," Wren said behind him. Her voice was stripped down to wire. "The seal. He spent everything—he *knew*—"

"We go back." Ines was already turning. "We can't leave him—"

"To do what?" She caught his arm, and her grip was iron and grief. "Fight the wardens? Fight the *collectors*? Ines. He bought us the vault. He bought us the *turn*. If we spend that walking back into their hands, then he spent it on nothing, and I will not—I will *not*—"

Her voice cracked on the last word, and Ines understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she wasn't arguing tactics. Her brother had gone into this mountain and never come out. Everyone she loved went into mountains and did not come out. And now the pattern had taken Oskar too, and she was holding Ines's arm as though the world were a ledge and he were the last thing keeping her on it.

Ines put his hand over hers.

"He counted the seconds," Ines said softly. "Forty-one. He'd want us to use every one."

Wren made a sound he had never heard from her—small, wounded, furious—and then she turned and ran again, and he ran beside her, and they did not speak of Oskar again, because grief had fused itself to fury somewhere in the dark behind them, and the alloy of the two was harder than either alone. It carried them down. Through the Gallery of Names, where the walls whispered *sold, sold, sold* and Ines whispered back, *not tonight*. Through the antechamber of coats. Through corridors he had memorized in blood and candle-smoke, his map unfolding in his mind with every stride, until at last they stood before a door that was not a door: a seam in the mountain's flesh, hairline-fine, marked on no chart but his.

Beyond it lay the Vault of Quiet Things.

Beyond it lay the courier's soul, and the shard with his own name on it, and the truth he had been running from and toward for thirteen turns.

Ines pressed his bleeding palm to the seam. The stone was warm. It felt, absurdly, like a hand reaching back.

"Hold on, Oskar," he whispered—not a prayer, because he didn't believe in prayers, but a promise, which was a different kind of faith entirely. "Whatever they're doing to you—hold on. We're coming back for everyone."

The seam opened.

And inside, in rows upon rows upon rows, glowing faintly in the dark like preserved fruit in a larder, the stolen souls of Mount Vesper waited to be claimed.
