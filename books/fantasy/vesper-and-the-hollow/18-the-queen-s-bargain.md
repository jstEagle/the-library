## 18. The Queen's Bargain

Wren had stolen from kings, from temples, from a dragon's hoard in the Saltspire Wastes, and she had never once been caught. She knew the anatomy of traps the way other thieves knew the backs of their hands: the bait, the trigger, the closing jaw.

Standing in the vault of quiet things with the thirteenth moon turning overhead and the Hollow Queen's shadow pooling across the floor like spilled ink, Wren finally understood that they had never been stealing at all.

They had been *delivered*.

"You rewrote my vault," the Queen said. Her voice was not loud. It did not need to be. It came from everywhere—the walls, the jars, the pale glass of the soul-lanterns, the space between Wren's heartbeat and the next. "Clever little cartographer. Clever thief. You cut the seams and stitched them backward, and now nothing in this room belongs to me."

Ines stood with his bloodied stylus still in his hand, the map spread across the vault's central table like an open wound. He had redrawn the boundaries—Wren had watched him do it, watched him press his palm flat against the parchment and *insist*, line by line, that the vault was not a prison but a door. The jars along the walls had begun to glow, softly, one by one, like windows lighting in a city at dusk. Inside each one, something stirred. Something that had been sitting very still for a very long time.

"Then let them go," Ines said.

The Queen smiled. Wren had seen her face before, in Oskar's fevered descriptions and in the friezes lining the mountain's throat—a woman carved from moonlight and hunger—but the descriptions had failed her. The Queen was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful: perfectly, and with the promise beneath it that one wrong step would drop you into the dark forever.

"I intend to," said the Queen. "That is the tragedy of anchors, cartographer. We cannot leave until we are replaced."

* * *

She told it simply, which somehow made it worse.

The mountain had not always been hollow. Once—before the ledgers, before the contracts, before the thirteen turning moons had been set spinning like gears in a lock—it had been a *keepsake*. A place where the dying could leave what they could not carry: a grief, a memory, a love too heavy for the road ahead. And someone had to hold the door. Someone had to sit at the center of all that surrendered weight and keep it from spilling out into the world.

The first anchor had been a woman who volunteered because her village was starving and the Court paid in grain. The second had been her daughter, who inherited the post the way farmers inherit fields. And somewhere across the centuries, the keepsake had curdled into a court, the keeping into a *taking*, and the anchor into a queen who could not die, could not sleep, could not walk out her own front gate, because every soul she held would pour through the opening like water through a broken dam—and souls, unanchored, did not return to the world. They scattered. They became the echoes in the deep, the voices in the Underways, the cold spots in old houses where children swore someone was standing.

"I have held this door for nine hundred years," the Queen said. She drifted closer, and her gown moved like smoke deciding to be silk. "Do you know what I remember of my own name? Nothing. That was the price of the crown. I gave it to the ledger so the ledger would hold me here, and now I am the ledger's hand, its pen, its jailer. I take souls because the mountain demands fuel, yes—but also because if I stop, if I grow soft, if I let even one go free—" her smile flickered, and beneath it, for half a heartbeat, Wren saw something ancient and exhausted and unbearably sad, "—the dam breaks, and everyone I have ever kept drowns in the dark between worlds."

"So find another way," Wren said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You're telling me there's no other way because you've had nine hundred years and never looked for one."

"I have looked." For the first time, the Queen sounded tired. Truly tired, down in the marrow of whatever she was now. "There is no other way. There is only a successor. One who takes the ledger into themselves. One who holds the door while the souls I keep are guided home—one by one, safely, over years, over decades. It can be done gently. It has never been done at all, because no one volunteers, and I cannot choose. The anchor must be given freely or the door will not accept them."

The lanterns flickered. Somewhere above them, the thirteenth moon completed another fraction of its turn, and the whole mountain groaned like a ship in a storm.

"The vault seals at the turn's completion," the Queen said. "Everything inside becomes mine again—your rewritten map included, cartographer. Your clever stitches unravel. Unless." She turned, and her attention settled on Wren like a physical weight, like a hand on the back of the neck. "Unless the door is held by new hands."

"No," Ines said immediately.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. The answer is no."

"Ines." Wren touched his arm. His sleeve was damp with blood from the mapwork; his hands were shaking, fine tremors he probably thought she couldn't see. "Let me hear it."

"There's nothing to hear. It's a cage with better curtains."

"It's nine hundred souls going home." She kept her voice level, the way she did when she was talking herself off a ledge—which, she supposed, was exactly what this was. "It's Oskar's people. My brother's—" The word snagged. She pushed past it. "Someone held this door alone for nine hundred years. Nine hundred years, Ines. Nobody should do that. But somebody has to, and I'm fast, and I'm light-fingered, and I've spent my whole life picking locks. This is just the biggest lock there is."

"No." He said it again, and this time his voice cracked down the middle, and Wren realized with a strange, floating clarity that she had never once heard Ines Ashgrove raise his voice. He was raising it now. For her. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to make yourself the sacrifice because you think your life weighs less than everyone else's. I know that trick. I invented that trick. I spent thirty years believing I was worth less than the maps I made, and it nearly killed me, and it killed—" his breath hitched, "—it got Oskar killed, because we were all too busy volunteering to actually *save each other*. Not this time. Not you."

The Queen watched them with the patience of stone.

"Touching," she said. "But the moon does not care about your feelings. The turn completes within the hour. Choose, or lose everything."

* * *

Wren turned away from both of them and walked the length of the vault, because she thought better on her feet and because she needed a moment where neither of them could see her face.

The jars glowed around her. She stopped before one near the end of the row—a small jar, its glass fogged with age, and inside it a curl of light no bigger than a moth. When she pressed her palm against the cool surface, the light drifted toward her hand. Toward her warmth.

"Tam," she whispered.

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. The rhythm of it was unmistakable, because she had grown up falling asleep to that rhythm drummed against her shoulder blade by fingers missing their top joint—the brother who used to tap out songs when he couldn't sleep, who had gone up Mount Vesper as a boy to sell three years of his memories for coin to feed them, and had never come back down.

He was in here. All these years, she'd told herself he was dead, because dead was easier than *kept*, and now here he was, moth-small and alive, drifting toward her palm like he knew her. Like some part of him remembered being loved.

"They feel you," the Queen said quietly. She had followed without sound. Of course she had. "The ones you love. Even after centuries. Even through the glass. That is why the anchor must be willing, thief. The door reads hearts, not words. It would taste your grief and know it for true."

"Then it'll taste mine," Wren said. "And it'll find it true."

"Wren." Ines had come to stand behind her. She could hear the fear in him, raw and unarmored, this man who had built his whole life out of not being seen, offering her the most visible thing he had: his terror, plain on his face, for anyone to read. "Listen to me. There's another way. There's always another way—I'm a cartographer. Finding ways is the entire job."

"Then find it fast," she said, "because I'm not letting those lights drown."

"You don't even know what it does to you. Nine hundred years alone in the dark. You'd forget Tam. You'd forget me. You'd forget your own name, and then what's left? What's the point of saving everyone if the person who saved them stops existing?"

"The point," Wren said, "is that they exist."

And she meant it. Gods help her, she meant it with everything she had. But even as she said it, some traitor part of her brain—the part that had kept her alive through twenty years of thieving—was already cataloguing exits. Because that was the thing about Wren that nobody ever understood, the thing that made her good at her work: she could want two opposite things completely, honestly, at the same time. She wanted to be the hero of this story. And she wanted, desperately, shamefully, to live.

She was still holding both when the Queen spoke again.

"There is a third option," the Queen said.

* * *

Wren spun. Ines went very still.

"You said there wasn't," he said.

"I said there was no other *anchor*." The Queen drifted toward the central table, toward the map, and where her shadow crossed it, the ink seemed to lean away from her. "I have had nine hundred years to study this ledger, cartographer. Do you know what a contract truly is? Not paper. Not blood. A contract is a *claim*. The Court holds these souls because names were written—given, sold, traded—and a written claim binds until it is unwritten. Every jar in this room is a sentence in a book. And books—" her pale fingers hovered above Ines's map, above the lines he had redrawn with his own blood, "—can be edited."

"Meaning what?" Wren asked.

"Meaning that the anchor need not be a person who stays." The Queen's eyes—moon-colored, depthless—fixed on Ines with an intensity that made the air itself feel thinner. "It must be a person who is *bound*. Bound past escape, past death, past forgetting. A claim so total that the door accepts it as permanent. There are cheaper currencies than a life, cartographer. You of all people should know. You've been trading in them your whole career."

"Names," Ines said.

His voice had gone strange. Flat. Careful. Wren knew that voice—it was the voice he used when he was reading a map and didn't want anyone to see what he'd found on it.

"A name is a claim," the Queen said. "The oldest kind. Give the ledger your name—surrender it wholly, write yourself out of every record that holds you—and the door will read the erasure as an anchoring. The vault opens. The souls go free. And you—" she smiled, and it was almost gentle, which was the cruelest thing she'd done all night, "—you walk out of here. Alive. Free. Simply... unnamed. Unclaimed. Unwritten. No ledger anywhere will say you ever existed. Not the Court's. Not the Guild's. Not the world's."

"That's not walking out free," Wren said sharply. "That's—"

"That's what I already was," Ines finished quietly.

The silence that followed had weight. Wren looked at him—at this man she had dragged through the Underways, fought collectors beside, shared fires and fears and the last warm crumbs of her guarded heart with—and saw him looking at the offer the way a starving man looks at bread. Not with temptation. With *recognition*.

"Ines. No. Listen to me. That's not the same thing. Being invisible was your *choice*. This is erasure. You'd forget us. You'd forget—"

"Would I?" He turned to her, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling, and it was the worst thing Wren had ever seen, because it was the smile of a man who had finally found the answer to a question he'd been asking his whole life. "Or would I finally be free of the ledger that sold me before I could walk? Wren. *Wren.* Think about what she's actually saying. Every contract unwritten. Every claim dissolved. If my name comes out of the ledger—the ledger that holds *my* sale, the sale that started all of this—then the Court's oldest claim dies with it. Maybe all of them do. Maybe that's not a bargain. Maybe that's the flaw in the whole machine."

"He speculates," the Queen said softly. "The door will decide."

"And if he's wrong?" Wren rounded on her. "If he gives up his name and the vault doesn't open and he's just—gone, unwritten, and we're still trapped here—"

"Then the moon completes its turn regardless, and we are all, as you say, gone." The Queen folded her hands. "The hour turns whether you are brave or careful, thief. Those are the only choices left. A life on the door hinge—or a name in the fire."

Above them, the mountain shuddered. Through the vault's high slit of a window, Wren could see it: the thirteenth moon, vast and white and final, sliding toward the last degree of its last turn. Light poured down through the mountain's throat and set every jar ablaze, hundreds of small souls burning like a field of stars brought indoors, and among them, moth-small, the one that beat in time with her brother's old lullaby.

Ines was already unrolling a fresh sheet of parchment.

"Ines." She grabbed his wrist. "Ines, look at me. Please. There has to be—we'll fight, we'll run, I'll steal the crown off her head, I'll—"

"Wren." He covered her hand with his own. His fingers were steady now. Whatever storm had been in him, he had walked through it and come out the other side, and what remained was the calmest thing she had ever witnessed, calm as the eye of something enormous. "You once told me you owed me a favor. From the alley. From the compass I catalogued and never reported."

"That favor's spent. I've been paying it back since the Underways."

"Then here's the last installment." He lifted her hand from his wrist and held it, briefly, like something precious he was memorizing the shape of. "When this is done—if I'm still standing there and I don't know why I'm crying—you tell me who I was. Promise me. Tell me the whole story. Start with the courier in the alley."

"Don't talk like that. Don't *plan* for that—"

"Wren." His voice broke, just slightly, on her name. "Say it anyway."

The moon touched the final degree. The vault began to hum. And Ines Ashgrove dipped his stylus in his own blood, set the nib against the parchment, and began to write the sentence that would erase him from the world—

* * *

—when the doors of the vault burst open, and Corvane Dusk walked in wearing guild silver and a smile, flanked by a dozen collectors, and said:

"Apologies for the interruption. I believe the Court and I have business—and I've brought you exactly what you asked for."
