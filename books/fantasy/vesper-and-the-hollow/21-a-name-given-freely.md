## 21. A Name Given Freely

The mountain let them go at dawn.

Not all at once—Mount Vesper did nothing all at once—but in a long, shuddering exhale that began somewhere deep beneath the thirteenth turning and rose through the rock like water through cloth. Ines felt it pass through him as they climbed the last of the Underway stairs: a warmth without heat, a pressure lifting off his chest the way a hand lifts from a shoulder it has held too long.

Behind them, the dark was full of voices.

They came up out of the stone in their hundreds—the freed shades, pale and thin as smoke over a candle—and they did not flee the mountain so much as drift out of it, unhurried, like guests leaving a house whose owner had finally died. Some passed close enough to touch. One paused before Ines, a woman with river-silt in her hair and an expression of mild, wondering confusion, and looked at him for a long moment.

"You're crying," she said, not unkindly.

He touched his own cheek. His fingers came away wet. He could not remember why. There was a hollow where the reason should have been, smooth-walled and clean, and when he reached for it he found only a strange, unburdened lightness—as if someone had carried his grief for him while he slept and forgotten to give it back.

"I don't know," he said honestly.

She smiled, or something near a smile, and drifted on toward the grey light of morning, joining the slow procession of souls winding down the mountainside toward the valley towns, toward kitchens and doorsteps and beds left cold for years. Somewhere among them, Ines knew, walked Oskar—or what remained of Oskar, borne on shoulders of light toward whatever dawn the moon-priest had spent his whole life reading about and never believed he'd see.

Wren walked beside him with her hand in his. She had not let go since the vault. Her fingers were cut and ink-stained and shaking, and she gripped his hand the way a climber grips rope.

"Don't let go either," she said.

"I don't know what I'd be letting go of," Ines said. It was meant to be a joke. It came out true instead, and Wren's grip tightened.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the ruins of a waystation above the tree line, one of the old courier posts that had once fed the mountain's trade in souls. The roof had half fallen in; the hearth still drew. Wren built a fire while Ines sat against the wall with his journal open on his knees, staring at pages he could no longer fully read.

His maps were intact. That was the cruel mercy of it. Every line he'd ever drawn remained—the Underways, the thirteen turns, the vault with its jars of quiet things—all rendered in his precise, patient hand. But the name signed in the corner of each page, the small careful *Ines Ashgrove* that had anchored forty years of work, was gone. Not crossed out. Not smudged. Simply absent, the way a word is absent from your mouth a moment after you've spoken it. Where it had been there was now a faint blankness, paper-colored, as though the ink had been unmade rather than removed.

He understood what he had done. He remembered doing it—remembered the blood, the pen, the terrible clarity of writing himself out of every ledger at once, the court's and the guild's and heaven's own, unbinding every contract that named him. What he could not remember was who he had been before. Faces surfaced and sank: Corvane Dusk's silver smile, a boy's hands copying star-charts by candlelight, a mother's voice saying a word he could no longer hear. All of it behind glass now. All of it someone else's story that he happened to be standing inside.

"You keep looking at that page like it owes you money," Wren said, settling beside him with two tin cups of tea that tasted mostly of pine needles.

"It's my signature," he said. "Was."

"Was." She blew on her tea. "You know what I keep thinking about? The market. Borrowed Names. You were going to sell yours for passage. You stood there with the broker's scale in front of you and you almost did it, because being no one felt safer than being known." She looked at him sideways, firelight catching the scar through her eyebrow. "And then you went and gave it away anyway. For free. To save everyone."

"Does that make me brave or stupid?"

"Both. Definitely both." She set down her cup. "But here's the thing nobody tells you about names. They're not property. The court thought they were—that's why it worked, why your name coming off the ledger broke the whole rotten machine. They built everything on owning people. But a name isn't owned. It's *given*. Every name anyone ever had was given to them by somebody who wanted to call them across a room." She turned to face him fully. "So we'll just have to find someone who wants to call you across a room."

"That's a lovely theory," Ines said. "I don't remember mine. I don't remember wanting anything."

"You want tea. You wanted me to stop walking when your feet started bleeding, and you pretended you didn't. You wanted Oskar's body carried gently, and you asked the shades yourself, in that polite cartographer voice." She counted on her fingers. "You want plenty. You just don't have a label for the wanting yet." She was quiet a moment, watching the fire. "Give it time. Names grow back. Like gardens."

* * *

It took eleven days to walk down out of the Vesper foothills, and the world met them halfway.

Word traveled faster than they did—which should have been impossible, but grief makes couriers of everyone. In the first village, an old man pressed bread into their hands and would not take payment. In the second, children followed them through the streets asking if it was true, if the mountain had opened, if their grandmother was coming home. On the road below the third, they passed a family standing at their gate in their best clothes, waiting, and Ines had to stop and look away because the waiting was worse than any mourning he could remember, and better than anything too.

By the time they reached the guild quarter in Lowmark, the scandal had done its work. The Guild of Impossible Things still stood—its brass doors, its impossible compass spinning in the atrium window—but the masters' wing was shuttered, and the great ledger hall stood open to the public for the first time in its history. Anyone could walk in and read the record of what had been sold, and to whom, and for how much. Crowds did, daily. They stood in the aisles between the shelves of contracts, reading aloud, weeping, raging, taking notes.

Corvane Dusk was gone. Some said fled; some said the survivors had escorted him out themselves, at night, with his belongings in a single case and no escort past the city gates. Ines found he felt almost nothing about it—a fact that disturbed him more than anger would have. Even his betrayals had been written out of him, it seemed. He was a page with the margins trimmed.

What remained of the guild had gathered in the map room, and it was there that Wren led him on their first evening back, past the sentries—who saluted her, actually saluted her, which told him how much the story had grown in the telling—into a room full of faces he half-knew.

Sera Vane, the archivist who had once slipped him extra candles, stood at the head of the table where Corvane used to sit. She was not sitting in the chair. She stood beside it, and the chair itself had been moved to the corner of the room, empty, facing the wall.

"Ashgrove," she began, and stopped, and colored. "I'm sorry. Habit. We've been—" She gestured helplessly. "We've been trying to decide what to call this place. What to call ourselves. A guild of impossible things seems—" she glanced at the crowds visible through the windows, still filing past the open ledger hall, "—less impossible than it used to. And less innocent."

"The guild sold souls," said a young surveyor Ines didn't recognize, flatly. "For three hundred years. Maybe longer. We can't be a guild anymore. Guilds protect their members. We protected our ledgers."

"Then what are we?" someone else asked.

They turned, all of them, to Ines. He felt the old terror rise—the terror of being seen, of being *counted*, of mattering enough to be looked at—and then, underneath it, something new. Something that felt less like vertigo and more like ground.

"You're the people who stayed," he said. "When the truth came out, you could have burned the records and run. You opened them instead. Whatever you call yourselves, start there." He hesitated. "I can't tell you what to be. I've lost the part of me that knew. But I can tell you what I'm going to do, if you'll have me do it here."

"Say it," Sera said.

"I'm going to map," Ines said. "Not realms that shouldn't exist. Not places to hide wonders in. I'm going to map the roads the freed souls are taking home, so no one gets lost on the way. I'm going to chart every door the Hollow Court kept, every contract it wrote, every name it took—so that nothing is ever *unwritten* again. And I'm going to teach anyone who wants to learn how to draw a map that tells the truth." He looked around the room. "A guild of impossible things hid the world from itself. Let this be a guild of *findable* things."

The silence afterward was long. Then the young surveyor laughed—not mockery, relief—and said, "Findable Things. Gods, it's terrible."

"It's perfect," said Sera Vane, and sat down in the chair after all, and the vote, such as it was, happened in the space of a few nods.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and Ines learned the shape of his new life the way he learned all geography: slowly, on foot, with cold hands.

He drew the homeward roads. He sat in kitchens while freed souls described the paths they'd taken out of the mountain, and he stitched their accounts together into charts that the reformed guild copied by the hundred and posted at crossroads throughout the valley: *If you are returning from Vesper, the low bridge at Merrow Ford is sound. The chapel at Stony Rest keeps a lamp lit all night. You are expected. You are welcome.* He watched a woman find her chart of the northern passes pinned to a tavern wall and stand before it crying, because someone had drawn, in careful letters along the border of the map, the words *THE WAY HOME IS KEPT.*

He taught classes. Twelve students the first season, then thirty. He taught them the old rule first—*map everything, touch nothing*—and then taught them when to break it. "A map is a promise," he told them. "Every line you draw says: this is real, this matters, someone will come looking. Draw accordingly."

Wren ran the other side of the house. Someone had to audit what remained of the guild's holdings, and it turned out that the finest thief in Lowmark made a merciless auditor. She tracked down stolen heirlooms and returned them. She traced sold names through three centuries of contracts and wrote letters to the living descendants: *Your great-grandmother's name was Maren. She liked, according to the record, the smell of rain.* She came home each evening smelling of ink and dust and fell asleep at the table mid-sentence, and Ines learned to drape a blanket over her without waking her, and considered this the finest skill he had ever acquired.

Some nights the hollowness where his name had been ached like a missing tooth. He would wake before dawn with the certainty that he had forgotten something vital, reach for it, and find the smooth blank wall. On those nights Wren, who slept badly since the mountain, would appear in his doorway with tea and say nothing, and the nothing was exactly right.

On one such night, near midwinter, she sat across from him for a long time before speaking.

"I've been thinking about what I said. At the waystation. About names being given."

"Mm."

"And I've been thinking that you've been walking around nameless for four months because everyone's waiting for you to remember the old one, and you can't, and it's making us all sad and stupid." She set down her cup with a decisive click. "So I'm done waiting. I'm giving you one. Not taking—giving. You get to refuse it. But you have to hear it first."

Ines set down his pen. His heart was doing something complicated.

"Wren—"

"Hear it first." She leaned forward, firelight in her eyes, and for a moment he saw the girl who had once picked his lock and owed him a favor, and the woman who had danced with a queen to buy him time, and the friend who had held his hand in the dark of the vault, all at once. "Vesper," she said. "That's the name. Vesper. Evening star. The light that shows up first, before it's properly dark, so people know the night isn't going to win." She shrugged, suddenly almost shy. "Also it'll annoy the mountain, and I know how much you'd enjoy that."

Ines sat very still. The word settled into him—not filling the hollow, nothing could fill the hollow, but furnishing it. Making it a room instead of a wound. *Vesper.* He tried it silently. Then, because a name given must be spoken to be real:

"Vesper," he said aloud. His voice cracked on the second syllable, and he found he was crying again, the way he'd cried on the mountainside without knowing why—except this time he knew exactly why, and the knowing was the gift.

"Well?" Wren said, gruffly, blinking hard herself. "Do you accept delivery?"

"Yes," Ines Ashgrove—no. Ines Vesper. *Vesper.* "Yes. I accept."

She grinned, wiped her eyes with her sleeve, and said, "Good. Because I've already had it stamped on your new journal covers, and the guild seal costs a fortune to change."

* * *

Spring came, and with it the first fair day of the rebuilt guild's public charter, and Ines Vesper stood at the open doors of the House of Findable Things watching the crowd, when he noticed the child.

She couldn't have been more than nine, gap-toothed and solemn, working her way against the flow of visitors with the focused determination of someone on official business. She stopped in front of him, produced from her coat a folded piece of paper gone soft at the creases, and held it up.

"Are you the mapmaker?" she asked. "The one the stories are about?"

"There are stories?" said Vesper, warily.

"Lots." She pushed the paper at him. "My brother found this. Down by the river, under a stone that wasn't there last summer. Nobody can read it. Mama said bring it to the guild, and Tam said bring it to *you*, because you like the ones that shouldn't exist."

Vesper took the paper. Unfolded it. And felt the floor tilt gently beneath him, the way it always did at the edge of a discovery.

The paper was old—older than the child, older than the guild, maybe older than the mountain. The ink had faded to the color of dried violets, but the linework was unmistakable: a hand-drawn chart of streets that matched no city he knew, rendered in a style he recognized the way one recognizes a parent's handwriting. And in the lower corner, where a cartographer signs their work, there was a mark he had never seen before and knew instantly, the way you know your own reflection in a stranger's window.

A small, careful symbol. A star, rising before dark.

He looked up. The child was watching him with bright, impatient eyes, waiting to see what the mapmaker would say.

Vesper smiled, and stepped aside, and held the door wide.

"Come in," he said. "Let's find out together."
