## 1. The Weight of Small Things

The dead woman's name was Maren Colle, and she had been dead for eleven days, and she would not stop talking about her hands.

"They were good hands," Maren said, hovering at Ines's shoulder in the way ghosts did, which was to say not hovering at all but standing exactly where she had always stood, close enough that if Ines turned her head she'd feel nothing but cold air and the memory of someone. "Strong hands. I kneaded bread with these hands for forty years. Tell them that."

"I'm telling them," Ines murmured. She held the finger-bone up to the light of her stall lamp — a knuckle-bone from the right index finger, clean and pale as riverstone after three days of boiling and scraping — and turned it slowly. The market noise washed around them: fried onions, a fiddler two rows over playing something in six-eight time, a fishmonger arguing with a boy about eels. Nobody looked twice at a bone-carver talking to herself. In Corvane's night market, talking to yourself was practically a trade qualification.

"Smaller," Maren said. "You're writing too large. My husband had bad eyes. He'll want to read it close."

Ines adjusted her grip on the graving tool and made the next stroke half the size of the last. This was the work, when it went well: a conversation conducted entirely through a piece of someone. The ghost dictated; the carver cut. The words sank into the bone the way water sinks into chalk, and for as long as the words held — a year, sometimes two, if the buyer kept it warm and dry and didn't ask rude questions — the dead woman's husband would be able to hold his wife's finger and hear, faintly, the way you hear rain through a wall, whatever she had most wanted him to know.

It was not magic. Ines was very careful about that word. Magic got you hanged in Corvane, or worse, taxed, which in Corvane amounted to the same thing drawn out longer. What Ines sold were *charms*. Keepsakes. Remembrances. Little carved comforts, no bigger than a thumb, priced to move. If a grieving man wanted to believe his dead wife's voice lived in a bit of bone, well. Grieving men believed all sorts of things. It wasn't her fault the belief happened to be true.

"Tell him I forgive him," Maren said.

Ines's tool paused. "For what?"

"That's between me and him." A pause, and then, because the dead had no talent for mystery: "The mill. He borrowed against the mill. I told him not to."

"Ah." Ines cut the letters small and neat. *I forgive you.* Three words, forty strokes of the knife. She'd done harder. She'd once carved *I never loved your brother* into a rib at a widow's insistence, and had spent the whole night afterward feeling like a woman who'd kicked a dog on its way out the door.

"And tell him," Maren added, "the recipe for the dark loaf is under the third floorboard by the hearth. Not the second. He'll check the second first, he always checks the second first, and then he'll give up, because he has no patience—"

"Maren." Ines set down the tool and flexed her fingers. "There isn't room on one finger-bone for the recipe. There's barely room for the forgiveness."

"Then put the floorboard. He can remember the forgiveness himself. He'd better."

There it was — the thing people never understood about the dead until they became them. They didn't want monuments. They wanted the small things handled. Somebody to remember where the bread recipe was. Somebody to pass along the message they'd rehearsed for years and never said aloud. The great griefs took care of themselves; it was the small ones that needed hiring out.

* * *

By midnight the charm was finished, wrapped in waxed linen, and tucked into the drawer beneath her counter with forty-one others, each labeled in her own cramped hand. *M. Colle — finger — for husband — forgiveness/floorboard.* The drawer was her ledger and her larder both. Tomorrow evening she'd carry four or five of them to the market stalls and sell them for enough coin to buy flour, lamp oil, and — if the month was kind — the good tallow candles instead of the ones that smelled like burning hair.

Ten years she'd kept this drawer. Ten years in Corvane, which was nine years and eleven months longer than she'd ever intended to stay anywhere.

She blew out the stall lamp and began packing up, and the voices came in around her like weather.

They always did this at night, when the market thinned. By day she could keep them at arm's length — the living were so loud, so warm, so insistently *present*, that the dead had to shout to compete, and mostly they didn't bother. But at night the city quieted, and the dead of Corvane rose up out of their walls and gutters and old battlefields-turned-cobbles like steam off a morning roof, and Ines stood in the middle of it with her ears full.

*...cold, it's cold, why is it always...*

*...told him, I told him about the horse, nobody listens...*

*...my daughter had a mole, here, just here, does anyone remember my daughter...*

*...bread. Just bread. Is that so much...*

None of it was addressed to her, mostly. That was the trick she'd learned early, the trick that had kept her alive: don't answer. The moment you answered, you belonged to them, and there were more of them than there were nights left in a life. So she packed her tools, and she let the voices wash past her like rain off oiled canvas, and she felt — as she felt every night, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before admitting it — like the last dry person in a flood.

"You could help them, you know," said a voice directly behind her, and this one was addressed to her, and it was Maren's, who had apparently decided not to move along to wherever dead bakers went. "You could do more than little trinkets. You could—"

"I could get hanged," Ines said mildly, folding the canvas over her counter. "I've seen the gallows. Charming spot. Lovely view of the river."

"The Warden doesn't care about charms."

"The Warden cares about everything. That's the whole job." She tied off the canvas. "Small things, Maren. Small things keep a person breathing. You of all people should appreciate that — I'm holding your entire legacy in a drawer right now, and it fits in my palm."

Maren made a sound that in a living person would have been a laugh. Then she faded, the way they did, mid-thought, mid-complaint, leaving Ines alone with the ordinary dead and the smell of onions and the long walk home.

Home was two rooms above a chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane, reached by stairs that complained in seven distinct dialects. Ines walked it every night the same way: hood up, eyes down, taking the street behind the tannery rather than the main road, because the main road passed the Warden's waystation and the grey-coated men there liked to stop anyone walking alone at night and ask questions. Questions were how it started. Questions, then names, then lists, then carts.

She had gotten very good at being unremarkable. It was, she sometimes thought, her true craft — finer than any carving. Ten years of practice at being a woman nobody remembered five minutes after seeing her. Plain cloak. Plain speech. A stall in the night market among forty other stalls selling candles and charms and cure-alls, none of it worth a second glance. When the tax assessor came she paid promptly and complained about her knees. When neighbors chattered she laughed in the right places and offered nothing. She had a story ready for every question: orphaned young, no family, came up from the coast, keeps to herself. All of it true, except the parts that mattered.

Her grandmother had taught her the carving. Her grandmother had taught her other things too, things Ines kept locked in a box at the bottom of her trunk under the winter blankets, and she did not open the box, and she did not think about the box, and this too was part of the art of staying small.

* * *

Thursday evening she sold three charms before the fiddler even tuned up. Maren's husband came for the finger-bone, a big stooped man with flour still in the creases of his knuckles despite everything, and when Ines pressed the linen bundle into his hand and told him, "Third floorboard, by the hearth — she says not the second," the man sat down right there on an empty crate and wept into his apron while the fishmonger pretended very hard to be reorganizing eels.

"She knew I'd look at the second," he managed at last.

"She knew," Ines agreed.

He paid double. She took only single, and told him the rest was for the funeral candles, which was a lie, because he'd already held the funeral, but it was the kind of lie that let a man keep his dignity, and dignity was worth more than coin in Corvane. He shuffled off clutching the bundle to his chest like a newborn, and Ines felt the small warm thing she always felt after a sale — the feeling that maybe, tonight, the world balanced. One widow heard. One recipe found. One man crying in a good way, for once.

Then the feeling passed, as it always did, and she was alone again behind her counter with her drawer of the dead, and the night market roared on around her without needing her at all.

That was the bargain, she reminded herself, counting coins into her purse. That was the deal she'd struck ten years ago and renewed every morning since: she stayed small, and she stayed safe, and she stayed *alone*, and loneliness was simply the price tag she'd agreed not to read too closely. Other people had families, friends, lovers who noticed when they didn't come home. Ines had a drawer of finger-bones and a landlady who only spoke to complain about the stairs.

It was fine. It was fine. She was fine.

"You're talking to yourself again," observed the candle-seller next door, a round cheerful widow named Petra who missed nothing and understood less.

"Trade secret," Ines said.

"Any charms tonight? My hip's been singing."

"Your hip needs a liniment, not a charm. Dead people don't know anything about hips."

"Mine might. Died of a fall, my mother. You'd think she'd have opinions."

Ines smiled despite herself. Petra was the closest thing she had to a friend, which was to say Petra talked at her three evenings a week and Ines occasionally answered, and neither of them called it anything. It was, like everything else in her life, carefully sized. Big enough to keep the silence out. Small enough to run from, if it ever came to that.

She was wrapping a placebo knot-charm for Petra's hip — oak, three grooves, absolutely no one inside it — when the market changed.

It was subtle at first. The fiddler faltered. The onion smoke seemed to thin. And underneath the ordinary noise of the night, Ines heard the dead go quiet — not fading, not dispersing, but *drawing back*, the way birds go silent before a storm, the way her grandmother's ghosts used to pull away from the windows when riders came up the valley road.

Ines had known the dead for twenty years. They were many things — tedious, mournful, funny, cruel, endlessly preoccupied with bread and grudges — but they were never, ever quiet. Not unless something frightened them.

And what frightened the dead of Corvane, all of them at once, in a single breath?

She looked up. At the far end of the market lane, torchlight moved wrong — too steady, too evenly spaced. Grey coats. Four of them, maybe five, moving through the crowd with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no. An inquisitor's sweep. They weren't looking at anyone in particular yet.

Yet.

"Petra," Ines said, very calmly, pressing the knot-charm into her neighbor's hand without taking payment. "Take this. Close your stall early tonight."

"It's barely half-past—"

"*Petra.*"

Petra looked at her face and, to her credit, asked nothing. She closed her stall.

Ines stood a moment longer in the thinning crowd, heart tapping quick against her ribs, and told herself the same thing she'd told herself ten thousand times: *small, unseen, unremarkable.* Grey coats swept the market every few months. They asked about smugglers, unlicensed physickers, foreign coin. They had never once stopped at her stall. There was no reason—

The dead began to whisper.

Not the Corvane dead. These voices came from somewhere deeper, older, threaded up through the ground itself like water through limestone, and they were saying a word she hadn't heard spoken aloud since she was a child — a word her grandmother had only ever written in ash, on stone, and scrubbed away with her sleeve before the ink of it could settle:

*Vesper.*

Ines's hands went cold around her purse.

And that was when she saw him — at the edge of the torchlight, at the mouth of the lane behind her stall, a man she didn't know, tall and wrong somehow, moving fast, one hand pressed to his side, leaving a dark thread of something on the cobbles behind him. His eyes found hers across the market with the terrible certainty of a man reading a sign.

He was coming straight for her.

Behind him, unhurried, patient as winter, walked a figure in a grey coat.

* * *

Ines Montrose had survived ten years in Corvane by knowing exactly one thing: when to be elsewhere.

Every instinct she owned screamed it now. *Go. Out the back, down the tanner's steps, take the purse, take the coat, leave the drawer, leave everything, be small somewhere else by dawn.* It was a good plan. It was the plan that had kept her breathing while braver, better people died.

She was already reaching for the canvas to abandon her stall forever when the man stumbled against her counter, and she saw his face properly — young, no older than thirty, grey-lipped, and utterly certain — and he said, in a language she hadn't heard since childhood, in the accent of a country that officially did not exist:

"You're the carver's granddaughter. Thank all the drowned gods. *Finally.*"

His hand came down on her counter, dripping, and pressed something oilskin-wrapped and map-shaped into her palms, and closed her fingers around it, and held them shut.

"Hold onto it," he whispered, in Corvane tongue now, slurring, sinking. "Whatever happens. Whatever they offer you. *Carry it north.* Swear it."

The grey coat was thirty paces away and closing.

And Ines Montrose, who had spent ten years perfecting the art of staying small, heard herself say the last thing in the world she meant to say:

"I swear."
