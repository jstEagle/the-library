## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The trouble with closing time, Ines had learned over ten years in Corvane, was that it never actually closed anything.

She could shutter the window, bar the door, snuff the lamp shaped like a fat sleeping cat — and still the voices would come drifting through the walls like smoke through a keyhole. The dead of Corvane did not keep market hours. They did not keep any hours at all, being dead, and they were constitutionally incapable of minding their own business, which was ironic, since minding their own business was more or less all they had left.

"Tell her the knot goes under," said the ghost of Mistress Halloway, who had been dead for six years and still could not let go of Ines's stitching technique. "Under, girl. You're doing it over. It's a wonder you ever sold a single charm."

"It's a wonder you ever sold a single opinion," Ines muttered, tying the knot under, because the dead woman was right and they both knew it.

The charm on her bench was a small one: a knuckle-bone from a farmer's wife named Odile, carved with a word for *safe roads*, strung on red thread. Odile herself stood at Ines's elbow in the lamplight, translucent as breath on glass, watching her own finger become a living woman's protection with an expression of profound satisfaction. The dead liked being useful. It was one of the few consolations of the condition, Ines suspected, along with not having to pay taxes.

"There," she said, holding it up to the light. "Safe roads, good weather, and your horse won't bite anyone who doesn't deserve it."

"I never promised the horse wouldn't bite," Odile said serenely. "I only promised the road would be safe. The horse has its own opinions."

Ines wrapped the charm in paper and set it in the basket by the door for morning delivery. Small things. That was the whole art of her life, distilled: small things, small charms, small enough to be called craft instead of magic, because craft was licensed and magic was hanged. A bone-carver who heard whispers was a curiosity. A bone-carver who admitted to hearing whispers was a problem, and Corvane solved its problems the way it solved most things — quickly, publicly, and with rope.

She was banking the fire when the knocking started.

Not knocking, precisely. More like a body arriving at her door at speed and negotiating with it using its face.

Ines froze, poker in hand. Her workshop sat at the crooked end of Tanner's Row, where the night market's noise went to die, and people did not stumble into it by accident. People who came here came on purpose, usually for charms, occasionally to spit at the door, once memorably to propose marriage, which had been the strangest visit of all.

The second knock was weaker. Then there was a sound like a sack of wet laundry sliding down wood, and then silence, and silence was worse.

*Don't open it,* said three ghosts at once, in the helpful chorus way they had. *You'll regret it. Nothing good comes through doors after dark.*

"You are literally all dead," Ines whispered. "Your advice on doors is suspect."

But she was already moving, which was the trouble with her, the flaw stitched into her somewhere between the hearing-things and the rest of it: when someone knocked, some stubborn animal part of her got up and answered. Ten years of staying small, of staying shut, of staying alive — and it took one wet thump against her door to undo all of it.

She unbarred the door and looked down at a man bleeding out on her step.

He was maybe forty, grey-stubbled, dressed like a carter but with hands that had never held reins — long fingers, ink-stained nails, the hands of a clerk or a scholar or a forger. There was blood everywhere, soaking his side, pooling black in the gutter, and his eyes were open and fixed on her face with an intensity that made her want to step back inside and pretend she'd never been born.

"You're the bone-carver," he said. His voice was wrecked, gravel dragged over gravel. "Montrose. Ines Montrose."

"No," said Ines.

"Yes," said every ghost within earshot, delighted. Ghosts had no loyalty whatsoever. They were gossips with eternity on their hands.

"They told me—" He coughed, and the cough was bad, wet and deep. "The dead told me. They said you hear them. They said you'd know what to do."

"The dead," Ines said carefully, "say a great many things. Last week one of them insisted I was the rightful Duchess of Marrow. I'm not. I'm a woman holding a fire poker, and you're dying on my step, and I'd very much like both of those facts to stop being true."

"North." His hand shot out and caught her wrist, and his grip was astonishing, iron in a drowning man. "You have to go north. Carry it north. Swear it."

"Carry what north? Sir, I don't even carry my own washing—"

Boots. Down the row, fast, hard-heeled, two sets of them, and behind them a lantern-swing of light lurching off the wet cobbles. And beneath the boots, faint but unmistakable, a voice speaking the flat, clipped cadence of a Warden's oath — the words inquisitors used when they were about to make someone else's evening considerably shorter.

The man's eyes went wide. With strength that frightened her, he hauled himself up and over her threshold, into her workshop, leaving a smear of red down her doorframe like a signature, and collapsed against her workbench, scattering bone dust and half-finished charms.

"In," he gasped. "Please. Please."

And Ines Montrose, who had spent ten years perfecting the art of the closed door, closed it behind him and dropped the bar.

* * *

"What is wrong with me," she whispered, turning to look at him. "What is genuinely, medically wrong with me."

"Compassion," offered Mistress Halloway, perched on the stool she'd haunted for years. "It's a disease. Terminal, in my experience."

The man had slid down the workbench to the floor and was trying to press his coat against his side, doing it badly, doing it the way people do everything when their blood is leaving them faster than their sense. Ines swore, grabbed her rag basket and the bottle of grain spirit she kept for cleaning wounds and, on memorable evenings, celebrating them, and knelt beside him.

"Let me see."

"Don't bother." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "I've seen this before. I know how it ends. Listen to me instead. We haven't much time, and they'll search this street."

"Then we should get you out of—"

"They'll search the street," he repeated, with the patience of the very tired, "and find nothing, because you will tell them nothing, because you are going to swear an oath first. Before I give you what I carried across half a country. Swear it, bone-carver. Swear on whatever you hold holy. On your hands, if you have nothing else. On your gift."

"I don't have a gift. I have a trade."

"Swear," he said, "on the dead."

And there it was. Not the accusation she'd braced for — the flinch, the crossed fingers, the little superstitious gesture people made around her when they finally said it aloud. Just a request, plain and level, as if he'd asked her to swear on her mother's grave, and somehow that was worse, because it meant he understood exactly what she was and hadn't wasted a heartbeat fearing it.

Outside, the boots passed. Slowed. Stopped, somewhere near the tannery, arguing about something. They would come back. Men in Warden's grey always came back; it was practically their defining feature.

"My name is Aldric Vane," the man said. "I was a cartographer. Before. For a country that doesn't exist anymore."

"That's a strange thing to be a cartographer of."

"It's a strange thing to be," he agreed. "Vesper. Say it and watch people's faces. Half of them flinch. The other half lie. There's no third kind of person, I've learned. Only flinchers and liars."

Ines opened her mouth to say something sensible — that he was feverish, that countries didn't simply stop existing, that names didn't frighten whole cities — and found that she couldn't say it, because the room had gone quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. Every ghost in her workshop, every whispering tenant of Tanner's Row, every restless dead thing for three streets around had gone utterly, attentively still, the way birds go still before a storm. She had never known them to be quiet. She had spent ten years wishing they would be quiet, and now that they were, it felt like the moment before a floor gives way.

"They remember," Aldric whispered, watching her face. "That's why I came to you. The living were made to forget. It was done to them — cut out, burned out, ledgered away. But the dead keep what they're given. Nobody thought to take it from them." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; he pressed his hand harder to his side. "Nobody ever thinks about the dead. That's our advantage. That's always been our advantage."

From somewhere inside her coat he drew a bundle wrapped in oilskin, tied with waxed cord, and pushed it into her hands. It was heavier than it looked. It seemed almost to lean into her palms, the way a sleeping cat leans, or a decision.

A map. She knew it without unwrapping it, knew it the way you know a heart is beating inside a chest. Something in the bundle pulsed against her gift like a second pulse, faint and far away and patient.

"Seven doors," Aldric said. "Thresholds. Marked on that map, scattered from here to the drowned coast. I found the first three. I ran out of country before I found the fourth." His breath hitched. "They can be opened. That's what the Wardens killed for, all these years — the fear that they can be opened. And you can open them, Ines Montrose. The dead say so. The dead have been saying so since you were a child, haven't they? Haven't they?"

She wanted to say no. She heard herself say, very quietly, "Since I was six."

"Then swear." He gripped her wrist again, and his fingers were cold now, cold in the way that stopped being dramatic and started being final. "Carry it north. Open what you can open. Don't let them burn it. Don't let them finish it. Swear on the dead, bone-carver, because the dead are the only ones left who'll hold you to it."

The boots were coming back. Closer now. She could hear the lantern-handle creaking in a gloved grip, could hear the inquisitor breathing through his nose, slow and methodical, a man who enjoyed his work the way some men enjoy carving.

Ines looked down at the oilskin bundle in her lap. She thought about ten years of small things. Small charms, small lies, small life, a survival built entirely out of staying too minor to matter. She thought about how she had told herself, every single day for a decade, that this was wisdom, and how tonight, kneeling in her own blood-spattered workshop, it felt for the first time exactly like what it had always been: hiding.

"All right," she said. "I swear. On the dead. On all of them, the chatty ones especially. May they haunt me into the next world if I fail."

Something in Aldric's face loosened. "Good," he breathed. "Oh, that's — good. My sister wasn't sure you would. Tam said—"

His head turned toward the door. The boots had stopped directly outside it.

The silence stretched. Through the gap under her door, lamplight broke around two shapes, standing, waiting. One of them knocked — politely, horribly politely, three measured raps.

"Warden's office," said a voice like a filing cabinet. "Open in the name of the peace."

Aldric's hand found hers and squeezed, once, with the last of the iron in him. "Back room," he mouthed. "The loose stone. Behind the shelf. I hid things there, months ago, when I planned this route. Take the map and go out the roof. Go north. Don't stop for anything. Don't stop for anyone."

"Anyone," Ines repeated, and hated how young her voice sounded.

"Anyone," he confirmed. Then, softer, with the ghost of that terrible smile: "Except yourself. Do stop for yourself, now and then. You strike me as a woman who forgets."

The knocking came again, less polite. Wood began to splinter somewhere near the lock.

Ines stuffed the oilskin bundle down her shirt, hauled Aldric by the arms into the back room, and shoved him behind the shelf of finished charms just as the door gave way and grey coats poured into her workshop like spilled ink.

* * *

They were thorough. That was the worst part. Not cruel, not yet — cruel would come later, if later was warranted — just thorough, in the way of people who had done this many times and taken pride in the craft of it. They turned over her baskets. They rifled her bone drawers. One of them held up a half-carved charm to the lantern and asked her, conversationally, what it was for.

"Safe childbirth," Ines said. "For the midwife on Bell Lane. She pays well and complains constantly."

"And these?" Bones, dozens of them, laid out on her bench like a terrible dinner service.

"Stock," she said. "Dead men's fingers pay better than dead women's, if you can believe it. Vanity. Even in death."

The lead inquisitor — tall, patient-faced, with the eyes of a man reading a ledger — watched her while his subordinates wrecked her home. He never blinked quite when other people blinked. Ines had met his kind before, in smaller versions, in the petty officials of her ten careful years, and she knew the type: he was not looking for contraband. He was looking for the shape of her fear, so he'd recognize it later.

"A man was seen on this street," he said. "Bleeding. Running. Perhaps twenty minutes ago."

"This is Tanner's Row," Ines said. "Someone is always bleeding. Usually the tanners."

Something flickered at the corner of her vision — Aldric, visible only to her, propped in the doorway of the back room, shaking his head slowly, mouthing *don't*. Behind him, filling the workshop like weather, stood the dead. All of them. Every ghost she'd ever hosted, plus a hundred she didn't know, packed shoulder to shoulder, and every one of them staring at the inquisitors with expressions Ines had never seen on the dead before.

They looked angry.

"Ma'am," said the youngest inquisitor suddenly, stepping back from the bone drawers, rubbing his arms. "It's freezing in here. Is there a draft?"

"No," said Ines pleasantly. "Old houses breathe. It's how they gossip."

The lead inquisitor looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and said, "Lock your door at night, bone-carver. Corvane isn't safe after dark."

"No," Ines agreed. "It really isn't."

They left. She listened to their boots fade all the way down Tanner's Row, and then past it, and then not at all, and only then did she let herself slide down the wall to the floor and discover that her hands were shaking so badly she could barely feel the oilskin bundle pressed against her ribs.

"He's gone quiet," Mistress Halloway reported from the back room, in the gentle tone she saved for genuinely bad news. "The man. He's gone very quiet."

Ines crawled to him.

Aldric Vane lay behind her shelf of charms with his hand pressed to his side and his eyes fixed on the ceiling, and he was smiling, and he was not going to survive the hour, and he knew it, and the knowing had made him calm as deep water.

"Told you," he whispered, when she took his hand. "Knew how it ends. Saw it... often enough. Cartographers. We die of borders, mostly."

"Don't talk. Save your—"

"Listen." His grip tightened. "My sister. Tam. She'll come looking for me. She'll find where I fell, and she'll follow the trail here, and she will think—" he coughed, wetly, "—she will think you killed me. For the map. She's lost everyone else. Let her be angry. Anger keeps her alive. Just... don't let her do anything stupid with it. And don't hate her. She's the best person I know, and I include myself, and I'm delightful."

"You're delirious."

"I'm a cartographer," he said, with immense dignity, "of a country that doesn't exist. Delirium is the job description." His eyes drifted to the bundle against her chest. "Open the first door. Just the first. When you see what's behind it... you'll understand why I bled for this. Why they all bled. Why it's worth—"

He stopped. Not dramatically. Just stopped, mid-word, the way a clock stops, and his hand went slack in hers, and the room filled up with a silence that had a man-shaped hole in it.

Then, softly, from everywhere and nowhere, the dead of Corvane exhaled — a sound like wind through a field, like ten thousand voices letting go of a held breath — and Ines understood that they had been waiting for him. That they had gathered all evening not to threaten the inquisitors but to escort him. That somewhere beyond the edge of her hearing, a cartographer of a vanished country was already asking directions.

Ines sat on her workshop floor, in the wreckage of her careful life, with a stranger's blood drying on her hands and a map leaning against her heart like a second heartbeat, and she did not cry, because crying was loud, and loud was dangerous, and she had survived ten years by being neither.

But she did say, quietly, to the empty air:

"I swore on the dead. So I suppose you're all witnesses now. Congratulations. You're legally involved."

The dead, being the dead, had opinions about this immediately.

And far to the south, moving fast through the dark toward Tanner's Row with grief and a knife, a woman named Tam had just found the blood on the cobblestones.
