## 3. Grey Coats in the Market

The trouble with burning ten years of work, Ines thought, was that it smelled like a funeral.

Not that she hadn't been to funerals. She'd been to plenty — mostly uninvited, standing at the back of mourning crowds because the dead inside the coffins had asked her to come and make sure nobody said anything *wrong* over them. The dead were particular about their eulogies. Old Mistress Halloway had once made Ines walk three miles in the rain to correct a nephew who'd described her as "a generous woman." "I was *thrifty*," Halloway's ghost had insisted, gripping Ines's shoulder with hands like winter. "There is a difference, girl, and my reputation depends on it."

So Ines knew funerals. And her workshop, as the first charm caught flame and the smoke rose sweet and bitter through the floorboards, smelled exactly like one.

"Steady," said the dead woman whose finger-bone she'd carved two nights ago. Her name was Petra, or had been; now she was mostly a pressure at Ines's left ear, like a hand cupped there. "Burn the love-knots first. Nobody will miss those."

"Everyone misses the love-knots," Ines muttered, feeding another handful of small carved bones into the stove. "They're my best sellers. Do you know how many people in this city are lovesick? All of them. It's the only reliable market."

"And how many of those charms actually worked?"

Ines paused, a knucklebone shaped like a sleeping bird hovering over the flames.

"That," she said, "is between me and my customers."

"It's between you and no one, because they never worked, because you never let them work. You carve them too small. Too quiet." Petra's voice had the patient tone of someone who had spent forty years managing a shop full of apprentices and had not stopped managing after death. "You've been making yourself small for so long you've forgotten you're allowed to take up space."

"I take up plenty of space," Ines said. "This whole room is mine. Look at it. Walls. A roof. A stove I'm currently using to destroy evidence."

She was talking too much, and she knew why. Talking kept the fear in its stall. Because downstairs — well, not downstairs, but two streets down and closing — grey coats were moving through Corvane's night market, asking questions.

* * *

She had heard them before she saw them, which was its own kind of curse. The living came to her ears as noise: footsteps, haggling, the fishwife's eternal argument with her husband about the price of eels. But the dead came as weather. A pressure change. A stillness.

And tonight the dead of the night market had gone quiet all at once, the way birds go quiet when the hawk's shadow crosses the field.

"They're here," Ines said, unnecessarily. The dead woman sighed at her ear.

"The man who died on your floor," Petra said. "The one you buried under the floorboards like a sensible person instead of reporting him like an idiot. They've found him, haven't they."

"They haven't found him. He's under six inches of oak and a false drawer of broken combs. Nobody looks under a bone-carver's floor. Everyone assumes we keep our secrets in plain sight, among the stock." Ines wiped her hands on her apron, then took the apron off, folded it, and put it in the stove too, because the apron had bone dust in its seams and bone dust could be read by people who knew how, and Warden's men knew how. "It's the map I'm worried about."

"Where is the map?"

"On my body. Where it stays."

"You should have burned it with everything else."

Ines looked at her — or rather, looked at the place where Petra stood, a faint disturbance in the lamplight, like heat rising off summer cobbles. Ten years of practice had taught Ines to see the dead the way you see stars at dusk: not directly, but out of the corner of the eye, and only if you already knew where to look.

"Burn the map," she repeated. "The dying wish of a man who bled out on my floor asking me to carry it north. Burn it. Wonderful. Then his ghost can haunt me forever, and believe me, Petra, ghosts who die with unfinished business are *insufferable*. They follow you around correcting your posture."

"You would deserve it."

"I would," Ines admitted. "That's the problem with doing the right thing. It follows you."

* * *

The knocking started an hour later, and it was polite, which was the worst part.

Warden's men were never polite when they wanted something small. Politeness meant they wanted something large, and they knew it, and they wanted you to know they knew.

"Ines Montrose?" The voice through the door was smooth as tallow. "Corvane City Wardens. We're conducting inquiries. Nothing to concern yourself with."

"Then why knock on my door at midnight?" Ines called back, keeping her voice pleasantly bored, the voice of a tired tradeswoman. She was, at that moment, lying flat on her back beneath her own bed, sliding floorboards aside with her fingertips, lowering herself into the crawlspace that ran between her shop and the chandler's next door. It was a space she'd built herself, eight years ago, in the paranoid months after a customer's husband had asked one question too many about where she sourced her materials. Everyone assumed bone-carvers robbed graves. Ines didn't rob graves. Graves were loud. The dead in graves were restless and chatty and full of complaints about the damp. No — Ines bought from hospitals, from executioners, from families who needed money more than relics. Legal, licensed, dull. Dullness was armor.

But the crawlspace wasn't for smuggling bones. It was for smuggling Ines.

"Madam Montrose, we can do this politely or otherwise."

"Give me a moment to dress!" she called, and slid the boards home above her head, and lay in the dark smelling of tallow and old wax and the ghost of burnt juniper, listening.

Boots. Four sets, maybe five. The door — locked, but locks were suggestions to men with crowbars — gave way with a sound like a rib cracking.

Light spilled down through the gaps in the floorboards. Shadows moved across them, long and slow, like fish passing under ice.

"Workshop," said the smooth voice, closer now. "Bone-carver. This is the one."

Another voice, rougher: "She keeps a lot of stock."

"They always do. Check the floor. Under everything. These little trades-folk hide things in their floors the way spiders hide in cracks."

Ines lay beneath them, breathing through her mouth, and felt something she had never felt in ten years of careful, small, gray life: rage. Not fear — or not only fear. Rage, hot and specific, at these boots on her floorboards, these strangers' hands lifting her carvings, her mother's thimble still sitting on the shelf where she'd left it that morning, expecting to come home to it.

And underneath the rage, stranger and worse, a sound. The dead of the house were gathering.

She hadn't asked them to. That was the thing she would remember later, turning it over on the road north like a stone with an odd shape: she hadn't asked. But the bones in her bins — the ones she hadn't burned, the honest stock, the femurs and ribs and skull-caps of people who had sold their remains to her fair and square — those dead were waking, and drifting up through the floor, and arranging themselves in the room above her head.

She felt them the way you feel a crowd pressing close at a hanging: a wall of attention, silent and total.

"What is that?" the smooth voice said suddenly.

"What's what?"

"It's cold. Why is it cold? There's a stove right there, still warm—"

"Drafty building."

"It's not the draft." A pause. When the voice came again it had lost some of its tallow smoothness. "Something's in here with us."

"There's nothing in here with us. It's a shop. Full of — saints' teeth, what is *that*?"

Ines realized, with a lurch, that one of the dead had decided to be visible. Not fully — the dead couldn't manage fully, not without a door like hers — but enough. Enough to make the lamplight bend wrong. Enough to make a grown man with a crowbar take two steps backward and knock over a display of carved beads.

"Rats," said the rough voice, without conviction.

"Rats don't make rooms go cold."

"Big rats."

Above her, Ines felt the dead settle into the corners of the room like frost forming on glass. Petra was there — she could feel her distinctly, arms crossed, radiating the satisfaction of a shopkeeper watching thieves get their comeuppance. And others. Old customers. Old friends of old customers. The woman whose hip-bone hung by the window. The soldier who'd sold her two finger-bones and a story he'd told twice, differently each time.

None of them spoke. None of them had to. Their silence was the loudest thing Ines had ever heard, and it pressed down on the grey coats like deep water, and the men who had kicked in her door stood in the middle of her shop and shuffled and did not find the map, did not find the body under the floor, did not find the crawlspace, because men who feel watched cannot search properly. Every man knows this. Every child knows this. You cannot look for something while something looks for you.

"We're done here," the smooth voice said at last, thinner than before. "She's not here. Check the neighbors. If she comes back—"

"She'll hang with the rest of them," the rough voice said. "Vesper-lover's map in a bone-carver's shop. The Warden will want her breathing until the questioning. Barely."

Boots receding. The door left hanging. Silence, ordinary silence, returning to the street like water filling a footprint.

Ines lay in the dark a while longer, because caution was a religion and she was devout. Above her, the dead began, slowly, to disperse — except Petra, who stayed, and after a moment said, in the tone of a woman delivering a bill:

"Well?"

"Well what?"

"'Well what,' she says. Lying in a hole while soldiers tear apart her life's work, and 'well what.'" A pause. "You can't come back up, girl. They'll watch the shop now. You know they'll watch."

"I know."

"So where will you go?"

Ines stared into the darkness an inch from her nose, where the crawlspace ran on toward the chandler's and beyond it the alley and beyond the alley the night market and beyond the market — north. Roads. Rivers. Seven marks on an oilskin map pressed against her ribs, each one a door, each one a word she couldn't read yet but could almost *hear*, the way you hear a conversation through a wall: voices, urgent, waiting.

"I'm going to do something stupid," she said.

"You're going to do something noble," Petra corrected. "It's important to use the right word. It's the same act, but the right word helps on cold nights."

* * *

Climbing out through the chandler's yard, Ines allowed herself one look back at her own windows.

Ten years. Ten years of staying small. Of carving charms deliberately too weak to attract notice, of charging just enough to live and never enough to prosper, of knowing her neighbors' names and letting them know nothing true about her. Of hearing the dead every hour of every day and telling no one, because the last person in Corvane who'd admitted to hearing things had been exhibited in the Warden's square for a week before the hanging. Smallness had kept her alive. Smallness had been her art, her craft, her masterpiece — a whole self, folded up tight as a paper boat, dry in a world of floods.

And one bleeding man had unfolded it in an evening.

"He chose you," Petra said beside her. Ines flinched; even after ten years, being addressed from empty air still startled her on bad days. "The dying man. He didn't stumble into your shop. He was carried past three other doors, and at yours, his feet moved. The dead steer the dying, sometimes. Toward whoever's listening."

"Then the dead can carry the map north," Ines said. "I hear they're very motivated."

"You're not funny."

"I'm a little funny. It's how I cope."

Petra didn't answer, and Ines realized, with a strange sinking feeling, that this was goodbye. Not because the dead woman was leaving — the dead didn't leave, exactly — but because whatever came next, out past the city walls, would need a different Ines than the one who had lived small and safe behind these windows. And Petra belonged to the small life. To the night market and the careful prices and the charms too quiet to matter.

"Come with me," Ines said anyway, because loneliness makes sentimentalists of us all.

"I'm a finger-bone in your stove, dear. I go where the ash goes." A pause, and then, gentler than any living voice Ines had heard in years: "Listen to me. You've spent ten years hiding among the living, pretending to be one of them. Tonight you hid among the dead, and they sheltered you. Think about what that means."

"That I should open a haunted inn?"

"That you were never one of them. The living, I mean. You've been walking a border your whole life, girl, and borders are dangerous places — but they're also the only places anyone ever crosses." The presence at her ear faded, thinned, became weather again. "Go north. Open the doors. And when you meet whatever's waiting on the other side of the first one — tell it Petra says hello."

Then she was gone, or folded away, and Ines stood alone in the chandler's yard with the map against her ribs and the smell of her own burned life drifting from her chimney into the indifferent dark.

She pulled her hood up. She turned north.

Behind her, somewhere in the city, grey coats were knocking on doors. Ahead of her, seven thresholds waited along roads she'd never walked, marked in the memories of a country that officially did not exist. Somewhere between the two, a sister with grief and a grudge was presumably also looking for the map, and Ines made a mental note to worry about that tomorrow, or possibly never.

"One step," she told herself, and the night market's dead murmured agreement all around her, soft as snowfall, and she stepped.

* * *

The road north out of Corvane passed the tithe-posts at dawn, and it was there, counting her coins for the third time and finding them insufficient in a manner that felt personally insulting, that Ines first noticed she was being followed.

Not by the Wardens. By a woman.

Tall, dark-haired, moving with the particular economy of someone who has decided violence is a tool she owns. She kept fifty paces back, matching Ines's pace exactly, and she made no effort whatsoever to hide.

Ines considered her options. Running would confirm guilt. Confronting her would confirm interest. And so Ines did what she had done for ten years, what she did best: she made herself small, put her head down, and walked — while behind her, patient as winter, the stranger followed, and at Ines's ear, faint and unwelcome, the dead whispered a single word.

*Sister.*

Ines closed her eyes briefly. "Of course," she muttered. "Of course you have a sister."

Ahead, the road bent toward the causeway, and the woman behind her quickened her stride.
