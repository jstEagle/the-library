## 4. What the Map Refuses to Say

The map did not like being read by firelight.

Ines discovered this on her second night out of Corvane, huddled under a leaning shelf of rock while rain stitched the dark together outside. She had unrolled the oilskin across her knees, held a stub of tallow candle close, and the ink — if it was ink — had gone sullen and grey, like a cat pretending to be asleep.

By daylight it was different. By daylight the lines moved.

Not obviously. Not the way a conjurer's cards move, all flourish and wink. But she would look away to check the road, look back, and find that the river had shifted its course by a finger's width, or that a hill had grown a second contour line where no hill had been. The map was not drawn so much as *remembered*, and memory, as Ines knew better than most, was a living thing that fidgeted.

"Stop that," she told it.

It stopped. Then, feeling observed, it started again.

She had left Corvane the way she did everything: small. One bundle, one knife, one coat with the good lining. She'd taken nothing from her workshop except her carving tools wrapped in flannel, because a bone-carver without tools was just a woman with opinions about bones. She had walked north through the grain fields with her hood up and her shoulders rounded into the shape of a person nobody would remember meeting, which was an art she had practiced for ten years and could perform in her sleep.

The trouble was the map. You could make yourself forgettable. It was much harder to make a country forgettable when the country kept insisting on itself.

* * *

On the third morning, at a crossroads shrine so old the god's face had weathered into a suggestion, Ines spread the oilskin flat on a milestone and finally admitted what she had been avoiding since the man died on her floor.

She couldn't read it.

Oh, she could read the roads and the towns — those were ordinary enough, though every town name sat oddly on the tongue, like words from a language she'd heard as a child and lost. But threaded through the cartography, fine as veins in marble, ran a second script. It curled around rivers. It pooled in valleys. And at seven points across the map — she had counted them twice, then a third time because the first two counts disagreed — the script gathered itself into tight knots, dense as seed pods.

Seven marks. Seven knots. She pressed her thumb against the first one, up near the northern hills, at a place marked only with a tiny drawing of a tollhouse.

The dead spoke through her hands sometimes. That was the gift, or the affliction, depending on the day and how many of them were talking. Bone remembered; she carved what it remembered into charms; people paid her for luck, for grief, for one more word from someone they'd loved. She had learned to keep the volume low, the way you learn to sleep beside a busy road.

But this was different. This wasn't a whisper rising out of a knucklebone. This came up through her thumb, through the milestone, through the ground itself, and it was not one voice. It was a chorus with the rough edges worn off, the way a river smooths stones, and it said:

*North past the tollhouse where the toll was never coin.*

Ines snatched her hand back and looked around the empty crossroads as though someone might have heard. A crow regarded her from the shrine's shoulder with professional indifference.

"That," she said, "was rude."

*The toll was never coin,* the voices repeated, patient, from everywhere and nowhere, from the oilskin and the stone and possibly the crow. *The toll was always this.*

"This" being what? Her sanity, probably. She sat down hard on the milestone and made herself breathe until the chorus faded to a hum, the way a bell keeps humming after the clapper stills.

Then, because she was who she was — a woman who had spent a decade making a living out of other people's dead — she put her hand back on the mark.

* * *

Here is what the map told her over the next three days, in fragments, in voices layered like sediment:

*We built the tollhouse in the year the apples failed, and my daughter cut the ribbon, and she is why I stay in this place, in the grain of this place, in the—*

*—ask the ferryman, but ask him in the old tongue, he pretends otherwise but his mother was—*

*—they took our names first. Did you know that? Before anything else. A clerk with grey gloves and a ledger, and he said, say it again, slower, and I said Vespera Ilune, and he wrote something else entirely, and that was the moment I understood we were already—*

At that word, the voices did something Ines had never heard the dead do before.

They flinched.

All of them at once, a thousand voices catching on the same syllable like cloth on a nail, and the map went cold under her fingers, and the word — she never learned what word it was, the sentence simply tore — hung unfinished in the air above the crossroads.

Vespera Ilune.

She turned the name over. It fit nowhere. It was not Corvanish, not hill-country, not the trading pidgin of the coast. It sounded old. It sounded like a name spoken slowly for a clerk with grey gloves.

That night, in the shelter of a hay barn whose owner she paid in silence and a charm against rats (the rats were imaginary; the charm was real enough), she asked a farmer's wife at the well, casually, the way you ask about weather.

"Ever hear of a place called Vespera Ilune?"

The woman's ladle stopped mid-pour. Water dripped. Her face did something complicated and quick, like a shutter slamming in wind, and settled into blandness.

"Don't know it," she said. "Sounds foreign."

"It's on an old map I found."

"Maps lie." The ladle resumed. "Good night to you."

Ines walked back to the barn and lay down in the hay and stared at the rafters. The woman hadn't lied the way people usually lied — badly, with darting eyes and too many details. She had lied the way you lie about a wound. Automatically. From long practice. As if the name were a hot stove everyone in the country had been taught, once, not to touch.

*They took our names first,* the dead had said.

And then, quieter, from somewhere deeper in the map, a single voice, dry as paper:

*You're reading me wrong, carver. I'm not written in ink. I'm written in us. Every line is somebody. Mind where you put your fingers.*

* * *

The fourth day brought rain again, and the fifth brought a checkpoint.

It was nothing official — no Warden grey, no banners, just two men in leather jacks collecting "road tithes" from travelers at a bridge, which meant robbing anyone who looked slow. Ines joined a knot of peddlers and let herself be robbed of four coppers with the serene expression of a woman carrying nothing worth a second glance.

It nearly worked. It would have worked. But one of the men, bored, flipping through a confiscated bundle of pamphlets, held one up to the light and said to his partner, "Look at this rubbish. *Remembering the Fallen Country.* Someone's still printing this tripe."

His partner spat over the bridge rail. "There's no fallen country. There's just the north woods and a lot of superstition."

"No," the first man agreed, and there was something strange in his voice, something rehearsed, a lesson recited so often it had gone hollow. "There's no such place. Never was."

They both laughed. Neither of them laughed with his eyes.

Ines kept walking north with her heart going like a mill wheel. Because she had heard that cadence before — not from the living, from the dead. That exact hollow recitation, that *never was*, was what ghosts sounded like when they were trying very hard to believe their own forgetting. She had heard it from spirits who died ashamed, who spent eternity insisting on a version of events that let them sleep.

Two living men, standing on a bridge in broad daylight, reciting their forgetting in unison.

Whatever Vespera Ilune was, it wasn't just erased. The erasure was *maintained*. Actively. Like a fire that someone tended.

*Now you see it,* said the map, smugly, from inside her bundle. Or perhaps that was her own thought wearing the map's voice. After four days on the road alone with a chorus of the dead, the boundary had gotten soft.

"I see it," she muttered. "I wish I didn't."

*Too late. You've got the ears for it. Why do you think he gave it to you?*

She stopped in the middle of the road. A cart rattled past; the driver gave her the wide berth owed to madwomen, which was fair.

The dying man. The stranger with the grey-coated shadow behind him and the blood coming out of him like he was a spilled inkwell. He had run past half of Corvane's night market — past fortune-tellers and hedge-witches and at least two actual magicians charging honest rates — and collapsed against *her* door. She had told herself it was chance. Chance was comfortable. Chance required nothing of her except bad luck.

But the map was right, in whatever way the map was ever right. He had been looking for her. Or looking for something she had, something in the blood, some knack the doors — the seven knots, the seven thresholds — required.

*The toll was never coin,* the voices had said. *The toll was always this.*

She walked on. North. Toward the little drawing of the tollhouse and the knot of script coiled around it like a sleeping adder.

* * *

On the sixth evening she reached the foothills, and on the seventh she saw the causeway.

It stretched across a wide brown marsh toward the northern hills, a mile of raised stone road, ancient, cracked, lined on both sides with leaning marker-stones so weathered that only their shadows still had shapes. The locals at the last farmstead had called it the Old Toll Road and then had refused, with that same practiced blankness, to say what it was a toll *for*.

Ines stood at the causeway's foot as the sun went down behind her, laying her shadow out long across the stones ahead, and unrolled the map one more time.

The first knot of script pulsed faintly under her thumb, up past the marsh, in the hills. Half a day's walk beyond the causeway. The tollhouse.

"Right," she told the map. "One door. One memory, if your arithmetic holds. I can spare one. Probably."

*I wouldn't,* said the map.

"You don't get a vote."

*I'm the only company you have. Be nicer.*

She was rolling the oilskin closed when she noticed the marker-stones.

Each one bore a name. Worn, yes — centuries of rain had eaten them down to whispers — but names, dozens of them, running the whole length of the causeway on both sides, and as the sunset struck them low and red, Ines realized she could *hear* them. Faintly. The way you hear a hive through a wall. Not the usual dead-murmur she carried everywhere, the ambient static of her life. These were organized. Waiting. They were not the scattered dead of accidents and fevers and old age.

These were people who had died *in one place, on purpose, at the same time*, and they had been waiting here, patient as the stones themselves, for something to come up the road.

For someone to come up the road.

Her hand drifted to her knife, absurdly — as if steel mattered against a mile of listening gravestones — and she was still standing there, telling herself firmly that ghosts in formation were not necessarily hostile ghosts, when she heard footsteps behind her.

Quick ones. Angry ones. The footsteps of someone who had been following her for days and had decided, somewhere back along the road, to stop following and start arriving.

Ines turned.

A woman stood at the foot of the causeway with a sword already drawn and grief worn openly, like a blade of its own, and she said, in a voice scraped raw:

"You're the bone-carver. The one my brother died in front of."

The marker-stones, all down the mile of road, went very, very quiet — the silence of an audience settling in to watch.
