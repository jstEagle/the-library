## 5. Tam

Tam had been following the bone-carver for six days, and in that time she had learned three things.

First: the woman walked like someone who had spent ten years practicing how to be overlooked. She kept to the edges of roads, wore colors the color of nothing, and when she passed other travelers she looked at their boots, not their faces. It was good work. Tam had nearly lost her twice on the second day, and Tam had been taught tracking by a brother who could follow a hawk through a snowstorm.

Second: she talked to herself. Constantly. Low murmurs under her breath, her lips moving like a woman counting coins she didn't trust. At first Tam thought it was madness. Then, watching her haggling at a crossroads stall — arguing a farmer down from an absurd price for dried plums with the cheerful ruthlessness of a fishwife — Tam decided it was probably just madness of the ordinary kind. The kind that came from being alone too long.

Third: she was not what Tam had expected, which was infuriating, because Tam had expected a murderer, and murderers were simple. Murderers ran, or they lied badly, or they had knives and no patience. This one bought plums.

"She didn't kill him," said a voice at Tam's elbow.

Tam did not jump. She had trained herself out of jumping years ago, though it cost her something every time. She turned. The old man who had spoken was leaning on the fence beside her, chewing something toothless and brown. He hadn't been there a moment ago. None of them were ever there a moment ago.

"Nobody asked you," Tam said.

"You're asking everybody," the old man said. "You've asked the ferryman, the toll-keeper's widow, two drovers, and a child. The child told you the bone-carver has a crooked nose. Very useful intelligence." He nodded down the road, where Ines Montrose was indeed walking, small and gray as a moth against the dusk. "She talks to us. Did you know that? Ten years, and she's never once pretended we aren't here."

"I know," Tam said, before she could stop herself.

The old man smiled with his three remaining teeth. "Yes. You would."

He wandered off then, the way they always did, dissolving into the hedgerow like smoke deciding to be a hedge. Tam stood in the cold and hated him a little, because he had said *us*, and because some traitorous part of her had understood exactly what he meant.

Her brother had talked to them too, near the end. That was the thing nobody warned you about people who heard the dead: they caught it like a fever, or maybe they'd always had it and only admitted it when dying made honesty cheap. In his last letter — the one that had sent her north, the one she'd read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth — Merric had written: *I've found a door out of Vesper, Tam. I know how mad that sounds. I know what you'll say. But there's a woman in Corvane who can open it.*

And then he had died on a bone-carver's floor with his blood soaking into somebody else's boards, and the woman had vanished north with his map, and every answer Tam had beaten out of Corvane's gutters said the same thing: inquisitors came asking after a bone-carver, and the next morning the bone-carver ran.

Guilty people ran. That was the whole architecture of justice, wasn't it? Simple as a knife.

*She didn't kill him,* the dead kept saying, in their various rotten voices, and Tam kept telling them to shut up, and they kept not shutting up, because the dead had never once in her life done anything she asked.

The causeway began where the road left the hills and went out across the flooded valley on a spine of old stone, half a mile of black water on either side, the drowned country's rooftops breaking the surface below like knuckles through skin. There was no way around it. The map — Merric's map, the one Tam had memorized from a single furious hour with it before the trail went cold — put the first threshold on the far side.

So Tam waited until dusk, and stepped out of the reeds onto the causeway, and drew her brother's knife.

* * *

The bone-carver saw her twenty paces off and stopped. Good instincts, Tam noted grimly. Most people would have kept walking and hoped. This one stopped, weighed the water on both sides, and visibly decided that a stranger with a knife on a narrow bridge was better odds than black water full of drowned houses.

"Evening," the bone-carver said. Her voice was steady. Her hand, drifting toward her coat pocket, was less so. "If this is robbery, I should tell you honestly that I have eleven coppers and a very opinionated bag of plums."

"My name is Tam Vael." She let the name sit there. Watched for the flinch. "Merric Vael was my brother."

There it was. Not a flinch, exactly — more like a door closing behind the eyes, quick and practiced. "I'm sorry for your loss," the bone-carver said.

"He died in your workshop."

"Yes."

"With inquisitors hunting him."

"Yes."

"And the next morning you burned your stock and ran north carrying his map."

The bone-carver's chin came up a fraction. "You've been talking to the market gossips. They charge less than I do and know half as much."

"Give me the map."

"No."

The word landed flat and final between them, and Tam felt something in her chest unclench — relief, horribly, at having her worst suspicion confirmed, because certainty was easier than doubt. "No?"

"No," Ines Montrose said. "He gave it to me. He made me swear. Whatever you think happened on my floor — and I can see exactly what you think, you have your brother's eyes and none of his patience — I swore an oath over a dying man, and I don't break those. It's the only rule I've got left."

"You don't even know what's on it."

"I know enough." Something moved behind her face then, something tired and old. "I know it's written in a dead language that isn't dead. I know the ink remembers things. And I know that men in grey coats tore apart my workshop looking for it, which means it's worth killing for, which means I'm not handing it to the first angry woman with a knife, no matter whose sister she is."

Tam came forward. She hadn't decided to; her body had simply concluded the conversation was over. The bone-carver backed up two steps, and her hand came out of her pocket, and there was a little carved thing in it — a sliver of bone, no bigger than a finger joint, wrapped in wire.

"That's far enough," the bone-carver said, and her voice changed. Dropped into something lower, older, like a well. "I mean it. I'd rather not—"

"You'd rather not what?" Tam laughed, and hated how it cracked. "Hex me? You sell love charms to farm girls. My brother bled out on your floor while you—"

"While I held his head and listened to him die," the bone-carver snapped, and for one moment she wasn't small at all. "Eleven hours, if you want the truth of it. Eleven hours of him telling me about a sister who'd come after me with a knife because grief needs somewhere to go. He said you'd be fair. He said you were the fairest person he knew." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "He was wrong about a lot of things, but I didn't think he was wrong about that."

The words went in like a splinter. Tam lunged.

Later, she would reconstruct the fight in pieces and never quite make them fit. She remembered the feint high, the cut low, the bone-carver twisting away with surprising speed — remembered the knife catching on the woman's sleeve, tearing cloth, drawing a line of red along her forearm — remembered thinking *good, now we're honest* —

And then the fog came up off the water.

Not fog. Tam knew fog. This rose too fast and moved with purpose, curling across the causeway in ropes and tendrils, and the temperature dropped so hard her breath cracked in the air, and out of the mist on either side of the bridge came shapes. Standing on the water. Standing *in* the water, hip-deep among the drowned rooftops, dozens of them, scores, gray figures with gray faces, and every one of them was looking at her.

They didn't move toward her. That was the worst part. They simply stood, in perfect silence, arranged along both sides of the causeway like a crowd at an execution, waiting to see what she'd do.

"They don't like knives," the bone-carver said quietly. She was pale and breathing hard, clutching her cut arm, but she wasn't surprised. Of course she wasn't surprised. "Especially here. This is their country, underneath. Every house down there is somebody's house. Put it away. Please. Before they decide you're a Warden."

"I'm not afraid of ghosts," Tam said, and her voice came out thin and stupid, because she was, suddenly, enormously, in a way that started in her knees.

"I know. That's why they came out." The bone-carver lowered her own hands slowly. "They don't usually show themselves. They did it for me. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They *know* me. Ten years of listening to them, carving their names, carrying their last words to whoever needed them — and tonight they stood up out of the water for a stranger they've never met, because she was holding a knife on me." Her eyes were bright and wet in the dying light. "Ask them. Go on. Ask them what happened in my workshop. They'll tell you. They tell everyone everything, that's the whole curse of them. Ask them about your brother."

Tam's throat wouldn't work. But the dead could hear a question whether it was spoken or not — that much she'd learned young, hiding under tables while her grandmother muttered at empty corners.

The nearest figure, a woman in the rotted finery of someone who'd drowned dressed for a festival, raised one gray hand. Not toward Tam. Toward the bone-carver. And then, one by one, all along the causeway, the others did the same — dozens of hands rising out of the mist, pointing, unmistakable, unanimous.

*Her. Trust her.* Or perhaps: *This one. This is ours.*

Tam's knife arm dropped. The blade felt suddenly obscene, a child's tantrum made of steel. The mist began to thin almost immediately, the figures sinking back into the dark water without hurry, without malice, the way a congregation sits down. The last to go was the drowned festival-woman, who turned her gray face to Tam for a long moment — and there was something in it, some expression Tam couldn't parse and would spend weeks trying to, before she sank.

Silence. Real silence, for the first time in six days. Tam realized the dead had been murmuring around her the whole journey, and she'd stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing a river.

"Well," said the bone-carver. She sat down abruptly on the causeway stones, right there in the middle of the bridge, and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Well. That was going to happen eventually, I suppose. I've been dreading it for six days. You walk loudly, by the way. For a tracker. Like a wardrobe falling downstairs."

"I do not walk loudly."

"You announce yourself to three counties." The bone-carver tipped her head back and looked up at Tam, sitting there in the road dust with blood drying on her sleeve, and there was — gods help her — humor in her face. Tired, ragged, but real. "Are you going to sit down? The stones are cold but they're honest company. Better than most."

Tam looked at her brother's knife. She sheathed it. Then, because her legs had quietly stopped taking orders, she sat down on the causeway with a murderer-or-not, half a mile from either shore, above a country full of the drowned.

For a while neither of them spoke. The water lapped. Somewhere below, a bell in a drowned church tower shifted on its rope and rang once, a single bloated note, and the bone-carver flinched and Tam pretended not to notice.

"Merric," Tam said finally. "What was he like? At the end."

Ines Montrose was quiet long enough that Tam thought she wouldn't answer. "Angry," she said at last. "Frightened, but angrier than frightened, which takes a particular kind of courage. He kept apologizing for bleeding on my floor. Kept making jokes. Terrible ones." A pause. "He said you'd come after me. He said — I'm going to say this wrong, I've rehearsed it for six days and I'm still going to say it wrong — he said, *she'll put a knife to your throat and then she'll apologize and then she'll carry your pack the rest of the way, because that's who she is.*"

Tam stared out at the black water. Her eyes were burning and she refused, absolutely refused, to let them do anything about it. "That sounds like him."

"He also said you snore."

"He *did not.*"

"He was very specific. Said it was like a mill grinding wet grain."

"I'm going to kill him," Tam said, and then heard it, and closed her eyes, and the bone-carver had the grace not to laugh, or at least to do it silently, which Tam could somehow hear anyway.

"The map goes north," Ines said, when the moment had passed. "To seven doors. I don't fully understand it yet — the map doesn't explain itself, it *remembers* at you, which is worse. But the first threshold is past this causeway, in the ruins of the old tollhouse. And whatever's happening, the Wardens are behind us and getting closer, and I can't outrun them alone anymore. I've tried. Six days of trying." She turned and looked at Tam straight on, and Tam saw that under the weariness there was iron, actual iron, the kind you don't forge so much as survive into. "So. Come with me. Not because you trust me — you'd be a fool to, and you clearly aren't a fool, you found me when the Wardens couldn't. Come because you want to watch me. Because if I turn out to be everything you fear, you'll be right there."

"And if you're not?"

"Then you'll get to see your brother's door opened," the bone-carver said softly. "Whatever's on the other side of it. I swear that too."

Tam sat with it. The cold stones. The drowned city breathing beneath them. Somewhere in the mist behind them, faint as a thought, the dead had started murmuring again — and for the first time, Tam noticed, they weren't saying *she didn't kill him*. They were saying something else, over and over, in a hundred ruined voices, soft as rain:

*North. North. Take her north.*

"All right," Tam said. "But I watch you. Every mile. You so much as look at that map sideways, we renegotiate."

"Fair," said the bone-carver, and got up, and offered Tam a dried plum from her pocket with the solemnity of a treaty signing. It was bruised and too sweet and the best thing Tam had eaten in a week.

They walked on together across the causeway as the last light failed, two women and a countryful of the dead, and ahead of them, just visible against the deepening sky, the broken silhouette of the tollhouse waited on its hill — and as they drew near, Tam heard it too, faintly, impossibly: a sound like a seam in the world, humming.
