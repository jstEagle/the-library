## 7. Roadside Justice

The tithe-gang found the family before we did.

Tam heard them first — she always heard men like that first, the way a dog hears a storm — the jingle of a collection chain, the wet slap of boots in mud, and under it all a sound she knew from her childhood and had spent eleven years trying to forget: a woman singing to a child in Vesperan, low, so as not to be heard.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen milestone on the old north road, a man and woman and two children, their cart broken-axled, their belongings wrapped in blankets that had once been good wool. The kind of family that had walked out of somewhere that no longer existed and was walking toward somewhere that would not have them. Tam knew the shape of it. She had been the child under the blanket once, listening to her mother sing quiet enough to fool the dark.

And now six men with iron-tipped staves stood around them in a loose crescent, and one of them — big, red-faced, wearing a tithe-master's brass badge on a chain — was reading aloud from a ledger as though it were scripture.

"—unregistered persons, three adults, two minors, carrying goods of unstamped origin. By order of the Provincial Warden's Office, all property is subject to seizure in lieu of registration fees. Fees assessed at—" he squinted at the page, enjoying himself "—forty crowns per head."

"We don't have forty crowns," the father said. He said it steadily, which took more courage than screaming would have. "We don't have four."

"Then we'll take it in kind." The tithe-master smiled. He had the smile of a man who had never once been interrupted. "The ox. The blankets. And the girl can come along to work off the balance. Kitchen duty. Honest work, for people of her—"

He didn't finish the word. Tam was already moving.

Later — much later, when she could think about it without her hands shaking — she would admit that Ines had said something first. Something sensible. *Wait. Let me count them. There are six, Tam, there are six of them.* But Ines said it in the voice of a person negotiating with weather, and Tam had stopped negotiating with the world a long time ago.

She came out of the gorse with her knife already open and put it against the tithe-master's throat before his badge finished swinging.

"Drop the ledger," she said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rage: it is very organized. It has lists. It keeps accounts going back years. Tam's rage had been keeping accounts since the night soldiers came to her village with grey coats and a writ that unmade the name of everything she loved, and every tithe-gang, every checkpoint clerk, every smug little man with a brass badge had a line in that ledger, and this man had just written *the girl can come along* in it, and the ink was still wet.

"Easy," the tithe-master said, hands up, voice going smooth. "Easy, now. You don't want to do anything foolish. This is lawful collection. There's procedures."

"There are," Tam agreed. "I've read them. Did you know a tithe-collector may not seize persons? Only goods. Page nine. You were about to seize a person."

"I misspoke."

"You did." She pressed the knife a fraction closer. "Say what you actually meant."

Behind him, his five men had arranged themselves into the particular formation of men deciding whether their wages covered this. One had a crossbow, half-raised, doing arithmetic about distances.

And then Ines was beside her — how did she move that quietly, a woman who limped slightly and complained constantly — saying, low and reasonable, "Tam. Six of them. The crossbow. Think."

"I am thinking."

"You're not. You're remembering. It's different, and it's worse."

Which was infuriating, because it was true, and because the bone-carver had known her for less than a month and should not have been allowed to say things like that yet.

The crossbowman made his decision. He raised the weapon properly, and Tam saw the bolt's future path clear as a written line — through Ines, who had stepped stupidly, bravely, into the space between Tam and the gang — and something in her went cold and simple.

She moved. She cut the tithe-master's cheek — a warning, only a warning, she told herself — spun him into the crossbow's line, and shouted, and the world got loud.

It should have ended there. A scuffle, a retreat, six bullies deciding an unarmed-looking pair of women wasn't worth it. That was the plan Ines had presumably been constructing out loud the whole time.

But the dead were there too.

Tam felt them before she understood them — a pressure in the air like the moment before summer lightning, a smell of river-stones and old smoke. The horses the gang had tied at the roadside screamed and tore loose. The crossbowman's bolt went wide into a fencepost. And the tithe-master, stumbling back from Tam's knife, trod on ground that was suddenly, impossibly soft, and sank to his knee in mud that had been hard-packed road a heartbeat earlier, and came up white-faced, staring at nothing over Tam's shoulder, at something standing behind her in the rain-smell air.

"The road," he whispered. "The road's *wrong*—"

"They're Vesperan," one of his men said, backing away, pointing not at Tam but at the refugee family, at the mother who had gone very still, at the grandmother's charm-knot hanging at the cart. "That's Vesperan craft, they've called something up, they're *hexed*—"

"They haven't hexed anything," Tam said. Her voice came out strange and flat. "You're just finally seeing what's been following you your whole life. It's only ever been waiting for you to deserve it."

The gang ran. All five of the running ones. They left the ledger in the mud, and the badge-chain snapped in the tithe-master's haste, and then it was just him, kneeling in the wrongness, and Tam standing over him with a knife, and the whole gray afternoon holding its breath.

* * *

"He's helpless," Ines said. "Tam. Look at him. He's done."

He was. On his knees, cheek bleeding where her blade had opened it, hands raised, blubbering about procedures, about how he had children, about how everyone did this, everyone collected, it was just the way things were.

Tam looked at him and saw a line of faces going back eleven years. The clerk who'd stamped her family's names out of the rolls while eating an apple. The neighbor who'd informed on the ferryman for teaching letters. Every small man who'd said *it's just the way things are* while the way things were swallowed a country whole.

"This is how it stays the way things are," she said. "People like you. Doing this. Being forgiven for it."

"Tam." Ines's hand touched her shoulder. Gentle. Infuriatingly gentle. "The family is watching. The children are watching. Whatever you do next, they carry it. Is that what you want to give them?"

"It's what was given to me," Tam said, "and I turned out fine," and heard, even as she said it, how much of a lie it was, and hated Ines a little for hearing it too.

"He'll report us," Ines said. "He'll bring the Wardens down on those people you're trying to protect. Killing him isn't justice, it's just noise. Walk away. Make him live with having knelt in the mud while a woman with a knife explained page nine to him. That's a longer sentence than death."

"It really isn't."

"No," Ines agreed quietly. "But it's a sentence *we* can serve alongside him. That matters. That's the only thing that matters, out here, with nothing above us but weather."

And Tam — gods help her — hesitated. She felt the knife-hand waver, felt the ledger of her rage flip open to a fresh page, felt the terrible arithmetic of mercy begin.

The tithe-master chose that moment to lunge for her ankle.

It was pathetic. A drowning-man grab, more panic than violence. But her body had been trained by worse years than these, and her body answered before her mind could vote. Knife. Throat. Done.

He fell backward into the mud and did not get up, and the road went very quiet, and the rain began, softly, like an apology no one had asked for.

* * *

Ines did not scream. Tam almost wished she had. Screaming would have been simpler to answer.

Instead the bone-carver stood over the body for a long moment, rain beading in her dark hair, and then crouched and closed the man's eyes with two fingers, briskly, the way she handled everything — like material, like work.

"You know what I hear right now?" Ines said. Her voice was steady. That was the worst part. "I hear him. He's still here, the way they're all still here, and he's confused, and he's calling for someone named Marta. His wife, I think. Or his mother. He can't tell anymore, which tells you what kind of man he was." She stood. "He'll be calling for her for a long time. Longer than he deserved. And I will hear every hour of it, because that's my gift, and my gift doesn't care about page nine."

"Ines—"

"Don't." Ines held up a hand. Rain ran off her fingers. "Don't tell me he deserved it. Maybe he did. I'm not arguing the accounting with you, Tam, I'd lose. What I'm telling you is that you just made me a witness, and I didn't get a vote, and neither did those children." She pointed at the family, huddled by their cart, the mother's hand clapped over the younger child's eyes a moment too late. "You wanted to save them from men who treat people as things to collect. Fine. Good. Necessary. But you didn't ask what saving costs the saved. Nobody ever does. Everybody just spends other people's innocence like it grows on trees."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ines said. "It isn't. Nothing out here is. Welcome to the road." She turned toward the family, and her voice changed entirely — warm, easy, a market-voice, a stranger's trustworthy voice. "Hello! Your axle's cracked clean through. We've a spare in our pack, if you'll let us help. No charge. We're going north ourselves."

Tam stood in the rain with a cooling corpse and a ledger full of names, and marveled, as she had marveled every day since the causeway, at how the woman could hold both things at once — the knife and the kindness — without either one leaking into the other.

Tam couldn't. Tam had only ever had the knife. Watching Ines was like watching someone juggle with one hand tied behind their back, and it made her feel clumsy and seen and, worst of all, *known*, which was the thing she had built her whole life to avoid.

* * *

They fixed the axle by firelight. The family's name was Corvane-ish, worn smooth — Pell — but the grandmother's charm-knot was pure Vesperan weave, eight-strand, the kind you couldn't buy anymore, only inherit. When Ines saw it she went still, and the grandmother saw her go still, and the two women looked at each other across the fire the way people look at each other across a border that officially does not exist.

"You're from the old country," the grandmother said. Not a question. In Vesperan.

"My mother was," Tam said, before she could stop herself.

"And you?" the grandmother asked Ines.

"A bone-carver," Ines said smoothly, in trade-tongue. "From Corvane. Born and bred."

Which was a lie, and Tam knew it was a lie, and filed it away with all the other small locked drawers the bone-carver carried — the way she read maps too fluently, the way thresholds seemed to lean toward her, the way the dead treated her less like a visitor and more like a niece. Everyone had locked drawers. Tam had a whole cabinet of them. But she was starting to suspect Ines's contained something bigger than smuggled grief.

The elder Pell girl, maybe ten, crept over to Tam while the adults talked axles and routes. She looked at the knife on Tam's belt with the frank appraisal of childhood.

"Did you kill that man?" she asked.

"Yes," Tam said. Adults lied to children about death; she'd been lied to, and it hadn't helped.

The girl considered this. "Was he bad?"

"He was doing a bad job. Some people think that's the same thing. Some people think it isn't. Grown-ups have been fighting about it for a very long time and we've mostly settled on shouting."

The girl nodded gravely, as if this confirmed her suspicions about grown-ups, and went back to the fire.

Later, when the family slept, Ines sat down next to Tam on the cart's tailboard and handed her half of a flat bread, torn unevenly, the bigger half offered outward, which was its own small argument.

"I'm not sorry," Tam said.

"I know."

"But I heard what you said. About the sentence we serve alongside him." She turned the bread in her fingers. "I'll carry it differently. Next time. I'll — ask first. Before the knife."

"That's all I wanted." Ines chewed. Swallowed. Watched the fire. "For the record, the dead helped you today. The road softened under him. That wasn't chance."

"I know. I felt them."

"They're getting louder the further north we go. Like they know where we're headed." Ines paused, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped its market-warmth and become something careful. "There's a town ahead. Three days, maybe four. On the map it isn't there. On any map it isn't there. But the ferryman's marks on the map's margin say otherwise, and the dead keep humming a name at me when I sleep — a name I can't quite catch, like a song from another room." She looked at Tam sideways. "A town that was erased, Tam. Whole. Not burned — *erased*. Ledgers rewritten, records pulled, roads rerouted around it. I've heard of villages being forgotten. I've never heard of forgetting being this thorough."

Tam stared into the fire and felt the old cold walk up her spine, the cold from the night the grey coats came, the cold of paperwork as a weapon.

"Neither have I," she said slowly. "But I've heard of someone thorough enough."

They looked at each other, and neither said the title aloud. They didn't need to. Somewhere south of them, in a tower full of clean ledgers, a man was presumably already reading a tithe-gang's report about a woman with a knife and a bone-carver who argued for mercy — and about a road that had turned traitor beneath a collector's knees.

Three days to a town that didn't exist. Tam tore her half of the bread in two and gave the bigger piece back to Ines, because apparently the argument wasn't over, it was just adjourned.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch. And Ines — if the dead hum that town's name tonight, wake me. I want to hear it too."

Ines nodded, lay back against the cart wheel, and within minutes her breathing slowed — and then, just at the edge of sleep, her lips moved, shaping a word over and over, soft as rain.

Tam leaned close to catch it.

The name the dead were humming was one Tam's mother used to sing, at the end of a lullaby, like a blessing.

It was the town where Tam had been born.
