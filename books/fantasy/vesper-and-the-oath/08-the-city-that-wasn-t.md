## 8. The City That Wasn't

The town appeared the way a lie does — all at once, and only when you were already committed to it.

They had walked three days out of the hills, following the map's second thread, and Ines had been watching for it since morning. The map didn't draw towns. It drew *gaps* — small blank places where the ink went thin and the dead voices underneath it went quiet in a way that wasn't silence but holding of breath. This gap had been widening for an hour, a pale absence on the oilskin, and then the road bent around a stand of birches and there it was: slate roofs, a crooked bell tower, laundry strung between houses like bunting, smoke rising from chimneys with the particular confidence of a place that has been making supper for a very long time.

"That's not on any survey I've seen," Tam said.

"You've seen surveys?"

"I've seen everything," Tam said, which was her way of admitting she hadn't.

The town had a name, or the ghost of one. A sign at the bridge read **WELKIN'S REST** in paint so fresh it looked embarrassed, as if someone had repainted it recently and often. Beneath it, older letters showed through like scars: something that might have been *Vess-* before weather and whitewash got to it.

Ines stopped at the bridge's edge and listened.

She always listened now; it was cheaper than asking questions. The dead in Corvane had been a murmur, a market-crowd hum she'd learned to walk through without flinching. Out here they were different — sparser, older, and lately, since the first threshold, *organized*, as if the whole country's dead had heard she was coming and were arranging themselves into something like a reception committee.

Here, though, the dead were strange. Not absent. *Careful.*

"They're whispering," she said.

"Dead do that."

"Not like this." She tilted her head, listening harder. "It's like — you know when you're in a house and someone upstairs stops talking because they've heard you come in? It's like that."

Tam's hand drifted toward her knife, which was Tam's answer to most atmospheric phenomena. "Then let's be quiet guests."

* * *

The trouble started at the inn, which was called the Ledger & Lamp, and the trouble was a woman named Marta who kept the taproom and was perhaps fifty and could not stop looking at Ines's hands.

Not her face. Her hands. Bone-carvers' hands are distinctive — nicked, stained faintly grey at the fingertips from years of dust, one thumbnail permanently split. Marta looked at them the way you'd look at a dog you thought was dead walking through your kitchen.

"Carver," Marta said. Not a question.

"Charms mostly," Ines said. "Small things."

"My mother wore a charm." Marta set down the tankard she was polishing with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "A little fish, carved from riverbone. She said her grandmother made it. She said—" Marta stopped. Her jaw worked. "There's no record of my mother, you know. Or this inn. Or the ferry. The assessor came through eleven years ago with his book and wrote down every roof and every cow, and when he got to us he turned two pages at once and rode on. Didn't see us. His horse drank at our trough and he didn't see us."

"That must have been frightening," Ines offered carefully.

"Frightening?" Marta laughed, and it was a terrible sound, bright and cracked. "Girl, it was the best day of our lives. We'd been waiting ten years to be forgotten properly. He just confirmed the work was done." She leaned across the bar. "But you saw us. Walking up the road, plain as sin. So either the forgetting is wearing thin—" and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—or you're carrying something that remembers."

Ines said nothing, which was its own confession. Beside her, Tam had gone very still in the way of a person calculating exits.

Marta read them both like a ledger. "Room's at the back. Two coppers. And if grey coats come asking, we've never had lodgers, we've never had an inn, and I have never in my life seen a bone-carver." She smiled, and it almost reached her eyes. "I've had practice."

* * *

The evening brought more of it, and each piece of it wronger than the last.

An old man by the fire insisted, cheerfully, to anyone who'd listen, that the bridge over the Welkin had been built by his grandfather — and then, catching himself, corrected that it had always been there, no builder at all, stone from stone, and then laughed at himself and said his grandfather *had* built it, damn the assessors, and what were they going to do, unbuild it?

Two women argued over whether the town's name had ever been anything but Welkin's Rest. One of them had been christened in a church whose records listed no such church. A boy of maybe twelve asked his father why the old folks got the name of the river wrong — it was the Welkin, everyone knew it was the Welkin — and his father, who was forty if he was a day, said gently, "It used to be called something else, lad," and the boy said, "No it wasn't," with the serene certainty of the young, and the father just looked at him, and looked at him, and finally said, "No. I suppose it wasn't," and drank his ale like a man taking medicine.

Ines sat with her back to the wall and felt the ground tilt under everything.

She had spent ten years believing the world was solid in exactly one way: that records were records. Names in books, lines on maps, taxes levied and graves dug — these were the bones of the world, and bones didn't lie. Bones were all she'd ever had that told the truth. But here was a town that existed, fully, stubbornly, smoking its chimneys and raising its children, while every book in every archive said otherwise — and worse, here were people who *agreed with the books*. Not the old ones. The young ones. The forgetting hadn't just been written down; it had been *taught*, passed down like eye color, until the children defended the erasure more fiercely than the clerks who'd made it.

"The Warden did this," she murmured to Tam. "One man did this?"

"One man with an army of clerks," Tam said. She was eating with the focused efficiency of someone who trusted meals more than conversations. "Erasure isn't a spell, carver. It's paperwork. Ten thousand ledgers, a generation of schoolmasters, and anyone loud enough to object quietly buried." She paused. "Sometimes not quietly."

"You say that like you admire the technique."

"I say that like I understand it." Tam's jaw tightened. "My brother understood it too, at the end. Said the scariest thing about Vesper wasn't that it fell. It's that within five years, you couldn't prove it had ever stood."

The fire popped. Across the room, the old man had begun singing under his breath — a tune Ines didn't know, in words that weren't quite Corvanish, vowels rounded wrong, and half the room over forty had gone quiet to listen, and the other half hadn't noticed at all.

The dead whispered on, careful as mice behind wainscoting.

* * *

The ferryman found them at dawn.

He was waiting at the river stairs below the inn, a barrel-chested old man mending a net that Ines suspected was purely decorative, and he said, before either of them spoke, "You'll be wanting the north bank, and you'll be wanting it before the mist burns off, and you'll be wanting me, because I'm the only boat that crosses anymore."

"Do you know us?" Tam asked, hand near her knife out of habit.

"I know the shape of trouble, and you're both wearing it." He jerked his chin at Ines. "And I know a listener when I see one. My wife was one. Heard the drowned, she did, right up till the end." Something moved behind his face and was put away. "Name's Odo. Get in."

The boat was flat-bottomed and patient. The mist lay on the water like a held note. As Odo poled them out, Ines listened to the river, and the river was *full* — not with the scattered dead of the road but with a congregation, hundreds deep, all of them pressed close beneath the surface, all of them silent in that deliberate, listening-back way.

"They know you," Odo said, watching her face. "Don't look surprised. Everything on this river knows you're coming. Word travels fast among folk who aren't in a hurry."

"What are they waiting for?"

"Same as the rest of us." He poled, unhurried. "Ten years ago I ferried a different kind of passenger. Clerks, mostly. Grey coats. Men with boxes of documents going south and men with shovels going wherever shovels were needed. They paid in new coin and never learned my name, which suited me fine." The pole dipped, rose, dripped. "Last one I carried was different. Young fellow. Grey coat, but it fit him wrong, like a borrowed coat always does. He stood where you're standing and looked at the water and said, 'How deep is it here?' And I told him: deeper than the ledgers say. And he laughed. First and last time I saw him laugh. He said, 'Deeper than the ledgers say. That's the truest thing anyone's told me in years.'"

Ines's mouth had gone dry. "This man. What was his name?"

"He gave it as Casimir. Whether that was his then or his after, I couldn't say." Odo looked at her steadily. "You know the name."

Everyone knew the name. The Warden. The grey hand of the realm, the man whose inquisitors swept markets and unmade towns. Ines had spent ten years being small specifically so that name would never turn toward her.

"He wasn't born grey," Odo said. "That's what nobody remembers, because he made sure of it. He came *from* here. From across the water. From the country that isn't." He tapped the butt of the pole against the hull, twice, like punctuation. "Whatever Vesper was, he was Vesperan to the marrow. And whatever was done to it — he didn't just allow it. By every account the old ones will still give, drunk enough, frightened enough — he *wrote* it. Held the pen himself."

The mist was thinning. On the north bank, the far shore resolved out of nothing, dark trees and a road going up.

"Why tell us this?" Tam asked. Her voice was flat, but Ines had learned to hear the strain under it. "Why help us at all?"

Odo shipped the pole and considered the water.

"Because my wife heard the drowned calling for ten years, and near the end she stopped hearing them as strangers." His voice roughened. "She said some of them sounded like family. Cousins. Folk whose names she'd been made to forget but whose voices she'd have known anywhere. She died angry about it. Angry's a poor inheritance, but it's the one she left me." He nodded at Ines. "You're going north to open something. Don't insult us both by denying it — the whole river knows. So I'll give you passage and one warning, free of charge, and then you're out of my boat and out of my life, and I'll deny ever seeing you."

"Say it, then," Ines said.

"The Warden isn't hunting a map." Odo's eyes were pale and certain as riverstone. "Men don't raise armies over paper. He's hunting what the map *opens*. He helped bury this country once, and I think — my wife thought, before the end — that he buried it for a reason. That there's something under Vesper that even Vesperans feared, and the erasure wasn't cruelty." He leaned closer, and the mist curled between them. "It was a lid."

The boat bumped the far shore. Tam stepped out first, boots finding the mud, and Ines followed, and the moment her foot touched land the dead rose around her like a tide — not whispering now but *singing*, low and vast, a chord held for ten years finally allowed to move —

— and beneath it, at the very bottom of the sound, something else. Something that was not grief and not memory and not patient at all.

Something that had noticed the lid lifting.

Odo was already poling away, not looking back. "Mind the north road!" he called once, gruffly, as if giving directions to a fair. "And mind what you wake!"

Then the mist took him, and Ines stood on the shore of a country that officially did not exist, holding a map that trembled faintly in her hands — not from wind — while far off, downstream, a grey-coated rider crested the southern ridge and stopped, and looked, and began to descend.
