## 9. The Warden's Ear

The interrogation room in the Warden's Bastion had been designed, three centuries ago, by a man who believed that fear lived in architecture. High windows too narrow to climb through. A table bolted to the floor at precisely the height that made sitting uncomfortable but not painful — discomfort, the old designers understood, was more persuasive than pain, because pain gave a prisoner something to be brave about.

Casimir Vane had sat on both sides of that table. He had never told anyone this, and he never would, and it was one of the small ironies he carried like a stone in his boot: the room had been built to frighten people like him, and now he owned it.

The prisoner's name was Aldric Fen. Junior inquisitor, third year of service, a round-faced young man from the river provinces who had joined the Wardens because his father had been a tithe-clerk and Aldric had wanted, in the vague way of twenty-two-year-olds, to matter more than a ledger. He had been captured four days ago outside Corvane, carrying dispatches he should have burned, and he had spent those four days in a cell being fed adequately and left entirely alone, which was Casimir's preferred opening move. Solitude did most of the work. Fear only needed to arrive afterward, like a landlord collecting rent on a house the tenant had already wrecked.

Casimir entered at the seventh hour after dawn, carrying two cups of tea.

This was deliberate. It always was. He set one cup before the prisoner and drank from the other himself, unhurried, and watched Aldric Fen try to decide whether the tea was poisoned, drugged, or simply tea. The boy's eyes went wet with confusion. Good. Confusion was honest. A man who thought he knew what was happening could lie all day; a man who couldn't find the floor kept grabbing for whatever was nearest, which was usually the truth.

"Drink," Casimir said. "It's from the market on Tallow Street. There's a woman there who adds honey and pretends it's her own idea. I've let her believe it for six years." He sat. "Aldric Fen. Third year. Your mother writes to you every fortnight and your handwriting in reply is better than your superiors' — I've read both. You are, by every account I have, a mediocre inquisitor with an unusually good memory."

"I—"

"You remembered the dispatches verbatim when they took them off you. That's why you're here instead of in a ditch. Sit still."

Aldric sat still. He looked, Casimir thought, like a man trying to remember whether he had ever actually wanted any of this, and finding the memory suspiciously thin. Casimir felt something that in a softer man might have been called sympathy. In him it was filed under *logistics*.

"Four days ago," Casimir said, "a man named Oren Sable died in the workshop of a bone-carver in Corvane's night market. Before that, Oren Sable stole something from my archive. Before that, Oren Sable was mine — trained, sworn, trusted. I want to know what he told the carver before he died. Not what he said. What he *told* her. There is a difference, and you're going to help me hear it."

"I don't know anything about—"

"Aldric." Casimir turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit so old he'd stopped noticing it. "You were assigned to the sweep of the night market two nights ago. You interviewed nine stallholders about a bone-carver. Your report says you found nothing. Your report is forty words long, which for you is roughly a sentence and a half, and you once wrote me six pages on the smuggling habits of eel-fishermen. So either you found nothing because there was nothing, which makes you useless, or you found nothing because you were told to find nothing, which makes you interesting. Which would you rather be?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, gulls argued over the Bastion's roofline — the sea had come inland over the centuries until the old fortress stood half-swallowed by salt marsh, and Casimir had long ago stopped minding the smell. It smelled, if anything, like honesty. Rot was honest.

"The sergeant told us to leave the bone-carver alone," Aldric said finally, very quietly. "Sergeant Halloway. He said she was already handled. That someone senior had claimed her."

"And did you believe him?"

"No." The word came out cracked. "Because Halloway doesn't say *handled*, sir. Halloway says *dead* or *arrested*. Handled is—" Aldric swallowed. "Handled is what we were told to say when we didn't want it written down who gave the order."

Casimir nodded slowly, as though this were mildly interesting news, when in fact it rearranged the entire shape of his week. Someone inside his own apparatus had put a hand over the bone-carver. Which meant someone inside his own apparatus knew what she was worth. Which meant the leak was not a trickle. It was a pipe.

"Describe the man who gave Halloway the instruction."

"I never saw him. It came as a note. Grey paper, no seal, but the hand was—" Aldric hesitated, then, with the terrible precision of the mediocre, produced something genuinely useful: "It was written in the old shorthand. The administrative one. Nobody under fifty uses it anymore."

Nobody under fifty used it because nobody under fifty had been taught it, because it had been retired along with everything else that smelled of a certain country that no longer appeared on maps. Casimir kept his face pleasant and his tea warm and felt, beneath both, the cold familiar turning of an old wound.

*Vesperan shorthand.* So one of the old brotherhood was still alive, still working, still — what? Protecting the girl? Hunting her for himself? With the old ones it was never possible to tell protection from predation. They had all learned their trade in the same schoolhouse, and the first lesson had been that mercy and theft wore identical gloves.

* * *

He dismissed Aldric Fen with a warning and a small purse of coin — enough to make the boy loyal for exactly as long as it took him to spend it — and climbed the spiral stair to his study alone, because Casimir Vane did not keep servants in the upper floors of the Bastion. There were things up there that no servant should dust.

The study had once been a chapel. He had removed the altar and replaced it with a table, and on the table lay the only object in the world he treated with reverence: a map of the northern provinces, drawn in his own hand, corrected eleven hundred times. Every road on it was accurate. Every town. Every ford, ferry, tollhouse, and ruin. And threaded through it, in ink the color of dried blood, were seven marks — seven places where the world had been stitched shut.

He stood over it the way other men stood over the graves of their wives.

People imagined — the inquisitors imagined, the ministers imagined, the prisoners in the lower cells certainly imagined — that Warden Casimir hunted sedition. That he was a bureaucrat of fear, a grey administrator of grey power. Let them imagine it. The misunderstanding was load-bearing.

What Casimir hunted was a wound.

Ten years ago, he had helped close one. He had been younger then, and Vesperan, and he had believed — right up until the night the sky over the capital turned the color of a bruise — that his country's scholars were merely curious. They had called it the Deep Door. They had called it *the way out*. Poets had written about it. Children had sung about it, in a language that now existed only in the mouths of old women pretending to forget. *The door out of Vesper,* the songs went, *where the road ends and the road home begins.*

They had dug down to it beneath the drowned quarter, and they had opened it a crack, and what had come through the crack had eaten the eastern districts in a single night — not burned, *eaten*, unmade, unwritten, whole streets gone from memory while the smoke was still rising. Casimir had watched his own mother's name slide out of his mind like water through fingers, and he had run to the Wardens, the enemy, the foreign grey coats, and he had knelt in the mud of a border post and given them everything: the locations, the rituals, the names of every scholar who had touched the work. He had helped them erase Vesper — its records, its language, its very mention — because erasure was the only seal that held. You could ward a door. But a country that had never existed could not dig one open again.

It had cost him everything except his life, and some nights he counted that a loss.

And now a dying fool of a former student had pressed a map into the hands of a bone-carver, and somewhere in the north the stitches were starting to itch.

Casimir poured himself a measure of plum brandy — Vesperan brandy, hoarded, the last bottle of a vineyard that no longer existed — and did not drink it. He never drank it. It was there to prove to himself that remembering was survivable.

* * *

The second visitor of the day arrived at dusk, escorted, wet, and furious, which for Sergeant Halloway constituted his natural state.

Halloway was a big man with a beard he trimmed to look military and hands he kept soft to look civilian, and he had served Casimir for eight years without once asking a question he wasn't paid to ask. Casimir valued this the way farmers value weather. He sat the sergeant down in the study — not the interrogation room; Halloway had earned furniture — and poured him tea, and waited.

"They slipped the net at the causeway," Halloway said. "The carver and a woman traveling with her. Sable's sister, by the look of her. Tam Sable. She killed two of my men with a boat-hook."

"Dead?"

"One of them. The other will wish he were."

"And the causeway tollhouse?"

Halloway's jaw worked. "Burned from the inside, sir. From the *inside*. And the locals won't talk. Not won't — can't, near enough. Ask them about the tollhouse and they look at you like you asked about their dead mother, and then they say there's never been a tollhouse there at all."

Casimir closed his eyes briefly. The pattern was older than the girl, older than the map, older than his own guilt: the land itself forgetting on command, the way a body goes numb around a wound. His seal, holding. Or fraying. With seals, he had learned, those were often the same thing.

"There's more," Halloway said, and hesitated, which was unlike him.

"Out with it."

"The fisherfolk at the flooded valley. My men tried to press a bargeman for information and the man went white as milk and said — he said the water's been *singing*. Whole families heard it. Said the drowned were calling names." Halloway shifted his bulk uncomfortably. "I flogged the lie out of him, sir. Standard procedure. But my corporal swears that when they rode the valley rim that night, he heard it too. Just for a moment. Like a choir under the water. And Corporal Vess has never lied to me in six years, mostly because he hasn't got the imagination."

Casimir set down his cup with great care, because his hand wished to do otherwise.

The drowned were calling names. Of course they were. The dead of the capital had gone into the flood with the rest of the erasure, sealed and silent, and silence, in Casimir's long experience, was not peace. Silence was pressure. And pressure, given ten years and a crack in the stitch, would find itself a voice.

Or a listener.

"Halloway," he said. "The bone-carver. Everything we know about her. Start with her name."

"Ines Montrose. Ten years in Corvane, licensed, quiet, pays her taxes, sells charms. Finger-bones, mostly. Little things. Nothing the guilds would call magic." Halloway grimaced. "There's a story the market folk tell, though. That her charms *work*. Not grand — nothing you'd notice unless you'd lost the thing she carved against. But the widow who wears one sleeps without dreaming of her husband. The sailor's knot-charm holds when rope should slip. Small mercies, the stallholders call it." He shrugged. "Superstition."

"Small mercies," Casimir repeated softly.

Because he knew what small mercies meant, in the trade of bones and voices. A carver who worked finger-bones and sold charms that eased grief was not doing craftwork. She was doing translation. She was hearing the dead dictate, and carving what they asked, and the dead — the dead were the one archive the Wardens had never managed to burn, because you cannot confiscate a ghost, and he had spent ten years telling himself that silence in the graveyards meant silence everywhere.

But if the drowned were singing, then the silence was breaking. And if it was breaking, it would break loudest around an ear.

An ear. One living ear in all the world that could hear what the seal had buried — could hear the door, could hear the lock, could hear, God help them all, the thing behind it turning over in its sleep. Oren Sable hadn't chosen her at random. Oren had known. Which meant the old brotherhood had known, which meant somewhere a Vesperan hand in grey shorthand was playing a game whose board Casimir could no longer see.

He rose and went to the window. Below, the marsh gleamed in the last light, and far out where the water met the sky lay the direction she was traveling — north, always north, toward the drowned valley, toward the capital, toward the door he had spent half his life and all his soul nailing shut.

They think I hunt a girl, he thought. They think I hunt a map.

He thought of his mother's name, the empty place where it had been, the way he sometimes reached for it in the dark the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth. He thought of the eastern districts, unwritten. He thought of the singing water.

I hunt the wound, he told the darkening glass. Before it opens again. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs.

It was almost true, and he clung to the *almost* the way drowning men cling to driftwood, and he knew he was doing it, and he did it anyway.

"Halloway. Double the riders on the north road. And send to the archive — I want every record of a woman named Montrose, and every record of her grandmother. Yes," he added, before the sergeant's eyebrows could finish rising, "her grandmother. Especially her grandmother."

When Halloway had gone, Casimir stood alone with his map and his undrunk brandy, and spoke aloud to the empty chapel-study, in a language that officially did not exist, the first full sentence he had permitted himself in ten years:

"*Hold, little sister. Hold the doors. I am coming to close them over your head if I must.*"

Far to the north, he did not doubt, the water sang on.
