## 10. Voices in the Deep Water

The valley had been a road once. That was the first thing Ines understood about it — not from any sign or milestone, but from the way the water lay. Flooded land drowned in a chaos of ripples and debris; this water lay smooth as poured glass, following a line too straight to be anything but a highway, with the drowned orchards standing in rows on either side like mourners who had been waiting a very long time.

"Corvane maps call it the Sorrow," Tam said, crouched at the edge of the causeway that ran out over the flood like a spine of grey stone. "Officially."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially nobody calls it anything. You don't name a thing you're pretending isn't there." Tam shouldered her pack and started walking without checking whether Ines followed, which by now was its own kind of courtesy. Ten days on the road together had taught Ines that Tam's affection came disguised as logistics: she always walked on the side of danger, always ate last, and never once said good morning.

The causeway was narrow enough that they walked single file, Ines behind, watching the water slide past a hand's breadth below the stone. It was clear near the surface — she could see pale shapes below, rooflines maybe, or shoulders — and then dark, and then nothing at all, a depth that felt less like distance than like held breath.

She heard them before she understood what she was hearing.

It began the way all the voices began, as a hum beneath her hearing, the way a tuning fork sings after you've stopped touching it. But these were not the small dead of Corvane's alleys, the pickpockets and grandmothers whose bones she'd carved into charms. These voices were vast. They came up through the stone of the causeway, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her, and they were saying one word in ten thousand overlapping mouths:

*Remember.*

"Ines." Tam's voice, far away, as if shouted across a field. "Ines, your eyes have gone strange."

Had they? She couldn't quite remember what her eyes usually did. The water was so close now. Hadn't she been walking beside it? The causeway seemed to have narrowed to a ribbon under her feet, and the water had risen to meet her ankles — no, that wasn't right either, but the distinction felt unimportant, because the voices were so *glad* to see her. That was the terrible thing. Not hunger, not malice. Gladness. Ten years of hearing the dead complain and bargain and grieve, and here for the first time was a chorus that sounded like a family at a door, arms open.

*You can hear us,* they said. *Oh, you can hear us. Come down. Come down where it's quiet. We've kept everything. We've kept the bread ovens and the bell-ropes and your grandmother's sewing scissors, come down and we'll give it all back—*

Her boot found the edge of the stone.

Then the world swung violently sideways, and she was flat on her back on the causeway with Tam's knee in her chest and Tam's hand fisted in her collar, and Tam's face above hers wearing an expression Ines had only ever seen on people holding something back from a fire.

"Don't," Tam said. Her voice shook, which Ines had never heard it do. "Don't you dare. I didn't drag you off a tithe-gang's chopping block to watch you drown yourself for a country you've never even seen."

"They weren't calling me to drown," Ines said. Her own voice sounded scraped. "That's the awful part. They were calling me home."

Tam stared at her. Then she hauled her up by both wrists, checked her over with brisk rough hands — pulse, pupils, the map-bundle still strapped beneath Ines's coat — and only when satisfied that all pieces were present did she sit down heavily on the causeway stones, as if her legs had filed a complaint.

"How long," Tam said, "have you been able to hear them?"

"The dead? Since I was a child. It got louder in Corvane; I thought it was just — more graves there. Older ones." Ines wrapped her arms around her knees. Below them the water had gone perfectly still again, patient as a letter waiting to be opened. "But those weren't Corvane dead. Those were—" She stopped, because saying the name aloud on open ground still felt like striking a match in a dry barn. "Them. The ones from the erased place."

"Vesperans." Tam said it flatly, defiantly, the way she said everything. "Say it. They can't take the word away if we keep using it."

"You say it like a weapon."

"I say everything like a weapon. It's my one talent." But there was no edge in it, and after a moment Tam looked out at the water instead of at Ines, which from Tam was nearly a confession. "My brother could hear them too. Near the end."

Ines went very still. In ten days, Tam had spoken of Merric perhaps four times, and each time in the past tense with the doors shut tight behind it. "Near the end?"

"He changed. In the last year. He'd stop in the middle of a sentence and tilt his head, like a dog hearing a whistle nobody else can hear. He said the water talked to him. There's no water anywhere near our village," Tam added, as if this proved something. "He started drawing. Little marks, over and over, on scraps, on the table, in the flour. I thought he'd gone soft in the head. I told him so." Her jaw worked. "I told him so at length."

Ines reached into her coat and drew out the oilskin bundle, unwrapping it carefully despite the wind. The map lay there, inked on skin that was not leather — she'd known that much since the second night — covered in the looping script that no living scholar would read and no ledger would admit existed. And scattered along its margins, in Merric's cruder hand, the same little marks he'd scratched into flour.

"He copied them from the voices," Ines said softly. "Or they copied themselves through him. These aren't decorations, Tam. They're thresholds. He was mapping the way home."

"He was mapping the way to get himself killed, is what he mapped." Tam's voice cracked down the middle of the sentence, and she pressed on through the break as though it were a fordable river. "You want to know how he found it? Fine. Sit. It's a long story and my legs are done arguing about it."

Ines sat. The water waited below, courteous as a listener.

* * *

"Our mother was Vesperan," Tam said. "I suppose that makes us half of everything, half-Vesperan, half-whatever our father was, which was a drunk with good cheekbones. Mother never spoke of it directly. You didn't, after the Erasure — you learned early that the word made rooms go cold and made certain neighbors remember debts you owed. But she kept things. A song she sang wrong on purpose, so it wouldn't be recognized. A way of braiding bread. She counted in the old numbers when she thought we were asleep. And every year on the same night she'd set an extra bowl at the table, fill it, and never explain it, and if you asked she'd say, 'For someone walking,' and that was the end of the conversation."

"A place setting for the exiled dead," Ines murmured. "That's old Vesperan practice. The voices told me — well. They tell me a great deal I don't ask about."

"Merric found her box when he was sixteen. Hidden under the floorboards, because of course it was; everyone's mother hid things under floorboards in those days. Inside was a scrap of cloth with writing on it, and a dried flower from a garden that officially had never bloomed, and a letter addressed to 'whoever among my children has the listening.' Not to her children. To whichever child turned out to hear the dead." Tam laughed, short and bitter. "She knew. All those years, she knew one of us might be born with ears, and she left instructions like a sensible woman leaving a recipe."

"What did the letter say?"

"That the country was not destroyed. That it was *folded*, the way you fold a letter, and that folding is not the same as burning, whatever the Wardens' histories claim. That the doors were sealed from the inside by people who loved it, to keep something safe, and that someday the seals would need hands that could hear. And that whoever had the listening must carry the map north and never, ever let a Warden touch it." Tam picked up a pebble and threw it at the water; it vanished without a ripple, swallowed whole. "Merric read that letter forty times. I know because I read it thirty-nine times after him, jealous, because I wanted it to be me. Isn't that a vile thing to admit? I wanted the burden. It looked like being chosen."

"It looks different from inside," Ines said quietly.

"Yes, well." Tam scrubbed a hand over her face. "Mother died two winters ago. Fever, quick, no drama, which she'd have hated. And at the very end, when the rest of us were blubbering uselessly around the bed, she grabbed Merric's wrist and pulled him down and said — I was close enough to hear it, gods help me, I've heard it every night since — she said, 'Walk for me. Set the bowl, and then walk. Someone's been waiting at the door for ten years and it's rude to leave them knocking.'"

The wind moved over the floodwater, and far out, something that might have been a reflection shifted.

"So he walked," Tam said. "Two years, following scraps and rumors and the marks in his head. He wrote to me twice. The second letter said he'd found the map, the true one, the one their own dead had hidden from the Wardens, and that he'd met a bone-carver in his dreams who could open what was shut. He said it like a joke. Merric joked when he was terrified; it was his worst quality." She turned and looked at Ines straight on, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and, underneath all of it, hopeful in a way that hurt worse than anger would have. "A bone-carver. In his dreams. And then he comes south, bleeding, and dies on the floor of a bone-carver's workshop, and presses the map into her hands, and makes her swear."

"I didn't kill him," Ines said. "I want you to know it plainly, though I think you've known it since the causeway fight. Whatever else I am, I didn't—"

"I know." Tam said it fast, before Ines could finish, as if the sentence were a weight she couldn't let Ines carry alone. "I've known since the tollhouse, when you gave up your mother's face to open that door and cried like it cost you blood. Thieves don't pay like that." She exhaled. "I stayed anyway. Partly to watch you. Partly because—" She gestured helplessly at the drowned valley, the drowned capital somewhere beneath it, the whole impossible errand. "Partly because somebody has to keep you out of the water, apparently, and I appear to have volunteered."

"You did pull me back rather dramatically."

"I have many dramatic talents. It runs in the family alongside the listening." Tam stood, offered her hand, and pulled Ines to her feet — and then, unexpectedly, did not let go at once. Her grip was warm and hard. "My brother died believing you'd open the doors. I'm choosing to believe it too. But hear me, Ines Montrose: when we get to the capital, when the voices start singing your name — and they will, I saw what happened today — you hold onto something real. Hold onto me. I'm unpleasant enough to be memorable."

Ines laughed — a wet, surprised sound, the first laugh in days. "That may be the kindest thing anyone's ever promised me."

"Don't spread it around. I have a reputation."

They walked on along the causeway as the light went long and gold over the flood. And it was Tam who stopped first, three miles on, where the causeway bent toward a drowned gatehouse whose towers broke the surface like the knuckles of a buried fist. She pointed at the lintel above the gate, where the wind had stripped the weeds away.

Carved there, worn but unmistakable, were the marks from the map's margin. Seven of them. And the seventh — the deepest cut, the oldest — sat directly above the flooded archway, glowing faintly in the evening light like an ember refusing to die.

"That's the next one, isn't it," Tam said.

Below the arch, in the green dark of the water, faces had begun to gather. Hundreds of them, patient and glad, looking up.

And one of them, Ines realized with a jolt that went through her like a bell-strike, wore her grandmother's face.
