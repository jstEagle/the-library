## 11. The Second and Third Doors

The map had lied to her twice already — once by omission, once by enthusiasm.

Ines sat on a cold hillside above a valley full of fog, holding the oilskin open across her knees while Tam built a fire that smoked more than it burned. The map's ink had shifted again. It did this when it was pleased with itself, she'd decided; the lines crawled like insects settling into new positions, and the second threshold mark had slid three miles east since morning, as if the door were playing hide-and-seek with them specifically.

"It's moved," Tam said, without looking up from the fire. She said it the way other people commented on weather.

"You can tell?"

"The smoke's blowing wrong." Tam fed a twig to the flames. "Your map does that. When we're close to one of its little doors, everything leans toward it. Smoke. Water. Birds." She paused. "You."

Ines looked down at her own hands. They were steady. That was something, at least. "I don't lean."

"You lean. You've been leaning all afternoon. You walk like a woman being reeled in on a line and you keep humming under your breath like a widow at a funeral who's just remembered a joke." Tam glanced up, and there was something almost gentle in the flatness of her face. "I'm not complaining. It's the most cheerful you've been since the causeway."

"I am not *humming*."

"Vesperan lullaby. My mother sang it. You're flat on the third phrase, by the way."

Ines closed her mouth, which had indeed been doing something of the sort. The dead hummed through her sometimes — they always had, a low tide of borrowed melodies — but lately the tide had been rising, and the songs were not the dead of Corvane anymore. These were older. Hungrier for attention. A whole country's worth of ghosts who had spent ten years being forgotten and had recently discovered someone who could hear them.

"They're excited," Ines said. "About tonight."

"They should be less excited. You look terrible."

"Thank you, Tam. Truly, your companionship is a balm."

"I'm told I have a soothing manner." Tam stood, brushed off her knees, and pointed east, where the fog was parting around something the way water parts around a stone. "There. Is that your door?"

It was. Even at this distance, Ines could feel it — a seam in the world, a held breath. And beneath it, fainter but unmistakable, a second seam. Close. Closer than any two thresholds had been before, so close that the map had drawn them nearly touching, two marks like a pair of eyes.

Two doors, within a night's walk of each other.

She should have thought harder about what that meant. Later, she would have all the time in the world to think about what that meant. But the dead were singing, and the seam was warm, and she was tired in a way that sleep didn't touch, and so instead she folded the map, shouldered her pack, and started downhill toward the first door with Tam's voice following her like a second shadow: *"You're leaning, bone-carver."*

* * *

The first door stood in the ruin of a shepherd's hut, or what had once been a shepherd's hut and was now mostly a suggestion of walls and one stubborn chimney. The threshold itself was nothing to see — a shimmer in the air between two stones, visible only because Ines knew where to look, the way you can only see your own breath on a cold day if you're looking for it.

But she could hear it. Gods, she could hear it. It sounded like a choir warming up behind a locked cathedral door, hundreds of voices murmuring in a language she shouldn't have known and somehow did.

"Last time," Tam said, positioning herself against the doorway with her arms crossed, "you cried afterward and wouldn't tell me why. So tonight, new rule. Before you open anything, you tell me what it costs."

Ines hesitated. The truth was that she didn't fully know until the door took it. The first time, it had taken her mother's face — plucked it out of her memory so cleanly that now, when she tried to recall her mother, she got a warm shape, a voice, a pair of hands kneading bread, and where the face should be, a smooth blank like an unfinished carving. She hadn't told Tam that either. There was a limit to how much helplessness a person could hand over to someone else to carry.

"It takes a memory," she said. "One per door. Something of mine, given to the country. I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think Vesper doesn't want payment exactly. I think it wants *company*. It's been alone with only its own memories for ten years, and it's lonely, and loneliness makes things grabby."

Tam considered this. "So it's not a toll. It's a hostage exchange."

"That's a horrible way to put it."

"But accurate?"

"...Accurate," Ines admitted. She pressed her palm to the air between the stones. It felt like pressing on the surface of a drum. "If I stop after this one—"

"You won't stop after this one." Tam's voice was quiet and certain. "Because the second one will be right there, and you'll tell yourself it would be wasteful not to, and I'll watch you do it, because apparently watching you do reckless things is my job now. My brother died for this map. So go on. But I'm counting what you lose, Ines. Someone has to remember what you forget."

That landed somewhere deep and uncomfortable, and Ines chose not to examine it. She opened the door.

Opening a threshold was not like unlocking anything. It was like agreeing. She let the seam widen, let the voices pour up through it — and they poured *into* her, a flood of Vesper: harvest fields under a green sky, a wedding where everyone danced in the shallows of a river, a city of white bridges, bells, bells, bells —

And then the price, taken gently, almost kindly, the way you'd lift a coin from a sleeping child's palm.

When she came back to herself she was sitting on the ground and Tam was crouched over her saying her name in the tone people used near fires.

"What did it take?" Tam asked.

Ines searched. Her childhood was intact. Corvane, the market, the workshop, the smell of bone dust — all there. Her grandmother's stories, still half-remembered, still hers. She went through herself shelf by shelf like a merchant taking inventory and found, at last, the gap.

"My sister's laugh," she said slowly. "I had a — no. No, I didn't. Did I?" She groped for it. There had been someone. A girl she'd grown up with, small and loud, someone whose laughter had been — there was a shape where the sound should be, a silhouette with no voice in it. "I had a sister. I know I had a sister. I can't remember what she sounded like when she was happy."

Tam was silent a moment. Then she said, roughly, "Then I'll hold it. Tell me about her. Everything except the laugh. I'll keep the shape of it until you can take it back."

"Why would you—"

"Because someone has to," Tam said, as if it were obvious, as if she hadn't spent five weeks threatening to gut Ines in her sleep. "Now get up. The other door's waiting, and you've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look of a woman about to say it would be wasteful not to."

* * *

They should have camped. Ines knew that even then, some sensible buried clerk in her mind stamping forms in triplicate: *DO NOT OPEN TWO DOORS IN ONE NIGHT. SEE ATTACHED REGULATIONS.* But the second threshold was barely a mile away, glowing in her hearing like a second moon, and the dead were singing so loudly now that she couldn't have slept anyway. They sang the way children sing on the last day of a journey — impatient, joyful, unbearable.

The second door stood in the open, in the middle of a fallow field, framed by nothing at all. Just a seam hanging in the starlight over the grass, patient as a held note.

"This one's different," Ines whispered.

"How?"

"The others were hidden. Built into places. This one's just — standing here. Like it's been waiting for us specifically." She swallowed. "Like it knows we're coming."

"Everything about this country knows we're coming," Tam said. "Fine. New rule, revised. What are you willing to lose? Think first. Actually think. Pick something. Offer it deliberately, maybe it'll take what you choose instead of rummaging."

It was good thinking. It was the kind of thinking Ines should have done four doors ago. She stood in the cold field and took inventory of herself, honestly, for perhaps the first time in her life.

Her mother's face: gone already. Her sister's laugh: in Tam's keeping. What remained was a modest estate. Ten years of Corvane. The workshop. The market stalls. The particular way lamplight came through her window at dawn. And her craft — the long apprenticeship of her hands, the weight of the graver, the way bone gave up its secrets grain by grain, every charm she'd ever carved, every ghost she'd ever served, the entire architecture of the only thing she'd ever been good at.

Surely it wouldn't take that. Surely the country wanted memories of *itself*, of Vesper, of the world it had lost — not the small professional competence of a charm-seller from Corvane.

"I offer my tenth year in Corvane," she said aloud, feeling foolish. "A bad year. Fever, hunger, a landlord named Osk. Take it. It's yours. It's practically a gift."

She opened the door.

This one did not pour. This one *pulled*.

The voices came through her like water through a broken dam, and they were not gentle, and they were not choirs — they were a crowd at a gate, a whole city trying to come home through a keyhole, and Ines was the keyhole, and they pressed through her and out into the night where they spread like light across the field, and she heard Tam shouting her name from very far away, and then—

Then the price. Taken not gently this time but thoroughly, the way a tide takes a sandcastle, and Ines felt it go and knew even as it left her what it was, and reached for it, and caught nothing.

* * *

She woke on her back in the wet grass with dawn graying the sky and Tam kneeling over her, and the first thing she noticed was that her hands felt wrong.

Not injured. Not numb. Wrong the way a tool feels when someone has moved it overnight — familiar in shape, foreign in placement. She raised them above her face and looked at them and they were her hands, scarred knuckle, burn-marked thumb, and yet.

"Talk to me," Tam said. "What did it take?"

"I don't—" Ines sat up. The field was empty now, the seam gone, the stars gone, the crowd of voices gone, and the silence where they had been was enormous. "It took the year I offered. Osk the landlord, the fever, all of it, gone, fine, good riddance. But it also—" Her throat tightened. "Tam. How do you carve a charm?"

Tam blinked. "How do I—what?"

"The graver. The angle. You hold it — you hold it—" Ines mimed it, and her hand made a fist, a stupid useless fist, the grip of someone who had never held a blade in her life. "There's a way. I know there's a way. My hands knew it this morning. There's a way you brace the bone against your thumb and roll the wrist and the graver goes in like — like—"

"Ines."

"—like a boat into water, there's a word for it, there's a whole *vocabulary*, I knew forty words for kinds of cuts, I knew which bones sing and which ones only whisper, I taught myself when I was fourteen with a fish knife and a chicken bone and I got it *wrong* seventeen times and the eighteenth time it *sang*—"

"Ines." Tam took her wrists, gently, and lowered her hands. "Breathe."

"I can't carve." The words came out very small. "It took my craft. Not the memories *around* it — I remember the workshop, I remember the customers, I remember every ghost I ever helped. I remember *that* I could do it. I just can't. It's like — it's like someone took the river and left the riverbed."

Tam didn't say anything for a long moment. Dawn came up properly, gilding the frost on the fallow field, making everything cruelly clear and ordinary. Somewhere a bird started its idiot morning song, entirely untroubled by the collapse of anyone's identity.

"So," Tam said finally, in the tone of a woman assembling facts into a report, "you opened two doors in one night, against the advice of nobody because I foolishly assumed you had survival instincts, and the country took your art as payment. Your craft. The thing you are."

"The thing I *was*." Ines laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you understand what I was before all this? I wasn't brave. I wasn't kind, particularly. I wasn't clever the way you're clever. I was a woman who could hear the dead and make small comforts out of their leavings, and that was the entire sum of me, and I was *fine* with that. I built a whole life on staying small. And now the small thing's gone too, and what's left?" She spread her empty hands. "A woman who hears the dead and can't answer them. A locksmith with no hands. What am I, Tam?"

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she sat down in the frost beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and was quiet for a while.

"When my brother found the map," Tam said at last, "he wasn't anyone special either. A ferryman's son with bad knees. He used to say he was a man defined entirely by his route — bank to bank, back and forth, and if you took away the river there'd be nothing left of him but his name. And then he found the map, and the river was taken away, and you know what? He turned out to be stubborn, and loyal, and funnier than he had any right to be. The route wasn't the man. The route was just where the man had been standing." She shrugged. "Maybe the carving isn't you, bone-carver. Maybe it's just where you've been standing. And maybe—" she nodded at the empty field, at the horizon, at the road north that neither of them had spoken aloud but both could see — "maybe you're being moved."

"To what?"

"No idea. But I'll tell you one thing I've learned watching you these five weeks." Tam stood, and pulled Ines up after her, and her grip was strong and certain. "Whatever the doors take from you, they haven't managed to take the part of you that keeps opening them anyway. That part's not craft. That part's just you. Stubborn as a mule and twice as likely to die of it."

Ines laughed despite herself, and then, because her hands needed something to do and no longer knew their old work, she used them for a new one: she hugged Tam Vael, briefly and awkwardly, before the other woman could escape.

"Well," Tam said, stepping back and looking profoundly betrayed by her own face, which had gone red. "That happened. Never speak of it."

"Never."

"Good." Tam retrieved the pack, checked the map — and stopped. "Ines. Come look at this."

The map had changed again. The remaining threshold marks had rearranged themselves, and beyond the fourth and fifth, past the mountains, the ink was drawing something new: not a mark but a cluster of tiny figures, huddled together like notes on a stave, and beside them, in handwriting that had never appeared on the map before — handwriting that looked unsettlingly like Ines's own — two words:

*They wait.*

And below that, smaller, almost shy:

*Bring fire. Bring bread. Bring the woman who remembers the songs.*

Tam read it twice. "Songs," she said. "You mean the lullaby you were humming. The one you're flat on."

Ines stared at the little clustered figures, and felt, faintly, through the enormous silence where her craft used to be, something new stirring — not the dead this time, but the living. Dozens of hearts, beating somewhere north, keeping a rhythm she somehow already knew.

"I think," she said slowly, "the country just gave me a job description."

"And no skills," said Tam. "Story of your life. Come on — we've got exiles to find, and you need to practice your scales."
