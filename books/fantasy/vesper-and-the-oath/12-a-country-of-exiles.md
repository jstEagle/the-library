## 12. A Country of Exiles

The goat found us before the people did.

We had been following a shepherd's track up out of the hills for half a day — Ines limping on her bad ankle, me carrying both packs because she refused to admit the ankle was bad — when a brown-and-white goat stepped out of a gorse thicket and stood in our path like a toll collector.

"Shoo," I said.

The goat did not shoo. It looked at me with the flat yellow patience of something that had been paid to wait, and then it turned and walked back into the gorse, pausing once to glance over its shoulder in a way I can only describe as expectant.

"It wants us to follow it," Ines said.

"Goats don't want things."

"This one does. It's got that look your brother had when he wanted you to carry his share of the firewood." She said it lightly, but we both went quiet for a step or two. We were getting better at saying Merric's name without flinching. Not good. Better.

I followed the goat. Ines followed me, complaining about her ankle in the tone of someone who has decided not to complain about her ankle. The track narrowed, dropped through a fold of hillside, and ended at a stone wall that looked exactly like every other stone wall in these hills except that it had a door in it, and the door had a woman standing in front of it with a scythe.

She was perhaps sixty, broad as a barrel, with grey hair cut short and hands like tree roots. The scythe was not raised, but it was present, the way a fact is present.

"That's far enough," she said. "State your business and your grandmother's name."

Ines blinked. "My grandmother's name?"

"The second one. Everyone has two. The one you say at the market and the one you say at home." The woman's eyes moved from Ines's face to mine and back. "You've been walking north for weeks. You're not bandits; bandits don't limp. And you—" she pointed the chin at Ines "—you walk like someone listening to a room nobody else can hear. So. Grandmother's name."

There was a long moment where I thought about the knife under my coat, and then Ines said, quietly, in a language I didn't know — three words, soft and round, like stones from a riverbed.

The woman with the scythe lowered it all the way to the ground and leaned on it, and her face did something complicated, like a shutter coming off a window.

"Well," she said. "That's the old accent, that is. Nobody under fifty speaks like that. Come in before the light sees you. And mind the goat, she'll take bribes."

* * *

The village — if you could call it that — hid inside the valley the way a coin hides in a fist. From the ridge above there was nothing: scrub, rock, a stream. But the stream ran through a sunken hollow where forty-odd houses had been built down into the earth itself, roofed with turf, their chimneys disguised as rock piles. Smoke rose thin and scattered so that from any distance it would read as mist.

"They call it the Hollow," our gatekeeper said as she led us down a path between houses that seemed to grow out of the hillside rather than sit upon it. "Officially, this is grazing land belonging to no one, because the tithe rolls say no one lives here. Unofficially—" she spread her arms at the turf roofs, the washing lines strung between walls, the smell of woodsmoke and baking bread "—unofficially, the ledgers are liars, and thank the dark for it."

"What do I call you?" Ines asked.

"Maren. Here, I'm Maren Ashfoot. In Corvane, for ten years, I was Maren the Widow Doss, God rest the fictional Mr. Doss, he was very convenient." She said it dryly, but I caught the thing underneath it, the same thing I'd been carrying since Merric died: the weight of a name you have to put down and pick up and put down again until you're not sure which one is yours.

Maren installed us in a spare room behind the bakery — "the baker is my cousin, everything here is somebody's cousin, you'll get used to it" — and told us supper was at sundown and that we should wash, because "the Hollow feeds people properly or not at all."

Then she paused in the doorway and looked at Ines with an expression I was beginning to learn meant a question had been asked and answered already, somewhere below words.

"You'll hear them tonight," Maren said. "The songs. Some of the young ones have never heard them outside a whisper. If that's too much for you, say so now, and we'll eat in the kitchen."

Ines looked at me. I shrugged, which was a lie, because it was already too much for me and nothing had happened yet.

"We came for this," Ines said. "Whatever this is."

* * *

Supper happened in the longhouse at the bottom of the Hollow, and I want to be honest about what it did to me, because I spent most of it pretending it wasn't doing anything.

There was bread. Real bread, dark and dense, with a crust that cracked when you tore it, made from grain grown in a hidden field and milled in a hidden mill by a miller who introduced himself three times because he was proud of it. There was stew with actual meat in it. There was a cheese that made Ines close her eyes and say something reverent in that riverbed language, which set off a small storm of delight among the older folk, who began feeding her words like birds, waiting to see which ones she'd catch.

And there were names.

That was the part I hadn't expected. In Corvane, everyone we'd met had been careful — names shortened, names swapped, names given only in trade. Here, a girl of maybe eight stood up on the bench and announced that her baby brother had been named last week, and the name was Edric, after her great-grandfather, and the whole room said it back — *Edric* — loud, unafraid, twice, the way you'd ring a bell.

Nobody flinched. Nobody glanced at the door.

I kept my face still and my hands busy tearing bread, and I thought about how Merric used to hum when he worked, tuneless little scraps of songs he claimed not to know the words to. He'd learned them from our mother, who'd learned them from hers, and none of us had ever known what they were *from*. They were just Merric's humming. That was all we'd been allowed to keep.

Here, a man with a fiddle struck up one of those tunes — the actual tune, whole, with words — and half the room sang it, and the other half listened with their eyes shut like people standing in the first rain after drought.

Ines was crying. She tried to hide it in her bowl, which fooled no one, least of all the eight-year-old, who patted her arm with the brisk unsentimental kindness of children and said, "It's all right, the fiddle man always makes new people cry, Auntie Sef says it's the price of admission."

"I'm fine," Ines said.

"You're crying."

"I'm *fine*," Ines said, crying, and the girl nodded solemnly as if this confirmed something about the world, and went back to her bread.

I passed Ines the cheese. It seemed like the correct ritual.

* * *

Afterward, when the benches were pushed back and the little ones carried off asleep, the music changed. Slower. Older. An ancient man they called Uncle Pell — though Maren confided he was no one's uncle and possibly no one's friend — took a stool by the fire and told stories in the old tongue, translating himself in a low mutter for those who needed it, which included me and, to my surprise, about a third of the room.

He told the story of Vesper's founding, which I had never heard, because there was no such story anywhere in any book I'd ever touched. He told it the way you tell a family story — with jokes in it, and arguments, and a bit where the audience shouted corrections at him.

"And then the river said—"

"*No such thing!*"

"—the river said, and I'll thank you all to let an old man lie in peace—"

The laughter rolled around the longhouse like something alive. And sitting in the middle of it, I understood — really understood, in my chest instead of my head — what Casimir's Wardens had actually done. Not burned a country. You could burn a country and it would stay burned, honest, finished. What they'd done was worse. They'd taken the telling away. They'd made it so that a boy like Merric could hum a song his mother gave him and never know it was a wall his great-great-grandmother had danced on.

They stole the *jokes*, I thought, absurdly near tears myself. They stole the part where everyone shouts at Uncle Pell.

Ines's hand found mine under the table. Neither of us said anything. Her palm was cool and her grip was steady, and we sat like that through three more stories, two of which were apparently about my left elbow, according to the corrections.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the longhouse had emptied to embers and snoring — I couldn't sleep. I went out into the Hollow's night air and found Ines already awake, sitting on a bench by the bakery door with a blanket around her shoulders, watching the stars.

"Can't sleep either?" she said.

"Too much bread. I think I'm dying."

"You're not dying. You're happy. It presents similarly."

I sat down next to her. For a while neither of us spoke, which was its own kind of language by then.

"They named a baby tonight," Ines said finally. "Edric. And everyone just... said it. Out loud. Like it was nothing."

"It is nothing. Where I grew up we said names all the time."

"Yes, well. Where I grew up, my grandmother's name was a locked box my mother kept in the back of her mouth. She'd start to say it sometimes and then swallow it, like it was a bone too big to go down." Ines pulled the blanket tighter. "I asked her once why we couldn't talk about her mother's family and she said, 'Because talking is how they find you.' Ten years old, and I learned that the dead aren't the only ones you have to keep quiet about. Sometimes it's the living."

A cat emerged from under the bakery door, considered us, and poured itself into Ines's lap with the entitlement of a tax collector. She scratched its ears.

"Tam," she said. "When we open the doors. When the country comes back, if it comes back — do you think it'll be like this? Bread and babies and old men lying badly?"

"I think it'll be like this," I said, "plus arguments. A country's mostly arguments with better weather."

That surprised a laugh out of her — a real one, quick and bright, gone almost before it arrived. I filed it away. I'd started collecting those without meaning to. Her laughs were rare enough to be worth the keeping.

"And if it costs more than we think?" she said, quieter now. "If the doors keep taking pieces of me? My mother's face is already gone, Tam. I opened one door and my own mother's face went with it, and I can't even mourn her properly because I can't remember what I'm mourning." The cat kneaded her knee. "Some nights I lie awake trying to reconstruct her from other things. The way she smelled. Woodsmoke and tallow. Is that a face? No. That's a candle."

I didn't have an answer. I wished Merric were there, because Merric always had answers, infuriating ones, delivered with that grin that made you want to hit him. Instead I had me, and me said:

"Then we make sure it buys something worth the price. That's all any of us can do with what we lose. Spend it well."

Ines was quiet a moment. "That's the wisest thing anyone's said to me since the ferryman."

"The ferryman said something wise?"

"He said, 'Mind the third plank, it's rotten.'"

"Deep man."

"Profound," she agreed, and the laugh came again, easier this time, and stayed a while.

* * *

We stayed three days. Maren declared it necessary — "your friend's ankle needs binding and your faces need feeding, both of you look like a pair of unpaid debts" — and nobody argued, least of all the ankle.

On the second day, the old women cornered Ines in the bakery and discovered she could hear their dead, and I watched from the doorway as a queue formed. Nothing dramatic. Small things. *Tell my sister the loom key is under the third hearthstone.* *Tell my son I forgave him before the end, he never let me finish the sentence.* Ines relayed each message with her sleeves pushed up and flour on her nose, and each time she spoke a dead person's wish aloud in the open air, the whole bakery went still, and then someone cried, and then someone else started laughing, and bread continued to happen around it all like a tide.

One of the old women — Sef, the aunt of the blunt child — took Ines's hands afterward and held them and said something long and formal in the old tongue, and Ines answered, haltingly, and Sef beamed like a lighthouse.

"What was that?" I asked Ines after.

"She thanked me for carrying the voices home. And she said—" Ines stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "She said my grandmother's family were millers, on the Vesper side. Downriver. She remembers the name."

"Do you want to tell me the name?"

"Not yet," Ines said. "I want to hold it a day first. Unspoken. Just mine."

I understood that completely. Some things you say and spend. Some things you keep in the pocket where they stay warm.

* * *

On the third morning, Maren woke us before dawn with a hand on my shoulder and no gentleness in it at all.

"Rider on the ridge," she said. "Grey coat. Alone, which is worse than many — alone means confident. He's been sitting there an hour, looking down at the valley like a man reading a ledger."

Ines was already up, already reaching for the map. "Does he know?"

"If he knew, there'd be twenty of them burning the Hollow by now. But he's *looking*, girl, and men who look eventually report." Maren's jaw was set. "We've survived ten years by being a hole in the paper. Holes close. So you two leave by the goat track before full light, and you leave *now*, and you don't come back until whatever you're doing is done."

"There's a rider," I said slowly, "and you're letting us go toward him."

"I'm letting you go *past* him, by the track under the ridge, which he can't see from above. And Tam—" Maren fixed me with eyes that had probably stared down scarier things than me, though I doubted it "—whatever's at the end of that map, finish it. Because my granddaughter is eight years old and she thinks Edric is a normal name to shout in a field. I'd like her to grow up in a world where she's right."

We went. Of course we went. But at the mouth of the goat track I looked back once, at the turf roofs steaming in the early cold, at the smoke rising thin and scattered, at Maren standing in her doorway with her arms crossed like a woman holding a door shut against a flood.

Behind us, faint across the valley, someone was singing. Getting the words wrong, probably. Being corrected.

Ahead, on the ridge, the grey coat turned his horse and began to ride — not toward the Hollow, I realized with a cold drop in my stomach, but along the crestline. North. The same direction as us. As if he already knew where the road went, and was simply going to arrive first and wait.

Ines saw it too. Her hand found the oilskin roll at her chest.

"He's not hunting us," she said quietly. "He's *herding* us."

The goat appeared from the gorse, fell in beside us, and trotted north.
