## 13. Betrayal at the Well

The exile who betrayed us was called Maren's father, before he was called anything else.

I want to be fair to him. I have spent the days since trying to be fair to him, and it is like trying to carve a charm from a stone: possible, maybe, but nothing sings in it. His name was Oskit Vael. He had two children, a boy of six with a cowlick that refused all discipline and a girl of nine who could whistle three notes of the old Vesperan songs without knowing what they were. He had a wife buried under a cairn on the far side of the valley with no name carved on it, because carving her name would have been evidence, and evidence gets people hanged.

He had been hiding for ten years. I had been hiding for ten years. The difference between us, I told myself in the dark afterward, is that I hid alone.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and the dead tell me I always get ahead of myself, because they have nowhere to be and so they resent anyone who moves quickly.

* * *

The community in the hills called itself nothing. That was the first thing you learned about them: they had gotten very good at not being called anything. A farmstead, a mill, a scatter of cottages around a spring that fed a stone well older than any of the countries currently pretending to matter. Forty-one souls, give or take, most of them over forty themselves, which Tam said grimly was what happened when a people stopped believing children were safe.

"They kept the language," she'd said our first night there, standing at the edge of the firelight while an old woman taught a reel to anyone whose knees would permit it. "Ten years. Do you know what it costs to keep a language alive in secret? You have to speak it in whispers. You have to teach it to babies the way you'd pass contraband." Her jaw worked. "My brother used to say a language whispered for a generation comes out changed. Comes out *smaller*."

"And yet," I said, "here it is."

Here it was. For five days it was ours too. They fed us black bread with real crust, bread made from grain milled in secret and baked in an oven hidden behind a false wall, and I will tell you something embarrassing: I cried into my first slice. Not elegantly. Not one shining tear. I stood in their kitchen with flour dust in the air and wept like a child while a woman named Sefa patted my shoulder and pretended this happened every day.

"You're bone-carver," Sefa said. She spoke Corvani with an accent like a song half-remembered. "They say you hear them."

"I hear them," I admitted.

"Do they—" She stopped. Started again. "Do they sound angry? The dead of Vesper. When they talk to you."

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty. "Mostly they sound *homesick*," I said. "Angry is just homesick that's been standing too long in the rain."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed, a startled bark of a laugh, and pressed another slice of bread into my hands, and for five days I let myself believe that this was what winning looked like. Warm rooms. Names spoken aloud after dark. An old man named Pell who played a fiddle with only four working strings and made them sound like forty.

Tam even laughed once. I heard it. I am keeping that memory somewhere the doors can't reach it.

* * *

Oskit Vael came to me on the fourth evening, down by the well, where the water sang a low note that the dead liked to gather around. I had noticed that about deep wells — they held echoes the way I held charms, patiently, against need.

"You're the one carrying the map," he said. No greeting. His eyes went to my satchel the way a starving man's eyes go to a window with bread behind it.

"I'm the one carrying a map," I said carefully.

"The seven doors." He said it in Vesperan first, *sev tarran*, and the word landed in my chest like a struck bell. "You mean to open them."

"I mean to try."

"Don't." He gripped my wrist. His hands were miller's hands, white with ghost-flour even now. "Bone-carver, listen to me. I watched them erase a country once. I know how it's done. It isn't done with armies. Armies are loud and armies leave graves and graves leave *witnesses*. It's done quietly. It's done with ledgers and names and one man in a grey coat deciding, and everyone else deciding to let him." His grip tightened. "If you open those doors, he'll come. Not soldiers first. *Him.* And everything here burns."

"Then we'll be gone before—"

"There is no *before*!" It burst out of him, and then he mastered himself, the way you could see he'd had ten years of practice mastering himself, folding everything down small enough to hide. When he spoke again his voice was level and terrible. "There is only *after*, and *after* is where my children live. Promise me you'll move on tomorrow. Promise me you won't open anything near here."

I promised him I'd think about it, which is the promise cowards make when they've already decided, and he knew it, and I saw him know it, and neither of us said anything else. He walked back up the path with his shoulders folded down small, a man shaped by a decade of being less than he was.

The dead around the well murmured. One of them — a woman, old, with a voice like dry leaves — said, clear as a bell across water:

*Watch the miller.*

I did watch him. That's the worst of it. I watched him all the next day, and I saw nothing but a tired father hauling sacks and ruffling his son's cowlick, and I told the leaf-voiced dead that grief makes everyone suspicious, and the dead said nothing, because the dead are under no obligation to argue with fools.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, when the night is at its thinnest and men's courage is thinnest with it.

I woke to Tam's hand clamped over my mouth and her whisper in my ear: "Horses. Many. North road." And then, worse: "No dogs. Warden's regulars don't use dogs. Dogs bark at ghosts." Her eyes were huge in the dark. "Ines. These are *his*."

What happened next happened fast and badly, the way such things do. There was no heroic defense; there were forty-one people who had spent ten years learning to run, and they ran. Sefa got the children out through the root cellar. Old Pell grabbed his broken fiddle and, God help him, his bow, and put himself in the doorway of the common house like a man guarding a bridge, and I heard — I will always hear — the wet sound the moment ended with.

Fire. Of course fire. Grey coats don't leave witnesses either.

I lost Tam in the smoke for ninety seconds that lasted a season, found her again shoving a family onto the goat track behind the mill. The map was against my ribs where it always was, oilskin slick with my sweat, and I remember thinking with a kind of hysterical clarity: *at least I still have the map, at least the map is safe,* and hating myself for the thought even as I had it, because Pell was dead and Sefa's kitchen was burning and I was inventorying my luggage.

"Ines!" Someone screaming my name from inside the smoke. "Ines, the cellar grate — it's jammed — *Ines!*"

I went toward it. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, even if it's only me and the dead: I went *toward* the screaming. I got as far as the well. And between me and the cellar grate stood three grey coats and a man with a hooked pole, and behind them the whole hillside was alight, and the dead of Vesper rose up around that well like floodwater and screamed at me in forty voices —

*Run. RUN. You carry the doors. Run, girl, or every death tonight is spent for nothing.*

So I ran.

That is the whole of my heroism, laid out plain: I ran. I took the south track with the fire painting my shadow enormous on the trees, and behind me people were screaming my name — not *help me*, understand, not even *save me*, but my *name*, as if the name itself were a door I could open for them, as if I were already the thing I would later become — and I ran, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you trip, and tripping is how you die, and dying was not on the itinerary.

I have never despised my own survival more.

* * *

I found Tam at dawn, three miles south, sitting on a fallen wall with blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers and a face like winter coming.

"Pell?" I asked, though I knew.

"Gone." She didn't soften it. She never softened things; softening was my job and I wasn't doing it. "Sefa's family made the tree line. Maybe twenty others scattered east. The rest—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking, and she looked at them with betrayal, as if they'd failed some personal oath. "Forty-one souls, Ines. I counted them our first night. I *counted them*, like a quartermaster, like it mattered, and now I can't stop doing sums."

"Oskit," I said.

"What about him?"

"He sold us." My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "He begged me to move on. I heard the dead warn me — *watch the miller* — and I watched him and saw nothing, because there was nothing to see. He didn't sell us for gold, Tam. He sold us for his children. The Wardens must have found him weeks ago and sat on him, patient as spiders, until we led them to everyone." I laughed, and it was an awful sound. "He was right, you know. That's the joke. He said if I opened the doors, Casimir would come, and everything here would burn. I hadn't opened anything. I was only *carrying the possibility*, and it burned anyway."

Tam was quiet a long time. Dawn came up grey and cold over the hills, and somewhere below us a crow was making its opinions known.

"When we find him," she said at last, "what do you want done?"

And there it was. Roadside justice, round two, except this time the question had teeth on both sides. I thought of the tithe-gang leader and our terrible argument in the rain, her blade and my objections, both of us right, both of us unforgivable. I thought of a boy of six with a cowlick, whistling three notes of a forbidden song.

"Nothing," I said. "We do nothing. He gave up his own soul to keep his children breathing, and Casimir will take the children anyway, because that's what Casimir *does* — he takes the payment and keeps the debt. Oskit's punishment is already complete. It's been complete since the day he signed." I wrapped my arms around myself. "I'm so tired of clean hands, Tam. Everyone wants clean hands. There aren't any. There's just the least dirty choice available, over and over, until you die."

Tam looked at me sideways. "That's the bleakest sermon I've ever heard."

"I'm a bone-carver. We specialize in small comforts."

Despite everything — despite the ash smell in our hair and the sums she couldn't stop doing — her mouth twitched. Then it faded, and she nodded south, down the valley, where the road bent toward the flooded lands and the drowned capital beyond.

"Twenty exiles scattered east," she said. "Word travels. Some of them will find us. The ones Casimir doesn't catch first." She stood, wincing, and shouldered her pack. "Which means every campfire from here to the water is a coin toss. Which means—"

She stopped. Because she'd seen it at the same moment I had: riders on the ridge above us. Three of them. Grey coats catching the early light.

Not hunting us. *Waiting* for us. Arranged neatly along our only road south, unhurried, the way men wait when they already know the answer — and the lead rider raised one hand, not in threat, in something almost like courtesy, and called down in a voice that carried clean across the cold morning air:

"Mistress Montrose. The Warden requests the pleasure of a conversation. He asks me to assure you — on his honor — that no harm needs come to you, your companion, or *anyone else you might name*."

On his honor. From the man who had burned forty-one souls for a map.

Tam's hand found her knife. My hand found the oilskin against my ribs. And the dead, rising quiet around me like frost forming, said in one voice, gentle and terrible:

*Go and hear him, keeper. Even traps are doors.*
