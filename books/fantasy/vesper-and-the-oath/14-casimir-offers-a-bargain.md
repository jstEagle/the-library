## 14. Casimir Offers a Bargain

Warden Casimir had interrogated forty-one people in his career, and he had learned that everyone broke the same way: not from pain, but from the moment they realized no one was coming.

The bone-carver had been in the cell for six hours. She had not asked for anyone. That interested him.

He stood outside her cell in the garrison at Harrow's Ford and watched her through the grate. She sat on the bench with her hands folded, as though waiting for a midwife or a tax assessor. Her wrists were chained to the wall — standard precaution for a woman who could open doors that did not exist — but she had arranged the chain so it didn't pull, which took a certain practical intelligence. She had also, he noticed, spent the first hour of her captivity talking quietly to the empty air in the corner, and whatever she'd said had made her smile once, briefly, like a woman remembering a joke.

*She hears them,* the captured inquisitor had told him, spitting blood and awe in equal measure. *The dead, Warden. She talks to them like neighbors.*

Casimir had killed a man for saying less, once, in another life. Now he simply adjusted his grey coat and went in.

The cell smelled of wet stone and old straw. The woman looked up at him without fear, which was either courage or exhaustion, and in her face he saw something that made his chest tighten before he could stop it: a Vesperan jawline. His grandmother's jawline. The country got into the bones, his mother used to say, and you couldn't carve it out.

"Madam Montrose," he said. "I apologize for the irons."

"They're very comfortable," Ines said. "I've had worse lodgings. I once slept in a granary with a haunted weathervane."

He almost smiled. He crushed it. "You've caused me a great deal of trouble."

"I've walked north," she said. "That's all I've done. Trouble seems to be following me. It's very committed. I admire its work ethic."

Casimir pulled the stool from the corner and sat, deliberately, at her level. This disarmed people. Power was most frightening when it chose to be small.

"I'm going to tell you a story," he said. "You may interrupt if you must. Most of your countrymen can't help it."

Her eyes narrowed slightly. *Your countrymen.* She'd caught it. Good. He wanted her off balance.

"Thirty years ago," he said, "there was a country between the Teeth and the sea. You won't find it on any map now. Its name was—"

"Vesper," Ines said.

The word landed in the cell like a dropped glass. Casimir kept his face still, but inside, something old and scarred turned over. Ten years since anyone had said it aloud in his presence. Ten years of *the northern territories*, of *the former provinces*, of the careful nothing-language the Wardens had built to bury a nation.

"You know the name," he said.

"I know it makes people flinch," Ines said. "I know it makes one ferryman pour me a free drink and another spit on the floor. I know my grandmother hummed songs in a language nobody would admit to teaching her." She tilted her head. "You say it like it hurts you."

"It does." He let the truth show, just a seam of it. Honesty was a blade; you only had to show the edge. "Because I helped erase it."

Ines went very still. Even her breathing stopped, and for a moment Casimir wondered if the dead in the room had gone still with her — if the air itself was listening.

"I was born there," he said. "In a city called Amareth, on a river that is now officially a drainage channel. I was nineteen when the doors began to open. Not your thresholds — the real door. The one underneath everything. And what came through—" He paused. He had rehearsed this part for thirty years and it still caught in his throat like a fishbone. "What came through wore our faces. Our dead came back, madam, but they came back *hungry*. They ate memory. They ate names. Whole villages went quiet — not dead, quiet. People walking around with holes where their children used to be, smiling, because they couldn't remember enough to grieve."

"That's not true," Ines said softly. But her voice wavered, and he heard the doubt in it, the small terrible seed of *what if*. "The dead I hear aren't hungry. They're sad. They're lonely. They want—"

"To go home. Yes. So did the things under Amareth, at first." He leaned forward. "Vesper opened the door, madam. Your country — proud, brilliant, reckless — found the way back to its own dead and pried it wide, and it took eleven thousand soldiers and every Warden alive to shut it again. And when it was shut, we understood that the door could never be merely closed. It had to be *forgotten*. Because a door everyone remembers is a door someone will always open." He spread his hands. "So we forgot it. On purpose. Every record, every ledger, every mind we could reach. I erased my own mother's grave, madam. I signed the order myself. And I have slept well every night since, because the alternative is what I saw in the dark under Amareth."

Silence. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the garrison floor.

Ines looked down at her chained hands. When she spoke, her voice was smaller. "The map. The thresholds. If I open them—"

"You feed it," Casimir said. "Every door you open is a crack in a seal I have bled thirty years to keep whole. You are being used, madam. By the dead, who are patient, and by whoever wrote that map, who was desperate. Give me the map. Walk away with your friend — yes, even Tam Vael, whose brother died for this folly. Go south. Carve your little charms. Live a long, small, safe life." He rose. "It is the only bargain I can offer, and it is more generous than you deserve, and I am offering it because you have her jawline and I am tired."

"Tired of what?"

"Of burying grandmothers."

He left her there with the doubt he had planted, and in the corridor he pressed his hand flat against the cold stone until it stopped shaking.

* * *

They gave Ines bread and watered wine and left her alone with the corner, where a soldier who'd died in this garrison twenty years ago was keeping her company out of professional courtesy. His name was Aldric. He was terrible gossip.

"He believes it," she murmured to the corner. "That's the worst part. He's not lying."

*Most of them aren't,* Aldric agreed. *Lies are cheap. Belief holds up better under torture.*

"Thank you, that's very comforting."

*I do my best.*

She turned the bread over in her fingers and thought about the fourth door. About the thing that had come through it — not a ghost, not a memory, but a *hunger* wearing the shape of hunger, something that had looked at her and Tam and seen not people but provisions. They had sealed it barely, badly, with Tam bleeding and Ines screaming words she hadn't known she knew. And Casimir's story fit that night like a key fits a lock, and she hated it, hated him for making it fit.

But.

But the drowned dead called to her across the floodwater, and they had never once felt hungry. They felt like homesickness given voices. They felt like her grandmother's humming. If Vesper's dead were monsters wearing memory, why did they sound so much like grief?

And if Casimir was telling the truth about the thing beneath Amareth — then what exactly had her grandmother's country opened? A wound? Or a door with two sides, one of which the Wardens had never bothered to look behind?

The lock clicked. Two guards marched her down a corridor, past a rack of confiscated goods — and there, tossed carelessly in an evidence bin between a smuggler's knives and a tax evader's silver candlesticks, lay the oilskin map.

Her map. Taken off her at capture, and filed. Filed, like a ledger entry. Like it was nothing.

Something cold and clear settled over her, the way it did when the dead spoke plainly. *They don't understand what they have,* whispered a voice near her ear — her grandmother's cadence, or close enough. *They think it's paper.*

The interrogation room was warmer than the cell. Casimir sat alone at a table with the map unrolled before him, weighted at the corners with inkwells, studying it the way a man studies a language he almost remembers.

"You're not destroying it," Ines observed, taking the offered chair.

"I'm considering how best to destroy it."

"You've had it for six hours and it's still in one piece. You could have burned it in the yard while I watched. Made a ceremony of it." She folded her hands, mirroring him. "You need me to destroy it. Or you need something from it. Which is it?"

For a long moment the Warden said nothing. Then, quietly, with the weariness of a man setting down something heavy: "The thresholds answer your blood. I cannot open them. I cannot close them properly anymore — the seals weaken each year, and each year I patch them with less understanding than the last. The men who built them are dead. I am the last one who remembers why." He tapped the map, on the mark for the drowned capital. "This one. The original door. If it opens, everything I did was for nothing, and the thing beneath Amareth walks out into a world that has forgotten how to fight it."

"And if it doesn't open," Ines said, "a whole country stays buried forever. Its dead stay exiled. Its people stay ghosts in their grandchildren's songs." She leaned forward. "Warden. You erased Vesper to keep the world safe. Fine. I believe you, mostly. But somewhere in thirty years of erasing, you stopped erasing a *danger* and started erasing a *people*. Their names. Their graves. Their language. Was that the seal too? Did my grandmother's lullabies threaten the world?"

Casimir's jaw tightened. "You don't know what it cost—"

"No," Ines said. "But I know what it costs *them*. I hear it every day. And here is my counter-offer, Warden, since we're trading: take me to the last threshold. Let me see the door with my own eyes, hear it with my own ears. If it's the mouth of a monster, I'll burn the map myself and sing your seal-song however you teach it to me. But if it's a door home—" She held his gaze. "Then you'll have to decide whether you're the man who saved the world or the man who murdered a country, and I don't think you've decided yet, or you wouldn't still be carrying that map around instead of burning it."

The silence stretched. Outside, rain had begun against the high windows, soft as fingers.

"You sound like her," Casimir said at last, and his voice cracked on it, just slightly. "My mother. Stubborn as a river."

"Good," said Ines. "Rivers get where they're going."

He stood, abruptly, and rang the bell for the guards — but as they came to take her back, he did something strange. He slid the map across the table, rolled and tied, and tucked it into her bound hands, folding her fingers over it.

"The escort to the capital leaves at dawn," he said, low, for her alone. "There will be an accident on the road. A wheel will break. Chains will fail. I cannot be seen to lose you twice." His eyes were hollow and fierce. "Go and look at your door, bone-carver. Look at it closely. And pray to whatever dead you trust that you find a monster — because a monster, at least, I would know how to kill."

The guards marched her out. Behind her, Warden Casimir stood alone in the lamplight with his empty table, and Ines, clutching the map against her chest, realized she had just been freed by the enemy — and had no idea whether it was mercy, a trap, or a man finally, after thirty years, wanting to be proven wrong.

Above the garrison gate, as they led her out into the rain, the dead of Harrow's Ford were singing. Quietly. In Vesperan.
