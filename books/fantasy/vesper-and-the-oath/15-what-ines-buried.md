## 15. What Ines Buried

The hills above the river road were the kind of place that made you understand why people believed in gods — not because anything holy lived there, but because nothing else did. No Wardens. No ledgers. Just wind moving through the bracken like a hand smoothing a bedsheet, and a ruined shepherd's hut with one good wall and a roof that leaked in three places, only one of which mattered.

Ines had been counting the leaks for two days. It was easier than thinking.

"Left corner again," Tam said from the doorway, where she stood wrapped in both their cloaks despite Ines's repeated objections. "If we tilt the pot under it, we can catch enough to drink. That's not water coming off the roof, by the way. That's the sky giving up on us personally."

"You're very cheerful for someone who was shot at yesterday."

"I'm very cheerful for someone who wasn't shot." Tam lowered herself carefully onto the flat stone they'd been using as a bench, her ribs clearly still objecting to the descent. The tithe-gang's parting gift, ten days old now, gone the color of a storm cloud. "How's the head?"

Ines touched her temple, where Casimir's man had clubbed her during the escape from the well. The bruise had settled into a dull, patient ache, like a debt collector who was willing to wait. "Still mine."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." She fed a twist of dry bracken into their little fire. It caught grudgingly, smoking first, apologizing later. "It's fine."

Tam watched her with those grey eyes that missed nothing except, apparently, the obvious. Ten days since the raid on the exile community. Ten days since Ines had run through burning gorse with the map pressed against her heart while people screamed her name behind her — *the bone-carver, take the bone-carver* — and she had run, because running was the one craft she'd never lost.

Ten days of Tam not asking the question sitting between them like an uninvited guest.

"You keep looking at me," Ines said.

"You keep flinching when I do it."

"I'm recovering. Flinching is therapeutic."

"Ines." Tam said her name the way she said most things — flat, economical, like she was paying by the syllable. But underneath it there was something else, something that had been growing since the flooded valley, since the night Tam had pulled her back from the black water and told her about her brother, about the map, about the promise to their mother. Something almost gentle. "Whatever it is. Say it before the fourth door does."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the slope, a crow made its opinion known.

Ines looked at her hands. They were a carver's hands, or they had been, before the second and third doors took the craft out of them — the muscle memory of the burin, the feel of grain, all of it gone like a room emptied overnight. She still knew *that* she had been a bone-carver. She just couldn't be one anymore. It was like being told a story about a stranger who happened to have her name.

She had thought losing her art was the worst thing the doors could take.

She'd been wrong. There was one thing left buried deeper than craft, and it was time to dig it up before someone else did.

"When the stranger came to my shop," she said, "dying on my floor — I told you he chose me because I was close. Because I heard things. That's what I let you believe."

Tam went very still. Stillness was Tam's version of shouting.

"He didn't choose me," Ines said. "He was sent to me. Or — no. He found me the way you find a well when you're dying of thirst. He didn't pick me out of Corvane. He picked me out of a list of names his mother gave him, and there were only two names left on it, and the other one was dead."

"What are you saying?"

"My grandmother," Ines said, and the word came out of her like a splinter that had been working toward the surface for years, "was Vesperan."

The silence afterward had texture. Weight. The fire crackled into it and got nowhere.

"She came south before the fall," Ines went on, because once the splinter was out the words just kept coming, forty years of pressure behind them. "Married a Corvane cooper. Changed her name so thoroughly that even I only ever knew her as Gran Odile, which is — I know now — about as Vesperan as a name gets. She used to sing to me. Songs in a language I thought was just nonsense she'd invented to annoy my grandfather. She taught me to listen. Not to hear — anyone can hear. To *listen*. To the spaces between things." Her laugh came out crooked. "I thought she meant birdsong. I built my whole life on a misunderstanding."

Tam hadn't moved. hadn't blinked. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and even, which anyone who knew her would recognize as the most dangerous register she owned. "The doors answer your blood."

"Yes."

"The thresholds that cost you your mother's face. Your craft. Every door we've opened — every price you've paid —"

"They would have opened for any of the bloodline. There just isn't any bloodline left. Except me." She spread her hands. "The last of Gran Odile's line, selling finger-bone charms in a night market, pretending to be nobody. And a dying man walking straight to my door because his mother remembered my grandmother's name."

"And you didn't tell me." Tam rose, slowly, the way weather changes. "Ten days. Through the valley. Through the raid. Through—" She stopped, jaw working. "I told you about Aldric. I told you what he promised our mother. I gave you—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Ines could see her hating it. "I gave you my brother's ghost, and you gave me a lie of omission with a bruise on it."

"It wasn't—"

"Don't." Tam held up a hand. "Don't tell me it wasn't a lie. You let me believe he stumbled into your shop by chance. You let me believe the map could have landed anywhere, with anyone, and my brother just happened to die holding it. Do you know what that belief was worth to me? It meant his death was *chance*. Chance is bearable. Chance is just the world being the world." She turned away, shoulders rigid. "But if he was sent — if he died because of some bloodline, some inheritance, some *plan* — then his death has a shape. And shaped things can be aimed at. And I've spent ten days walking beside the reason my brother is dead, calling her *Ines*, sharing my cloak with her."

"The reason your brother is dead," Ines said quietly, "is the Warden. Same as everything else."

"Don't deflect."

"I'm not deflecting, I'm correcting the record." She stood too, and her legs complained, and she ignored them. "You want to be angry? Be angry. You've earned it. But be angry at the right address. I didn't kill Aldric. I didn't send him. I didn't ask to be born with Vesper in my veins like a fever. My grandmother hid it so well that *I* didn't know until a man bled out on my floor whispering a country's name and every dead voice I've ever heard suddenly started singing harmony." Her hands were shaking. She let them. "You think I kept it secret because I'm cunning? I kept it secret because I was ashamed. Because the moment I say it out loud, I stop being a woman carrying a map and become a *key*. Keys get used, Tam. By Wardens. By exiles. By sisters with grudges. Everyone wants to turn me and nobody asks the key how it feels about doors."

That stopped Tam mid-stride. Ines watched the anger hit something in her and ricochet — saw her wrestle with it, the way she wrestled with everything, like a wrestler who'd never once considered letting go.

"The key," Tam said finally, "is also the person who pulled me out of a tithe-gang's grip. Who argued with me for an hour about whether killing that man was justice or murder, and was probably right, and I hate that. Who walked into black water because the drowned called her, and came back." She sat back down on the stone, heavily, like the anger had been load-bearing and she'd just removed it. "I'm allowed to be furious and grateful in the same breath. Nobody said feelings take turns."

"No," Ines admitted. "They don't. Believe me. I've been terrified of you and fond of you since the causeway."

"Fond." Tam tested the word like a coin she suspected of being clipped. "That's a generous term for the woman who ambushed you."

"You brought bread. Stale bread. Stolen bread. It's the thought that counts."

"It was *provisions*."

"It was a bribe and we both know it."

Something happened to Tam's face then — a twitch at the corner of her mouth, quickly suppressed, but unmistakable. In ten days Ines had catalogued perhaps four expressions on her; this was a fifth, and it suited her embarrassingly well.

Then it faded, and Tam looked into the fire, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped its armor entirely, and that was somehow harder to hear than the shouting.

"Aldric knew," she said. "Didn't he. About your grandmother. That's why he ran to you instead of to anyone else in Corvane. He knew the doors would open for you."

"He knew. His mother knew. Some network of the old families kept names alive, passed down like contraband. Gran Odile must have left her name with someone before she died — a friend, a cousin, someone who promised." Ines swallowed. "He said — that first night, with the blood coming up between the floorboards — he said, *she'll hear us, tell her it's the singing kind.* I thought he was delirious. He wasn't. He was introducing himself."

"The singing kind."

"The dead of Vesper don't talk the way Corvane's dead do. Corvane's ghosts mutter. Complain. Ask me to fix their fences, more or less. The Vesperan dead *sing*. I heard it the first time on the road north and I thought I was going mad. Turns out I was just going home." She laughed, and it hurt. "Gran Odile's songs. All those nonsense syllables. They're the same song, Tam. Every one of them. I've been humming my grandmother's country my whole life without knowing it had a name."

Tam was quiet a long moment. Then: "My mother used to hum something. She said it was a lullaby from her side of the family. We assumed she meant somewhere ordinary. A village. A valley." Her eyes came up, and they were wet, and she let them be, which for Tam was roughly equivalent to weeping openly. "Aldric found the map because our mother's family was Vesperan too. Didn't they have to be. That's why he could carry it. Why the exiles trusted him."

"We're the same country, you and I," Ines said softly. "Just different verses."

"Oh, don't." Tam dragged a hand down her face. "Don't make me feel things *and* say things like that in the same hour. I have limits."

"Your limits are structural. Like the roof."

"The roof is doing its best."

"So am I." Ines sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and let the silence be a shared thing instead of a contested one. The fire settled. Down the hill, the crow had found quieter opinions. "I should have told you at the causeway. I told myself I'd tell you when I understood it myself, and then I told myself I'd tell you after the next door, and then the raid happened, and secrets are comfortable the way bruises are comfortable — you get used to carrying them and start calling the carrying strength."

"I know," Tam said. "I've been carrying my brother's death the same way. Calling it purpose." She glanced sideways. "We're very stupid, the two of us. It's a wonder we've survived."

"We survive *because* we're stupid. Smart women would have quit at the first threshold."

"Smart women wouldn't have opened it."

"There you go."

Tam leaned her head briefly against Ines's shoulder — a gesture lasting perhaps two seconds, worth more than the whole conversation — and then straightened, businesslike again, because grief and affection could only be permitted in small doses before she developed hives. "So. The bloodline. If you're the last, then the map's final door needs you specifically. Not just any carrier. *You.*"

"That seems to be the shape of it."

"And the Warden knows."

"Casimir has known since the well, I'd guess. Maybe since the market. He interrogated that inquisitor for a reason." Ines stared into the flames. "He'll stop trying to take the map from us soon. Why steal the key when you can simply collect it?"

"Cheerful."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them grim."

Tam snorted — an actual, unguarded snort, immediately mortifying to them both — and reached for the pot to shift it under the leaking corner. And that was when Ines heard it.

Not the crow. Not the wind. Underneath everything, thin as wire, rising out of the eastern dark where the hills fell away toward the lowlands — a thread of singing. The Vesperan dead. But wrong. Not the warm, layered chorus she'd come to know, the voices of the drowned and the scattered singing their country back into shape.

This was one voice. Cold. Patient. Repeating a single phrase over and over, in Gran Odile's language, and the phrase translated itself in Ines's chest before her mind could catch up:

*The fourth door is already open. The fourth door is already open.*

She was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, the pot clattering, Tam swearing and reaching for her knife.

"Ines?"

"The fourth door," Ines said. Her mouth had gone dry as chalk. "Tam — the fourth door. It's ahead of us on the map. Three days' walk." She turned to face the east, where the singing threaded down through the dark like smoke finding a draft. "And something over there is telling me we're already too late."
