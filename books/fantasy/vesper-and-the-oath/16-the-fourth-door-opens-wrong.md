## 16. The Fourth Door Opens Wrong

The fourth threshold was supposed to be three days' walk further north, in a place the map called *the Quiet Orchard*, where the dead had promised — through the map's whispering margins — that the door would be patient and the opening would be gentle.

We did not have three days.

We had smoke on the southern ridge at dawn, and dogs, and the particular quality of silence that meant Casimir's riders had stopped chasing us and started herding us.

"They're pushing us east," Tam said, crouched at the treeline with her spyglass made of two polished river stones and a length of copper tube she'd bought off a peddler who had assured her it was definitely not stolen from a Warden's officer. "Off the high ground. Toward the fen road."

"Then we go west."

"West is the orchard. Three days." She lowered the stones and looked at me, and I saw her doing the arithmetic I was already doing: three days of open country against riders who knew it, with eleven exiles behind us who could barely walk, let alone run. Old Sefa with her bad hip. The twins, who were nine. Maren, who hadn't spoken since the raid at the well except once, in her sleep, in Vesperan, when she'd said something about bread.

"The map says the door will wait," I said.

"The map," Tam said, "is a dead man's handwriting in a language nobody speaks anymore, drawn by a country that doesn't exist. Forgive me if I don't treat its promises like scripture."

"It's been right so far."

"It's been right *enough* so far. There's a difference, and I've buried people in it."

I wanted to argue. I was, if I'm honest, very good at arguing with Tam by then; ten weeks on the road had given us both a fluency in each other's tempers that felt almost like intimacy, though neither of us would have used that word under torture. But I looked back down the slope at our ragged procession — the exiles wrapped in every scrap of wool they owned, moving with the shuffling patience of people who had already lost everything once and were braced to lose it again — and I thought about what happened to them if the riders caught us.

The map went into my hands. The map stayed in my hands. That was the whole architecture of everything.

"We open it tonight," I said. "Wherever we are when night falls. Even if it's wrong."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved through the pines above us, carrying the faint sweet rot of the fens to the east.

"You've opened three doors," she said finally. "Each one cost you more than the last. You can't carve anymore. You told me you looked at your own knives last week and couldn't remember which end was which." Her voice wasn't cruel. That was somehow worse. "What's left after the fourth one, Ines?"

I didn't have an answer. I had a grandmother's blood and a dying stranger's oath and a growing suspicion that I was spending myself like a woman with money she believed would never run out.

"I'll tell you what's left," I said. "I'll tell you tomorrow."

* * *

Night fell early, the way autumn nights do — all at once, like a lid coming down. We found shelter in the shell of a shepherd's cot, its roof long collapsed, its walls still standing waist-high and thick enough to break the wind. The exiles huddled in the lee of the south wall with what fire we dared, small and screened with turf. Tam set watches. I sat in the middle of the ruin with the map spread across my knees and listened for the door.

Here is what I never managed to explain properly to anyone, not even Tam, not even after everything: the thresholds don't look like anything. They aren't doors. They're seams — places where the world was stitched shut over a wound, and if you have the right ears, or the right blood, you can hear the stitching. A kind of hum. A held note. Like a singer breathing in and never being allowed to let the sound out.

I heard it twenty paces north of the fire, where the ground rose into a low bank of old field-stone.

"There," I said.

Tam came and stood beside me. She couldn't hear it — no one could — but she'd learned to read my face the way sailors read weather. "Bad?" she asked.

"Close. And..." I tilted my head. "Thin. The other doors felt deep. This one feels like it's just under the skin of things."

"Because we're early," Tam said. It wasn't a question.

"Because we're early."

She looked at me for a while. Firelight moved on her face, and for once she didn't look angry or grim or like a woman composing a eulogy for everyone she loved. She just looked tired, and young, and like someone's little sister, which is what she was, underneath everything, before the world got hold of her.

"My brother trusted you," she said. "He was an idiot about a great many things, but he wasn't wrong about people. So I'm going to ask you one time, and then I'll stand wherever you need me to stand. Is this door going to open clean?"

I thought about lying to her. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the dead keep of my sins: I considered it, carefully, for the length of one full breath.

"I don't know," I said.

She nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some test she hadn't wanted me to pass. "All right," she said. "Wake me when it's time. And Ines — if it goes wrong, you get the exiles out first. Not me. Not yourself. Them. Swear it."

"That's a terrible oath. I refuse to die heroically, it's undignified."

"*Swear it.*"

"I swear," I said, and the strange thing was how much it cost, saying it, and how much lighter I felt after — the way oaths work on people like me, the way they bind tighter than any law ever written.

* * *

I opened the door at moonrise, alone, kneeling on the field-stone bank with my palms flat against the cold earth and the map's whispers rising around me like breath on glass.

The other openings had been hard, but they had been *clean*. A price taken, a seam parted, memory pouring out of me like water from a tipped jug — my mother's face, my craft, my tenth summer, my father's voice saying my name — and in exchange, vision. Vesper whole. Vesper burning. Vesper in spring with washing strung between houses and someone laughing in a language I was learning in my sleep.

This one fought me.

Not resisted — I want to be precise, because precision is all I have left of myself some days. Resisting implies a will opposed to yours. This was different. The seam was too new, too shallow, the stitching still wet, and when I pulled at it, the note beneath the ground changed key. It dropped. Down and down, past hearing, into a register I felt in my teeth and my sternum and the backs of my eyes.

And something on the other side noticed me.

You know how a room changes when someone walks in? Even blindfolded, even deaf, you know. The air rearranges itself around a person. That is what happened to the night. The dark behind the seam leaned toward me the way a starving man leans toward a lit window.

*I should have stopped.* I have replayed that moment perhaps four thousand times. I felt the wrongness — I felt it clearly, the way you feel ice underfoot a half-second before it cracks — and I kept pulling anyway, because the riders were a day behind us, because Maren hadn't spoken in six weeks, because I had sworn an oath to a dead man and I had built my entire life on the principle that oaths outlive the people who make them.

The seam parted.

It was not a door. Doors lead somewhere. This was a *mouth*.

Cold came through first — not winter-cold, not fen-cold, but a cold with appetite in it, a cold that wanted. Then the smell: iron and old water and something sweeter underneath, something like flowers rotting in a sealed room. And then —

Then it touched my mind, and I understood, in one white-hot instant, why a country had to die.

It didn't speak. Speaking would have required it to be a person, and it was not a person; it was the absence where persons had been scraped away, a hunger shaped like whatever it was eating. It found my memories — the ones I had left, the thinning inventory of me — and it began to *drink*. Not violently. Gently. The way you'd sip something you intended to finish over a very long time.

I felt it take my name.

Not Ines — that it left, finding it dry, uninteresting. It took the *weight* of my name, the way it felt in my mother's mouth, the way it sounded when Tam shouted it across a campsite, and I felt that weight go down into the dark like a stone into a well, and I heard — distantly, horribly — the well swallow.

Somewhere behind me, someone screamed my name, and I couldn't tell whose voice it was, because the thing had drunk the sound of it.

Hands seized my shoulders. Tam. She was shouting words I couldn't parse, shaking me, and her grip was the only real thing left in the world, so I held onto it. Behind her the exiles were awake and screaming, and old Sefa was dragging the children back from the bank, and the seam was widening, widening, the mouth stretching, and through it I could see — not see, *perceive*, there are no honest words for this — the vast slow turning of the thing beneath Vesper, the thing that had slept under the drowned capital since before the fall, the thing Casimir had sealed at the cost of erasing an entire country from every record, every mind, every grave.

*Oh,* I thought, with the small clear part of me that was still mine. *Oh, he was right.*

That may be the worst sentence I have ever thought.

* * *

How we closed it. I will tell you, because it matters, and because I only remember pieces.

Tam had a knife at my throat before I understood what she was doing — not threatening me, anchoring me. Pain, sharp and specific and *hers*, a sensation the hunger couldn't drink because it belonged entirely to the moment, entirely to her hand. "Come back," she was saying, over and over, in English and then, God help her, in broken Vesperan she'd learned from the exiles, "*Kemeth, kemeth*" — come home, come home — and the words landed in me like hooks.

And the dead came.

They had been walking with us since the drowned valley, invisible, patient, the exiled dead of Vesper following their last chance at home. I had felt them as comfort, as company, as a low warm chorus at the edge of my hearing. Now they threw themselves into the gap. I felt them go past me like a wind — hundreds of them, thousands, all the unburied and unmourned, hurling themselves bodily into the seam to weigh it shut, ghosts lying down across a wound the way farmers lie across a breach in a dike.

They didn't destroy it. They couldn't. But they were *many*, and hunger, however vast, must eat one thing at a time, and they gave it themselves, memory by memory, name by name, a feast of the forgotten poured into a bottomless throat — and while it swallowed, Tam dragged me backward off the bank, and the map, flung loose from my coat, lay on the stones between us, and it was *screaming*. Ink writhing. Dead voices pouring out of it in every direction at once, and among them, cutting through everything, one voice I knew:

My grandmother's. Saying, in Vesperan, a word I now understood for the first time.

*Shut it. Shut it. Shut it.*

I put my bleeding palms on the seam and I pushed, with everything the hunger hadn't managed to drink, with the oath and the fear and Tam's knife-hand and the image of nine-year-old twins asleep against old Sefa's hips, and the stitching took. The seam drew closed. The note beneath the ground climbed back up out of that terrible register and became, once more, a held breath instead of an open mouth.

The silence afterward was total. Even the pines had stopped.

* * *

I don't remember much of the rest of that night. They tell me I walked, which is more than I'd have guessed. I remember firelight. I remember Tam sitting beside me with her arm around my shoulders, which she had never done, holding me up the way you'd hold up a wall you weren't sure would keep standing. I remember Maren — silent, wordless Maren — pressing a strip of dried apple into my hand, solemnly, like a sacrament, and I remember that I ate it and it tasted like the best thing I had ever eaten, because it tasted like the living world.

Dawn came grey and ordinary. The riders' smoke was visible on the southern ridge again, closer.

I sat against the cot's old wall and watched the light come up and tried to take inventory of myself. Name: present, but lighter. Memories: thinner. Somewhere in me there was a new-shaped hole, smooth-edged, and I couldn't tell what had lived in it, and that frightened me more than the hunger had. At least the hunger had been outside. This was a room in my own house I could no longer enter.

Tam crouched in front of me. Her face was bruised where I'd thrashed against her. Neither of us mentioned it.

"Now you know," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"What Casimir knows. What he's carrying around inside him every day, alone, while we've all been calling him a monster." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it at all. "He erased a country to bury that. And we're digging it up."

I looked at my hands. Bone-carver's hands, scarred, capable, and for ten weeks now unable to remember what half their knowledge had been. I thought about the exiles sleeping behind us, dreaming in a forbidden language. I thought about my grandmother's voice screaming *shut it*, and about the fact that she had also, once, pressed this bloodline into the world knowing exactly what doors it could open.

"He's not entirely wrong," I said. Each word cost something. "That thing has to stay buried. If I believed otherwise before last night, I don't anymore."

"But," Tam said. She knew me well enough by then to hear the shape of a sentence before it finished.

"But the doors aren't the seal. I felt it — the hunger isn't *behind* the thresholds, it's *beneath* everything, and the thresholds are stitches over the wound, not the wound itself. Casimir didn't close the mouth. He just nailed the floorboards down and burned the house's name off the deed." I looked up at her. "And the dead — Tam, the dead of Vesper threw themselves into that gap to save us. His own countrymen. The people he erased stood between that thing and a bone-carver and her ragged column of refugees. Whatever else is true, they don't deserve to be forgotten for it. They *earned* remembering."

"So what do we do?"

I turned and looked south, at the smoke on the ridge, and then north, toward the flooded valley and the drowned capital and the last, vast, patient door.

"We stop running," I said. "We stop sneaking. We walk straight down the middle of the road to the water, in daylight, where every Warden's spy can see us — and we let Casimir come to *us*. Because he's spent ten years believing he's the only one who knows what's under there." I felt something settle in my chest, hard and calm, like a keel finding water. "He needs to learn there's someone else who knows now. Someone who's seen it and lived. And someone who's going to open that final door whether he likes it or not — because the dead didn't throw themselves into the dark to buy us time for *nothing*."

Tam studied me for a long moment. Then she stood, rolled her shoulders, and raised her voice across the campsite in the flat commanding tone that had, over ten weeks, become as familiar to me as my own heartbeat.

"Up," she called to the exiles. "Everyone up. We march at first light — and today, friends, we march loud."

Behind us, on the southern ridge, the smoke thickened.

Ahead of us, far to the north, beneath forty feet of black floodwater, something ancient shifted in its sleep — and, for the first time in ten years, the dead of Vesper began to sing.
