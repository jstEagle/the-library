## 17. The Long March to the Water

The exiles came to them at dawn on the fourth day out of the hills, strung along the frozen road like beads off a broken necklace.

Ines saw them first as a smudge against the grey — carts, and people walking beside the carts because the horses had been eaten months ago, and a woman carrying a child who was too old to be carried but was too cold to walk. Tam's hand went to her knife before Ines caught her wrist.

"They're ours," Ines said. "Look at their faces."

She didn't know how else to explain it. She had spent ten years learning to read the living the way she read the dead, and these faces had the same look — the look of people standing in a doorway of a house they'd been told never existed, waiting for someone to tell them they could come in.

There were forty-one of them. The survivors of the raid at the well, plus families from three other hiding places, drawn by word passed mouth to mouth in a language that officially had no words: *the bone-carver is going to the water. The one who opens doors. She is going home.*

"I never said that," Ines murmured, watching them come.

"You didn't have to," said an old man with a shepherd's crook and a limp, close enough now to be heard. "The dead said it for you. They've been saying it since the well." He looked at her the way people look at weather. "My name was Havel Ostrow. It isn't anymore — the ledgers took it — but I'm asking you to use it anyway. Just between us. Just for the road."

Ines used it. And something in the old man's shoulders came down half an inch, like a door unbarred.

Tam counted heads twice, checked the carts for weapons, and found only bread tools and a grandfather's sword so rusted it would sooner fall apart than cut anyone. Then she stood beside Ines and watched forty-one people arrange themselves around them without being asked, and said, quietly, "We're not travelers anymore."

"No," Ines said.

"We're a procession."

* * *

They walked.

That was most of it, in truth. The romance of a great march lasts about a morning; after that it is feet, and frost, and the arithmetic of bread. Ines learned the procession the way she learned anything — by its small sounds. Havel's crook tapping stone. The child, Mirela, singing under her breath a counting song whose numbers belonged to no living country. The widow Sefa arguing cheerfully with her son about whether you could make soup from pine needles if you were stubborn enough. (You could, apparently. You just had to be *very* stubborn.)

And underneath all of it, growing louder with every northward mile, the other sound. The dead.

They had been with her since Corvane, a hum at the edge of hearing, but here they had weight and direction. They walked where the living walked. When the procession stopped at dusk, Ines felt them stop too, settling around the fires like kin taking seats at a table, and when the column moved at dawn she felt the whole invisible country shift its weight and follow.

"Do you see them?" she asked Tam one grey afternoon.

"No." Tam walked with her blade loose in its sheath, always, even now. "But the dogs won't go near the rear of the column, and there are no dogs back there. And last night old Havel greeted somebody at the edge of the firelight. He bowed." Her jaw worked. "He bowed to nobody."

"He bowed to his wife," Ines said gently. "She's been walking beside him since the valley. She wants him to know the soup was better with more salt."

Tam stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, cracked sound, like ice giving way — and had to turn away and pretend to check the treeline. "More salt," she repeated. "Ten years dead and still correcting his cooking." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and pretended that was also about the wind.

* * *

Casimir read the reports by lamplight in a tent that smelled of ink and wet wool, and did not like what they told him, which was nothing.

Fourteen riders harrying the column's flanks, and fourteen riders reporting shadows. Not ambushes — he had ordered no ambushes; he wanted the map taken whole and the bone-carver taken breathing — but *shadows*. A scout who swore the column he'd been shadowing for two days had forty-one souls in it and then, at dusk, forty-two. A picket who heard footsteps pass his post in the night, dozens of them, unhurried, and found no prints in the snow. Another man simply refused to ride the rear any longer and would not say why, which was worse than if he'd said.

"Superstition," said Captain Vey, who had come up from the coast and believed in nothing he couldn't flog.

"Yes," Casimir said. "That is precisely what worries me."

He knew this country. That was the thing none of his officers understood, the thing he could never say aloud: he had helped unmake it, and it had never once let him forget the shape of what he'd undone. He had sealed the door beneath the drowned capital with his own hands and his own name, and every mile the bone-carver marched north, he felt the seal the way a man feels an old break in cold weather.

He spread the map-copy on his table — the sketch his artists had made from a dying man's blood before the oilskin reached Ines Montrose — and put his finger on the water. There. Where Vesper's capital had stood until he helped stand it down into the flood.

"She's not fleeing," he said softly. "I thought she was running. She isn't. She's leading them."

"To what?"

"Home," Casimir said, and hated how much the word cost him. "Ride ahead. Burn the causeway bridges. I want the water between us and them, and I want it wide."

* * *

The tithe-gang caught them at the ferry crossing, or thought they did.

There were nine of them, armed men with Warden-grey sashes worn over whatever armor they'd started with, and they came down on the column's tail expecting weepers and runners. What they got was Havel Ostrow stepping calmly into their path with his crook planted like a fence post, and behind him forty people who had already lost everything a tithe-gang knew how to take.

What they also got, though only Ines saw it clearly, was the other column. The dead had stopped walking. They stood in the snow on either side of the road, ranks deep, faces turned toward the grey-sashed men, and the air went cold in a way that had nothing to do with winter. One of the gang's horses screamed and bolted. Two men dropped their weapons without being touched.

Tam, however, required no ghosts. She went through the gang's left flank like a whetstone through soft wood, and when it was over, the leader knelt in the slush with her knife at his throat, and everyone waited for the sentence.

"Please," the man said. "I've got daughters."

"So did Sefa," Tam said. "You burned her village for a grain levy in the spring. I've heard the story four nights running around your own campfires' worth of stolen firewood." The knife pressed. "Say something worth my mercy."

"Mercy outside the law is just murder with better manners," the man gasped — and it was Ines who said it, stepping forward, quoting herself from a hillside argument weeks ago that neither of them had gotten over. "But so is vengeance, Tam. Let the law have him. Let there *be* a law again someday. That's what we're marching toward, isn't it? A place where this—" she gestured at the kneeling man, the snow, the knife— "isn't the only court there is."

For a long moment Tam didn't move. Snow fell on the blade, on the man's throat, on the forty-one watching faces.

Then she pulled the knife back and cut the grey sash off him instead, one clean stroke. "Walk south," she said. "Tell them what you saw. Tell them the dead are coming home, and they're done paying tithes." She stood over him while he scrambled up and ran, and only when he was a dot on the white road did she say, to no one, "One day you'll be wrong about this, bone-carver."

"One day," Ines agreed. "Not today, though."

"Not today," Tam admitted, and something in her voice had changed — had been changing for miles, for weeks — into something almost like surrender, and almost like rest.

* * *

Winter closed its hand.

They slept in barns that the ledgers said were empty and weren't, in ruins that the maps said had never been built and had. Farmers left bread on fence posts and pretended not to watch the column pass. Once, crossing a frozen stream at dawn, Mirela slipped, and before any living hand could reach her the ice held — held long past reason, long past sense, a full breath, two — until Havel's grandson dragged her out, and the old man stood looking down at the river with tears freezing in his beard and said, "Thank you, love," to the water.

The dead grew thicker as they went north. Ines stopped trying to count them. Some nights the column seemed to walk inside a crowd, a market-day press of unseen elbows, and the voices layered over each other until she caught whole conversations: arguments about harvests, gossip about a wedding fifty years drowned, a child asking *are we nearly there* in a language the world had outlawed and that Ines, to her own astonishment, was beginning to understand.

Her grandmother's language. She had never known that until the road taught her.

"You're limping," Tam observed one evening, dropping onto the straw beside her.

"I'm walking," Ines corrected. "Limping implies I intend to stop."

"Eat." Tam pushed half a loaf at her — real wheat bread, traded from a farm whose owner had wept while handing it over, saying it was the first time in ten years she'd baked for a reason. Around them the barn settled, the exiles slept, and somewhere near the doorway Havel snored in time with a second, softer snore that only Ines could hear. "How far now?"

"Three days. Maybe four, if the causeway bridges are burned."

"They'll be burned."

"Then four."

Tam chewed in silence awhile. "I used to think I was following you to keep you honest," she said at last. "Watch you. Make sure my brother's death bought something true." She turned the loaf over in her hands. "Somewhere back there I stopped being the watcher."

"I know," Ines said. "You started being the rear guard. You've been checking the column's tail every night for a week."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know that too." Ines leaned her shoulder against Tam's, briefly, the way you lean on a wall that has proven it will hold. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we walk into the water's country, and I want you sharp."

"And you?"

"I'll sleep when the dead do." Ines smiled tiredly. "Which, thank every saint there is, will be soon."

* * *

On the third morning they crested the last ridge, and the flood opened below them — grey to the horizon, flat as hammered tin, holding the sky the way a mirror holds a face. Somewhere under that water lay streets, bells, gardens, a palace, a hundred thousand lives. Somewhere under that water stood the door.

The column stopped. Nobody spoke. Forty-one living people stood in the snow and looked at the grave of their country, and Ines felt the dead surge forward around her like a tide reaching a shore it had been denied for ten years — felt them press against the ridge line, straining, *yearning*, and heard, rising faint and vast from the flooded valley, a sound she had no word for and understood completely:

The dead, cheering.

"Well," said Havel Ostrow, wiping his face with his sleeve, his voice wobbling between laughter and grief. "It's about time somebody invited us home."

Below them, at the drowned shoreline, something glinted — tents, horses, the disciplined geometry of a military camp. Grey coats. Hundreds of them. And on the water's edge, small with distance but unmistakable, a single figure standing alone, facing the ridge, waiting.

Tam's hand found her knife. Ines's hand found Tam's arm.

"He's not guarding the door," Ines said slowly, listening to the voices, to the one voice beneath all the others that sounded almost like her grandmother's. "He's guarding us from it. Or himself from it. Come on — he and I have a bargain to finish."

They started down together, the living and the dead, forty-one and numberless, toward the water.
