## 18. The Drowned Capital

The water began to speak three days before they reached it.

At first it was only a murmur under the wind, the way a shell holds a sea. Then it was words. Then it was names — hundreds of them, thousands, spoken in a language Ines had never learned and understood perfectly, because the dead had been teaching it to her in her sleep since the first threshold opened.

*Vesperan,* she thought, standing at the edge of the flooded valley with her boots sinking into black mud. *They're all speaking Vesperan.*

"Can you hear them?" Tam asked. She stood a pace behind, as she always did now, close enough to catch Ines if the water called too loudly, far enough to pretend she wasn't hovering.

"They're singing," Ines said. "Badly."

Tam made a sound that was almost a laugh. "The dead of your grandmother's country sing badly?"

"Somebody has to." Ines squinted across the water. It stretched to the horizon, grey-green and patient, and beneath it — she could feel it the way you feel a stone through the sole of your boot — lay the capital. The drowned city. The last threshold. "They've been waiting ten years. I imagine their pitch has suffered."

It was gallows humor and they both knew it. But Tam smiled anyway, briefly, the way she did when Ines said something flippant about dying, which was often lately, and which Tam had stopped correcting somewhere around the fourth door.

Behind them, the procession waited. Forty-one exiles, what remained of the community scattered at the well, plus the nine who had joined on the road, plus old Marette who insisted she was coming to see the water one more time before anyone told her she couldn't. They had walked out of autumn into winter together, harried by Warden outriders, fed by villages that remembered Vesper differently than their ledgers did, and accompanied — invisibly, but Ines felt them like warmth from a hearth — by the dead of Vesper, who had marched alongside them for two hundred miles without once asking for anything.

That was the thing about these dead that Ines had never managed to explain to Tam. The dead of Corvane wanted things. Charms, vengeance, remembrance, small comforts. The dead of Vesper wanted only to go home, and they were so patient about it that it broke her heart a little more each day.

"We cross at dawn?" Tam asked.

"We wade at dawn," Ines corrected. "The door's under the water. Under the city. Under—" she gestured vaguely at everything, "—all of it."

"How far down?"

"I don't know. The map's gone quiet. It's been quiet since we passed the third door, like it's finished talking and is just watching now." She patted her coat pocket, where the oilskin sat, blank and warm. A blank map should have frightened her. Instead it felt like a held breath.

Tam looked out at the water for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "My brother would have hated this. He couldn't swim."

Ines turned. In all their months together, Tam had spoken of Sarel perhaps six times, each time like setting down something heavy and picking it right back up. "Then we'll be swimming for him too."

"That's not how swimming works."

"It's how *oaths* work," Ines said. "I've found they're much less sensible than swimming."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and they went into the water.

Marette and the oldest exiles stayed on the shore with the children, keeping a fire going, because someone had to witness the return even if they couldn't make the descent. The rest followed Ines down a road that had been a road once and was now a corridor of silt and drowned fences, walking into water that rose from ankle to knee to waist to chest, cold enough to stop breath, clear enough — impossibly, blessedly clear — to show the way ahead.

And the dead came down to meet them.

Ines had expected resistance, or grief, or the terrible hungry pull she'd felt crossing the flooded valley. Instead the water filled with light the color of late afternoon, and shapes gathered along the drowned road like neighbors come out to watch a homecoming parade. An old man in a ferryman's coat bowed to her. A girl of maybe eight ran alongside the column, skipping, her feet not disturbing the silt. A woman pressed something into Ines's hand as she passed — a button, brass, worn smooth — and was gone before Ines could thank her.

"They're happy," Ines said, and her voice cracked on it. "Tam. They're *happy.*"

"Don't cry underwater," Tam advised. "It looks exactly the same but feels worse."

The city rose around them as they descended — or rather, they sank toward it, and it revealed itself the way a face reveals itself when you finally look directly at it. Rooftops first, then spires, then whole streets laid out in a geometry that made Ines's chest ache with recognition she had no right to feel. She had never seen this place. Her grandmother had left it before Ines was born. And yet her feet knew the turns. Her hands knew the shape of the door-handles they passed.

"This is what the doors took from me," she whispered. "This is what they were showing me. Not memories. *Directions.*"

The water deepened. The exiles behind her began to breathe strangely — not drowning, none of them drowning, the dead would not allow it, but breathing water the way you breathe air in a dream, without questioning it. Ahead, at the bottom of a great sunken square, around a fountain still frozen mid-splash, stood a door.

Not a seam in the air this time. Not a crack, not a threshold-mark scratched into stone. A *door* — tall as a cathedral gate, dark wood banded in iron, set freestanding in the middle of the square with nothing behind it, framed by two pillars carved with seven marks. The seven thresholds. This was the first of them, the original, the mother-door, and every other door they'd opened had been a finger pointing back here.

It was patient. That was the only word for it. It had been waiting under the water for ten years and it could wait ten thousand more, and it knew it, and so did she.

The ghosts of those who closed it stood before it.

There were twelve of them, arranged in a crescent, and they were different from the other dead. Older in the way mountains are older than hills. Their clothes were of a fashion Ines had seen only in fragments — visions from the first threshold, Vesper whole and burning — and their faces were turned toward her with an attention that made her want to apologize for existing.

The one at the center stepped forward. A woman, tall, grey-haired, with ink stains on her fingers and a bone stylus hanging at her throat.

Ines knew her.

She had never seen her in her life, and she knew her the way she knew the turns of the drowned streets: by blood, by inheritance, by the shape of a face she'd been told about in secondhand stories until it had worn grooves in her imagination. The woman who had taught her mother to carve. The woman whose finger-bone Ines had once, unknowingly, used for a luck charm, because a Corvane trader sold it to her in a lot of assorted relics and she hadn't asked whose it was.

"Oh," Ines said. The water took the sound and made it small. "*Oh.*"

"You have my hands," her grandmother said. Her voice was the voice from the map, the one that had read itself to Ines on the road north in dead voices. "I wondered, all these years, who got the hands."

* * *

They spoke — if you could call it speaking, this conversation that happened half in words and half in memory, the dead pressing understanding into her faster than speech allowed. The exiles hung back at the edge of the square, weeping openly, reaching toward relatives among the twelve and being gently refused. These twelve had a single duty, and they would not set it aside, not even for love.

"You closed it," Ines said. "You closed the door. All of you. Why?"

"Because it was killing us," her grandmother said simply. "Or rather — because staying was killing us, and leaving was erasing us, and we chose erasure over death. There is a difference. It took me forty years to understand it, and I had to die to finish understanding."

"The thing beneath," Ines said. "Casimir calls it a wound. He says Vesper opened something that should have stayed shut."

"Casimir," her grandmother said, and the name moved through the twelve like a cold current. "He was a boy here. He carried water for the temple. He was not wrong, Ines. That is the worst part. He was not wrong about the hunger, only about the cure." She gestured at the door. "We sealed it with ourselves. Twelve keepers. Our names, our memories, our deaths given to the lock. And the country paid the same price in mirror — erased from every record, every ledger, every living mind that could be made to forget. A country locked away, and a lock made of a country."

"But the doors — the seven—"

"Were our apology," her grandmother said. "A way back, if anyone could ever find them. If anyone could pay the tolls. We never believed anyone would. We hoped it the way you hope for rain in a drought — sincerely, and without expectation." Her eyes, which were Ines's own eyes returned to her from the past, crinkled. "You paid four tolls to get here. Four pieces of yourself. Your mother's face. Your craft, twice over, though you clawed some of it back. And something else you haven't told the angry one with the knives."

Ines flushed. "Her name is Tam."

"I know. I've been listening to her threaten you for two hundred miles. It's very romantic."

"It is *not*—"

"Ines." Her grandmother's voice gentled, and the gentleness was worse than any sternness. "Before you open it, you must hear the question. We are bound to ask it. Every keeper before me asked it of every soul who reached this square, and in ten years, no one has. So hear it."

The twelve drew closer. The water stilled. Even the singing dead beyond the square fell silent, and the silence was enormous, the silence of a whole country holding its breath.

"*Is a country worth what it will cost you?*"

And the cost unrolled before her, the way the tolls always showed themselves at the last moment, honest as a blade. Not another memory. She had braced for another memory, and it wasn't that.

It was permanence. The door needed a keeper on the living side — an anchor, a hinge, a name nailed to the frame. Whoever opened it would become what the twelve had been, but reversed: not a ghost guarding a sealed country, but a living woman holding an open one. She would stand at the threshold until she died there. She would remember everything — every exile's name, every street, every song sung badly — so that the country could forget nothing ever again. And in exchange, her own name would thin. She would keep her hands, her craft, her stubbornness, her terrible jokes. But *Ines Montrose*, the name her mother gave her, the name carved into no ledger anywhere — that would pour into the door like water into dry ground, and become its mortar.

The country would become real again. She would become, in any record that mattered, no one.

"And if I say no?" Ines asked. Her voice shook. "If I turn around?"

"Then we seal it again," her grandmother said, "with what strength remains to us, and the exiles live out their lives in exile, and the songs stay badly sung, and someday the last person who remembers Vesper dies, and the country goes out like a candle. No blame. No shame. It is a terrible price, and you owe us nothing. You are not the debt, child. You are only the one who happened to arrive."

"But my blood—"

"Your blood opened the locks. It does not obligate you to hold the door." Her grandmother smiled, tired and fierce. "Love obligates nothing. That is what makes it love."

Ines looked back. Across the square, Tam stood with the exiles, arms crossed against the cold, watching her with an expression she had learned to read over two hundred miles: *whatever you choose, I will make sure you survive choosing it.* Behind Tam, old Marette's face was wet. Somewhere above them all, forty-one fires' worth of people waited on a shore for a country to decide whether it existed.

Ten years, Ines thought. Ten years of being small. Of carving charms too minor to call magic, of hearing the dead and pretending she didn't, of surviving so carefully that survival had become the entire shape of her life. She had spent a decade making herself hard to notice, and here, at the bottom of a drowned city, she understood at last why that had never felt like living: because she had been practicing, all along, for a role that required her to be unforgettable.

"I used your finger-bone for a charm once," she said to her grandmother. "I didn't know it was yours. A trader sold it to me. I carved a protection word into it and sold it to a baker's wife, and it worked, and I always wondered why that one worked better than the others."

Her grandmother laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and several of the twelve looked scandalized. "Did it protect her?"

"For years, as far as I know."

"Then I was glad to be useful." The laughter settled into something steadier. "Well, carver? The question stands. Is a country worth what it will cost you?"

Ines looked at the door. At the pillars. At the button in her pocket from the woman who had pressed it there, and the eight-year-old who had skipped beside her, and the ferryman who had bowed. At the whole patient, hopeful, badly-singing weight of a nation that had chosen erasure over death and left a lamp burning in the window anyway.

"No," she said.

Her grandmother's face flickered — and Ines hurried on, because she had learned from the dead that precision matters.

"A country isn't worth it. Countries are lines and ledgers; Casimir was right about that much. But *these people* are worth it. The ones on the shore and the ones in the water and the ones who'll be born after. And I'm not paying for the country." She lifted her chin. "I'm paying for the homecoming. Those are different purchases, and I'll thank you to write down the distinction, since apparently I'm about to become the writing-down."

For a moment the twelve simply looked at her. Then her grandmother pressed both ink-stained hands together, the way Ines's mother had done before beginning any difficult carving, and Ines realized she was seeing the origin of a gesture she'd inherited without knowing it.

"Then come and put your hand on the wood," her grandmother said, "and we will begin—"

The water shuddered.

Not the gentle tremor of the dead moving — something else, something wrong, a pressure rolling through the flood from the south like a fist closing. The twelve turned as one. Far above, distorted by fathoms of green water, lights appeared along the southern ridge of the valley. Torches. Many torches. And beneath them, faint but unmistakable even through the deep, the beat of a drum that Ines had heard only once before, at the well, and never forgotten.

The Wardens had found them. And riding at the head of that grey column, she knew without knowing how she knew, came a boy who had once carried water for the temple, coming to lock the door forever with the last key he had left — her.

"He can't reach the square before you're through," Tam said. She had crossed to Ines's side without Ines seeing her move, knife already drawn, which was somehow both alarming and comforting. "How long do you need?"

Ines looked at the door. At her grandmother. At the whole drowned, patient, waiting weight of Vesper.

"Less than he thinks," she said. "Hold the stairs."
