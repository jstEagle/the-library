## 19. Casimir's Last Stand

The Warden of Corvane had not ridden a horse in eleven years, and his body was filing a formal complaint.

Casimir Vael ignored it. He had ignored worse — the ache in his knees on the stairs of the Bastion, the way his hands had begun to tremble when he held a pen too long, the dreams. Especially the dreams. A man who had drowned a country in its own history could hardly complain about a little water in his sleep.

He rode at the head of forty grey coats through the last pass before the flooded valley, and the land around him was wrong in ways only he could name.

The reeds were growing where no seed had been sown. The water in the ditches ran clear instead of brackish. And the birds — the birds were back, after ten years of nothing but crows and gulls picking at the mudflats. Small brown birds, singing songs he had not heard since he was nine years old, when his mother had stood in a kitchen in Vesper and told him that the wrens always knew spring first.

*Stop,* he told himself. *That kitchen never existed. You signed the paper yourself.*

He had signed a great many papers. That was the thing no one understood about erasure. It was not fire and it was not slaughter, mostly. It was paperwork. Ledgers amended, maps redrawn, gravestones chiseled smooth and sold as scrap. He had been very good at it. He had been so good at it that somewhere along the way he had stopped being a man from Vesper and become simply a man, which was what he had wanted, and also the worst thing that had ever happened to him.

"Riders ahead," said Sergeant Ivo, riding up beside him. "On the ridge. Civilians, by the look. And — Warden, there's something odd about them."

"There is," Casimir said. Because there was. He could feel them the way you feel a draft under a door: the exiled dead of Vesper, walking alongside their living kin, invisible to everyone but him and one bone-carver who should have stayed in her little shop carving love-charms until she died comfortably of old age.

He did not hate the bone-carver. That surprised people who knew him, and would have surprised her more. Hate required believing a person mattered, and Ines Montrose was, in the end, a key. You did not hate a key. You hated the lock, and the door, and above all the thing behind the door, which had teeth.

"The prisoner said she hears the dead," Ivo offered. Conversationally, the way men do when they are nervous and hoping their superior will tell them not to be.

"She does." Casimir kept his eyes on the ridge. "Which means she can open what I closed. Which means everything I have done for eleven years is a house of cards, and she is walking toward it whistling."

"And if she opens it?"

Casimir was quiet for a moment. Below them, past the last line of hills, the floodwaters of the drowned capital caught the morning light and held it, vast and silver and patient. Somewhere under all that water lay streets he had walked as a boy. A schoolhouse. His mother's grave, which officially did not exist, because his mother officially had never existed, which meant that for eleven years he had been grieving a woman the world insisted he had invented.

"If she opens it," he said, "the country comes back. Every street. every grave. Every song." He let that sit, because Ivo was young and deserved to hear the beautiful version first. "And underneath it comes back too."

"Underneath, Warden?"

"You've read the sealed reports. All three of them."

Ivo had. Casimir watched the color leave the young man's face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"Then we ride faster," Ivo said.

"We ride faster," Casimir agreed, and thought, with a bitterness that tasted almost like humor: *Eleven years of careful work, undone by a carver of finger-bone charms and a girl with a grudge.* If the world ended today, at least it would end absurdly.

* * *

They came down to the shore of the drowned capital at dusk, and that was where the procession found them.

It was not an army. That was the horror of it. It was perhaps two hundred people — old men, women with children on their hips, a boy leading a goat — walking along the ridgeline in the last light, and around them walked the others, the ones without bodies, and even Casimir could not see them, but he could *hear* them now, this close to the door. A low murmur under the wind, like a congregation settling into pews. Like a city waking up.

His city.

"No," he said aloud, to no one, and spurred his horse down the slope.

The confrontation happened on the shingle beach at the water's edge, where the flood began — where Vesper had been made to stop. The exiles stood in a ragged line, and they did not run, which infuriated him more than any charge would have. They just stood there, holding their children and their bundles, looking at him with the expressions of people who had expected this and come anyway.

And at the front of them, sword drawn, standing between him and the water like the world's most stubborn fence post, was Tam.

"You," Casimir said.

"Me," said Tam. She looked terrible. Everyone did, this far into winter — hollow-eyed, wind-scoured, wrapped in mismatched wool. But her sword arm was steady, and her eyes had the particular flat shine he had learned to recognize in eleven years of hunting desperate people. "Warden Casimir. Or should I say—"

"Don't," he said quietly. "Don't say the name."

"Casimir of Vesper," she said anyway, loudly enough for the whole line to hear. "Son of Maren. Of Copperlane Street. Of the country you erased."

The murmur of the dead rose, and for one vertiginous moment Casimir felt two hundred ghosts turn to look at him, and every one of them knew his mother's bread, his father's laugh, the scar on his knee from the stairs of the schoolhouse. He gripped his saddle horn and made himself breathe.

"I'm going to say something," he told Tam, "and you're going to think it's a lie, because it sounds like a lie. Then I'm going to show you the proof, and you're going to wish it were a lie."

"Try me."

"Your friend is about to open the door. Not the door out. The door *down*. There are two things beneath this water, girl. One of them is my country." He dismounted, slowly, hands visible, an old man's trick that still worked. "The other one ate it."

Tam's blade did not waver. "You drowned them."

"I *sealed* them. The drowning—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, hated the crack, hated that grief still lived in him like a tenant who refused to be evicted. "The drowning was the seal. Water over the threshold, salt in the stones, the whole capital sunk and struck from record, because a door that no one remembers cannot be opened. I didn't kill Vesper. I buried it alive to keep something from getting out. Do you know what I found in the deep archives, Tam? Do you know what your brother's map leads to?"

Silence. Even the dead seemed to lean closer.

"Vesper didn't fall," Casimir said. "Vesper was sacrificed. Three hundred years ago, the last kings opened the original door — a way home, they called it, a road back to the old country, the first country. And something answered. Something that wears memories like clothing. It came up through the door wearing the faces of the beloved dead, and it took the capital in a single night, and the kings drowned their own city to close the wound. That's the sealed report. That's why I finished the job — because the seal was failing, because the forgetting was wearing thin, because people like your brother kept finding fragments of maps." He stepped forward. "The bone-carver isn't opening a way home. She's opening a mouth."

For a long moment Tam said nothing. And then, horribly, she laughed — a short, cracked, exhausted sound.

"You know what's funny?" she said. "Ines got the same speech from you already. In chains, in your tent. And she believed you for a day. Maybe two. And then she remembered that you're the man who burned the exile camps, and unmade the town of Harrow's Ford, and hunted her across half a country." Her voice hardened. "You keep telling the truth, Warden. That's the worst part. You tell the truth like a knife, and then you use it like one."

"The truth doesn't stop being true because I'm holding it."

"No," Tam agreed. "But here's mine." She pointed her sword at the water, at the pale shape rising far out in the flood — the spire of the old palace, breaking the surface like a drowned man's hand. "My brother died getting that map. My mother died waiting for a country that never came back. Two hundred people walked into winter to get here. Whatever's under that water — and maybe you're right, maybe it has teeth — those people decided it was worth the risk. That's not madness, Warden. That's a country deciding to exist again. And I don't care if it costs us everything, because you already took everything, and left us nothing *but* the risk."

Behind her, the crowd murmured agreement — the living ones. The dead ones said nothing at all, but the pressure of their attention was like weather.

Casimir looked past her, at the water, and felt the old fear rise in him, the fear from the archives, from the drawings of the thing the last kings had called *the Guest*. He had spent eleven years being hated so that no one else would ever have to see what he had seen in those pages. And now a girl with a sword was going to make him do it all again.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, and drew his own blade. "Hold the line. No one touches the civilians. The tall one is mine."

* * *

It should not have been a fair fight, and it wasn't — it was worse than unfair, it was *chaotic*, because the dead chose sides.

Tam fought like someone who had learned brawling in fish markets and fencing from a brother who'd had more enthusiasm than training, and Casimir fought like a man who had studied every treatise ever written and forgotten none of them, and between those two styles the beach became a kind of terrible dance. She drove him back toward the rocks; he turned her with a parry that nearly took her off her feet; she spat seawater and came on again. Around them the grey coats waded into the exile line and found, to their mounting terror, that the exiles did not scatter — and that sometimes a grey coat would stumble on perfectly flat ground, or freeze mid-swing, hands shaking, whispering *mother?* to empty air.

"That's cheating," Casimir grunted, blocking a cut aimed at his knee.

"All's fair," Tam panted, "in haunting and war."

"You're not funny."

"My brother said I was hilarious. He's dead. Dead men don't lie." She feinted left, and it was good, it was genuinely good, and it bought her exactly the half-second she needed — because in that half-second, out on the water, a light came on.

Far below the surface, down where the drowned streets began, a seam of gold split the dark. Thin. Growing. The sound that came with it was not loud — it was worse than loud. It was *familiar*. A bell. The evening bell of Vesper, ringing across the water the way it had rung every dusk of Casimir's childhood, calling everyone home.

Every fighter on the beach stopped. Grey coat and exile alike. Even the horses.

"Oh no," Casimir whispered. "No, no — she's at the door. She's *at the door*, and it's ringing the bell—"

Because that was how the Guest worked. That was what the archives said, in the archivist's shaking hand. It did not roar. It *invited*. It wore the voices of the dead you loved, and it called you down the way a hearth calls, and you went gladly, and it put you on like a coat.

Tam heard it too. He saw it hit her — saw her face crumple around the sound of a bell and a voice inside it, some voice she knew. For one heartbeat her sword point dropped.

Casimir did not take the opening. That was the thing about him that his enemies had never understood and his few friends had never survived explaining: he could not stop protecting people, even the ones ruining his life's work. Instead he grabbed her shoulder and shoved her toward the rocks, away from the waterline, away from the gold light.

"Listen to me," he said, fast, both of them soaked in bell-sound. "Whatever you hear down there — whatever voice — it is not your brother. It is not anyone. It is a lure with excellent taste in memories. The bone-carver has minutes, maybe less. I have to go down after her."

"You're going to *help* her?"

"I'm going to stop her," Casimir said, already moving, boots splashing into the shallows, "or die trying, or possibly both, in that order. Are you coming or not?"

Tam stared at him — at the Warden of Corvane, hip-deep in the resurrection of the country he had drowned, holding out one hand like a man offering a dance.

"You are the most infuriating man I have ever met," she said, and took his hand, and together — Warden and avenger, jailer and thief, two people protecting the same world in opposite directions — they waded into the golden water as the bell rang on, and beneath their feet, far below, a door that had waited three hundred years began, at last, to open.
