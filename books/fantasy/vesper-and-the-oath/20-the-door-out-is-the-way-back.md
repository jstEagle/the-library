## 20. The Door Out Is the Way Back

The water parted for her the way a crowd parts for a funeral — slowly, reluctantly, with a great deal of murmuring on either side.

Ines walked down through the drowned capital on a road that had no business existing. The floodwaters stood above her like green glass walls, fish drifting past at eye level, and yet the air in her lungs stayed sweet and dry. The dead were holding it back for her. She could feel them doing it, hundreds of them, thousands, their attention pressed against the water from every direction like hands cupped around a candle flame.

Behind her, muffled and distant as a shout through a wall, came the sounds of Tam holding off Casimir. Steel on steel. A voice she knew, cracking with effort: *"Go, you stubborn bone-carver, GO—"*

She went.

* * *

The road descended through streets she half-remembered from visions that weren't hers. A fountain square where children had chalked hopscotch grids. A bakery with its shutters still closed against a morning ten years gone. Everything was silted and soft and patient, the way things are when they have been waiting so long that waiting has become their whole personality.

"You're stalling," said her grandmother's voice, somewhere to her left.

"I'm observing," Ines said. "There's a difference. Stalling is what cowards do. Observing is what artists do."

"Mm." Her grandmother — Maren, though Ines hadn't known the name until three days ago, until the dead of Vesper had begun saying it to her like a greeting — fell into step beside her. She was more solid here than she had ever been above the water. Her hands looked like hands instead of suggestions. "You carve to avoid feeling things. I recognize the technique. It's hereditary."

"That's not fair."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "Most true things aren't."

The road ended at a door.

Ines had opened six thresholds by now, and each had been small — a seam in the air, a crack between moments, something you'd miss if you blinked. This was not that. This was a door the way a cathedral is a shed. It rose out of the flooded plaza ahead of her, taller than the buildings around it, older than the buildings around it, made of a dark wood that drank the light and gave back nothing. No hinges. No handle. Only a seam down the center, thin as a hair, glowing faintly with the same pale gold she'd seen in every vision of Vesper whole.

And carved across its face, in letters deep enough to hold shadow, words in the old tongue. She couldn't read them. But she could hear them, the way she heard everything the dead wanted her to know, and the hearing went through her like cold water:

*THE WAY OUT IS THE WAY BACK.*

"Oh," Ines said. "Oh, you absolute—"

She sat down on a drowned step. It was dry. Of course it was dry. Everything here was exactly as accommodating as the dead wished it to be, and the dead apparently wanted her comfortable for this conversation, which meant they knew what they were about to ask, which meant—

"Say it," she said. "Just say it plainly. I've spent ten years listening to people talk in circles because they think plainness is rude. I would love some rudeness right now."

Maren sat beside her. For a moment she only looked at the door, and her face did something complicated — grief and longing and guilt all trying to use the same muscles.

"The country is a memory," she said. "That's literal, Ines, not poetry. When Casimir and his fellows sealed the doors and unmade the records, Vesper didn't die. Dying would have been kinder. It became a memory with nowhere to live. Ten years of a whole nation — its songs, its recipes, its arguments about whose turn it was to fix the mill fence — all of it homeless, all of it held in the dead, who cannot carry it back alone. A memory needs a keeper. Something living. Something that remembers on purpose."

"And the door needs—"

"A living memory to anchor the return. Someone to stand at the threshold and hold the shape of the country while it pours itself back into the world. Otherwise it comes back wrong. Otherwise what comes through is hunger." She touched Ines's wrist, gently. "You saw what slipped through the fourth door when you forced it. That was one fragment. One. Imagine a country's worth."

"So someone has to be the anchor." Ines stared at the door. "Someone who knows Vesper. Who carries it. Whose blood answers the thresholds." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You know, when the dying man put that map in my hands, I thought I was being asked to deliver a package. Walk north, hand it over, done. Nobody mentioned the delivery was me."

"He didn't choose you," Maren said quietly. "I did. Before I died. I chose your grandmother's line, and I chose you, because you were the only one left who could hear us and had not been taught to hate what you heard."

Ines thought about that. About ten years of charms too small to be called magic, of staying small, of keeping her head down and her gift quiet and her heart quieter. All of it, it turned out, had been preparation for the largest thing anyone could ask of anyone.

"What does it cost?" she asked. "Exactly. No poetry."

Maren was silent for a long moment. Around them, the water trembled faintly, and Ines realized the dead were leaning in. All of them. Every soul in the drowned city, waiting to hear whether the bone-carver would say yes.

"A name," Maren said finally. "Yours. Not killed — given. Given freely to the country, to hold, so that it has one living name inside it while it rebuilds all the rest. You will forget yourself as you are now. Your craft. Your years in Corvane. The oath. Possibly Tam." Her voice caught slightly on the name. "You will remain. But you will remain the way a foundation remains under a house. Necessary. Unseen. Unremembered."

* * *

Ines got up and walked to the door and put her palm flat against the wood, because she wanted to know what she was agreeing to, and the door — obligingly, terribly — showed her.

It showed her herself, all of herself, laid out like her carving tools on a workbench.

The finger-bone charm from the night market, the first one, the dead woman who'd dictated the words and then thanked her. The smell of hot fat and cheap wine in Corvane's market alleys. The exact weight of a good carving knife. Her mother's face — no. That one was already gone, spent at the first threshold, and she felt its absence now the way you feel a missing tooth. Tam's face, furious and grieving on the causeway. Tam's face, softer, much later, over a fire, saying *you're the first person in ten years who hasn't lied to me.* The taste of real bread at the exiles' hidden village. The sound of the community screaming her name as the Wardens came. The map in her hands, oilskin-wrapped, heavier than paper should ever be.

Her collected selves. All of them. And the door asking, politely, in the manner of a very large animal that could crush you but would rather not: *all of these? May I?*

"Yes," Ines said.

Then she sat back down on the step, because her legs had opinions, and said, "But I want it noted that I'm doing this under protest. Formal protest. Someone should write it down."

"We'll carve it," said a new voice, wry and young, one of the drowned dead near her elbow. "We've got nothing but time and stone."

"Good. Put 'under protest' in large letters."

"Ines." Maren took her hands. Hers were warm. Ines hadn't expected that; she'd braced for grave-cold, and the warmth undid her more than the cold would have. "You can refuse. Do you understand? There is no shame in it. We have waited ten years. We could wait ten more, and hate ourselves a little less for asking."

"But the hunger comes through the cracks while we wait," Ines said. "Casimir told me that much truth, whatever else he lied about. And Tam is up there bleeding to buy me time, and the exiles marched all winter on feet that froze, and—" She stopped. Breathed. "My grandmother taught my mother a proverb, probably. What is it? The one about doors?"

Maren smiled, wet-eyed. "*A door is only a wall that agreed to something.*"

"That's terrible."

"It's better in Vesperan."

"It would have to be." Ines laughed, and then, because laughing made it easier, kept going until it turned into something else, and Maren held her while she did it, and several thousand ghosts pretended very hard to look elsewhere.

* * *

When she was done, she stood, and brushed off her coat, and faced the door.

"How does it work? Do I knock? There's no handle. Ten years of thresholds and none of them have come with instructions."

"You offer," Maren said. "Out loud. Name and craft and self. And then you open it — you, not it. It has been closed from both sides for ten years. Someone living has to agree to stand in the frame."

"Stand in the frame," Ines repeated. "While an entire country pours through me."

"Briefly."

"How briefly?"

"It's best not to count."

"Wonderful. Terrific. Really selling it." She rolled her shoulders. Cracked her neck. Did all the small pointless rituals of a woman preparing to do a large unpointless thing. Then she stopped, and looked back the way she had come, up through the water toward the surface, toward the sound of fighting that had, she noticed with a lurch, gone quiet.

If it had gone quiet, then either Tam had won or Tam had lost, and either way Ines owed her more than a door opened in secret. She owed her a goodbye the other woman would never remember receiving.

"Tam," she said to the water, to the dark, to the girl with the vengeful eyes and the brother's map and the mother's promise. "You were right to follow me. You were right to distrust me. You were wrong about one thing — I didn't kill him. I carried him. The whole way. Every mile. He got heavier and I got stronger and neither of us knew why until now." Her throat tightened. "Keep the map. It'll go blank, and you'll be furious, and you'll keep it anyway, because you're like that. And if someday a woman in a quiet workshop carves a charm that hums when you walk past — be kind to her. She's doing her best."

The water shivered. Somewhere far above, faintly, impossibly, she heard a voice answer — ragged, furious, alive: *"Don't you dare die down there, Montrose!"*

"Noted," Ines whispered. "Under protest."

She turned to the door.

"I am Ines Montrose," she said, and her voice rang strangely in the drowned plaza, as though the acoustics had been waiting all decade for exactly this sentence. "Bone-carver. Charm-seller. Hearer of the dead. Daughter of Lira, granddaughter of Sefa, of the line of Maren. I kept myself small for ten years so I would survive, and I survived so I could be here, and I am done being small."

The seam in the door brightened.

"I give my name to Vesper. My craft, my memories, my collected selves — take them, hold them, build with them. Be the house. I'll be the foundation. Under protest." She swallowed. "Open."

She put both hands against the seam and pulled, and the door — vast, patient, older than the erasure — agreed to something.

Light poured out. Not the pale gold of the visions but every color at once, the whole remembered spectrum of a country: harvest-orange and river-grey, the blue of festival banners, the brown of bread, the red of a mother's scarf. It came through her like a river through a bridge, and it took as it passed — gently, the way snow takes the shape of the ground it lands on. Her name went first. Then the market smells. Then the knife-weight, the craft, the ten careful years. She felt each one lift free without pain, without grief even, with only a strange lightness, like setting down a pack after a long march and discovering she could float.

She stood in the frame. She held the shape. Above her, the green glass walls of the flood began, all at once, to move.

The water did not rush away. It *rose* — upward and outward, peeling back from the streets like a blanket drawn off a sleeper, and beneath it the drowned capital drew its first breath in ten years. Bells that had been silent began, somewhere, to consider ringing. Grass decided, all at once, to grow through the cracks of the plaza. And the dead — the dead stopped being dead in a slow tide, flesh returning to bone returning to breath, thousands of people waking in their own doorways, blinking at a sun they'd forgotten the angle of.

Ines felt herself thinning with every soul that woke. That was the bargain. That was fine. That was —

That was when the last thing the door took surprised her: not a memory but a habit. The habit of hiding. Of staying small. It lifted out of her like a splinter drawn clean, and what remained in its place was enormous and simple and hers, and she understood, in the final moment before understanding itself dissolved, that she had not been erased at all.

She had been *spent*. The way a seed is spent. The way a door is spent, opening.

The light crested. The bells rang. Somewhere above, on the shore of a sea that was rapidly becoming a valley again, a woman named Tam stood holding a blank map and screaming her name into the wind.

And in a workshop that did not exist yet, in a country remembering itself street by street, a quiet space waited — patient as foundations — for a woman who carved charms, and kept an oath she could not recall making, and sometimes, in the evenings, heard a single voice that sounded almost like her own.

Under protest, the space seemed to say, in large letters.

She would get there. They all would.
