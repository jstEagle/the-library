## 21. Vesper, Remembered

The water went first.

It did not drain the way water drains — no gurgling retreat, no mud left gasping in the sun. It simply *left*, the way a crowd leaves a square when the bell rings: all at once, and then not there at all. One moment the drowned capital lay beneath forty feet of grey flood; the next, the towers stood dripping in the morning light like men rising from a long sleep, shaking off the years.

Tam stood on the shore of what had been, until yesterday, a lake, and watched a country come back into existence.

She was holding the map. She had been holding it since Ines walked down into the dark beneath the threshold and did not walk back up. The oilskin was damp, the parchment inside it sodden and soft as old bread, and while she watched, the last faint lines of ink lifted from its surface like smoke from a snuffed candle, rising, thinning, gone. The map was blank now. Whatever it had needed to say, it had said.

"Well," Tam said to no one. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word, which was embarrassing, so she said it again, harder. "Well."

Behind her, three hundred exiles stood in the shallows of a sea that wasn't there anymore, and wept, and laughed, and called out street names to each other like children naming constellations.

* * *

The land rose slowly, as if it were embarrassed about the whole business.

Where the flood had been, there were streets. Not ruins — streets. Cobbles swept clean by some diligence that had outlasted the drowning. Doorways with their lintels intact. A fountain in a small square, dry for ten years, that began — Tam saw it happen, saw it with her own eyes — to trickle, then pour, as though someone upstream had remembered to turn the tap.

The exiles did not rush in. That was the thing that undid her, later, when she tried to describe it: they did not rush. They walked. An old woman named Sera Vail, who had kept the language alive in a cellar for a decade and taught Tam more of it in one night than she'd learned in a lifetime of eavesdropping, took off her boots before she crossed into the city, because — she said — you don't track the mud of your exile into your mother's house.

Then she stepped onto the cobblestones, and the cobblestones *knew her*. Tam heard it — everyone heard it — a sound like a great soft breath moving through the stone, through the walls, through the bells in the tower that began, unbidden, to ring. Not an alarm. Something older. The sound a city makes when it says a name it has been holding in its mouth for ten years.

Sera Vail stood in the middle of the square with her bare feet on the stones and sobbed like a girl.

Tam had never much cared for crying. She'd spent ten years doing it privately, efficiently, in the manner of a woman wringing out a rag — get it done, get back to work. But standing at the edge of Vesper's returned capital, watching a city remember itself street by street, she found her own face wet and made no particular effort to fix it.

"You'd have hated this," she told the blank map. "All this feeling, just lying around where anyone could trip over it."

The map, being blank, said nothing. It had gotten very good at that.

* * *

They found Casimir at noon, sitting on the steps of the Warden's former waystation above the valley, his grey coat spread over his knees like a man who had taken it off because it no longer fit him — which, in every way that mattered, it didn't.

He did not run. Tam had half expected him to run, or to fight, or to produce from somewhere in that ruined fortress of a man one final terrible argument. Instead he sat with his hands open on his thighs, palms up, the posture of a man showing he holds nothing, and watched the city rise across the water with an expression Tam could not read and did not especially want to.

The exiles gathered around him in a wide, silent crescent. Three hundred people, and not one of them spoke. Sera Vail came forward last, barefoot still, her boots dangling from one hand.

"Vesperan," she said to him. It was not a question. It was not even an accusation. It was simply the truest thing anyone could say about him, and she said it the way you'd set a stone on a grave.

"I was," Casimir said. His voice was hoarse, scraped hollow. "I sealed the door. I erased my own country. I hunted a bone-carver across half the world to finish it." He looked up, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who has been carrying something enormous for a very long time and has only just discovered how heavy it was. "And I would do it again, if the thing beneath the door stirred. Ask me. I'll swear it."

"We believe you," Sera Vail said. "That's what makes it hard."

Someone in the crowd — Tam never learned who — shouted for execution. A few voices took it up, raw and recent, thinking of the raid, of the well, of names screamed in the dark. The shout moved through the crescent like wind through wheat, and for a moment Tam felt the whole gathering tilt toward violence, that awful easy gravity.

Then Sera Vail raised her hand, and the wheat stilled.

"No," she said. "Not here. Not on our first day home. We are not going to make our streets out of the same stone he used." She turned to Casimir. "You will leave. You will walk out of Vesper and keep walking, and you will live the rest of your life in a country that remembers you did this. Every border will know your name. Every record will carry it. You wanted us forgotten — so we will make sure *you* are remembered. That is your sentence."

Casimir closed his eyes. When he opened them again, something in them had broken loose — not relief, exactly. Something adjacent to relief. The look of a man who has been sentenced to carry his weight instead of being allowed to put it down.

"The door is closed?" he asked. "Truly closed?"

"It's not a door anymore," Tam said, before she could stop herself. Everyone turned to look at her. She shrugged, uncomfortable. "It's a hearth. There's a difference."

Casimir studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the smallest bow a man can make, picked up his grey coat, and walked west along the shore without looking back. The exiles parted to let him pass, and closed behind him like water, and that was the last any of them saw of Warden Casimir — a grey figure growing smaller against a horizon that was, at last, only a horizon.

"That's it?" the execution-shouter demanded. "He just *walks*?"

"He just walks," Sera Vail said. "Justice isn't always loud. Sometimes it's just accurate." And she put her boots back on, because the square was hers now, and a woman can wear boots in her own house.

* * *

Tam found the workshop on the third day, following a rumor.

The rumor was this: in the Street of Small Trades, in a shop with a carved sign over the door — a charm shaped like a knucklebone, worn smooth by weather that hadn't touched it in ten years — there worked a woman who made charms too small to be called magic. She didn't remember making them. She didn't remember much of anything, the neighbors said kindly, but she was pleasant, and her hands knew their work, and sometimes she said strange things in a language nobody taught her.

Tam stood outside the shop for the better part of an hour, working up the courage of a woman who had once ambushed a stranger on a causeway without breaking stride.

Inside, Ines Montrose was carving.

She sat at a bench by the window with a sliver of bone in her hands and a knife moving in strokes too fine to follow, and she was humming — humming, low and tuneless, a melody Tam recognized from the night in the hidden settlement, from the music that had risen out of a cellar full of people pretending, for one evening, that they existed. A Vesperan lullaby. Ines could not have known where she'd learned it. Her hands and her throat remembered anyway.

Tam pushed the door open. The little bell over it rang, bright and stupid, and Ines looked up.

Her face was the same face. That was the cruelty and the mercy of it. The same sharp, tired, watchful face, minus ten years of listening to the dead, minus the weight of seven thresholds, minus — Tam's chest tightened — minus everything they had built between the causeway and the water.

"Can I help you?" Ines said. Her accent was Corvane market-flat, but underneath it, faint as a watermark, something else.

"I'm looking for a charm," Tam said. Her voice came out rougher than intended. She cleared her throat. "A protection charm. For a traveler."

Ines smiled — a quick, wry, self-deprecating smile that Tam knew, that she had seen on riverbanks and rooftops and once, memorably, upside down out of a flooded culvert. "Fair warning: my charms are small. Too small to be called magic, people say." She held up the sliver of bone she'd been carving. "But they hold. That's the whole trick, really. They hold."

"What do they cost?"

"For a traveler?" Ines considered. "Nothing today. Call it a housewarming gift. This whole city feels like it just moved in." She laughed at her own joke, then paused, frowning slightly, as if the laugh had arrived from somewhere unexpected. "Strange. I keep making jokes like that. Like I'm celebrating something I can't name."

"You earned a celebration," Tam said quietly.

"Did I?" Ines tilted her head. For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a shape passing under ice. "You talk like we've met."

"We haven't," Tam said. It was almost entirely a lie. "My brother knew you. He spoke well of you."

Something flickered across Ines's face — grief with no memory attached to it, mourning arriving for a person she couldn't place. "Did he," she said softly. "That's kind of him. What was his name?"

And here Tam found the trap waiting for her, patient as a snare: because if she said the name, Ines would feel the loss of a man she'd sworn an oath to and couldn't recall, and what good would that do either of them? And if she didn't —

"He'd want you to keep carving," Tam said instead. "That's all he'd want. Trust me."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. Then she finished the charm — three turns of the knife, a word in a language she shouldn't have known spoken too softly to catch — and pressed it into Tam's palm, folding her fingers over it.

"There. Safe roads." She hesitated. "Come back sometime, if you like. I get the feeling I'm meant to know you. Don't ask me how I know. I just carve bones; the knowing part was never my department."

Tam laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the first in longer than she could calculate. "Maybe I will," she said. "Maybe I will."

Outside, the evening light lay gold along the Street of Small Trades, and somewhere across the city a bell was ringing the hour in a pattern no living person had heard in ten years, and every soul who heard it stopped what they were doing to listen, and then went on, gently, with their lives.

Tam walked down to the shore, where the map — blank, soft, finished — still sat in her satchel next to the new charm. She took both out and held them up side by side: the record of everything that had been given, and the small proof that something of it remained.

"You'd better be comfortable in there," she told the charm. "She gave you away for free. She never gave anything away for free. I'd know."

The charm, being a charm, said nothing. But it was warm in her hand, warmer than the evening justified, and it stayed warm the whole walk home — and if, some nights, when the city slept, a woman in a workshop on the Street of Small Trades paused over her carving and spoke a single word aloud in a language she had never been taught, checking it against nothing, correcting no one, keeping it safe the way you keep a coal alive under ash —

— well. The dead weren't the only ones who could hold a memory.

Some things, given away, refuse to leave.
