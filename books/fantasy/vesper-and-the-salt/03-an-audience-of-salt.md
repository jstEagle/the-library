## 3. An Audience of Salt

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the guild balanced its accounts against the tide tables, and the tide tables had been wrong for three weeks running.

Coral read the letter twice at her desk in the flooded archive, water lapping at the legs of her stool, lamplight shivering on the black surface below. The seal was royal—two crowns and an anchor—and the hand that had written *the Keeper of Accounts will present himself before His Majesty* belonged to a chancery clerk with a cramped, hurried style. But the paper was guild stock. Heavy rag, salted edges. Someone in the palace had asked the Ledgerkeepers what to write, or someone in the guild had written it themselves.

She thought of the page in no hand she knew. *A prayer, answered.* She had not slept well since.

"You'll go," Oren said that evening, in his office above the waterline, where the air smelled of tallow and old vellum instead of brine. He did not look up from his own ledger. "The treasury audit is ours by charter. It has been forty years since a keeper was called to court, so mind your manners and mind your figures."

"Forty years," Coral said. "Why now?"

Oren's pen paused—a hesitation of half a heartbeat, no more, but Coral had spent eleven years learning to read the guildmaster the way she read ledgers, by what he declined to record. "Because kings grow anxious as they age," he said. "Anxiety makes for sloppy bookkeeping. Go count his coins and come home."

It was a reasonable answer. That was what troubled her. Oren always had reasonable answers, the way a good forgery always had the right watermark.

* * *

Vesper's palace sat on the high ground above the harbor like a man who had climbed onto a roof to escape a flood and forgotten how to come down. Coral crossed the outer ward in the grey of morning, her guild sash—grey wool, unadorned, deliberately forgettable—damp from sea fog. Servants looked through her. Guards looked past her. This was the guild's oldest trick: be the thing no one remembers seeing. She had built her whole life inside that trick.

Then, on the steps of the great hall, a girl of perhaps nineteen stopped looking through her.

Princess Isolde wore mourning grey without being in mourning, which Coral would later understand was simply how she dressed: the color of someone perpetually attending a funeral no one else had noticed yet. Her hair was dark, pulled back severely, and her eyes were the pale grey-green of harbor water over sand—the kind of water that looks shallow until you step in it.

"You're the Ledgerkeeper." Not a question.

"I'm an apprentice, Your Highness."

"The last one, I'm told. The guild hasn't taken a student in a decade." Isolde descended two steps so they stood nearly level, which was either courtesy or strategy, and Coral suspected it was never courtesy with this woman. "My father's steward says the treasury books have been audited every year for forty years and every year they're found perfect. Perfect books are a kind of lie, aren't they? In my experience, only perfect things are hiding something."

"In my experience," Coral said carefully, "perfect things are usually just well kept."

Something flickered across the princess's face—not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe. One careful person marking another. "My father will want you at ten o'clock. Don't let him keep you past noon. He forgets—" She stopped. Chose again. "He tires easily these days."

There it was. The pause where a word should have been. Coral had spent three weeks staring at a page written in no hand she knew, and she had developed a sensitivity to missing words the way sailors develop a sensitivity to weather.

"I'll be brief, Highness."

"No," Isolde said, turning back up the stairs. "Be thorough. Be very thorough. And if you find something that shouldn't be there—" she glanced back, and her voice dropped to something almost private, almost pleading, "—come find me before you tell anyone else. Anyone."

* * *

King Aldric received her in a small room rather than a grand one, and Coral understood why within moments: grand rooms required processions, and processions required remembering who everyone was.

He sat by a window overlooking the harbor, wrapped in a fur robe though it was nearly summer, and when he rose to greet her he moved like a man wading upstream. He was not an old man. The records said fifty-eight. His face said eighty, and his eyes said worse than that—they said *nothing*, a beautiful polished nothing, the way a stone worn smooth by water says nothing about the mountain it used to be.

"The Ledgerkeeper," he said warmly. "Or is it Ledgerkeeperess? Forgive me. My mother would have known. My mother—" He frowned, reached for something, and Coral watched him reach: the small involuntary motion of a hand closing on empty air. "She liked numbers, I think. Or bread. She made—" The frown deepened, then smoothed away with terrible efficiency, like a tide erasing footprints. "You're here about the treasury."

"Yes, Majesty."

"Good. Good. Count everything twice. My father always said—" He stopped again. This time he laughed, softly, at himself. "I say that often. *My father always said.* I can't recall a single thing he actually said. Isn't that strange?"

It was not strange. It was the most horrifying sentence Coral had ever heard spoken cheerfully aloud.

She opened her case and began the audit, because the audit was the only solid ground available. The treasury ledgers were immaculate. Every entry matched every receipt; every receipt matched every coin; the coins themselves rang true when she tested them. Forty years of flawless accounting, exactly as Isolde had said. Exactly as suspicious as Isolde had said.

And threaded through it all, in the margins, in the interstices between entries, she found them.

Notations. Tiny ones, in a clerk's shorthand any guild member could read but no outsider ever could. *Disbursed: one summer, aged nine, the smell of rain on the schoolyard.* *Disbursed: the name of the king's first hound.* *Disbursed: his brother's face, in full.* *Disbursed: the taste of wine.*

No payment recorded. No counterparty. No consideration received—which violated the first law of the Ledger, the law carved into the guildhouse door: *nothing trades for nothing.*

"Majesty," she said, keeping her voice level, "these disbursements in the margins. Do you know what they refer to?"

Aldric looked at the page upside down, politely, as though he might read it. "Ah. Yes. The wishes." He said it the way a man says *the weather*, or *my knees*. "The crown grants them, you see. Has done for forty years. Wonderful service. Saved my life when I was a boy, near the end—there was a fever, and my father prayed, and something answered. Afterward it stayed. It stays still." He touched the crown resting on the table beside him—an old thing, salt-dark silver, worked into shapes that might have been waves or might have been fingers. "Though lately I find I've wished most of what there was to wish. One runs out, apparently." Another smile, warm and hollow as a shell. "Do you know, I used to have a daughter's laugh memorized? All of them. The real one and the polite one and the one she did when she was being wicked. I wrote them down once so I wouldn't lose them, and then I lost the paper, and then I lost—" He gestured vaguely at his own temple. "Well. You can see how the accounting goes."

Coral's pen had stopped moving. She made herself start it again.

Each wish granted cost him a memory. She understood that now, watching him search for his mother among bread and numbers and find neither. The crown fed, and he emptied, and the guild's ledgers recorded each withdrawal in tidy marginal shorthand as though a man's entire interior life were a line item—and perhaps to the Ledger it was, and perhaps that was the true crime, the one underneath all the others.

Forty years of withdrawals. Nearly empty. What remained?

"Majesty," she heard herself say, "may I ask what you wished for recently?"

"Oh, small things. To sleep well. To feel less afraid." He gazed out at the harbor, at the grey water over the drowned archive where Coral kept her ledgers, and for a moment his face cleared into something young and lost. "When Isolde comes in, remind me to ask after her studies. She thinks I don't notice her. I want her to know I notice. Write that down, if you write things down. *He notices.*"

The door opened behind her, and Coral did not need to turn to know who it was. She felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm.

Isolde entered, bowed precisely, and took her place by the window. "Father. You sent for me."

"Did I?" Aldric said pleasantly.

The silence lasted two seconds. Coral watched the princess absorb it—the small flinch, mastered instantly, the hands folding together, the voice arriving steady. "You did. Yesterday. About the harvest accounts."

"Of course, of course. Sit, sit. The Ledgerkeeper and I were just discussing the treasury. Everything is in order, she tells me. Everything is always in order." He beamed at them both, at his perfect books and his perfect kingdom, and Coral saw Isolde look at her father the way you look at a house you grew up in while it burns slowly, from the inside, with no flames anyone else can see.

* * *

The princess walked her out personally, through corridors going quiet with midday, and did not speak until they reached the colonnade above the outer ward.

"Well?" Isolde said. "Your thoroughness. What did it find?"

Coral weighed her answer. Eleven years of training said: report nothing, confirm nothing, be the thing no one remembers seeing. But the girl beside her had asked her father about harvest accounts he'd never mentioned, had absorbed an erasure in real time and stood straight through it, and Coral found that the training and the truth had begun pulling in different directions, like a current under a calm surface.

"Your father is nearly empty," she said quietly. "Whoever holds his debts has taken almost everything. There may be a year left. There may be less."

Isolde closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet and furious and utterly unsurprised, and Coral understood that the princess had known the shape of it for years—had only needed someone to say the arithmetic aloud.

"I knew it was taking him," Isolde said. "I didn't know it was *counting*." She turned, and gripped Coral's wrist, hard, harbor-water eyes gone bright. "The crown came from somewhere. Debts have holders. Find me the holder, Ledgerkeeper. Find me the account, and I will get you anything—witnesses, access, protection from your own guild if it comes to that. Because it will come to that. Won't it."

It was not a question either. And Coral, who had survived by being invisible, by confirming nothing, by keeping every dangerous truth locked in salt and shadow, heard herself answer:

"It already has. Your Highness—I've seen the handwriting of the thing that holds the debt. It's been writing in our ledgers. Prayers, answered. And I don't think it knows we can read them."

Above them, in the window of the small room, King Aldric lifted the salt-dark crown, considered it for a long moment, and set it gently back upon his head—as a man might scratch an itch, or feed a dog that had begun, patiently, to stare.
