## 4. The Guildmaster's Confession

I waited three days before I went to him.

Three days of carrying the copied pages against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my coat like a second heart. Three days of balancing the guild's accounts with hands that did not shake—because if they shook, someone would notice, and noticing was the one currency the Ledgerkeepers never traded in. Three days of watching Oren across the counting room, watching him dip his pen and touch his tongue to the nib the way he had since I was nine years old, when he found me stealing bread from the guildhouse kitchen and instead of turning me out taught me to write.

Forty years of silence, purchased and paid for. That was what the ledger said. And Oren Vail had signed it.

He worked late on the third night. He always worked late; sleep was a debt he'd traded away decades ago, back when such trades were still fashionable among the ambitious. When the last of the junior clerks filed out through the tide-door, I climbed the spiral stair to his study and closed the door behind me without asking leave.

He didn't look up. "You've been standing outside that door for a quarter hour, Coral. I could hear you deciding."

"You could hear me?"

"I could hear you *not* knocking." He set down his pen and finally raised his eyes—grey as harbor water, patient as stone. "So. Whatever it is, it's bad enough to need courage and small enough to fear me. Sit down."

I didn't sit. I took the pages from my coat lining and laid them on his desk, one by one, smoothing each flat with two fingers the way he'd taught me. The anomalous page first—the prayer answered in no hand we kept. Then the drowned sailor's entry, cross-referenced. Then the thread I'd followed for two nights without sleeping: a payment schedule, recurring quarterly, drawn from the royal treasury to an account numbered but never named, stretching back forty-one years.

"Tell me who holds account ninety-nine," I said.

Oren looked at the pages for a long time. Long enough that the lamp guttered and I thought he would simply deny everything, call it forgery, call me a thief, and where would I be then? An illegitimate archivist with no family and no name worth burning.

Instead he said, "Close the shutter."

* * *

The study window faced the harbor. Beyond the shutter's slats, the tide was coming in over the flooded archive below us, black water rising through the stacks where the impossible things were kept. Oren poured himself wine and did not offer me any. That, more than anything, told me how old this wound was.

"Account ninety-nine is mine," he said. "Or rather, it isn't mine at all. It belongs to the crown. I merely hold the key." He drank. "You found the prayer page."

"The handwriting matches nothing in our registry. Not living, not dead. Not human, maybe."

"Not human," he agreed. "Not anymore. Perhaps not ever, entirely. We called them the old gods once. Before the Ledgerkeepers, before contracts, they answered prayers freely. Which was precisely the problem. A gift given freely cannot be entered in a ledger, Coral. No debt, no record, no order. My predecessors built this guild to do one thing: make the miraculous accountable. Bind every wonder to a price so that wonder could be governed." His mouth twisted. "We were so proud of ourselves. We caged the sea and called it bookkeeping."

"And the crown."

"Ah." He set down the cup. "Yes. The crown."

He told it plainly, the way he'd taught me to record testimony—no adjectives, no mercy. Forty-one years ago, Queen Maren of Vesper came to the guildhouse at night, alone, veiled. Her son—the prince Aldric, eleven years old—was dying of the grey fever. The physicians had given him three days. She had heard, as queens hear things they are not supposed to hear, that there was a god under the harbor that still listened, starving, and that the guild held the only contract by which it could be lawfully summoned.

"She begged," Oren said. "I want you to understand that. Queens are not made to beg, and she did it beautifully, which is worse somehow. And I told her the truth: the god does not take gold. It takes memory. Every prayer answered, a life remembered, paid out of the petitioner. She said, 'Then take mine.' But the god refused her. It wanted the boy's own memories—it wanted to grow up inside him, to live a childhood it never had. It would take his memories as he made them, year by year, wish by wish, until there was nothing left. And in exchange, he would not die. He would live. Long past his natural span, whole and crowned, as long as the payments held."

"So she sold her son's mind to save his body."

"She sold his future to buy his present. And I wrote the contract." His voice didn't waver, but his hand, resting on the desk, had gone white at the knuckle. "I was thirty years old and I believed I was saving a child. The queen died six years later. Aldric ascended at seventeen, already wearing the thing, already forgetting. Do you know what his first royal act was? He wished for his mother's face to stop hurting him. He'd forgotten she was dead. The crown granted it—stripped the grief clean out—and he thanked it. Thanked it, Coral. He has been thanking it for twenty-four years while it eats him alive."

"The quarterly payments—"

"Silence. Mine and the guild's. The treasury pays, the ledgers close, and no auditor ever asks why the king remembers his coronation better than his wedding day." He met my eyes at last. "Every guildmaster since has known. It's the first thing passed along, like the keys to the deep archive. I swore the same oath my master swore before me."

"And now you're passing it to me."

"Now I'm passing it to you." He almost smiled. "You found it yourself, which pleases me. I'd have hated to think I trained a fool."

* * *

I stood at the window a long moment, looking down at the black water swallowing the archive shelves. Somewhere beneath it, in the salt cathedral I'd only ever seen in diagrams, something old and starving was chewing through a king one memory at a time. And somewhere behind me sat the man who had written the terms.

"You could have stopped it," I said. "Any year. You could have let him die when the payments ran thin. You could have gone to Isolde—"

"To the princess? And tell her what? That her father is a husk propped up by a parasite, that her grandfather's throne rests on a crime?" Oren rose, and for the first time since I'd known him he looked old—not aged, but worn through, like a ledger reread too many times. "Coral. The kingdom is at peace. Trade flows. The plague hasn't crossed the border in a generation because the crown wishes it away, and each wish costs Aldric a piece of himself he'll never miss because missing requires remembering. If we void that contract, we don't free a man. We unmake a king. What's left when the crown lets go? Half a person, drowning in forty years of returned grief. Or nothing at all. Is that your justice?"

"It's the truth."

"The truth is an entry, not a verdict." He came around the desk and stood close, and his voice dropped into the register he used for correcting my penmanship, gentle and absolute. "Here is what happens now. You will take those pages downstairs and enter them into the official record with amendments. The anomalous hand becomes a clerical error. The sailor's prayer becomes a misfiled petition. Account ninety-nine becomes a routine disbursement. You will initial the corrections as junior archivist, and I will countersign, and tomorrow morning none of this will have happened."

"And if I refuse?"

He said it without cruelty, which was the cruelest way to say it: "Then you will be the girl I found stealing bread, with no name, no guild, and no proof of anything except theft. I will grieve it. I have grieved many things in this room."

I looked at him—my teacher, my rescuer, the closest thing to a father the world had ever grudgingly allowed me—and I understood that he meant every word of it as kindness. That was the worst discovery of the night. Not the crime. The kindness.

"You taught me that a ledger is a promise," I said quietly. "That the whole guild exists because someone has to remember what everyone else agrees to forget."

"Yes."

"Then you taught me too well."

For a moment something moved behind his eyes—regret, or fear, or the ghost of the thirty-year-old who'd written that first contract believing he was saving a child. Then it closed over, smooth as harbor water.

"Think carefully," he said. "Loyalty is also a debt, Coral. And yours is owed."

I gathered the pages off his desk. All but one. I left the quarterly payment schedule where it lay, squared neatly against the blotter, and walked out without another word.

Behind me, I heard him pick up his pen.

And beneath my feet, far down under the flooding archive, in the dark where the salt cathedral waited, something turned over in its sleep—and began, very softly, to count.
