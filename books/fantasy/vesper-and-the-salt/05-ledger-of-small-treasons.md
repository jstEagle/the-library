## 5. Ledger of Small Treasons

Isolde had learned to read her father the way sailors read weather.

A cloud on the horizon meant nothing yet; a stillness in the rigging meant everything. So when King Aldric paused at the top of the great stair that morning and looked at the banner of Vesper hanging above the hall—looked at it for four full seconds, brow furrowed, as though he were trying to remember why a fish should matter to him—she knew the storm had moved closer.

"Father," she said, taking his arm. "The harvest petitions are at midday."

"Of course." He smiled at her, warm and correct, the smile of a man greeting a pleasant stranger. "And you are...?"

The world went very quiet.

"I'm Isolde," she said. "Your daughter."

"Yes." The word came fast, relieved, like a man catching a rope thrown to him. "Yes. Of course you are. Forgive me—the crown gives me headaches, and headaches give me... gaps." He touched his temple, where the circlet of state sat beneath the heavier thing he wore only in council. "You have your mother's eyes."

Her mother had been dead eleven years. He had never once, before this winter, needed the comparison to place her.

She walked him to his study herself, saw him seated, saw the cup of willow tea placed in his hand, and then she went to find the one person in the palace who might know what was eating him alive.

* * *

Coral Delacroix kept to the library's east wing the way a mouse keeps to walls—close, quick, gone if you looked directly at her. Isolde had watched her for three days now, since the audit began: the Ledgerkeeper with her ink-stained fingers and her ledger-satchel worn crosswise like a soldier's bandolier, moving through the treasury rooms with her head down and her eyes cataloguing everything.

Isolde had also watched her stop, twice, in front of the display case in the long gallery where the old regalia rested under glass. Both times, Coral had looked not at the crown itself but at the space behind it, as though reading something written on the air.

That was enough. Isolde had spent six months learning to follow threads no one else could see. Her father's memory had begun fraying a year ago—small things first, a minister's name, the words to an old blessing—and everyone at court had called it age, or grief, or the weight of rule. But Isolde had found the pattern underneath: every loss came within hours of a wish granted. A drought ended in the eastern provinces; her father forgot the name of the river he'd fished as a boy. A rebellion dissolved without a sword drawn; he forgot his own coronation oath. The ledger of his miracles and the ledger of his losses balanced perfectly, and Isolde had learned from her mother's people—coastal folk, salt-folk—that perfect balance is never an accident. Someone is keeping accounts.

So she waited until the fourth bell, when the library emptied of clerks, and she stood in the shadow of the astronomy stacks until Coral Delacroix came down the aisle with an armload of folios, and then Isolde stepped out and let the girl see her.

Coral didn't startle. That was interesting. She simply stopped, adjusted the folios against her hip, and inclined her head with exactly the right degree of deference. "Your Highness."

"You've been looking at the crown," Isolde said.

"I've been auditing the treasury. The regalia inventory falls under—"

"Don't." Isolde stepped closer, into the lamplight, so the girl could see her face and understand that she was done being managed. "I've watched you look at it the way I look at it. Like something's wrong with it. Like it's *hungry*." She let that sit. "My father forgot who I was this morning. Four seconds. Then he remembered, because I reminded him, the way you'd remind a man of a debt. So before you give me another sentence of guild-approved nothing, ask yourself how much time he has left, and whether you want to spend yours lying to me."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Lamplight moved on the spines of ten thousand books around them. When she finally spoke, her voice was quieter and more honest than anything she'd offered in three days of audits.

"How often?"

"What?"

"The forgetting. How often does it happen now?"

"Twice last week. Once today." Isolde crossed her arms. "It's accelerating. You knew that already, didn't you? You've seen the entries."

Something flickered across Coral's face—there and gone, quick as a page turning. "I've seen entries," she said carefully. "Anomalies. Transactions recorded in the royal account that don't match any instrument of trade my guild recognizes. Wishes aren't supposed to be *ledgered*, Your Highness. They're not supposed to be transactions at all."

"But they are."

"They are." Coral set the folios down on the reading table, and Isolde noticed her hands weren't quite steady. "Someone bound a contract to the crown. An old one. Older than the guild's current charter. And whatever sits on the other side of that contract has been collecting payment for—" she did arithmetic with her eyes, the way accountants do, "—forty years, at least. Maybe longer."

Forty years. Her father had worn the crown for thirty-eight. "Can it be undone?"

"That depends on the terms." Coral's jaw tightened. "And the terms depend on records I'm not certain I'm permitted to discuss."

"There it is," Isolde said. "*Permitted.*" She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what I've learned this year, Ledgerkeeper? Everyone in this palace is permitted to watch my father die politely. The physicians are permitted to prescribe rest. The ministers are permitted to call it age. The guild is permitted to audit its way around the truth. Everyone has permission for everything except the one thing that might save him." She leaned on the table, close enough to see the ink stain on Coral's knuckle, shaped like a small bird. "So here is what I'm offering. Not permission. Alliance."

* * *

They sat across from each other at the reading table like two merchants opening negotiations, which, Isolde thought, was probably the only language they'd ever share.

"Help me save my father," Isolde said. "Whatever the contract is, whoever holds the other end—I'll get you access to every record in the palace. Private correspondence, physician logs, my grandfather's papers. Things no auditor would ever be shown. In exchange, you tell me what you find, all of it, and when this ends—" she held the girl's gaze, "—I shield you. Whatever your guild did, whoever has to answer for it, you will not stand alone in front of them. My word on it. And my word, for what it's worth in this city, is still worth something."

Coral was silent for a long time. Isolde watched her think, watched her weigh things the way her guild must have trained her to—costs against costs, debts against debts—and thought: *she is deciding how much truth I can afford.*

"You should know what you're offering to shield me from," Coral said at last. "If the contract is what I think it is—if it was brokered through my guild—then someone in the guild committed a crime against the crown. Against your family. Exposing it would mean..." She stopped. Started again. "The guild is the only family I have. I was nobody when they took me in. Nobody. Whatever they've done, I—" Her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat with visible effort. "I need you to understand that I'm not choosing lightly."

"I'm not asking you to choose tonight."

"No," Coral agreed softly. "You're asking me to choose eventually. That's worse."

Isolde almost smiled. There it was—the honest thing, finally, dragged up out of the girl like a hook through deep water. "Then tell me what you can tell me. Tonight. And we'll see how far honesty takes us."

Coral opened one of the folios—not the guild ledgers, Isolde noticed, but a copy, in Coral's own hand, of entries transcribed from somewhere else. Dates. Amounts. And beside each amount, in a column Coral had clearly added herself, a list of things lost. *Name of childhood nurse. Memory of mother's voice. The taste of honeycake. Face of first love.* Forty years of small erasures, itemized in neat, careful script.

Isolde read three lines and had to put her hand flat on the table to keep it steady.

"He pays in memories," she said. "Every wish."

"Every wish," Coral said quietly. "And the wishes have gotten bigger. Look at the intervals. Early years, decades apart—small trades, survival trades. Then five years ago, the intervals collapse. Something changed. Either the crown grew hungry, or..."

"Or someone started wishing more," Isolde finished. "My father doesn't wish, Ledgerkeeper. He *prays*. He prays over every hard decision. He thinks it's his duty." She felt the horror settle into her bones, cold and specific. "He's been feeding it himself. Every crisis, every war scare, every famine—he goes to the crown and asks for help, thinking he's serving Vesper, and it eats him a little more each time."

Coral said nothing. But her silence had a shape, and Isolde had spent a lifetime reading silences in council chambers full of men who lied for a living. This silence was the kind that comes from someone holding back a specific thing—a name, a date, a door they don't want to open.

"You know who brokered it," Isolde said.

"I know there are records I haven't finished reading."

"You know *who*."

"I know," Coral said, very carefully, "that the original contract predates my apprenticeship by twenty years, and that the only person in the guild senior enough to have signed it is Guildmaster Oren." She closed the folio. "And I know that if I say that sentence out loud again, in daylight, to anyone, both of us are finished. Him for what he did. Me for saying it."

"Then we work in the dark a little longer." Isolde reached across the table and turned the folio back open, back to the column of losses. "But hear me, Coral Delacroix. I don't care what it costs your guild. I don't care what it costs the crown, or the throne, or me. My father gave thirty-eight years to this kingdom and it has been eating him bite by bite while everyone he trusted stood by and called it duty. If Oren sold him—and I think you believe he did—then Oren will answer for it, and anyone who helped him bury it will answer too." She softened, just slightly, because the girl across from her looked less like an auditor now and more like what she probably was: someone young and alone, standing on ice that was cracking under her whole life. "I'd rather it not cost *you*. That's the offer. Take it or don't."

Coral looked at her for a long moment. And Isolde understood, watching her, that the girl was about to lie to her—not about everything, but about something, some piece held back behind the teeth—and that knowing it didn't make the alliance worthless. It made it honest. Two women, each holding half a map, agreeing to walk together anyway.

"All right," Coral said. "We work together. I'll need three days to finish tracing the payment schedule. There's a clause structure in the original contract—old law, pre-charter—and if I'm right, there may be a way to void it without..." She hesitated. "Without paying its price."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we find another way." Coral gathered her folios, and for just a moment her hand lingered on the copied pages, protective, the way a hand lingers on a wound. "Your Highness—one condition of my own. If it comes to a choice between the truth and the guild, I need you to promise me you won't decide for me. Whatever I choose, I choose it myself."

"Agreed," Isolde said, and meant it, and wondered even as she said it how long either of them would keep their word.

Coral bowed—shallow, unpracticed, more like a closing of accounts than courtesy—and slipped away down the aisle between the shelves, mouse-quick, gone.

Isolde stayed at the table a while longer, alone with the lamp and the list of everything her father had already paid. Near the bottom of the page, dated eight days ago, one line made her blood go cold:

*Memory: daughter's name (partial). Recoverable.*

Recoverable. As though it were a debt that could be bought back. As though her father might one day wake and know her fully again—or as though the god behind the crown had marked her, specifically, as collateral it wasn't finished collecting.

She blew out the lamp and went to write to the one person outside the palace who might know what *recoverable* meant in the grammar of old gods.

Three days, Coral had said. Isolde intended to spend them well.
