## 6. The God Beneath the Crown

The contract had no signature.

That was the first thing Coral understood, sitting in the flooded archive at three in the morning with a lantern guttering and the tide pressing against the walls like something impatient. Every contract the Ledgerkeepers kept bore two marks: the seller's, taken in blood or salt or breath, and the broker's—the guild's seal, pressed in wax that never melted. Forty years of entries on the crown's account, and every one of them carried Oren's seal. But the original page, the founding entry, the one that bound a dying prince to a starving god—its signature line was blank.

Not unsigned. *Unsignable.* The ink refused to hold a name. She'd written her own finger across it once, just to test, and the parchment had drunk the touch of her skin and shown her nothing.

Coral knew what wrote itself without a hand. She'd found that first anomalous page weeks ago—a prayer answered in handwriting no scribe owned. She'd spent every night since following its thread through the deep ledgers, down through the guild's oldest vaults where the accounts grew strange and the water stood knee-deep and cold as confession.

Tonight the thread ended here, in a ledger so old its cover was pressed kelp-pulp, its pages stiff as dried skin. The final entry was not ink at all but an indentation, words pressed into the page from the other side—as if something beneath the harbor had written up through the world to reach her.

*COME AND COUNT.*

Ledgerkeepers did not receive summons. They issued them. Coral sat with the book open on her knees for a long time, listening to the harbor breathe above her, and understood that she had already decided. She'd decided the moment she'd seen Aldric's eyes go white mid-sentence in the throne room, the moment she'd watched a king pay for a wish with the memory of his own wedding day and smile about it, because he could not remember what he'd lost.

She took the ledger, her father's knife, and nothing else.

* * *

The way down was not on any map of Vesper's underworks, because maps were made by people who believed the harbor had a floor.

Coral followed the contract's terms instead. That was the trick no one taught apprentices: a bargain was a road, and if you read its clauses closely enough, it would carry you. *The debtor shall keep his seat upon the shore of sleep,* one line read, and the stair behind the archive's eastern wall went down farther than any stair should. *Payment shall be rendered in the currency of remembrance,* said another, and the air began to taste of salt and old summers, of things remembered rather than smelled.

The water rose around her ankles, then her knees. It did not feel like drowning. It felt like being *counted*—a pressure along her skin, patient and thorough, taking inventory of everything she carried. Her illegitimacy. Her forged induction papers. The name she'd been born with and buried. Her fear of Oren. Her worse fear of losing him.

"You can have none of it," she told the dark. "I'm not the account in question."

The pressure withdrew, almost politely.

The last stair opened into light.

* * *

She had expected a cathedral of salt and gotten one, but not the way she'd imagined. No crusted pillars, no mineral grotesques. This was salt the way grief is salt—not a substance but a condition. The whole vast space was lit from within by a pale, sourceless glow, like moonlight that had forgotten the moon, and everywhere the light touched, small crystals bloomed and dissolved and bloomed again, a city building and unbuilding itself forever.

In the center stood a dais, and on the dais stood a mirror.

It was the crown's reflection—that much she understood immediately, the way you understand a word you've never seen written. A tall oval glass in a frame of fused coral, and in the glass hung the crown of Vesper, turning slowly, alone, no head beneath it. And around the crown, coiled like smoke given patience, was a shape.

It had been a god once. You could see the architecture of divinity still in it—the suggestion of many arms, the ghost of a face too large for any human proportion, the way the light bent toward it hungrily even now. But it was thin. That was the horror of it, the thing Coral's body understood before her mind consented: the god was *thin*, stretched over its own bones like a sail in a windless season, translucent in places, frayed at the edges where worship had worn through and nothing remained to mend it.

"You are the keeper." Its voice came from everywhere, from the salt, from her own teeth. It was not a booming voice. It was a dry one, cracked, like pages turned too often. "I have waited four hundred years for a keeper to come down."

"I'm not—" Coral stopped. Corrected herself. "Coral Delacroix. Apprentice. Acting keeper, I suppose, since no one else will read what needs reading."

"Acting." The shape tasted the word. "Yes. Everything in your guild acts. Your master acted mercy, forty years past. The king acts kingship. Even I—" a sound that might have been laughter, if erosion could laugh, "—I act alive."

"Why did you summon me?"

"Because someone finally followed the terms." The crown turned in the mirror. "Do you know what I was, keeper? Before? I was prayed to by ten thousand voices. Fishermen's wives. Drowning men. Children lost on the ice. I answered *everything*. Do you understand what that costs? A prayer is a debt offered, and I honored every one, and they loved me for it, and their love fed me, and I grew fat with grace." The frayed edges shivered. "Then your kind came. Not your guild—your *species*. You learned to want things from each other instead of from the sky. And the prayers thinned. And I thinned. And I lay down here to starve with some dignity, as gods do."

"And then a boy came," Coral said quietly. "A dying prince."

"A boy came." The voice went soft, and that softness was worse than anything loud could have been. "He was drowning. Not in water—in blood, in fever, in the particular death that takes princes young. And he prayed. Not to me. He didn't know me. He prayed to anyone. And I was *right there*, keeper, starving, and here was a voice, a real voice, asking. Do you know what it is to be hungry for four hundred years and hear someone ask?"

Coral did not answer. Her throat had closed.

"I gave him life. It was nearly nothing left of me—all my reserves, my hoarded centuries—and I thought, this is the end, and it is a good end, spent on a child. But your guildmaster found us. He was young then, younger than you. He saw a god dying and a prince living and he did what your kind does. He *brokered*." The crown spun faster. "He wrote the contract. The boy lives; the god feeds. Not on worship—I'd have died before accepting scraps of that, charity dressed as devotion—but on memory. On the king's own remembering. Every wish the crown grants, Aldric pays in the only coin he has left that I can digest."

"The memories of his people," Coral said. "His daughter. His dead wife. Himself."

"He offers them," the god said. "That is the clause your master crafted most carefully, and the cruellest, and the kindest. I cannot take. He must give. So each morning the king wakes and chooses what to spend, and he always chooses, and he always chooses wrong, because a man emptying himself cannot see which memories are load-bearing." A pause. "He gave me Isolde's birth last winter. He wept while he did it and did not know why. I held it in my mouth all night like a stone and could not swallow."

Something in Coral's chest cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there.

"You're starving," she said. "Even now. Even fed."

"Memory is thin bread." The shape drew inward. "And he is nearly empty. When he is empty, the contract completes, and I take the kingdom's dreams as principal—or I die. Those are the terms. Your master wrote them to be airtight. He was very good at his work."

Coral looked at the thing in the mirror—at the god wearing a crown's reflection like a beggar wearing a stolen coat—and felt the terrible, unfashionable truth settle over her: *this monster is also a victim.* Oren had saved a boy by chaining a dying creature to him, and both of them had been paying ever since, and the guild had collected its silence-fees for forty years and called it stewardship.

"What do you want from me?" she asked. "You didn't bring me down here for sympathy."

"No." The light dimmed, gathered, focused. In the mirror, behind the crown, a second image surfaced: the throne room, the court, Isolde standing at her father's shoulder. "I want to renegotiate. Your law permits it. A contract may be amended by mutual consent before completion, witnessed by a keeper of standing."

"I'm not a keeper of standing. I'm not anything. My papers are forged, my name is borrowed—"

"*I know what you are.*" For the first time the voice had weight, and the salt cathedral rang with it, and Coral's knees nearly buckled. Then it gentled again, terribly. "I have counted you, Coral Delacroix. I count everyone who enters my water. You are a girl who built herself out of ledger paper and called it a self. You know better than anyone alive that a name is only an entry, and entries can be rewritten." The crown stilled. "Help me amend the terms. Find a payment I can live on that isn't a man's mind. Do this, and when the reckoning comes—and it is coming, keeper, your guildmaster's debts are forty years deep—I will speak for you. I will tell the court what Oren did. I will make you real."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I complete the contract as written, and the king empties, and your princess watches her father forget her face, and I will be sorry, and it will happen anyway." The god's frayed edges drifted like torn silk. "I am not cruel, keeper. Hunger is not cruelty. But it is not negotiable either. Only terms are negotiable. Bring me new ones."

Coral stood in the blooming, dissolving salt and understood bargains at last—not as the guild taught them, instruments of order, but as what they truly were: two hungers meeting, each pretending to be justice. Oren's hunger for absolution. The king's hunger for life. The god's hunger to exist. Her own hunger, oldest of all, to finally *belong* somewhere enough that someone would speak her name without checking the books.

Every one of those hungers wanted her to say yes right now.

So she said the harder thing.

"I'll find your terms," she said. "But I don't trade in futures. I don't sell testimony before I've verified it. If I come back with terms, you'll get them because they're *right*, not because you bought me. And if the right terms end with you dying clean instead of feeding dirty—" she met the reflection's light with her own eyes, "—then you'll accept that too. That's my price. Witnessed now."

The silence lasted long enough for a hundred crystal cities to bloom and fall.

"Witnessed," the god said at last, and there was something in its voice she hadn't heard from it before—something almost like relief. Almost like hope. "You drive a hard bargain, keeper. Your master would be proud."

"That," said Coral, turning toward the stairs, "is the cruelest thing anyone has said to me all year."

She climbed. Behind her the light held steady, and above her, faint through fathoms of harbor water, she could hear the palace bells ringing the hour—and beneath them, thinner, another bell entirely, one she'd only ever read about in the guild's oldest ledgers.

The alarm of the Ledgerkeepers, rung only when records were seized.

Someone had found her copies.
