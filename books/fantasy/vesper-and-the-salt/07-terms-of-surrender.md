## 7. Terms of Surrender

Isolde had learned to measure her father's memory the way sailors measured weather: by small signs, by what failed to arrive.

He still knew her name. That was the first thing she checked each morning, before the stewards brought the watered wine, before the chamberlain read out the day's petitions. She would stand in the doorway of his study and wait for him to look up and say it—*Isolde*—and for forty years of her life that word had been as reliable as sunrise. Lately she counted the pause before he said it. Three heartbeats. Five. This morning, seven.

"Isolde," he said at last, smiling, and she smiled back because a king's daughter learns early which smiles are load-bearing. "Come in. Sit. I was just—" He looked down at his desk, at nothing, at the space where something used to be. "I was just thinking about your mother."

Her mother had been dead eleven years. Her mother had hated this study, avoided it, done her correspondence in the solar because the light was better there. Isolde knew this the way she knew her own hands. Her father apparently no longer did.

"She preferred the solar," Isolde said gently.

"Did she?" He laughed, warm and untroubled, and the warmth was the worst part. He wasn't grieving a lost memory; he didn't know one was gone. The crown sat on his head even indoors now—he'd stopped taking it off weeks ago—and it caught the morning light like a ring of frozen seawater, and beneath it his eyes were clear and blue and increasingly vacant, a house with the furniture being carried out room by room while the owner waved cheerfully from the window.

"Papa." She crossed to him and took his hands. They were cold. They were always cold now. "Do you remember last week? The harvest petition from the eastern valleys?"

"The harvest," he repeated, pleased. "Yes. Good rains this year."

There had been no rain. There had been drought, and grubs, and three villages on the edge of starvation, and she had spent six days negotiating grain shipments because her father—who could once recite the yield of every valley in Vesper from memory—had wished the problem away. *I wish the people fed*, he'd said, touching the crown, and the crown had solved it somehow, somewhere, in some ledger Isolde couldn't see, and the price had come out of him in silence.

"Papa, who is Coral Delacroix?"

The pause stretched. Ten heartbeats. Twelve.

"A Ledgerkeeper," he said slowly. "They keep... accounts. Of things." His brow furrowed, a fisherman squinting at a horizon. "Why do I know that name?"

"You met her. A month ago. She audited the treasury."

"Did she find anything?" He said it lightly, but his grip tightened on hers, and Isolde saw it—the flicker underneath, the man still in there, drowning quietly and reaching up. "Isolde, why do I feel as though I've forgotten something important? Why do I always feel that way lately?"

Because you are being eaten, she thought. Because you traded pieces of yourself to a dead god wearing a crown, one wish at a time, and nobody told you the coin was your own soul.

"I don't know," she lied, and kissed his cold hands, and hated herself for both.

She stayed an hour. She talked to him about her childhood, feeding him memories like a nurse spooning broth into a patient, watching each one land or not land. The pony named Turnip. The time she'd fallen in the fountain at midsummer and come up with a water-lily on her head and refused, at age five, to remove it for two days. He laughed at that one. He remembered the fountain. He did not remember the lily, or the girl wearing it, or that the girl had been her.

By the time she rose to leave, he was calling her *my dear*. Not cruelly. Not coldly. Just the way a man speaks to a pleasant stranger in a garden.

At the door she turned back. "Papa. Do you know my name?"

He looked at her with those clear blue eyes, and behind them something vast and patient and hungry looked back, and for one terrible instant she was certain the crown itself was regarding her through him, weighing her, deciding whether she was worth the cost of remembering.

"Of course," King Aldric said. "You're the princess."

* * *

Coral came to her at dusk, soaked to the knees, gray-faced, carrying nothing.

That was the detail Isolde fixated on later, standing in the antechamber while the guards fetched dry cloaks: Coral carried nothing. A woman whose whole life was paper, whose fingers were always ink-stained, whose satchel had been practically sewn to her shoulder since the day Isolde met her—arriving empty-handed, arms wrapped around herself like someone holding a wound closed.

"They took everything," Coral said, before Isolde could speak. "Oren found the copies."

They were alone in the antechamber off the west gallery, the one place Isolde had determined had neither listening servants nor convenient keyholes. Rain worked at the tall windows. Coral stood dripping on the stone as if she'd forgotten how to be indoors.

"How much did he find?"

"All of it. The contract terms. The payment schedule. Forty years of withdrawals from my own guild's vaults, itemized in Oren's hand—I copied his handwriting so well I forgot it was evidence against him and not just practice." A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh if laughter ever went to funerals. "He wasn't even angry. That's what I can't—Isolde, he wasn't *angry*. He sat me down like a child who'd broken a plate and explained that I'd misunderstood my role. That the ledgers are the guild, and the guild is the ledgers, and a keeper who keeps false copies isn't a keeper at all."

"He expelled you."

"He struck my name from the rolls. Do you know what that means, for us? It isn't dismissal. It's erasure. Every record I ever entered, every audit I performed, every debt I balanced—it all stays, but the *authorship* goes. I become unwritten. Thirty years from now, scholars will read my work and there will be no me in it anywhere." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "And then he burned my copies in front of me. All of them. He made me watch."

Isolde stepped forward and took Coral's icy hands. "The originals. In the deep archive—"

"Sealed. Warded. And Oren has already sent word to the palace treasurer that the royal accounts show *no irregularity whatsoever*. By tomorrow the official position will be that everything is in order." Coral's eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady, and Isolde recognized the expression, because she wore it herself most mornings: grief and fury fused into something harder than either alone. "He thinks burning the copies ends it. He thinks without paper, there's no case."

"Is he right?"

For a long moment Coral didn't answer. Rain filled the silence. Then she said, quietly, "No. Because paper was never where the truth lived. It lives in the drowned sailor who walked out of the harbor demanding his debt. It lives in the widow in Saltrow whose husband's death was traded against a bridge contract. It lives in—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It lives in your father. In what's left of him. Oren can burn every page in Vesper and the king will still be missing forty years of himself, and everyone at court can see it, they just don't have a word for it yet."

"Then we give them the word," Isolde said. "We give them all of it. Publicly. Before the court."

"With what proof? With what standing? I'm nobody now, Isolde. Less than nobody. I'm a name struck from a roll." But her hands had tightened around Isolde's, and Isolde held on.

"You're the last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers," Isolde said. "Which means you're the only person alive who knows their law better than they do. Find me the clause. Find me the thread that unravels the whole garment. You told me once that every contract has one—a flaw, an escape, a door the drafters left themselves. Find ours."

Coral stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes—not hope exactly, hope was too gentle a word—but its fierce, desperate cousin. "There is one clause," she said slowly. "The oldest one. Older than the guild. *A ledger may be burned by its keeper.* Total voidance. Every debt, every contract, every entry—annulled. It's never been invoked. It would destroy the guild as surely as exposing it would."

"Then it destroys the guild," Isolde said. "My father is worth more than your guild."

"And the god?" Coral's voice dropped. "If the crown's contract is voided, the god starves. It's been starving for centuries anyway—we ended its worship, we built our kingdom on the bones of its altars, and then Oren gave it one man to feed on instead of a nation of faithful. If we burn the ledgers, we don't free your father and punish a monster. We kill a starving creature that's only guilty of accepting the meal we served it." She shook her head. "I've met it, Isolde. Under the harbor. It offered me a trade, and the terrible thing—the truly terrible thing—is that it was *fair*. Fairer than my own guildmaster has ever been."

Isolde let that sit. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere below them, beneath the harbor stones, she imagined the old god listening—as it seemed to listen to everything now, through salt and crown and tide.

"Then we offer it something better than a meal," she said finally. "Amnesty. Witness. A place in the open, where it doesn't have to eat a king in the dark. If it refuses—if it insists on consuming him—then we burn everything and let it starve in the light, where all of Vesper can watch what it chose." She lifted Coral's hands. "Can you build that case? Witnesses, terms, the reading of accounts—all of it?"

"I have nothing," Coral whispered. "No seal, no roll, no name."

"You have me," Isolde said. "And tomorrow morning, you'll have a princess's warrant and a room in the east tower. Build your case, Keeper Delacroix. Whatever you were before tonight—you're that again, starting now. My keeper. Vesper's keeper."

Coral looked down at their joined hands, at the rain-streaked windows, at the whole ruined architecture of her life lying in ash behind her. When she raised her head, her eyes were dry at last.

"Then we start with the drowned sailor," she said. "He's owed a message. And I intend to deliver it—in front of the entire court."

Far below, under the harbor, something ancient turned over in its sleep, and began, very quietly, to listen.
