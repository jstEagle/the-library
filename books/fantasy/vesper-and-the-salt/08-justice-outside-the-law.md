## 8. Justice Outside the Law

The safehouse was a counting house once, before the harbor silted up and the merchants moved inland. Coral chose it for that reason. It had shelves.

She stood in the empty hall at dawn, her single satchel of salvaged pages on the floor beside her, and counted what she had left. One cloak. One pen. One name she was no longer permitted to use—*Apprentice Delacroix*, struck from the rolls at midnight while Oren watched with his mild, terrible patience—and one truth that could not be struck from anywhere, because it had never been written down.

They had taken her ledgers. They had not taken her memory of them.

"You're smiling," Isolde said from the doorway. The princess wore a merchant's coat two sizes too large and looked, for the first time since Coral had met her, like someone unburdened by being recognized. "Should I be worried?"

"I'm inventorying." Coral lifted the satchel. "Forty-one pages. Copies of copies. Oren thinks he seized my work. He seized my *drafts*."

"And the originals?"

"Are in my head." She tapped her temple. "Eleven years of training. Do you know what they actually teach a Ledgerkeeper? Not arithmetic. *Retention*. A ledger can burn. A ledger can be forged. The only record that can't be tampered with is the one behind your eyes." She paused. "I just never expected to use it against them."

Isolde crossed the hall, her boots ringing on the old tile, and set down a canvas sack that clinked. "Then let's begin. My father has perhaps three weeks. Maybe four. The god is hungry now that it knows we're looking."

"Then we don't look quietly." Coral unrolled the first page on a counting table scarred by a century of honest commerce. "We do what the guild has spent two hundred years making sure no one ever does."

"Which is?"

"We open the books."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and by noon the counting tables were buried in paper.

Coral had spent eleven years balancing impossible accounts: memories traded for favors, years of life sold to buy harvests, grief pawned and never redeemed. She knew the shape of the guild's crimes the way a tide-pooler knows the shape of the shore. But knowing was not proving, and proof required witnesses—and the guild's witnesses were, by design, people no court would believe.

Drowned men. The mad. The very old. The traded-away.

"They were careful," Isolde said, reading over her shoulder. "Every contract, every payment, routed through intermediaries. Deniable."

"The contracts are deniable. The *people* aren't." Coral pulled a page free—a shipping manifest from sixty years ago, annotated in Oren's younger hand. "Look. The *Kestrel's Pride*, lost with all hands off the Teeth. Except." She tapped the margin. "'One recovered. Debt restructured.' They pulled a sailor out of the water, Isolde. And then they kept him on the books."

"Kept him how?"

"As collateral. His death was traded—he gave it up as surety for something, I don't know what yet—and so he couldn't die until the debt cleared. Sixty years walking around Vesper with a death in escrow." Coral looked up. "I've seen him. Two nights ago. He walked into the guildhouse out of the rain, dry as kindling, and asked for his account."

Isolde went still. "You know where he is?"

"I know where he *goes*. Men like him haunt the places the city forgot. And this city has a great many forgotten places." She began rolling the pages into a leather tube. "Get your coat, Your Highness. We're going fishing."

* * *

The Ramshorn was a tavern built into the ribs of a beached whaling ship, down where the cobblestones gave way to mud and the lamplighters had long ago stopped coming. Coral had been there exactly once, on guild business, collecting a debt from a woman who had sold her shadow. She remembered the smell—tar, salt, spilled beer—and she remembered the rule: *don't stare.*

Tonight everyone stared anyway, because Isolde walked in wearing her borrowed coat like an apology and the whole room smelled royal blood the way sharks smell it.

Coral found the sailor at the end of the bar, exactly where she'd known he would be: a man shaped like driftwood, gray as ash, with eyes the color of deep water. Brann. She'd read his name in the manifest. Sixty years dead on the books and alive on the stools.

He saw her coming and did not flinch. "Ledgerkeeper," he said. It was not a greeting.

"Former," Coral said, and sat beside him. "Struck off last night. Which means, Brann, son of the *Kestrel's Pride*, that for the first time in sixty years, no one from the guild can touch your account."

That got her his full attention. Around them the tavern noise continued, but their corner went quiet, the way rooms do when something important is happening at low volume.

"They told me my debt was eternal," he said. His voice was rust and rope. "They told me the sea gave me back because the paperwork wasn't finished."

"The sea gave you back because you *bought your way back*. You traded your death. For a message." Coral kept her voice gentle. "A prayer, Brann. You carried a prayer for someone, and the price was your dying. I want to know whose prayer. And I want you to tell it to the whole kingdom."

The old sailor looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, a sound like a hull taking water.

"Sixty years," he said, "I've waited for someone to ask me the right question." He drained his cup. "It was my daughter's prayer. She prayed to the god under the harbor—the old one, the one before the crown—to bring her father home from the wreck. And something answered. Something said, *a death for a crossing.* So I rowed. Sixty years I've been rowing, girl, and every year the god gets thinner and the crown gets fatter, and nobody asks why the king forgets things." He turned to face her fully. "You want a witness? I'll give you testimony that'll crack the throne room floor. But hear me: I'm not the only one. There's a woman in Saltrow who sold her shadow to pay a tithe. A midwife who bought back a child's fever with ten years of her own life. A whole street of people in the Low Quarter who traded their *names*—that's why nobody remembers the fire. The guild has been harvesting this city for a century and calling it bookkeeping."

Coral felt something ignite in her chest that she hadn't felt since Oren had torn her badge away: not fear. Purpose.

"How many?" she asked.

Brann smiled with too many teeth. "How many nights have you got?"

* * *

They came, over the following days, the way the tide comes—not all at once, but inevitably.

The woman without a shadow, who stood always in lamplight so hers couldn't be missed. The midwife with young eyes and ancient hands. Eleven nameless ones from the Low Quarter, who arrived together and introduced themselves only as *we*. A customs clerk who had witnessed Oren's signature on documents predating his official appointment by twenty years. A priest, defrocked, who had records of the old god's last congregation and the exact date worship ended—the date the hunger began.

Isolde handled the ones who needed dignity; she had a talent for kneeling. Coral handled the ones who needed accuracy; she had a talent for listening. Between them, page by page, testimony by testimony, they assembled something the guild had no protocol for: a public account. Not a ledger—ledgers could be seized, forged, burned. An *audience*. Witnesses who would stand in the light and speak.

"It won't hold in any court," Isolde said on the fifth night, reviewing the growing stack. "My father's judges answer to the crown. The crown answers to the crown. We'd be asking the thief to try himself."

"That's why there's no court." Coral spread the master sheet across three tables pushed together—the full architecture of the case, drawn in her precise hand. "Here's what I learned from Oren, the one useful thing: a contract is only as strong as its secrecy. Every clause, every payment, every quiet consumption of a king's mind—it all depends on no one ever reading the terms aloud." She looked up. "So we read them aloud. In the throne room. Before the assembled court, before the god itself. We force it into the open. A secret bargain can devour a king in the dark—but a *public* one has to defend itself. Even gods must argue their case when the whole kingdom is listening."

"And if it refuses?"

"Then it breaks its own contract. Contracts bind both ways, Isolde. That's the entire religion of my guild, and for once I'm grateful for it." Coral rolled the master sheet closed. "We announce a public reading of accounts. We summon the guild, the crown, and the creditor. And we make them all sit in the same room and hear what forty years of silence cost."

Isolde was quiet a moment. "My father will be there. What's left of him."

"Yes."

"He might not know me, by then."

Coral reached across the table and took the princess's hand—the first time she had dared. "Then he'll hear your name spoken by a hundred voices at once. And maybe some part of him that the crown hasn't eaten will remember it."

Isolde's fingers tightened around hers. Outside, the harbor fog pressed against the windows, and somewhere beneath it, in the salt cathedral under the water, something old turned its attention toward them—and Coral felt it the way you feel weather changing.

It knew what they were building. Let it come and argue.

That night, a boy arrived at the counting house door, breathless, soaked to the knees, carrying a single sheet of heavy cream-colored paper sealed with the crest of the guild. Coral broke it open and read it twice.

Oren's hand. Of course. Even his threats were elegant.

*"You have until the new moon,"* it read. *"After that, the account closes—and I am afraid, child, that closing accounts is the one thing I have always done well."*

Coral showed it to Isolde. The princess read it and raised her chin.

"New moon," she said. "Nine days."

"Then we'd better finish the list," Coral said, and reached for her pen.
