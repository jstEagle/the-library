## 9. The Reading of Accounts

The throne room had never held this many people. Coral knew because she had audited it—every seat, every gallery, every inch of marble catalogued in a ledger she'd written herself at nineteen, when the guild still trusted her with its inventories. Four hundred souls, give or take. Today every one of them was accounted for, and not one of them knew what they were about to hear.

She stood at the base of the dais with a ledger bound in grey salt-calf leather and her hands, remarkably, steady.

King Aldric sat on the throne beneath the crown that was eating him. He looked better than he had in weeks—the god had dressed him for the occasion, filling the hollows of his face with borrowed light—but Coral could see the seams now. The way his eyes took an extra beat to find Isolde in the crowd. The way his left hand worried at the armrest like a man trying to remember why he'd come into a room.

Isolde stood at the foot of the dais, in armor instead of silk, having informed the Master of Ceremonies that she would attend her father's audit armed or not at all. Behind her, arranged along the wall where petitioners usually knelt, stood the witnesses. The drowned sailor, Brannoc, saltwater still weeping from the hems of his coat. A woman who had traded her sister's laughter to cure a fever. An old man who had sold forty years of his own future and arrived at the end of them early. They had no lawyer. They had Coral.

Guildmaster Oren sat among the court officials in his formal robes, silver chains of office stacked on his chest like anchors. He had not looked at her once since she entered. That, more than anything, told her he understood exactly what was coming.

A herald struck the floor three times. "The court will hear the reading of accounts."

Coral opened the ledger.

"My name," she said, and her voice carried further than she expected, as if the room itself were leaning in, "is Coral Delacroix. I am—or I was—the last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers' Guild. For eleven years I have kept accounts of trades in things that cannot be weighed: memories, years, names, deaths. This morning I am reading from the true ledger. Not the copy the guild keeps for the crown's inspectors. The original."

Oren rose halfway out of his seat. "This is a forgery—"

"Then you'll have no trouble identifying which entries are false," Coral said. "You wrote most of them."

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere above, gulls cried against the harbor glass.

She began at the beginning, because that was where ledgers began, and because the beginning was the crime.

"Entry one. Dated the ninth of Frostmonth, forty-one years ago. *Received: one contract, brokered by Guildmaster Oren Vael, on behalf of the entity known as the Crown-Beneath-the-Harbor, hereafter the Debtor. Consideration: the life of Prince Aldric Vesper, aged nine, drowning of lungfever, given six hours to live. Terms: the Debtor shall preserve the prince's life entire. Payment: one memory per wish granted by the crown, in perpetuity, until the account is exhausted.*"

She looked up. "In perpetuity. Those words are Oren's. He signed them. He traded a dying boy's whole future—not just his childhood, not just his griefs, but every memory he would ever make—to save his life. And then the guild was paid in silence. Entry two: *Paid to Oren Vael: silence, ongoing, valued at one guildhouse, one harbor charter, and the king's continued ignorance.*"

"Those are lies," Oren said, but his voice cracked on the second word, and four hundred people heard it crack.

"Entry seven," Coral continued. "*The Debtor grants King Aldric's wish for rain during the drought of his third year. Cost: the king's memory of his wife's voice.*" She turned the page. "Entry twelve. *The Debtor grants victory at the border. Cost: the king's memory of his daughter's birth.*"

Isolde made a small sound. Coral did not look at her. If she looked at her, she would stop reading, and she could not stop reading.

"Entry thirty-one. *The Debtor grants the king's wish that his councilors be loyal. Cost: the king's memory of every argument he ever lost, and therefore every lesson those arguments taught.*" She turned another page, and another, letting the dates fall like stones. "There are two hundred and six entries in this ledger. Two hundred and six wishes. The king who sits before you has paid for every good year of his reign with pieces of himself, and none of you knew, because the guild was paid to keep the books closed."

"The guild," Oren said, standing now, chains rattling, "kept the kingdom *stable*. You are a disgraced apprentice reading fantasies to a frightened court—"

"I'm reading your handwriting." She held the ledger open toward him. "Forty-one years of it. You taught me the hand yourself, Master. You said a ledgerkeeper's script should be legible to God."

Someone in the gallery laughed—a short, shocked sound—and the spell of Oren's authority broke visibly, like ice going out of a river.

And then the crown answered.

Light came off the king's brow like heat off summer stone. The court shrank back; guards drew steel that would have been useless against weather. Aldric did not rise from the throne. His body stayed seated, hands flat on the arms, but something else stood up inside him, and when he spoke, the voice was layered—one man's voice and beneath it something older, vast and patient and starving, the sound Coral had heard in the salt cathedral under the harbor.

"You read my debts aloud," the god said through the king's mouth. "Very well, keeper. Let us settle accounts before all of them."

It turned the crowned head slowly, surveying the court, and every person it touched flinched.

"I claim what I am owed. Forty-one years of service rendered. I kept the boy alive when every physician failed. I gave the kingdom rain, and victory, and peace. I ate his memories because that was the price, and the price was *agreed*—" the voice rose, and the windows hummed, "—by your guildmaster, in ink, in witness, under seal. You do not get to call it theft now that the bill has come due. A bargain is a bargain. It is the only law older than kings."

"It is," Coral said.

The god's attention came around to her like a lighthouse beam.

"And you know our law better than any soul in this room," it said. "You have been down to my cathedral. You have seen my hunger. So tell me, keeper of nothing—what does the ledger say?"

Coral's heart was slamming against her ribs. She let it. Fear was just another entry; she'd learned to record it and keep working.

"The ledger says you're right," she said. "Every debt in this book is valid. Every payment was agreed. If we stopped here, the crown would consume the king down to the last memory, and the law would call it justice." She paused. "But there is one clause you haven't heard. The oldest one. Older than your contract. Older than the guild."

The god went still. Even the light dimmed slightly, listening.

"Read it," it said.

"A ledger may be burned by its keeper." Coral lifted the grey book so the court could see it. "Not by a king. Not by a mob. By the keeper whose hand holds the accounts. When the keeper burns the ledger, all debts recorded within are voided. Not forgiven—*voided*. As if they were never written. The creditor loses the claim. The debtor loses the debt. Both walk away owing nothing, holding nothing." Her voice didn't shake. She had rehearsed this in the dark for six nights, and rehearsal had burned the shaking out of her. "That is the oldest clause. You know it's true. It's why you never let a keeper stand alone with the books."

For the first time, the god sounded less than certain. "If you burn that ledger, I starve. I have eaten nothing but one man's memories for forty years. Worship ended before your grandmother was born. That book is the last altar I have."

"I know," Coral said quietly. "I've seen what starving looks like on you. It looks like him." She nodded at Aldric, who was watching her now with his own eyes—just the king, briefly surfaced, understanding perhaps half of what was happening and trusting her anyway, which was either faith or the last luxury of a nearly empty man.

"There is another way," the god said. "Negotiate. New terms. I release the king gradually—I can ration, I can take only what he will not miss—"

"You took his daughter's birth," Coral said. "He will miss everything. There is nothing left he won't miss." She glanced at Isolde, standing rigid at the dais, tears cutting clean lines down her face, saying nothing, giving Coral nothing, trusting her with silence instead. "New terms are just the old hunger with a longer leash. No."

"Then name your price, keeper. Everyone has one. Your guildmaster did. What do you want? Wealth? Your name restored? The girl beside you?" The voice softened, went almost gentle, and that was the most frightening thing it had done all day. "I granted wishes for a living once. I remember how. Ask."

And there it was—the point of no return, and Coral found it wasn't a cliff at all. It was a doorway, and she had been walking toward it since the night she found a page written in no hand she knew.

"No," she said again. "That's how it starts. That's how it started with him." She looked at Oren, who had sunk back into his seat, grey as ash. "One kind wish, forty-one years ago. Look what it cost."

She struck flint from her pocket. The court gasped as one animal.

"Coral Delacroix," the herald began, "you are warned—"

"Burn it," Isolde said, loud enough for the galleries. "Burn it."

The god surged forward through the king, light pouring off the crown in sheets, and the temperature of the room dropped as though the harbor itself had risen up through the floors. "Keeper," it said, and now the patience was gone, "if you burn that book, I will have nothing. Do you understand what you unmake? I will be *nothing*."

"Yes," Coral said. "I understand exactly. I've been nothing my whole life—an illegitimate apprentice, a ghost in my own guild, a name no one wrote down. Nothing isn't the end." She touched flame to the corner of the first page. "Sometimes it's the beginning."

Fire took the entry about a drowned prince and ran.

* * *

What happened next, no ledger would ever record, because the ledger was burning in her hands and she fed it page after page while the god screamed through the king's mouth—not in rage, Coral would realize later, but in something far more human: terror of the dark, terror of the empty plate, terror of ending. The contracts unraveled as the pages curled. She felt them go, one by one, like knots slipping loose—forty-one years of binding coming undone in the space of a hundred heartbeats.

The crown's light guttered. Dimmed. And in the last moment before the final page became ash, the voice that filled the throne room was no longer vast at all. It was small, and old, and tired, and it sounded grateful.

"Oh," it said. "Oh, that's better."

Then the crown was only metal, and King Aldric slumped forward on his throne, breathing, alive, and reaching blindly for his daughter's hand.

Coral stood in a drift of her own ashes, holding nothing, keeper of nothing, while four hundred people stared at her—and at the door behind her, where Guildmaster Oren Vael was rising to his feet with murder and ruin in his eyes, and the guards, uncertain whose law still stood, were looking to the empty throne for orders that no one alive could give them.
