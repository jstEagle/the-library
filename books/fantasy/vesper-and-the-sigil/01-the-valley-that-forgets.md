## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The lock on Widow Sefu's memory-cabinet was a good one. Gideon Okafor had to give it that much.

Three tumblers, a false gate, and a spring-loaded pin that would have snapped shut like a turtle's jaw if she'd rushed it. Most locks in the valley were honest work — the kind a farmer's son built from a pattern his father taught him, all bluntness and no imagination. But this one had been made by old Tomas Ferro before his hands quit him, back when he still took pride in making things that kept people out.

Gideon appreciated that in a lock. It was a form of respect, really. Anyone could open a bad lock. It took craft to deserve a good one.

She crouched in the dark of the widow's pantry, knees complaining — she was thirty-one going on sixty, as far as her joints were concerned — and listened with her ear pressed to the cabinet's side while she worked the picks. Inside the house above her, the widow slept. Or didn't. That was the thing about grief; it kept its own hours.

The third tumbler gave with a click so soft it might have been a moth landing.

*There you are,* Gideon thought, and felt the small warm pride she always felt at this moment, the moment before the taking. She was very good at this. Not at stealing — stealing was easy, stealing was for children swiping apples off carts. What Gideon did required something finer. She took what people couldn't hold anymore. The things too heavy to carry and too precious to drop. And she did it gently, which was more than anyone else in the valley could say.

The jar sat on the top shelf of the cabinet, wrapped in wool against the cold. Memory-jars always looked disappointing to the uninitiated — squat glass things, thumb-sized to fist-sized depending on what they held, stoppered with wax and labeled in careful script. This one was large. Large enough that the label had been written twice, the first attempt crossed out because the widow's hand had shaken too badly to finish it.

*For Amara,* said the second line. *Who I loved longer than I knew how.*

Gideon held the jar up to the thin moonlight through the pantry window and watched the memory move inside. They all moved differently, memories. Joy sloshed bright and quick, like water over stones. Fear sat still and dark and waited. Grief — real grief, the kind that had lived in a person for years — turned slow and heavy, like honey poured in winter. This one turned like that. It caught the light and let it go, caught it and let it go, patient as tide.

Somewhere in there was a daughter. A laugh, probably. A specific way of saying *Mama* when she wanted something. The smell of whatever Amara had cooked on the last ordinary morning, before the fever came through the low farms and took one person in every fourth house, the way fevers do, without malice or preference, just arithmetic.

The widow had bottled it herself two winters ago. That was how it worked here — that was how everything worked here, in the Valley of Vell, where magic had died or never been born, depending on which elder you asked, and the only enchantment left in the world was the kind people made with their own hands and their own stubbornness. Memory-jars were the valley's one great invention. You couldn't cast a spell in Vell. You couldn't charm a chicken or curse a neighbor or light a candle with a word. But you could take the worst thing that ever happened to you, fold it small, and put it in glass. And then, for a price, someone like Gideon could come and take it away.

Not destroy it. Nobody destroyed memories; even the thieves had their scruples, and besides, a destroyed memory left a hole shaped like itself that ached worse than the original. No — taken memories went into the trade, sold downriver to towns that had no scruples at all, where poor folk bought other people's joys by the spoonful because their own lives hadn't provided enough. Gideon tried not to think about that part. She was not proud of how successfully she managed it.

She'd been hired, three weeks ago, by the widow herself. That was the arrangement most of her clients preferred, though few admitted it afterward. They came to her back door at dusk, hooded, embarrassed, the way people visit certain kinds of doctors. They sat in her kitchen and drank tea they didn't touch and described, in voices that broke at different places, the thing they needed taken. And Gideon listened — really listened, because the listening mattered, because a memory taken carelessly left ragged edges — and then she quoted a price, and then she told them to sleep well, and then she went to work.

The widow Sefu had wept through the whole telling. Not loudly. Widows in this valley wept the way rain fell in autumn: steadily, quietly, as though it were simply weather now. She'd said, *I know it's wrong. I know a mother shouldn't want to forget her own child. But I can't — I get up in the morning and for half a breath I've forgotten she's gone, and then I remember, and that half-breath is killing me faster than the grief ever could.*

And Gideon had nodded, because she understood that better than the widow knew. There were things in her own head she'd have paid good coin to lose, if she could only find them. Her past before age eleven was a locked room in a house she owned, and no pick she owned fit it. She suspected — in the way you suspect a draft comes from somewhere — that the room was why she'd become what she'd become. If you can't take your own memories out, take everyone else's. It wasn't philosophy. It was plumbing.

Now, in the pantry, she did the delicate part.

You couldn't just unstopper a jar and pour. Well — you could, but the memory would scatter, and the widow would wake tomorrow with a grief-shaped absence and no idea what had been removed, which was its own cruelty. No. The taking had to be done whole, clean, with the edges intact, so that what remained behind was not a wound but a smooth place, a healed-over thing, a scar the owner could touch someday and feel nothing but the shape of having survived.

Gideon set the jar on the shelf, thumbed the wax seal loose — it cracked with a sound like a knuckle popping — and laid two fingers on the rim of the glass. Then she breathed out, long and slow, the way her teacher Maren had taught her twenty years ago, before Maren had retired to the coast with a fortune and a clear conscience, or possibly neither.

*Take it like you're lifting a sleeping cat,* Maren used to say. *Firm enough that it knows it's held. Gentle enough that it doesn't wake.*

The memory rose. Gideon felt it come up through her fingertips and settle into her — not into her mind, exactly, but into some ledger-room behind her mind, where she kept the ones she carried. It was heavy. Heavier than most. Somewhere inside it was a birthday, a scraped knee bandaged with a strip of blue cloth, a voice singing slightly flat, a coffin too small, dirt on black gloves, a chair at a table that stayed empty at every meal for two years until the widow stopped setting it and then couldn't stop seeing it anyway.

Gideon held it the way you hold anything breakable and enormous: carefully, and with your whole self.

Then she stoppered the empty jar, resealed it with fresh wax from her kit — tinted to match, because details mattered — and set it back on the shelf wrapped in its wool. The cabinet closed. The lock, she noted with professional affection, showed no mark at all. Tomas Ferro would have approved of the opening almost as much as the keeping.

She let herself out through the pantry window, which she'd oiled the hinges of on her first visit, and stood a moment in the widow's garden among the winter kale and the bare apple tree, letting her heartbeat come down.

That was the part nobody talked about, the after. Clients thought the thief's work ended at the taking. It didn't. The taking was mechanics. The after was the part where you stood in a stranger's garden at two in the morning holding the worst day of their life inside your chest, and you felt it — you always felt it, once, fully, before it settled into storage — and you understood that tomorrow that woman would wake up and reach for her daughter the way she reached every morning, and her hand would close on smooth air instead of broken glass, and she would not know whether to be grateful or horrified, and she would probably be both, and she would live.

Somebody had to be able to carry that. In the whole valley, with its four hundred hearths and its wards at the rim and its elders who spoke of the outside world the way sailors speak of storms, only Gideon Okafor could. Which meant, if you followed the logic all the way home, that four hundred households slept easier because one woman couldn't sleep at all.

She was fine with that. Mostly. On nights like this she was fine with it.

She pocketed the jar — it rode against her ribs, warm as a loaf — and slipped out through the gap in the garden wall that she'd widened herself two seasons ago, and took the lane toward home at an unhurried walk, because running drew eyes, and in Vell the watchmen had nothing to do but notice things.

* * *

The Valley of Vell was shaped, roughly, like a cupped hand, and everything about it conspired to keep what it held.

The rim was stone and pine and, above the treeline, the wards — tall grey standing-stones set in a ring by founders whose names had worn off them, humming with a magic that was the valley's great paradox: the wards were magical, and their whole purpose was to keep magic out. Nothing enchanted could cross the rim. Nothing spell-wrought, nothing cursed, nothing blessed, nothing whispered-to. Traders from beyond brought goods up the pass and swore the color drained out of their charms by the second milestone, talismans going dull as river pebbles, love-potions turning to vinegar. The valley ate magic the way sand eats water.

The elders said this was a blessing. The elders said a great many things. Gideon had noticed early that the people who loudest called the valley safe were the people who'd never been anywhere else.

What it actually was, was quiet. Quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Four hundred hearths, one market square, a mill on the river Kess, a chapel where nobody prayed to anything in particular but everybody went anyway because the acoustics were good for crying. People in Vell lived long, careful lives. They married within three families, argued about drainage ditches, and bottled their dead. The valley forgot nothing and remembered poorly — that was the joke Gideon had never quite dared say aloud. Every grief in Vell sat in glass on a shelf, catalogued and dusted and visited on feast days, and the people themselves walked around lighter each year and stranger each year, until some of them, the ones who'd sold too much, smiled at funerals and couldn't tell you why.

Gideon's cottage sat at the end of Tanner's Row, behind a fence of woven hazel, under a roof she re-thatched herself every autumn because hiring help meant questions. She got home a little before three, banked the fire, and performed the night's ritual.

At the back of her bedroom, behind a panel that looked like wall, was her hoard.

Every thief had one. It was the trade's dirty secret, or rather its obvious one: you couldn't take thousands of memories and sell them all, because selling them all meant becoming the kind of person who sold griefs by weight, and even thieves had to look at themselves in the mornings. So the rule, unwritten but universal, was that you kept a portion. The ones too raw to sell. The ones that felt like they belonged to you somehow, though you couldn't say why. The ones that whispered.

Gideon's hoard filled one wall, floor to ceiling, jars upon jars upon jars, each labeled in her own tight hand. She had taken them over nineteen years of work. A fisherman's terror of deep water. A bride's joy, sold to her by a husband who couldn't bear being outshone. A boy's whole summer, aged nine, grass-stained and endless. A mother's lullaby — she'd never learned who had paid for that one to be taken, or why, and the not-knowing had kept her up more than one night.

She added the widow's grief to the shelf in its proper place — third row, between a miller's shame and a soldier's cowardice — and stood looking at it a moment.

It hummed. Not literally. But memories did that, the big ones, when you'd just taken them: they sat in your hoard and made themselves known, the way a new dog circles the room deciding which chair is theirs. This one would settle in a week. By then the widow would have woken a dozen mornings reaching for a daughter who now arrived in her mind only as a smooth warm fact — *I had a daughter, I loved her, she is gone* — without the teeth in it. Some clients sent thank-you bread. Some spat when Gideon passed in the market. Both reactions, she'd decided long ago, were correct.

She was tired in the particular way that came after carrying someone's worst hour. She should sleep.

Instead she lit a candle and pulled the map drawer.

Not a map of anywhere. A habit, rather — a sheet of paper where she sketched the valley from memory, over and over, the rim, the river, the four hundred hearths marked as tiny strokes. She'd done it since she was a girl. Maren had caught her at it once and laughed and said, *Thief, you're the only person in Vell trying to draw her way out.* Gideon hadn't known what she'd meant. She still didn't, quite. But the drawing soothed something, the way scratching soothes an itch whose cause you can't see.

Tonight the strokes wouldn't come right. Her hand kept drifting to the northern rim, to the pass, to the grey line of wards, and drawing little question marks along it like birds on a wire.

Outside, the wind had changed. She noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song — not with the mind first, but with the skin. Wind in Vell came down off the western hills, predictable as taxes. This wind came from the north. From the pass. From *outside*, over the wards, which was impossible, because nothing crossed the wards, not even weather, hardly.

Gideon stood at her window with the candle and looked north, where the dark was a shade too complete, and told herself the sensible things. Weather wandered. Wards faltered. Elders lied.

Then, far off, at the very edge of hearing — at the edge of the lamplight's reach, where the lane bent toward the rim road — she heard footsteps. Unhurried. Wrong-footed. The gait of someone walking on a leg that had recently stopped agreeing with him.

Nobody came to Vell. That was the first law of the place, older than the wards: the valley that forgets does not receive visitors, because there is nothing here worth remembering.

The footsteps came closer anyway.

Gideon blew out her candle, and stood in the dark with nineteen years of other people's ghosts at her back, and waited to see what the impossible wanted.
