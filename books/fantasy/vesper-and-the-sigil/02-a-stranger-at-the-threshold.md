## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

The impossible knocked.

Twice, politely, and then a third time that was less a knock than a slow collapse of knuckles against wood, the sound a hand makes when it has stopped taking orders from the rest of the body.

Gideon stood in her dark kitchen with the widow's grief still warm in its jar under her arm and did not move. This was not cowardice. Cowardice would have been easier. This was procedure. In nineteen years of lifting memories out of other people's heads, she had learned one rule above all the others: *when something strange arrives, wait for it to become either dangerous or dead.* Usually it managed both in short order.

The knock came again — or rather, the absence of a knock did, followed by a long, wet sound of someone sliding down her doorframe to sit on her step like a sack of turnips that had opinions about architecture.

"Oh, for pity's sake," Gideon said to the dark.

She set the grief-jar on the shelf with her other stock — carefully, because grief rolls if you let it — and lit the candle again, and opened the door with her boot ready and her face arranged into the particular expression that had gotten her out of eleven confrontations and two marriages: mild, unimpressed, faintly disappointed, as though the world were a dinner guest who had arrived late and slightly drunk.

The stranger was dying on her doorstep.

There was no gentler way to put it, so Gideon didn't try. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, dressed in traveling clothes that had once been fine and were now mostly road — dust in the seams, blood down the left side where something had opened him from shoulder to hip with what looked like enthusiasm. His boots were wrong for the valley: too good, too worn in the wrong places, the kind of boots a man wore when he walked cities, not lanes. And his eyes —

His eyes were the problem. Not because they were strange, but because they were *familiar*, in the way a word you've written ten thousand times becomes suddenly foreign when you stare at it. She knew the shape of him. She could not say how.

"You're bleeding on my step," she said.

"Am I." He considered this information as though it had been delivered by courier, requiring a signature. "I'd hoped it was rain."

"It isn't raining."

"No." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; she watched him pay for it. "It never does here, does it. I remember that now. Funny, the things you forget until you're standing in them."

Nobody came to Vell. The wards saw to it, and the valley's own nature saw to it twice over — a place that forgot itself could hardly be found by anyone else. Yet here was a man who spoke of Vell's weather like an old grievance, sitting in a spreading pool of himself on her threshold, looking up at her with those familiar-unfamiliar eyes as though he'd been expected.

As though *she* had been expected.

"I'm going to ask you three questions," Gideon said. "If your answers are bad, I'm closing the door."

"That seems fair."

"What's your name?"

"Corvus." He coughed, wetly. "Though I suspect that won't mean anything to you yet."

"Yet is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Second question: how did you get past the rim wards? Nobody gets past the rim wards. That's rather their whole personality."

"They weren't guarding against me." He fumbled at his coat, and Gideon's hand went to the little knife in her sleeve, but all he produced was a scroll case, wax-sealed, black wax stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize — a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye half-closed. "They were guarding against *this*. I carried it through the way you carry anything past a sleeping dog. Quietly. On my person. Where it can't be smelled."

"And the third question?"

He looked at her then — really looked, the way no one in Vell ever looked at her, because everyone in Vell had known her since before she'd been anyone worth looking at twice. He looked at her the way a man looks at a house he grew up in after thirty years away, checking which windows had changed.

"Do you know," he said softly, "you have her chin exactly."

The candle guttered, though there was no draft.

"I don't answer riddles before breakfast," Gideon said, "and it's nearly midnight, so you've got a full day's wait ahead of you. Goodnight."

She began to close the door. His hand shot out — faster than a dying man's hand had any right to be — and caught the edge, and pressed the scroll case into her palm, folding her fingers over it with both of his, and his hands were cold as river stones.

"Thirteen turns," he whispered.

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's how I know it's yours." His grip tightened, and there was desperation in it now, the polish gone off his voice. "Listen to me. Thirteen turns of the moon. When the thirteenth turns, the ledger closes, and whatever's owed gets collected, and it has been owed a very long time. Longer than you've been alive. Longer than—" A cough took him, deep and ruinous. "Take it to the mountain. You'll understand on the road. You won't understand here. It doesn't let you understand here."

"Who's *it*?"

"The court beneath." His eyes had begun to lose their focus, drifting toward some middle distance where perhaps better days were kept. "You have to go where magic still lives, girl. It can't follow you into the valley, but it can wait. Oh, it can wait. It's been waiting nineteen years—"

"Nineteen years," Gideon repeated, and hated herself for repeating it, for the small eager lurch the number gave somewhere under her ribs. Nineteen years was precisely the length of time she had lived in Vell, which was to say the length of time she had existed at all, as far as anyone in the valley could prove. Before Vell, nothing. She had arrived, they said, as a child of nine or ten, walking down the rim road alone with empty hands and no story, and the valley had folded her in the way valleys do — without questions, because questions require remembering the answers.

She had always assumed she'd come from somewhere. Everyone comes from somewhere. But in nineteen years she had never once felt the pull of it, the homesickness the other transplants spoke of over cheap wine — the ache for a village, a mother, a language of childhood. Her past was simply a room with the furniture removed. She had furnished it herself, piece by piece, with stolen goods.

And now a dying man on her step was counting years at her like coins.

"Who are you?" she asked, and her voice came out lower than she intended. "Truly. No riddles."

"An old debtor," Corvus said. "A messenger who read the message too late to save himself. A man who loved—" He stopped. Started again. "I made a promise to a woman with your chin, a long time ago, and I am twenty years late keeping it, and I find that dying does not excuse the debt, it only transfers it." He pushed the scroll case harder into her hand. "Thirteen turns. One is already spent. I spent it getting here."

"The moons are twelve to a year. Everyone knows that."

"Not everyone." He was smiling again, that expensive smile, but it had gone soft at the edges. "Not where I've been. There are thirteen, girl. There have always been thirteen. The thirteenth is just shy."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

Gideon looked down at the scroll case in her hand. Black wax, the half-closed eye. It weighed almost nothing. It should have weighed almost nothing. Instead it sat in her palm like a stone pulled from a riverbed, cold and heavier than its size and faintly, unpleasantly *aware* of her, the way a cat is aware of you when it has decided to adopt you whether you consent or not.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said. "I have a trade here. I have a life. It's small, but it's mine, and I built every stick of it myself, and I am not about to abandon it because a bleeding stranger delivers a parcel with my name — which, incidentally, is not on it. There's no name on this at all."

"There will be," Corvus said. "Ink knows its own."

"That's not even a real saying."

"It is where I'm from."

"Where's that?"

But his eyes had closed. Just like that — mid-smile, mid-mystery, the rudest possible exit — and his breathing went shallow and rattling, and Gideon stood on her own threshold holding a sealed map and the certain knowledge that the night had just stopped being ordinary forever.

She should call for help. Widow Sefu had skill with wounds; old Tammas had tinctures. There was a whole lane of people who would come running if she raised her voice, because that was the other thing about Vell: nobody locked their doors, because nobody remembered why you would.

Instead she crouched down beside him, set two fingers against his throat, and found a pulse like a moth trapped under cloth. Faint. Stubborn. Going.

"You're dying," she told him.

"Mm." He didn't open his eyes. "I gathered. Somewhere around the second mile past the rim, actually. Very educational stretch of road."

"Why come all this way to die on a stranger's step?"

"Because you're not a stranger." His lips barely moved. "And because the message needed delivering inside the valley, where it can't be taken back. Whatever happens to me out there — whoever comes asking — the map is safe in here. Nothing follows it in. Nothing can make you give it up. Do you see? I've hidden it in the one place in the world with no doors." A breath. "I hid it with *you*."

Something cold walked down Gideon's spine on small feet.

"Corvus." She said the name deliberately, testing it. "Whoever comes asking — who comes asking?"

"Hollow men," he murmured. "Riders. They smell stolen goods, and this—" his hand twitched toward the case in hers, "—this is the most stolen thing in the world. Don't let them taste it. Don't let them taste *you*. You're—" Another cough, longer this time, and when it passed his voice had dropped to a thread. "You're more stolen than you know."

Then, because the night apparently hadn't finished insulting her, he added, distinctly, with the last clarity he seemed to possess:

"Tell Perrin I'm sorry. Tell him it wasn't his fault. Tell him—" and here his voice cracked clean through, the polished messenger gone, leaving only a tired man at the end of a very long road, "—tell him I chose it. He'll understand. He never believes he's forgiven unless you explain the arithmetic."

"Perrin who?"

But Corvus Osei — though she would not learn his second name for many months, and would curse it more than once — had spent his last clear words. What remained was breathing, and then less breathing, and Gideon sat on her cold step in the smallest hours of the morning holding a map that hummed faintly against her palm like a struck bell nobody else could hear, watching the sky over the rim go from black to iron to the color of old ash.

She did not sleep. She tried, once, lying on her bed with the scroll case on the table across the room, and found that she could feel it the way you can feel a stare — patiently, from across the room, *waiting*.

Near dawn she gave up, made tea she didn't drink, and did what she always did when the world misbehaved: she made a list.

One. A dying man had crossed wards that could not be crossed, carrying a thing the wards existed to keep out.

Two. He had known her — or thought he had — well enough to recognize her face in a doorway at midnight.

Three. Nineteen years. Thirteen moons. A court beneath a mountain. Hollow riders who smelled stolen goods. None of these were things a sane valley contained.

Four. *You're more stolen than you know.*

She wrote that one down and then immediately regretted it, because seeing it in her own handwriting made it real in a way that hearing it had not. She stared at the words for a long time. Then she turned the paper over, because Gideon Okafor had survived nineteen years in a valley that forgot by knowing exactly which thoughts to pick up and which ones to leave in the road.

The sun came up over the rim, gold and indifferent. Down the lane, Widow Sefu was already sweeping her step, and the smell of bread was moving through the morning like a rumor, and everything was exactly as it had always been.

Except that when Gideon finally slept, near midmorning, curled fully clothed on top of her blankets like a woman expecting to run, she dreamed of a kitchen she had never seen. Two pairs of small shoes by the door. A lullaby in a language she didn't speak, sung in a voice that made her chest hurt in a place she couldn't point to.

And when she woke, the scroll case was on the pillow beside her head.

She had left it on the table. She was sure of it. She was absolutely, professionally, thief-to-the-bone sure of it — and yet here it lay, warm as a sleeping bird, the black wax seal unbroken, the half-closed eye staring up at the underside of her jaw.

Gideon lay very still and looked at it for a long moment.

"Well," she said finally, to the ceiling, to the valley, to whatever patient thing had decided it owned her now. "That's not ominous at all."

Outside, the first thin crescent of the new moon hung pale over the rim road, already climbing.

Turn one, spent. Twelve to go.

* * *

By evening, Gideon had tried to get rid of the map four times.

She had buried it in the garden, where it surfaced overnight like a seed deciding it preferred the air. She had weighted it with river stones and dropped it down the old well, and heard it — distinctly, impossibly — *knock* from the bottom, twice, politely. She had given it to her hearth fire, which burned merrily around it for an hour without singeing the wax, and finally spat it back out onto the hearthstone, unharmed and faintly smug. The fourth attempt she did not count, because it consisted mainly of putting it in a drawer and refusing to look at it, which even she admitted was less a disposal method than a relationship.

So it was that she found herself at dusk standing in front of Widow Sefu's door, holding a plate of borrowed courage in the form of the widow's own returned bread tin, rehearsing sentences.

*I found a man on my step. He died. He gave me a map. The map won't leave. Also he mentioned my chin.*

Every version sounded worse than the last.

Sefu opened the door before she knocked — she always did, another of the widow's unsettling habits — and looked at Gideon, and then at the tin, and then at Gideon's face, which Gideon suspected was wearing the expression of a woman who had slept badly beside a humming map and buried it in a garden.

"You look," Sefu said, "like my late husband the day the goat got into the stillhouse. Come in. There's stew."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You're the only thief in the world with honest appetites." The widow stepped aside. "Sit. Talk. Or don't talk and eat, that works too, I've known you nine years and you've never once managed silence and a full mouth at the same time."

Gideon sat. The stew was lamb and root vegetables and too much pepper, which was to say it was perfect, and she ate half the bowl before she trusted her voice.

"Sefu. Has anyone ever come to Vell? Since you've been here. Anyone at all, from outside, arriving?"

The widow stirred her stew and did not answer at once, and Gideon had known her long enough to know that the pause was the answer.

"No," Sefu said at last. "Not in forty years. Why?"

Gideon turned her spoon over. Turned it back. "No reason. Hypothetically. If someone did arrive — someone hurt, someone carrying something — what would the elders say?"

"The elders would say burn it and bury the ashes and then bury the hole." Sefu's eyes, sharp as a thrush's, settled on her. "And then they'd ask you what you were doing at the rim at midnight to meet him. Because that's where he'd have come from, hypothetically. The rim road. Which is where you go when you're working, isn't it, child?"

The stew sat heavy in Gideon's stomach.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, with dignity.

"Mm." Sefu refilled her bowl without being asked, which in the widow's grammar was both forgiveness and accusation. "Eat your hypotheticals. And Gideon —" the old woman's voice softened, and that softness frightened Gideon more than any threat the elders could devise, "— whatever it is you've gotten hold of, whatever it's whispering to you: things that arrive in this valley wanting to leave again usually know something we don't. The valley forgets. That's its mercy and its sickness. But you —"

She reached across the table and, briefly, touched Gideon's wrist.

"You've always remembered *too much*, for someone with no past. Haven't you. All those things you carry for other people. I've often wondered where you put them. I've often wondered what else is in there, rattling around, waiting for someone to come collect."

Gideon's hand drifted, without her permission, to the pocket where the scroll case sat, warm through the cloth.

Outside the window, over the rim, the new moon was rising — thin as a paring, white as bone.

Turn two had begun.
