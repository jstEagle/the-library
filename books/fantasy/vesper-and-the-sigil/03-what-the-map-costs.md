## 3. What the Map Costs

The map did not like the floor.

Gideon discovered this at roughly half past midnight, when she set it down on her worktable — carefully, the way one sets down a sleeping cat or a grudge — and it began to smoke.

Not dramatically. Not with flames leaping and her curtains catching. Just a thin curl of grey rising off the wax seal, the way breath rises off a cup of tea in winter, as if the map were offended. She watched it for a long moment from across the room, where she had retreated with her arms crossed in what she would later describe to herself as caution rather than cowardice.

"You don't get to have opinions," she told it. "You're paper."

The smoking stopped. Then it started again, more insistently, the way a child repeats a question when the first answer is unsatisfactory.

Gideon had been a thief of small things for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that everything had opinions. Memory-jars hummed when they were happy and went sullenly silent when they weren't. Locks developed preferences about who touched them. The widow Sefu's grief, which Gideon had lifted only two days before the stranger arrived, had spent its first hour in her satchel weeping faintly through the cork until Gideon had wrapped it in wool to muffle the sound.

But paper was supposed to be simple. Paper was the honest man's material. Paper held still.

She crossed the room, picked the map up, and the burning stopped so abruptly that she felt the absence of heat like a hand withdrawn. In her grip, the wax seal was cool. The parchment lay flat against her palms, docile as a lamb.

"There," she said. "That wasn't so hard."

The ink moved.

She nearly dropped it. Across the face of the map — which had shown, the night before, a winding road north out of the valley, past landmarks she didn't recognize, toward a mountain drawn with almost childish menace — the lines were rearranging themselves. Roads slid. Rivers wandered. And in the blank space where the mountain's peak should have been, something new was forming: not ink exactly, but the memory of ink, a picture assembling itself out of nothing the way frost assembles on a window.

A kitchen. Small, low-ceilinged, with a crooked shelf holding two clay pots and a chipped blue bowl. A woman stood at the hearth with her back turned, and though Gideon could not see her face, she knew — with the strange, unearned certainty of a dream — that the woman was singing.

Gideon knew the song.

That was the impossible part. She had never heard it in her life. She was sure of this the way she was sure of her own hands: completely, and without evidence. But her mouth shaped the melody before her mind caught up, three notes of a lullaby in a language she did not speak, and then she clamped her jaw shut so hard her teeth clicked.

The kitchen faded. The roads crawled back into place. The map was just a map again, smug and silent.

Gideon sat down on the floor, because standing suddenly seemed ambitious, and held the thing at arm's length.

"Right," she said to the empty room. "We're going to talk about boundaries."

* * *

The trouble with living alone above your own shop is that there is no one to tell you that you've been awake for two days talking to parchment. Gideon figured it out on her own eventually, mostly because she caught herself arranging her stolen memories by how much they seemed to *like* the map — the widow's grief had gone quiet near it, restful even, while a jar of some farmer's first harvest practically rattled with excitement — and decided that this was a sign of either genius or madness, and that both required sleep.

Sleep did not come. Or rather, sleep came, but it brought the kitchen with it.

She dreamed of the low ceiling and the crooked shelf. She dreamed of the woman singing, and this time the woman turned around, and Gideon woke with the shape of a face dissolving behind her eyes like sugar in water, and the lullaby's last note still ringing somewhere in her chest, and tears on her face that she had no memory of producing.

She sat up in the grey light of morning and looked at the map, which had placed itself on her pillow at some point during the night. She did not remember carrying it to bed.

"That," she said, and her voice came out rough, "is deeply rude."

The map said nothing, but she had the distinct impression it was pleased with itself.

* * *

By noon she had tried everything a professional would try.

She locked it in her strongbox with three of her best wards, the ones she used for jars that fought back. When she opened the box an hour later, the map was sitting on top of the wards, neatly folded, radiating patience.

She buried it in the garden behind the shop, under the rosemary, at a depth she considered respectful for documents. It took her twenty minutes of digging to find it again after the soil began, very faintly, to warm — not burning the roots, not yet, but making its position clear.

She gave it to the river. This was the plan, anyway. She walked down to the Greywash with the map under her arm and stood on the bank for a quarter of an hour working up the nerve, and in that time she noticed that the water made her uneasy in a way she couldn't name — a flinch deep in her body whenever she looked at the current, some old animal knowledge with no story attached — and by the time she'd puzzled over that, the sun had moved and she'd talked herself into trying the fire instead, tomorrow, definitely tomorrow.

It was the flinch that frightened her most, afterward. Not the moving ink, not the map's stubborn refusal to be discarded. The flinch. Because Gideon Okafor knew every item in her own inventory. She had built her whole self out of things she could name: thief, orphan, keeper of other people's worst hours, excellent at locks, mediocre at cooking, fond of rain. A gap — a place where a feeling lived without a reason — was not supposed to exist in her. She was the woman who took apart other people's memories for a living. Her own interior was supposed to be tidy.

The map, she suspected, knew about the gap. The map seemed to be aimed at it.

"Thirteen turns," she muttered, pacing her shop floor that evening, the map riding in her satchel against her hip like a warm stone. "What does that even mean? Turns of what? Who counts like that? Nobody says turns. People say days. Weeks. *Moons*, if you're being dramatic."

Her own voice answered her from the back of her head, in a register she didn't use: *thirteen moons*. And beneath it, faint as the smell of bread from a neighbor's window, the lullaby started up again.

She went to find the elders.

* * *

The valley council met in the old tithe barn, because the valley had stopped paying tithes a hundred years ago and the building had been repurposed the way valley things always were — gradually, without ceremony, until nobody remembered it had ever been anything else. There were seven elders. Six of them were asleep within the first twenty minutes, which Gideon considered fair; she had attended precisely one meeting in her life and had sworn off the practice. The seventh was Elder Nkechi, who was ninety-one, sharp as a fishhook, and had been waiting for Gideon at the door.

"You look terrible," Nkechi said.

"I've been having a week."

"So I hear." The old woman's eyes went to the satchel. "Word travels. A stranger at the rim, bleeding, dying, handing you a sealed thing. You know what the last stranger at the rim brought us?"

"Famine?"

"A very good recipe for pickled onions, actually, but he also stole the miller's horse, so the point stands." Nkechi led her inside, past the dozing council, to a bench near the door where their conversation wouldn't wake anyone important. "Show me."

Gideon hesitated. Then she laughed at herself for hesitating — the map wasn't hers, not really, and certainly not precious — and unwrapped it.

Nkechi did not touch it. She leaned close, close enough that her breath stirred the parchment, and studied the roads, the rivers, the mountain with its childish menace. For a long time she said nothing. Around them the barn creaked and an elder snored in a key that suggested dreams of grain quotas.

"This is court-work," Nkechi said at last.

"I know. The seal—"

"Not the seal. The *hand*." She tapped the air above the map without touching it. "I saw documents like this once, when I was young and foolish enough to travel. The Under-Court keeps its accounts beautifully. Everything measured, everything balanced, everything paid." Her eyes came up, and they were not unkind, but they were not soft either. "Nothing under the mountain is free, girl. Not a road. Not a favor. Certainly not a dying man's last breath. He gave you this for a reason, and I promise you the reason costs more than you have."

"It's a map," Gideon said. "It shows a road."

"It shows a road *to you*. That's different." Nkechi straightened, joints crackling like green wood. "And it won't leave you, will it? I can see it from here. It's wearing you like a coat."

Gideon's hand drifted, without her permission, to the satchel strap. She made herself stop. Made herself put both hands flat on her knees, visible, controlled, a professional demonstrating competence.

"It burns when I set it down," she admitted. "The ink moves. It shows me—" She stopped. The kitchen felt private in a way she couldn't justify. Nothing about a stranger's map should be private. "Scenes. Things I don't remember. Things I'm fairly sure never happened to me."

"And yet."

"And yet," Gideon agreed quietly.

Nkechi nodded slowly, and for a moment she looked less like a fishhook and more like what she was: a woman who had buried a husband, two children, and one entire century, and had done it all inside a valley where magic couldn't follow, using nothing but her own two hands and her own stubborn heart.

"Burn it," she said.

"I—"

"Tonight. In your shop fire, with salt in the flame, and don't watch it die — turn your back, count to a hundred, sweep the ashes out the door before dawn. Whatever debt that dying man tied to you, ashes break knots better than anything else in this world. It's why we burn letters. It's why we burn the dead." She gripped Gideon's wrist, her fingers surprisingly strong. "You are a thief, Gideon Okafor, and a good one, and thieves survive by knowing what not to steal. This map is trying to steal *you*. Can you feel it? The wanting? That little hook behind your ribs that says *just look one more time, just follow one more road*?"

Gideon could feel it. She had been feeling it for two days, and she had been telling herself it was curiosity, professional interest, due diligence. Standing in the tithe barn with an old woman's hand on her wrist, she had to admit it felt less like curiosity and more like thirst.

"Yes," she said.

"Then burn it. And Gideon—" Nkechi released her wrist, and her voice dropped, almost gentle. "Whatever it showed you. The scenes you're sure never happened. Ask yourself why a piece of paper knows your mother's kitchen."

Gideon went cold all over. "I never said it was my mother's kitchen."

"No," Nkechi agreed. "You didn't."

* * *

She walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and the evening's bread, and the map rode warm against her hip, and she made plans.

That was the honest truth of it, and she made herself look at it squarely, the way she looked at a lock before picking it: she was not going to burn the map. She had known it since the tithe barn, probably since the garden, possibly since the riverbank where she'd stood flinching at water for reasons she couldn't name. Nkechi was wise and kind and right about everything, and Gideon was going to ignore all of it, because behind her ribs that little hook had become a hand, and the hand was pulling, and underneath the pull — beneath the dread and the irritation and the very reasonable fear — was something she hadn't felt in eleven years of competent, careful, lonely thieving.

She wanted to know.

Not wanted the way she wanted rent money. Wanted the way a jar wants its lid off. Somewhere in her was a room she'd never entered, and the map had its key, and every hour she carried it the key fit a little deeper into the lock.

At her shop door she stopped. The lamp inside was lit.

She had left it dark. She was certain she had left it dark — she was a thief, she noticed things, noticing was the whole profession — and yet warm light spilled through the shutters onto the step, and from inside came a sound that stopped her heart and restarted it sideways:

Humming. Three notes. Her three notes, the lullaby's opening phrase, hummed in a voice like hers but older, like hers but someone else's.

Gideon stood on her own doorstep, hand on the latch, and understood with perfect clarity that she was at a threshold of the kind that doesn't offer itself twice. Behind her: the valley, the rules, the tidy self she'd built, Nkechi's sensible fire. Ahead of her: whatever waited in her lamplit shop, humming her mother's song in a kitchen that didn't exist.

The map pressed against her hip, patient as a held breath.

She opened the door.

Inside, on her worktable, surrounded by her jars, sat the map — unfolded, spread wide, its ink alive and crawling — and beside it, in a neat row, every memory-jar she owned, each one glowing faintly, each one turned toward the parchment like flowers toward a window. And floating up from the map's surface, thin as smoke and twice as persistent, came the smell of bread baking, and a woman's voice singing, and beneath the song, so quiet she might have imagined it, a second voice — smaller, younger, laughing — calling a name.

Not *Gideon*.

Something else. Something that fit her mouth like a key fits a lock she'd forgotten she owned.

The map waited. The valley elders' advice waited, sixty paces away and getting further every second. Thirteen turns, the dying man had said, and the moon through her window was already three nights old, already counting.

Gideon closed the door behind her.
