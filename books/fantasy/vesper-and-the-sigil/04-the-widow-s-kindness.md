## 4. The Widow's Kindness

Widow Sefu had buried two husbands, one son, and a great many opinions about other people, and she had learned that grief was like bread dough: it needed to be worked, or it went hard and useless in the middle.

So when the thief came for hers, Sefu was, in a manner of speaking, ready.

Not that she'd known it at the time. She'd woken on the third morning after the theft with her hands full of nothing and her heart full of quiet, and she had sat on the edge of her bed for a long while, testing the silence where her sorrow used to live. It was a strange sensation. Like reaching for a tooth and finding smooth gum. She knew — because everyone in the valley knew — what had happened. Memory-thieves worked the valley the way moths worked wool: quietly, thoroughly, and always leaving you with a garment that fit but didn't hang right anymore.

Most people raged. Most people went to the wardens, who shrugged their shrug of shrugs, because magic was dead here and stolen memories were like spilled milk — no law could unspill them. Most people spent a season hollow-eyed and then filled the hole with something else: drink, religion, a new goat.

Sefu made bread instead.

She had been making bread since before dawn, working the dough with more force than it strictly required, thinking all the while. Because here was the thing nobody understood about having your grief stolen: you didn't stop missing him. You just stopped knowing *why* you missed him. The shape remained. The name remained. Amara still set out three cups some mornings out of forty years of habit, and then stood there staring at the third cup like it had grown there overnight, wondering why her hands had done such a foolish thing.

The thief hadn't taken her love. Just the weight of it. And Sefu found she wanted the weight back.

Which was how, on a bright cold morning with the smell of woodsmoke lying over the town like a blanket, Widow Sefu wrapped two loaves in cloth, tucked a small clay pot of honey under her arm, and set out to find the woman who had robbed her.

* * *

Finding her was not difficult. Sefu had lived in this valley sixty-one years, and she knew every face the way she knew her own kneading board. The thief was new — come up from somewhere south, they said, renting the room above the cooper's shop, keeping odd hours, wearing a coat too fine for these parts and boots too worn for anywhere else. A woman of contradictions, which Sefu privately approved of. Contradictions kept a person interesting.

She was also, according to the cooper's wife, who noticed everything and understood perhaps half of it, a person who ate alone, spoke to no one, and flinched when children ran past her window.

"Lonely as a left shoe," the cooper's wife had said, and Sefu had thought: *good.* Lonely people could be talked to. Angry people could only be shouted at, and shouting had never once in Sefu's life gotten her anything but a sore throat and a reputation.

She found the thief at the market, of all places, standing in front of old Tam's stall and examining a jar of pickled plums with the intensity of a scholar reading scripture.

"You want the ones from the back," Sefu said, coming up beside her. "Tam puts the pretty ones in front and the sour ones behind. He thinks nobody knows."

The thief turned. Up close she was younger than Sefu had guessed — thirty, maybe, with careful eyes and a face that did its smiling only under direct orders. "I wasn't—"

"You were deciding whether to buy plums or whether buying plums would make you look like a person who buys plums." Sefu held out the cloth-wrapped loaves. "Here. Bread. You owe me."

Something flickered across the young woman's face — quick, guilty, gone. "I don't think I owe you anything."

"Oh, I think you do." Sefu pushed the loaves into her arms, because a gift refused is a conversation ended, and Sefu had not walked all this way to be ended. "Two loaves. One for the taking, one for the listening. Come along. My house is warm and my tea is strong enough to stand a spoon in."

"I'm not really—" The thief looked down at the bread as if it might be armed. "Why aren't you angry?"

That, at least, was an honest question, and Sefu rewarded honesty. "Because anger is for people who've lost something by accident," she said. "You took mine on purpose. That means you had a reason, and reasons can be bargained with. Walk."

And — this was the part Sefu would remember later, turning it over like a coin — the thief walked.

* * *

Her house smelled of rosemary and yesterday's fire, and Sefu put the thief to work slicing bread because idle hands in Sefu's kitchen were an insult to the kitchen. The young woman cut even slices, precise as a surgeon, which told Sefu a good deal about her.

"Gideon," the thief said at last, when the tea was poured and the honey opened. "My name. Since we're doing this."

"Sefu. Though everyone calls me Widow Sefu, which has always struck me as redundant. I'm unlikely to be confused with any other Sefus." She sat, folded her hands, and studied her guest. Gideon sat like a cat near a door — comfortable, but ready. "Now. You have my grief in whatever jar or bottle or pocket you keep such things. I want it back."

Gideon's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth. "That's not how it works."

"Explain to me how it works, then. I've got time. I'm a widow; time is mostly what I have."

"It doesn't—" Gideon set the cup down. For a moment she looked genuinely younger, caught out, a girl called before the headmistress. "What I take, I keep. It's not — I can't give it back. It doesn't work that way."

"Why not?"

"Because—" The girl's jaw tightened. "It just doesn't."

"Mm." Sefu sipped her tea. "In sixty-one years I've found that 'it just doesn't' usually means 'I don't know' or 'I won't.' Which is it?"

For a long moment Gideon said nothing. Then, quietly, in a voice that had clearly been used for lying so often it had forgotten its own truth: "I don't know. Nobody ever asked for one back before. They ask if it hurts. They ask if I feel anything. Nobody asks to have it returned."

"Well, I'm asking." Sefu leaned forward. "And I'll tell you why, since you're doing me the courtesy of sitting in my kitchen looking miserable. Three days ago I woke up without my sorrow, and do you know what I felt? Nothing. Nothing where my husband was. I stood at his grave and felt like a woman visiting a stranger's stone. And I thought — that thief didn't steal my pain. She stole the last of him I had left. Because the hurting was *his*, girl. Every ache of it. It was the last thing he gave me, and you pocketed it like a coin."

Gideon had gone very still. Stillness, Sefu noted, seemed to be her native language.

"I didn't think of it like that," she said finally.

"No. Thieves rarely do. We're all excellent at counting what we take and terrible at counting what we leave." Sefu reached over and patted the girl's hand once, briskly, the way you'd pat a horse you meant to ride again. "But here's the thing. I didn't bring you here only to scold. Scolding keeps better in a letter. I brought you here because of a story."

"A story."

"An old one. Older than me, and I'm older than most things in this valley." Sefu settled back, and let her voice drop into the register she'd used for her son when he was small and afraid of thunder — low, unhurried, certain. "When I was a girl, my grandmother told me about the year of the black frost. Before your time, before the wardens, when the valley was poorer than poor. Crops failed two seasons running. People were eating bark soup and calling it stew. And up the mountain road, in those days, there was still traffic. Court folk. Beautiful, terrible people with silver in their smiles, and they came down offering bargains."

"Bargains," Gideon repeated. Her hand had drifted, unconsciously, to her coat pocket — where, Sefu now noticed, something flat and heavy sat, wrapped against the body's warmth.

"The court beneath the mountain," Sefu said. "They bought things, in the famine years. Not land — they had land. Not gold — they minted their own. They bought *selves*. Memories, mostly. Years of a life, sold off to feed a family through winter. And sometimes — my grandmother swore this part on her mother's grave — sometimes they bought bigger. Souls. Whole souls, of children especially. Children were prized. A child's soul keeps well, she said. Like salted meat. Doesn't spoil."

"That's a story," Gideon said. But her voice had thinned.

"It's a story the way bread is flour. Everything true in it has been ground down small." Sefu met her eyes. "There was a family, my grandmother said. Twin girls. The year of the black frost. The mother went up the mountain road with one daughter's hand in each of hers, and came back down holding only one. And the one she brought home grew up and never spoke of it, and the whole valley agreed never to speak of it either, because that is what valleys are *for*, girl — forgetting. This valley forgets better than anywhere. Why do you think your kind works here? Rich soil."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, and a dog complained about it.

"What happened to the sister who stayed?" Gideon asked. Her voice was very careful now, the way a person walks on ice.

"My grandmother didn't say. Stories like that lose their endings — people carry them too long and the ending wears off first." Sefu refilled the girl's cup without asking. "But I'll tell you what stayed with me. My grandmother said the mother paid someone, afterward. Paid a thief — this was when thieves still had craft, mind, not like today's jar-pickers — to take the memory of the mountain road out of her own head. Paid her last coin for it. Because she couldn't bear to remember which hand she'd let go of."

Gideon's face had gone the color of the flour sack. She was gripping her teacup hard enough that Sefu half expected it to crack.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because three nights ago, a stranger bled to death at the valley's rim, and the whole town knows it, and the whole town also knows he pressed something into your hands before he went. And because you've been walking around since with a face like a woman carrying a lit lantern through a hay barn." Sefu spread her hands, palms up. "I'm an old woman. I've buried everyone worth burying. I have no fear left to spend, and no patience for pretending. Whatever's coming down that mountain road, girl — it's been coming a long time. Longer than you know, I'd wager."

"You don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course I don't. I know *bread*. I know that when dough rises wrong, it's not the oven's fault — it's something done long before, in the mixing." Sefu stood, gathered the honey pot, and pressed it into Gideon's hands alongside the second loaf. "Take it. Eat well tonight. And when you go wherever that map of yours is dragging you —"

Gideon flinched. Actually flinched, full-body, at the word *map.*

"— when you go," Sefu continued gently, "remember that somebody in this valley wants her grief back. Not for the pain. For the man. Do you understand the difference?"

For a moment the thief looked at her with an expression Sefu couldn't read — something raw and startled, like a fox surprised at a henhouse door left open on purpose.

"Yes," Gideon said at last, barely above a whisper. "I think I'm starting to."

"Good." Sefu shooed her toward the door. "Then eat the bread, mind the plums, and come tell me what you find. Old women make excellent keepers of secrets — we've got the room."

* * *

She watched from her window as the thief walked away down the lane, shoulders hunched around the map hidden in her coat like a coal she couldn't put down. Halfway to the corner, Gideon stopped. Stood there a long moment, motionless in the cold light. Then she looked back — not at Sefu's window, Sefu was sure of it, but at the mountains. At the rim of the valley, where the world supposedly ended and the wards began.

At the place where, Sefu now remembered with a small cold start, another stranger had arrived just last week asking questions about twin girls. Asking at the tavern, at the mill, at the chapel. A soft-spoken traveler with ink-stained fingers, who had left the same day the dying man appeared at the rim.

Two strangers in one month, after sixty years of none.

Sefu turned back to her kitchen, tied on her apron, and began the evening's dough. But her hands moved slower than usual, and her mind kept returning to her grandmother's voice, thin as smoke: *the mother paid her last coin to forget which hand she'd let go of.*

Some debts, Sefu thought, kneading, pressing, working the grief into the flour where it belonged — some debts don't die with the debtor. They just wait for someone with pockets deep enough to carry them.

Outside, far off toward the rim, a bell began to ring. The wardens' bell. The one that rang, in all Sefu's sixty-one years, only when something crossed the boundary that should not have been able to cross it.

She wiped her hands and went to see.
