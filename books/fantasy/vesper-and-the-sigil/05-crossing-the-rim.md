## 5. Crossing the Rim

The wardens changed shifts at the third bell, and they were lazy about it.

Gideon had watched them for six nights from the crook of an old rowan tree above the eastern path, a thermos of bitter tea going cold in her hands and a blanket around her shoulders that smelled like mothballs and someone else's grandmother — she'd bought it secondhand, which in the valley meant it came pre-haunted with small domestic memories she hadn't asked for. The wardens were two brothers named Tam and Odell Fitch, and their entire method of guarding the valley's rim consisted of walking the same quarter-mile stretch, complaining about their feet, and checking each other's dice for cheating.

Tonight Tam was limping. Tonight Odell was carrying the lantern on his bad side. Tonight, Gideon decided, draining the last of her tea, was tonight.

She'd spent three days preparing, which for Gideon Okafor was practically a religious festival. Her pack held: bread wrapped in wax cloth (Sefu's, though the widow didn't know she'd taken it — or maybe she did; the woman had left the loaf out on her windowsill like bait, like a blessing), a skin of water, flint, a coil of good rope, and the map.

The map rode against her chest, sewn into the lining of her coat, because setting it down anywhere — table, floor, another room — made it burn through whatever separated it from her skin. Not fire-burn. *Wanting*-burn. It heated up like a cat pressing itself against you, insistent and warm and impossible to ignore, until she picked it back up just to make it stop. She had tried throwing it in the river. It had been waiting on the bank when she got there, dry as bone, its ink rearranged into what looked suspiciously like a picture of her doing exactly that.

"You're not the boss of me," she'd told it.

It had gone pleasantly cool for an hour, smug in the way only satisfied objects can be smug.

The elders had voted to destroy it, of course. Elder Mbeki had stood up at the meeting with his hands trembling and said that things from outside the valley did not belong inside the valley, that the whole reason their grandmothers had built this place was so that magic could not follow them home, and here was Gideon Okafor carrying a piece of the outside stitched to her ribs like a smuggled bird. Elder Nwosu had said gentler words but meant harsher ones. They'd given her until the next full dark to burn it.

Gideon had thanked them very sincerely, gone home, and started packing.

It wasn't defiance, exactly. Or — no. Fine. It was exactly defiance. But it was also this: every time she closed her eyes, the map showed her something. A kitchen with blue tiles. Two pairs of shoes by a door, one pair scuffed at the toes from kicking stones. A lullaby hummed off-key by a voice she could almost — *almost* —

She was not a curious woman by nature. Curiosity got thieves hanged. But she was a thorough one, and thoroughness demanded she find out why a stranger had bled out at the valley's edge whispering *thirteen turns* while pressing a map into her hands specifically. Not into Sefu's hands. Not into the wardens'. Hers.

There was a debt in it somewhere. Gideon understood debts the way fish understood water.

* * *

The Fitch brothers reached the end of their patrol at half past the third bell, exchanged the ritual complaint about their feet, and settled onto their bench with a jug between them. Gideon gave them ten minutes — long enough for the jug to do its work, short enough that they wouldn't fall fully asleep and start snoring loud enough to wake the goats.

Then she went over the wall.

Not the actual wall — the valley didn't have one, being defended instead by the simple fact that nothing outside wanted in. No, the boundary was stranger than stone. The elders called it the Rim, and if you stood at it and squinted, you could almost see it: a faint shimmer in the air, like heat off summer cobbles, except it never went away and it never warmed anything. Inside the Rim, magic died. That was the whole point. The valley was a place where a memory-thief couldn't simply *take* — she had to work with jars and locks and sleight of hand like any honest criminal. Outside the Rim, the world was supposedly full of wonders, and none of them, the elders said, were kind.

Gideon crouched in the shadow of the rowan, checked her knots, checked her bread, checked the map's warmth against her sternum — present, patient, purring — and waited for the wardens' laughter to crest, because men mid-laugh never look up.

She went up the scree on the east side where the shimmer ran thin, rope over rock, boots placed soft. Halfway up, she paused and looked back at the valley: the low roofs silvered by moonlight, the smoke standing straight up from cold chimneys, Sefu's little house with its stubborn garden. Somewhere in that darkness were forty years of her work — griefs lifted, terrors eased, one particularly memorable haunting of a guilty man's conscience that she still thought of as her masterpiece.

"Right," she told the valley quietly. "Don't do anything I wouldn't do."

The valley, being magic-dead and mostly asleep, declined to answer, which Gideon felt was fair.

She climbed the last ten yards, put her hand out past the shimmer—

—and the world *inhaled.*

* * *

Later, Gideon would try to describe it to herself and fail, but she would keep trying anyway, because some things demand it. Crossing the Rim was like being born deaf and then, at thirty-some years old, hearing for the first time — except the sound wasn't sound. It was everything. The grass under her hands had *opinions*. The wind coming down off the mountain carried voices the way a river carries leaves. The moon overhead wasn't a white coin but a vast attentive eye, and it was looking directly at her, and it seemed amused.

Magic flooded back into the world all at once, everywhere, ordinary as weather. And Gideon's pack — Gideon's carefully curated, professionally sealed, forty-years-in-the-collecting pack of stolen memories — woke up.

The first whisper came from the bread tin, which was absurd, because she kept her spare vials in there. A child's voice, thin and delighted: *"—and when the lantern swings three times, we run for the orchard—"*

Gideon froze halfway over a boulder.

The second whisper rose from the inner pocket where she kept the widow's grief — no, she'd returned most of that, but there was a residue, a thread of it she'd kept for study, sealed in glass — and now the glass murmured like a kettle: *"—he always burned the toast, every morning, forty years, and I would give anything—"*

"No," Gideon said out loud. "No, no, you are *sealed*, you are stoppered, you have *corks*—"

But corks, apparently, were a valley technology. Outside the Rim, where magic lived and breathed and stuck its fingers into everything, her stolen memories weren't objects anymore. They were *voices.* The pack at her back rustled and murmured like a rookery. A summer afternoon giggled from behind her left shoulder. Somebody's dead father cleared his throat politely near her right ear. A fear she'd lifted off a soldier twenty years ago began, softly, to sob.

Gideon sat down hard on the boulder and pressed both palms over her ears, which helped not at all, because the whispers weren't coming through her ears. They were coming through the place where the memories touched her — through the thief's bond she'd spent a lifetime pretending was just professional technique.

"Oh," she said, very small. "Oh, this is what it sounds like."

In the valley, her hoard was silent. In the valley, she could carry forty lives on her back and hear nothing but her own footsteps. She had told herself for decades that this was discipline. That she was a careful craftsman who kept her materials orderly.

She had not known she was deaf. Now the world had turned the sound on, and the world was *louder than she was.*

"Stop it," she whispered to the pack. "All of you. We are going to walk to the mountain, retrieve one soul, settle one debt, and come home, and you will behave like the inanimate objects you legally are."

The soldier's fear sniffled. The summer afternoon giggled again, higher this time, giddy. And beneath all of them, warm against her sternum, the map pulsed once — slow, satisfied, like a heart that had finally gotten its owner to listen.

*"Thirteen turns,"* it whispered, in a voice that was almost the dying stranger's and almost, horribly, not.

Gideon got up. There was nothing else to do. She cinched the pack straps tight as she could make them, tucked the whispering grief-glass deep among the bread, and walked north under the too-attentive moon, talking steadily to herself — about the weather, about the Fitch brothers' dice, about anything — because a voice, even her own, even shaking, was a rope she could hold onto in all that water.

She made perhaps three miles before dawn greyed the sky and the whispers drowsed down to a mutter, and she allowed herself, briefly, to feel proud. She had crossed the Rim. She had not turned back. She had not wept, although it had been close around the part with the sobbing soldier.

That was when she heard the whistling.

It came from ahead — a jaunty, complicated tune, the kind of thing a person whistles when they want you to know they've noticed you and aren't worried. Gideon's hand went to her knife. On a rock beside the road sat a magpie. Not near a rock. *On* it, upright and glossy, head cocked, one eye fixed on her with an expression of profound administrative displeasure.

"You're late," said the magpie.

Gideon looked left. She looked right. The road was empty in both directions, and the whispers in her pack had gone suddenly, attentively silent, the way a room goes quiet when the interesting guest arrives.

"I've been waiting nine days," the magpie continued, "on the strength of a promise made by a dying idiot, and do you know what I hate? Waiting. Also promises. Also dying idiots." It ruffled its feathers. "You'd be the thief, then."

"How—" Gideon started.

"And you're leaking," the magpie added, wrinkling its beak at her pack. "Honestly, it's like traveling with an unstoppered jug of soup. We'll need to do something about that before the road, or every hungry thing between here and the mountain will follow you like you're ringing a dinner bell." It hopped once, closer, and its eye glittered black and bright and far too knowing. "My name is Perrin. I owe your dead man a favor I intend to pay quickly and resent thoroughly. Get up. We have twelve turns left, and you've already wasted one sitting on that rock talking to your luggage."

The map against Gideon's chest went warm — warmer than it had ever been in the valley — and pulsed twice, quick and eager, like a knock at a door she hadn't known she'd opened.
