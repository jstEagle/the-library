## 6. The Magpie Road

The world beyond the rim was too much.

Gideon had been out of the valley for two days now, and she still hadn't gotten used to it. In the valley, silence was a substance — thick, dependable, the kind of quiet you could lean your whole life against. Out here, everything *spoke*. The grass whispered about rain. The stones muttered old heat back at the evening sky. And her stolen memories, which in the valley had sat obedient and mute in their jars, now murmured constantly against her ribs like cats demanding to be fed.

She'd taken to walking with her arms crossed over her satchel, as if that would hush them. It didn't. Somewhere around midday they had started humming — all of them, forty-odd jars of other people's griefs and joys and one widow's terrible beautiful grief, harmonizing on a note that made her teeth ache.

"Stop that," she told them.

They didn't stop. They never stopped. That was the trouble with memories: even bottled, they were still true, and truth was the most persistent sound in the world.

She was following the map. The map, at least, behaved itself out here — or rather, it misbehaved in ways she was beginning to understand. The ink crawled each dawn into new shapes: a road here, a river there, and always, always, a small black mark moving along the route ahead of her, like a fly drawn in charcoal. She had decided, after much argument with herself, that the fly was her destination. She had also decided, after slightly less argument, that if the fly turned out to be an actual fly, she was going to burn the map and take up honest work.

Honest work being, presumably, anything that didn't involve following a dead man's ink through country she didn't know toward a court beneath a mountain that ate girls' souls.

The road bent north along a ridge, and the ridge was lined with trees — tall ones, dark ones, the kind that looked like they kept secrets the way other trees kept birds. Which was, Gideon supposed, the same thing.

That was when the bird started following *her*.

It was a magpie, glossy black-and-white, and it did not behave like any magpie she'd ever seen. Ordinary magpies were thieves of opportunity — buttons, beetles, bright things left carelessly. This one was a thief of *attention*. It perched where she couldn't help looking at it. It flew ahead and waited. It cocked its head at her satchel with an expression she could only describe as appraising.

"Shoo," she said.

The magpie said nothing, which was somehow ruder than speech.

By late afternoon she had named it Annoyance, and by dusk she had begun talking to it, which was how she knew the loneliness from the valley had followed her across the rim after all. She camped under the secret-keeping trees, built a small fire, ate hard bread, and addressed her remarks to the bird sitting in the lowest branch like a debt collector.

"I know you're not a bird," she said. "Birds don't smirk."

The magpie preened.

"And I know you've been following me since the ford. So either you want something or you're the world's most committed nuisance, and I've met enough of both to know the difference is smaller than people think."

The magpie tilted its head. Then, with the unhurried insolence of a creature who has never once been sorry, it dropped something into the dust at the edge of her firelight.

A button. Silver, engraved, with a thread of black silk still knotted through it.

Gideon went very still.

She knew that button. She had seen it last on the coat of a bleeding stranger who had collapsed against the valley's threshold stones and pressed a wax-sealed map into her hands with fingers already going cold. She had buried him — or rather, the valley's wardens had buried him, in the magic-dead ground where nothing grew and nothing lingered, and she had stood at the edge of the grave feeling a grief she had no right to, because she hadn't known him, except that some part of her insisted she had, insisted so loudly that she'd had to steal a stranger's calm just to sleep that night.

"You knew him," she said quietly.

The magpie looked at her with one eye, then the other, the way birds do, as if checking whether she was worth the cost of both.

Then it spread its wings, and the wings became arms, and the feathers became a coat, and the whole impossible transformation happened in the space between one heartbeat and the next, so smoothly that Gideon's eyes insisted it had always been a man sitting in the tree, and only her memory of the magpie argued otherwise.

He dropped out of the branches and landed light as an insult. Youngish, maybe her age, with sharp features and hair that held an oil-slick shine even in firelight, and eyes that moved constantly — cataloguing, pricing, filing away. He wore his coat buttoned wrong on purpose, she suspected, because a man like this wanted you to notice the buttons.

"Perrin," he said. "And before you ask — yes, I knew him. No, I won't tell you how. And no, I don't like you."

"Gideon." She found, to her surprise, that she wasn't afraid. Perhaps the fear had been used up on the rim crossing. Or perhaps there was something about a man who announced his dislike upfront that put a body at ease; liars warmed up to you first. "You've got me at a disadvantage. You know three things about me and I know none about you."

"I know four things about you," Perrin said, settling onto a rock with the particular grace of someone who had spent years landing on things narrower than rocks. "One: you're a memory-thief, valley-trained, which means you're good and you hate it. Two: you're carrying more jars than any sane thief travels with, which means either greed or compulsion, and your face says compulsion. Three: you're following my sister's map."

The fire popped. Gideon kept her voice level. "Your sister's."

"My sister's," Perrin said, and for just a moment the constant motion in his eyes went still, and what was underneath was so raw that Gideon had to look at the fire instead. "Which brings us to four: you have something she died getting out of that mountain, and I have been following you for two days trying to decide whether to rob you or help you, and I'll be honest, it's still close."

"It's not close," Gideon said. "You dropped me a button. Thieves don't announce themselves to the people they're robbing."

"Good thieves do. It's the *why* of it that's still up for debate." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the oil-slick hair. "Here's my problem, valley-girl. My sister owed debts. Lots of them. She collected them too — favors, promises, small kindnesses banked against bad weather. And when she died — " his jaw worked — "when they killed her, every debt she held came due at once, scattered out to whoever she'd marked. I got mine." He tapped his own chest. "*Find the thief. Keep her alive to the mountain.* That's the favor I owe. That's the leash around my neck. And I resent it, deeply, personally, with my whole heart, because keeping thieves alive is *work*, and you look like a lot of work."

"So don't," Gideon said. "I didn't ask for your sister's leash or yours. I can find the mountain myself."

"No," Perrin said, "you really can't. The map shows roads. Roads are what the court *watches*. You'd be taken inside a week, and your jars with you, and then my sister died for a woman who lasted six days." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a magpie deciding whether something shiny was worth the dive. "Whereas I know the unroads. The magpie roads. The ways that go over and under and through while the court's riders stare at empty highways wondering where everyone went."

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. The fire cracked. Behind her ribs, her jars hummed, and she noticed — with a thief's reflexive attention — that Perrin's eyes flicked toward the sound, weighed it, and filed it away.

"What do you want?" she asked. "Not the leash. You. Everyone wants something, and guides who work for free are guides who are paid in ways you haven't spotted yet."

"There it is," Perrin said, with something that might have been approval. "Valley training. Good. Yes, I want payment. One thing. One memory, of my choosing, freely given — not stolen, not bought, *given*. Given memories keep their warmth. Stolen ones go cold in the jar. I want a warm one."

"That's a strange price."

"I'm a strange man." He shrugged. "I collect warm things. It's a magpie habit we never grow out of, those of us who shift. Buttons, coins, kindnesses, memories. We hoard what glows." His eyes went distant, briefly. "My sister used to say the world runs out of warm things faster than cold ones. She was usually right about depressing stuff like that."

Gideon turned the offer over. A memory, freely given, of his choosing. She thought of the Ferryman stories, of tolls paid in childhood summers, of the way the poor in the outer towns sold their pasts to eat — she'd seen the bottled-light villages yet, but the map promised them, and the map had been right about everything so far, which was its own kind of menace.

"One memory," she said. "Of your choosing. But I set the boundary: nothing that makes me *me*. Nothing from before I came to the valley. Those aren't mine to give — I don't even know what's in them anymore." This was almost true. There was a sealed jar in her hoard labeled in handwriting she refused to think about, and she had not opened it, and she would not, and the not-opening had become a load-bearing wall of her entire personality.

Perrin watched her say this with the particular attention of someone listening to what a person doesn't say.

"Noted," he said. "Nothing from before the valley. I choose later. Deal?"

"Deal."

He spat in his palm. She looked at it. He looked at her looking at it.

"We're not doing that," she said.

"We're absolutely doing that, it's traditional—"

"We are two adults entering a business arrangement, not children sealing a blood pact over frogs."

"The spit is *binding*, valley-girl. Memory-oaths need a medium. Spit's cheaper than blood and less dramatic than tears, though honestly tears hold better—" 

"Spit," Gideon said, "is disgusting," and shook his hand anyway, dry-palmed, and felt the oath settle somewhere behind her sternum with a faint warm click, like a jar lid seating home. Perrin grinned — a real grin this time, quick and crooked, gone as fast as it came.

"There. Done. Now you're stuck with me till the mountain."

"Wonderful," Gideon said. "I've always wanted a traveling companion who might turn into a bird and leave."

"Oh, I'd never leave." He began unpacking a bedroll from somewhere — she genuinely could not see where he'd been keeping it, and decided not to pursue the matter, as pursuing the matter of where magpies keep things was widely understood to be a road to madness. "Leaving's for people without debts. I'm the opposite of leaving. I'm *arriving*, repeatedly, at inconvenient moments, until the job's done."

He paused, and the firelight did something kind to his face, smoothing the sharpness down for a moment.

"She'd have liked you," he said, not looking at her. "My sister. She liked people who hated needing help. Said they were the only honest ones — everyone else pretends they don't mind."

Gideon didn't answer. The jars hummed behind her ribs. Somewhere north, past the dark line of the ridge, the mountain waited with its thirteen turning moons, and somewhere in her satchel a sealed jar with her mother's handwriting on it sat in the dark, patient as a debt.

* * *

They broke camp at gray dawn, and Gideon learned within the hour why they called them the magpie roads.

Perrin did not take paths. He took *implications* of paths — a deer trail that became a streambed that became a gap in a hedge that became, absurdly, the roofline of a fallen barn, walked heel-to-toe along the ridgepole as if this were a normal way for people to travel. He moved through the world the way water moves through cracks: finding every seam, every shortcut, every place where the land had forgotten to finish a thought.

"The court watches roads," he explained, hopping a fence without breaking stride, "because courts are slow things. Big things. A ledger the size of theirs can't chase a magpie. By the time they've written down where you were, you're somewhere you haven't been yet."

"That's not wisdom," Gideon said, "that's just being annoying at scale."

"Thank you," Perrin said sincerely.

At midmorning they crossed a field where crows had gathered — dozens of them, silent, watching. Perrin stopped. The crows stopped. For a long moment there was a conversation happening entirely in feather-angles and head-tilts, and then the crows rose as one and streamed east, and Perrin relaxed by a degree.

"Court riders passed here yesterday," he said. "Three of them. Hollow-eyed. They're casting wider than usual."

"Looking for the map?"

"Looking for *you*. Or for whoever carries my sister's theft." He glanced sideways at her. "How attached are you to all those jars? Asking professionally."

"Very."

"Hm." He said nothing else, but Gideon had spent a lifetime reading the silences of people who knew more than they said, and this silence had a shape, and the shape was *trouble*.

They walked. Toward noon the land opened into a long green valley-not-valley — a real one, with farms — and Perrin led her along its shadowed lip rather than down among the fields, and from that height Gideon saw the town.

It sat in the valley floor like a lamp collection: hundreds of small lights in windows, and every light a different color. Amber, rose, deep blue-green, gold. Not flame-colored. Not candle-colored. *Memory*-colored.

"Bottled light," Perrin said, watching her watch it. "The poor sell their pasts to the light-brokers, who sell them to the towns, who burn them in lamps. A wedding burns warmer than a birthday. A grief burns longest of all — slow, blue, lasts a whole winter if you ration it." He said it flatly, a recitation, but his feathers — the ones along his collarbone that never quite became skin — lifted slightly, the way a bird's do before a storm. "First summer light costs about a meal a day. Most families sell the father's army years first. Nobody misses those, supposedly."

Gideon said nothing. Her jars hummed. Forty-odd warmths against her ribs, and below her, a whole town burning other people's yesterdays to get through the night.

"That's what the court buys," Perrin said quietly. "That's the trade underneath everything. The mountain pays best, so the brokers feed it first. Every soul in that vault came up a road like this one, priced by weight." He looked at her, and for once both his eyes were still. "Still glad you asked what I want?"

"No," Gideon said honestly.

"Good. Honest. My sister would've liked you." He turned down off the ridge. "Come on. There's a bridge ahead, and a man on it, and you're going to hate him."

"Why?"

"Because he's fair," Perrin said, "and fairness is the cruelest thing there is when you're the one paying."

The bridge appeared at dusk — old stone, older than the farms, spanning a river that ran black and smooth as poured glass. And standing at its center, exactly where Perrin had promised, was a figure robed in the color of deep water, holding out one open palm.

Gideon stopped at the near end of the bridge. The jars against her ribs had gone very quiet, the way animals go quiet before an earthquake.

"Toll?" she called.

The Ferryman's hood tilted. When he spoke, his voice was the sound a river makes under ice.

"Memories," he said. "Freely given keeps the stone standing. Taken ones crack it. Choose what you will miss least — knowing, thief, that you are always wrong about that."

Gideon stood at the foot of the bridge with the evening coming down and forty stolen lives murmuring against her heart, and reached — slowly, the way you reach into your own chest — for the one memory she had always told herself she could spare.

A childhood summer. Warm grass. A river she couldn't quite picture anymore, even as she held it.

She held it out in her open palm, glowing amber-gold, and tried to believe she'd chosen well.
