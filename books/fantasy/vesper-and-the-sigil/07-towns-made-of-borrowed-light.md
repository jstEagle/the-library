## 7. Towns Made of Borrowed Light

Perrin had a theory about grief, and the theory was this: it traveled at the speed of a lie.

His sister had died telling one — *thirteen turns, Perrin, you'll know what to do* — and he had spent three days and most of his coin outrunning the truth of it, only to have the truth catch him at a crossroads in the shape of a woman with a thief's hands and a map that hummed when he got too close. So now he walked beside Gideon Okafor, guide by obligation, and told himself the arrangement was temporary.

She was quiet for the first hour out of the crossroads. He preferred her quiet. When she talked she asked questions, and questions were dangerous things to carry near a man with feathers under his skin.

"You're sulking," she said eventually.

"Magpies don't sulk."

"Magpies absolutely sulk. I've seen them fight their own reflections over a bottle cap."

"That was strategic," Perrin said. "The reflection had information."

Gideon made a sound that was almost a laugh and mostly not, and they walked on through country that grew stranger the farther they came from the valley. The grass here remembered being grazed. The fences leaned toward each other like old friends. And ahead, as the light went amber and then bruise-colored, a town appeared on the slope below them — a town that glowed.

Not torchlight. Not lamplight. The windows of the houses burned soft and shifting, gold going blue going green, each one a different color, each one flickering with something that looked almost like weather.

"Bottled memories," Perrin said, before she could ask. "That's how they light the outer towns now. Cheaper than oil, if you can stomach the price."

"What price?"

He gestured with his chin at the town. "Look at the windows. Really look."

She looked. He watched her look — watched the moment the flickering resolved itself into something recognizable, because every window held a different season of somebody's life. A wedding, maybe, burning gold in one house. A childhood summer, green-gold in another. A first snowfall, white-blue, guttering gently in a window where a family sat down to supper inside its glow.

"They sell them," Gideon said slowly. "Their own memories. To eat."

"To burn, mostly. Eating's the expensive version." Perrin shrugged, and felt his feathers ruffle along his forearms where no feathers were, the way they did when he was ashamed of his species' honesty about shiny things. "A memory burns longer than wood. A good one — a wedding, a birth — can light a house for a month. The poor sell their pasts to keep warm, and the rich buy them up and burn the best ones at parties so the whole room remembers being happy for an evening."

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's obscene."

"Yes," Perrin said. "Welcome to the outer world. Try not to say it so loudly; some of these people are proud of their lamps."

* * *

They came down into the town as full dark settled, and Perrin chose the tavern with the dimmest windows on purpose. A place that burned cheap memories was a place that hadn't stripped its people bare yet, and people who still had their pasts were people who might still be kind.

The tavern keeper was a broad woman named Osa who lit their corner table with a single small jar — a memory of someone's first day of school, by the smell of it, chalk dust and new leather satchel — and brought bread and stew without being asked twice.

"Travelers," she said, wiping her hands. "You'll be wanting news."

"We'll be wanting beds," Perrin said.

"News comes free with the beds." She leaned in. "There's a market tomorrow. Third of the month. Memory-brokers come up from the river towns. If you've anything to sell—"

"We're not selling," Gideon said, too fast.

Osa looked at her with the particular gentleness of a woman who had seen too many travelers go pale at the word *sell*. "No," she agreed. "Most folk who pass through aren't selling. Most folk who live here already did." She tapped the little jar of school-day light. "This one's mine. My first day. I kept the worst of it, mind — I remember my mother's face, remember the road, remember being frightened. But the shine of it, the part that made it worth having? That went into the jar twenty years ago when the winter took our goats and we'd have starved otherwise." She smiled, and the smile was real, but it sat on her face like furniture in an empty room. "I don't miss it. That's the trick of it. You can't miss what you can't quite recall."

She left them the bread and went back to her barrels, and Perrin watched Gideon turn the little jar of light slowly on the table between her hands, watching the chalk-dust gold of it swirl.

"This is what I do," she said quietly. It wasn't a question and wasn't quite a confession. "Back in the valley. I take memories from people who can't hold them anymore. Grief, mostly. I thought—" Her jaw tightened. "I thought I was helping."

"You probably were."

"I put them in jars." She looked up at him, and there was something raw under the thief's composure, something that had been rattling around since the valley rim. "Locked jars, sealed jars, jars people pay me to keep. And out here they burn them for light."

"Different economies," Perrin said. "Same jars."

"No. It isn't the same." She set the jar down with more force than necessary, and the school-day light shivered. "In the valley, magic is dead. What I take stays taken because nothing there can move. Out here—" she gestured at the glowing windows, the whole town burning other people's yesterdays — "out here everything is alive and for sale, and nobody asked any of them whether they wanted their childhoods turned into lamp oil."

"Now you're angry."

"I'm *not* angry."

"You're very angry," Perrin said, with the calm of long experience. "You get a line right here." He touched the space between his own eyebrows. "Like a bird before a storm. I'd know. I'm usually the storm."

* * *

The market opened with the sun, and Perrin regretted everything.

He had meant to skirt it. Markets were bad places for a magpie-shifter — too many bright things calling to the part of him that had never learned the difference between *want* and *need* — but the main road ran straight through the square, and the square was already filling when they came down from the tavern: stalls unshuttering, brokers hanging their wares on lines like laundry, and everywhere the soft colored glow of memories for sale, strung up in hundreds of glass jars that caught the morning and threw it back changed.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. It was genuinely, terribly beautiful, a square full of bottled dawns and first kisses and days at the sea, and the poor of the town moved through it touching the jars the way you'd touch the glass over a saint's picture.

"Stay close," Perrin murmured. "And don't touch anything. Especially don't touch anything that glows blue. Blue ones are griefs, and griefs stick."

"I know how jars work," Gideon said. "I'm a professional."

"A professional," Perrin said, "of the valley. Where the jars sit still. Out here a jar can be *hungry*, and the hungry ones are always the prettiest—"

He lost her within four minutes.

He found her again at a broker's stall at the far edge of the square, standing very still in front of a display line, and he knew before he reached her that something was wrong, because her stillness was the wrong kind. It was the stillness of a person looking at their own grave.

The stall belonged to a river-town broker, a fat comfortable man with rings on every finger, and his wares were finer than the rest — older jars, valley-made jars, heavy glass with wax seals stamped in a pattern Perrin didn't recognize but Gideon clearly did.

There were nine of them on the line. Each one glowed a different color. Each seal carried the same mark: a small pressed sigil, neat and precise, the work of a careful hand.

Gideon's hand.

"That one," she said to the broker, and her voice came out level, which Perrin recognized as an achievement, "the green one. Where did you get it?"

The broker smiled the smile of a man who smelled coin. "Valley goods. Rare, those. Dead-magic preservation, keeps the color true for decades. There's a collector in the river towns pays triple for valley seals—"

"*Where did you get it.*"

"Trade caravan, lady, six months back, I couldn't tell you—"

"Someone stole these." She wasn't touching the jars. Her hands hung at her sides, curled slightly, like claws trying to decide whether to be fists. "These were taken from my keeping. From a locked vault, behind wards, in a valley where nothing gets in or out without the wardens knowing."

"Then your wardens want firing," the broker said cheerfully, and reached for the green jar. "Now, are you buying or admiring? Because admiration's free but it blocks the light for paying customers—"

"How much."

"Gideon," Perrin said quietly.

"How much for all nine?"

"Lady, these are premium stock, valley-sealed, you're looking at—"

"How. Much."

He told her. It was most of what she carried and all of what Perrin carried besides, and she paid it without haggling, which Perrin knew for a fact she was good at, which meant she wasn't thinking, which meant the thing in her chest had gotten loose and was driving.

She took the nine jars down off the line herself, one at a time, cradling each like a sleeping child, and the broker counted coins with fat happy fingers, and the crowd flowed around them, and above them the town glowed with everybody's sold and burning pasts.

Only when they were clear of the square, up the far lane where the light of the market couldn't reach, did she stop and set the jars down in the grass with terrible gentleness, and stand over them shaking.

"He sold them," she said. "Somebody *sold* them. Somebody got into my vault — my vault, Perrin, I built the locks myself — and took nine jars of other people's griefs and carried them out and *sold them to a man with rings*." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Do you understand what's in these? A widow's grief. A father's. A woman who lost a son in the flood year. I took them because carrying them was killing those people, and I swore — I *swore* — they'd be safe. And now they're merchandise. Now they're *lamp oil*."

"Somebody knew the valley," Perrin said. "Somebody knew your locks."

"Nobody knows my locks."

"Then somebody knew *you*." He said it carefully, watching her face. "Thief to thief: nobody cracks a vault cold. They either had the key, or they had the maker of the key."

She stared at him. The anger in her face hit the wall of that sentence and stopped, and behind it something colder moved in, something that looked at the nine glowing jars in the grass and saw not stolen goods but evidence.

"The wardens," she said slowly. "The elders demanded I destroy the map. They said it was court-touched, dangerous. And then a week later my vault is emptied and my seals are on sale in a river-market two hundred miles away." She crouched, touched one jar — just the glass, not the seal — and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "They knew what I was carrying. They knew what I *was*. Maybe they always did."

"Or," Perrin said, because fair was fair, "it's coincidence, and caravans get robbed, and the world is mostly stupid instead of sinister."

"Is that what you believe?"

"I believe," Perrin said, "that magpies survive by assuming everything shiny is bait until proven otherwise. And right now, lady, everything around us is very shiny indeed." He nodded at the jars. "Including that map in your pack. Which has been humming since we entered this town. Did you notice? It doesn't usually hum."

She went still. Then, slowly, she shouldered the jars — all nine, wrapped in her cloak, heavier than glass had any right to be — and pulled the map from her pack.

The ink had rearranged itself. Where the road to the mountain had been drawn plain and certain since the valley, there was now a second line, branching off before the toll-bridge, curving back east through country they had just crossed — and at the end of the branch, small and precise in ink that gleamed wet as though freshly written, a mark Perrin recognized from the broker's stall.

The valley seal. Her seal.

Below it, in handwriting that was not the dying stranger's and was not Gideon's and was old-fashioned and careful and heartbreakingly familiar in a way Perrin could not name, three words had been added:

*Follow the jars.*

Gideon read it twice. When she looked up, the anger was gone — burned off, burned down, gone to ash — and what was left underneath was worse, and truer, and much more afraid.

"Someone's been emptying my vault," she said, "and leaving me a trail."

* * *

They walked out of the town before noon, nine jars riding in Gideon's pack like passengers, the map humming its new direction, and Perrin flew a wide circle overhead — because sometimes a man needed to be a bird about things, and because from the air he could see what the ground hid: the road east, winding back the way they'd come, and on it, far off but unmistakable, a rider moving at a pace that had nothing casual in it.

Hollow-eyed. Even at that distance, Perrin's sharp eyes caught the wrongness of him — a man-shaped absence, riding toward the town they'd just left, toward the market, toward the broker with the rings.

Toward questions about valley seals.

Perrin dropped out of the sky, landed, and shifted, and Gideon took one look at his face and stopped walking.

"What."

"Hunters ride for that market," he said. "Court hunters, by the look. They'll reach the broker by nightfall, and the broker will tell them a woman bought nine valley jars this morning and paid without haggling." He watched the color leave her face. "So. New plan, same direction, much faster: we follow the jars east, and we pray whoever left this trail is kinder than the things following it."

Gideon adjusted the pack on her shoulders. For a moment she looked back at the town, at its hundred borrowed lights flickering in the windows of houses full of people who had sold their brightest hours to survive — and her expression, Perrin thought, was not pity anymore.

It was promise.

"Then we walk," she said, and walked.
