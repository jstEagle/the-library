## 8. The First Gate

The map had been leading them downhill for two days, which Gideon considered a personal insult. Mountains were supposed to be climbed. That was the entire point of mountains. But the ink kept rearranging itself into a single winding line that descended through pine forest and out onto a shelf of grey stone above a river valley, and when she tilted the parchment to catch the morning light, the line pulsed once, patiently, like a finger tapping a table.

"It knows where it's going," Perrin said from his perch on a boulder, in the tone of a man reporting bad weather.

"Maps don't know things."

"This one does. Also, I know things, and I'm telling you: we're close to the bridge." He said *bridge* the way other people said *plague*.

Gideon rolled up the map and tucked it against her ribs, where it sat warm as a sleeping cat. It had stopped burning her since they'd crossed the rim — or rather, it had transferred its affections. These days it only grew hot when she thought about turning back, which was a level of passive aggression she hadn't known parchment could achieve.

"You could tell me what's on this bridge," she said. "Since you're so full of knowing."

"I could," Perrin agreed. He hopped down from the boulder — he always hopped; walking was apparently beneath him — and fell into step beside her, hands laced behind his head. "But then you'd have that look on your face. The one where you're doing sums."

"I don't do sums."

"You do sums. You did sums about me. I watched you decide how many memories I was worth versus how much trouble." He grinned at her sideways. "For the record, I'm worth more than trouble. Barely."

The trees thinned. The stone shelf broadened. And there it was.

The river ran fast and green below them, maybe forty feet down, loud enough that Gideon felt it in her back teeth. Spanning it was a bridge of black timber, old and sound, with no railing on either side — just a flat road across empty air. At its near end stood a small house built of the same dark wood, with a chimney smoking cheerfully and a garden of white flowers that Gideon's eyes kept sliding off of, the way they slid off a word you'd read ten times without understanding.

And on the porch, in a rocking chair, sat an enormous man knitting.

"That's him," Perrin said. "The Ferryman."

"He's *knitting*."

"He knits. It's part of the whole thing. Don't comment on the knitting. Whatever you do, don't ask what he's making."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were absolutely going to. You have the face. The *what is he making* face." Perrin stopped at the edge of the shelf and did not go one step further. "This is where I remind you that I owe your dead stranger a favor, not a death. Bridges like this one take payment, and the payment is—" he wiggled his fingers near his temple, "—the personal kind."

"I gathered that from the name."

"Gathered it from the name," Perrin muttered. "She gathers everything. Fine. Go gather." He sat down on a flat rock, produced a dried strip of some unidentifiable meat from somewhere inside his coat, and began chewing with the studied indifference of a man who intended to watch every second of this.

* * *

The Ferryman looked up as she approached, and Gideon revised her estimate of his size upward. He wasn't fat so much as *substantial*, the way a doorframe is substantial, the way a mountain is substantial. His beard was grey and braided into three plaits, each tied off with a different colored thread — and she recognized, with a small cold start, that the threads matched the yarn pooling in his lap. He was knitting something long and narrow and many-colored, and it went over the side of the porch and down the steps and coiled off toward the bridge itself, disappearing between the planks.

A scarf? A rope? A very ambitious sock?

Don't ask, she reminded herself.

"Traveler," the Ferryman said. His voice was low and warm, like a banked fire. "One crossing?"

"One crossing."

"One way?" His needles clicked. "Or do you expect to come back?"

The question landed heavier than it should have. Gideon thought of the map against her ribs, warm and insistent. "I expect to come back."

"Mm." He rocked once, twice. "They all say that. Some of them even mean it." He set the knitting down across his knees — carefully, the way you'd set down a sleeping child — and folded his enormous hands atop it. "You know my terms."

"Perrin mentioned. Memories."

"Memories," the Ferryman agreed. "Not the dregs. Not the things you'd trade anyway. The toll is a memory you would rather keep. That's the whole arrangement. That's why the bridge holds." He nodded at the span behind him. "Every plank is paid for. Every board remembers somebody's wedding day, somebody's mother's voice, somebody's last afternoon before a war. A bridge made of things people loved doesn't rot. Nothing loves rot enough to hold it up."

Gideon looked at the bridge again, and now that she knew what to look for, she couldn't stop seeing it: faint variations in the wood grain, like faces in a fire. One plank seemed almost to shimmer with sunlight through leaves. Another had a darkness in it that might have been rain on a window.

"How much?" she asked. "For one crossing."

"For one person, one way, keeping her return in mind?" The Ferryman's eyes — brown, kind, and utterly without mercy — assessed her. "One memory. Fairly large. Something from childhood, I think. Childhood weighs more. It hasn't been worn smooth yet."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk along the river until you find a ford, which is nine days downstream, through country I won't describe because describing it would be cruel, and you'll lose those days while your moons run down." He picked the knitting back up. "Thirteen turns, isn't it? I can hear it ticking on you. Everyone on a deadline ticks."

Her hand went to the map before she could stop it.

"There it is," the Ferryman said softly. "Yes. I know that seal. Sit down, traveler. Let's talk about what you'll pay."

* * *

There was a second chair on the porch, smaller, wooden, clearly meant for exactly this purpose. Gideon sat in it and felt absurd, like a customer at a shop whose currency was herself.

"How does it work?" she asked. "The taking."

"Gently, if you let it. Badly, if you fight it." He set his needles down. "I'll show you what I can see — everyone carries their memories where I can see them, like fruit on a tree. You pick one. You hand it over. You walk across. On the far side, you'll notice it's gone the way you notice a tooth is gone: the tongue keeps going to the space."

Gideon had stolen hundreds of memories. She knew precisely what removal felt like from the outside — the jar going light, the grief settling into glass like sediment. She had never once been on the inside of the transaction.

"What happens to them?" she asked. "The memories you collect."

"They become the bridge." He said it simply, without drama. "Somebody's first snowfall is holding up the third plank. Somebody's wedding is under the middle arch, bearing the most weight, because weddings are strong. Your memory will work too. They always do." He leaned forward. "Now. Let's see the tree."

It was the strangest sensation of her life, stranger than the rim-crossing, stranger than the whispering jars. She felt him *look* — not at her but through her, into the place where she kept her hoard. And her hoard, her precious professional inventory, flared open like a market stall at dawn.

She felt him pass over the widow's grief (too heavy, wrong flavor), over the baker's pride, over a soldier's homecoming, over a hundred small thefts she'd carried so long they'd gone smooth as river stones.

Then he stopped.

"Ah," he said.

"Don't," Gideon said, though she didn't know yet what she was refusing.

"The summer," the Ferryman said. "The one with the river."

And it rose in her unbidden, because being shown your own memory is like being handed your own heart: you cannot pretend it isn't yours.

She was eight. Maybe nine. There was a river — not this one, a gentler one, brown-gold and slow, the kind of river that moved like syrup. There was a willow tree whose branches made a green room, a secret room, and she and someone else had dragged an old crate under it and called it a house. Someone else. A girl. Her own height, her own laugh, two pairs of sandals drying on the bank, side by side, one pair scuffed, one pair newer because its owner was more careful—

There was a game they played where they named the fish. Every fish got a name and a job. The big lazy carp was the Mayor. A darting minnow was the Mayor's extremely fast secretary. They spent whole afternoons on civil administration. There was a thunderstorm one evening where they sat under the willow and watched lightning stitch the far hills and weren't afraid, weren't afraid at all, because the green room held, because they held each other, because that was the whole point of a sister—

"Sister," Gideon said aloud, and her voice came out cracked down the middle.

The Ferryman's needles had gone still. "That's the one," he said quietly. "I'm sorry. It usually is."

"No." She stood up. Her legs felt borrowed. "No, pick another. Pick anything else. Take the soldier's homecoming, take the baker's—"

"The toll is a memory you would rather keep," the Ferryman said, and there was no cruelty in it at all, which somehow made it worse. "That's the price. If I took the ones you didn't want, the bridge would fall by winter."

"But I don't even understand it." She was gripping the porch rail. "I don't remember having a sister. I stole that summer from — from someone, years ago, I must have, that's what I do, I *take* — and I never even opened the jar, I just carried it, and now you tell me—" Her voice failed.

"Traveler." The Ferryman waited until she looked at him. "I ferry souls and memories both, and I've learned to tell them apart. That summer isn't stolen goods. Stolen goods sit in a person like coins in a pocket. That one sits like a scar. Like something that grew there." He paused. "Like something that was put there so you wouldn't lose it entirely."

The map, against her ribs, went suddenly, fiercely warm.

Perrin, down on his rock, had stopped chewing.

* * *

She tried to bargain. She wanted it noted, for posterity, that she tried.

"Half," she said. "Take half the summer. Take the storm night, keep the fish-naming. Take the Mayor."

"The memory comes whole or it doesn't come," the Ferryman said. "You can't amputate an afternoon."

"Then take a different one. Take my mother's face. I barely use it."

Something flickered across his broad face — pity, or its professional cousin. "Child. If I take your mother's face, you'll spend the rest of this journey trying to remember what you gave up, and it will eat you alive, because a face is a question and questions gnaw. But that summer—" he tapped his chest, right over the heart, "—that one is an answer. People survive losing answers much better than they survive losing questions. Trust an old man who has watched several thousand people walk away from this porch."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to comfort. It was meant to be useful. Comfort is extra." He resumed rocking. "So. Will you pay?"

Gideon looked at the bridge. In the middle distance, the sun caught the water below it, and the river ran on, indifferent, loud, going somewhere without asking anyone's permission.

Thirteen turns. Twelve and a half now, maybe less. A girl sold beneath the mountain, fading moon by moon, whoever she was. And a summer under a willow with a sister whose name Gideon could not find anywhere in herself, a sister-shaped hole wearing a memory like a coat.

If she gave it up, she'd forget why the willow mattered. She'd forget there was a willow at all. She'd cross the bridge lighter and emptier and she wouldn't even know what she was missing — except, the Ferryman had said, the tongue keeps going to the space.

"Will I feel it go?" she asked.

"Yes."

"All at once?"

"Like a tide going out," he said. "You'll feel the edge of it longest. The edges are what hurt. The middle goes easy, the way sleep does."

Gideon closed her eyes. She went down into the green room one last time — deliberately, slowly, a thief cataloguing a final inventory. The Mayor carp, gold and unhurried. The secretary minnow, all business. The crate-house with its moss roof. Two pairs of sandals. The laugh that matched hers exactly, pitched half a note higher, the laugh of someone more careful, someone who checked the riverbed before jumping, someone who —

Someone whose name was just there, just at the surface, just about to break through like a fish rising—

"A-"

The Ferryman touched her forehead with one enormous gentle thumb, and the tide went out.

* * *

It didn't hurt the way she expected. It hurt the way a numb limb hurts coming back — inverted, wrong-directional, an ache for something present becoming an ache for something absent. She felt the edges first, exactly as promised: the smell of river mud thinning, the green of the willow going grey, the laugh sliding out of reach like a fish she'd reached for and missed. The middle went easy. The middle went like sleep.

Then it was done, and she was standing on a porch holding a cup of tea she didn't remember being handed, and the Ferryman was watching her with sad brown eyes, and there was a space in her.

She ran her tongue over it, metaphorically. Again. Again. The tongue keeps going to the space.

"Do you know what I took?" she asked. Her voice sounded fine. That seemed obscene.

"No," the Ferryman said. "And neither do you. That's the arrangement." He nodded toward the bridge. "Third plank from the near end, left side. Come see."

She walked to the edge of the porch and looked, and there — she couldn't have said how she knew, but she knew — was her summer. The grain of the wood held sunlight-through-leaves. Under her palm, faintly, the plank was warm. Somewhere in it, a carp named the Mayor went on being gold and unhurried, forever, carrying strangers across a river, and it would never know it had once been an afternoon, and neither, now, would she.

"It's a good plank," the Ferryman said behind her. "Strong. It'll hold people for a hundred years." A pause, the click of needles resuming. "That's not nothing, traveler. Very few of us get to be useful after we're gone."

Gideon wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, the way you'd wipe down a counter. Businesslike. Done.

"Cross when you're ready," the Ferryman said. "And traveler — the woman who sealed your map. She crossed here too. Going the other direction. She paid with something small and smiled the whole time, and I've ferried enough souls to know a smile like that is the most expensive thing there is." His needles clicked. "Whatever she sent you to do, she thought it was worth more than a smile. Make of that what you will."

* * *

The crossing took ninety seconds and lasted approximately a decade. Without railings, the bridge demanded a particular quality of attention; Gideon fixed her eyes on the far bank and walked, and beneath her feet her summer bore her weight without complaint, steady as anything, and she hated it a little for being so good at its new job.

On the far side, Perrin was waiting. He'd come across ahead of her — she hadn't even seen him pay, hadn't seen what he'd given, and when she glanced at him he was wearing the specific blankness of a man standing next to a fresh gap.

Neither of them asked. That was the etiquette of the bridge, apparently: you didn't ask what the other person had left on it.

They walked. Pine forest again, climbing now, the map tugging gently northeastward. After twenty minutes of silence, Perrin cleared his throat.

"You've got a limp," he said.

"I do not have a limp."

"You favor your left leg. Just slightly. Like it used to hurt." He looked over at her. "Does your left leg used-to-hurt?"

Gideon stopped walking. She turned her attention inward, down to the space, the gap, the tongue-returning-to-the-space — and found, around the edges of the absence, a fringe of things she couldn't account for. A flinch at the sound of running water. An instinct to check riverbanks before approaching them. And yes — a faint, sourceless conviction that her left knee had once been badly scraped, in a summer she could not, for the life of her, locate.

"I don't know," she said honestly.

Perrin studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him quiet in a way he rarely was. "How many times are you going to pay like that?" he asked. "Before there's not enough left of you to finish the trip?"

Gideon looked at the map in her hands. The ink had rearranged itself again — the winding line now ended at a mark shaped like a small open jar, and beneath it, in ink that was definitely, definitely new, three words had appeared:

*You knew her.*

Behind them, faint across the water, she could hear the Ferryman's needles clicking, patient as tide.
