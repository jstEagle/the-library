## 9. Echoes in Her Hands

The river was called the Sigh, according to Perrin, because it made that sound all night — a long exhale of water over stones, like something enormous and patient settling down to sleep.

Gideon hated it.

She couldn't have said why. That was the whole problem, actually. She stood on the bank where they'd stopped to make camp, and her body had opinions her mind couldn't account for: shoulders drawn up around her ears, hands curled into fists she hadn't authorized, a cold crawling feeling along the back of her neck as though someone were standing behind her with a bucket.

"You're doing the thing again," Perrin said from the fire he was building. He said it the way other people commented on weather. "The flinching thing."

"I'm not flinching."

"You flinched when I said *river*. You flinched when you looked at it. You just flinched now, when I said you were flinching. It's a very flinchy performance."

Gideon turned her back on the water with great dignity and sat down by the fire. "I don't care for rivers. Plenty of people don't care for rivers."

"Sure." Perrin arranged his sticks with the fussy precision of a bird building a nest, which, to be fair, was exactly what he was. "And plenty of people don't care for rivers so much that their whole body goes rigid and they start breathing like a woman being chased."

"Is there any more of that bread?"

"There's half a loaf, and you're changing the subject, and I'm letting you, because I'm generous." He passed it over. His eyes stayed on her a moment longer than the joke required — quick, black, clever eyes, the kind that missed nothing, which was one of the many things Gideon found insufferable about him. "You know what's strange about you, thief?"

"So many things. Pick one."

"You talk about your own memories like they're inventory. 'I don't care for rivers.' Not 'rivers frighten me.' Not 'something happened.' You say it the way a merchant says 'we're out of flour.'" He struck his flint, and sparks jumped, and firelight climbed his sharp face. "Most people's pasts are attached to them. Yours sounds like stock you haven't counted lately."

Gideon chewed the bread and did not answer, mostly because she had no answer that wasn't terrifying.

Here was what she knew: three days ago, at the Ferryman's bridge, she had paid a toll. The Ferryman took memories the way other toll-keepers took coin, and the price of crossing had been a childhood summer — one specific summer, though even that was hard to hold onto now. She remembered *that* she'd paid it. She remembered the shape of the moment: the Ferryman's long grey hands, the sensation of lifting something out of herself, the way the world had gone briefly thin, like paper held up to the sun.

She could not remember the summer itself.

That was how it worked. She was a professional; she understood the mechanics better than anyone alive. When you took a memory from someone, you took the whole of it — not just the facts but the feel of it, the weight, the way it sat in a person and colored everything near it. The widow whose grief she'd stolen hadn't merely forgotten her husband's death. She'd forgotten that grief had ever lived in her, the way a room forgets a fire once the ashes are swept.

Gideon had always thought of this as clean work. Tidy. A service, honestly — she took what people couldn't hold, and she carried it, and she was very good at carrying.

She had not considered, until now, what it would be like to be the room.

* * *

They walked upstream the next morning, because the map wanted them north and the map had never once cared about Gideon's preferences. The Sigh kept pace beside them, chattering over its stones, and Gideon kept her eyes fixed firmly ahead and pretended the sound wasn't scraping along her nerves like a nail.

Perrin, naturally, noticed.

"You walk like the ground owes you money," he observed, hopping a fallen log with the easy grace of someone whose bones were only borrowed from a boy-shaped arrangement. In his true form he was a magpie, glossy and insolent, and sometimes Gideon suspected the human shape was the disguise and the insolence was the truth. "Left side of the trail. Always left side. You've done it since the bridge."

"Have I?"

"Every campsite, every path. You put me between yourself and open water without ever appearing to notice you've done it." He glanced back at the river, then at her. "You know why, I take it."

"I told you. I don't care for rivers."

"Mm." He let it go, which was somehow worse than pressing. Perrin had a magpie's relationship with shiny things, and apparently her gaps qualified. He circled them, came back, worried at them. "What else is missing? Since the toll. And don't say nothing, because I watched you spend twenty minutes yesterday trying to remember whether you like honey."

"I was deciding."

"You were *grieving*, and you didn't know what for. There's a difference, and it's horrible." He said it lightly, but his feathers — the ones at his collar that never quite lay flat — had risen. "You looked at that honey like a woman at a grave."

Gideon stopped walking.

It wasn't anger, exactly, that moved through her. It was something colder and more precise. She turned and looked at the river — forced herself to look at it, white water and grey stone, entirely ordinary — and reached inward, the way she'd done ten thousand times in her trade, feeling along the shelves of herself for the thing that should be there.

There was a hole. Of course there was a hole; she'd sold the contents knowingly. But here was the part she hadn't understood until this moment, standing in the sun with water-sound in her ears: the hole had edges, and the edges remembered. Some part of her still flinched at rivers. Some part of her still loved honey, or had loved it, or had been taught to love it by someone whose face was gone. The feelings remained like footprints in a house after the family has moved out — evidence of living, with no one left inside to claim it.

"I paid a summer," she said quietly. "One summer. That's all."

"And summers are load-bearing," Perrin said. "That's the trick nobody tells you about memories. They're not separate little jars. They're threads, and pull one out and the whole weave shifts." He kicked a pebble into the water and watched it vanish. "My sister used to say—" He stopped. The sentence died so completely that Gideon looked over sharply.

"What?"

"Nothing. Old habit. She's dead; I keep quoting her anyway." He shrugged, too fast, and started walking again. "Come on. The map wants us north, and the map has never once cared about our preferences."

He'd stolen her line. She almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

That night the dreams were wrong.

Not nightmares — Gideon had survived enough of her own trade to be nearly immune to nightmares, which tended to be crude things, all teeth and falling. These were subtler. These were absences shaped like presences.

She dreamed of a kitchen she had never seen, except that some animal part of her clearly *had* seen it, because in the dream she knew where the third floorboard creaked and which cabinet stuck and that the window over the basin caught the evening light in a way that made washing dishes almost bearable. She dreamed of a voice singing something with a rising, looping melody, and she woke with the tune in her mouth and no words to put under it, humming a lullaby to an empty dark like a fool.

She dreamed of small shoes. Two pairs of them, side by side by a door, one pair scuffed at the toes, one pair newer, and in the dream she understood with total certainty that the scuffed pair was hers, and then she woke and did not understand anything at all, because she had never owned small shoes, or she had, or —

She sat up in the cold. The fire had burned to a red eye. Across the embers, Perrin slept in his bird-shape, tucked into a hollow of his own folded wings, and the Sigh went on sighing, and Gideon pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and took inventory.

Her name: Gideon Okafor. Present.

Her trade: memory-thief, seventeen years, self-taught, valley-bound. Present.

Her mother's face: 

She reached for it and found a shelf. The shelf was not empty — there were things on it, impressions, a smell of oil and cloth, the sense of hands braiding hair with more efficiency than tenderness — but the face itself, the actual face, was gone. Had it been gone before the bridge? She tried to remember when she'd last seen it and found the question slippery, eel-like, diving away into dark water.

Had she ever seen it?

"That's not possible," she whispered to the fire. "I would know. I'd know if I'd sold my own mother's face. I keep records."

She did keep records. Every theft logged in a ledger she carried against her ribs, every jar labeled in her own hand, dates and donors and contents, a thief's meticulous bookkeeping. Tomorrow — today, soon — she would sit down with that ledger and reconcile it against herself, item by item, and find out exactly what the Ferryman had taken and whether the edges of the hole matched.

The thought steadied her the way only procedure could. She lay back down. She did not sleep again, but she stopped humming eventually, which was progress.

* * *

The reconciliation happened two mornings later, on a flat grey rock above the river while Perrin fished — badly, theatrically, complaining the entire time that fish had no respect for a predator of his caliber.

Gideon opened the ledger across her knees.

The entries marched backward through her life in her own careful hand. *Widow Sefu, grief, jar seven.* *Cooper Anselm, first love, jar twelve.* *Miller Odile, the drowning of her son — returned at her request, no fee.* On and on, years of it, a career laid out in ink.

She checked each entry against herself. The cooper's first love: yes, she still carried it, a warm amber weight, a boy named Tam laughing in an orchard that wasn't hers. The miller's sorrow: present, heavy as wet sand. Entry by entry, the ledger and the woman agreed.

Until the early pages.

The earliest entries were different. The handwriting was the same — unmistakably hers — but the labels were wrong. Not names of donors. Just fragments. *Summer, age nine, the swimming hole.* *A song, wordless.* *Two pairs of shoes.* *Mother —*

The last word trailed off into a scratch, as if the pen had been snatched away mid-letter.

Gideon stared at it. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and unpleasant.

These weren't thefts. She knew her own work; these pages lacked the donor notes, the fees, the tidy professionalism of the rest. These were — what? Notes toward something? Memories she'd written down before losing them? But she'd never lost a memory in her life. Taking was her nature; keeping was her discipline. She had never once misplaced so much as a stranger's headache.

Unless.

Unless the holes weren't only the Ferryman's making. Unless there had been holes already, old ones, foundational ones, and the ledger was her own past self leaving markers at the edges of them — *here, something was dug out; here, the ground gives way* — the way miners' wives tied ribbons to unstable beams.

"Thief." Perrin's voice, closer than expected. He'd come up behind her, dripping line forgotten in one hand, and he was looking not at her face but at the page. Magpie eyes. Nothing got past them. "Those aren't job records."

"No."

"They're a map." He sat down beside her, uninvited, unrepentant. "Of you. Somebody drew a map of you and hid it in your own handwriting, and you've been carrying it against your ribs for years without reading it."

"It's my handwriting," Gideon said. It came out smaller than she intended.

"Or somebody who knew your hand very well indeed." He was quiet a moment. For once he didn't make a joke, and the absence of the joke rang louder than any quip. Then, gently, in the tone of a man handing someone bad news wrapped in kindness: "How many of you is missing, Gideon? Honestly. Count."

She tried to count.

She started with the obvious: the summer, gone to the Ferryman, edges raw. The mother's face, gone to — somewhere, sometime, nobody. The lullaby, present as melody, absent as meaning. The shoes. The swimming hole. The reason her body braced at the sound of water, the reason she slept on her left side with her back to walls, the reason she could not abide — she discovered, probing — the smell of wax seals, which was absurd, which was nothing, which was a hole wearing the shape of a fear.

The holes weren't scattered. That was the horror of it, the true horror, the kind that arrived without drama and simply sat down beside you like Perrin had. The holes had a pattern. They clustered. Around a kitchen. Around a door with shoes beside it. Around a woman's hands and a song and a season of water. Around, if she followed the negative space honestly, all the way down — around whatever had come before the valley, before the trade, before Gideon Okafor decided to become a person made entirely of other people's leftovers.

"I built myself out of stolen goods," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "Every piece of me I'm proud of — the patience, the steady hands, the way I can carry anything — I learned it carrying other people's lives. And underneath it there's supposed to be a mine, or a girl, or a — something. And someone has been in there ahead of me. Digging. Long before the Ferryman. Long before the map."

Perrin said nothing. He was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him — stripped of mockery, stripped even of curiosity, just open and grim and present.

"The journey's supposed to reveal things," she went on. "That's what maps do. But it's revealing me by subtraction. Every mile, another gap. I pay a toll, I lose a thread, and the weave shifts, and I find out the weave was thinner than I thought. I'm being erased, Perrin. From the outside in. And the worst part—" Her breath caught. She made herself say it. "The worst part is I think the erasure started before we ever left the valley. I think I've been disappearing for years, and I called it competence."

The river sighed below them. She didn't flinch this time, but only because she was watching for it, holding herself rigid, a woman guarding a house whose doors no longer locked.

Perrin picked up the ledger carefully, the way he handled eggs and other fragile valuable things, and read the scratched final entry aloud. "*Mother—*" He looked up. "Whoever wrote this wanted whoever found it to go looking. That's what unfinished sentences are. They're hooks." His black eyes glinted, and for once the glint was aimed outward, at the world, at the road ahead, rather than at her wounds. "So we go looking. North. Wherever the map says. Because I'll tell you what I know about missing things, thief — I've lost a sister, and I can promise you the not-knowing is worse than any knowing. Even the terrible ones."

Gideon closed the ledger and held it against her ribs, where it had always lived, and felt it differently now — not a record of her work but a wound dressed in ink.

"North," she agreed.

But that night, as they packed camp, Perrin paused at the edge of the firelight and looked back at her with his head cocked, bird-curious and suddenly solemn.

"One more thing," he said. "Back at the market town — the one with the bottled lights. I saw a stall there selling memories by the taste. Cheap stuff, mostly, but the dealer had a nose like nothing I've ever seen. Could tell a memory's origin from one sip. Where it was made. Who stamped it." He hesitated. "If you want to know who's been digging in you — who left those holes, and why — that's the man to ask. If you've got the stomach to let him taste what's left."

Gideon looked at the darkening river, at the country beyond it full of strangers who bought and sold the stuff of souls, and felt the holes in herself shift like water finding its level.

"Where do I find him?" she asked.
