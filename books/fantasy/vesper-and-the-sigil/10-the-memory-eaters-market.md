## 10. The Memory-Eaters' Market

The market found them before they found it.

They had been walking since the second moon rose — Perrin insisting they take the long way around a hill he refused to name, Gideon refusing to ask why because asking why was how you got stories out of Perrin, and stories from Perrin were like coins from a miser's fist: technically possible, deeply unpleasant for everyone involved.

Then the road began to smell of candle smoke and old paper, and the dark ahead of them filled up with light that had no source, and Perrin said, "Ah," in the tone of a man who has just remembered an appointment he'd hoped to die before keeping.

"Ah what?" said Gideon.

"The market." He pulled his collar up. "Rules before we go in. One: don't taste anything offered to you. Two: don't tell anyone your real name, or any name, or really speak at all if you can manage it. Three: if anyone offers you a bargain that sounds too good—"

"It's too good."

"No, it's *accurate*. That's worse. The things here are exactly what they claim to be. That's what makes them dangerous." He glanced at her sidelong. "You'll fit right in."

"I'm choosing to take that as a compliment."

"You would."

* * *

The Memory-Eaters' Market did not sit on the land so much as perch on it, the way a crow perches on a fence post: lightly, briefly, with an air of having been there longer than the fence. It filled a hollow between two hills that Gideon would have sworn, walking past on the other side of the ridge, contained nothing but bracken and stones. Now the hollow blazed. Stalls of black wood crowded together under lanterns of colored glass, and every lantern burned without flame — lit instead by something inside the glass that moved like smoke thinking hard thoughts.

And the people. The people were the reason Perrin wanted her silent.

They looked ordinary at first glance — travelers, traders, a woman selling roasted chestnuts, a man arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the freshness of fish that Gideon strongly suspected had never been alive. But when she looked twice, she saw the seams. A merchant whose eyes were the flat gray of a jar left empty too long. A woman laughing at a joke with someone else's laugh — literally someone else's; Gideon caught the echo of a second voice underneath hers, a man's chuckle worn like a borrowed coat. Children ran between the stalls playing a game whose rules changed whenever an adult came near, and their laughter had the strange doubled quality of something recorded and replayed by hearts that hadn't yet learned to make their own.

"They eat memories," Gideon said quietly. "That's not a figure of speech, is it."

"Best not to think of it as eating," Perrin said. "Think of it as... drinking. Eating leaves bones. Drinking leaves the glass. Most of them prefer to leave the glass standing, because a broken glass can't come back and buy more." He nodded toward a stall where a fat man in purple was weeping gently into his cup while a dealer patted his hand and poured him another. "Repeat customers are the whole business model."

"And I'm supposed to find the court's location here."

"You're supposed to *ask*. What happens after you ask is between you and whatever's left of your evening." He stopped at the mouth of the main lane and turned to face her, and for once there was no mockery in his magpie-bright face, only a careful, practiced seriousness. "Gideon. Listen to me. These people can taste a memory the way you or I taste wine — one sip and they know the vintage, the soil, the year, whether the gardener wept over the vines. If even one of them tastes something of yours that touches the court..." He didn't finish. He didn't have to. She finished it herself, silently: *the court will know you're coming.*

"So I shouldn't let anyone taste anything."

"So you should let exactly one person taste exactly one thing, and you should choose him the way you'd choose a surgeon. Slowly, and with a knife hidden somewhere on your person."

"Do I get a knife?"

"You get me. Which is better, because I'm sharper and I complain less."

"That last part is demonstrably false."

"See? Sharp."

* * *

They walked the lanes.

It should have been horrifying. It was, in places — the stall where memories were sold by weight, the little brass scales with a jar of childhood on one pan and a stack of coins on the other, the dealer calling out prices like a fishwife (*first kisses! first kisses, barely used!*) — but there was also, uncomfortably, warmth in it. An old eater sat by a brazier telling stories to a circle of listeners, and each story made the listeners gasp and clutch each other, and Gideon realized after a moment that the stories weren't his. He was pouring other people's lives into the fire like oil, giving away, freely, the memories he'd drunk over two hundred years, because a memory hoarded goes stale but a memory told stays warm. The listeners knew. They kept coming back anyway. Maybe because of it.

A stall sold nothing but lullabies, arranged by language, by century, by whether the mother had been happy. A boy of maybe ten sat behind it, cross-legged, solemn as a bishop, and when Perrin paused to listen to one — just one, drifting faintly from an open jar — the boy said, "That one's from before the war. You can tell because nobody's afraid in it."

Perrin paid for it. He didn't haggle, which Gideon noted and filed away under *things to ask about later, possibly never.* He held the little jar cupped in both hands like a bird with a broken wing, and his face did something complicated, and Gideon kindly pretended not to see.

"The court," she said, when they'd moved on. "Who here would know its roads?"

"Not know. Have tasted." Perrin tucked the jar into his coat, over his heart, which was either sentiment or storage and with Perrin was usually both. "Anyone who's eaten deep in this region has tasted something that passed through the mountain's hands. Court-stamped goods move through the trade like bad coin. So we want the oldest eater here, the one with the longest palate, and we want him curious enough to help and vain enough to do it for free."

"And if he's neither?"

"Then we pay. And then we run, because payment means contract, and contract means—" He stopped. His head tilted, birdlike, toward a stall at the lane's end, set apart from the others on a low rise, hung with no lanterns and needing none, because the man behind it glowed. Not metaphorically. Faintly, around the edges, the way cheese rind glows in certain cellars, or the way a cat's eyes catch lamplight. Old light. Light that had been swallowed so many times it had become part of the swallower.

"Oh good," Perrin said, without enthusiasm. "He's in."

"Who is?"

"Mama Kesse. Don't let the name fool you. He chose it himself, two hundred years ago, and he'll gut anyone who laughs." Perrin straightened his collar, checked his reflection in a polished jar lid, failed to improve it, and sighed. "Right. Follow my lead, say nothing, and if he offers you a sip of anything, remember rule one."

"You never gave me a rule one."

"Exactly. Well done."

* * *

Mama Kesse received them seated on a cushion the size of a cart wheel, wrapped in robes of a color Gideon's valley vocabulary couldn't parse — somewhere between bruise and dusk — eating, of all things, an orange. He peeled it in one unbroken spiral, which Gideon recognized as a flex. Everything about him was a flex. The glow. The stillness. The way three younger eaters hovered at the edges of his stall like moons, pretending to arrange jars while straining to hear.

"Perrin," Mama Kesse said. His voice was soft and vast, like wind through a very large empty house. "I tasted you coming. Guilt, feathers, and a lullaby you haven't opened yet. How touching. Who's your friend?"

"A customer," Perrin said smoothly. "She wants directions."

"To where?"

"The court."

The orange spiral stopped. The three moon-eaters went very still. Somewhere down the lane, the lullaby boy closed his jars early.

Mama Kesse set the orange down with great care, as though it might be listening too. "Now that," he said, "is either the bravest or stupidest request I've heard this decade, and I heard a man ask me to remove his own wedding, so the bar is high." His glowing eyes settled on Gideon, and she felt them the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. "Directions to the Under-Court aren't sold, girl. They're traded. And the only coin I accept—" he smiled, and his teeth were very white and very even, the teeth of a man who had never needed to chew anything difficult "—is a taste."

"One sip," Perrin said quickly. "One memory, her choice, shallow pour. Standard terms."

"Standard terms," Mama Kesse agreed pleasantly, "for standard customers." The eyes stayed on Gideon. "But I know you, magpie. You wouldn't bring anyone here unless you had to, and you wouldn't bring anyone here at all unless the dead made you. So. Let's skip the haggling, shall we? I'll give her the roads. In exchange, I choose what I taste."

"No," said Gideon.

Everyone looked at her, including, briefly, a passing chestnut seller.

"She speaks," Mama Kesse murmured, delighted. "Go on."

"I choose the memory. That's the price. Take it or I walk, and I'll find my roads somewhere else, and you'll spend the rest of the night wondering what I was carrying that made me willing to walk away from the only palate in fifty miles that could read it." She had no idea where the words were coming from. Some older part of her, some thief's instinct deeper than thought, had taken the wheel, and it spoke with a confidence she envied. "You're curious. Curiosity is the one hunger you've never managed to drink dry. I can starve you beautifully or feed you. Your choice."

Silence. The three moon-eaters exchanged glances that said, in some dialect of raised eyebrows, *is she allowed to do that?*

Mama Kesse laughed. It rolled through the hollow and rattled the jar-stalls, and the lullaby boy peeked back out of his booth. "Ohh," he said, wiping one glowing eye. "Oh, I've missed this. Fine. Fine, girl. One sip, your choice, shallow pour. But choose well — a coward's sip tells me nothing, and if I learn nothing, I learn nothing *on purpose*, and I'll be insulted."

Gideon stood very still and took inventory.

She carried, in the hollow places behind her ribs, everything she had ever stolen: the widow Sefu's grief, returned now, but the shape of it still known to her; a baker's pride; a soldier's shame; a hundred small mercies and cruises of other people's hearts. She needed something true enough to satisfy a palate two centuries deep, and safe enough not to touch the mountain.

She reached for a summer afternoon belonging to a cooper's daughter — sunlight through barrel staves, the smell of oak shavings, a first kiss behind the workshop. Safe. Pleasant. Perfectly forgettable.

Her hand stopped.

Because beneath the cooper's summer, the way a stone sits beneath moss, was something else. Something she had carried so long she'd stopped feeling its weight: a memory with no owner she could name. Hers, presumably — thieves always kept one or two of their own by accident — but wrong somehow. Cold at the center. A memory of standing in a doorway, watching a road, watching a small figure go up that road into the hills, and a sound like her mother crying, except she had no memory of her mother crying, except this one, which meant—

"Girl," Mama Kesse said softly. "You're doing arithmetic. I can smell it. It smells lovely."

She should have picked the cooper's summer. Every sensible bone in her body voted for the cooper's summer.

She tilted the cold one forward instead.

* * *

The tasting took less time than a heartbeat and lasted approximately a year.

Mama Kesse didn't touch her. He leaned in, breathed in — one slow inhalation, like a man standing over fresh bread — and his eyes half-closed, and Gideon felt the sip leave her the way you feel a word leave your mouth: gone, but still yours somehow, still echoing in the shape of your throat.

Then Mama Kesse recoiled.

Not politely. Not the refined wince of a sommelier finding cork in the wine. He physically flung himself backward off his cushion, robes flying, knocked over a shelf of vintages, and came up against his own stall with three hundred years of composure in ruins around him. The three moon-eaters rushed forward. The chestnut seller dropped his pan. Down the lane, someone screamed, though whether at the scene or at unrelated market business was never established.

"Court-stamped," Mama Kesse said.

His voice had changed. The vast softness was gone; what remained was thin and sharp and, worst of all, *afraid*.

"That memory is court-stamped. Do you understand what I'm telling you, girl? That's not a flavor. That's a *seal*. The Under-Court pressed its mark into that memory when it was made — the way a mint presses a king's face into a coin. I've tasted a thousand thousand memories, and I have tasted that seal exactly four times in my life, and three of those people are dead and the fourth begged me to drink the tasting out of her and I did, gladly, and I still dream about it." He pointed one trembling finger at her, and the finger glowed brighter than the rest of him, which Gideon suspected was involuntary. "You don't *carry* court-stamped goods. People don't carry them. They're not portable. They're *anchored*. That memory grew in you. It was grown there. On purpose. By them."

The market had gone quiet around them. Quiet the way a forest goes quiet — everything still present, everything listening.

Perrin had gone pale under his tan. "Kesse," he said carefully. "You're saying the court marked her. When she was a child."

"I'm saying the court doesn't stamp children." Mama Kesse pushed himself upright, straightened his magnificent robes with shaking hands, and looked at Gideon with an expression she had never once seen directed at her in her life: pity, from a memory-eater, which was like receiving sympathy from a tax collector. "I'm saying the court stamps *property*, and property gets stamped at the moment of sale. And I'm saying, girl, whoever you are, whatever you think you are — part of you has been theirs since before you could walk."

The cold memory sat in her chest where the sip had been, hollowed now, ajar, like a door left open onto a room she'd lived in all her life without ever turning on the light.

"The roads," she said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like someone else's accomplishment. "We had terms."

Mama Kesse stared at her for a long moment. Then he laughed again — quieter this time, and with something almost like respect in it — and drew from his sleeve a scrap of black cloth, and breathed onto it, and the breath became ink, and the ink became a map.

"Three roads up the mountain," he said. "The gold door takes coin. The iron door takes blood. The bone door—" his eyes flicked to hers, and away "—the bone door takes nothing, because it knows you've got nothing left. Go before the third moon sets. And girl—" he hesitated, and Gideon would remember, later, that a creature like him had hesitated, "—when you get there, and you will get there, ask them about the twin clause. Ask them loudly, in front of witnesses. They hate that."

"Twin clause?" said Perrin. "What twin clause?"

But Mama Kesse had already turned away, gathering his fallen oranges, his fallen dignity, his fallen three hundred years, and would say nothing more to either of them that night.

They walked out of the market under a sky going gray toward dawn, and Gideon carried the map, and the map was warm, and the cold place in her chest was colder, and behind them the lanterns of borrowed light flickered once, all together, like a held breath.

Perrin stopped at the edge of the hollow. He looked at the horizon, where the second moon was setting, and his jaw worked several times around words that wouldn't come.

"Gideon," he said finally. "There's something you need to know about the person who gave you that map. About why she had it. About who she was." He exhaled. "And I think you need to know it tonight, because I don't think either of us is sleeping anyway."
