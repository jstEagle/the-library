## 11. Thirteen Turns, One Spent

Perrin had three rules for guiding, and he'd broken all of them in the space of a single week.

Rule one: never take a client who doesn't know what they're paying for. Rule two: never travel after dark on roads with names. Rule three — and this was the one that had kept him alive through two famines, one war, and a regrettable season working for the salt merchants — never, ever get attached.

He looked across the cold campfire at Gideon Okafor, who was sitting very still the way she did when she was listening to her own stolen memories whisper, and admitted to himself that rule three had not merely been broken but set on fire and pushed off a cliff.

"Your soup's boiling over," he said.

"It's not soup. It's nettles and regret."

"That's what soup is." He leaned over and rescued the pot before it could become a tragedy. "Eat. You've been chewing on that map's silence for two days like it owes you money."

Gideon took the bowl without looking at him. Above them, the first moon hung low and swollen over the ridge line, so close to setting it seemed embarrassed to still be up. Thirteen turns, the dying stranger had said. And now one of those turns was nearly spent, sliding down behind the mountains like a coin into a pocket, and they were maybe a fifth of the way to anywhere.

Perrin stirred his own nettles and regret and thought: *tonight*. If he was going to tell her, it should be tonight, while the moon was dying and the truth would have somewhere dark to hide in afterward.

He'd been rehearsing versions of it for days. In the generous version, he was a hero of quiet sacrifice, delivering vital intelligence with modesty and grace. In the version more likely to occur, Gideon threw him into a ravine.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like you're counting my teeth."

"I'm a magpie," Perrin said. "We count everything. Shiny things especially. You currently rate about a six."

"I rated an eight last week."

"You spilled soup on your boots. I'm flexible, but standards are standards."

She almost smiled. He watched her almost do it, watched the almost get caught and swallowed by whatever weight she carried, and felt something in his chest go tight and uncomfortable. *That*, he thought. That is exactly why there's a rule three.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, one of the old road-markers from before the courts, carved with directions to cities that no longer existed or had changed their names out of shame. Perrin knew the stone. He'd known it since he was small enough to ride in his mother's pack basket, back when he traveled these roads with someone else.

Gideon banked the fire and settled against the stone with her map in her lap. It glowed faintly even now, its ink crawling into new shapes whenever she wasn't looking directly at it — a doorway here, a stairway there, once, horribly, a pair of small shoes side by side. She'd gone pale when that one appeared and hadn't mentioned it since.

"One moon down," she said quietly. "Twelve left."

"Mm."

"At this pace we'll reach the mountain around the eleventh turn and spend the twelfth standing outside it wondering what to say."

"So we go faster."

"We went faster. The wolves happened." She rubbed her forearm where a hunter's graze had scabbed over. "And the bridge. And your detour through the marsh that you swore was a shortcut."

"It *was* a shortcut. It cut four hours off the journey and only cost us six. That's basic arithmetic."

"That's theft with extra steps."

"And you'd know all about that," Perrin said, and immediately wished he could climb inside the waystone and live there.

Gideon looked up. Her eyes in the firelight were steady and tired and far too perceptive. "Yes," she said. "I would. What's your point, Perrin?"

Here it was, then. The opening he'd been waiting for, arriving whether he was ready or not, the way openings always did. He turned his bowl in his hands. Magpies were famous for many things — thieving, chatter, an eye for anything bright — but nobody ever praised their courage, and Perrin understood now that this was because magpies had none. They simply got attached to things and then panicked beautifully when the things were threatened.

"My name isn't Perrin," he said.

Gideon blinked. "It's not?"

"No. Well. It is. It's half of it. It's the half I kept." He took a breath. "My sister named me. We hatched — we were born, stop looking at me like that, shifters are born, it's a whole thing, very undignified — anyway. We were born in a rookery village on the north face of the Gray Teeth, and our mother named us both, and my sister decided 'Perrin' suited me better than what our mother picked, and she was right, so it stuck. She had that kind of mind. She could look at a thing and know what it truly was. Names, people, locks. Especially locks."

"Perrin," Gideon said slowly, "what are you telling me?"

He reached into his shirt and drew out the cord he wore there, the one he'd worn since the night a bleeding stranger collapsed at the valley's edge and pressed a wax-sealed map into a thief's hands. On the cord hung a single black feather, edged in silver, the kind no ordinary magpie grew.

"She stole the map," Perrin said. "From the court itself. From under the mountain. And it killed her getting it out, and she spent her last strength carrying it to the one place magic couldn't follow her — because she knew they'd send hunters, and hunters can't smell stolen goods where the world goes dead." His voice cracked on the last words, and he hated it, and kept going anyway. "The stranger at your threshold. The one who gave you thirteen turns. That was my sister. Her name was Vesper."

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, a nightjar called and got no answer.

Gideon didn't speak for a long time. When she finally did, her voice was careful, the way a person walks on river ice. "You've known this the whole time."

"Yes."

"Since the threshold. Since before the rim. Since the marsh, the bridge, the wolves — since I paid a childhood summer to cross a bridge, Perrin, you knew what this map was and where it came from, and you let me walk into all of it thinking I was following a stranger's dying whim."

"Yes," he said again. It sounded worse every time.

"Why?"

"Because she told me not to tell you!" It came out louder than he meant, and a bird somewhere complained about it. He lowered his voice. "Because she was dying, Gideon, and she held my wrist hard enough to leave marks, and she said: *the girl who takes the map must choose it freely. If she knows whose hand carried it, she'll think she owes me. If she thinks she owes me, she'll come for the wrong reasons, and the wrong reasons get people eaten under that mountain.*" He laughed, a short ugly sound. "So I let you believe it was chance. A stranger, a whim, a debt you never agreed to. Which, incidentally, is exactly what it was. I just knew the shape of the debt."

Gideon stood up. She didn't pace — she wasn't a pacer — she just stood, very straight, looking at the dying moon as if it might arbitrate.

"You could have told me a hundred times," she said. "The market. After the Ferryman. When I fed that hunter a false memory and shook for an hour afterward. Any of those moments, you chose to make a joke instead."

"Yes."

"Do you have anything to offer besides *yes*?"

Perrin considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty even if it gutted him. "I have this," he said. "Every time I almost told you, I heard her voice saying *freely, Perrin, she has to choose it freely,* and every time I realized I wasn't keeping her secret anymore. I was keeping mine. Because if you knew, you might turn around. And if you turned around, Vesper would have died for nothing, and I—" His throat closed. He worked past it. "I have lost everyone, Gideon. Mother, father, the rookery, and now her. She's the last bright thing I had, and I couldn't bear for her last act to be a failure. So I let you carry the risk alone while I carried the knowledge alone, and I told myself that was loyalty, but it was cowardice wearing loyalty's coat. There. That's my offering. It's not much. It's what I have."

The moon touched the ridge line. Its light went long and red across the valley floor, and for a moment everything — the waystone, the dead fire, Gideon's face — was stained the color of old blood.

"You're right that it's not much," Gideon said.

"I know."

"But you're wrong about one thing." She turned to face him. "You said she died for nothing if I turn around. That's not how debts work. Not real ones. She gave the map to *me.* Whatever she meant by it, whatever she saw when she looked at a thief in a magic-dead valley and decided she was the one — that part doesn't fail if I quit. It fails if I quit *lying to myself about why I'm going.*" She held out her hand. "So give me the rest. All of it. Everything you know about her, about the court, about why a soul-thief's sister stole a map and ran toward the mountain instead of away. Freely given, Perrin. No more coats."

* * *

They sat back down, closer to the coals than before, and Perrin talked until his voice went rough.

He told her about growing up with Vesper in the rookery villages, where shifter children learned early that the world paid best for what it could take from you. He told her how she'd found work as a lock-breaker in the mountain towns, honest-adjacent work, until the day she came home white-faced and said she'd seen the under-court's vaults — galleries and galleries of bottled souls, ten thousand jars, each one a person somebody had sold or gambled or traded away. He told her how she'd spent three years getting hired into the court's service, dusting ledgers, mending seals, being small and harmless and forgettable, learning the vault's rhythms the way water learns a crack in stone.

"She always said the court has one weakness," Perrin said. "Everything there is bought. Every jar, every soul, every favor — purchased, stamped, filed. They're magnificent at owning things." He looked at Gideon. "But ownership is a ledger, and a ledger can be unbalanced. She stole the map because the map is the court's own accounting of one particular debt. A girl sold beneath the mountain. A contract with a flaw in it. She found the flaw, Gideon. She found it and she copied the proof and she ran."

"A flaw," Gideon repeated. Her hands had gone still in her lap, which he'd learned meant her mind was moving very fast indeed. "What kind of flaw?"

"I don't know. She wouldn't say. She said the map would show whoever was meant to see it — that the ink rearranges, that it's keyed somehow." He hesitated. "There's one more thing. The last thing she said to me, after *thirteen turns.*"

Gideon waited.

"She said: *tell her the debt is hers, but so is the way out. Tell her the court keeps copies.*" Perrin spread his hands. "That's all. I've turned it over a thousand times. Copies of what? Souls? Contracts? I don't know. But Vesper didn't waste words near the end. She'd been rationing them for days, like a woman spending her last coins."

Gideon was silent for a long moment. Then she lifted the map and turned it toward the coals, and the heat-light caught the ink, and both of them watched the lines crawl — a mountain, a stair, and now, faintly, branching off the stair like a root system, dozens upon dozens of small sealed shapes. Jars. Rows and rows of jars.

"Copies," Gideon whispered.

The last sliver of the first moon slipped below the ridge, and the world went properly dark, and in that darkness the map's ink gleamed like something alive.

* * *

Later, when Gideon had rolled into her blanket and her breathing had gone slow, Perrin sat awake by the ashes and did arithmetic of his own.

One moon spent. Twelve remaining. Ahead of them: the salt flats, the memory-eaters' territory, the mountain's three doors, and whatever waited beneath. Behind them: a grave he hadn't dug yet, because he'd buried his sister's name instead of her body, and some nights that felt like the heavier loss.

He should feel lighter, he supposed. The secret was out; the coat was off. Instead he felt hollowed, the way a nest feels after the fledglings go — emptied of purpose and full of echo.

Then Gideon spoke from inside her blanket, drowsy and unguarded, and the echo filled up.

"Perrin. Your sister. Vesper." A pause. "Was she the one who taught you to pick locks?"

"She was the one who taught me everything. Why?"

"Because the first memory I ever stole," Gideon said, "before I knew what I was doing, before any of it — I stole the feeling of having a sister. Off a traveler, years ago. I never knew why that one mattered to me. I kept it closest of all of them." The blanket shifted; he caught the shine of her eyes in the dark. "It feels different tonight. Like it fits better than it used to."

Perrin stared at her. Something cold walked up his spine on small quick feet, and it wasn't fear, exactly. It was the feeling of a lock beginning, at last, to turn.

"Go to sleep, Gideon Okafor," he said softly.

But he sat up long after she obeyed, watching the moonless dark, thinking about sisters, and keys, and the twelve turns left to spend.
