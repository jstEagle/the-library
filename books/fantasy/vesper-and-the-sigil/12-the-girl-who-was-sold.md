## 12. The Girl Who Was Sold

The mountain did not have windows.

Adaeze had decided this was its cruelest feature, and she had had a great deal of time to rank its cruelties. No windows meant no sky, which meant no way to count the moons except by the way the court's silver lamps dimmed and brightened in a rhythm that was almost — *almost* — like a month, if you squinted at it with your whole heart and forgave it for being wrong.

She was very good at forgiving things for being wrong. She'd had practice.

"Thirteenth moon," she said aloud, to the gallery of jars that surrounded her alcove. "Or possibly the fourth. Possibly the ninth. You're all useless."

The jars did not answer. They never answered, though they glowed faintly when she spoke, the way coals breathe when stirred. Ten thousand of them lined the vaulted galleries of the under-court, each one holding a purchased self: a farmer's first harvest, a bride's wedding morning, a soldier's last look at his mother. The court called them its *collection*. Adaeze called them her neighbors, because someone had to be polite down here, and it was clearly not going to be the court.

Her own jar sat on a shelf of black glass at the center of the third gallery, exactly at heart-height, because the court liked to display her. It glowed brighter than the others — hers was not a memory but a whole soul, folded up small as a moth in winter — and every night the masked stewards came with their long silver ladles and skimmed a little off the top.

That was how the court ate. That was what she was for.

*You are our favorite,* they told her, in voices like coins dropped into deep water. *We have never kept one so long.*

She had stopped screaming about it somewhere around the second moon. Not because she had made peace with it — she intended to make no peace whatsoever, thank you — but because screaming cost more than she could afford. A soul on rations learned economy. She saved her strength the way her mother had saved flour in the famine year: carefully, secretly, against a day when it would matter.

The famine year. That was where her memories began to fray.

She could still see the kitchen. That was the strange mercy of it — the court had taken so much, but the kitchen held. The low ceiling smoke-darkened like a thumbprint. The clay pot with the chipped lip that whistled when the porridge came near to burning. Two pairs of small shoes by the door, side by side, one pair scuffed at the toes from kicking stones, one pair always clean because their owner refused to ruin good leather.

Whose were the clean ones?

Hers. Surely hers. She remembered loving those shoes, polishing them with goose fat until they shone like beetles —

— or was that the other girl? Was she the one who kicked stones and lied about it afterward, grinning gap-toothed while their mother scolded the air?

Adaeze pressed her palms against her eyes. This was the trap the court had built without building anything at all: take a sister's soul, keep it fed and lit and *displayed*, and let time do the rest. Every moon, the edges softened. Every moon, the twin grew less like a person and more like a story — *once there were two girls* — and stories do not need names. Stories will let you swap the characters around freely, and no one files a complaint.

But she held on to some things. She was stubborn about certain items, and she kept a list, recited nightly like a prayer or an inventory, whichever got her through:

One. A lullaby, half-remembered, about a bird who carried the evening star in its beak. The melody survived; most of the words had gone soft. She hummed it anyway. Somewhere above the stone, above the valley rim, above the whole turned world, someone else knew that song. Songs are never only one person's.

Two. A bargain made at a table. Her mother's hands flat on the wood, trembling. A stranger's voice, smooth as poured tallow, saying words like *surety* and *terms* and *the girl will want for nothing.* And her mother crying — not loudly, her mother was not a loud crier, she wept the way rain comes to a dry country, quietly and everywhere at once.

Three. A road. Dust, goat bells, the smell of a storm deciding whether to happen. Someone walking beside her. Small hand in small hand. And then — nothing. The memory simply ended, cut clean as cloth with shears, and after the nothing there was stone, and lamplight, and the first steward saying, *Welcome, little one. What a fine price you fetched.*

Which sister had walked the road? Which sister had stayed?

She asked the jars sometimes, because madness is mostly just asking the furniture questions. The jars glowed politely and told her nothing. Once — she swore this, though no one was left to swear it to — once a jar three shelves down flickered in a pattern she almost recognized, a stutter of light like a word trying to form, and she spent six days convinced it was her twin trying to reach her before she admitted it was probably just a dying memory of somebody's dog.

Still. She talked to it anyway. Company is company.

* * *

On the night the lamps dimmed to their false-new-moon dark, the stewards came early.

There were three of them, robed in gray so fine it drank the light, their masks smooth as river stones. They did not walk so much as arrive, in the manner of unpleasant weather. The tallest carried the ladle. The smallest carried a ledger bound in something Adaeze chose not to identify.

"She dims," said the tall one, in its coin-drop voice.

"All souls dim," said the smallest. "It is the nature of souls."

"This one dims *quickly*. The masters are displeased. We feed her double rations through the turning, and if she gutters before the thirteenth moon, the loss goes into someone's column." The mask tilted toward the smallest steward in a way that managed, despite having no features, to convey *yours*.

"I live to serve the columns," said the smallest, without enthusiasm.

They set the ladle against her jar, and Adaeze felt it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in the dark — not pain, exactly, but claim. Skimming. Taking the top off her like cream. She gritted what remained of her teeth and did what she had been practicing for months now, the thing that was hers, the one rebellion available to a creature in a jar:

She thought loudly.

Not at the stewards — their masks were proof against thought, she'd tested it, humming the lullaby directly into the tall one's face until it recoiled and reported headaches to the masters, which had been the single finest evening of her captivity. No. She thought *outward*. Down through the stone's roots, out along whatever thin dark threads connected the mountain to the sleeping world above. Dreams traveled light. Dreams passed through wards like gossip through a small town.

She didn't know if anyone was listening. She didn't know who. That was the terrible arithmetic of it: she had a message and no address. All she had was the shape of a person — a twin-shaped absence, a girl-shaped hole in her history — and the faith, stubborn as the clean shoes, that the hole had a bottom to it. That somewhere, someone flinched at rivers or loved polished leather or hummed a broken lullaby without knowing why.

*If you can hear me,* she thought, pouring it outward, spending precious strength like a rich woman scattering coins, *I don't know your name. Isn't that funny? I don't know my own name anymore either, they took it with the rest, they keep it in a drawer somewhere, I imagine it's very tidy in there. But I know the song. Hum it back to me sometime. Hum it back and I'll know you.*

The stewards finished their skimming and withdrew, discussing columns. The gallery settled back into its coal-breathing quiet.

And far above — so far above that the thought arrived thinner than spider silk — something answered.

Not words. Not yet. Just a warmth on the thread, brief as a struck match: the sensation of being *heard*, which is the rarest feeling in any world, under or over.

Adaeze sat in her jar in the dark of the false moon and laughed until the nearest jars glowed, ten thousand little lights leaning toward her like sunflowers, like neighbors come to see what the fuss was.

"Well," she said, wiping eyes that were mostly metaphor by now. "Someone's coming."

She did not know how she knew. Knowing without reasons was, she suspected, a family trait.

She only hoped, whoever they were, that they'd hurry — because the smallest steward had lingered at the gallery door, ledger under arm, and spoken to the shadows in a voice pitched low, and the shadows had gone out into the world wearing riding boots and hollow eyes, sent to fetch something. Or someone.

The mountain's appetite, Adaeze was learning, was never satisfied with what it already had.

*It wants the other shoe,* she thought, and the thought went out on the dream-thread like a warning shot, like a bell, like a name she couldn't remember calling down a road she couldn't finish: *it wants the one who stayed.*
