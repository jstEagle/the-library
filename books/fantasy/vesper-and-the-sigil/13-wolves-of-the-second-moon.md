## 13. Wolves of the Second Moon

The second moon rose the color of an old bruise, and Perrin stopped walking so suddenly that Gideon nearly collided with his back.

"What," she said.

"Listen."

She listened. Wind in the pines. Somewhere far off, a nightbird doing its idiotic three-note call. The creak of her own pack straps. "I hear nothing."

"Exactly." Perrin's feathers — he was mostly man-shaped tonight, but his hair had gone sleek and oil-dark along his jaw, which it always did when he was frightened — rippled once. "The forest's gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone's standing behind you."

Gideon had spent eleven years in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and even she knew what that particular silence meant out here. She shifted the map-case higher on her shoulder and felt it pulse against her hip, faint and warm, like a sleeping animal. The map always knew before they did.

"Hunters?" she asked.

"Court hunters." Perrin turned, and in the bruise-light his eyes were more magpie than man: quick, black, catching glints that weren't there. "Hollow-eyes. They ride for the court beneath the mountain, and they smell stolen goods the way I smell shiny things. Which is to say—" he tapped his own chest "—irresistibly."

"And we're carrying?"

"We're carrying *you*, love. A walking hoard of other people's pasts. To them you're not a woman. You're an unguarded market stall."

Gideon's hand went, without her permission, to the jars nested in wool at the bottom of her pack. Eleven years of thefts, wrapped and padded like eggs. She'd told herself they were inventory. Tools of trade. But crossing the rim had changed their voices; now they whispered against her back all night, fragments of summers and funerals and first kisses, murmuring like a congregation that wouldn't quite fall asleep.

"How long do we have?"

Perrin tilted his head, listening with some sense she didn't have. His face did something complicated. "Less than it takes to explain the plan I don't have."

* * *

They ran.

Not well — Gideon was a thief, not a runner, and eleven years of valley living had softened her lungs for anything steeper than a rooftop — but they ran, down through the pines where the ground was soft enough to swallow their footfalls, Perrin flitting ahead in bursts of black-and-white that were almost too fast to follow.

"They'll circle us," he called back. "That's what they do. Wolves of the second moon, the old stories say. They don't chase. They *arrive where you were going.*"

"That's not how chasing works."

"You'd be amazed how little the court cares about how things work."

He was right, of course. They broke from the treeline into a shallow bowl of moorland — heather, standing stones, a stream chuckling its way east — and the hunters were already there. Five of them, mounted on horses that were too still, arranged across the bowl's mouth as if they'd been waiting since the world began.

They were wrong in a way Gideon's eyes kept trying to correct. Their armor was fine — court-fine, silver chased with dark enamel — but under the helms there was nothing. Not faces hidden by shadow. Shadow shaped like absence, where faces should have been. The horses breathed. The riders didn't.

"Oh," Gideon said quietly. "Oh, that's unpleasant."

"Told you." Perrin had gone very flat against a standing stone, feathers tight to his body. "Don't run. Running marks you as goods trying to escape. Goods trying to escape get taken *broken*."

One rider dismounted. It moved the way furniture would move if furniture resented being furniture — smoothly, heavily, without any of the small wasteful motions of a living thing. It walked toward them across the heather, and where its boots pressed, the plants grayed.

Gideon's whole body wanted to bolt. Instead she did what she'd done in a hundred locked rooms: she made herself small, made herself quiet, made herself *inventory*. Just another traveler. Nothing worth taking.

The hunter stopped ten feet away. Its hollow face turned toward her pack, and Gideon felt it the way you feel a hand pass over your skin without touching — a pressure, a *reading*. It was smelling her. Smelling the widow's grief, the ferryman's summer she'd paid away (no — that one was gone, spent at the bridge), the smuggler's guilt, the child's fear of wells, all forty-odd jars and all the loose memories she carried loose in her own head, the ones she'd swallowed over years of trade because a memory-thief who stored nothing looked innocent.

It smelled all of it. And it leaned closer, hungry as a debt collector.

"Perrin," she breathed. "What do they actually do with—"

"Don't find out."

The hunter raised one gauntleted hand toward her pack. And something in Gideon — something older than caution, older than the valley, some instinct she hadn't known she owned — woke up and said *no.*

* * *

Here is what no one had ever told Gideon Okafor about her gift, perhaps because no one had ever been fool enough or desperate enough to try it:

A memory-thief doesn't only take. Taking is just the visible half of the craft, the way a pickpocket's lift is only half the art — the other half is the *planting*, the thing slipped in while everyone watches the empty space where the wallet used to be. For eleven years Gideon had planted nothing but blanks. Little absences, sewn into a mark's mind: *you never saw me, you never missed this jar, this hour is unimportant.*

But a blank is just a memory wearing a mask.

And the hunter's hollow face was leaning toward hers, and its attention was a cold ring tightening around her skull, and there was no door to slip out of, no window, no alley — so Gideon reached into the place where she kept her craft, and instead of taking, she *gave.*

She built it fast, the way she built everything: from scraps. A kitchen, borrowed from a widow's jar. Rain on a tin roof, from a farmer's grief. A woman's voice singing — she didn't know whose voice, it surfaced from somewhere deep and unlabeled, some sealed room inside her she didn't stop to question — singing a lullaby in a language she almost understood. A table set for three. A mother laughing. A dog, because every good memory needs a dog. She stitched them together with a thief's speed, made them warm, made them *true-feeling*, gave them a name — *this is yours, this happened to you, this is your happiest day* — and she poured the whole thing into the hunter's hollow head like water into an empty jug.

The effect was immediate and terrible.

The hunter went rigid. Then it shuddered, armor clattering, and made a sound — the first sound she'd heard it make — a thin, high keening that had nothing human in it and everything human in it at once. Its gauntlets flew to its absent face. It sank to its knees in the heather, and the heather under it greened, came back to life, bloomed — because whatever the court had hollowed out of this creature, it had left the vessel yearning, and Gideon had just filled that yearning to the brim with a kitchen and rain and a dog and a lullaby.

"*No,*" the hunter said, in a voice like a rusted hinge remembering it had once been a bell. "*No — I had — I had a—*" And then it simply folded, forward into the flowers, and lay still, and did not move again, and the other four riders turned their hollow faces toward Gideon in perfect, terrible unison.

"Gideon," Perrin whispered. "What did you *do?*"

"I don't know." Her hands were shaking. "I don't — stay behind me."

The remaining hunters didn't charge. That was somehow worse. They sat their too-still horses and regarded her, and she could feel them reassessing — the way a merchant reassesses a customer who turns out to be carrying a knife. One of them made a sound, low in its absent throat, that might have been a signal or might have been hunger.

Then, as one, they wheeled their horses and rode — not away, but *around*, circling the bowl's rim at a distance, patient as wolves deciding whether the prey is worth the teeth.

"They're waiting for orders," Perrin breathed. "Or for you to tire. Hollow-eyes don't tire. We do."

Gideon stared at the fallen hunter. Up close, now that she dared look, she could see what her gift had done to it: the armor had cracked along the seams, and through the cracks seeped light — ordinary, golden, lamplight-colored, the exact color of a kitchen at evening. Whatever she'd given it, it was holding onto. Dying full, after however many years of being emptied.

She felt sick.

Not because she'd killed it. Because of how *easy* it had been. Because some part of her, deep in the sealed rooms of herself, had known exactly how to do it — had done it with the fluency of long practice, the way you know your own signature. And because the memory she'd built it from hadn't been assembled from her jars at all. The lullaby, the voice, the table set for three — those had come from somewhere else. From *her*. From whatever it was she'd been before she was Gideon Okafor, before the valley, before the trade.

Her gift wasn't just a lockpick, she realized. It was a weapon. It had always been a weapon. She was carrying a blade she'd never once looked at directly, and the mountain ahead of her — the court, the contract, the dying stranger's thirteen turns — was exactly the kind of place that made people reach for blades.

"Gideon." Perrin touched her arm. His feathers were flat with fear, but his eyes, when she made herself meet them, were steady. "Whatever you're thinking right now, and I can see it's something heavy — think it while walking. Circle's tightening."

* * *

They ran again, west this time, along the stream, because running water muddled the hunters' scent-sense — Perrin swore it did, and Gideon was in no position to argue with anyone's folklore. Behind them the hoofbeats kept their lazy, circling pace, never gaining, never falling behind. Patient. The patience was the worst part. It was the patience of a ledger. Of a contract. Of something that believed, correctly, that everything owed to it eventually comes home.

Twice, the hunters cut ahead of them — arriving, exactly as promised, where Gideon was going — and twice she fed them. The second time she built a memory of a harbor town, gulls and fish-guts and a girl dancing on a breakwater, and the hunter took it and keened and fell among the reeds, and the reeds bowed around it as if in mourning. The third time she barely needed to build anything; she gave it raw weather, a storm off some remembered coast, thunder and the smell of ozone, and it dropped its reins and stood in the shallows with its arms open like a man greeting a son come home from sea.

By the fourth, her head ached as though she'd been crying for hours. Building memories cost something. She'd always known that — taking cost the taker a little warmth, a little sleep — but giving cost more, giving cost *substance*, and she could feel the gaps opening in herself like doors into dark rooms. She tried to remember her mother's face and found a kitchen with no one in it. She tried to remember why she flinched at rivers and found nothing at all, just the flinch, orphaned, floating free of its reason.

"Perrin," she said, wading the stream for the sixth time, "how far to anywhere?"

"There's a ruin," he said, and for once he didn't dress it up in banter. "Burned library, half a day north. There's a man there. Archivist. Blind as a stone and knows everything, which between you and me is a suspicious combination. If anyone knows what hollow-eyes fear besides kitchens, it's him."

"You've been leading me toward him this whole time."

"I've been leading you *away* from everything else." He glanced back at the circling riders, silhouetted now against the setting second moon. "And I'll tell you something for free, thief, since you keep feeding monsters your insides and pretending it doesn't cost you. Those things back there? The court didn't make them out of nothing. Hollow-eyes are *made from people*. People who sold themselves, or were sold. Every one you put down was somebody once."

Gideon stopped mid-stream, cold water pulling at her knees.

Every one she put down had knelt in the flowers and died *grateful*. Had died holding a kitchen, a harbor, a storm — died fuller than it had lived in years. She thought of the light seeping through the cracked armor, gold as lamplight, and felt the sickness turn over into something else. Not quite horror. Not quite grief. Something with teeth of its own.

"The court empties people," she said slowly, "and I fill them. Isn't that a funny pair of trades to be related."

"Funny isn't the word I'd use."

"No." She looked north, toward the burned library she couldn't see yet, toward the archivist who read maps by touch, toward whatever truth was waiting there with its paperwork in order. The map pulsed warm against her hip, and for the first time she wondered — really wondered — whether the dying stranger had chosen her not despite what she was, but *because* of it. Whether a thief who could give was exactly the weapon a stolen soul required.

Behind them, the last hunter stopped circling. It sat its horse on the ridge, hollow face tipped up to the fading moon, and it did not follow.

It had seen enough. It was going home to report.

"Perrin," Gideon said, climbing the far bank, wringing out her hem, "walk fast."
