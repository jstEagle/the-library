## 14. The Archivist of Ash

The library announced itself a mile before they reached it, by smell.

"Smoke," Perrin said, wrinkling his nose. "Old smoke. Decades old."

"Burned libraries keep their fires," Gideon said. "Everyone knows that."

"Everyone who?"

"Everyone who grew up somewhere with books."

Perrin made a sound like a magpie being asked to apologize — a short, offended clatter — and said nothing else for the better part of an hour, which Gideon counted as a small victory and did not mention aloud, because mentioning victories aloud was how you lost them.

They came over a ridge of blackened hills in the late afternoon, and there it was: a great shell of a building, its roof gone, its walls standing like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. Ash had drifted into every corner of the ruin and settled in soft grey drifts, so that the whole place looked dusted with snow that had never learned how to melt. Here and there, charred shelves rose from the ash like drowned masts. The air was utterly still. Even the wind seemed to have decided this was a place for whispering.

"There's someone in there," Perrin said quietly.

"I know."

"You *know* or you're guessing?"

Gideon considered. "I'm guessing with confidence."

"That's not a thing."

"It's absolutely a thing. It's most of what thieves do." She started down the slope. "Come on. If the map wanted us here, the map wanted us here."

The map had been behaving strangely all day — stranger than usual, which was saying something. It no longer burned when she set it down; instead it went cold, cold as river-stone, and it had begun to feel *heavy*, as though the ink were accumulating weight with every mile they traveled toward whatever it wanted. She'd caught herself talking to it that morning. Just once. Just a small "*all right, I'm going*" before she climbed out of her bedroll. She had decided not to think about that.

Inside, the ruin opened up into what must once have been a reading hall vast enough to hold three hundred desks. Now it held ash, and silence, and one small figure sitting at the only intact table, dead center of the hall, as though the fire had parted around him out of professional courtesy.

He was old — old in the way of trees, of stones, of things that had been old for a very long time and intended to continue. His eyes were closed, and stayed closed. His hands rested on the table before him, palms up, and they were the strangest thing about him: black to the wrist, as though he'd plunged both arms into the fire itself and drawn them back unchanged except for the color. When he spoke, his voice was dry and warm, like paper left too long in the sun.

"You walk loudly," he said, "for a thief."

Gideon stopped. Behind her, she heard Perrin's feathers ruffle — she could hear them now, when he was startled; she'd learned that much about him. "We didn't mean to intrude," she said.

"No one means to intrude on grief. They simply arrive in it." He turned his face toward her without opening his eyes. It was uncanny, how precisely he found her. "You've brought something that wants reading. I can smell the wax seal from here. And underneath the wax—" his nostrils flared, delicately, "—ink. Court ink. Sit down, girl. My name is Odion. I was the archivist here, back when there were books to archive. Now I am the archivist of ash, which is a shorter job description but longer hours."

Gideon glanced at Perrin. Perrin shrugged with his entire body, which was very expressive of him and communicated nothing useful.

She sat.

* * *

"The map first," Odion said, holding out his black hands. "Give it here. Don't be precious about it. Everything in this room was precious once, and look at us now."

She hesitated. She had not let the map out of her reach since the valley's edge, since a dying stranger had pressed it into her hands along with thirteen words and thirteen turns of the moon. Setting it down made it burn. Giving it away felt, absurdly, like giving away a tooth.

"It won't leave you," Odion said, watching her hesitation with those closed eyes. "That's rather the point of court documents. They bind. Give it here and see."

She gave it to him.

And nothing happened. No burning, no cold, no rearranging ink. The map lay across his blackened palms like a sleeping thing finally comfortable, and Gideon felt its absence in her chest like a pulled tooth after all — a hollow, a whistling gap.

Odion began to read.

Not with his fingers tracing lines. With his whole hands, moving slowly over the surface, pressing down here and there, lifting, pressing again. His head tilted. His lips moved sometimes, silently. Once he made a small *hmm* in the back of his throat, the way a physician does over a symptom.

"This is a contract," he said at last. "You knew that?"

"It's a map."

"It's a map the way a wedding ring is a circle of metal. Yes, it points. But pointing is not its purpose." His fingers moved to the upper corner, where the wax seal had cracked during their journey. "This is a surety bond. A soul held against payment. Do you know what a surety is, thief?"

"Something held until a debt is paid."

"Mm. And do you know whose debt?" He didn't wait for her answer. His hands kept moving, reading. "Two names on the original instrument. Two girls. Twins, by the spacing of the signatures — children don't sign these things themselves, of course, the parent signs for both, but the court likes both names written. Likes the symmetry. Courts are sentimental in the way that vultures are sentimental: they appreciate a well-arranged carcass."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere high above, ash sifted down through the missing roof, fine as flour.

"One name is Adaeze," Odion continued. "That's the soul in surety. That's the girl beneath the mountain, if my touch hasn't failed me, and my touch has never failed me; it's the only part of me the fire improved." He paused. His hands went still on the parchment. "The second name is smudged. Deliberately smudged, and recently — within the year, I'd say. Someone rubbed it out with a wet thumb and great urgency. But ink remembers, girl. Ink always remembers. Press hard enough and it gives up what it was made to hold."

"Can you read it?" Gideon asked. Her voice came out strange. Flat. She noticed herself noticing that, the way you notice a floor tilting.

"I can read the shape of it." He lifted the map and held it toward her, though of course he looked at nothing. "I can tell you that it begins with the same letter as the first name. I can tell you it has the same rhythm — two beats, then a fall. Sisters' names often do. Mothers choose them in pairs, like shoes." He set the map down between them on the table. "But I will not say the name, because I suspect you already know it, and because some readings should be done by their owner."

The silence stretched. Perrin shifted behind her. "Gideon," he said carefully, "your hands—"

She looked down. Her hands were flat on the table, and they were trembling, and she hadn't given them permission.

"I stole memories," she said. "For a living. Twenty years. Hundreds of them. You learn to read people's pasts the way a butcher reads meat — you learn what's gristle, what's fat, what's the good red center. And in twenty years I never once—" She stopped. Started again. "Every memory I carry. Every single theft I ever made. I told myself I took them because people couldn't hold them. Because I was doing them a kindness. And the map's ink shows me scenes I almost remember, and the Ferryman gave back a summer I sold, and a memory-eater tasted my past and called it *court-stamped*, and I have been walking away from a conclusion for six moons the way you walk around a hole in the road."

"And now?" Odion asked gently.

"Now the hole is under my feet."

* * *

Odion poured tea. This surprised her — a kettle, cups, a tin of leaves kept in a charred cabinet that had somehow survived — but he moved through his ruined hall with the ease of long habit, finding things by memory in a place where memory was the only thing left. The tea was smoky. Of course it was. Everything here would taste of smoke until the end of the world.

"Tell me about the fire," Gideon said, because she needed, desperately, to talk about anything else.

"Ah." He settled back into his chair. "Well. The court came for the books. Not to burn them — burning was the *after*. First they came to buy. They bought our histories, our genealogies, our records of births and debts and names. Anything that proved who people were. Then, once they owned the proof, they burned the rest, so no one could ever check their arithmetic." He smiled, and it was a terrible gentle thing. "A library is just a ledger of souls, girl. Burn the ledger and everyone in it becomes negotiable."

"They bought *names*?"

"They bought everything written. Names most of all. A name is the only thing the court cannot make." He said it lightly, tossing it off, and Gideon would remember later that he said it lightly, and wonder why. "When your parents named you, they wrote you into existence. The court prefers a world where existence comes with a receipt."

Perrin had gone very still on his bench. "The dying woman," he said. "My— the woman who gave Gideon the map. She worked for them. She said they kept records of everything. She said that was their real vault, not the jars. The jars are just—" He searched for the word.

"Furniture," Gideon said.

"Collateral," Odion corrected. "The jars are collateral. The ledger is the asset." He refilled her cup without asking. "Now. Ask me what you came to ask. You've been circling it since you sat down, and circling is for birds." A pause. "No offense meant, young man."

"Some taken," Perrin said.

Gideon wrapped her hands around the cup. The warmth helped. She watched the steam rise and thought about how to ask a question whose answer might unmake her, and discovered there was no graceful way, so she asked it the way she picked locks: directly, and braced for the click.

"If a mother sold one daughter to the court," she said, "and kept the other — and paid someone to steal the memory of it from everyone who remembered, including herself — what would be left? In the daughter who stayed. What would she be made of?"

Odion was quiet for a long moment. The ash sifted. Somewhere in the walls, the building ticked as it cooled, though it had been cooling for thirty years.

"She'd be made of gaps," he said at last. "Gaps dressed up as choices. She'd be very good at taking things from other people, because taking is the only relationship with the past she was permitted. She'd flinch at rivers, perhaps, and not know why. She'd be competent, and lonely, and excellent at her work, and she would believe — truly believe — that she chose her life." He set down his own cup. "And one day a dying stranger would hand her a piece of paper, and the paper would begin showing her the shape of the hole she lives in."

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I've read ten thousand contracts, child, and every one of them is a story about a person being turned into a sum. And sums always leave a remainder." He leaned forward, and even blind, even ancient, his attention had weight, like a hand on her shoulder. "Here is the truth's shape, and you may hate it, and you may throw your tea at me, and I will still pour you another cup afterward. Two girls. One contract. One soul kept as surety beneath the mountain. One daughter kept above it — kept, mind you, the way you keep a coin in a strongbox, the way you keep a spare. The court holds Adaeze. But the contract binds *both* names, and a binding runs through blood like a stitch through cloth. If one name is Adaeze, and the other name was smudged out by a desperate thumb within the year—"

"Don't," Gideon said.

"—then the debt was never one girl's," Odion finished softly. "It was always two. And the question that has been chasing you up mountains and across salt flats is not *who was sold.*"

The tea had gone cold in her hands. Or her hands had gone cold around the tea. She couldn't have said which.

"The question," Odion said, "is *which sister stayed.*"

* * *

They camped that night in the reading hall, because the map had gone quiet and heavy and neither of them suggested leaving. Odion refused their offer of food and accepted their company instead, sitting by the small fire with his black hands open to the warmth.

Perrin fell asleep first, curled with his back to the wall, one arm thrown over his eyes like a boy. Gideon sat turning the map over and over in her hands. It didn't burn anymore. It lay against her palms like something exhausted, like something that had finally delivered its message and was waiting to see what she'd do.

"The smudge," she said quietly, to Odion. "Someone rubbed out the second name. Recently. Who does that? Who smudges out a name on a court document?"

"Someone who loved the person carrying it," Odion said. "Or someone who feared what would happen if the court remembered it. The two are usually the same person, in my experience." He tilted his head. "Your dying stranger. Tell me about her again."

"She was bleeding. She was already gone, mostly. She pressed this into my hands and said *thirteen turns* and died before I could ask her a single cursed question."

"Mm. And she stole this from the court itself, yes? From their ledgers?" He smiled at the fire. "Then ask yourself, thief, with your fine butcher's eye for reading meat: why would a court clerk steal a contract, smudge out one name, cross a valley that magic cannot enter, and die delivering it to a stranger — unless the stranger was not a stranger at all?"

Gideon looked at the map. At the smudged corner. At the ink that showed her kitchens and lullabies and two pairs of small shoes whenever she wasn't looking directly at it.

"I keep almost remembering," she whispered. "There's something sealed up inside me. Something I can't get into. I've carried it for years and told myself it was just another theft, just another jar somebody couldn't hold. But it's labeled. I've never told anyone that. It's labeled in handwriting I know, and I've never once been able to bring myself to open it, and I tell myself it's because opening stolen goods is bad practice—"

"Girl." Odion's voice was very kind. "In forty years of archiving, I learned one rule above all others. Would you like it? It's free. The only free thing left in this country."

She nodded, not trusting her voice.

"A sealed jar doesn't protect you from what's inside it," he said. "It protects what's inside from being *changed by your waiting.* Whatever your mother put in that jar — and we both know now whose hand that is — it has been waiting, patient as ink, for exactly as long as you have been afraid of it. And fear, child, is just interest accruing on a debt you haven't read yet."

The fire popped. Perrin murmured in his sleep and turned over.

Gideon looked down at the map in her hands, and for the first time since the valley's edge, she looked at it *directly* — really looked, the way she'd never dared, the way you don't look directly at the sun or at the thing you suspect is true.

The ink stirred. Rearranged. And there, blooming up through the parchment like flowers through snow, was a kitchen. A low kitchen with a blue door. Two pairs of small shoes side by side. A woman's hands kneading bread, humming, and the hum was a lullaby, and Gideon's mouth knew the tune before her mind did, and her lips were moving with it before she could stop them—

She wrenched her eyes away, heart slamming.

"Not yet," she said aloud, to the map, to the jar, to the mother whose handwriting waited inside her like a letter unopened for twenty years. "Not yet. But soon."

Behind her, soft as ash falling, Odion said:

"Yes. That's what she said too."
