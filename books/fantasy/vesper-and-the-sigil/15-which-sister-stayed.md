## 15. Which Sister Stayed

The hoard lived under the floor of Widow Sefu's back room, which was the safest place in the valley for it, because nobody ever suspected a thief of hiding her treasures beneath the floorboards of the one person she'd robbed.

Gideon had chosen the spot years ago with a thief's logic: if anyone came looking, they'd tear apart her own cottage first, and Sefu's house would be the last place they searched — right up until they remembered the widow whose grief had gone missing. Then they'd come here second-to-last, after checking the well, the granary, and old Tam's legendary cache of smuggled pepper, which did not exist and never had. By that time Gideon would be over the rim and three valleys gone.

It was good logic. She clung to it now like a drowning woman clinging to a plank that was, on inspection, mostly splinters.

She knelt on the cold earth with the map spread beside her and the jars arranged around her in careful rows, forty-one of them, each wrapped in wool against the damp. Forty-one stolen memories. The sum total of a life's work, laid out in the lantern light like a market stall for a customer who would never come.

"Right," she said aloud, because the silence was getting ideas. "Proof. We're looking for proof."

The jars did not answer. They never did, but she talked to them anyway; it was either that or listen to the whispers, and since crossing the rim the whispers had grown from a murmur into something closer to conversation. Not out loud — not yet, not down here in the magic-dead valley where even her stolen memories sulked and went quiet — but she could feel them pressing at the inside of her skull like cats at a door. *Open us. Open us. You know you want to.*

"I don't want to," she told them. "I want *evidence*. There's a difference."

There was. Evidence was a thing you looked at with your eyes and weighed with your head. What she was afraid of wasn't evidence. It was recognition.

She started at the beginning, because that was what a sensible person did, and Gideon Okafor liked to think of herself as a sensible person who happened to steal memories for a living, which was a bit like calling yourself a sensible person who happened to juggle knives over sleeping children.

The first jar was old work, clumsy work — a fisherman's terror of deep water, taken off him in exchange for three silver pieces and a lie about how the fear would grow back gentler. It hadn't. He'd drowned two summers later in water barely past his waist, and Gideon had kept the jar ever since without quite knowing why. Guilt, probably. Or the collector's disease that every thief of her particular trade caught eventually: the inability to let go of anything once it was yours.

She set it aside. Nothing there but a dead man's fear and her own.

* * *

The archivist's words had followed her all the way down from the burned library, up through the passes and across the border stones and home again, walking day and night because stopping meant thinking. *Two girls. One contract. One soul kept as surety.* And then, in that dry papery voice that made truth sound like an inventory: *I cannot tell you which sister stayed. The record does not say. But records are written by the ones who leave, child, and the ones who stay are rarely written anywhere at all.*

Which sister stayed.

The question had a shape, and the shape was wrong, and Gideon had spent the entire journey home refusing to look directly at it, the way you refuse to look directly at a hole in the floorboards because looking might mean admitting you'll have to fix it, and fixing it means money, and money means the winter is going to be lean.

But she was home now. Home was the valley that forgot, where magic couldn't follow and neither, in theory, could truth. And the question sat down across from her at Sefu's table like an uninvited guest who intended to stay through winter, and so she had done the only thing left to do.

She had gone to her hoard to prove herself innocent.

"You understand," she said to the jars, unscrewing the lid of the second one, "that this is purely administrative."

The jar held a baker's memory of his mother teaching him to knead dough, flour to the elbows, laughter in the warm dark before dawn. Gideon had taken it eight years ago from a man who wanted to forget how much he missed her. She turned it in her hands. Flour. Laughter. A kitchen.

Her stomach did something complicated.

"Not relevant," she said firmly, and put it down, and reached for the third.

The trouble — and she had known this would be the trouble, which is why she'd delayed three days, rewrapping the jars twice, alphabetizing them by the names of the people she'd stolen them from, which took the better part of an afternoon and accomplished nothing except the discovery that she had robbed an alarming number of men named Bram — the trouble was that memories were not evidence. Memories were weather. You didn't examine weather; you stood in it. And every jar she opened put her standing in someone else's rain, and more and more lately the rain felt like it was falling on her own ground.

A girl learning to whistle through a blade of grass. A woman braiding her daughter's hair by firelight, two plaits, always two, because the girl cried if her sister got one and she didn't—

Gideon stopped.

She read the label. *Maren of the ford, grief for her daughters.* Taken four years ago. Maren had lost both girls to fever, wanted to stop waking up reaching for two small beds. Standard work. Clean work. Gideon had done it kindly, even, sitting with the woman half the night while she wept, taking the worst of it slow.

Two plaits. Two beds. Two.

"It's nothing," Gideon said. Her voice sounded fine. Her voice sounded absolutely steady, and she was proud of it. "Lots of things come in twos. Shoes. Moons. Ears." She touched her ear, just to check. Still there. Both of them. "This proves nothing whatsoever."

She opened the next jar anyway. And the next. Because that was the thing about denial that nobody warned you about: it didn't feel like denial from the inside. From the inside it felt like thoroughness. Like due diligence. Like a professional conducting a systematic search of forty-one containers, one at a time, in order, hands only slightly unsteady, breathing carefully regulated, telling herself with each lid that came off: *this one will be ordinary, this one will be somebody else's life, this one will prove I'm me.*

* * *

Somewhere around jar nineteen — a shepherd's first snowfall, blue and clean and utterly unhelpful — the lantern guttered, and Gideon realized she'd been at it for hours, and that her knees had gone numb, and that she was crying without having noticed, which was frankly humiliating for a woman of her discipline.

She wiped her face with her sleeve. "Dust," she informed the empty room. "Cellars are dusty."

The cellar agreed with her, in the way cellars do, which is to say it said nothing at all and let her keep her dignity.

She should sleep. That was the sensible move. Sleep, and tomorrow she'd go to Sefu, or better yet not go to Sefu, or better yet burn the whole hoard and the map with it and take up a new trade in a valley where no one had ever heard of memory at all. She could sell turnips. Turnips asked nothing of you. Turnips did not rearrange their ink into scenes of kitchens you almost remembered.

Instead she reached for jar twenty-six.

Later — much later, in the years when she told this story to Adaeze by firelight, editing freely — Gideon would claim she chose that jar at random. But there are no random choices in a cellar at midnight when your whole self is on trial, and some part of her, the part that had been a thief since before she could write, the part that knew where everything was hidden including the things hidden from itself — that part had known exactly which jar her hand was going to find.

It was smaller than the others. Older. The wax seal was different too — not her rough thumb-print in tallow, the way she sealed her work, but a careful, deliberate pour, smooth as poured cream, pressed once with a ring she didn't recognize. And the label was not in her hand.

It was in her mother's.

She knew it the way you know a lullaby — not the words, but the tune of it. The tall letters leaning slightly left, like women leaning into wind. The little loop on the *d* that curled back on itself, shy. She had never seen her mother's handwriting. She was certain of this. She was certain of it the way she was certain of her own name, which was to say: completely, and with a small cold voice underneath asking *are you?*

The label said one word.

*Gideon.*

Not *for Gideon.* Not *property of.* Just the name, in that leaning hand, on a jar of memory she had no record of ever stealing, sealed with wax she had never poured, sitting in her hoard like a letter that had been waiting decades to be opened.

The cellar went very quiet. Even the whispering memories behind her eyes went still, all of them listening.

"No," Gideon said.

Her voice cracked on it, which annoyed her enormously.

"No. This isn't — someone planted this. Someone's been in my hoard. Perrin—" But Perrin had never been down here; she'd only shown him the trapdoor yesterday, and he'd looked at it the way a cat looks at a covered birdcage, with polite unbearable longing. "Or the elders. Or the Ferryman, he owes me a rebate, the smug river-ghoul, he could have—"

She heard herself. She heard the list getting longer and stupider, and she stopped, because a thief knows a locked thing when she holds one, and this jar was locked with a care that had nothing to do with keeping thieves out. This was locked the way you lock a thing you cannot bear to look at yourself. This was a door shut from the inside.

Her mother's handwriting. On a jar. In her hoard.

Which meant her mother had been here. Which meant her mother had put it here, or sent it here, or — and this thought arrived like ice water — which meant the hoard itself, the great fortress of forty-one stolen lives she'd built her trade and her pride and her entire self upon, had a foundation she had never examined. A jar she'd never catalogued. A gap in her own ledger, and Gideon Okafor's ledgers were perfect, were famous for being perfect, were the one thing about herself she had never doubted.

Until now.

She held the jar up to the lantern light. Something moved inside — not liquid, not quite; a slow shifting grey-gold, like fog remembering it used to be a field. Memory-stuff. Living memory, undimmed, preserved with a skill better than hers. Whoever had bottled it had loved it, or feared it, or both.

"Open it," whispered the memories behind her eyes, all together now, a congregation, a chorus. *Open it open it open—*

"Shut *up*." 

And then, quieter, to herself, in the reasonable tone she used on clients who wanted to forget things they shouldn't: "You don't have to. Nobody says you have to. It's not evidence. It's a jar. Jars can sit. Jars are very good at sitting. You could bury it. You could drop it down a well. You could give it to the river and let the Ferryman have it, he collects things, it's practically charity—"

Her thumb was resting on the seal. She didn't remember putting it there.

The wax was cool and smooth under her skin, and through it, faint as a heartbeat, she could feel the memory wanting out. Wanting *her*. And underneath the wanting, so faint she might have invented it, something else — a warmth, a specific warmth, the warmth of a kitchen with bread in it, of a voice singing low, of arms that knew exactly how to hold a frightened child, and Gideon snatched her hand back as if the wax had burned her.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole terrible thing of it.

She recognized the warmth.

Not the memory — she didn't have the memory, wouldn't have it until the seal broke, maybe not even then. But the shape of it. The way you recognize a house you grew up in from the outside, years later, without being able to name the street. Her body knew it. Her body had been keeping the knowledge somewhere the thefts couldn't reach, folded up small, all these years, waiting.

"Which sister stayed," she said to the jar, and her voice came out flat and strange. "That's what the old man asked. That's the question. And you're the answer, aren't you. You're sitting in my cellar wearing my name in my mother's hand, and you're the answer."

The jar said nothing. Jars are very good at that.

She thought about opening it. She genuinely tried. She sat there in the dust with her thumb on the seal for what felt like an hour, telling herself all the brave things, all the true things — *you crossed the rim, you paid the Ferryman, you fed a hunter a false memory so vivid it unmade him, you are not a coward, Gideon Okafor does not flinch* — and every single one of them bounced off the same small simple fact:

If she opened it, and it said what she was afraid it said, then everything she was would be a lie with excellent posture.

Her trade. Her rules. Her perfect ledgers. The careful competent lonely architecture of Gideon Okafor, built stone by stone on the assumption that she was a woman who took other people's memories because she had none of her own worth keeping. If the jar said what it might say, then she hadn't been a thief at all. She'd been a fugitive. Running from her own life with her back turned, calling it a career.

Thieves could live with themselves. Fugitives had to turn around sometime.

"I can't," she said. It came out very small. "Not tonight. Tonight I can't, and that's — that's allowed. That's a strategic delay. Even generals retreat."

She wrapped the jar in wool, more wool than it needed, three layers, like swaddling. She did not put it back in its place in the rows. She put it in her coat, against her chest, where she could feel it — a small hard weight, a second heartbeat, a question with a seal on it — and climbed the ladder, and closed the trapdoor, and barred it, though bars had never once stopped her and would not stop her now.

Upstairs, the widow's house breathed around her, warm and asleep. On the table, the map lay where she'd left it, ink shifting in the moonlight, and she looked at it with new eyes — her mother's map, her mother's errand, delivered by a dying stranger with thirteen turns left on some clock Gideon was only beginning to understand. An apology, the archivist had hinted. Delivered late.

"Well," Gideon told the map, and her voice was steadier than she had any right to expect. "You're not getting opened tonight either. Apparently I'm on strike against revelations."

Outside, the moon was rising — fat and low and counting something. She counted along out of habit, the way Perrin had taught her, moons marked against the stranger's dying whisper. Seven gone. Six left.

Six left, and a sealed jar against her ribs, and a question she could not make herself ask, and somewhere beneath a mountain a girl fading a little more each night, waiting for a sister who might be a stranger, or a thief, or nobody at all.

Gideon lay down on the widow's spare bed with her boots on, one hand pressed flat over the jar, and slept the way soldiers sleep: lightly, armed, and dreaming of kitchens.

She woke near dawn to a soft scrape downstairs — the particular, practiced scrape of a trapdoor being lifted by someone who hoped it wasn't watched.

Perrin, she thought, already moving, was going to have a very bad morning.
