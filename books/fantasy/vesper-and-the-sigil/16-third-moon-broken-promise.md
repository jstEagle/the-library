## 16. Third Moon, Broken Promise

Perrin had stolen from kings, priests, a dragon's hoard (one coin, and the dragon never missed it, which he counted as his finest work), and once, memorably, from a wedding — the rings, mid-ceremony, off the pillow, while disguised as a flower arrangement. He was very good at stealing. It was practically his whole personality.

So it offended him deeply, on a professional level, how hard it was to steal one wax-sealed map from a sleeping woman.

The problem was Gideon Okafor didn't sleep like other people. She slept like someone who had spent her whole life taking things from others and expected the favor returned at any moment. Her breathing stayed even, but her hand lay near the map's oilcloth wrapping even in sleep, fingers curled loosely around it the way a child holds a parent's sleeve.

He'd tried three nights running. Night one, she'd rolled over and pinned the corner of the cloth under her elbow without waking. Night two, a dream made her mutter something in a language Perrin didn't know, and her grip had tightened until the knuckles went pale. Tonight — night three, third moon fat and low over the salt flats' edge, the two of them camped in the lee of a fallen waystone — tonight she'd finally gone deep. He could tell by the stillness of her, the total surrender of the body that only came when exhaustion won its argument with vigilance.

*Now or never*, he told himself, and hated how much *never* appealed.

Because here was the thing, the thing he'd been chewing on since the second moon, since the wolves, since he'd watched her feed a hunter a false memory so vivid the man had simply come apart like wet paper: this quest was going to kill them both. Not maybe. Not possibly. The map led under the mountain, and nothing that went under the mountain came back out wearing its own face. His sister had proven that. Ama had come out of there dying, holding this map, spending her last breath on a stranger with thief's hands because she thought — what? That debts could be paid after death? That a magpie-hearted guide would honor a promise made to a corpse?

Perrin looked at Gideon's sleeping face, at the new hollows under her eyes where memories used to be, at the gap he'd noticed growing in her — the way she flinched at rivers now without knowing why, the way she'd stared at a jar of summer light in the last town for a full minute before saying, quietly, "I think I owned this once."

She was being erased. One toll, one payment, one hungry market at a time. And when they reached the court beneath the mountain, whatever was left of her would be exactly the kind of thing courts collected.

Better it ended here. Better she woke furious on the wrong side of the rim, alive, magic-dead, whole — hating him, yes, but hating him in a valley where nothing could follow her. She'd get over it. People got over magpies. It was practically traditional.

"Sorry, Okafor," he murmured. "You can invoice me later."

Her fingers slid free of the oilcloth like water off a feather. He had the map. He had the map, and it hadn't burned him, and no ink crawled toward scenes he almost remembered, because it wasn't *his* past coiled in that wax seal — it was hers, and Ama's, and it wanted nothing from him except to be carried away fast.

He was three steps into the dark when the guilt hit, which was embarrassing, because he'd budgeted for the guilt to arrive somewhere around the rim.

It arrived early and it brought friends.

*She gave you bread,* said one friend. *You were starving after the market town and she split her last loaf without asking what you'd done to deserve it.*

*She caught you,* said another friend, referring to the incident with the salt merchant's purse, and instead of rage she'd laughed until she wheezed and then taught him the better pickpocket's misdirection she used, freely, like a fool, like a person who trusted magpie-hearted guides.

*Your sister chose her,* said the worst friend, the one with Ama's voice. *Not you. Me. She handed the map to a stranger because she knew you'd do exactly this.*

"Shut up," Perrin told the night.

The night did not shut up. The night contained, as it happened, Gideon Okafor, sitting up.

Not lunging. Not shouting. Sitting up with her blanket pooled around her waist, watching him with an expression he couldn't read in the moonlight, though he got the impression it wasn't fury.

"How long," she said.

"...How long what."

"How long have you been trying?"

Perrin considered lying. He was good at lying. But there was something about a woman who had watched him fail three consecutive nights and apparently slept through all of them out of *politeness* that made lying feel like kicking a limping dog.

"Three nights," he admitted.

"And tonight you got it because I let go." She held out her hand, palm up. Not grabbing. Just open. "Give it back, Perrin."

"I'm not giving it back. I'm saving your life. There's a difference, and the difference is I'm doing it for free, which if you knew anything about me—"

"You're doing it because you're scared."

That landed harder than it should have. He stood there with the map against his chest and the third moon pouring down like cold milk, and everything he'd rehearsed — the speech about self-preservation, about the wolves, about the arithmetic of survival — crumbled in his mouth like old bread.

"Of course I'm scared," he said. "I'm the only sensible person on this entire road. You're trading pieces of yourself away like a drunk paying a bar tab. Every moon that turns, there's less of you standing there, and you keep *smiling about it*, like forgetting is some kind of progress. My sister died carrying this thing. Died *of* carrying this thing. And now it's eating you too, and I'm supposed to just walk beside it happening and make jokes?" His voice cracked, which he covered with a cough, badly. "I've buried everyone, Okafor. Everyone. I am not burying you too. So I'm taking the map, and I'm going back to the valley, and you—" His throat closed. "You can hate me somewhere safe."

Silence. Wind moving through the dry grass. Somewhere far off, the salt flats sang their low buried-voices hum, and Gideon turned her head toward it like a woman listening to a name.

Then she did the last thing he expected, which was to pat the ground beside her.

"Sit down, Perrin."

"I'm not—"

"Sit *down*. You've been circling my bedroll for three nights like a moth with a conscience. Your wings must be tired."

He sat. Because his legs decided to without consulting him, which was happening to him a lot lately, and he was starting to suspect it was her fault.

Gideon held out her hand again. This time he put the map in it, and the relief that went through him was so violent and immediate that he understood, with sick clarity, that some part of him had been waiting three nights to be stopped.

Instead of tucking it away, she pressed the oilcloth bundle back toward him. "Hold this."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to give you something, and I want your hands full so you can't run while I do it."

"That's — that is *deeply* manipulative, and I want you to know I respect it enormously—"

"Perrin."

He shut up. Held the map. Watched her close her eyes.

And then she took hold of something behind her ribs — he saw it happen, saw the small wince, the way her breath caught — and she pulled, and she opened her hands between them, and there was a memory sitting in her palms like a coal that glowed instead of burned.

"I don't pay people in stolen goods anymore," she said. "I learned that lesson recently and it cost me a river I can't remember. So this one's not taken. This one's mine, and I'm giving it. Freely. No debt. No ledger. No string attached, not even a short one."

"What is it?"

"The day my mother taught me to bake bread." Her voice went strange and soft. "I was six. Flour everywhere. She let me shape my own loaf and it came out looking like a boot, and we laughed so hard the neighbors complained, and she wrote my name in flour on the table and said, *whatever else they take from you, girl, nobody can take the making.*" She swallowed. "I've kept it sealed for years because it hurt too much to carry and too much to lose. But it turns out keeping a thing locked up isn't the same as having it. So."

She pushed it toward him. "Take it. It's yours. Not a payment. A gift. That's the whole point — gifts don't balance anything. They just... exist. Like people do."

Perrin stared at the little glowing coal of somebody's childhood, warm against his palms, and felt something in his chest crack along a line he'd thought had scarred over years ago.

"I can't—"

"You can. And here's the part you're going to hate: I'm not doing it to be kind. I'm doing it because you're right — I *am* scared, and I *am* coming apart piece by piece, and I cannot do the next moons alone. I need a friend more than I need a guide. Friends are rarer." She shrugged, and her eyes were bright and fierce in the moonlight. "So I'm buying one. With bread."

"That's not how friendship works."

"It's exactly how friendship works. Ask any baker."

And Perrin — who had stolen from kings and dragons and weddings, who had never once in his life been given anything that didn't come with a hook hidden in it — held a dead woman's memory of flour and laughter in his cupped hands and felt it settle into him like a seed into soil. He would carry it. He would keep it safe. And someday, he understood dimly, he would give it back to her, and that would be the point of the whole exercise: not the taking, not the keeping, but the *returning*.

"Oh, I hate this," he said thickly. "I hate this so much."

"I know."

"My sister would have loved you. That's the worst part. She'd have found you *hilarious*."

"She sounds like she had terrible taste."

"She did. She liked me." He wiped his face with his sleeve, aggressively, as though attacking an enemy. "Fine. Fine! We go on. Under the mountain, into the court, certain doom, all of it. But I'm keeping the map in my coat from now on, and you're getting a new bedroll, because yours has a hole in it and I refuse to mourn a woman who died of a draft."

"Generous."

"I contain multitudes." He tucked the memory carefully into the inner pocket where he kept the things that mattered — it was a shallow pocket; it always had been — and then paused, struck by a thought. "Okafor. The bread memory. When this is over. I give it back?"

"When this is over," she said, "you give it back. That's the deal."

"And if it's not over? If we don't—"

"Then you keep it," Gideon said simply. "Somebody should remember my mother taught me something besides thieving."

Above them the third moon leaned westward, one turn closer to thirteen, and far off across the dark the salt flats changed their song — lower now, slower, and underneath the buried voices Perrin could have sworn he heard a new note, thin and clear and patient, like something beneath the mountain turning over in its sleep and beginning, at last, to listen.

Gideon heard it too. He saw her go still, head tilted, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and certain in a way that raised every feather he had.

"She knows I'm coming," Gideon said. "Whoever she is — she knows."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, as the third moon thinned to a nail-paring above the eastern ridgeline. Perrin walked ahead, map secure in his coat, the bread-memory warm against his heart, and found — to his considerable irritation — that he was humming. A lullaby. One he didn't know, in a key he shouldn't recognize, that had come with the memory like a fly in amber.

"Stop that," Gideon said from behind him.

"Stop what?"

"Humming. That song." A pause. "Where did you hear that song?"

Perrin frowned. "Nowhere. It came with the bread. Why? What is it?"

Gideon had gone very white in the grey morning light, and when she answered, her voice was barely more than breath.

"It's the song from my dreams," she said. "The ones I've been having since the second moon. Someone's been singing it to me from under the mountain, Perrin. And now it's in your pocket too."

Behind them, far to the south, the first light touched the salt flats, and the flats began — softly, sweetly, terribly — to sing it back.
