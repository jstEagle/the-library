## 17. The Salt Flats Sing

The flats began where the road gave up.

One moment there was a track of sorts — rutted, apologetic, the kind of path that existed mostly out of habit — and then it simply stopped, and the world went white. Salt stretched to every horizon, cracked into hexagons like the shell of some enormous sleeping thing. The sun sat on it and bounced back up again, so that they walked inside a brightness with no top and no bottom.

"Beautiful," Perrin said, in the tone of a man reading his own death warrant.

"You said that about the marsh."

"The marsh had shade."

"The marsh also had leeches."

"The marsh," Perrin said, adjusting the strap of his pack with great dignity, "had *character*. This has nothing. This is what the world looks like after it's been robbed." He glanced sideways at her, and immediately looked sorry he'd said it.

Gideon let it go. She was getting better at letting things go; she'd had practice lately, letting whole summers go, whole childhoods, whole reasons for flinching at rivers. One more careless metaphor from a magpie wasn't going to crack her open.

Probably.

They walked. The salt crunched under their boots in a way that was satisfying for the first hundred steps and maddening for the next ten thousand. By midday the heat came up off the ground in visible waves, and Perrin had abandoned dignity entirely, walking with his shirt off and his feathers — the ones that showed when he forgot to be careful — fluffed out flat to catch any breeze that might wander by and take pity on him.

"There should be a well at the center," he said. "Old maps mark one. A shrine, maybe."

"Your sister's map doesn't."

"My sister's map is a *drama queen*," Perrin said fondly. "It only shows you what it wants you to see. Which, historically, has been trouble."

The map, currently rolled tight in Gideon's breast pocket, gave a small warm pulse against her ribs, as if offended. It did that sometimes. She'd stopped apologizing to it.

They walked on.

It was near evening — the light gone long and gold, their shadows stretched out behind them like two thin black debts — when Gideon first heard the humming.

She stopped mid-stride. "Perrin."

"I heard it." He'd gone still in the way birds go still, head cocked, one eye fixed on the ground as though the ground owed him money. "That's not wind. Wind doesn't hold a note."

He was right. It wasn't wind. It came up through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her ankles, a low sustained tone like a finger run around the rim of a glass — except the glass was the entire world, and the note didn't waver or fade. It just *was*, patient as geology.

And underneath the note — she bent closer, pressing her palm flat against the salt, which still held the day's heat like a held breath — underneath it were voices.

Not voices, exactly. Not words. More like the memory of voices, the shape a voice leaves behind in a room after everyone has gone home. The flats were full of them. Hundreds. Thousands. Layered over each other like sediment, like the pages of a book pressed shut for centuries.

"Perrin," she said slowly, "what is this place?"

He crouched beside her, and for once he didn't joke. That frightened her more than anything else could have. "My grandmother told me about it once," he said quietly. "When I was small. I thought it was a story to keep children off the flats after dark." He swallowed. "They say this used to be a lake. Before the courts, before the contracts. And when the famine years came — the first ones, the bad ones — people came here from everywhere. Whole villages. And they couldn't carry what they knew. So they buried it."

"Buried it how?"

"The old way. The way before jars, before markets, before anyone thought to put a price on it." He touched the salt crust gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "You sing your memory into the ground and you walk away lighter. The land keeps it for you. They called it giving something back to the water." He paused. "There isn't any water anymore. But the land remembers being a lake. I think—" He shook his head. "I think that's why it holds them so well. Everything here thinks it's still holding rain."

Gideon knelt there a long moment, hand flat on the white earth, listening to the hum of ten thousand griefs that someone, somewhere, had decided were too heavy to carry across a border.

"They never came back for them?" she asked.

"Some did. Most didn't." Perrin's voice was very soft. "Once you've sung something into the ground, you can't take it back out alone. You need—" He stopped.

"Need what?"

"Someone who knows how to listen. Really listen. Someone who's spent their life handling other people's memories without breaking them." He turned and looked at her, and his black-and-white eye caught the last of the light. "Someone like a thief, Gideon."

* * *

They made camp where they stood, because there was nowhere else to stand. Perrin built a fire out of dried scrub he'd been hoarding in his pack since the last hills, muttering the whole time about foresight and about certain companions who mocked his hoarding right up until it saved their lives. Gideon ate hard bread and watched the stars come out over the flats, and the humming went on beneath everything, low and constant, like the world breathing in its sleep.

She slept badly, which was normal. What wasn't normal was what she dreamed.

She dreamed of a kitchen. Not the archetypal kitchen, not a symbol — a specific one, with a crooked shelf and a clay pot with a chipped rim and a window that stuck in the rainy season and had to be thumped twice, low then high, exactly the way her hands remembered even though the rest of her didn't. Someone was singing in that kitchen. A woman's voice, low and rough at the edges, the kind of voice that had done a lot of talking and not much complaining. The song had no words she could catch. It didn't need any. Her body knew it the way her body knew the window: two notes down, one held, a little lift at the end like a smile.

She woke with tears already cold on her face and no memory of having started crying.

The fire had burned to embers. Perrin sat up at the edge of the light, watching her with an expression she couldn't read in the dark.

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was *considering* saying something," he admitted. "I've decided against it. Mostly because whatever it is, you'll throw bread at me, and we don't have enough bread."

She almost laughed. It came out as something else, something wetter. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and stared at the embers.

"It's my mother," she said. It was the first time she'd said the word aloud in longer than she could measure, and it felt strange in her mouth, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. "The song. It's hers. I don't remember her face. I don't remember her name. But I remember the song, and I don't know why, and I don't know where it came from, because everything I have is stolen, Perrin, everything, and none of it is mine—"

"Gideon."

"—and if it's stolen, whose is it? Because if some poor woman somewhere is missing a lullaby the way Widow Sefu was missing her grief, then I'm worse than a thief, I'm a—"

"*Gideon.*" He said it firmly, the way you'd say it to a horse that was about to bolt. "Listen to the ground."

She listened.

The humming had changed. Or rather — she held her breath and listened harder — it hadn't changed, but she was hearing it differently now, awake instead of dreaming, and one thread of it had come forward out of the weave. Close. Very close. Directly beneath the place where she lay, as though the flats had spent all night arranging themselves around her like a crowd around a stage.

Two notes down. One held. A little lift at the end, like a smile.

Her mother's song, sung into the salt by her mother's own voice, however many years ago, in whatever desperate season had driven a woman to bury the sound of herself in the earth and walk away.

"She was here," Gideon whispered. "She stood right here."

"Or somewhere close. Sound travels funny in these places." Perrin hesitated, then reached over and put his hand briefly on her shoulder — an awkward, unpracticed gesture, like a man patting a bird that might bite him. "Do you want to dig?"

"No," she said.

"Do you want to not-dig loudly, or not-dig quietly?"

She laughed despite everything, a short broken sound. "Loudly, I think."

* * *

They didn't dig. There was nothing to dig for — the memories weren't objects, weren't buried like coins in a pot. They were *in* the land, part of it now, and getting them out would take something Gideon wasn't sure she had. But she spent the next morning walking the flats in slow spirals around their campsite, barefoot, because boots, she discovered, muffled the voices, and she could not bear to be muffled. The salt was cool now, firm underfoot, and every few steps another thread of the hum rose to meet her.

An old man's laugh, buried whole. A child counting to ten in a language Gideon half-recognized. A woman reciting names — a long list of names, over and over, the way you'd recite them if the people they belonged to were gone and the list was all you'd been allowed to keep. A wedding song. An argument, fierce and funny, that ended in the same laughter as the laugh. A prayer. Another prayer. Another.

Ten thousand small surrenders, pressed into the white earth like flowers in a book.

"This is what the court does," Gideon said at midday, standing in the middle of all that buried love with her fists clenched at her sides. "Isn't it. Only they don't wait to be asked. They don't give people a choice about putting things down. They just *take*, and they keep, and they charge interest." She looked out at the horizon, where the mountain was finally visible again — a smudge of dark on the northern edge of the world, patient as a debt. "This place is everything they'll never understand. People *gave* this away. Freely. To spare each other."

Perrin, who had been trailing her at a respectful distance with his arms crossed against the morning chill, uncrossed them. "You know what my sister would say?"

"That I'm standing in the middle of a graveyard getting sentimental."

"Well. Yes. Also that." He came to stand beside her. "She'd say the difference between the court and this place is the difference between a cage and a door. Same bars, sometimes. All in whether anyone chose them." He nudged her with his elbow. "She was smarter than me. Don't tell her I said that. She's dead, but she'd find a way to be insufferable about it."

Gideon was still deciding whether to laugh when the voice came.

It rose out of the ground directly in front of her — not the hum, not the sedimentary murmur, but a single clear thread pulling itself up through the salt like a root growing toward light. And it was speaking. Not singing. Speaking. In her mother's voice, the voice from the kitchen, the voice from the dream, rough-edged and warm and unbearably alive:

*"—and if you're hearing this, then you found the flats, which means you're going the wrong way on purpose, stubborn girl, because nobody crosses the flats unless they mean to reach the mountain—"*

Gideon's knees nearly went.

*"—I paid a thief to make me forget, and it worked, and I want you to know that forgetting was the cruelest thing I ever did to myself, and I did it to survive you, and I am not asking forgiveness, I am telling you how it was—"*

"Perrin," Gideon managed. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Perrin, is this—"

"Memories don't usually talk *at* you," Perrin said. His feathers had risen all along his forearms, the bird in him bristling. "Unless they were left for someone specific. Unless they were *addressed*."

*"—the map will find you, because maps are promises and I kept mine late. Thirteen turns, little star. Whatever you learn about me, whatever you learn about yourself — finish it. Finish it and come home. Home was never the valley. Home was never even me. Home is whoever's waiting at the end of the road, and she has been waiting so much longer than you have—"*

The voice frayed. Thinned. The last words came out on the little lift, the smile-note, the one her body knew:

*"—two notes down, one held. Sing it back someday, and wherever I am, I'll hear it."*

Then silence. Real silence, the hum dimmed all around them, as if the entire lakebed of buried hearts had leaned in to listen and was now, respectfully, looking away.

Gideon stood in the white emptiness with her mother's voice still ringing in her skull and her heart doing something complicated and painful in her chest. She became distantly aware that she was crying again, freely, messily, and that Perrin had turned around to give her privacy while remaining close enough to intervene, which was such a perfectly Perrin thing to do that it almost made her cry harder.

"Little star," she said at last, wiping her face. "She called me little star."

"Vesper," Perrin said quietly, from behind her. "Evening star. First light you can see at dusk." He came around to face her, and his expression was gentler than she'd ever seen it. "It's a good name. My sister would've liked it."

"It's not my name. My name is Gideon Okafor. It's the only thing I own outright. I checked."

"Of course it is." He handed her the waterskin. "Drink. Crying dehydrates you. It's why I avoid it."

She drank. The water tasted of the skin and of iron and of the long road, and she was grateful for every ordinary mouthful of it.

They walked north that afternoon, toward the smudge of mountain, and the flats sang beneath them the whole way — softer now, or perhaps she was simply learning to carry it, the way you learn to carry a tune. Two notes down, one held. She found herself humming it under her breath as they walked, quietly, an offering rather than a theft, and if the land noticed, it didn't object. Somewhere under miles of salt, she liked to think, a tired woman who had walked away from everything finally heard her daughter coming home.

They camped that night at the flats' far edge, where the first gray stones of the foothills broke through the white like knuckles through skin. Gideon took first watch, wrapped in her cloak, watching the moon rise — fat and silver, six nights past new, climbing its slow ladder toward thirteen.

She was so busy watching it that she didn't notice the figure until it spoke.

"Now *that*," said a voice she knew, silky and hungry and appallingly familiar, "is a lullaby worth tasting."

The memory-eater from the market stepped out of the shadows between the stones, smiling, and his eyes went straight to her breast pocket — to the sealed jar she had carried, unopened, through everything.

"And I do believe," he said, "you've brought dessert."
