## 18. Court-Stamped

The salt flats were three days behind them, and Gideon still heard the humming in her teeth.

She'd tried to explain it to Perrin that first night, sitting over a fire so small it was more apology than warmth. *The land sang, Perrin. It sang my mother's voice.* He had nodded the way you nod at someone who has been drinking, kindly and without belief, and then he had said, "Well. Flats do that," and gone to sleep with his back to her, feathers ruffled in a way that meant he was thinking too hard to face anyone.

So she stopped talking about it. But she kept hearing it.

*Come home, little bird,* the flats had hummed, in a voice worn smooth as river stone. *Come home, come home.* And now, every time the wind moved through the scrub pines where they'd made camp, she heard the same cadence, the same lullaby-shape, though the words wouldn't resolve. Her mother's voice, dissolved into geography. She didn't know whether that was a mercy or a cruelty. She suspected it was both, which seemed to be the theme of her entire existence lately.

The sealed jar rode against her ribs in its oilcloth wrapping, tied to the inside of her coat where a thief keeps the things she cannot bear to lose and cannot bear to look at. It had been there since the burned library. Since the archivist's blind fingers had traced the map and said, in his ash-soft voice, *two girls, one contract, one soul kept as surety*. Since she had gone digging through her own hoard — thirty years of stolen griefs and first loves and childhood summers, all of them hers by theft, none of them hers by right — and found, at the very bottom, wrapped in wax and twine, a jar labeled in handwriting she knew better than her own.

*M for the little one who stayed.*

Her mother's hand. Her mother's seal.

She had not opened it. She had told herself this was discipline, that a good thief never cracks a container until she knows what's guarding it. She had almost believed herself.

Perrin knew about the jar. Of course he did; he was a magpie, and magpies know where every shiny thing sleeps. But he hadn't asked to see it, which was either respect or strategy, and with Perrin it was usually both.

They were camped in a fold of hills called the Cinder Teeth, old volcanic spires worn down to gray nubs, when the smell arrived.

It came on the evening wind: something like candle smoke and something like the inside of a coin purse and something like — Gideon's stomach turned before her mind caught up — like a memory being tasted by the wrong mouth. She knew that smell. The market under the tarpaulins, the rows of connoisseurs swirling other people's pasts in little glass cups, and the eater himself, the one with the gray silk gloves, the one who had tasted a drop of her memory from the air alone and gone white as milk.

*Court-stamped,* he had whispered, like a man finding a crown in a beggar's purse. *Where did you get court-stamped goods?*

She had run, because running is what thieves do, and Perrin had covered their tracks with a flurry of black wings and profanity. She'd hoped that was the end of him.

Hopes, she was learning, were just debts you took out against your future self.

"Gideon." Perrin's voice, low, from the edge of the camp. He was standing very still, head cocked, one eye fixed on the dark between two spires. "We have a guest."

"I smelled."

"He's been circling since noon. I thought he'd give up." A pause. "Magpies don't circle for six hours unless we're interested. He's not a magpie."

"No," Gideon said. "He's worse. He's patient."

She stood, untied the jar from her ribs — no. She checked that it was tied, that the knots held, that the oilcloth sat snug. Then she walked out to meet the dark between the spires, because if the man wanted a conversation, better on her feet than cornered.

He stepped out of the shadows like smoke deciding to be polite. Tall, narrow, wrapped in that same gray silk, gloved to the fingertips. His face was unremarkable in the way of faces that have eaten so many others they've forgotten to keep one of their own — pleasant, forgettable, with eyes that were slightly too wet, like stones from a riverbed.

"Thief," he said warmly. "You're a difficult woman to follow."

"You followed badly, then. I've been here all evening."

"Ah, but I wasn't following *you*." He smiled, and the smile had too many memories in it, most of them borrowed. "I was following the scent. Do you know what you smell like, from a distance? From a distance, you smell like a market stall left open in the rain. Thirty years of goods, all jumbled together. Grief, and summer grass, and somebody's wedding, and a dog — whose dog was that, by the way? Lovely animal. Terrible ending."

"Get to it."

"Direct. Good. Connoisseurs appreciate directness; it leaves more time for tasting." He drifted a step closer, and Gideon held her ground, mostly out of stubbornness and partly because her boots felt nailed down. "In the market, you gave me a taste. An accident — your wards slipped, or you let them, I've never decided which flatters you less. And what I tasted was stamped. Sealed. The mark of the Under-Court pressed into the memory itself, the way a chandler presses his mark into wax." His wet eyes gleamed. "Court-stamped goods don't circulate, thief. Court-stamped goods are *inventory*. Which means somewhere in that lovely cluttered hoard of yours is a piece of inventory that walked out of the mountain."

"Or," Gideon said, "your palate's off. Happens with age."

"My palate," he said gently, "is perfect. It's the only part of me that is." He spread his gloved hands. "So. Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I am old and rich and tired of chasing. Name any price. Any. Gold, passage, protection — I hold favors from people whose names would curdle your blood. Or—" and here his voice dropped into something almost tender, almost human, and that was the frightening part, "—or a trade. One memory of mine for the jar. I own a sunset over a city that no longer exists. I own a mother's voice singing. I could give you back things you never had."

Gideon's heart was doing something complicated and painful.

Because that was the hook of it, wasn't it. *A mother's voice singing.* Three days since the salt flats, and she would have cut off a finger to hear that voice again whole instead of dissolved into wind. The eater knew. Eaters always know; hunger makes them excellent readers. He had looked at her once, at a woman carrying a sealed jar against her ribs like a splinter working toward her heart, and he had known exactly which bait to tie to the line.

"That's a generous offer," she said, and was proud of how steady it came out.

"It is ruinously generous. That's how you know it matters." He tilted his head. "What's in the jar, thief?"

"Nothing. Wax and sentiment."

"Mm." He breathed in, long and slow, through the nose, and Gideon watched his pupils blow wide, watched his whole body lean toward her like a plant toward light, and terror went through her cold and clean as well water. Because she had seen hunger before — she dealt in it daily; half her clients wept with wanting — but this was different. This was a man looking at a meal and a revelation at once. This was hunger with theology behind it.

"Oh," he breathed. "Oh, it's not even sealed properly anymore, is it. You've carried it too close. Want leaks, thief. Want gets into the wax. I can smell it from here — I can smell *her*—"

"Stop."

"—the little one who stayed, oh, that's a label, that's a *story*, that's—"

Gideon drew her knife. It was a small knife, honest, good for cutting twine and liars' purses, and it looked absurd pointed at a creature made of appetite. "You'll stop speaking about that jar now."

The eater looked at the knife. Then he laughed — softly, delightedly, the laugh of a man at a very good play. "You won't use it. Not because you're squeamish. Because if you spill me, everything I know spills with me, and you are *dying* to know what I know." He clasped his hands. "Aren't you. You've been dying since the library. Two girls, one contract — the archivist told you the shape of it, but shapes aren't names, are they? Shapes aren't *which sister*. You've been carrying a question against your ribs for weeks, and I am offering to answer it, and you're holding a butter knife about it."

The fire crackled behind her. Somewhere upslope, Perrin made a small sound — a warning, or a prayer, hard to tell with magpies.

"What do you know?" Gideon asked. Her voice came out low and rough.

"I know what the court stamps and why. I know that souls are sold but names are kept — everyone in my trade knows that much; it's why we can't eat a name, only the memories wearing it. I know that contracts beneath the mountain are written twice, once in ink and once in flesh, and the flesh copy doesn't burn." His wet eyes fixed on the oilcloth bundle. "And I know that whatever is in that jar, the court wants it back so badly that they've had hunters on the roads since the second moon. Hollow-eyed riders, thief. You've met them. You fed one a lie so vivid it unmade him — yes, I heard about that too; the roads gossip worse than magpies. They're not hunting the map anymore. They're hunting the *jar*."

The night got very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"So here is the truth of my offer, stripped of manners," the eater said. "Sell me the jar tonight, and the hunters lose the scent by morning — my appetite hides things, thief; nothing vanishes down my throat and comes back out again. Keep it, and every rider between here and the mountain will smell what I smell. You are carrying a lit lantern through a dark full of wolves, and you're doing it because you can't bear to look inside the lantern." He smiled again, gentler now, which was worse. "I'm not your enemy. I'm just the first honest thing you've met on this road. Sell it to me. Open it yourself. Either way, the not-knowing ends tonight. Those are the only doors out of this hillside."

Gideon stood very still.

And the terrible thing — the thing she would think about for many nights after — was that some traitorous cellar of her mind had already begun to bargain. *One taste,* it whispered. *Let him taste it and tell you. You don't have to open it yourself. Let someone else carry the knowing for a while, the way you've carried other people's griefs. That's your whole trade, isn't it? Taking what others can't hold.*

It would be so easy. It would cost her nothing but the last thing she had.

Her hand rose — slowly, without her permission — toward the oilcloth bundle.

Behind her, quick and light across the scree, came the click of a magpie's feet on stone, and then Perrin's voice, human-shaped, dry as chalk: "If you sell our luggage to the gentleman in the gloves, I want it noted that I objected. Loudly. In writing."

The eater turned his wet smile toward Perrin. "The bird has opinions."

"The bird has a sister buried under a cairn who stole that map out of the mountain with her fingernails," Perrin said, and his voice didn't waver, but his feathers did, along his jaw where the shift lived. "Whatever's in that jar, she bled for the road it's traveling. So no. It's not for sale. Not for gold, not for sunsets, not for dead mothers' voices." He hopped up onto the rock beside Gideon, arms crossed, and Gideon realized her hand was still hovering an inch from the bundle, trembling. "Especially not for those."

Silence. The eater regarded them both, and something flickered behind his forgettable face — disappointment, yes, but underneath it, faintly, something like relief. As if some small drowned remnant of the man he'd been before the eating was glad, in a distant way, to be refused.

"Well," he said at last. "Loyalty. How quaint. How *expensive*." He began to fold backward into the dark between the spires, becoming smoke becoming shadow becoming suggestion. "Keep your lantern lit, thief. The wolves are closer than you think — the third moon turns tomorrow, and riders ride fastest on the turn. And when you finally open that jar — you will, you know; want always wins, it's the oldest law there is — remember that I offered to buy it kindly. Remember that some of us down here in the dark were only ever hungry. Not cruel. Just hungry."

The dark closed over him like water.

Gideon stood a long moment with her hand pressed flat against the oilcloth, feeling the shape of the jar beneath, round and patient as an egg. Her heart was going like a trapped bird. Perrin said nothing, which for Perrin was practically a eulogy.

Finally she sat down by the fire. Finally she spoke.

"She offered me my mother's voice, Perrin. Singing." The flames bent and swayed. "Do you know how much I want that?"

"Yeah," Perrin said quietly. "I do, actually." He settled beside her, feathers settling, and after a moment he added, in the tone of a bird pretending not to care about the answer: "That lullaby from the flats. The one you say the land was humming. What was the tune?"

And Gideon opened her mouth to say *I don't know, I couldn't make out the words* —

—and found, to her horror, that she was humming it. Low, barely voiced, a thread of melody rising out of her without her leave, a tune she had no memory of learning, in a voice that was almost — for one uncanny half-second before she strangled it silent — not entirely her own.

The fire popped. Perrin stared at her.

"Gideon," he said slowly. "Where did that come from?"

She looked down at the oilcloth bundle against her ribs, and beneath her palm, faint as a heartbeat, she could have sworn the jar had gone warm.

"I don't know," she said. "But I think it's starting to answer back."
