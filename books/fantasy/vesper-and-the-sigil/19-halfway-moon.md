## 19. Halfway Moon

Adaeze had learned to count the moons by the color of the light that leaked through the vault ceiling.

It was not a real sky. She had understood that within her first week below — the great dome above the Court of Purchased Selves was painted glass, and behind the glass burned lamps tended by servants who never spoke and never blinked quite right. But the lamps were changed on a schedule, and the schedule followed the moon above, and so Adaeze told time the way she'd once told it in the kitchen at home: by watching for a change in the light and naming it after something she loved.

The first moon had been white as flour. The second, honey-colored. This one — the sixth, though she'd lost track of whether the court counted from her arrival or from some older bargain — came down through the dome thin and silver, like milk poured into water.

Halfway, she thought. Halfway gone.

She could feel it in her hands. That was the strange part. Not her heart, not her head — her hands. They had begun to look translucent at the edges when she held them up to the lamp-light, like paper held against a window. Some mornings she woke and could see the shape of her own fingers through the blanket.

"You're fading," said the jar beside her bed.

"I know," Adaeze said.

"It's very rude of you."

The jar's name was Ossai, and he had been in the vault for two hundred years, and he complained the way old men complain about weather: constantly, comfortably, and without any expectation that anything would be done about it. He was one of the purchased souls shelved nearest her alcove — the court kept its favorite acquisitions close, the way misers keep their best coins in a locked drawer rather than spending them — and in the long silver hours between feedings, he was the only conversation she had.

"Rude," Adaeze repeated. "I'm being *eaten*, Ossai."

"Yes, but slowly, and with excellent manners. There's a difference between being devoured and being *dined upon*. You should be flattered. They served you at the Equinox Banquet last year. The Under-Court itself tasted you." A pause, the sound a jar makes when its occupant shifts his weight, which is no sound at all and yet somehow audible in the vault's hush. "They said you were notes of grief and summer. Very sophisticated pairing."

"That's horrifying."

"That's *reputation*, girl. Do you know how many souls down here never get tasted? Shelf nine hasn't been opened since the reign of the second Masked Regent. You're practically famous."

Adaeze lay back and looked up at the painted glass, where the false moonlight pooled like spilled silver. Famous. Fading. Fed upon at banquets by things wearing masks over faces that might not have been faces underneath.

And still, somewhere under all of it, stubborn as a weed growing through a floorboard: hope.

* * *

Here is what the court did not understand about souls, or perhaps understood and dismissed, because courts dismiss whatever cannot be taxed:

A soul is not a jar of wine. It does not simply sit and wait to be poured.

Adaeze had been sold — that word again, *sold*, and every time she thought it she felt the shape of her mother's tears, warm on a face she could no longer fully picture — and the contract had taken her soul out of her body and sealed it here, in this alcove, in this vault, in this mountain's cold throat. Her body, as far as she knew, walked and worked somewhere above, empty as a coat on a hook. What remained in the vault was the essential her: memory, love, will, the particular stubbornness that had made her, at age seven, refuse to eat her vegetables for an entire season until her mother wept with laughter and gave up.

That stubbornness was still hers. The court could sip it, but they couldn't drink it dry, because a soul regenerates around its deepest wound the way water regenerates around a stone.

Her deepest wound was the twin.

She didn't remember her sister's face anymore. That was the first thing the fading took — not the big memories, which fought back, but the small ones, the load-bearing ones. The face went first. Then the specific sound of her voice. Then the games they'd played, dissolving into a general warmth, a sense of *someone*, a hand holding hers in a dark that might have been a bedroom or might have been this vault.

But the lullaby stayed. The lullaby was sunk too deep for even the court's long straws.

*"Sleep, my pearl, the moon is bread,*
*I'll break a piece for your small bed—"*

She sang it sometimes, quietly, when the feeding-lamps dimmed. Ossai pretended not to listen, which was his way of listening closely.

"You hum that every half-moon," he said one night. "Why?"

"I don't know why the timing matters. It just feels right. Like the song belongs to the moon somehow."

"Hm." Ossai was quiet a moment. "My wife used to sing to our goats. Terrible songs. Off-key. The goats loved her anyway." His voice did the thing it did when he talked about before — went smooth and distant, like a stone worn by water. "Everything I was is in this jar, girl. Two hundred years, and I can tell you the goats' names but not my wife's face. Do you know what I've concluded?"

"What?"

"The court thinks it bought us whole. But a soul isn't a *thing*. It's a *direction*. It's which way you lean. And they can't buy which way I lean." A pause. "I lean toward her. Still. After two hundred years. Let them put *that* in their ledger."

Adaeze stared at the ceiling. Somewhere above, past the painted glass, past the galleries of bottled selves, past the bone stair and the three doors and the whole enormous weight of the mountain, there was a sky with a real moon in it, and a world, and — maybe — someone leaning toward her.

"Ossai," she said slowly, "what happens to dreams?"

"Dreams? Whose dreams?"

"Souls'. If a soul dreams, where does the dream go? The body's not here. The soul's not *there*. Where does it go?"

The jar was silent for so long she thought he'd drifted into whatever passed for sleep among the purchased. Then: "Now *that's* a question. In two hundred years, no one's asked me a question. They ask me to recite. They ask me to remember. Nobody asks me to *think*." Another silence, shorter. "I'd say... a dream needs somewhere to land. Like rain. And if your body's far away..." 

"Then maybe it lands somewhere else."

"Maybe it lands wherever you lean."

Adaeze sat up in her alcove, and for the first time in six moons, she smiled at the painted sky.

* * *

The problem, of course, was that she had almost nothing left to send.

Fading was not dramatic. It was not a candle guttering; it was ink in water, a slow diffusion of self into the vault's hungry air. Each feeding took a little more. She had tried, in the third moon, to hold on to everything — gripping her memories like a child clutching sweets — and had learned that gripped things fade faster, because the court noticed tension in the ledger and came to smooth it out.

So she had learned instead to be boring.

When the tasters came — robed figures with masked faces and long silver straws that made her think, absurdly, of the drinking birds her mother had once pointed at in a market stall — she offered them the shallow stuff. The taste of plain porridge. The boredom of a long rainy afternoon. The memory of counting fence posts, all four hundred of them, twice. She buried her treasures under acres of dullness, and the tasters, sipping lazily, found nothing worth returning for.

*Let them drink fence posts,* she thought viciously. *Let them choke on weather.*

And in the hollow spaces the court left unguarded — the hours between feedings, the silver light, the quiet — she began to build messages.

Not letters. She had no ink, no hands, no paper. Dreams were built of other materials. She started with what was strongest: the lullaby. She sang it into herself every night, over and over, until it was less a song than a heartbeat, and then she wrapped other things inside it, the way you hide medicine in honey. A kitchen. Smoke rising from a clay stove. Two pairs of small shoes side by side at a door, one pair scuffed from kicking stones, one pair kept clean because its owner was the careful one.

She didn't know if the shoes were true. That was the terror of it — her memory of her sister had worn down to a warm blur, and she was building a message out of guesswork and longing. Maybe the shoes were wrong. Maybe the kitchen was wrong. Maybe she was sending a stranger's childhood into the night and it would land nowhere, or worse, land in the wrong dreams entirely, in the dreams of some masked courtier who would wake and say, *how curious, I dreamed of a kitchen,* and think nothing of it.

But Ossai had said it: a dream lands wherever you lean.

And she leaned toward a sister whose face she'd lost but whose absence she felt in her body like a missing tooth — that constant, probing soreness where something used to be.

*If you're out there,* she thought, folding the kitchen into the lullaby, folding the shoes into the smoke, *if you're anywhere, if mother sent someone up the road instead of me, if anyone remembers — then catch this. Catch this, catch this, catch this.*

On the seventh repetition, the dream left her.

She felt it go — a lightness, a letting-down, like setting down a basket she'd forgotten she was carrying. It rose through the vault's ceiling, through the painted glass, through galleries full of sleeping jars, and she imagined it climbing the bone stair two steps at a time, humming the lullaby off-key, kicking stones.

"Well?" Ossai said. "Did it work?"

"I don't know yet."

"How will you know?"

"I'll know if I stop feeling so alone."

The jar considered this. "That's either very wise or very sad."

"Both," Adaeze said. "Mostly both."

* * *

The feedings got harder after that. Whether the court sensed something — a stirring in the ledger, a soul behaving oddly — or whether it was simply the halfway moon, when the lamps ran brightest and the Under-Court grew greedy, she couldn't say. But the tasters came more often, and their straws reached deeper, and one night a figure paused at her alcove longer than usual, its mask tilted, reading her the way a merchant reads a coin suspected of being clipped.

"Notes of defiance," it murmured, in a voice like dust settling. "New flavor."

"Fence posts," Adaeze said. "Mostly fence posts."

The mask tilted further. For a moment she was sure it would drink deep, would find the lullaby coiled at her center like a protected heart, would carry her secret up to the Under-Court and have her sealed in iron, silenced, leaned in a direction no dream could follow.

Then a bell rang somewhere in the upper galleries — the summons-bell, the one that meant a magistrate wanted tasting, now, immediately — and the figure straightened with the alacrity of a servant who knows better than to keep power waiting, and swept away, robes whispering.

Adaeze let out a breath she didn't technically need.

"You're going to get us both shelved on nine," Ossai grumbled.

"Worth it."

"Nothing is worth shelf nine. There's a soul on nine who believes he's a teapot. Two hundred years, girl. He whistles when it rains."

Despite everything, Adaeze laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real. The court could take her memories one strawful at a time. It could thin her hands to paper and her past to fog. But apparently it could not take the particular pleasure of making an old man complain, and so long as she had that, she had a direction to lean.

That night, she built a second message. Smaller. Sharper. She was learning the craft of it, the way she'd once learned to knead bread — by feel, by failure, by doing it again. This one she packed with a single image, distilled: two girls, identical, standing in a doorway while their mother cried, and one girl stepping forward onto the mountain road, and one staying, and neither of them knowing which was which anymore, not even her, not even now.

*Which sister stayed?* she asked the image, and sent it climbing into the dark. *Do you know? Does anyone know? Does it matter — will you come anyway?*

The dream rose. The vault slept. On shelf nine, faintly, a teapot whistled.

And far above, in a world Adaeze could only imagine, the halfway moon stood full and silver over a valley that forgot, over a rim, over a road — and somewhere along that road, in whatever shelter a thief and a magpie had found, a girl with stolen memories tucked against her ribs stirred in her sleep, hearing, for the first time in years, a lullaby she had no idea she knew.
