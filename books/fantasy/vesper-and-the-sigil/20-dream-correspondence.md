## 20. Dream Correspondence

The dreams began on the night of the half-moon's waning, and they began, as all the worst things in Gideon Okafor's life did, with bread.

Not real bread. Dream bread. The kind that sits warm on a wooden board and never cools, in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and cardamom and something else she couldn't name — something that made her chest ache before she was even fully asleep enough to know she was dreaming.

She stood in the kitchen. She was small. Her hands were small, flour-dusted, holding a wooden spoon too big for them. And someone was singing.

The lullaby had no words she could hold onto. It was melody the way water is wet — it simply was, moving through her, around her, a voice low and warm and cracked at the edges like an old clay pot that had been mended and mended again. A mother's voice. She knew it the way you know your own heartbeat: not by thinking, but by being unable to imagine yourself without it.

*Hush now, little ember,* the voice sang, or seemed to sing, or something like it. *Bank your light against the cold—*

She woke with tears on her face and no memory of crying, sitting bolt upright in the lean-to Perrin had built them outside the salt flats' edge, her heart hammering a rhythm that matched nothing.

"Gideon." Perrin's voice came sharp through the dark. He slept perched, always, on the lowest branch of the scraggly acacia nearby, because he said sleeping on the ground was for people who hadn't spent years as a bird and didn't know better. "You were singing."

"I don't sing," she said.

"You do when you're asleep. Badly, I'll add. You've been humming the same four notes over and over for the last hour and I want you to know it has been the longest hour of my life, and I once spent three days in a tax collector's pocket."

Gideon wiped her face, furious at the dampness there. "Go back to sleep, Perrin."

"I can't. You've infected me. I'll be humming it till dawn." A rustle of feathers, a grumble, and then — softer, because he was incapable of staying prickly for more than a minute at a time, which was one of the many things she'd never say out loud that she loved about him — "Bad dream?"

"No," she said, which was true, and also a lie, and also the most frightening answer available to her. "It wasn't bad at all. That's what's wrong with it."

He didn't ask what she meant. That was another thing about Perrin. He knew when a door was locked and had the decency not to pick it, even though picking locks was, technically, his entire nature.

* * *

The kitchen came back the next night. And the next.

Each time, a little more of it assembled itself, the way a picture does when you're standing too close and backing away one step at a time. First the bread, then the board, then the window above the basin where two clay cups sat upside down to dry — always two, never one, never three. Then the floor, packed earth swept clean, with a worn path between the hearth and the table where feet had walked ten thousand times.

Then, on the fourth night, the shoes.

They sat by the door. Two pairs. Small ones, child-sized, leather gone soft and dark at the toes from running. One pair slightly larger than the other. One pair with a strap mended in red thread, clumsy, loving stitches, the kind of repair a mother does at midnight by firelight because her daughter cried when the cobbler said the shoe was past saving.

Gideon looked down at her own dream-feet. They were bare. Small. Standing just inside the doorway, just beside the shoes, as if she had only that moment kicked them off.

And a voice behind her — the mended-pot voice, the lullaby voice — said, gently, amused: *Adaeze, how many times must I tell you? Shoes by the mat.*

The name went through her like a spear through water.

She woke gasping, and this time Perrin was already down from his branch, crouched beside her, his dark eyes wide and human and frightened in a way she'd only seen once before — the night the hollow-eyed riders came.

"Your name," he said. "You said a name. Whose name is Adaeze?"

"I don't know," Gideon whispered.

And then, because she was a thief and thieves are honest in exactly one direction, she heard herself add: "That's a lie. I know it the way you know a song you've forgotten you learned. It's mine. Or it was. Or—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Or it belongs to someone who used to have my face."

Perrin was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its banter entirely, and that was somehow worse than anything. "The jar," he said. "Gideon. The jar in your pack. The one you've been carrying since the burned library and pretending is just ballast."

* * *

Here is what Gideon Okafor knew about the sealed jar, and had known, and had arranged her whole mind around not knowing:

She had found it in her own hoard. Not bought, not stolen from any living soul — dug up from the cache she'd buried her first year in the valley, back when she'd been new to the trade and paranoid enough to hide her best work from herself. Among the jars of other people's griefs and joys and ordinary Tuesdays, wrapped in oilcloth at the very bottom, had been this one. Clay, unremarkable, sealed with black wax. And labeled in a hand she recognized without understanding how: a slanted, careful script, letters formed by someone who had learned to write but rarely had cause to practice.

The label read: *For whichever of you comes looking. Forgive me. — M.*

Her mother's hand. She had known that the way she knew the lullaby. The way she knew the name.

And for six moons — six moons of ferry-men and memory-eaters and hunters and salt flats and a sister fading somewhere beneath a mountain — she had carried the jar and not opened it.

"It might be nothing," she'd told Perrin, back on the flats, when he'd first raised it. "It might be someone else's mother. Someone else's apology."

"You buried it in your own cache," Perrin had said.

"People misfile things."

"Under oilcloth. At the bottom. Under a false stone you carved a magpie into so you'd remember it was yours." He'd tilted his head, bird-quick. "I'm a thief too, Gideon. Don't lie to a colleague."

So now, in the grey hour before dawn, with the dream-kitchen still burning behind her eyes and the name *Adaeze* still ringing in her ears like a struck bell, Gideon took the jar out of her pack and held it in both hands and did not open it.

"Take your time," Perrin said, from a tactful distance, which for Perrin was four feet instead of two.

"What if it's worse than not knowing?"

"Then you'll know it's worse, and you'll stop wondering, and wondering is the thing that's been eating you alive since the library." He picked at a feather on his sleeve — a nervous habit, the one part of him the shifting never quite smoothed away. "My sister stole a map out of a court that eats souls rather than keep wondering what was in its vaults. Look how well that turned out for her."

"That's not comforting."

"No. But it's honest, and we're short on honest right now." He paused. "Also, if you don't open it soon, I'm going to open it myself, and you'll be furious, and honestly I could use the distraction from being terrified."

Despite everything — despite the dream-bread and the dream-shoes and the name that fit her mouth too well — Gideon laughed. It came out broken in the middle, but it came out.

* * *

The wax broke cleanly, which felt wrong. Six moons of travel should have cracked it, chipped it, weathered it. Instead it parted like a held breath finally released, and the smell that rose out of the jar was cardamom and woodsmoke and something underneath, something older, the smell of a kitchen at dusk.

The memory poured into her the way memories always did when she opened a jar — but this time she wasn't the thief standing outside the experience, sifting someone else's life through professional fingers. This time she fell in.

Famine year. She was seven, maybe eight. The maize had failed twice running, and the valley's magic-dead soil gave nothing back no matter how it was begged. She sat at the kitchen table — *the* kitchen table, the one from the dreams, scarred wood, wobbly-legged — watching her mother count coins that weren't there. Across the table sat Adaeze.

Her twin. Her mirror. Same gap in the teeth, same stubborn cowlick, same thin wrists. Adaeze, who was quieter than her, watchful, who always let Gideon speak first because Gideon was the loud one, the hungry one, the one who climbed the fig tree and swore she saw the mountain move.

A stranger sat in the corner. Court-dressed, court-smelling, wrong in the valley the way a fish is wrong in a tree. He spoke softly, kindly, which was the most horrifying thing about him. Words drifted across the table like ash:

*...only one need go... the court pays in grain enough for the family... a place of honor... she will want for nothing...*

And her mother wept without sound, the way she did everything without sound, and looked at her daughters — one on each side of the table, two pairs of small shoes by the door — and Gideon understood, with the terrible clarity children have and adults spend their lives recovering, that a choice was being made between them.

Then the memory shifted. Later. Night. Her mother kneeling before her, holding her face in those mended-pot hands, saying words that came slow and heavy as stones dropped in deep water:

*"You will forget, my heart. I've paid a man to make you forget. Because if you remember, you will hate me, and I couldn't bear it — and if you remember, you will follow her, and the mountain takes followers too. So you will forget your sister. You will forget the bargain. You will grow up angry at me for reasons you can't name, and that anger will keep you alive, and someday — someday, if you're clever, if you're stubborn, if you're everything I know you are — you'll find your way back to this truth on your own. And when you do, know that every day I kept you, I grieved the one I sent away. Know that the map is coming. Forgive me. Forgive me. Forgive—"*

The memory ended.

Gideon came back to herself sitting in the dirt of the lean-to, the empty jar in her lap, sobbing in great ugly gulps she had no memory of starting, and Perrin's arms were around her, awkward and fierce, his chin hooked over her shoulder, saying nothing at all because there was nothing to say.

* * *

Later — much later, when the sun was properly up and she had run out of tears and moved on to the dry, ringing emptiness that comes after — she sat with her back against the acacia and tried to assemble herself out of the pieces.

"So," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "I'm the sister who stayed."

"You're the sister who stayed," Perrin agreed quietly.

"She chose me." Gideon stared at her hands. Thief's hands, quick and scarred. Seven-year-old hands, flour-dusted, holding a spoon too big for them. "Our mother chose me. And then she paid someone to steal it from me so I wouldn't have to live with the choosing. And I grew up and became the person who steals things from people so *they* don't have to live with their choosings." She laughed, and it was a horrible sound. "Do you see it? My whole trade. My whole *life*. I've been doing her kindness over and over, for strangers, for coin. Taking the weight off. I built an entire self out of my mother's guilt and called it a profession."

Perrin considered this with the seriousness he reserved for stolen goods and dead sisters. "Or," he said, "you built it out of love and just filed it under the wrong label. You're good at misfiling. I've seen your cache."

"Perrin."

"I'm serious. You take grief from widows so they can sleep. You took a summer from yourself so a child could survive a famine's worth of sorrow. Same act. Different scale." He shrugged. "Thieves' logic, maybe. But it's the only kind I trust."

Gideon wanted to argue. She reached for the argument and found she didn't have it. What she had instead was the lullaby, rising unbidden — *hush now, little ember, bank your light against the cold* — and the realization that landed on her like snowfall, soft and total:

Adaeze remembered it too. Somewhere under the mountain, in whatever passed for her days, her twin was dreaming the same kitchen, the same song, the same two pairs of shoes. That was what the dreams were. Not hauntings. *Letters.* Her sister, fading moon by moon, spending what strength remained to send word home the only road left open — through sleep, through blood, through the shared country of a kitchen neither of them had entered in twenty years.

"She's reaching for me," Gideon said aloud. "And I almost didn't open the jar. I sat on the truth for six moons because I was afraid of it, while she—" Her jaw tightened. "No more. No more afraid. No more sealed jars, literal or otherwise."

She stood. Her legs held. That surprised her; she'd expected them to be borrowed for the occasion.

"The contract," she said, pacing now, the old thief's energy flooding back into her limbs, transmuted into something harder and cleaner than it had ever been. "The court holds her soul. Fine. We knew that. But souls aren't the only thing a person is, and contracts are documents, and documents have edges, and edges have flaws, and I have spent my whole life finding the flaw in the lock." She turned to Perrin, and something in her face made him sit up straighter on his branch. "I know who I am now. All of it. The stolen memories, the trade, the valley, the flinch at rivers — it's not a wall anymore, Perrin. It's a map. *My* map. And I am going to walk it straight down that mountain's throat and get my sister back."

Perrin grinned — sharp-beaked, bright-eyed, entirely himself. "There she is. I was starting to worry the despairing silence was permanent. Very dull traveling company, despairing silence."

"Perrin."

"Yes?"

"Thank you. For not letting me stay in the dirt."

He waved this off with a flutter of fingers, embarrassed, already turning to kick dirt over the fire. "You'd have done the same. Eventually. After several more days. Which we don't have, incidentally — the twelfth moon turns in—" he counted on his fingers, frowned, counted again, "—far too few nights. So. Down the mountain's throat, you said. Through the throat of a court that eats souls and stamps memories and employs hollow-eyed riders." He glanced back at her, and beneath the grin there was steel. "Any idea how we get in?"

Gideon looked south, toward the mountain that had taken her sister and her name and her childhood and thought it could keep them.

"Yes," she said, and meant it with every fragment of the self she was only now beginning to own. "We knock."

But that night, and every night after, the kitchen waited for her in her sleep — and on the seventh night, for the first time, someone was standing in it. A girl with her own face, her own gap-toothed smile, wearing shoes with a strap mended in red thread, who looked straight at her across the dream-table and said, in a voice worn thin as paper:

*You opened it. Oh — oh, you opened it. Sister. Hurry.*
