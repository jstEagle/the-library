## 21. The Bargain Replayed

The year the rains failed, Ngozi Okafor learned that hunger has a way of rearranging a person's priorities.

She had always considered herself a woman of firm principles. She tithed at the shrine even when the harvest was thin. She returned borrowed things washed and folded. She had once walked four miles in the rain to return a neighbor's ladle, because a debt was a debt, and her mother had raised her to believe that what you owed defined you more honestly than what you owned.

That was before the third month of no rain. Before the goats stopped giving milk and the millet granary held nothing but dust and the memory of dust. Before she caught herself standing in the market square doing arithmetic that made her sick — counting her daughters the way other mothers counted chickens.

Adaeze was born first, by seven minutes, which Adaeze would later — if there was a later — insist made her the elder and therefore entitled to the larger portion of everything. Gideon was born second, screaming so loudly that the midwife said the child had come into the world already arguing her case. They were identical in every way that mattered to strangers and different in every way that mattered to their mother. Adaeze laughed first. Gideon talked first. Adaeze could charm honey out of a bee; Gideon could find where the bee kept it.

They were six years old the year the sky forgot them.

* * *

The stranger came on an evening when the light itself seemed too tired to finish setting.

Ngozi saw him from the doorway: a tall man in gray traveling clothes, walking up the road with the unhurried gait of someone who had never once been rained on unexpectedly. He stopped at their gate — they still had a gate then, though the fence it belonged to had long since been eaten by the cooking fire — and he inclined his head as though he were the one paying a courtesy call.

"Mother of two," he said. Not a question.

"Mother of none," Ngozi said, "if you're selling something."

He smiled. It was a kind smile, which was the worst part. Cruelty she would have known how to fight. "I'm buying," he said.

Inside, the girls were playing the game they played every evening now, since there was no supper to interrupt: Adaeze would hide a pebble in one fist and Gideon would guess which hand, except Gideon cheated, cheerfully and constantly, watching Adaeze's eyes instead of her hands, and Adaeze knew she cheated and let her win anyway, half the time, just to see her sister's face do its small triumphant thing.

Ngozi stood between the door and that sound and felt the arithmetic move in her chest like a stone.

"Come outside," she said.

* * *

What the man offered was this: the court beneath the mountain paid for children. Not for labor — he was quick to clarify that, as though it were the part that might have troubled her — but for souls. A child's soul, fresh and unspent, was worth more than a harvest. Worth more than ten harvests. Worth grain enough to carry the valley through famine and out the other side, worth a roof that didn't leak, worth years of life that hunger would otherwise take from all three of them.

"And the child?" Ngozi asked. Her voice came out very flat, the voice of a woman watching herself from a great distance. "What happens to the child?"

"She is honored," the man said. "Fed. Kept. The court treasures what it buys." He paused, and his kindness flickered — just once — into something older and wearier. "She will not grow old, mother. But she will not be hungry either."

"And I'm meant to choose."

"You are meant to choose which one," he said gently. "The choosing is the only part that's yours."

Later — much later, in a life she barely recognized — Ngozi would try to remember whether she had wept at that moment, and she could not. What she remembered instead was absurdly specific: a gecko on the gatepost, blinking at her with its flat golden eye, utterly indifferent to the end of the world happening three feet below it.

"I need until morning," she said.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat between her daughters' sleeping mats and watched them breathe, and she did the cruelest thing a mother can do, which is to compare them.

Not their souls — she refused to weigh those; whatever the court bought, it wasn't the whole of a girl, and she clung to that like a plank in floodwater. But she compared their chances. Adaeze was the dreamer, the one who narrated elaborate stories to the chickens, who believed with her entire being that the moon followed her personally. Adaeze would go wherever she was told believing it was an adventure. Gideon — Gideon watched doors. Gideon noticed when the salt jar moved. Gideon, at six, had already figured out that adults lied about small things and had begun keeping a mental ledger of it.

If either girl could survive the mountain, it was Gideon.

And there it was. The thought arrived fully formed, wearing her own voice, and Ngozi hated it with everything left in her body. Because the other side of that reasoning was a coin with a face on it, and the face was Adaeze's, and the reasoning went: *and if either girl can be spared, it should be the one who couldn't.*

She sat until the geckos stopped calling and the sky went the color of ash, and she understood that whichever choice she made, she was going to make it forever.

In the gray before dawn, Adaeze woke, found her mother sitting vigil, and crawled into her lap without a word, the way she did during storms. And Ngozi held her and smelled the sleep-and-dust smell of her hair and heard herself say, before she knew she was saying anything:

"My star. My evening star. Do you know why I call you that?"

"Because I came at dusk," Adaeze mumbled.

"Because you're the first light I look for," Ngozi said, "and the last one I'll lose."

Adaeze, six years old and mostly asleep, patted her mother's arm consolingly and went back under. And Ngozi sat in the dark holding her and made the bargain with herself that she would spend the rest of her life trying to unmake: *the dreamer goes, because the dreamer will believe it's beautiful. The watcher stays, because the watcher will survive me.*

It did not feel like choosing. That was the unforgivable part. It felt like arithmetic.

* * *

The morning of the departure, she told the girls they were going on a journey — just one daughter and Mama, up the mountain road, to visit a great house where there was food enough for everyone. Gideon's eyes narrowed immediately, which nearly undid her. Six years old, and already auditing.

"Why can't I come?" Gideon demanded.

"Because someone must stay and guard the house," Ngozi said, and heard how flimsy it sounded, and pressed on anyway, because a lie told badly was still a bridge if you walked fast enough. "Guarding is the most important job. I'm trusting you with everything we have."

Gideon considered this. Ngozi watched her consider it — watched the little ledger open behind those eyes, watch the scales tip — and then Gideon lifted her chin in a way that was pure Okafor stubbornness and said, "All right. But you have to bring her back by suppertime."

"I promise," Ngozi said, and broke it before suppertime, and would break it for thirteen years, and her daughter would grow up in the wreckage of that sentence without ever knowing it had been spoken.

At the gate, Adaeze waved until she was out of sight. She never looked afraid. That was the mercy and the horror of it both: the girl believed in the adventure all the way up the road, chattering about the great house, asking whether it would have stairs, whether the people there would want to hear her stories, whether the moon would still follow her underground or if she'd have to share somebody else's.

"The moon follows whoever it loves," Ngozi said. "It won't lose you."

"Obviously," said Adaeze, with her sister's exact chin-lift, and Ngozi had to stop walking for a moment and pretend to adjust a sandal strap so her daughter wouldn't see her face.

At the mountain's bone-colored door, the gray man took Adaeze's hand, and Adaeze looked back once — just once, bright-eyed, waving — and then the door closed, and the sound it made was not a slam but a soft click, like a jar being sealed.

Ngozi walked home alone. Suppertime came. Gideon set the table for three, because she was six and hope is stubborn, and then un-set it place by place as the light died, without saying a word, and Ngozi watched her do it and understood that her clever daughter had already written the true story in her head and simply chosen, for now, to keep it to herself.

That was the night Ngozi began looking for a thief.

* * *

Thieves were easier to find than bread, that year. The valley filled with them the way a wound fills with flies — men and women who'd lost farms and families and scruples in roughly that order. Ngozi interviewed four before she found the right one.

The first wanted payment in advance and looked at Gideon when he thought Ngozi wasn't watching, which ended the interview. The second was honest, which was useless. The third was competent but smelled of the mountain, and Ngozi trusted nothing that smelled of the mountain anymore.

The fourth was a small, sharp-faced woman named Bisi who drank Ngozi's terrible sorghum beer without complaint and listened to the whole request with her head tilted like a bird considering a worm.

"You want me to steal a memory," Bisi said. "From your own head."

"From my own head," Ngozi confirmed. "One memory. One year. Everything from the day the stranger came to the day after the door closed. All of it."

Bisi turned her cup slowly on the table. "You understand what you're asking. You'll wake up with a hole in you. You'll know your girl had a sister — everyone in the village knows — but you won't know why there's only one pair of shoes by the door. You'll grieve without knowing what for. That's not peace, mother. That's a wound that thinks it's skin."

"I know what I'm asking."

"Do you?" Bisi leaned forward. "Because here's what I've seen, and I've done this work twenty years. Mothers who forget don't stop grieving. They just lose the reason, and the reason is the handle. Without the handle, you can't put the grief down. You can't work it. You just carry it raw."

"Then I'll carry it raw," Ngozi said. "But I'll carry it *alive*. Do you understand? If I remember, I will spend every day of her childhood dying of it, and she will feel me dying, and it will teach her that love is a debt she owes for a crime she didn't commit. I won't raise my daughter inside my guilt. Take the memory. Take all of it. Leave her clean."

Bisi was quiet a long moment. Then, softly: "And yourself?"

"What about myself?"

"What do *you* get to be, with the memory gone?"

Ngozi looked at the single pair of shoes by the door — Gideon's, scuffed, one lace chewed — and said, "Hers. Just hers. For once in my life, entirely hers."

Bisi sighed the sigh of a woman who had long ago stopped believing she could talk anyone out of anything, and reached into her bag for the jars.

"One condition," she said. "I keep the memory. Sealed. In my keeping, not yours. Because someday — maybe not soon, maybe when you're old, maybe when you're dead and your girl finds it — someday someone will need it back. A debt that size doesn't vanish. It waits. And when it comes due, someone will have to pay it with the truth."

"Then let it wait," Ngozi said, "until someone stronger than me can carry it."

* * *

The theft itself was gentle, which surprised her. Bisi had her breathe slow and think of the mountain road, and then it was like water rising — the gecko on the gatepost went first, then the gray man's kind smile, then Adaeze's hand in the gray man's hand, then the wave, the click of the door, the walk home, the table set for three —

— and then Ngozi woke on her sleeping mat with sunlight on the floor and a single pair of shoes by the door and a grief in her chest the size of a country, with no borders, no name, no flag.

Gideon found her crying and climbed into her lap, six years old and fierce about it. "Mama. Mama, what's wrong?"

"I don't know, my heart," Ngozi said, and held her tight, and meant it in the worst possible way. "I don't know."

* * *

Years passed. The rains remembered themselves eventually. Gideon grew — sharp-eyed, quick-fingered, keeper of ledgers, hater of unlocked doors — and Ngozi loved her with the whole force of a love that had nowhere else to go, and sometimes, watching her daughter sleep, she would feel the shape of the hole in her memory press against the inside of her skull like a face against a window, and she would whisper into the dark:

*I'm sorry. Whichever one you are. I'm sorry.*

Because here was the truth she had never told Bisi, the truth she had carried raw all these years: some mornings, waking, she did not know which daughter she had sent up the mountain. The memory was gone, but the guilt remained, and guilt without detail is generous — it accuses everyone equally. Some days she mourned the dreamer. Some days she mourned the watcher. Most days she suspected that the woman who had made that choice was someone she wouldn't recognize, and that perhaps that was the real reason she'd had the memory stolen: not to protect Gideon from her grief, but to protect herself from her own verdict.

Then, in the autumn of Gideon's nineteenth year, Bisi came back.

The thief stood in the doorway looking older and smaller, hands shaking around a wax-sealed map that seemed, even from across the room, to be *moving* — ink crawling like ants across the surface. There was blood on her sleeve, and her breath whistled wrong, and Ngozi knew, the way mothers know, that this was a woman arriving at the end of something.

"It's come due," Bisi said. "The debt. I stole from the wrong people to keep it safe, and they found me, and I've run farther than my body agrees with." She held out the map with both hands, the way you return something sacred. "Your daughter. Does she have your stubbornness?"

"She has worse," Ngozi said. "She has mine and her father's."

"Good." Bisi managed a cracked smile. "She'll need it. Listen to me, mother, quickly, because I haven't got the breath for the whole story. The soul in the mountain — the court's favorite, the one they feed on — she's fading. Thirteen moons, and the thirteenth turns soon. And there are things on this map that shouldn't exist, things your daughter needs to see with her own eyes." She swayed, caught the doorframe. "There's a letter too. I wrote it down, everything you paid me to take, word for word as you said it that night. It's with the map. When she reads it —"

"When she reads it," Ngozi repeated, "she'll hate me."

Bisi looked at her for a long moment, and her bird-sharp eyes were almost tender.

"No," she said. "She'll do something far more dangerous than hate you. She'll understand you. Now help me to a chair, and put the kettle on, and send for your girl — because I intend to die having finished a job, and I only have the strength left to start it."

Beyond the window, low over the western hills, the evening star came out — first light Ngozi looked for, last one she'd ever lose — and Ngozi Okafor went to fetch her daughter, carrying at last, in her two empty hands, the truth.
