## 22. I Am the Debt

The jar sat open on the floor between them like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak.

Gideon did not remember picking it up. She did not remember breaking the wax seal with her mother's handwriting — *for whichever daughter finds this first* — or tipping it, or breathing in. But she must have done all of those things, because the memory was inside her now, unspooling slow and terrible as ink through water.

A kitchen. A low fire. Two pairs of small shoes by the door, one pair scuffed at the toes because the girl who wore them kicked stones for the sheer pleasure of hearing them fly.

Her mother's hands, kneading dough, singing half a lullaby before her voice broke on the second verse.

And a voice — small, insistent, coming from somewhere near Gideon's own height — saying, *Tell me again which one of us is going.*

*You're going,* said a girl's voice, and it was Gideon's own, younger, brighter, crueler in the way only children can be cruel without knowing it. *Mama said you're the strong one.*

*I don't want to be the strong one,* said the other voice, and Gideon knew it, knew it the way you know your own reflection in a window, knew it from dreams she'd spent twenty years refusing to have. *I want to stay and make bread.*

*Adaeze.* The name arrived in Gideon's mouth whole, like a stone she had swallowed long ago and only now coughed up. *Your name is Adaeze.*

The kitchen dissolved. The jar lay empty at her knees. And Gideon Okafor sat very still on the floor of a waystation room with dust in the corners, holding nothing, being nothing, while outside the sixth moon rose fat and indifferent over the hills.

* * *

She didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough that the candle burned down to a puddle. Long enough that when Perrin came back from scouting the road, he stopped in the doorway with his feathers half-ruffled into hair and his face doing something complicated.

"Gideon."

"I had a sister," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. She had expected it to shatter. Perhaps it already had, and this calm was what grew in the cracks. "Her name was Adaeze. I used to steal her share of the honey cake and blame the neighbor's dog."

Perrin crouched down across from her, careful as a man approaching a bird with a broken wing. "That's — good? That's what we wanted, isn't it. To know."

"We?" She looked up at him. "There's no *we* in this, Perrin. There never was. There's just me, walking a debt I signed before I could walk." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow as a jar. "Do you want to hear the funny part? There's always a funny part. My mother paid a thief to steal the memory from herself. That's why I became —" She gestured at herself, at the satchel of jars, at the whole architecture of her life. "Memory-thief. Valley-bound. Excellent at taking what others can't hold. I built an entire self out of the shape of the hole she left in me."

"Gideon—"

"The map." Her hand found it where it lay against her chest, warm as a living thing. "It was never a rescue mission. It was an apology. Delivered late by twenty years because my mother couldn't bear to carry it herself, so she hired someone to carry it for her, and that someone was —" She stopped. Started again. "The stranger. The dying man at the rim. He was carrying my mother's sorry to me, and I thought it was a job."

"That's not nothing," Perrin said quietly. "An apology is not nothing."

"It's worse than nothing." She pressed her palms against her eyes. "Because now every single thing I am turns out to be a wall. You understand? Every skill, every habit, every clever little trick — I told myself I chose them. I told myself I was *self-made*, which is a thief's joke if there ever was one, because everything I've ever owned was taken from somebody else. Including myself." She lowered her hands. "My mother couldn't look at me after she sent Adaeze away. Do you know how I know? Because I remember it now — no. No, I don't. I remember being *told* about it, in the memory, in her voice, in the part where she says she paid a thief to take it away because every time she braided my hair she saw two heads instead of one."

Perrin said nothing. He was, Gideon reflected distantly, better at silence than most people were at speech. It was probably the magpie in him. Magpies knew the value of a thing left unclaimed.

"She kept me," Gideon went on, because the words had started and would not stop, a leak in a dam she'd spent a lifetime building. "Famine year. The court came offering gold for a child's soul — a loan, they called it, a *surety*, as if souls were sacks of grain you could buy back after harvest. And my mother chose. She put us both on the road and she chose, and she chose me, and I don't even get to hate her properly because she also couldn't live with it, so she cut the choice out of her own head and let it fall into mine." A breath. "I've been carrying her guilt around for twenty years and calling it a personality."

"That's not fair," Perrin said.

"No," Gideon agreed. "It isn't. Not to her, not to Adaeze, not to me. Nothing about this has ever been fair. That's rather the point of contracts, isn't it? They're fairness-shaped holes where fairness should be."

He shifted closer. "What are you going to do?"

"What I've always done." She looked at the empty jar. "Take something. Give something. Balance the ledger." Her mouth twisted. "Except this time the ledger is me."

* * *

Sleep did not come, which was fine, because sleep was for people who expected to wake up as themselves.

Instead Gideon sat by the dead fire and took inventory, the way she did before any heist. Old habit. Except tonight the vault she was cataloguing was her own head, and every shelf held something that turned out to be borrowed.

Competence: stolen. Learned from a fence named Odile who had bought her off a caravan at nine years old and taught her fingers their trade. She had always believed she'd chosen Odile. Now she wondered whether a lonely child chooses anything, or simply grabs whatever floats past the wreck.

Cleverness: stolen. A gift for false memories, for feeding people vivid lies they'd swear to — that had come from somewhere too, hadn't it? From a girl who'd learned early that stories were safer than truths. From a household where the truth was a thing you sold your daughter to escape.

Even her name. *Gideon Okafor.* She rolled it around her mouth like a strange coin. Her mother had named her — she remembered that much now, a scrap the sealed jar hadn't held but the salt flats had sung loose — named her for a great-uncle who once argued a river into changing course. A name meant to be stubborn. Meant to hold.

It wasn't enough. Names weren't enough. The court had taught her that much: they held Adaeze's soul, but names were never sold. Which meant somewhere beneath the mountain, her twin existed as a soul in a jar with no name at all, and Gideon existed as a name wrapped around a wall of other people's grief, and neither of them, strictly speaking, was a person yet.

"Stop that," she said aloud to the dark.

The dark, naturally, said nothing back. The valley had trained her well in that particular conversation.

But the thought wouldn't stop, so she let it finish. Let it walk its full circle: *everything you built yourself from was a wall.* And then — because she was, whatever else, still a professional — she examined the wall for flaws.

Walls had a purpose. Walls kept things out. And what had the wall kept out?

Not the truth; the truth had gotten through eventually, the way water gets through everything. No. What the wall had kept out was the *weight*. Twenty years of weight, and she had walked upright under it, had crossed a kingdom under it, had fed a hunter a false memory so vivid it unmade him, had rallied thieves and ferrymen and archivists to a cause she didn't understand. The wall hadn't made her hollow. The wall had made her *load-bearing*.

"Huh," said Gideon Okafor, to the dark, and this time the dark felt less like an absence and more like an audience.

* * *

Near dawn, Perrin appeared in the doorway with bread — actual bread, valley-style, dense and faintly sweet, and Gideon's chest seized before her mind caught up.

"Sefu's recipe," Perrin said, watching her face. "Or near enough. I asked at the last village. Don't look at me like that; I can be thoughtful. I'm famously thoughtful. Ask anyone. Ask literally anyone except everyone who's ever met me."

Despite everything — despite the hollowed-out morning and the ledger of herself balanced in red — Gideon felt her mouth twitch. "You bribed a baker."

"I *traded* with a baker. There's a difference and it's legal in at least three provinces." He tore the loaf and handed her half, and they ate in silence for a while, shoulder to shoulder against the cold hearthstone.

"You know what the widow told me," Gideon said eventually, "back in the valley, before I left? She found me even though I'd stolen her grief. Brought me bread. Told me a story about twin girls sold beneath the mountain." She huffed a small, disbelieving breath. "I thought she was being kind. Kindness unsettles me more than threats do — you know that about me by now. But it wasn't kindness, or not only. She knew. Some part of the valley knew, and she was handing me the thread and waiting to see if I'd pull it."

"People are like that," Perrin said. "They hand you threads. Whether you weave or hang yourself is your business." He paused. "That sounded better in my head."

"It usually does."

He bumped his shoulder against hers, gentle as a landing bird. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much, I'm a magpie, my opinion of worth is famously deranged — I don't think the wall was a waste."

"Perrin."

"I'm serious. Shut up and listen, this happens maybe twice a year." He turned to face her fully, and behind the ruffled hair his eyes were steady and dark and entirely human. "Whoever your mother meant to make with that apology — some penitent ghost who spends twenty years weeping — that's not who showed up. What showed up was a woman who steals grief from widows because they can't hold it. Who gives memories away free when a friend betrays her. Who is currently planning, I assume, because I've watched you plan, to walk into a mountain full of monsters and *renegotiate*." He shrugged. "The wall kept out the weight. Fine. But you built doors in it. Doors everywhere. You just didn't know they were yours."

Gideon stared at him.

Then, because she was who she was — whoever that turned out to be — she reached into her satchel, past the jars, and pulled out a small stoppered vial of grey light. "Here."

"What is it?"

"A memory. Freely given, no ledger, no debt. It's the smell of rain on a hot road and the feeling that somewhere ahead of you there's shade and someone waiting in it." She pressed it into his hand. "I stole it from a merchant eleven years ago and I've never known why I kept it. I think maybe I was saving it for the road home. Whichever road that turns out to be."

Perrin closed his fist around the vial with a care that would have embarrassed him if he'd noticed himself doing it. "This doesn't count toward the favor I owe your mother's map," he said gruffly. "This is separate. This is — friendship rates."

"Heaven forbid."

"Heaven absolutely forbid."

* * *

They broke camp as the sun cleared the ridge, and Gideon stood a moment looking east, toward the smudge on the horizon that was the mountain, that had always been the mountain, that had been the shape of her whole life drawn in outline before she could read.

*Adaeze,* she thought, testing the name again, aiming it east like an arrow. *Hold on. Your sister is coming. The one who stayed. The one who owes you everything.*

The map warmed against her sternum, and its ink stirred, and for the first time she read it not as instructions but as handwriting — her mother's looping, careful script, the same hand that had written *for whichever daughter finds this first*, the same hand that had braided two heads of hair and seen two futures and chosen wrong and known it and spent twenty years and one dying stranger trying to say so.

An apology, delivered late.

"Well," Gideon said softly, shouldering her satchel of ten thousand other people's yesterdays and one newly recovered morning in a kitchen, "better late than never. Come on, Perrin. We have a court to rob."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the horizon darkened — and on the eastern wind came the sound of hooves.
