## 23. Sixth Moon, Empty Hands

Perrin had a theory about grief. He'd developed it over the course of losing a mother to fever, a father to the river, and a sister to a mountain, so he felt reasonably qualified to hold opinions on the subject.

The theory was this: grief was like weather. It came, it soaked you through, it passed — or it didn't, and you learned to live under a permanent gray sky and call that living too. What grief was *not*, in his experience, was quiet. Grief talked. It wailed at funerals and muttered in taverns and made people say absurd things to strangers on roads. Even his sister, who had been the most stubbornly silent person he'd ever loved, had wept openly when she'd left him with a stolen map and a promise she couldn't keep.

Gideon Okafor did not grieve like any of this. Gideon grieved by *stopping*.

She had stopped three days ago.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower, halfway between the salt flats and the foothills of the mountain itself, close enough now that on clear nights Perrin could see the court's lights bleeding up through the rock like lanterns behind skin. Three days ago they had crested a ridge, Gideon had looked at the mountain for a long time, and then she had sat down on a flat stone and hadn't gotten up.

Not dramatically. That was the unnerving part. She ate when he put food in front of her. She drank when he pressed the waterskin into her hands. She moved the camp when the wind turned. But she said nothing, and she looked at nothing, and she walked only when he physically pulled her up by the elbow and set her walking north, like a cart horse that had decided its pulling days were done.

Perrin had tried everything.

He had tried sympathy, which lasted about an hour before he ran out of material. He had tried anger, which bounced off her like rain off slate. He had tried leaving her alone, which had nearly worked — she had simply remained where he left her, sitting with her hands in her lap, until a patrol of hollow-eyed riders passed within half a mile and he'd had to go back and haul her into the rocks while she offered no assistance whatsoever, dead weight and empty eyes.

Now, on the third evening, he crouched in front of her with a tin cup of nettle tea and held it out like an offering to a very disappointing god.

"Tea," he said.

Nothing.

"It's terrible. I found it growing out of what I'm fairly sure is a grave. You'd think that would interest you. Grave-adjacent botany."

Her eyes moved to the cup. That was progress, technically. Her eyes had not moved for anything in two days except the moon, which she watched rise each night with an expression Perrin could only describe as *bookkeeping* — as though she were counting something, and the count kept coming out wrong.

"Gideon."

"I heard you." Her voice was rust. "I'm here."

"You're not, though. That's my whole point. The body's here. The rest of you is somewhere I can't follow, and believe me, I've checked. I've been places no one should follow anyone."

She took the tea. Small mercy. He sat down across from her, folding his long legs under himself, and studied her face in the firelight. She looked thinner. Not starved — she ate what he gave her — but worn down, as if the last six moons had been sanding her, patiently, grain by grain, down to something smooth and featureless.

He knew that look. He'd seen it in his own reflection once, the winter after Nkiru died, sitting in a wayhouse outside Vell with a cup just like this one and absolutely no reason to drink it.

That was the thing nobody told you about other people's despair: it wasn't dramatic. It was *convenient*. It asked nothing of anyone. A person who simply stopped required nothing but food and water and the occasional tug northward, and the world was happy to provide exactly that much and not one drop more. The world, Perrin had learned, would let you sit on a stone indefinitely, provided the stone was comfortable enough.

He was not going to let the world get away with it.

* * *

The trouble with dragging someone onward by sheer stubbornness, Perrin reflected the next morning, was that stubbornness was a two-player game, and Gideon had withdrawn from play entirely.

He tried conversation first. He was, he felt, good company. He told her about the time he'd shifted into magpie form inside a noblewoman's hat shop and been chased four streets by a man wielding a broom with religious conviction. He told her about the ferryman's face when Perrin had paid a toll with a memory of eating a lemon, purely out of spite. He told her a joke he'd heard from a tinker, involving a priest, a goat, and a tax collector, and he told it well, and at the end of it Gideon blinked slowly at the fire like a cat acknowledging that sounds had occurred.

"You're doing the thing," she said finally.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you talk enough for both of us so you don't have to feel anything."

Perrin opened his mouth. Closed it. "That's — I'll have you know that is a beloved personality trait, not a coping mechanism."

"Mm."

"And even if it were, which it isn't, at least I'm *doing* it. At least there's noise coming out of me. You've gone so quiet the crows have started giving you a wide berth. Do you know how hard it is to offend a crow? They're professionals."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile; it might have been indigestion. Perrin seized on it like a drowning man seizing driftwood.

"There it is. There's the face. I saw it. Don't deny it, I have excellent eyesight, it's the whole — " he gestured at himself, wings and all, metaphorically — "magpie heritage."

"Perrin." She set down her cup. For a moment he thought she was going to say something real, something that mattered, and he braced himself the way you brace for cold water. But all she said was: "Why are you still here?"

And there it was. The actual question, surfacing at last from beneath all that stillness, and it wasn't the question he'd expected. Not *why bother*, not *what's the point*. Why are *you* here. As if he were the inexplicable one. As if a man walking beside her needed accounting for more than a woman sitting on a stone waiting for the world to finish with her.

"Because," Perrin said, "you owe me a memory."

"That's not why."

"No," he admitted. "It's not."

She waited. He hated her a little, in the fond way, for making him say it.

"Because everyone I've ever walked beside has either died or left, and I have decided — unilaterally, without consulting you — that you're not going to be one of them. That's it. That's the whole speech. I lost my mother, my father, and my sister, and I have spent eleven years being very careful never to stand next to anyone long enough for the universe to add them to the list, and then my sister went and got herself killed delivering a map to a stranger, and now I'm standing next to *you*, and the universe is looking at you the way it looked at them. And I am telling the universe no." He jabbed a finger at the sky, at the mountain, at the general arrangement of creation. "*No.* You don't get her. Find your own thief."

Gideon stared at him.

"That was," she said at last, "a lot of words."

"I contain multitudes."

"You're shaking."

"I'm *passionate*."

She looked down at her hands then, and he watched her turn them over, palms up, as though checking whether they still belonged to her. Six moons ago those hands had been the most dangerous things in the valley — quick, sure, always moving, always taking. Now they lay open and empty in her lap like something surrendered.

"She chose to forget," Gideon said quietly. "My mother. She paid a thief to take it out of her own head. And I built myself out of stolen pieces because there was nothing underneath. There was never anything underneath, Perrin. I'm not a person who lost herself. I'm a pile of other people's memories wearing a coat."

"Well," Perrin said, "the coat fits."

She looked up sharply. He shrugged, uncomfortable, committed now.

"I mean it. I've known you since the rim road. I've watched you give water to a dying man you'd never met. I've watched you forgive me — *me* — for stealing your map, which frankly should have ended our acquaintance right there, and instead you handed me a memory like it was nothing, like friendship was something you could just *give away*. Whatever you were built out of, Gideon, whatever's missing — what I've seen is what you *did* with it. And what you did with it was better than what most people do with whole selves."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them a hawk rode the thermals off the foothills, screaming its one note over and over.

"You sound like the archivist," Gideon said finally.

"The archivist is blind and lives in ash. I'm handsome and live nowhere. We're very different."

* * *

He got her walking that afternoon. Not far — two miles along the old trade road toward the mountain's approaches, until the bone door country began and the air took on that mineral smell, like caves and old coins. But she walked under her own weight, and she answered when he pointed things out, mostly in monosyllables, but answers nonetheless. Perrin counted it a victory and celebrated internally with a small parade.

By evening, though, the stillness was creeping back. He could see it happening — the way her gaze slid off the fire and fixed on the middle distance, the way her breathing slowed like a lamp running out of oil. Tomorrow she'd be worse. The next day worse again. Despair wasn't an event, he understood now; it was a tide, and it came back twice daily whether you liked it or not, and you could not fight a tide by standing in it shouting.

You could, however, refuse to let someone sit alone in it.

So that night, instead of letting her drift, he sat down beside her — closer than he usually bothered, shoulder almost touching hers — and said, "Tell me about her."

Gideon went very still. "About who."

"Your sister. Adaeze. You opened the jar. You remembered. So tell me. Not the court stuff, not the contract. Just — her. What was she like? Did she steal sweets? Was she the loud one or the quiet one? Every pair of twins has a loud one."

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then, slowly, like a door opening on rusted hinges:

"Loud," Gideon said. "Adaeze was loud. She'd sing at the top of her voice in the market and mother would pretend not to know us." A pause. "I was the quiet one. I hid behind mother's skirts and memorized where everyone kept their money."

"A thief at five. Shocking."

"She used to make up games. She'd say, *let's pretend we're rich ladies*, and we'd walk around the yard with our noses in the air carrying sticks for parasols. Or she'd say, *let's pretend we're the mountain's daughters*, and she'd tell me the mountain was our grandmother and someday she'd come collect us." Gideon's voice caught. "She came to collect one of us."

Perrin said nothing. Some silences were doors and some were walls, and this one, he judged, was a door.

"She remembers the lullaby," Gideon went on, barely audible. "From the dreams. Two pairs of shoes by the door, and the lullaby, and she doesn't remember my face but she remembers *me*, some part of her does, and she's fading, Perrin. Halfway gone already. And every day I sit on stones feeling sorry for myself is a day she has less of herself left to save." Her hands curled into fists. "I know that. I *know* it. Knowing doesn't move my feet."

"No," Perrin said. "It doesn't. That's not how feet work."

She turned to look at him.

"Here's what I learned," he said, "after Nkiru. Grief tells you that you have to *feel* your way back to moving. That you have to sort it all out first, understand it, carry it properly, and then you'll be able to walk. It's lying. It's the most convincing liar there is. You don't wait to move until it makes sense. You move, and eventually — maybe — it makes sense. In that order. Always in that order." He nudged her boot with his own. "So tomorrow morning, I'm going to get up, and I'm going to be insufferable, and I'm going to make tea out of grave-weeds, and I'm going to pull you up by the elbow and set you walking north whether your heart comes with us or not. And the day after that. And the day after that. Until your feet remember that they belong to a woman who walks *toward* things."

"That's a horrible plan."

"It's the only plan I've got. I'm a magpie. We're not planners. We're *persisters*."

And there — unmistakably this time, no mistaking it — the smile. Small, cracked, real. It transformed her face the way light transforms a room, and Perrin felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched for three days.

"Grave-weeds," she said.

"Excellent grave-weeds. Very fresh."

"You're ridiculous."

"I'm loyal," Perrin corrected. "Ridiculousness is incidental."

* * *

They walked at dawn.

He hadn't needed to pull her up. That was the thing he would remember later, the thing he turned over in his mind like a coin he suspected was worth more than it looked: when the gray light came, Gideon Okafor rose on her own, rolled her blanket, shouldered her pack, and stood facing north before he'd even finished choking down his tea. As if his stubbornness had been a rope thrown to a drowning woman, and all these days she'd been holding it underwater, and now, at last, she'd begun to climb.

They made eight miles that day. Nine the next. She spoke more — not much, but more — asking questions about the approaches, the doors, the guards' rotations, her thief's mind grinding slowly back to life like a mill wheel after a dry season. On the third day she laughed. Actually laughed, short and startled, at his impression of the Ferryman's offended dignity, and then pressed her hand over her mouth as though the laugh had escaped without permission, and Perrin pretended not to notice because noticing would have ruined it.

On the fourth day, the mountain filled half the sky.

They camped within sight of the three doors — gold glinting far to the east where the rich road climbed, iron gleaming dull and sullen to the west, and between them, half-hidden in a fold of rock, the pale suggestion of the third. Bone. Even at this distance it seemed to drink the light around it.

Gideon stood looking at it for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was steady, and it was her own voice again — not the rust, not the emptiness, but the woman who had crossed the rim with a map burning against her ribs.

"Perrin. When this is over — if it's over — what will you do?"

He considered. "Open a shop," he said. "Somewhere quiet. Sell nothing. Sit in it."

"That's your dream? An empty shop?"

"A shop where nobody owes anybody anything." He glanced sideways at her. "Might need help running it. If you know anyone with experience handling things that don't belong to them."

She huffed — almost a laugh — and then went quiet again, but it was a different quiet now, a gathering quiet, the stillness of a drawn bow rather than an abandoned cart.

That night the sixth moon rose full over the mountain, and Perrin woke near midnight to find Gideon standing at the edge of the camp, facing the bone door, her silhouette thin and black against the court's distant glow.

"They know we're coming," she said softly, without turning. "The hunters, the eaters, the Ferryman's ledgers — word travels. By the time we reach that door, they'll have had six moons to prepare a welcome."

"Probably a banner," Perrin agreed, coming to stand beside her. "The Under-Court strikes me as a banner people."

"And whatever's waiting down there—" She stopped. Started again. "Whatever happens, I want you to hear this now, while I still mean it plainly. You didn't lose me. Back there, on the stone. I was as good as gone, and you refused it, and I'm standing here because of that. Whatever else happens under the mountain — whatever it costs — that part is already won. Do you understand?"

Perrin looked at her, this woman he had dragged bodily out of despair by sheer refusal, and found, annoyingly, that his throat was tight.

"Don't get sentimental," he managed. "It doesn't suit you. You're meant to be the grim one. I'm the heart of this partnership."

"Obviously."

But she reached out and gripped his forearm, thief-strong, and held it a moment, and that was better than any banner.

Below them, in its fold of rock, the bone door waited — patient as debt, pale as a held breath — and somewhere beneath it, in a vault of ten thousand jars, a girl who remembered a lullaby was running out of moons.

"Tomorrow," Gideon said.

"Tomorrow," Perrin agreed. "But tonight — sleep. Even heroes sleep. Especially heroes. It's very important for the complexion."

And for the first time in six days, Gideon Okafor laughed in the dark, and the sound carried further than either of them knew — down the fold of rock, past the pale door, into the listening dark of the mountain, where something ancient turned its attention toward the sound and began, slowly, to smile.
