## 24. The Ferryman's Rebate

The bridge was exactly as ugly as Gideon remembered it.

That was the strange thing about memory — hers especially. She had crossed this span of black timber and old rope six moons ago, paid a summer of her childhood to a silent man in a gray coat, and walked on. In the months since, she had rebuilt the crossing in her mind a dozen times: sometimes the planks were wider, sometimes the river beneath ran silver instead of brown, sometimes the Ferryman had a beard. But no. Here it all was, mean and crooked and real, sagging over water the color of steeped tea, and here he was too, standing at the far end as if he had never moved at all.

"You're staring," Perrin said.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring. There's a difference, and the difference is that assessing has a plan in it." He hunched his shoulders inside his coat, feathers rippling along his collar where they always did when he was nervous, which was always, which he would deny under torture. "We don't have to cross here. There's a ford two days north. Cold, but free."

"The map says the bridge."

"The map," Perrin said, "has been wrong before."

"The map has never once been wrong. It's been *cruel* before. It's been *vague* before. Once it drew a picture of my mother's hands for three days straight, which I'd call unforgivable if it hadn't also shown us the salt flats' safe path." Gideon tapped the wax-sealed roll against her palm. "Wrong is not one of its sins. Come on."

The river muttered under the bridge like an old woman complaining about her knees. Halfway across, Gideon's boots found the plank that creaked — the same plank, she realized, the same exact note, a low groan like a door in a house you grew up in — and something in her chest went tight and strange. She had paid for this crossing with a summer. She knew that the way you know a tooth is missing: by the shape of the gap. There had been a season once, warm and endless, full of river-water and somebody's laughter, and she could not find it anywhere in herself. She knew its edges. She knew she had loved it. The middle was gone, scooped out clean, and the Ferryman had tasted it, nodded once, and let her pass.

He was watching them come now. He never seemed to watch; his eyes were the flat gray of the coat he wore, and yet you always caught him already looking at you, as though looking were something he'd finished doing earlier and was only now getting around to admitting.

"Thief," he said. It might have been a greeting. With him, it probably was.

"Ferryman." She stopped an arm's length away, out of habit. You didn't stand close to a man who ate memories. You especially didn't stand close when your pockets were full of them — the sealed jar in her satchel, the one labeled in her mother's hand, pressed against her hip like a second heartbeat. Six moons of road, and she still hadn't opened it. Some nights she told herself that was discipline. Most nights she knew it was fear wearing discipline's clothes.

Perrin sidled up beside her and did his best impression of a man with no opinions. It was not convincing. Perrin had never once had no opinions; he simply stored them up and spent them later, with interest, at the worst possible moment.

"We need the far bank," Gideon said. "You know what's coming. You've seen the moons turn."

"I see everything that crosses," the Ferryman said. "That is the toll-taker's curse. Nothing passes me unseen, and nothing passes me free." His gaze drifted down to her satchel, unhurried, and Gideon felt the jar there the way you feel a stranger's eyes on the back of your neck. "You carry court-stamped goods. You carry grief that isn't yours. And you carry" — the pause was very small and very deliberate — "a question you haven't opened."

"That's none of those things is payment," Gideon said. "I have coin."

"Coin." He said the word the way Perrin said *honest work*. "I took a summer from you once. Do you remember it?"

"No," Gideon said. "That was rather the point."

"Yes." Something moved behind the gray eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a scar he'd given someone. "I remember it. That is the other half of my trade, thief, and nobody ever thinks about it. Everyone worries about what they lose. Nobody asks what I keep. I hold every season I've ever taken. I am a very crowded man."

Perrin made a noise that was almost a laugh and strangled it into a cough.

Gideon folded her arms. "If this is a speech about the loneliness of your profession, I'll warn you, I've heard better from a man who eats memories for a living at the market, and even he managed to make it interesting."

"He makes it interesting because he lies," the Ferryman said. "I don't." He extended one hand, palm up, and for a moment Gideon thought he meant to take something — and her whole body braced, thief-quick, hand going to the satchel strap — but his palm stayed empty. Waiting. "Your mother," he said, "crossed this bridge twenty-two years ago."

The world went quiet. Not the polite quiet of surprise. The heavy quiet, the kind that presses down on your ears, the quiet of a room just after something breaks.

"She was carrying two jars," the Ferryman went on, in the tone of a man reading from a ledger he'd memorized long ago and never stopped being sorry about. "One held a lullaby. One held a name. She could not pay the toll in either — those weren't hers to spend, she said, they belonged to her daughters — so she paid in something else. She paid in a promise. She said: *one day my other girl will come this way, going toward the mountain, and whatever she has already paid you, give her back one thing, unasked, and tell her it was owed.*"

Gideon's mouth was dry. "Her other girl."

"Her words." The Ferryman's gray eyes rested on her, patient as weather. "I have waited twenty-two years to discharge a debt, thief. My ledgers do not forgive delay, and neither do I, but this one entry has sat open longer than any other, and I will confess — and you may keep this between us and the river — that I have grown tired of looking at it."

He turned his palm over.

And the summer came back.

There was no light, no chime, no theater of it. That was the terrible kindness of the thing: it arrived the way ordinary memory arrives, sideways, without announcement, so that one moment Gideon was standing on a rotten bridge arguing with a man in a gray coat, and the next moment she was eight years old and it was impossibly hot and the river — *this* river, this same tea-brown muttering river — was full of fish that tickled your ankles if you stood still long enough, and Adaeze —

— Adaeze was shrieking with laughter because Gideon had slipped and gone in fully clothed, and their mother was on the bank with her skirt pinned up pretending to be angry and fooling absolutely no one, and the sun was so thick you could lean against it, and there was a whole day of it, hours and hours of it, the taste of dust and sweet figs and the specific squeak of wet leather sandals, and Gideon stood on the bridge in the cold of the sixth moon's end and felt every hour of it pour back into the hollow where it had been scooped out, filling her up like water finding its level.

"Oh," she said.

It was not a word. It was barely a sound. But it was all she had.

She had sold this. She had handed it over herself, gladly, because the mountain was ahead and the moons were counting down and a summer seemed a fair price for passage. She had told herself it was a small thing to lose. A cheap thing. A child's thing.

It was not small. It had never been small. It was her mother laughing on a riverbank, and she had carried it out of her own chest with her own hands and set it on a scale like scrap iron.

"Gideon," Perrin said quietly. He had gone very still, the way he did around wounded things. Even his feathers had stopped moving.

"I'm fine," she said, which was a lie so obvious that the river laughed at it, gurgling under the planks.

She was crying. She discovered this with a kind of distant academic interest, the way you discover rain has started while you're indoors. Her face was wet. Her hands were shaking. And the summer was *there*, whole, warm against the backs of her eyes, and she held onto it with both fists the way you'd hold a child in a crowd — not because it was trying to leave, but because everything else in the world suddenly looked like it might.

"I don't understand," she managed, when she could manage words at all. "Why would she — she sent me *away*. She kept Adaeze's soul in a vault and sent me up a road with nothing, and then she — she paid a thief to steal her own memory of it, and she —" The sentences were tangling. She pulled them apart with a thief's patience. "Why would she arrange grace for me? I'm not the one who needed forgiving."

"Aren't you?" the Ferryman asked.

And there it was, the question she'd been stepping around for six moons like a hole in the road. Because yes, she'd opened the jar, finally, under a half-moon with Perrin pretending to sleep ten feet away and failing magnificently — and she knew now. She was the sister who stayed. She was the daughter whose name was on the contract. She was the trade that wasn't made, the twin who got to grow up eating figs by a hot river while her sister fed a court beneath a mountain. And some rotten little corner of her had whispered, all this time, *you are the debt that should have been paid instead.*

"Don't answer that," Perrin said sharply, to the Ferryman, of all people. "Don't you dare philosophize at her on a bridge. Bridges are for crossing. Philosophy is for taverns, where there's wine."

The Ferryman regarded him for a long moment. Then, astonishingly, the corner of his mouth moved. On another man it would have been called a smile. On him it was more like the memory of one, kept in good repair.

"Your mother also said," he went on, "that you would argue with whoever delivered this. She said it the way people say things they're proud of."

Gideon laughed. It came out cracked and wet and surprised her more than the crying had. "Of course she did. Of course she —" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Twenty-two years. She planned this before I was born, essentially. Before I could walk. She looked at a baby and thought, *she'll be stubborn enough to need a bridge built ahead of her.*"

"Mothers do that," Perrin said softly. He was looking at the river. "Mine used to pre-chew the arguments."

"Your mother was a magpie."

"My mother was a *visionary*."

The Ferryman let them have the moment. That was a kind of courtesy too, Gideon thought — a man who dealt in the contents of souls, knowing when not to reach for one. When the silence had settled into something bearable, he spoke again.

"There is a condition on the rebate. There is always a condition; my ledger demands symmetry." He nodded back the way they'd come, south, toward the flats and the market and the burned library. "What I returned, I returned. It is yours, whole, and no toll of mine will ever touch it again — I mark it exempt. But hear the rest. Your mother's promise bought you one gift, freely given, so that you would know such a thing exists. Grace, thief. Unearned, unsolicited, arriving anyway. She wanted you to carry the knowledge of it across the mountain's threshold." His gray eyes were steady. "Because what waits for you there deals only in debts. And it will tell you, in its fine legal voice, that everything is owed and nothing is given. Your mother wanted you to be able to say: *I know otherwise. I have proof.*"

Gideon looked at him. The summer sat warm in her chest, absurd and indestructible, figs and river-water and her mother's badly faked anger.

"She sounds," Gideon said finally, "like she was smarter than everyone gave her credit for."

"She sounded," the Ferryman said, "like a woman who had done a terrible thing and intended to spend the rest of her life paying it forward in installments." He stepped aside, clearing the way to the far bank, and gestured with one gray sleeve. "Cross. No toll. The debt is discharged, and I confess I am glad — my ledger has a cleaner look about it, and I am a vain man about my ledger."

They crossed. The creaking plank groaned its familiar note under Gideon's boots, and this time the sound didn't hurt; it sounded like home reporting in. On the far side the land climbed gently toward a horizon where, faint and blue-gray and patient, the mountain waited with its three doors and its masked court and its ten thousand bottled selves.

Perrin fell into step beside her. For a while he said nothing, which for Perrin was roughly equivalent to another man delivering an oration.

"You cried," he said at last.

"I did not cry. I experienced precipitation."

"On a bridge. In front of a toll-taker. I've known you through wolves, Gideon. Through the salt flats singing. Through the night you read your mother's handwriting on a jar and put it back unopened like a coward." He ducked the elbow she aimed at him, grinning, feathers bright. "This is going in my permanent collection. Top shelf. I'm having it engraved."

"Perrin."

"Hm?"

She stopped walking. Ahead, the road bent toward the mountain's approaches, and somewhere past that bend were doors — gold, iron, bone — and a court that believed everything in the world had a price tag already written. She touched her satchel, where the sealed jar rode, and then touched her own sternum, where the summer lived now, exempt, untouchable, hers.

"When we get there," she said, "they're going to show me a contract with my name on it. They're going to tell me the ledger balances and nothing is given and everyone pays." She could feel the grin starting before she decided to allow it — small, sharp, a thief's grin, the kind that had gotten her through locked doors and locked years alike. "And I'm going to tell them about a bridge."

Perrin studied her for a moment. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him. "Grace as a weapon," he said. "Your mother would be proud."

"That," said Gideon Okafor, and started walking toward the mountain, "is the general idea."

Behind them, faint over the mutter of the river, she thought she heard the Ferryman close his ledger — one soft, final page-turn, like a door shutting gently on twenty-two years.

The mountain waited. For the first time since the valley, Gideon found she was not afraid of it.

She was *ready* for it.
