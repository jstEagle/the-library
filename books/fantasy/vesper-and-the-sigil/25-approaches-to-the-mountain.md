## 25. Approaches to the Mountain

The mountain did not look like a place where souls were kept. It looked like a place where weather was invented.

Gideon stood at the edge of the last meadow before the scree fields began and studied it the way she'd once studied locked memory-jars: patiently, looking for the seam. The peak rose out of a permanent collar of cloud, gray-white and indifferent, its slopes stitched with terraces that might have been farmland once or might have been teeth. Somewhere inside all that stone was her sister.

*Adaeze.* Even now, eight days after opening the jar, the name sat strangely in her mouth, like a coin from a country she'd left as a child. She practiced saying it when she walked. *Adaeze. Adaeze.* As if repetition could weld the word to something real.

Behind her, Perrin flopped down in the grass with the theatrical exhaustion of a man who had been walking for two days and wanted it noted in the historical record.

"I want it noted," he said, "in the historical record, that I have walked for two days."

"Noted," Gideon said.

"Also that my feet have opinions, and those opinions are profane."

"Noted." She didn't turn around. She was counting doors.

Because there were doors. That was the thing the map had promised and the map had delivered, ink rearranging itself one final time on the last night's camp into a diagram so simple it felt like an insult: three lines converging on the mountain's flank, each ending in a small square, each labeled in her mother's careful hand. *Gold. Iron. Bone.*

Now she could see them. They were cut into the mountainside at intervals along its base like windows into nothing — three archways, each glowing faintly with its own light. The first burned warm and honeyed, gold light spilling down the rock like syrup. The second glowed a hard industrial orange, the color of forge-coals. And the third —

The third gave off no color at all. It was simply a darkness shaped like a doorway, and looking at it too long made Gideon's stolen memories shift uneasily in their shelves, the way dogs go quiet before a storm.

"Well," Perrin said, coming up beside her and squinting. "That's cheerful. Three doors. I give it excellent odds at least one of them is a trap."

"All of them are traps," Gideon said. "The question is which trap is honest about it."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said. I'm going to embroider it on a cushion."

She almost smiled. Eight days ago she wouldn't have. Eight days ago she'd been a hollowed-out woman sitting motionless by a dead fire while a magpie-shifter shouted encouragement, insults, and finally, in desperation, the entire libretto of a bawdy song about a fishmonger's wife, trying to drag her back into the world. It hadn't worked, exactly. What had worked was simpler and worse: he had sat down next to her, put no hand on her shoulder, offered no comfort, and said, "I'm not going anywhere. So you may as well be miserable where I can watch, because if you're going to do it alone anyway you're just being stubborn for style."

It was, she reflected, the single most effective speech anyone had ever given her.

They descended toward the doors through the scree, boots crunching, the air thinning and sharpening as they went. Other travelers appeared as they drew closer — the first people Gideon had seen since the salt flats, and every one of them moving with the particular hunched determination of pilgrims who know the destination will disappoint them.

There weren't many. A dozen, perhaps, strung out across the slopes like beads on three separate strings, each string feeding into one door.

"Look at them," Perrin murmured. He'd gone quiet in the way he did around other people's pain, which Gideon had learned was his most reliable tell. Under all the feathers and the chatter, he noticed everything. "They're sorting themselves."

He was right. Nobody was being turned away; nobody was choosing randomly either. Gideon watched a merchant party — six riders, silk hoods, guards with polished pikes — wind confidently up the switchbacks toward the golden door without a moment's hesitation, the way water finds a channel. A band of mercenaries in mismatched armor, scarred and loud, made straight for the iron door, joking loudly enough that Gideon suspected the jokes were load-bearing.

And the rest — the thin ones, the ragged ones, a woman carrying a child too old to carry and too sick to walk, an old man with nothing but a blanket and a spoon tied to his belt — they drifted, slow and unprotesting, toward the dark door.

"The map said," Gideon recited, half to herself, "*gold for the rich, iron for the strong, bone for the desperate.*"

"It also said 'do not open the jar until the dreams come,' and look how well we obeyed that." Perrin hopped a loose stone with the grace of someone whose bones were mostly suggestion. "What I want to know is: who's doing the sorting? Because nobody's checking purses at that gold door. Look — that merchant has guards, sure, but the guards aren't guarding him *from* anything. There's no tollbooth. No clerk. No little man with a ledger asking intrusive questions about income."

"No," Gideon said slowly. She was watching the doors themselves. "The doors are doing it."

As they came within a hundred yards of the base, she felt it begin — a pressure against her mind, gentle as a hand laid flat on a table, asking rather than demanding. *Show me what you have.* Not words. More like the sensation of being weighed, except the scale wasn't measuring her body. It was measuring everything else. Her pockets. Her hoard. The sealed places inside herself where memories lived now instead of other people.

She flinched away from it and the pressure withdrew, polite as a butler.

"Gideon?" Perrin had caught her arm. "You went gray. Grayer. You went a color gray hasn't even heard of."

"It looked at me." She flexed her fingers, grounding herself. "The doors. All three of them, I think. They're reading us."

"Reading us how?"

"Reading what we have." She turned to look at him properly. "Perrin. How much money do you have?"

He patted himself down with great ceremony. "Four buttons, a dried apricot of uncertain vintage, and a favor owed to me by a duck in Vell, which I mention only because the duck is very powerful in local politics."

"So not rich."

"I contain multitudes, but none of them are solvent."

"Then don't touch the gold door." She started walking again, toward the space between the iron and the bone, wanting to see both up close before committing. "And whatever happens, don't let it read you longer than it needs to. These things take samples."

"You say that like you know these things."

"I know collectors." She kept her voice level. "I *was* one."

* * *

The golden door was beautiful, and that was the first warning.

Up close, the archway blazed like a sunset trapped in stone, and the light it cast was warm in a way that got under the skin and loosened things. Gideon stood at the edge of its glow — deliberately outside it, after the reading-pressure brushed her again and she shut her inner shelves fast — and watched a silk-hooded merchant walk into the light without pausing.

The man didn't vanish. That was the horrible part. He simply kept walking, through the archway and onward, into a passage beyond that shouldn't have fit inside the mountain's skin — a corridor of soft gold light stretching back farther than geometry allowed. And as he walked, he *shone*. His outline gilded. His steps slowed, grew heavier, more contented. By ten paces in, he was smiling. By twenty, he had stopped looking around at all. He walked deeper and deeper into the warm, and the warmth took payment as it went, skimming him like cream off milk, and he never once thought to object, because it felt so *good* to stop worrying.

"He'll come out the other side," said a voice near Gideon's elbow.

She startled. An old woman had settled onto a boulder beside the path, wrapped in layers of patched wool, gnawing on a strip of dried meat with the serenity of someone who had been watching this exact scene for a very long time. Her eyes were bright and unimpressed.

"If there is another side," Gideon said.

"There is. I've seen them come out. Richer than they went in, in every way that counts on paper, and lighter by everything that doesn't." The woman shrugged. "The gold door takes your wants. All of them. You walk in wanting things and you walk out wanting nothing, and a man who wants nothing will sign anything, serve anyone, sit in any gilded room forever. The court loves the rich. They arrive pre-tenderized."

"And the iron?"

"See for yourself. It's louder."

It was. From the direction of the iron door came the ring of hammer on anvil, though there was no smithy visible anywhere on the slope. The mercenaries were gathered at its threshold, and Gideon watched one of them — a big woman with a broken nose and a captain's scars — step into the orange glow.

The change was immediate. The woman's spine straightened. Her fists clenched. Her face set into an expression of such ferocious purpose that Gideon's own chest tightened in sympathy, because she recognized it. She had worn that face for years. It was the face of a person who has found the one thing they will not put down.

But the iron door didn't stop there. The glow deepened, and the mercenary began to *burn*, in a way — not flesh, but everything softer than flesh. Doubt, mercy, hesitation, the little voice that says *maybe we should turn back* — all of it rendered down like fat in a hot pan. When she strode through the archway, she moved like a blade being thrown.

"She keeps her strength," the old woman said, conversationally. "She keeps nothing else. The court likes the strong ones too. A soldier who cannot hesitate is worth ten who can. They put them at the gates and they stand there until they rust." She spat a gristly bit into the scree. "Both doors work fine. Both doors deliver. That's the trick of it, girl. The court doesn't need to cheat you. It only needs to let you choose the price you think you can afford."

Gideon's mouth had gone dry. "And the third door?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Gideon's face seemed to settle something, because her voice gentled, losing its tour-guide dryness.

"The bone door takes everything," she said. "Not your wants. Not your strength. Your *stakes*. You walk in with something you cannot afford to lose, and the door weighs it, and if it finds you truly desperate — if it finds there is nothing in you you would keep over the reason you came — then it opens, and it lets you pass *whole*. Ungilded. Unrendered. Nothing skimmed." She smiled, and it was a terrible, kind smile. "Because a person with nothing left to lose can't be bought, can't be bent, and can't be threatened. The court hates them. But the contract is old, older than the court, and the old law says the bone door must open for them. So the court does the only thing it can."

"Which is?"

"Makes sure there are as few of them as possible." The old woman stood, joints crackling like green wood, and gathered her blanket and her spoon. "Most folk who reach this mountain still have something. Pride. Fear. A name they're fond of." Her bright eyes flicked over Gideon, lingering — Gideon could have sworn — on the space just behind her eyes, where a mother's handwriting lived inside a sealed jar that was no longer sealed. "Desperation isn't poverty, girl. Anyone can be poor. Desperation is knowing exactly what you'd burn, and having already struck the match."

She shuffled off down the slope toward the bone door, unhurried, and the darkness accepted her without ceremony.

* * *

Perrin found her twenty minutes later, sitting on a rock equidistant between the iron door's forge-glow and the bone door's patient dark, eating the ancient apricot with the focus of a man delaying a conversation.

Gideon let him finish it. Then: "You've decided something."

"I've decided several things. One of them involves the duck."

"Perrin."

He sighed, and some of the performance dropped out of him, and underneath it he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with his feet. "I've been thinking about what the old woman said. About stakes." He turned the apricot pit over in his fingers. "You know what I've got? I've got a sister's grave I haven't finished digging. I've got a shop I keep imagining, someday, somewhere, that returns what thieves take. I've got—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I've got you, a little bit, if I'm honest, which I resent enormously and won't be repeating."

Gideon said nothing. Her throat had done something complicated.

"And here's my problem." He held up the pit as if presenting evidence. "The bone door takes your stakes. Everything you can't afford to lose. So if I walk through it with you — if I stand in that doorway and it goes weighing me — it's going to find all of that. The grave. The shop. The—" he gestured vaguely at her, wincing, "—the *you*, a little bit. And it's going to take them, isn't it? That's the price of passage. You pass whole because you arrive empty-handed. And I don't *want* to be empty-handed. I spent thirty years learning to hold things."

The wind came down off the glacier and moved the grass in long silver strokes. Far above, the cloud collar churned around the peak, hiding whatever the mountain wore at its crown.

"So here's what I've decided," Perrin said. "I'm taking the iron door."

Gideon's head came up sharply. "Absolutely not. Did you not hear—"

"I heard. Hesitation gets rendered. Fine. I'll miss it." He grinned, and it was the grin he used when the truth underneath was too heavy to carry bare. "But listen. Someone has to be on the other side of this mountain when you come out. Someone who remembers your name — your *real* one, the one you're about to go feed to a contract. Someone who knew you before, so that after, there's one person alive who can tell you who you were." He shrugged. "A strong body gets through the court fine. Strong bodies are useful down there. And strong bodies forget how to hesitate, but they don't forget *people* — that's not hesitation, that's just... inventory."

"Perrin—"

"Don't argue, it'll render badly on both of us." He stood, brushing off his knees, and for a moment his shape flickered — a shimmer of black-and-white at the edges, wings wanting out, wanting *up*, wanting to carry her over all of this the way he'd once carried a dying stranger's message. He mastered it. "Besides. If we both go through the bone door and it empties us, who's going to make the jokes? You? I've heard your jokes. They're more of a threat than a gift."

Gideon laughed — a short, cracked, unexpected sound — and found that behind it her eyes were burning. "This is the worst plan you've ever had."

"I once smuggled myself into a duke's birthday party inside a cake. This is *fine* by comparison."

She stood up. Below them, the three doors waited in their three lights, and the strings of pilgrims thinned as one by one the desperate, the armored, and the wealthy fed themselves into the mountain. The old woman with the spoon was long gone into the dark.

Gideon looked at the bone door for a long time.

It wasn't frightening, she realized. That was the strangest part. The gold door tempted and the iron door dared, but the bone door simply *waited*, the way a grave waits, the way a mother's apology waits eleven years in wax and ink. It had no interest in persuading her. Persuasion was for people who still had alternatives.

And she had none. That was the whole point. That was, she understood suddenly, the entire architecture of her life for the past year — the map that wouldn't leave her, the moons ticking down, the jar she couldn't help opening, the sister's name she couldn't stop practicing. Every road had narrowed, patiently, kindly, until only one remained, and the one remaining road required her to arrive with open hands.

Her mother had known. Had walked this same slope, maybe, or sent the map in her stead, and labeled the doors in that careful hand because she'd known her daughter would need to understand the arithmetic before she could accept it.

*Gold for the rich. Iron for the strong. Bone for the desperate.*

Gideon Okafor had four buttons' worth of wealth, a thief's strength she was about to stop needing, and a sister underground whom she could not afford to lose — which meant, by the door's own merciless logic, that she was exactly desperate enough.

"Walk me to it," she said.

Perrin's grin faded into something older and better. "Always."

They went down together through the last of the grass, past the boulder where the old woman had sat, onto bare stone where the mountain's shadow began. The bone door grew as they approached — a tall dark archway framed in pale rock that might have been marble and might have been something else, something with a faint grain to it, like the cross-section of enormous bone. Up close, the darkness inside wasn't empty. It was full of a low, vast quiet, the quiet of a held breath, and Gideon felt her stolen memories stir on their shelves and go still, listening.

At the threshold, Perrin stopped. He took her hands. His grip was warm and slightly feathered, which should have been funny and somehow wasn't.

"Ninth moon tonight," he said. "Thirteen turns. You've got time. You've got the flaw in the contract, and you've got a parliament of thieves, and you've got—" he squeezed, "—me, a little bit, on the other side, refusing to rust quietly."

"I know."

"And Gideon." His voice dropped. For once there was no joke waiting behind it. "Whatever the door takes — whatever you have to set down to walk through — it stays set down *out here*. With me. I'll hold it. That's the whole job. That's what I'm for."

She nodded, because speaking had become temporarily impossible.

Then she turned to the dark, squared her shoulders, and stepped into the doorway — and the bone door woke, and began to weigh her.

It did not ask. It simply opened her, shelf by shelf, the way she had once opened other people's jars: the widow's grief, the ferryman's summer, the salt flats and her mother's voice, the dream-kitchen with its two pairs of small shoes, the name she'd carried like a coat for thirty years. It weighed each one with terrible patience, testing which she would refuse to surrender.

And Gideon stood in the balance, emptying her hands, and whispered into the dark:

"Take all of it. I'm not here for me."

Somewhere far below, deep in the mountain's throat, something ancient stirred — a ledger turning its own page — and the bone door swung wide, and the stair went down farther than any light she owned could follow.
