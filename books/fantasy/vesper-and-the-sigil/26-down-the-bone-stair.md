## 26. Down the Bone Stair

The bone door closed behind them with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Gideon had expected darkness. What she got instead was light — a pale, milky glow rising from somewhere far below, as though the mountain's throat were lit from the inside by something swallowed and still burning. It caught the walls around them, and she wished it hadn't.

The stair was carved from vertebrae. Actual vertebrae, each one the size of a dinner table, fused spine to spine in a descending spiral that vanished into the glow. Whoever had built this had not been subtle about what they wanted visitors to understand: *you are small, you are inside something that once lived, and it does not live anymore because of us.*

"Cheerful," Perrin said behind her. His voice was doing a poor job of its usual dryness. "I've been in crypts. I've been in *nice* crypts. This is not a nice crypt."

"It's not a crypt," Gideon said. She kept her hand on the wall — warm, faintly greasy, like bone left too long near a fire. "Crypts are for the dead. These people are still paying."

She started down.

* * *

The first gallery opened off the stair at maybe two hundred steps — an alcove in the mountain's flesh, shelved floor to ceiling like a merchant's storeroom. The shelves held jars.

She knew jars. She had spent eleven years buying, selling, stealing, and occasionally weeping over jars. But these were different from anything in the valley or the markets beyond. Valley jars were clay, honest clay, made by potters who signed their work. Market jars were glass, cheap glass, the kind that chipped if you looked at it wrong.

These were crystal, and each one was lit from within by a color that had no business existing outside of dreams. And each one held a face.

Not a picture of a face. A face. Small, pressed against the crystal from the inside, mouth moving without sound. Some of them were sleeping. Some of them were not.

"Oh," Perrin said, very quietly. All the magpie had gone out of him. He sounded like a boy. "Oh, that's — those are *people.*"

"They're souls," Gideon said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She had expected it to shake. "Purchased souls. The archivist told us. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That 'soul' would mean something smaller. A spark. A warmth." She stepped closer to the nearest shelf. "It means a whole person, folded up small enough to shelve."

The soul in the nearest jar was an old man. She could tell even through the distortion of the crystal — the deep lines around his eyes, the way his mouth moved in patterns that looked less like screaming and more like *talking*, like he was telling someone a story and couldn't understand why no one was answering. His jar bore a small brass plate, engraved in a script so fine she had to lean close:

*SOLD BY HIS SONS, FOUR HENS AND A ROOF REPAIR.*

Below that, in the court's own hand, colder and more elegant:

*PAID IN FULL.*

Gideon stood there a long moment. Then she put her palm flat against the crystal, the way she'd seen Widow Sefu press her hand to bread dough, gentle, like touching something alive.

The old man stopped talking. He turned. He looked at her hand.

He smiled.

It was the smile that did it. Not the shelves, not the brass plates, not the cold accounting of it all — the *smile*. Because she understood it instantly, the way a thief understands a lock, and what she understood was this: he wasn't smiling because he was at peace. He was smiling because a stranger had touched his jar, and he had been alone so long that any touch at all felt like grace.

Something in Gideon's chest went from cold to hot so fast it made her dizzy.

"Gideon." Perrin's hand on her shoulder. "We can't open them."

"I know."

"There'll be wards. There'll be—"

"*I know.*" She hadn't raised her voice. She was almost sure she hadn't. But Perrin took his hand back like she'd bitten it, and when she glanced at him his feathers — the ones along his jaw that always rippled when he was nervous — were standing straight out. "I know we can't. Not yet. Not on the way down. But Perrin—" She turned back to the shelf, to the rows and rows and rows of lit faces receding into the dark of the gallery. "I need you to understand that I am going to remember every single one of these. Every face. Every plate. Every price. I have a thief's memory and I have never once used it for anything I'm ashamed of, and I am going to carry this room out of here if I have to carve it into my own bones."

Perrin looked at her for a moment. Then he nodded slowly, and some of the fear went out of his posture, replaced by something steadier.

"Well," he said. "There she is. I was starting to worry the mountain had scared you polite."

* * *

They descended.

The galleries kept coming. That was the thing she hadn't been prepared for — not one vault, not one terrible room, but *galleries*, dozens of them, opening off the spiral stair like the chambers of a heart. Some were small, holding only a few dozen jars. One was vast enough that she couldn't see the far wall, and the glow of ten thousand bottled souls rose off it like heat haze off summer stone.

And the plates. She read them. She couldn't stop reading them.

*SOLD BY HER HUSBAND, DEBTS FORGIVEN.*

*TRADED BY HER VILLAGE, RAIN FOR A SEASON.*

*BOUGHT FROM A FAMINE YEAR, PRICE NEGOTIABLE.*

*PAID IN FULL.*

*PAID IN FULL.*

*PAID IN FULL.*

"That one," Perrin said hoarsely, three galleries down. He was pointing at a jar near the floor, set apart from the others, half-hidden behind a support rib of the mountain itself. The soul inside was young — younger than either of them — and the plate read only: *FOUND WANDERING. NO CLAIMANT.*

"No claimant," Gideon repeated.

"They didn't even buy him," Perrin said. "They just — found him. And kept him. Like a coin on the road." His voice cracked on the last word, and he turned away fast, fussing with the strap of his pack, and Gideon pretended not to see him wipe his face, because that was the kind of friend she had decided to be.

Somewhere below them, faintly, came music. Court music — strings and something reedy, playing a tune with too many notes in it, beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful. The sound drifted up the stair and wrapped around the galleries, and Gideon realized with a fresh lurch of nausea that the court could hear its own collection from the dance floor. They dined above this. They waltzed above this.

Her fear had been dying by degrees since the bone door. Now she checked its pulse and found nothing there at all. In its place was something cleaner and much more dangerous.

"Funny thing," she said lightly, as they started down again. "I've been frightened of this mountain my whole life. Even before I knew it existed, I think. The valley teaches you that magic is the monster, doesn't it? Magic is what takes things. Magic is why we lock our memories in jars and live small and safe."

"And?" Perrin said.

"And the monster has *accounting ledgers.*" She gestured at the nearest shelf, at the neat brass plates, the tidy script. "Magic doesn't write *paid in full*. People do. People sat at desks with good lamplight and decided what a grandmother was worth in hens. I've been afraid of a storm my whole life, and it turns out the storm is just men with excellent penmanship."

Perrin snorted despite himself. "You should write pamphlets."

"I should write *invoices*," Gideon said. "I'm going to send them a bill. Item one: one stolen childhood, returned with interest. Item two: ten thousand souls, plus damages. Item three—" She paused at the next landing, where the stair bent around a sheer drop into the glow, and the music floated up clearer, and somewhere beneath the strings she heard — or thought she heard — a voice singing under the tune. Thin. Far down. Familiar in a way that made her ribs ache.

"Item three?" Perrin prompted.

"I haven't decided yet," Gideon said. "But it's going to be expensive."

* * *

Near the bottom, the galleries changed.

The last flight of stairs passed through a chamber unlike the others — no shelves here, but pedestals, each one holding a single jar under a dome of glass, each one labeled in gold instead of brass. Favorites, she thought. Prizes. Souls the court kept apart the way a miser keeps his best coins in a separate purse.

She almost walked past the third pedestal. Then she stopped, because the handwriting on the gold plate snagged her eye — not the court's cold script but something older, rounder, written by a hand that had learned letters in a kitchen rather than a counting house.

The plate said: *ADAEZE.*

Gideon did not move for a long time.

The jar on the pedestal glowed a soft amber, warmer than any of the others, and inside it — folded small, folded the way grief folds a person small — was a girl. Her sister. Her twin. The other pair of shoes by the kitchen door. The voice in the lullaby that Gideon had carried in a sealed jar for twenty years without knowing whose it was.

"She's here," Perrin whispered. "That's really—"

"Yes."

"She looks like you."

"I know."

"She looks *exactly* like you, Gideon, it's frankly unsettling—"

"*I know.*" But there was no edge in it. There was nothing in her but the hot clean thing, and underneath the hot clean thing, something else — something that had no name yet, that felt like standing in a doorway between everything she'd been and everything she might choose to be.

Adaeze's folded soul stirred against the crystal. Turned. Looked at her.

And smiled — the same way the old man had smiled, the smile of the long-alone offered any touch at all — except that halfway through, the smile faltered, and changed, and became something else entirely. Recognition. The recognition of someone seeing a face they'd known before they knew what faces were.

Her lips moved against the crystal. No sound came out. But Gideon had grown up reading mouths through memory-jar glass, and she caught it anyway, three silent syllables shaped slow and wondering:

*You stayed.*

The music below swelled. Somewhere beneath it, the court was laughing at something — a bright, many-voiced laughter, the laughter of people who have never once had to check a price tag.

Gideon pressed her hand to the crystal one last time, then drew it back, and when she turned toward the final stair her eyes were dry and her voice was perfectly steady and Perrin later swore it was the most frightening thing he'd ever witnessed.

"Come on," she said. "Let's go ruin their evening."

At the foot of the bone stair, doors of black marble stood open onto a hall of light and music — and flanking those doors, tall and patient as furniture, stood two masked figures who had clearly been waiting for her.

"The Under-Court," one said pleasantly, "will see the thief now."
