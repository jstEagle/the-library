## 27. Audience with the Under-Court

The bone stair ended the way all stairs end: with a door.

This one was not bone, though it had been carved to look like it — pale wood polished smooth as a knuckle, inlaid with a pattern of small white squares that Gideon realized, after a long and unwelcome moment, were teeth. Real ones, by the look of them. Someone had collected them. Someone had *arranged* them, in spirals, like a garden of small surrenders.

Perrin stood at her shoulder, feathers ruffled flat against his neck in the way they went when he was frightened and pretending not to be. He had been human-shaped for three days straight now, which for Perrin was roughly equivalent to a man holding his breath.

"I could go back up," he said. "Not abandoning you. Just — reconnoitering. From very far away."

"You'd leave me alone down here?"

"Obviously not." He tugged his coat straight. "I'm saying I could reconnoiter *loudly*. Draw attention. Magpies are excellent distractions."

"Magpies are excellent thieves."

"Those are the same skill set, and I resent the tone."

Gideon almost smiled. It was strange what her face chose to do in the last hour before she might die. She pressed her palm against the tooth-inlaid door, felt it give like skin, and stepped through before her courage could reconsider.

* * *

The hall beneath the mountain was not what she had expected, which was itself a kind of disappointment. She had imagined something vast and terrible — a cavern hung with chains, a throne of fused skulls, the architecture of nightmare. Instead the Under-Court sat in a room no larger than Widow Sefu's parlor, low-ceilinged and warm, lit by a hundred bottled lights that glowed amber and honey and one defiant green. Comfortable chairs. A long table. And around that table, the court.

They wore masks. Of course they wore masks; the map had promised masks, and the archivist had described them, and still nothing quite prepared Gideon for the reality of eleven faces that were not faces — porcelain, lacquered wood, hammered tin, each painted with an expression frozen somewhere between amusement and appetite. They did not move as she entered. They did not speak. Eleven masked heads turned in perfect unison, like weathervanes finding the wind, and Gideon understood that she had been expected since approximately the moment of her birth.

"Good evening," said the nearest mask — a smiling thing of white porcelain with rosy painted cheeks. Its voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of someone who has never once been interrupted. "You've come a long way down. May we offer you tea?"

"I've come for Adaeze Okafor," Gideon said. "Her soul is held here against every law of decency, and I am demanding its release."

A pause. Then the porcelain mask laughed — a light, delighted sound, quickly joined by the others until the whole table was chuckling like a dinner party that had just heard an excellent joke about the vicar.

"Oh, *demanding*," said the porcelain mask, dabbing at its painted cheek with a lace handkerchief that appeared from nowhere. "Forgive us. It's been so long since anyone demanded anything of this court. The last was a king, oh — four hundred years ago? He also came down the bone stair. He also used the word *decency*." The mask tilted. "We kept his teeth. You may have noticed them on your way in. We found the spiral arrangement soothing."

Gideon kept her feet planted and her chin level. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart was conducting business at a frankly humiliating tempo, but she had spent twenty years standing in strangers' kitchens lifting their griefs out of locked jars, and she knew how to be still while everything in her screamed run.

"My sister was sold to you," she said. "Thirteen years ago. Famine year. My mother signed whatever you put in front of her because her children were starving, and you knew it, and you took advantage. That contract is rotten at the root. I'm here to pull it up."

"Mm," said the court, in eleven voices at once, a sound like wind through a keyhole. The green-bottled light flickered. "And you are?"

"Gideon Okafor."

The laughter stopped. All at once, completely, the way a flock of birds stops singing. The silence that followed had texture — thick, interested, the silence of eleven things leaning forward without moving.

"Gideon Okafor," repeated the porcelain mask slowly, tasting it. "Yes. Yes, we thought so. You have her hands."

"I have my own hands."

"Do you?" The mask gestured gently, and one of the bottled lights drifted down from the ceiling to hover between them. Inside it, instead of flame, Gideon saw a memory moving — a kitchen, steam on a window, two pairs of small shoes by the door. Her stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "We are a court of records, Miss Okafor. Everything sold to us is catalogued, cross-referenced, and preserved. Your mother's contract is one of our most cherished holdings. Would you like to hear the terms recited? We do voices."

"I want Adaeze released. That's all I came for."

"And yet." The porcelain mask spread its hands, a gesture of such warm regret that Gideon briefly hated it personally. "Here is the difficulty. Here is always the difficulty. Contracts bind both ways."

"They bind *you*—"

"They bind everyone who signs." The mask's voice remained gentle, almost pitying, which was infinitely worse than cruelty. "Your mother signed for herself, yes. But a soul cannot be sold by proxy, child — not truly, not under the old law. The seller must have claim. And so your mother, clever woman, grieving woman, made claim the only way the law allows." The mask leaned forward, rosy cheeks catching the light. "She offered both daughters. One soul to keep, one name to seal. Two girls, one contract. You have heard this already, haven't you? From the blind man in the burned library. He does love to tell half a story."

"The archivist said you held Adaeze's soul as surety."

"He said true things. He simply stopped before the uncomfortable ones." The mask produced, from somewhere within its immaculate sleeve, a scroll. It unrolled across the table with a whisper, and Gideon saw the ink — deep violet, the same violet as the map's rearranging letters, the same violet that had haunted her dreams since the rim. "Adaeze's soul, held as surety. And here—" a lacquered fingernail tapped a line near the bottom, "—the surety's release, conditional upon presentation of the co-signatory. The other daughter. The one who stayed."

The hall was very quiet. The bottled lights hummed.

"That's not a signature," Gideon said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "That's a child. I was six years old. You can't hold a six-year-old to a contract."

"We can," said the court, pleasantly, "and we do. It is, if you'll forgive the phrase, ironclad. Your mother wept when she signed it — we noted that too, we note everything, sentiment is such useful ink — but grief does not void a signature. Nor does youth. Nor does forgetting." The mask tilted its head, and the painted smile seemed suddenly less decorative. "Nor does walking away for thirteen years and building yourself a new life out of other people's memories, though we admit that last part showed initiative."

Perrin made a small sound behind her, like a kettle deciding whether to scream.

Gideon stared at the scroll. She could not read the words from where she stood — the script swam, rearranging, the way the map's ink had always rearranged — but she didn't need to read it. She had known, hadn't she? Some part of her had known since the salt flats sang with her mother's voice, since the sealed jar labeled in that careful hand, since the dream of the kitchen and the lullaby and the two pairs of shoes. She had walked down a thousand steps into the dark telling herself she was a rescuer, a thief with a conscience, a hero of the particular sort that exists only in stories told by the people they save.

And the whole time, her name had been waiting at the bottom, filed and stamped.

"So the map," she said. "My mother's apology. The whole journey. It wasn't to free Adaeze at all."

"It was to bring *you*," said the porcelain mask, with genuine warmth. "Your mother was many things, but she was never stupid. She knew the court would not release a surety for love or for money or for tears. She knew the only thing that could open this vault was the co-signatory, walking in of her own free will, to honor the family's obligation." The mask folded its hands. "You are the payment, Miss Okafor. You always were. We simply had the good manners to wait."

* * *

For a moment — a long, ringing moment — Gideon heard nothing at all. The hall, the lights, the eleven masks, even Perrin's breathing: all of it receded, and she stood alone inside her own skull, looking at the shape of her life as if seeing it from above for the first time.

Every stolen memory. Every jar she had ever lifted, every grief she had carried out of a stranger's house, every fragment she had hoarded against the hollow feeling in her chest — she had built herself from salvage because there had been nothing else to build from. She had thought herself empty. She had thought the emptiness was a wound, an accident, a thief's occupational hazard.

It was a ledger entry. She was a debt, walking around wearing boots.

"Well," said Perrin, into the silence, because Perrin had never once in his life respected a dramatic pause. "That's — hm. That's not ideal."

"Perrin," Gideon said.

"Yes."

"Shut up."

"Absolutely."

The porcelain mask watched her with what might have been compassion, if compassion could be painted on. "Take a moment," it offered kindly. "We have all eternity and no appointments. Tea really is available."

"I don't want tea." Gideon's voice steadied as she said it, surprising her. "I want to understand the terms. If I'm the co-signatory — if my name releases the surety — then say it plainly. What happens to Adaeze if I honor this?"

"She goes free." The mask said it simply. "Soul restored, debts cleared, the girl walks out of this mountain whole. That part of the contract has never been in dispute. We are scrupulous, Miss Okafor. Whatever else we are, we pay what we owe and collect what we're owed, and we do not confuse the two."

"And me?"

"Ah." The mask's smile did not change, but somehow it became wider. "That depends entirely on which parts of the agreement you choose to read closely. Most signatories don't. Most signatories are grieving, or starving, or six years old." A pause, delicately weighted. "You, however, are a thief. And thieves, we find, read everything."

Something shifted in Gideon's chest — not hope, not yet, but the place where hope would go if she cleared the ground for it. Because the mask was right. She was a thief. She had spent her whole life reading the fine print of other people's hearts, finding the loose latch, the unwatched drawer, the clause nobody enforced. If there was a flaw in this document — and there was always a flaw, there was *always* a flaw, that was the first lesson of her trade — then she would find it, even if she had to read every violet line with her eyes bleeding.

But not yet. Not while eleven masks were watching her hands.

"Then I'll need to see the full contract," she said. "All of it. Every page, every amendment, every marginal note. If you expect me to honor a signature I gave at six years old, you'll extend me the courtesy of knowing what I signed."

The court murmured among itself, a sound like pages turning. The porcelain mask exchanged a glance with a tin mask beside it — or performed the exchange; it was impossible to tell what happened behind those faces — and then inclined its head with the graciousness of a host whose trap has sprung exactly as designed.

"Naturally," it said. "We would think less of you if you didn't ask. The document will be provided. Take whatever time you require." The mask rose, and the rest of the court rose with it, eleven shapes unfolding in eerie unison. "In the meantime, you will be our guests. Rooms have been prepared. Dinner, if you wish it — the kitchen here is genuinely excellent, we employ a very talented ghost. And tomorrow, Miss Okafor, we will begin the reading."

As they moved to escort her out — two masks drifting toward her with the unhurried menace of well-fed cats — Gideon caught one last sight of the scroll on the table, the violet ink shifting, rearranging, almost legible.

And there, at the bottom of the page, in the space where her six-year-old hand had once been guided to make its mark, she saw something that stopped her heart mid-beat.

The signature line was blank.

Not crossed out. Not faded. *Blank* — as though the name had been lifted out of it, cleanly, recently, by someone who knew exactly how to take a thing without leaving a mark.

Someone, Gideon thought, with a thief's hands.

Someone very much like her.
