## 28. The Fine Print of Souls

The contract was brought to her on a tray.

That was the first insult. The second was the paper itself — vellum so fine it seemed to glow from within, like something that had never known dirt or weather or the honest grime of a working hand. A court scribe in a silver mask set the tray on the table before Gideon with the ceremony of a man presenting a crown, bowed, and withdrew without a word.

Gideon looked at the contract for a long moment before touching it.

She sat in a small room off the great audience hall, a room clearly designed to make visitors feel like specimens. One chair. One table. One lamp burning with cold white light that made everything look slightly dead. Somewhere beyond the walls, the mountain hummed its low, patient hum, and ten thousand bottled souls glowed in their galleries like a city that had forgotten it was a prison.

Perrin had wanted to come in with her. She'd told him no.

"If I'm going to rob someone," she'd said, "I do better without an audience."

"You're not robbing them. You're *negotiating*."

"Perrin." She'd held his eyes. "Everything is robbing, if you look at it right. Some people just use nicer trays."

He'd laughed at that — a short, startled laugh, the kind he made when she said something true enough to frighten him — and then he'd squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and gone to wait by the bone door with his feathers ruffled and his jaw set like a man planning seventeen escape routes.

Now she was alone with the contract, and the contract was alone with her, and only one of them knew what it was doing.

She picked it up.

The vellum was warm. That should have frightened her more than it did. Documents weren't supposed to be warm; documents were supposed to be dead things, ink on skin, agreements between people who were mostly lying. But this one had a pulse to it, faint and slow, like something sleeping. Like something *fed*.

She read.

The language was old court-formal, dense as packed earth, but Gideon Okafor had spent twenty years reading things people didn't want read — ledgers kept in code, letters written in invisible ink, wills designed to be misunderstood. She'd once unpicked a merchant's double-entry books in an afternoon and found where he'd hidden his second wife's fortune from his first. Reading was a lockpick. You just had to find the seam.

So she read slowly. She read twice. On the third pass, she stopped reading for meaning and started reading for *structure*, the way a thief stops looking at a house and starts looking at its bones.

And there — third pass, ninth clause, buried under four layers of subordinate phrasing designed to make a reader's eye slide past it like water over glass —

*Item the Ninth: Whereas the party of the first part (hereafter "the Mother") does convey unto the Court one soul entire, being the soul of the child Adaeze, together with all appurtenances thereof, all claims thereof, all fruits thereof—*

There it was. *All appurtenances thereof.* The court's favorite trick, she realized. The phrase meant *everything attached*, and it was designed to mean everything. Soul, body, breath, dreams, descendants, the very shadow the girl cast on sunny days. It was the legal equivalent of a net with no holes.

Except.

Gideon set the contract down. Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a strange, distant pride — somewhere inside her, the thief who had lifted a widow's grief out of a locked jar was still on duty, still cool, still counting.

Because she had spent two decades in the memory trade, and there was one thing every thief in the trade learned before they learned anything else. Before you learned to take. Before you learned to sell. The first lesson, the one whispered to apprentices on their first night:

*A soul and a name are not the same thing.*

A soul was what you were. A name was what you were *called* — and being called was a thing that happened *between* people. A name lived in mouths. In mothers' voices. In the shout across a market square, the whisper at a graveside, the lullaby sung low over a cradle. You could bottle a soul in a jar of green glass and shelve it in a mountain. But a name? A name was distributed. A name was *out there*, held in trust by everyone who had ever spoken it.

And clause nine conveyed the soul. Clause nine conveyed the appurtenances. Clause nine went on, and on, and on, in language so exhaustive it circled back around to exhaustion —

But nowhere — not in clause nine, not in clause twelve, not in the long recital of the Mother's debts or the Court's perpetual rights — nowhere in the whole warm, breathing, suffocating document did the word *name* appear.

Not once.

Gideon read it a fourth time just to be sure. Then a fifth, because hope was a dangerous thing and she wanted it armed rather than naive. She checked the definitions section, where the court defined *soul* in terms so broad they'd have swallowed the moon if they could. She checked the warranties. She checked the little clause about successors and assigns, the one that let the court repossess Adaeze's soul from anyone who stole it, in perpetuity, world without end.

Nothing. No name. No grant of it, no waiver of it, no quiet little line claiming it as an appurtenance. The drafters — and they had been careful drafters, careful as she was, careful in exactly her own way — had enumerated everything they could think of.

And they had not thought of names.

Or — and this was the thought that made her smile, slow and sharp, in the empty white-lit room — maybe they had thought of names, and decided no one would ever be fool enough to fight a contract over a word. Maybe they'd assumed that a name without a soul was nothing, a shell, an echo with no one home. Why claim it? Who would want it?

*Me,* Gideon thought. *I would. I do.*

Because here was what the court had never understood about thieves, the thing you could only know if you'd spent your life handling other people's precious things: ownership wasn't a fact. Ownership was a *story*. Deeds and contracts and clauses were just stories told loudly enough that people stopped telling better ones. And the best story always won — not the oldest one, not the loudest one, but the one people *needed* to believe.

The court's story was: we bought her, all of her, forever.

Gideon's story could be: you bought her soul. Fine. Keep it — keep the jar, keep the glow, keep your galleries of purchased selves. But her *name* was never yours. It was given free, in a kitchen, by a woman crying into her hands, and a thing given freely cannot be sold, because it was never owned. And a girl who still has her name is not fully bought. A girl who still has her name can be *called*.

Called home. Called back. Called out of the dark by someone standing at the mouth of a labyrinth shouting it into the deep.

Her hands were shaking now. She let them.

She thought of Adaeze — the fading girl in the dream-kitchen, the lullaby shared between strangers, the two pairs of small shoes by a door that existed only in sleep. She thought: *you kept your name. They took everything else, starved you moon by moon, fed on you like a vintage — but they never took the one thing that was never theirs to take, because our mother, in her guilt, in her grief, in her terrible cleverness, never put it on the paper.*

Maybe the mother hadn't known. Maybe she'd left it out by instinct, the way you leave one window unlatched. Or maybe — and Gideon's chest tightened at this — maybe some part of her had done it on purpose. Had sat across from those masked lawyers with her daughters already divided, one bound for the mountain road and one staying behind, and had signed away a soul while keeping back a single word, a single thread, trusting that someday, somehow, someone would find it and pull.

*Thirteen turns,* the dying stranger had whispered. *Thirteen turns.*

Not a deadline. An instruction. Thirteen chances to pull the thread.

Gideon stood up. The contract lay on the table, warm and smug and fatally incomplete, and she looked down at it the way she used to look down at a merchant's strongbox just before the lid came open.

There was a knock — two soft raps, Perrin's signal, the one that meant *trouble, but the interesting kind*. She crossed the room and opened the door a crack. His face appeared in the gap, human-shaped for once, though a feather had escaped at his temple and refused to lie flat.

"Well?" he whispered. "Did they offer you eternity? They offered me eternity once. Very rude about the counteroffer."

"They gave me the contract."

"And?"

"And," Gideon said, and she felt the grin arrive on her face like sunrise over the salt flats, fierce and uninvited, "they made a mistake."

Perrin blinked. "Court's don't make mistakes. Courts make *precedents*."

"Then we're about to make history." She pulled the door wider, already moving, already planning — because finding the flaw was half the theft, and the other half was getting out with the goods. "Get ready to run. And Perrin —"

"Yeah?"

"When we get my sister's name shouted loud enough, I want the whole mountain to hear it."

Somewhere below them, deep in the humming dark, ten thousand jars flickered — just once, all together, like a held breath.

Like something answering to a name it hadn't heard in thirteen years.
