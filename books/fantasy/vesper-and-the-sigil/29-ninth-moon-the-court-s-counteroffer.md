## 29. Ninth Moon, The Court's Counteroffer

The ninth moon came in through the high windows of Adaeze's chamber like a guest who had learned, over many visits, exactly how unwelcome it was.

It was a thin moon now, a paring of silver, and its light fell across the floor in a long pale stripe that Adaeze had privately named the Corridor of Bad News. Everything that happened to her arrived by that stripe. The servants did not knock; they simply appeared at the edge of the light, as though they had been standing there all along and had only just decided to be visible. The messages from her dreams arrived there too, or rather their consequences did: on the mornings after she managed to push an image through the veil — a kitchen, a lullaby, two pairs of small shoes — the chamber would be subtly wrong. A chair turned to face the wall. Her tea gone cold before she touched it. Once, every mirror in the room covered in black silk, which she understood to be the court's way of saying *we saw that* without wasting words.

She had been leaving messages in dreams for three moons now, and she had no idea if any of them landed anywhere. That was the particular cruelty of it. She could shout into the dark forever, and the dark never once shouted back, but neither did it tell her to stop, and so she chose to believe — stubbornly, unglamorously, the way you believe in rain during a drought — that somewhere out there, someone was getting wet.

Tonight, though, it was not a servant waiting at the edge of the moonlight.

It was the Under-Court itself.

They never came singly. They came as a rustle, a gathering, seven masked figures arranged in the corridor of light with the ease of people who owned the concept of arrangement. Their masks were porcelain and bone and one, Adaeze had noticed long ago, appeared to be made of pressed flowers that never wilted and never bloomed. They did not walk so much as arrive in sequence, each figure completing the picture the previous one had begun.

"Little soul," said the center mask, whose voice was warm the way a banked fire is warm — warm because it is deciding whether to burn your house down. "You have been busy."

Adaeze set down the cup of tea she had been holding for the better part of an hour without drinking. It was a habit she'd developed early: always have something in your hands. Hands full of teacup were hands that could not tremble visibly.

"I've been resting," she said. "I'm told it's good for the complexion."

"You have been *writing*," said the flower-mask, and its voice was dry as petals in a closed book. "In places where writing is not permitted."

"I'm a very poor reader of rules. No one taught me letters until I was six, and by then my teacher despaired of me." This was true, more or less. What she did not say was that she had spent nine moons teaching herself the exact grammar of the veil between sleep and waking, the way another prisoner might spend nine moons filing through bars. "Also, I get bored. Boredom is dangerous in a girl like me. My mother used to say—"

"We know what your mother used to say," said the center mask, gently, and the gentleness was worse than any threat, because it meant they had read her. All of her. Every memory the court had purchased along with her soul was a book on their shelves, and they had clearly done their reading. "Your mother said many things. We hold several of them. Would you like to hear them? We find our guests often do. It is, after all, the only way they hear anything anymore."

Adaeze kept her face still. Nine moons in the mountain had taught her that the court's favorite game was not cruelty — cruelty was crude, and the court disdained crude things the way a jeweler disdains a hammer. Their favorite game was *offering*. Every horror they committed, they committed as a transaction, and every transaction began with an offer so reasonable-sounding that refusing it felt like the unreasonable thing.

So she waited. Patience, she had learned, was the only currency they respected, because it was the only one they couldn't mint themselves.

The center mask inclined its head, acknowledging the wait, perhaps even approving of it.

"The ledger turns tonight," it said. "Nine moons of thirteen have passed. Your soul has thinned accordingly — you feel it, we know. The dreams cost you. The remembering costs you. By the twelfth turn there will be very little left of Adaeze to argue with anyone, and by the thirteenth, nothing at all but a sweet residue we will decant into the court's oldest vessels, where you will keep company with kings and poets and one extremely tedious fisherman who sold us his youth in 1740."

"He sounds lovely," Adaeze said. "I'll lead the book club."

Something that might have been amusement passed behind the masks. Or indigestion. With porcelain, it was hard to tell.

"*However,*" the center mask continued, and here it was, here was the pivot, the whole reason for the visit, arriving with the smooth inevitability of a trap closing in slow motion. "*However.* The court is not without mercy, and more to the point, the court is not without taste. You interest us, little soul. In nine moons you have not broken. You have not gone quiet. You have instead done something we have not seen in four hundred years of acquisition."

"Blinked too much?"

"You have *reached outward*." The flower-mask said it with something close to reverence, which was the most frightening thing anyone had said to her yet. "Every fading soul turns inward. Curdles. Becomes a puddle of what it was. But you — you have been spending yourself like coin. Throwing your last warmth at a stranger's sleeping mind, night after night, on the barest hope of landing. Do you know what that is, little soul?"

"Bad budgeting?"

"It is *value*," said the center mask. "And the court collects value."

Adaeze's hands, wrapped around the cold teacup, went very still.

Here it was. She had known since the first moon that this moment would come — not because she was clever, though she liked to think she was a little clever, but because she had grown up in a house where famine taught everyone the shape of a bargain before they could name it. When a powerful party tells you that you interest them, they are not complimenting you. They are pricing you.

"And so," the center mask went on, spreading hands that were gloved in shadow, "the court makes you a counteroffer. Hear it fully before you answer, as courtesy demands."

"I wouldn't dream of interrupting," said Adaeze. "I've been starved of conversation for nine moons. Go on for hours, please."

"The court offers you eternity. Not the thin eternity of a decanted residue — a true one. A place among us. Not a vessel: a *voice*. You would sit at the turning of the ledger. You would wear a mask of your own choosing — the flower-mask is vacant, and frankly, someone should fill it who understands humor. You would be fed, not drained. Honored, not shelved. The poor souls in the galleries below would look up at you the way the drowning look at a lit window." The voice softened, and the softness was masterful, genuinely masterful, and Adaeze hated how much of her wanted to lean toward it. "You would want for nothing. You would *fade* for nothing. All of this, in exchange for one small thing."

"One small thing," Adaeze repeated.

"A denunciation." The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and they all listened for the splash. "Your sister comes for us even now. We have watched her cross the salt flats. We have watched her gather allies — thieves, ferry-men, archivists, the usual rabble of the unpaid. She will reach the vault by the twelfth turn, and she will make her claim of kinship, and the law will grind her fine, because contracts bind both ways and her name is inked beside yours on the original page. Unless." The mask tilted. "Unless the kinship is renounced. Unless the soul herself, freely and before witnesses, declares: *this woman is no sister of mine. Let her claim die on my lips.* One sentence, little soul. Spoken once. And then eternity, warm and lit and yours."

The silence afterward had texture. Adaeze sat in it and let it press against her like deep water, and she examined herself carefully, the way you examine a bruise, to see how bad it was.

Because here was the truth she would not say aloud: she did not know Gideon Okafor.

She knew a shape. She knew a lullaby hummed off-key by someone who had only ever heard it secondhand. She knew two pairs of small shoes by a kitchen door, one pair scuffed at the toes because that child dragged her feet when she walked, and Adaeze knew — with the certainty of the body and not the memory — that the dragging feet had been hers, and therefore the other pair had belonged to the sister. She knew a mother's tears in a doorway, and a hand letting go of hers, and a road going up, and none of it assembled into a person. There was no face. There was no shared joke, no remembered quarrel, no scar with a story. Gideon Okafor was a door in a house Adaeze had lived in for nine moons but had never once been able to open.

And the court knew that. Of course they knew that. That was the elegance of the offer. They weren't asking her to betray someone she loved; they were asking her to deny someone she barely knew, and dress it up as mercy for them both. *This woman is no sister of mine* — wasn't it, in the strictest sense, almost true? Wasn't there a version of her, a tired, fading, frightened version, that could say those words and mean nothing by them, because there was nothing in her to mean it *with*?

The masks waited. They were very good at waiting. Waiting was, Adaeze suspected, the court's native language, and everything else was translation.

She thought about the dragging feet. She thought about the scuffed toes, and about how she knew they were hers, and about what kind of knowledge that was — knowledge that lived lower than memory, in some cellar of the self where the court's ledgers had never reached. They had bought her soul. They had bought her memories, or most of them. But nobody had ever figured out how to purchase the plain animal fact of a twin: the fact that you had come into the world side by side, two heartbeats keeping time in one small dark room, and that some part of you had been listening to the other heartbeat ever since, even through stone, even through nine moons of mountain, even through forgetting.

They could buy what she remembered. They could not buy what she *was*.

"Well?" said the flower-mask, and there was impatience under the dryness now, the first crack in all that porcelain. "The court does not make this offer twice."

"That's a shame," Adaeze said. "It's a very good offer. Honestly. Someone should take it." She set down the teacup with great care, because her hands had started trembling after all, and she was not going to give them the satisfaction of watching the cup rattle against the saucer. "But I'm afraid I can't."

"You cannot? Or you will not?"

"Oh, I *can*." She stood, because her legs needed the work and because sitting felt like shrinking. "I've had nine moons to compose the sentence. I know exactly how it would sound. I'd give it real pathos. A little catch in the voice, right at 'no sister of mine.' You'd love it. Your ledgers would purr." She looked at the center mask, straight into the blankness where eyes would be, because that was the trick of facing masks — you looked where the eyes should be, and eventually the thing wearing the mask flinched first. "But here's what your ledgers don't have a column for. Love isn't a memory. If it were, you'd own all of mine already, and I'd have signed your sentence weeks ago. Love is a *decision*. You make it fresh every morning, out of whatever scraps you have. And I have decided — with almost no evidence, on the strength of one lullaby and two pairs of shoes — that the woman coming up your mountain is my sister, and that I will stand next to her claim until the thirteenth moon takes me or she does."

The masks did not move. But the room moved around them; the candle flames leaned, all together, like grass under wind.

"She is a thief," said the flower-mask. "A taker of other people's pasts. Whatever she tells you she is, understand what she is: a woman who has made a trade of souls' leavings. Is that the sister you choose?"

"Yes," Adaeze said. "Especially that part. Because I've thought about it, you see. Why would a thief come all this way? Thieves follow value, and you're offering her nothing but trouble. So either she's the worst thief in history, or—" her voice caught, and she let it, deliberately, giving them the pathos she'd promised, aimed like a knife, "—or she's coming for something she can't put a price on. Which means somewhere under all that stealing, there's a person who remembers me the way I remember her. From the outside in. From the feet up." She smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and she was proud of it. "You bought my soul, gentlemen. You should have shopped around. It came with a twin attached, and twins are non-refundable."

For a long moment, nothing. Then the center mask sighed — a soft, papery sound, the sigh of an institution rather than a being.

"So be it. The offer is refused and entered into the record. The ledger turns as it must." The figure turned toward the dark, then paused, and the pause was the cruelest thing it had done all evening. "You should know, little soul, that your refusal changes nothing except the manner of your ending. The thief will fail. They always do. And when she fails, you will fade knowing that eternity stood open before you, and that you chose a stranger over it."

Adaeze considered that. Considered it honestly, the way she tried to consider everything, because a decision you didn't look at squarely wasn't a decision, it was just a reflex wearing a costume.

"No," she said quietly. "I'll fade knowing I chose *family* over it. You keep calling her a stranger because it's the only way the arithmetic works for you. But strangers are people you haven't decided about yet." She lifted her chin. "I decided nine moons ago. I decide again tonight. Ask me tomorrow, and I'll decide again. That's the thing about decisions, your honor. They renew."

The masks withdrew the way they had come, in sequence, each figure subtracting itself from the picture until only the flower-mask remained, and it lingered at the edge of the moonlight a beat longer than protocol required.

"For what it is worth," it said, in a voice suddenly, startlingly young, "the fisherman in 1740 also refused. He is still in the galleries. He is still tedious. But he is *himself*, and the vessels around him are not." A pause. "The court finds this inconvenient."

Then it was gone, and the Corridor of Bad News lay empty, and Adaeze was alone with a cold cup of tea and a heart that was pounding like a girl's and not like a fading soul's at all.

She sat back down. She picked up the tea. She drank it, cold and bitter, because it was hers and she was still here to drink it.

*Thirteen turns,* the dying stranger had whispered, according to the story the dreams sometimes carried back to her, fragments riding in on the same current she sent her messages out on. *Thirteen turns.* Four remained. Somewhere above her, a thief with her sister's stubbornness was gathering thieves and ferrymen and blind archivists into something, and the court had just shown her its teeth, which meant the court was worried, which meant—

Which meant the mountain was louder tonight than it had been in nine moons. Down in the galleries, ten thousand bottled souls were humming. She could feel it through the floor, faint and rising, a sound like a crowd inhaling before it speaks.

Adaeze pressed her palm flat against the cold stone and listened.

Whatever was coming, it was coming soon. And for the first time since the road went up and the hand let go, she intended to be ready when it arrived.

* * *

That night, she dreamed of a kitchen.

Not the old kitchen — a new one, half-built, walls of borrowed light and shelves made of returned things. Two pairs of shoes sat by a door that didn't exist yet. And on the table, in handwriting she almost recognized, a note that read:

*Hold on. We're coming down the stair.*

Adaeze woke with her hand still pressed to the floor, and the humming in the galleries had changed key.

It sounded, impossibly, like singing.
