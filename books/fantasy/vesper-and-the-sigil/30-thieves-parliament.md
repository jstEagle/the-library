## 30. Thieves' Parliament

The memory-eater's name was Ossian, which surprised Gideon, because she had assumed a creature who drank other people's childhoods for a living would have a name like a cough, or a debt, or a door closing. Instead he was called Ossian, wore a coat with forty-one pockets, and apologized when he stepped on her shadow.

"You're certain about this," Perrin said for the fourth time as they descended into the market's under-hall. He said it the way he said most things — like a man complaining about weather that hadn't arrived yet.

"No," Gideon said. "I'm certain about almost nothing. But I'm certain the court bought from every one of these people, and I'm certain the court never paid full price, and if there's one thing thieves understand, it's being shortchanged."

"That's not loyalty. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic holds better than loyalty. Loyalty you have to feed. Arithmetic just sits there being true."

Perrin muttered something in magpie that she'd learned by now meant *I hate that this is reasonable*, and followed her down.

* * *

The under-hall had been a wine cellar once, back when the town above had been rich enough to waste space on pleasure instead of memory. Now the racks held jars instead of bottles, and the light came from lamps filled with somebody's summer afternoon — Gideon tried not to look too closely at which summer, whose afternoon. The memory-eaters of the bazaar had gathered because Ossian had told them a thief was coming with a proposition, and memory-eaters, like all connoisseurs, could not resist the possibility of something new.

There were eleven of them. They sat in a loose crescent, and they watched her the way cats watch a bird that has made the unusual decision to enter the house.

Gideon stood in the middle of the floor with nothing but a map, a jar sealed in her mother's handwriting, and a speech she had rehearsed against the side of Perrin's head all morning.

"I'll be brief," she began.

"Nobody is ever brief," said an old woman near the front, whose teeth were stained violet from decades of tasting other people's weddings. "But go on."

"The court beneath the mountain has been buying from you for generations. Souls, memories, names — whatever you couldn't hold, they offered coin or safety or silence, and you sold. And I know why." She raised a hand before anyone could bristle. "I know why because I've been a seller too. I stole grief from a widow because taking things is easier than watching people break. We are all in the business of making unbearable things portable." She let that sit. "But here's what I learned reading their contract with my own eyes: the court's ledger balances both ways. Every purchase creates a debt. Every soul they hold, they hold *on account*. And accounts can be called."

A young eater at the back — barely more than a boy, with ink-stained fingers and the nervous look of someone who'd only recently graduated from tasting to swallowing — spoke up. "Called how? You walk up to the mountain and ask politely?"

"You demand your memories back. All at once. Every seller, every debt, on the same night."

Silence. Then the violet-toothed woman laughed, a dry sound like pages turning. "Child. There are thousands of us. Thousands of contracts, written across centuries, in a language designed to be misunderstood. The court's clerks would laugh themselves into hernias."

"They won't have clerks to spare," Gideon said. "Because on the night we call, the ledgers will be drowning. Eleven eaters here. The Ferryman at the bridge, who's been collecting tolls for the court since before the bridge had rails. The archivist, who keeps copies of everything the court ever burned. And me." She touched the map at her hip. "Inside the vault, pulling the thread that unravels the whole coat."

"And if the thread doesn't pull?" the boy asked.

"Then I'll be standing inside a mountain full of angry immortals holding a jar of stolen soul, and my problems will become very immediate and very short." She shrugged. "I've had worse odds. I once stole a memory from a woman who was awake at the time."

That got a laugh — startled, unwilling, real. Perrin, leaning against a wine rack, rolled his eyes at the ceiling as if to say *she does this everywhere we go now.*

Ossian stood. He smoothed his forty-one-pocketed coat and looked around the crescent of his colleagues, and then at Gideon, and his voice was quiet in the way that makes rooms lean in.

"When I tasted her memory at my stall," he said, nodding at Gideon, "it was court-stamped. Which means somewhere in her past, the court handled her — priced her, filed her, put its seal on her life like wax on a letter. I recoiled. I regretted it. Because I realized that everything I have ever eaten, someone first had to sell out of desperation, and the court has been standing behind every one of those desperate moments, holding out its hand and calling itself a merchant." He turned to the others. "We are not merchants. We are the fence posts of a trade we didn't build. I say we pull the post."

One by one — some quickly, some after long looks at their jars, some after arguments conducted entirely in eyebrow — the eleven agreed. The violet-toothed woman was last. She looked at Gideon a long moment.

"My mother sold her wedding to them," she said. "She couldn't remember my father's face for the last forty years of her life. She used to ask me to describe him, and I'd lie, because I'd never met him either." She tapped her violet teeth with one finger. "New moon. That's when their clerks do the balancing. That's when we call."

"New moon," Gideon said, and felt the word settle into her bones like a stone dropped into a well — far down, but coming.

* * *

The Ferryman did not live at the bridge. This disappointed Gideon, who had imagined him as the kind of creature who slept standing up among the ropes like a horse. Instead he kept a small house on the riverbank with a garden of white flowers that Gideon suspected were grown from somebody's funeral, and he answered the door already holding two cups of tea, as though he'd known she was coming, which — being a Ferryman — he probably had.

"You want a rebate," he said, before she'd crossed the threshold.

"I want a withdrawal. On behalf of everyone who ever paid your toll."

He handed her a cup. It was chamomile, which felt pointed. "Sit down, thief. Let me tell you about debts."

Perrin took the second cup and perched on the windowsill, because Perrin had never once in his life sat in a chair like a person.

"I have ferried this crossing for six hundred years," the Ferryman said. "Do you know why?"

"Because you love boats?" Perrin offered.

"Because I owe." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd say *because it rains here.* "Long before your mother was born, before the mountain grew its court, I was a man with a wife and a son and a farm on the wrong side of a flood. The water took the farm. The fever took the son. And a thing wearing a clerk's face came to me in the reeds and said: *we will return your son's last day to you — every hour of it, whole — if you will keep our bridge.*" He looked into his tea. "I have spent six hundred years charging grieving people for the privilege of keeping their worst nights. And every coin goes into the ledger, and the ledger says I am paid, and the ledger lies, because there is no wage that covers it."

Gideon set down her cup. "Your son's last day. Do you still have it?"

"I keep it under the floorboards. I have never dared taste it. What if it's changed? What if remembering it wrong is all I have left of him?" His hands were steady; his voice was not, quite. "When you call the debt, thief — call mine first. Call it loud. I want the clerks to hear my name in their books and know the bridge is closed."

"It's done," Gideon said. And then, because the widow Sefu had taught her that kindness costs nothing and unsettles everyone: "You could taste it after. When the ledger's balanced. You might find it's exactly as you remember. Mothers usually are."

The Ferryman looked at her for a long time, and something in his ancient face moved like ice going out on a river. "Your mother," he said, "would be proud of you. Or furious. With mothers it's often the same thing."

* * *

The archivist received them in the burned library, because the archivist received everyone in the burned library; it was, as far as anyone knew, where the archivist lived, slept, ate, and conducted a long-term argument with the concept of fire.

"Gideon Okafor," the blind old man said, before she'd spoken. His milky eyes found her anyway, the way they always did, aimed slightly left of true. "And the magpie. And the smell of a plan, which smells like every plan I ever catalogued: ink, nerves, and unwashed optimism."

"We need the copies," Gideon said. "Every contract the court ever wrote. Every sale, every signature, every soul on deposit. On the new moon, we call them all home at once."

The archivist was quiet for so long that Perrin shifted his weight and Gideon began composing retreat strategies. Then the old man laughed — a rusted hinge of a laugh, delighted.

"Do you know what I have been doing for fifty years?" He spread his scorched hands over the shelves of ash-gray volumes. "Copying. Everything the court burned, I copied from memory before the ashes cooled. Every contract. Every clause. I have ten thousand pages of their fine print and no one to read it to and no way to use it, because I am blind and old and the court is eternal." His grin showed teeth gone brown as old paper. "And now a thief walks in and asks to borrow the entire library for a burglary. Child, I have been waiting for you since before you were born. Possibly literally. Some of these prophecies are very vague."

"There are conditions," Gideon started.

"There are always conditions. Mine are simple. One: when the vault opens, I get my eyes back — they took them as surety on a book I borrowed in my youth, and I would like to see fire do its proper work one more time, at a comfortable distance. Two:" he leveled a finger at Perrin, "the bird stops nesting in my shelving."

"That was one time," Perrin said.

"It was four times."

"Three times and one misunderstanding."

Gideon left them arguing and walked the aisles alone, trailing her fingers along spines that held the shapes of everything the court had ever taken. Ten thousand pages. Ten thousand names. Somewhere in the ash and ink was her own contract, and Adaeze's, and her mother's trembling signature, and the seal the memory-eater had tasted in her past.

Once, this would have frightened her — the sheer size of what she was trying to move. But walking those aisles, she felt something else instead. Not a wall anymore. A web. A ferryman with a floorboard secret. Eleven eaters with violet teeth and old grievances. An archivist who'd been building a weapon out of paperwork for half a century. A magpie who complained constantly and had never once actually left. A widow's bread. A sister's dream-messages, still arriving faint as breath on glass.

She had spent her whole life believing she worked alone because alone was what she was. It turned out alone was just what she'd been practicing.

* * *

They gathered one last time in the under-hall, three nights before the new moon — the eaters, Perrin, Gideon, and the archivist, who had insisted on coming and been carried down the stairs on a chair by two of the younger eaters, directing operations the entire way like a general at a siege.

Ossian unrolled the master list across a table: names, contracts, dates, debts. It covered the whole table and spilled onto a second table brought for the purpose.

"The call goes out at moonset," Gideon said. "Every seller, every debt, spoken at once. The Ferryman closes the bridge. The archivist's copies prove every claim. And while the clerks drown in paper—" she tapped the map, "—I'm in the vault."

"And if the court refuses?" the boy asked. "Contracts bind both ways. They said so. They'll say it to you too."

Gideon smiled, and it was the smile Perrin had learned to distrust and then to trust and then to simply enjoy, the way you enjoy weather.

"Contracts bind both ways," she agreed. "That's not their shield. That's my crowbar."

The archivist raised his teacup — someone had remembered to bring him tea, which had earned them all a five-minute lecture on gratitude and shelf maintenance. "To the conspiracy of the sold."

"To the sold," the room echoed.

"To arithmetic," Perrin added solemnly, and even the violet-toothed woman laughed.

Later, walking back through the sleeping town with the list folded against her heart, Gideon felt the sealed jar knock gently against her hip — her mother's handwriting, her sister's name, twelve moons nearly spent. One moon left. One call to make.

Above the rooftops, the thirteenth moon was rising thin and hungry, and Gideon Okafor — thief, twin, author of herself — smiled at it like an old colleague about to be robbed.
