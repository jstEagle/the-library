## 31. The Great Withdrawal

The new moon came on a Tuesday, which Perrin found deeply unsatisfying.

"Should be dramatic," he muttered, perched on a ridge above the mountain with his feathers ruffled against the cold. "End of the month. Court's ledgers at their emptiest. The whole sky going dark like a held breath. And instead it's *Tuesday*."

Below him, in the shadow of the bone door, the conspirators were gathering. He counted them again, though counting changed nothing: the Ferryman standing at the edge of his own bridge like a man about to burn his boat, his pole planted in the black water. The blind archivist, Ash, wrapped in three coats and her own terrible calm, feeling her way along the road by touch. Forty-odd memory-eaters from the market, pale as grubs, blinking in the open air — most of them hadn't stood under a real sky in years. Even a handful of villagers from the bottled-light towns, people who had sold their weddings and their children's first steps and come anyway, holding each other's elbows for courage.

And Gideon, small and steady at the front of them all, looking up at the mountain that had eaten her sister.

Perrin's chest did something complicated. He'd known Gideon for nine moons now, and he still wasn't used to the fact that she kept *staying*. Everyone he loved left or died or turned into a map. But there she was, checking the knots on strangers' packs, saying something to the Ferryman that made the old man's mouth twitch.

"You're stalling," said a voice behind him.

He didn't startle. Magpies never startle; it's undignified. He turned instead and found Adaeze climbing the last of the ridge, wrapped in Gideon's spare cloak, her breath silvering the air. Nine moons of dream-messages and two weeks of actual daylight, and she still moved like someone who expected walls to be further apart than they were.

"I'm supervising," Perrin said. "It's different. It has more dignity."

"Mm." She settled beside him on the cold rock, close enough that their shoulders touched. Below, the crowd had gone quiet. "My sister is down there about to do something insane."

"Your sister does something insane approximately every four days. I've timed it."

"And yet you're here."

"And yet I'm here," Perrin agreed gloomily. "It's the favor. It started as the favor and then somewhere around the salt flats it stopped being the favor and became—" he gestured vaguely at everything, "—*this*. Loyalty. Disgusting word. Sounds like a disease of cattle."

Adaeze laughed — a small, startled sound, like she was still learning what her laugh sounded like outside the dark. Then her face went serious, and she looked down at the gathering with an expression Perrin recognized from mirrors: the look of someone watching a person they cannot afford to lose.

"The plan," she said. "Tell me the part where nobody dies."

"That's the part I keep skipping," Perrin said. "It's very short."

* * *

Gideon stood before the assembled conspiracy of the sold and discovered that she had absolutely no idea how to give a speech.

She'd rehearsed something on the walk over. Something about ledgers, and balance, and the arithmetic of souls. But forty memory-eaters were staring at her with hungry, hopeful faces, and the Ferryman was leaning on his pole with the patience of a man who had heard every speech ever made at his bridge, and the words she'd rehearsed evaporated like breath on glass.

So she told them the truth instead.

"The court thinks you're inventory," she said. Her voice carried in the cold air better than she expected. "That's all any of us ever were to them. Lines in a ledger. Jars on a shelf. They bought your summers and your griefs and your mothers' voices, and they wrote it all down neat and tidy because writing it down makes it *theirs*." She held up the map — her mother's map, wax seal broken, ink crawling restlessly across its surface even now. "But here's what a thief knows that a court doesn't. A ledger is just a list. And a list can be *overwhelmed*."

A murmur went through the crowd. The Ferryman's eyes glinted.

"On the new moon, their accounts are thinnest. The court spends memories like coin, and tonight the vault runs low. So every one of you — every soul they've ever bought from, every contract they've ever signed — walks up to that door tonight and demands your property back." She let that land. "All at once. All together. Not politely. Not one at a time, the way they're used to, the way they've *trained* you to come — alone, ashamed, grateful for scraps. Together. A thousand claims in a single hour. Let their clerks drown in paper. Let their ledger smoke."

An old woman near the front — one of the bottled-light villagers, a grandmother who had sold the memory of her late husband's face so her grandchildren could eat that winter — raised a trembling hand.

"And while the clerks are drowning?" she asked.

Gideon smiled, and it was not a nice smile. It was, several observers would later agree, a magnificent one.

"While the clerks are drowning," she said, "a thief goes shopping."

* * *

Inside the mountain, the Under-Court felt the new moon arrive the way a miser feels winter: as a tightening.

The masked figures sat in their gallery above the great ledger hall, where ten thousand years of purchases lay recorded in books the size of doors. The clerks — thin, gray, diligent creatures made mostly of ink and anxiety — moved among the shelves with their pens, performing the new-moon reconciliation, when every account was weighed and every debt confirmed.

It was, by tradition, the quietest night of the month.

The first claim arrived at the bone door at moonset exactly.

It was the Ferryman, which surprised no one who knew him, and it was delivered in a voice like a river deciding to flood.

"I demand," he announced to the astonished gate-clerk, "the return of all payments rendered at my bridge since the year of the red frost. Itemized. In full. With interest."

The clerk's pen stopped. "There are... eleven hundred entries—"

"Then I suggest," said the Ferryman pleasantly, "you begin."

Before the clerk could produce a second syllable, the archivist Ash arrived, sightless eyes aimed at the ceiling, and laid a burned hand flat upon the door.

"I demand the return of the library," she said. "Every volume. Every ash. Every memory the court purchased from the burning and then failed to protect. You bought our pasts out of the fire, gentlemen. Tonight I've come to collect the arson."

And then the market-eaters came, in a river, a flood, a *congregation* — each one pressing forward with a claim, a contract, a name, a date, a grievance honed through months of secret meetings in back rooms that smelled of tallow and stolen childhoods. The grandmother demanded her husband's face. A fisherman demanded twenty years of dawns. Twins who had sold their shared birthday demanded it back *jointly*, on the grounds that neither could remember it without the other, and the resulting jurisdictional dispute would occupy three clerks until dawn.

The ledger hall filled with noise. The great books began, for the first time in living memory, to fall *behind*.

In the gallery, the Under-Court rose from their seats, masks tilting, and for the first time in ten thousand years the masked faces turned toward one another in something adjacent to confusion.

"This is coordinated," said one.

"This is impossible," said another. "They have no standing. Contracts bind—"

"Contracts bind both ways," murmured the eldest mask, whose voice was dry as old vellum. "That was always the flaw. Fetch the senior reconcilers. Fetch *all* of them."

Which meant, though none of the court realized it yet, that the vault below now stood guarded by exactly no one of consequence.

* * *

Gideon came down the bone stair two steps at a time, Perrin at her shoulder in his man-shape with his coat flapping, both of them moving through galleries of bottled light that pulsed like the insides of some enormous sleeping animal.

Above them, muffled by a hundred tons of mountain, the noise of the withdrawal rolled down like distant surf. Shouting. Pens scratching. The Ferryman's voice rising periodically over it all like a lighthouse, itemizing.

"He's *enjoying* this," Perrin panted.

"He's waited sixty years to say 'with interest' to somebody official." Gideon didn't slow. "Keep count of the turnings. We want the third gallery."

"Why the third?"

"Because the vault proper is on the fourth, and the fourth will be warded, and the third is where they stage the *disputed* items." She glanced at him, and even in the jar-light her grin was sharp. "Everything we need is disputed now. Ash made sure of it. Every claim filed tonight flags its holdings for review. The whole vault is legally *in motion*."

Perrin stared at her. "You're telling me you're robbing them with *paperwork*."

"I'm telling you the best locks are made of rules," Gideon said. "And rules, magpie, are just stories everyone agreed to stop questioning."

The third gallery opened around them — shelves and shelves of jars in transit, each tagged with gray dispute-slips fluttering like moths. Somewhere a single junior clerk sat at a desk buried to the ears in flags, weeping quietly and stamping things at random.

Gideon walked straight past him. He was far too busy to notice a thief, a magpie, and a ghost of a girl moving through his chaos like fish through reeds.

"There." Adaeze's voice, barely a breath.

At the gallery's heart, on a shelf set slightly apart — reserved, honored, *displayed* — sat two jars side by side. One held a slow golden swirl that turned gently on itself, patient as a held note. The other was darker, quieter, with a hairline crack running through its seal that no clerk had ever thought to mend.

Gideon reached out and touched the cracked one first. Her own copy. The self the court had kept as surety all these years, the girl she might have been if the famine had gone the other way.

"It's warm," she whispered.

"They kept it fed," Adaeze said. "Souls don't like being shelved. Yours has been pushing at the lid for thirteen years." A pause. "Like someone I used to know."

Above them, the surf-noise of the withdrawal swelled — a roar now, hundreds of voices, the Ferryman bellowing something about *fraudulent conveyance*, the great books of the court audibly beginning to smoke.

And then, beneath it all, a sound that froze Gideon's blood where she stood.

A bell. One clean, cold stroke, rolling down through the mountain's bones.

In the gallery above, the eldest mask had risen from its seat, and its voice carried through stone itself:

"*Find the thief.*"

* * *

Perrin felt it before he understood it — the particular prickle at the nape that meant hunters. Hollow-eyed riders had hunted them across three moons once; he knew their smell, which was the smell of a room after a quarrel.

"They've pulled the guards off the withdrawal," he hissed. "All of them. Coming down the stair."

"How many?"

"All of them, Gideon. *All of them.*"

She looked at the two jars on the shelf. Her sister's soul, gold and patient. Her own name, cracked and waiting. And behind them, bootsteps already ringing off the bone stair, dozens strong, closing fast.

There was no time to carry both. There was barely time to carry one.

Perrin saw her face do the thing it did — the thief's arithmetic, the terrible fast math of what can be saved — and he stepped forward and put his body between her and the stair, spreading his arms, feathers bursting up the back of his neck.

"Gideon," he said. "Take the gold one. *Now.*"

"But my name—"

"Is a story," he snapped, "and you have *always* been good at writing those. GO."

She grabbed Adaeze's jar. It was lighter than she expected — souls usually are; it's the guilt that's heavy — and then the first hunter rounded the stair, hollow-eyed, hand outstretched, and Gideon did the only thing left in her thief's arsenal.

She took the memory of the hunt itself — the orders, the bell, the eldest mask's cold voice — ripped it whole from the nearest hunter's mind, and threw it back at him doubled, tripled, a storm of *find the thief, find the thief, find the thief* with no thief in it anywhere, only the command, echoing, directionless, devouring its own tail.

The hunter stopped. Blinked. Turned to the hunter behind him.

"*You're* the thief," he said, with perfect conviction.

What followed was, Perrin would maintain for the rest of his life, the finest brawl he had never started. Hunters fell upon hunters in the narrow gallery, each one certain the thief stood opposite, while Gideon ran — jar clutched to her chest, gold light leaking between her fingers like sunrise through a shut door — toward the fourth gallery, the vault proper, the ward, and the one thing she had come all this way to leave behind:

her own name, still sitting on its shelf, waiting to be spent.

Behind her, Perrin wheeled overhead in a whirlwind of black and white, screaming directions that helped no one, and ahead the ward shimmered like heat over a road.

Gideon put out her hand — and stopped.

Because the ward wasn't keeping her out.

It was keeping something *in*. And through the shimmer, in the vault's deep dark, something with her mother's handwriting on it had begun, very softly, to sing.
