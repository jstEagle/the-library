## 32. The Vault of Purchased Selves

The Great Withdrawal had gone off like a kettle left too long on the fire.

Gideon felt it even here, three galleries down and still descending — a shudder through the mountain's bones as ten thousand ledgers screamed at once. Somewhere above her, the Ferryman was demanding back every summer he'd ever ferried. The archivist, blind and serene, had walked into the counting hall and asked them to read *her* a list for once. The memory-eaters had simply sat down in the marketplace of the court and begun to eat.

And Perrin — Perrin was on the surface doing what Perrin did best, which was being loudly, magnificently distracting. She'd left him at the gold door with instructions to complain about his accommodations until someone important came to shut him up.

"Complaining is an art form," he'd said, drawing himself up to his full unimpressive height. "I've been studying under masters my whole life."

"I know," she'd said. "I'm one of them."

Now it was just her and the dark and the stair, and the sound of her own breathing, which seemed very loud in a place built entirely of other people's held breath.

* * *

The bone stair ended without ceremony. One moment she was descending; the next, the walls simply opened, and Gideon stood at the edge of the Vault of Purchased Selves.

She had imagined — when she'd let herself imagine it at all — something like the valley's memory-jars writ large. Rows of shelving, perhaps. A cellar. Something with a door and a lock and a reasonable amount of dust.

It was a cathedral.

Ten thousand jars, at least, arranged in concentric rings that spiraled down into a floor she couldn't see the bottom of. Each jar glowed faintly from within, and each glow was a different color, because each soul remembered different things: amber for harvests, blue-green for rivers, a deep bruised violet that she suspected meant grief, and one distant ring that burned pure white, so bright it hurt to look at, and she did not go near it and did not ask herself why.

The light didn't come from lamps. It came from the jars themselves, souls giving off the only light they were permitted, illuminating the vault that held them. The whole vast space was lit by its prisoners.

"Well," Gideon said quietly, to no one, because saying something out loud was how she kept from screaming. "That's obscene."

Her voice fell flat and dead in all that glowing silence. No echo. The vault swallowed echoes the way it swallowed everything else.

She started walking.

The map — her mother's map, the stranger's dying gift, the thing that had dragged her across half the world — had gone quiet since she entered the mountain. But she found she didn't need it. Her feet knew. That was the horror of it, or maybe the mercy: some part of her had always known the way down here, the way a body knows the road to a house it lived in before it could walk.

Ring by ring, she descended. She passed jars labeled in elegant court-script, and she made herself read the names, because someone should. *Oluwaseun. Maren. Ijeoma-of-the-third-famine. A boy called Tam with a chip in his jar like a chip in a tooth.* Ten thousand names, and not one of them had been sold willingly, whatever the contracts said. Contracts were written by the people who benefited from them; she'd known that since she was small enough to steal bread and call it redistribution.

*Ijeoma-of-the-third-famine.* Gideon stopped. Touched the glass. Inside, something turned toward her hand like a plant toward sun, and she snatched her fingers back.

"Not yet," she whispered. "Hold on. I'm coming back for all of you if I can."

It was a stupid promise. She was one thief with a stolen knife and a plan held together with spite and moonlight. But the soul in the jar pressed against the glass anyway, hopeful as a dog, and Gideon had to walk away fast before she did something reckless and useless, like try to carry ten thousand jars up a stair designed for one.

*Adaeze first,* she told herself, in her mother's voice, which she was allowed to use now. *One debt at a time.*

* * *

She found the innermost ring near dawn, if dawn meant anything this far underground. The jars here were older. The script on their labels was older too, less elegant, more hurried — labels written by clerks who hadn't yet learned to make cruelty beautiful.

And there, on a shelf at knee height, between a jar labeled *Bargain, famine year, twin set, one retained* and another whose label had been scratched out so thoroughly the glass was gouged —

There was Adaeze's name.

Gideon knelt. Her knees hit stone and she barely felt it.

The jar was smaller than she'd expected. Souls, apparently, condensed rather than expanded — everything a person was, folded down tight as a seed. Inside, a soft gold light moved the way light moves on water, restless, patient, waiting. And though there was no face in it, nothing she could point to and say *there, that's my sister*, Gideon knew. The way you know your own reflection in a dark window. The way you know a lullaby from three notes.

"Hello," she said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle. "I'm — I'm late. Thirteen turns late, actually, which I understand is poor form."

The light pressed against the glass.

"Yeah," Gideon said. "Me too."

She got her fingers under the jar's wax seal — the same kind of seal that had been on the map, she realized, the same red, the same sigil, her mother had bought this wax from the same stationer or stolen it from the same clerk, and wasn't that just like her mother, thorough to the last —

And stopped.

Because beside Adaeze's jar, close enough that their glows bled together at the edges, stood a second jar.

Same size. Same age. Same hurried clerk-script.

And the label read: **OKAFOR, GIDEON.**

* * *

For a long moment she did nothing at all. She knelt on the cold stone of the innermost ring of the Vault of Purchased Selves and looked at her own name in someone else's handwriting, and thought, with a strange floating calm, *ah. There it is.*

Then the calm cracked, because calm always does, and everything underneath came up at once.

"They kept a copy." Her voice came out wrong, thin. "They kept a — the whole time? The whole *time*?"

The jar didn't answer. Jars never do. But inside it, the light moved differently than Adaeze's — sharper, quicker, flickering instead of flowing, and Gideon recognized the rhythm before she could stop herself from recognizing it. She knew that flicker. She saw it every time she caught her reflection mid-thought. It was the particular staccato way her own attention jumped from thing to thing, lock to lock, exit to exit.

The court hadn't just recorded her. They'd kept her. Some clerk, thirteen years ago, had bottled a copy of a six-year-old girl along with the sister she'd been traded for, filed her on a shelf, and forgotten her between a scratched-out label and a famine bargain.

A spare. An overage. A clerical error with a heartbeat.

"No wonder you never fit anywhere," Gideon told herself, out loud, because the vault was listening and she wanted it to hear. "No wonder the valley took you in and magic wouldn't follow. You were already somebody's inventory."

She put her hand flat on the glass. The light inside flinched — actually flinched, drew back from her palm the way a stray dog draws back from a raised hand — and Gideon's stomach dropped through the floor, through the rings below, through the mountain itself.

It was afraid of her.

Or no. Not of her. Of the hand. Of hands, reaching toward glass, taking things away. It had been on this shelf for thirteen years watching hands come and take, and it had learned what hands meant.

"Oh," Gideon said softly. "Oh, no. Sweetheart. I'm not —"

She stopped. Started again, slower, the way Widow Sefu talked to frightened animals, the way Sefu had once talked to *her*, bread in one hand and no demands in the other.

"I'm not here to take you somewhere worse," she said. "I'm here to take you somewhere better. There's a difference. It's a small difference, I'll grant you, from in there. But it's the whole world from out here."

The light crept back toward her palm, one cautious degree at a time.

And Gideon sat down properly on the floor, cross-legged, thief-style, ready to wait as long as it took, and let herself think about what she was actually looking at.

Two jars. Two girls. One contract. The court had Adaeze's soul — the payment, the surety, the thing the ledger demanded. And they had *her* — not her soul exactly, but the shape of her, the record, the receipt. A copy made on the day of the sale, when two six-year-olds stood on the mountain road and one went up and one went down.

Which meant the court didn't just hold her sister. They held proof of everything Gideon had spent thirteen years building herself out of. Every stolen memory in her hoard, every skill, every scar, every cleverness — all of it assembled by a girl running from a shelf in a vault, and here was the shelf, and here was the girl-shaped hole she'd been fleeing since before she could remember fleeing.

"The final choice arrives early," she murmured. "That's typical. Nothing in my life has ever arrived on schedule."

Because she could see it now, laid out plain as a pickpocket's mark. To break the contract fully — truly, legally, in the way this awful place understood — the ledger had to balance. A soul returned required a soul surrendered, or something a soul weighed. And sitting right here, conveniently pre-bottled, pre-weighed, pre-labeled in her own name, was exactly what the court would accept.

They'd kept her copy for thirteen years like a coat checked at a door.

All she had to do was claim it.

* * *

She didn't touch it. Not yet. Instead she did what she did best, what she'd been doing since chapter one of her own life, whenever anyone bothered to write one: she looked at the lock.

The seal on her jar was the same wax as Adaeze's. Same sigil. Same clerk's thumbprint pressed into the cooling wax — she could see it, a whorl and a nick, the nick shaped like a crescent moon. Thirteen moons. Even the thumbprint was mocking her.

But the binding thread was different. Adaeze's jar was bound with black thread, court-standard, the kind that dissolved only to the hand that held the original contract. Hers was bound with red.

Red thread. She knew red thread. Half the black-market jars in the outer towns were sealed with red thread, because red thread meant *provisional*. Red thread meant *pending final settlement*. Red thread meant the paperwork was incomplete.

Someone down here had never finished filing her.

Gideon laughed — a short, disbelieving bark that echoed nowhere and died immediately, swallowed by ten thousand listening lights.

"Thirteen years of terror," she said to the jar, to the vault, to the whole rotten mountain, "and it comes down to a clerk who couldn't be bothered to finish the paperwork. You beautiful, bureaucratic disaster."

The light inside her jar flickered — and she would have sworn, then and ever after, that it flickered *wryly*. In agreement. In the exact cadence of someone who has just heard a joke they're not quite free enough to laugh at yet.

She reached out and touched the red thread, just one fingertip, feather-light.

The thread hummed.

Not dangerously — not yet. But it hummed the way a plucked wire hums, and far above her, through galleries and halls and the counting chamber where ledgers were screaming, Gideon felt something turn its attention downward. The mountain noticed. The court noticed. Somewhere, a masked face lifted toward the innermost ring like a hound catching scent.

*Of course,* she thought. *Of course touching it announces me. Nothing in this place is free, including curiosity.*

She had perhaps minutes. Maybe less. Above her, the Great Withdrawal was tearing the court's attention in ten thousand directions, but she had just tugged on the one thread connected to the one jar the court had been keeping warm for thirteen years, and whatever mask-wearing thing administered this place was going to come down those stairs at a speed undignified for its age.

Gideon looked at Adaeze's jar. Black thread. Unbreakable without the contract.

She looked at her own. Red thread. Breakable, probably, by anyone — but breaking it would mean claiming what was inside, and claiming what was inside might mean becoming it, or erasing it, or signing something she couldn't unsign. The archivist's voice came back to her across the burned miles: *Names were never sold, child. Names are the one thing they cannot take. Which means they are also the one thing you can give.*

Give a name, balance a ledger. Free a sister, empty a self.

The choice she'd thought she'd have weeks to prepare for was kneeling in front of her, glowing, wearing her own handwriting on its label.

Footsteps on the bone stair. Slow. Heavy. Unhurried in the way of things that have never once needed to hurry.

Gideon picked up both jars — her sister in her left hand, herself in her right — and stood.

"All right," she said to the darkness, to the footsteps, to the thirteen years and the thirteen moons and the clerk with the nick in his thumbnail. "Let's talk terms."

The light in her left hand pressed gratefully against the glass.

The light in her right hand flickered — quick, sharp, hers — and waited to see what she would choose.
