## 33. Twelfth Moon, What a Name Is Worth

The ledger room of the Under-Court smelled of cold iron and old paper, and Gideon thought, absurdly, that it reminded her of Widow Sefu's pantry — if the widow had kept her bread in jars and her jars in the dark for a thousand years.

She stood before the great book. It lay open on a lectern of black stone, its pages wide as doors, and the ink on them moved when she wasn't looking, rearranging itself the way her stolen map used to. Beside the lectern stood a masked clerk whose face was smooth porcelain except for a small mouth-hole through which came a voice like a quill scratching.

"The contract," the clerk said, "binds both ways. You have read this."

"I've read it." She had read it four times now, with a thief's eye and then with a sister's, and then with both at once, which was the hardest way to read anything. "And I've found your flaw."

A murmur went around the gallery of masks above. The Under-Court did not like the word *flaw*. It was, Gideon suspected, the only word in their language that could make them flinch.

"The court holds Adaeze's soul," she said. "Ten thousand souls besides hers. But names—" she tapped the page, and the ink scurried away from her finger like ants from rain— "names were never sold. My mother sold a soul. The court took a soul. But Adaeze's name stayed with Adaeze, and mine stayed with me, and there is nothing in your fine print that says otherwise."

The clerk's porcelain face did not change, but the scratching sound it made went quiet.

"Correct," it said at last, and the gallery murmured again, louder, angrier. "The names were retained by the sellers. This is why the contract cannot be fully broken while any signatory lives. A soul may be returned. A name, once surrendered to balance the ledger, is gone."

"Gone where?"

"Gone." The clerk said it the way you'd say *eaten*.

* * *

They gave her a night to decide, which she suspected was not mercy but procedure, and which she spent in a cell that was really a guest room, because the court was very insistent on the difference. Perrin sat on the windowsill with his feathers ruffled up, which was what he did instead of pacing.

"So let me say the stupid thing out loud so we can both stop thinking it," he said. "Don't do it."

"Perrin—"

"No, hear the whole stupid thing. There's a version of tomorrow where you walk out of here with your name, your memories, your hoard, your whole thieving self intact, and Adaeze walks out too because I have a plan involving fire, and the plan is fire."

"It's a bad plan."

"It's a *short* plan. That's different." He hopped down and landed on the table, close enough that she could see the oil-sheen of worry in his black eyes even in his man-shape. "Gideon. Gideon Okafor. Say it. Say your name."

"Why?"

"Because I want to hear it while it's still yours."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "Gideon Okafor," and heard how it sounded — like a key turning in a lock she'd built herself, board by board, over twenty years in a valley that forgot things so she wouldn't have to.

"That name is made of stolen goods," Perrin said softly. "You know that. Every piece of it. And you're the best thief I ever met, so of course you built yourself out of the best stuff. But—" he picked at the table grain with one claw— "my sister stole that map from the court because she believed people shouldn't be *kept*. Not souls. Not names. Not grief in jars. And I resented her for dying about it, and I resented you for carrying it, and somewhere on the magpie road I stopped resenting either of you, and I'd very much like to go back to being annoyed instead of sad, so: don't do it. Find another way."

"There isn't another way."

"There's always another way. It's usually worse and it's usually fire."

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and Perrin looked so pleased with himself that she almost forgave him for the fire plan.

"Perrin," she said. "When I was six years old, my mother held my hand and told me a story about two sisters who shared everything, even their dreams. And I thought it was just a story. I built a whole person on top of that story without knowing it was true. Do you understand? Everything I am — the thief, the valley, the trade, the loneliness I got so good at — all of it was built on a foundation I didn't pour. Someone else chose it for me. My mother chose. The court chose. Even the famine chose." She turned her hands over, palms up, empty. "This is the first choice anyone in my family has ever gotten to make freely. My mother paid a thief to steal her memory. Adaeze was sold before she could speak for herself. But me — I'm standing here, awake, knowing exactly what it costs. Nobody chose this for me. For once, somebody gets to choose."

Perrin was quiet for a while. Magpies are not good at quiet; it cost him visibly.

"And what do I call you afterward?" he said finally. "I'm not clever enough to improvise. I need time to workshop something. I was thinking maybe 'Trouble.' Or 'That Woman Who Owes Me Nothing, Which Is New.'"

"You'll think of something."

"I'll think of something," he agreed, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he pretended it hadn't, and she pretended not to notice, because that was friendship too.

* * *

At moonrise they brought her back to the ledger room, and the twelfth moon hung in the high shaft of the mountain's throat like a coin someone had forgotten to spend.

Adaeze was there. They had let the sisters meet that afternoon — an hour, no more — and it had been the strangest hour of Gideon's life: sitting across from her own face, her own hands, her own way of tucking hair behind the ear, talking to a girl who knew a lullaby but not a childhood, who remembered a kitchen and a mother's tears but not which sister stayed. Adaeze had taken her hand across the table and said, "You don't have to. Whatever it is. I've been in a jar for twenty years; I can wait twenty more."

Which was, of course, a lie, and both of them knew it. The fading showed at her edges now, like a photograph left in sun.

Now Adaeze stood beside the lectern, and the masked court filled the gallery above, and the clerk unrolled a fresh page with ceremony.

"The surrender must be spoken," the clerk said. "And written. And witnessed. In that order."

"Convenient," Gideon said. "I'm good at all three."

She had asked for one thing before the signing, and the court, eager to be rid of the whole affair, had granted it: a pen, and paper, and a private hour. Because if she was going to write herself blank, she wanted to do it properly — the way an author does, not the way a debtor does.

So now, while the court waited and Perrin stood rigid as a fence post and Adaeze watched with her twin's eyes, Gideon dipped the pen and wrote.

Not the surrender. Not yet. First, a list.

*Things worth keeping,* she wrote, though she would keep none of them in the legal sense. *The smell of Sefu's bread on baking day. The weight of a memory-jar in both hands, warm from the shelf. The sound Perrin makes when he's pretending not to be scared, which is humming. The color of the valley at dusk, which is the exact purple of a bruise healing. My mother's voice on the salt flats, singing. Two pairs of small shoes by a door.*

Her hand shook on the last line. She kept writing anyway.

*I will not remember these. Someone else will have to. That is the price and I pay it with my eyes open, which is more than anyone in this room has ever done.*

Then, below the list, in her careful thief's script, the words the ledger required:

*I, Gideon Okafor, surrender my name.*

She read it aloud. Her voice did not shake, which surprised her; she had expected it to. The ink lifted off the page as she spoke, letter by letter, and rose into the air like a flock of small dark birds, and flew up into the gallery, and the court drank it.

It did not hurt. That was the terrible part. She had braced for pain, for tearing, for the vertigo of the rim-crossing a hundred times worse. Instead it felt like setting down a jar she had carried so long she'd forgotten its weight — and then feeling the lightness, and grieving the lightness, because the lightness meant the thing was gone.

"Gideon Okafor," said the clerk, tasting the name the way the memory-eaters tasted wine. "Thief of the valley. Sister who stayed. Author of herself." A pause. "The ledger balances."

The great book closed with a sound like a mountain swallowing.

And inside her, quietly, a door shut. She felt it go — the name, the identity, the twenty-year architecture of self — folding up into a space that was suddenly, enormously empty. Not blank the way paper is blank. Blank the way a cleared field is blank: full of possibility and utterly without shelter.

She reached for *her mother's face* and found a shape with warmth in it. She reached for *the valley* and found a place she had lived. She reached for *thief* and found a word, and nothing behind it.

"Well," she said, to the room, to the court, to whoever she was now. "That's that."

The gallery of masks was silent. Somewhere in it, she understood, contracts were unraveling — ten thousand threads going slack at once, the Great Withdrawal still echoing through the ledgers, every sold soul suddenly unowned. The court was not angry. The court was *bookkeeping*, and the books had come out even, and there was nothing in the rules about what happened next because no one had ever been stupid enough, or brave enough, or free enough to try it.

Adaeze crossed the floor and took her hand.

"What do I call you?" her sister whispered.

The woman who had been Gideon Okafor opened her mouth — and found, waiting there, patient as a seed, the answer she had written in her private hour, the one line she had added beneath the surrender, the one thing the ink-birds had refused to carry away because it was never part of the name at all:

"Vesper," she said. "For now. Until I grow into it."

Above them, the twelfth moon touched the edge of the mountain's throat and began, slowly, to turn.

One turn left.
