## 34. The Thirteenth Turn

### Gideon

The vault shook like a jar of bees.

Gideon had heard the Great Withdrawal begin as a sound she felt in her teeth first — a low, grinding hum, the sound of ten thousand ledgers being opened at once, of every memory-eater in the market, every sold soul, every cheated ghost demanding their property back from the Under-Court simultaneously. Above them, through miles of bone and stone, the court's clerks would be screaming. She hoped they were screaming politely. It seemed like the sort of place that would insist on it.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Adaeze asked.

They stood before the shelf where two jars waited side by side. One held a soul that glowed like a lamp behind frosted glass — her sister, or what remained of her sister after twelve moons of the court feeding slowly, delicately, the way a gourmand eats an oyster. The other jar was smaller. It bore Gideon's name in a clerk's careful hand, and inside it swam something that looked, when she let herself look, terribly like a girl of about nine, standing very still in a kitchen, waiting to be chosen.

"Not exactly," Gideon said. "But nothing tonight has gone exactly, so I'm choosing to feel encouraged."

Perrin appeared at the end of the aisle at a dead sprint, feathers exploding off him in all directions, which was what happened when he ran instead of flew and got nervous about it. "The ledgers are burning," he reported breathlessly. "Actual fire. There is a clerk chasing a memory-eater down the third gallery with a quill, and the quill is *enchanted*, and honestly I think the eater is winning."

"The contract," Gideon said. "The flaw."

"I know, I know." Perrin pressed his back against the shelf beside them, catching his breath. "Names were never sold. You say it one more time, though. I like hearing it."

"The court holds Adaeze's soul," Gideon said, "but not her name. And a soul without its name isn't property. It's just grief in a jar."

"And yours?"

She looked at the small jar with her name on it. Twelve moons ago — no, longer, years, a whole life — she would have said the thing inside it was everything she was. The stolen hoard, the sealed jar in her mother's hand, the summer by the river, the lullaby, the twin. She had built Gideon Okafor out of other people's memories because her own had been taken, and then she had walked into this mountain to give all of it back.

"That's the payment," she said. "A name for a name. The ledger balances only if I surrender mine."

"You could lie," Perrin said. He said it gently, which was unlike him. "You're a thief. Lying to a ledger is practically your native language."

"It would know." She touched the jar. Cold glass, warm light. "Contracts like this can smell a lie. My mother taught me that without meaning to — you can't steal your own grief out of yourself. You have to hand it over."

Somewhere far above, something enormous fell over. The whole vault listed slightly, like a ship taking water, and a thousand jars rattled on their shelves in a sound like applause.

Adaeze put her hand over Gideon's on the glass.

"Sister," she said. The word still fit strangely in her mouth; they had been strangers sharing a lullaby only days ago. "When you open mine — whatever happens — don't watch the door. Watch me. I want the first thing I see to be family, not architecture."

Gideon laughed, and it came out wet. "You've been in a jar for thirteen years and you're already giving me stage directions."

"I've had time to think about it."

* * *

### Adaeze

Here is what Adaeze remembered of freedom: a kitchen. Two pairs of shoes by the door, one pair always muddier than the other. A song their mother sang with a mistake in it, sung the same wrong way every night, until the mistake became part of the song.

Here is what she remembered of the bargain: rain. A man with a ledger. Her mother crying in a way that made no sound at all, which Adaeze had understood even at nine was worse than the loud kind.

And here is what she knew now, as the thirteenth moon came around the mountain like a slow white eye: that somewhere above her, in the world of weather and bread and mud, there had been another girl all along. Not the one who was sold. The one who stayed. And the one who stayed had spent thirteen years not knowing either, carrying a hole shaped exactly like Adaeze and calling it loneliness.

*She started leaving messages in dreams,* the fading part of her had thought, months ago, when hope was a thin gruel. *It's not much. But it's fighting.*

The messages had worked. That was the miracle she kept turning over, the way you turn a stone found on a path. Somewhere up there, a thief had opened a jar labeled in their mother's hand and wept, and then come down a bone stair into the dark for her.

Now the dark was coming apart.

She felt it first as hunger stopping. For thirteen moons the court had fed on her in sips — her brightness, her quickness, her knack for finding the joke in a bad hour — and she had learned to live on what remained, the way a plant lives on a crack of light. Now the sips stopped. The great ledger overhead was burning, and everything the court had bought was being called home, and Adaeze felt herself filling up like a well in spring.

"Gideon," she said, because saying the name was itself a kind of eating, a feast. "Gideon, I can feel my own edges again."

Her sister — and what a word, what a *country* — had the seal of the jar under her thumbs. Around them the vault groaned. Jars were going dark all down the galleries, souls winking out of captivity as the withdrawal reached them, and in the gloom Adaeze could hear voices rising, faint and astonished, the sound of ten thousand selves remembering themselves.

"When this opens," Gideon said, "you'll be — I don't actually know what you'll be. The archivist said the soul returns to the body, but your body—"

"Was left at the door thirteen years ago," Adaeze finished. "I know. I've done the arithmetic." She smiled, and felt her smile arrive all the way to her own eyes, which hadn't happened in moons. "I'll be light for a while. Maybe a long while. Maybe I'll be a rumor. But I'll be *mine*, and I'll be out, and I intend to be extremely annoying about both."

Perrin made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You two are going to be unbearable together. I can already tell. Two of you. The gods are cruel."

"Open it," Adaeze said.

The moon turned.

* * *

### Gideon

Later — much later, on a mountainside, under a sky rinsed clean — Gideon would try to describe the moment the seal broke, and never quite manage it. Words were thieves too, she decided. They took a moment and gave you back a sketch.

What she could say was this: the light did not burst from the jar. It *stood up*. It rose out of the glass like someone getting out of a chair after a long illness, unhurried, certain, and it filled the vault aisle from floor to ceiling, and every remaining jar on every remaining shelf lit in answer, ten thousand small flames saluting one freed soul, and the sound they made was not a sound at all but a feeling — the feeling of a held breath finally let go.

And the mountain began to die.

Not violently. That was the terrible, beautiful part. The Under-Court had never been a fortress; it had been a *ledger*, and a ledger with nothing written in it is just paper. As the last contracts burned and the last names went home, the lights along the galleries guttered out one by one, gallery by gallery, like a city going to sleep forever. The gold door, the iron door, the bone door — she felt them close in sequence above, softly, the way a shopkeeper turns a sign.

The masked clerks, when she saw them — and she saw only three, hurrying down a side gallery with armfuls of burning books — had stopped being frightening. Without the ledger they were just people in masks, and the masks had nothing left to hide.

"Go," one of them said as they passed. Not cruelly. Almost kindly. "Before the stair forgets how to be a stair."

The stair was already forgetting. As they climbed — Gideon with the empty jar tucked under her arm like a loaf of bread, Perrin half-carrying a shimmering, laughing, insubstantial Adaeze between them — the steps behind them softened into slope, the walls into warm dark stone, the whole throat of the mountain closing gently behind them like a book whose story was finished.

"My legs!" Adaeze crowed, drifting a foot above each step. "Gideon, I have *legs*, technically, in a manner of speaking, and they are *magnificent*—"

"You don't have feet yet."

"I have the *idea* of feet, and the idea is glorious."

"Save some glory for the exit," Perrin panted. His feathers had mostly given up; he looked like a boy who'd lost a fight with a pillow. Behind and below them the last lights went out — a long exhale of darkness rolling up the mountain's spine — and above them, impossibly far, a coin of grey light widened: the mouth of the bone door, opening onto the twelfth-night sky.

Gideon climbed toward it with an empty jar under her arm and no name in her mouth.

That was the loss inside the triumph, riding it like a burr on wool. She had signed the surrender an hour ago, in the wrecked audience chamber, with the court's own quill: *Gideon Okafor*, given freely, to balance the books. And the name had gone out of her the way the lights went out of the galleries — gently, gallery by gallery, memory by memory, until what remained was a person-shaped space full of chosen things: Perrin's stubborn loyalty. Bread shared by a widow. A mother's apology, delivered late but delivered. A lullaby with a mistake in it, sung the same wrong way every night.

She didn't know what to call herself. She suspected that was the point.

"Almost there," Perrin said. "And when we get out, I'm sleeping for a week, and then I'm opening that shop, and neither of you is allowed to help, you're both disasters—"

"Perrin." Adaeze's voice had gone soft. "Look."

The mouth of the door filled with moonlight — the thirteenth moon, complete, whole, turning its last degree — and pouring down through it came the smell of cold grass and pine, the whole living world leaning in to meet them.

Gideon took her sister's hand. It felt like holding a handful of warm wind, and like holding a hand, both at once.

"Together?" she asked.

"Together," said Adaeze. "Sister-who-stayed."

"And you," Gideon said, stepping up into the light, "are the sister who's free."

Behind them, the last light in the mountain went out. Ahead of them, on the moonlit slope, someone was waiting — a small figure wrapped in a shawl, standing beside two pairs of small shoes set neatly in the grass, as if she had known, as if she had always known, exactly where the road would end.

Adaeze's grip tightened. "Gideon," she whispered, "is that—"

"I know," Gideon said. Her heart, nameless and new, recognized her anyway. "I know who that is."

They climbed out of the mountain, into the cold and the starlight, toward the woman with the shoes.
