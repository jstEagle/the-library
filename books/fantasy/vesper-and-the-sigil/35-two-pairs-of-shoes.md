## 35. Two Pairs of Shoes

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, finger by finger, with a reluctance you could feel in your bones.

I climbed because Gideon pulled me, and I climbed because my legs remembered how even when the rest of me did not. Behind us the bone stair groaned and settled like an old man lowering himself into a chair he suspected was a trick. The lights of the court went out one gallery at a time, not all at once — that would have been too merciful — and each darkness that swallowed a floor of bottled souls made a sound I will spend the rest of my life trying not to hear: a soft *pop*, ten thousand times over, like rain beginning.

"Keep moving," Gideon said. Her voice was strange. Not hoarse from shouting, though she had shouted plenty. Strange the way a room is strange after the furniture's been moved. Familiar shapes, wrong places.

"I am moving," I said. "I'm excellent at moving. I've been moving for twelve moons."

"You know what I mean."

"I don't, actually." I stopped on a ledge where snow had gathered in the shape of nothing at all, just snow being snow, and I turned to look at her. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it? I don't know anything. I know a lullaby and a kitchen and two pairs of small shoes, and now I know a woman who gave up her name to carry me out of a mountain, and none of it connects. It's like being handed a basket of thread with no cloth attached."

Gideon looked at me for a long moment. The moon — the thirteenth moon, full and fat and finally finished with its turning — lit half her face and left the other half to the dark, which seemed fair, given everything.

"We'll find the cloth," she said.

"That's not how thread works."

"I'm a thief, not a weaver. Give me time."

* * *

We came down out of the tree line near dawn, onto a slope of grass still silvered with frost, and there we stopped, because neither of us seemed to know what people did next.

Here is what no one tells you about being rescued: it is very awkward.

For twelve moons I had been Adaeze-of-the-court, the favorite, the kept thing, the soul in the jar who learned to leave messages in dreams because fading quietly felt too much like agreeing. I had planned this morning a hundred times. In my plans, my sister and I fell into each other's arms and wept, and everything missing in me came home like birds to a roost.

In reality, we stood three paces apart on a cold hillside and could not think of a single thing to say.

Gideon cleared her throat. "Are you cold?"

"A little."

"There's a blanket in the pack. Perrin stole it off a caravan and then felt bad about it and paid for it, which ruined the whole gesture, but it's warm."

"Thank you."

She got the blanket out. She handed it over. Our fingers did not touch, because we were both being extremely careful about that, the way you're careful walking through a room full of glass.

"This is ridiculous," I said finally.

"Yes."

"We're sisters."

"So they tell me."

"We share a mother. We shared a cradle. There is presumably a lullaby—"

"*Hush now, the river's asleep,*" Gideon said.

"—and the moonlight keeps the—"

"*—keeps the small things safe.*"

We stared at each other.

Neither of us moved toward the other. But something in my chest, some door I hadn't known was shut, drifted open an inch, and cold clean air came through it, and I had to sit down right there on the frosty grass because my knees had opinions.

"She sang it," I said. "Our mother. In the kitchen. I have maybe nine seconds of her. Steam on the window. Her hands. The song. That's all the court left me — they skimmed me like cream, took everything rich, and what floated on top was too thin to be worth drinking. Nine seconds of kitchen."

Gideon sat down across from me, cross-legged, deliberate, like someone settling in for a long negotiation. Which, I suppose, she was. With herself as much as with me.

"I have more than nine seconds," she said quietly. "But most of them aren't mine. They're stolen. Hundreds of them, jars and jars of other people's lives I carried so long I forgot which ones I'd bought and which ones I'd taken and which ones—" She stopped. Started again. "Which ones were mine before anyone wrote a label on them."

"And now?"

"Now I'm nobody. Legally speaking." She said it lightly, and the lightness was the saddest thing I'd heard in twelve moons, and I have heard the court's musicians play lullabies to souls they were draining. "I signed away Gideon Okafor. The ledger's balanced. Whoever walks around in this body next is going to have to be built from scratch."

I wrapped the blanket tighter. "What will you call yourself?"

"Haven't decided. Perrin suggested 'Trouble.' He says it suits me and saves everyone the trouble of learning a new name."

"Perrin," I said, testing it. "The magpie man. He waited at the bottom?"

"He's waiting at the valley. He said — and I quote — 'I'll be at the rim, and if either of you takes longer than four days I'm coming in after you, and I want it noted that I complained about this in advance.'" A smile crossed her face, quick and real, gone again. "He complains about everything he loves. It's how you know."

* * *

The sun came up properly, and the frost burned off, and we did the thing that strangers do instead of embracing: we ate.

Gideon produced bread — actual bread, dense and dark, wrapped in cloth — and my hands shook so badly getting it to my mouth that she pretended not to notice, which was the kindest theft I ever witnessed.

"Widow Sefu's recipe," she said. "Or close enough. She taught me before I left. She said, 'If you meet the girl, feed her first. Everything else can wait until after bread.' She was right about the grief in her jar, and she was right about this."

"The widow whose memory you stole."

"My first job in the valley. I took her mourning because she asked me to. She couldn't hold it anymore." Gideon tore her own piece of bread and looked at it. "She found me anyway. Fed me anyway. Told me a story about twin girls sold beneath the mountain, and I sat there eating her bread thinking, *what a terrible thing to happen to somebody else.*"

"And it was happening to you the whole time."

"It was happening to both of us the whole time." She glanced up. "Does that make you angry? It makes me angry. I keep expecting the anger and getting — I don't know. Weather."

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty more than she deserved comfort, and because the court had never once offered me the chance to feel a feeling all the way through without skimming it, and I was not going to waste the opportunity.

"I'm angry at the court," I said. "Furiously. I'm angry at the famine, and the ledgers, and every masked face that tasted my name like wine. But you—" I turned the bread over in my hands. "You were a baby. So was I. Whatever was decided, neither of us decided it. And then you spent your whole life carrying pieces of a girl you didn't know existed, and when you found out, you didn't run. You walked into a mountain and unmade yourself. I can't be angry at that. I've tried. It won't take."

"It might later."

"It might later," I agreed. "Feelings are patient. That's the worst thing about them."

That surprised a laugh out of her — short, rusty, like a gate opening after years — and I filed the sound away as carefully as the court had ever filed anything, except that this I kept freely, and keeping it felt entirely different.

* * *

Midmorning, we walked. Neither of us suggested it; our feet simply conspired, and we went along the mountainside through scrub pine and loose shale, following the path down and away, and somewhere in the walking the careful distance between us shrank from three paces to two, and then to one-and-a-half, and neither of us remarked on it, because remarking would have scared it off like a bird.

"I should tell you what I remember," I said eventually. "All of it. It isn't much, but it's honest, and honest is new to me."

"All right."

"I remember the kitchen. I remember steam. I remember being carried up a road in the dark, and the person carrying me crying so hard I thought it was raining indoors. I remember the court's first mask leaning over my cradle-jar, and a voice saying, 'She'll do.' I remember twelve moons of being drunk slowly, and learning to hide the best parts of myself in dreams so they'd have less to sip." I paused. "And I remember a dream I sent that wasn't meant for you specifically. I sent it everywhere, like throwing seeds. Two pairs of shoes by a door. Small ones. Red."

"I saw it," Gideon said. "It's why I opened the jar. My mother's handwriting was on the seal, and I couldn't bear to break it, and then your dream came and the shoes were — they were ours, weren't they. Mine had a buckle missing."

"One buckle. You cried about it for a week, apparently, according to the nine seconds of mother I have, in which she is laughing about it."

"She laughed about it?" Something moved across Gideon's face — grief and warmth tangled together, indistinguishable, which I suspect is how family works. "I don't have that. I have the flinch. I flinch at rivers and for thirty years I didn't know why, and it turns out the reason drowned with a summer I sold to a Ferryman. He gave it back, near the end. I stood on his bridge holding a season like a cup of water, and I cried in front of a man made of tolls."

"What was in the summer?"

"Adaeze." She said my name carefully, like setting down something breakable. "You were in it. Both of us, knee-deep in the river, and you dared me to catch a fish with my hands, and I caught one, and it was the proudest moment of my entire childhood, and I sold it for passage and didn't even know what I'd lost." She shook her head. "The court took you. I took myself apart piece by piece, and called it a living. Between the two of us, we've been thoroughly robbed."

"Then here's what I propose," I said, and my heart was going hard against my ribs, because I had thought about this on the stair, in the dark, while the galleries went out behind us pop by pop. "Everything the court left me — the kitchen, the song, the road, the shoes — I offer you half. Freely. No ledger, no toll, no contract. Half of everything I remember, and half of everything I ever remember after this, if you want it. And you give me half of whatever you choose to keep. Not the stolen stuff — those belong back with their owners, I understand that. Just yours. The ones that are truly yours. The fish. The bridge. The widow's bread. Perrin complaining."

Gideon had gone very still.

"You don't have to," I added quickly. "I know what it costs, giving memory. I've watched professionals do it for a living. If it's too—"

"Do you know what I was going to do?" she interrupted. "Down in the vault, before the thirteenth turn, when I thought one of us might not walk out. I was going to pour my hoard into you. All of it. Every jar. So that whoever survived would walk out rich." She laughed at herself, softly. "Twelve moons of learning that memories are debts and prices and contracts, and my grand final gesture was going to be another theft — stealing you a past you never asked for. You just did it better in one sentence. *Offered.* Half. Freely." She looked at me, and her eyes were bright and wet and furious about being either. "Yes. Obviously yes. But I want terms."

"Terms," I said, delighted despite everything. "Go on."

"First: we start slow. One memory each, tonight, by a fire. Not a flood. I've seen what a flood does to a person and it isn't pretty and usually involves the magpie man laughing at them."

"Agreed."

"Second: either of us can refuse any memory, forever, no explanation owed. Some things should stay where they grew."

"Agreed."

"Third." She hesitated, and I watched her gather herself the way you gather yourself before stepping off a ledge into water you're fairly sure is deep enough. "Third: when we get to the valley, we plant something. Widow Sefu says grief grows best over old graves, and I figure memory's the same. Over the place where the jars used to be buried — mine, the ones I emptied returning them — we put a garden. And whatever grows there belongs to both of us equally, and neither of us has to remember why we planted it to enjoy the tomatoes."

I laughed — really laughed, the first one the court had never managed to tax — and stuck out my hand, formal as a merchant, solemn as a child.

Gideon Okafor, who was no longer Gideon Okafor, who was currently nobody at all, shook it. Her grip was warm and calloused and trembled slightly, and so did mine, and we stood there on the mountainside shaking hands like strangers closing a deal, which was exactly what we were, and exactly what we weren't, and both of us were grinning like fools by the end of it.

"Sisters," I said, trying the word out loud for the first time. It fit strangely, like a shoe with a missing buckle. "Give it a week. It'll wear in."

* * *

We made camp early, in the lee of a granite shoulder, and Gideon built a fire with the brisk competence of someone who has slept outdoors her whole life, and I sat as close to it as dignity allowed, because twelve moons underground had given me strong opinions about fire, all of them favorable.

Perrin's blanket went around both our shoulders eventually. Neither of us discussed this. It simply happened, the way the distance between us had shrunk on the path — by inches, by consent, by cold.

"All right," Gideon said. "One each. You first or me?"

"You. I want to see how it's done properly. All my experience of memory-sharing involves masks and tongs."

She settled back against the stone. "Close your eyes. It's easier received that way, Perrin says. He also says it's easier pickpocketed that way, but I'm choosing to trust you."

"I'd never. I don't know how."

"That," she said, "is the most restful thing anyone's said to me in years."

And she gave me the summer.

Not all of it — half, as promised, split cleanly down the middle like a fruit — and it arrived the way warm water arrives, all at once and everywhere. Sunlight through green leaves. Cold river water climbing my shins. My own small hands, chubbier than I remembered hands being, cupped around a fish that fought like a fury, and beside me a girl with a missing buckle on her shoe screaming with laughter, and a mother's voice from the bank calling us in for supper, and the whole world smelling of mud and evening and safety—

I opened my eyes and I was crying, and so was she, and above the fire the stars had come out in their usual indifferent thousands.

"Your turn," Gideon managed.

So I gave her the kitchen. All nine seconds of it, doubled — hers back plus mine, the steam, the hands, the laugh about the buckle, the song rising under everything like a river under ice. And when it settled into her I saw her mouth move silently, shaping the words, *hush now, the river's asleep,* and I knew she was hearing our mother sing it in a voice I'd only ever owned secondhand, and that now, thanks to her, I owned firsthand too.

We were quiet a long while.

"The cloth," I said at last. "Between the threads. I think I found a corner of it."

"Yeah," she said. "Me too."

Far below us, at the foot of the mountain, the road bent west toward the valley — toward a widow with bread, a magpie with complaints, a garden plot waiting over empty ground. And far above us, the thirteenth moon was setting, and behind it, faint as breath on glass, the new moon was already coming.

Gideon watched it rise for a moment, and something in her face shifted — resolve settling into place like a buckle finding its hole.

"Four days' walk to the rim," she said. "And Adaeze — there's something I have to show you when we get there. Something I took out of the vault besides you. I haven't opened it yet. I couldn't. Not alone."

The fire cracked. The new moon climbed.

"What is it?" I asked.

She reached into her pack, and her hand closed around something small and round and glass, and she did not bring it out.
