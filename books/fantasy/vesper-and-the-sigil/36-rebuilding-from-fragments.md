## 36. Rebuilding from Fragments

The first thing she learned about being no one was that the mornings were louder.

Not the world — the world was the same gray-gold hush it had always been at dawn in the foothills below the mountain. But without a name to stand between her and everything else, every sound arrived unfiltered. The drip of snowmelt off the eaves of Perrin's lean-to. The creak of his wings when he stretched in magpie form on the roof beam, because he still slept as a bird some nights and claimed it was "for professional reasons." The slow, even breathing of Adaeze in the bedroll beside hers.

She lay there and listened to all of it, and there was nothing in her head that said *you are the sort of person who minds this* or *you are the sort of person who loves this*. There was just the listening, and then, after a moment, a small decision: she liked it.

That was how it worked now, she was discovering. Everything was a decision.

Before — before the vault, before the ledger, before she had signed away *Gideon Okafor* in ink that drank itself into her skin and left not even a scar — she had been made the way most people are made: by accident. By inheritance. By a famine year and a mother's terrible arithmetic and a valley that forgot things so thoroughly that forgetting became a kind of architecture. She had built herself out of stolen goods and called the building a self.

Now the building was gone. And the strange, terrifying, liberating truth of it was this: the plot of land was still hers.

She sat up. Adaeze stirred but didn't wake. Her sister — the word still fit strangely, like a shoe bought for someone else's foot, though she was growing fond of the pinch — slept with one hand curled near her face the way children do, though she had been out of the mountain less than a season and would be a long time learning to sleep like someone free.

Outside, Perrin was arguing with a chicken.

"—I'm simply saying," he was saying, in his human shape, hands on hips, "that if you're going to live here, you could contribute something. Shiny things. Information. A general air of menace."

The chicken looked at him with the flat contempt only chickens can manage.

"Don't take it personally," she said, ducking out through the flap. "He argues with everything. It's how he shows affection."

"I do not argue with *everything*," Perrin said. "I argue selectively, and with taste." He turned, and his eyes did the thing they did sometimes now — the quick flicker over her face, checking, counting, making sure all of her had arrived again today. He'd been doing it since the mountain. She pretended not to notice, because letting him know she noticed would only make him do it more theatrically. "Sleep well, No-One?"

It had started as his joke and become, against all her expectations, something almost comfortable. Not a name exactly. More like a coat you wore while your real clothes were being made.

"Well enough," she said. "I dreamed about bread."

"Again?"

"Again."

Perrin nodded sagely, as if this were profound. "Bread is a respectable dream. Better than mine. I dreamed I owed a dead woman a favor, and then I woke up and remembered I don't anymore, and honestly it was the finest morning of my life."

But he said it lightly, and lightly was how he carried his sister's map now, buried under the floorboards of a shop he hadn't opened yet, in a valley he swore he'd only be staying in "until spring, obviously, I'm not sentimental." It was nearly summer.

* * *

The rebuilding, she had learned, could not be done all at once. That was the first rule, and she'd learned it the hard way in the second week, when she'd tried to sit down and simply *decide who she was*, start to finish, the way she used to plan a heist. She'd gotten as far as *someone who is good with locks* before the whole enterprise collapsed under its own weight, because being good with locks wasn't a self, it was a tool, and tools don't tell you what they're for.

So instead she did it the way you build anything worth having. One piece at a time. Chosen pieces.

Piece the first: the widow's bread.

She could still taste it. That was the miracle of it — Widow Sefu's kitchen, the loaf torn warm from the oven, the old woman's hands dusted white, saying *eat first, talk after; grief keeps, hunger doesn't.* Gideon had stolen that woman's grief once, lifted it out of a locked jar like a pickpocket lifting a purse, and told herself it was mercy. It hadn't been mercy. It had been theft wearing mercy's coat. But the bread had been real, freely given, given *after* Sefu knew what she was — and that, she decided, was a piece worth keeping. Not the theft. The bread.

*I am someone who lets people feed me,* she wrote, on the third morning, in the little book Perrin had produced from somewhere ("a guide is prepared for all contingencies, including literacy"). *And who says thank you properly.*

Adaeze read it over her shoulder. "That's a strange thing to put in a book about yourself."

"It's the truest thing in it so far."

"You've written three lines."

"Quality over quantity."

Her sister laughed — Adaeze laughed easily now, the way water runs once the ice breaks — and went to poke at the fire, and she watched her go and felt the second piece settle into place without her even deciding it, which was its own lesson: some pieces choose you back.

Piece the second: Adaeze's offered memories.

They had agreed on terms, sitting on the mountainside in the ash-light of the dying court, two strangers sharing a lullaby. Adaeze would give her half of what she remembered — their mother's face, the kitchen with the crooked shelf, the game they'd invented with two pairs of small shoes lined up by the door — and she would take none of it unasked. That mattered more than she could explain. Every memory she owned before had been taken or bought or bargained for. These came with open hands behind them.

But there was a catch neither of them had foreseen, and the catch was this: Adaeze remembered their mother *lovingly*, and the memories arrived lovingly, and love was harder to hold than grief. When Adaeze gave her the memory of their mother singing — the lullaby with the descending line like steps going down into dark, sung bright, sung anyway — she had to sit alone for an hour afterward, because the woman in the memory was both the mother who had sold a daughter and the mother who had spent thirteen years and her last coin buying the way back, and both women were true, and holding them together in one chest was like carrying a jar full of bees.

She kept it anyway. Bees make honey. That was becoming another rule.

"I gave you the shoes yet?" Adaeze asked her one evening, apropos of nothing, mending a sock with more enthusiasm than skill.

"Not yet."

"They're coming. I'm saving them. You should save the good ones too, you know. Ration yourself." She bit the thread. "A soul spends a year being eaten slowly, you learn rationing."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"No you won't. You have thief habits. You hoard." Adaeze pointed the needle at her. "I remember that much without even trying. Even at four years old, one of us was already hiding biscuits under the mattress, and Mother never could prove which."

Something warmed in her chest, and she examined it carefully, the way she used to examine unfamiliar locks — and found it was pride. Ordinary, borrowed, secondhand pride, in a crime committed at age four. She entered it in the book.

*Piece the third: I may have always been a biscuit criminal. Some things survive even a sold name.*

* * *

Perrin found her at the river on the ninth day, which surprised her, because Perrin avoided the river the way cats avoid baths, and she had stopped flinching at rivers only recently herself — since the Ferryman gave her back her summer, since she stood on his bridge weeping over a season she'd sold and he'd returned unasked, payment on a debt owed to a dead woman's daughter.

The summer fit strangely now. It was a child's summer, all heat and river-water and her mother's voice calling them in before dark, and it belonged to a girl named Gideon Okafor, and Gideon Okafor was gone. But the sunburn was still in it. The joy was still in it. She had decided those were transferable.

"You're doing it again," Perrin said, landing on the bank in a flurry of black-and-white feathers and becoming a man mid-stride, which never got less disorienting to watch. "The staring-at-the-water-like-it-owes-you-money thing."

"It doesn't owe me money. It owes me an explanation. Rivers took my summer, and then a bridge over a river gave it back. Rivers are inconsistent."

"Rivers are rivers. People are inconsistent. Rivers just get blamed." He sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was as close as Perrin ever came to saying anything sincere without a seven-minute preamble. "Can I ask you something without you getting strange about it?"

"I'm always strange about things. It's one of my chosen pieces."

"When you build her — the new one. Whoever she turns out to be." He picked up a stone and skipped it badly. "Am I in there? Or am I staff?"

She looked at him. His profile was very casual and his hands were not.

"Perrin. You dragged me across the salt flats by sheer stubbornness when I'd gone quiet as a grave. You confessed your sister's ghost to me at midnight because guilt shared was trust earned. You tried to steal my map, failed, got forgiven, and stayed anyway, which by any honest accounting makes you either the bravest man I know or the worst thief, and I've decided it's both." She paused. "You're not a piece. You're load-bearing."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Load-bearing," he repeated, tasting it. "Hm. Yes. All right. I accept." He skipped another stone, worse than the first. "For the record, you're in mine too. The new one I'm building. The one where I'm a respectable shopkeeper who returns stolen memories free of charge and definitely isn't sentimental."

"That sounds like a terrible business model."

"It's a *legacy*," he said with dignity. "My sister stole a map from a court and died getting it out. The least I can do is run a shop where nobody has to steal anything ever again." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and pointed at her with sudden ferocity. "And you'll help. When we cross the rim. You owe the valley a reckoning, No-One, and I intend to watch it happen while eating something."

There it was. The hook under the bait. The rim.

She had known it was coming — known since the mountain, since the plan formed in whispers between moons. To bring Adaeze home. To break the valley's oldest rule, the one that said magic dies at the border, the one that had made her a thief in a land where magic couldn't follow. The valley could not follow what refused to be followed. She meant to refuse.

But that was tomorrow's wall. Today's wall was smaller, and she'd been circling it for nine days.

"Perrin," she said. "Do you remember my name?"

He blinked. "Of course I—"

He stopped.

She watched him reach for it and find the space where it had been, smooth and empty as a jar after the thief comes through. Because that was the cost, and the cost had teeth: *Gideon Okafor* hadn't just been surrendered by her. It had been *paid* — paid into the court's ledger, balanced against a soul — and a debt paid is a debt erased, and an erased name takes everyone's memory of it with it. The widow would greet her kindly and not know why the kindness felt familiar. Her mother, if her mother lived, would look at her daughter and see a stranger shaped like a stranger.

Only Adaeze remembered, and only partly — because Adaeze had been beneath the mountain when the name was spoken aloud in the world, and souls keep their own ledgers.

"Gideon," Perrin said at last, carefully, watching her face. "Gideon Okafor. Your sister says it. I've heard her say it." He frowned. "But it's like... a word in a language I don't speak. I can repeat the sounds. They don't mean anyone."

"I know."

"Do you want it back? We could— there are ways, the eaters at the market, if any survived the withdrawal, maybe someone held a copy—"

"No."

It came out faster and firmer than she expected, and she sat with her own answer for a moment, surprised by it. Then she understood. This was the last test, and she'd passed it without studying.

"No," she said again, gentler. "That's the whole point, Perrin. If I take it back, I'm a thief again. Stealing myself out of a ledger." She looked at the river, running on without needing anyone's permission. "I want to be *given* a name. Or better — I want to grow one. Like the garden."

"What garden?"

"The one I'm going to plant." She stood, and brushed off her knees, and felt the strangest thing rise in her, which was not grief and not fear but something like appetite. "Over the old jars. Where I used to bury my thefts. Something should grow there that nobody had to lose."

Perrin stared at her. "You want to plant a garden over a graveyard of stolen memories."

"I want to plant a garden over a graveyard of stolen memories," she confirmed.

"That is the single most unsettling thing you've ever said, and I once watched you feed a hunter a false memory until he unraveled like cheap rope."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't entirely a compli—"

"It was entirely a compliment. Come on. Adaeze promised me the shoes tonight, and I intend to be insufferable about it."

* * *

That night, by the fire, Adaeze gave her the shoes.

Two pairs of small ones, side by side by the door of a kitchen with a crooked shelf. One pair scuffed at the toes because its owner climbed everything. One pair pristine because its owner was careful, always careful, even at four, even before anyone knew what careful would cost.

In the memory, their mother knelt between them, straightening laces that didn't need straightening, and hummed the lullaby with the descending line, and both girls laughed at something neither twin could now recall, and outside the window the mountain stood dark against the stars, patient as a creditor.

She held the memory the way you hold a bird — gently, ready to let it go if it wanted.

And in the quiet after, she opened her book and wrote the last line of the night, the one she'd been circling for nine days:

*I was never the sum of what was taken from me. I am what I choose to carry.*

Then, because identity was also curation and curation required honesty, she added:

*Also: I choose to carry biscuits. Under the mattress. Some traditions must continue.*

Adaeze was asleep. Perrin was on the roof in feathered form, pretending to keep watch, actually snoring. The fire ticked. Somewhere east, past three days' hard walking, the rim of the valley waited — the line where magic died, the line she had crossed once as a thief and would cross again as something else entirely, with her sister's hand in hers and a rule older than the wardens about to learn what refusal looked like.

She banked the fire, tucked the book inside her coat, over her heart, where important documents belong.

Tomorrow, they walked home.
