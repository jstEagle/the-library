## 37. The Return Through the Rim

The mountain let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, finger by finger, with a creaking that Gideon felt in her teeth.

They climbed for two days out of the dark. The bone stair gave way to galleries, the galleries to tunnels where the air tasted of rain instead of dust, and then, on the morning of the second day, they came up through a crack in the mountainside no wider than a bread oven and found themselves blinking in ordinary daylight, on an ordinary slope, covered in ordinary soot.

Adaeze sat down immediately and did not get up for some time.

"I had forgotten," she said, "that the sky was *large*."

"You'd have remembered it eventually," Gideon said. "It's very insistent."

Perrin landed on a boulder nearby in his magpie shape, shook himself into his boy shape mid-hop — he never could resist showing off the transition, even now, even exhausted, even with ash in his feathers — and looked back the way they'd come. The mountain stood behind them, sullen and dimmed, its lights gone out one by one like a house whose family had finally moved away.

"It's not following us," he said, in the tone of someone who doesn't trust good news.

"Of course it's not following," Gideon said. "Its ledger balanced. It has no claim."

"That's not what I mean." He turned. His eyes were still bright and quick, but there was something older in them now, something the last thirteen moons had put there. "I mean the valley. Your valley. The magic-dead place. You're carrying enough enchantment to light a small city, Gideon. That girl alone—" he nodded at Adaeze, who was lying flat on her back on the grass, arms spread, watching clouds as though they were a play she'd been denied tickets to for years "—is a soul the court fed on for twelve years. She's practically glowing. And you're going to walk her over the rim into a valley that eats magic for breakfast?"

"Yes," Gideon said.

"Just yes? No plan? No clever thief reasoning?"

"The reasoning is simple." Gideon sat down beside Adaeze, because her legs had also filed a complaint. "Everyone says the valley exiles magic. Everyone says nothing enchanted can cross the rim. But I've been thinking about what the archivist told us, and what the Ferryman said, and what my mother—" the word still fit strangely in her mouth, like a shoe half a size wrong, warm but unfamiliar "—what she understood when she sent me there. The valley doesn't eat magic. It just refuses to hold it. Magic can't follow you in because magic only goes where it's wanted, or feared, or owed. And the valley wants nothing, fears nothing, owes nothing."

"So?"

"So it can't follow what refuses to be followed." She shrugged. "We're not bringing magic in. We're bringing *us* in. If the magic wants to come along, it'll have to learn to be quiet about it."

Perrin stared at her. Then he laughed — a short, startled bark of a laugh, the kind he usually saved for other people's misfortunes.

"You're going to smuggle yourself across your own border," he said. "That's the most Gideon Okafor thing I've ever heard."

Gideon flinched at the name. She couldn't help it. It was still tender, the way a healing wound is tender — the name was hers again now, in a sense, since she had signed it away and then, walking down the bone stair with Adaeze's hand in hers, had quietly picked it back up off the floor. But it didn't sit the way it used to. It was less like a skin and more like a coat: something she wore because she chose to, and could take off.

"We should rest tonight," she said, to change the subject, which was a thief's oldest trick and worked on friends nearly as well as on strangers. "Cross the rim tomorrow at dawn. Borders are honest at dawn. Everything else is asleep."

* * *

They rested. Or rather, Adaeze slept the deep, untroubled sleep of someone who had not slept untroubled in twelve years, and Perrin dozed upright like a bird, and Gideon lay awake looking at the stars and doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said aloud, the thing she'd tucked behind the joke about smuggling: she did not know if it would work. The theory was sound — she believed it was sound — but theories had a way of meeting reality at borders, and reality usually won. If she was wrong, the valley wouldn't just refuse Adaeze's soul. It might refuse *her*. The woman who walked out of the mountain was not entirely the woman who had walked in; too much had been poured out and poured in, the name surrendered and reclaimed, the stolen hoard given back or burned away, the sealed jar opened and its grief finally grieved. What was left of her was mostly choice. And choices, as far as she knew, had never been tested against a warding line.

She thought about Widow Sefu's bread. She thought about the small stone cottage with its locked memory-jars, most of them empty now, returned to their owners by post or by Perrin's increasingly elaborate delivery schemes. She thought about the fact that home was a word she had used all her life without ever once checking whether it applied to her.

Beside her, Adaeze turned over in her sleep and murmured something. A lullaby fragment. Their mother's lullaby — the one from the kitchen in the dream, the one with two pairs of small shoes by the door. Gideon listened until the melody faded, and found, to her surprise, that she was humming the next line before she knew she knew it.

Well, she thought. That's one thing the valley can't take from me. It was never magic to begin with. It was just ours.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and gold, and the rim of the valley came into view an hour later: a long ridge of dark stone, unremarkable in every way except that everyone in the world knew what it meant. On this side, wonder. On that side, none. Gideon had lived her whole adult life on the none side and had called it safety.

Perrin shifted to his bird shape and back again three times in quick succession, the way he did when he was nervous and pretending it was vanity.

"If this goes badly," he said, "I want it noted that I suggested we simply stay here. There are perfectly good villages. I know a man in Lowmarsh who owes me—"

"Perrin."

"—several favors, actually, and his wife makes a pie that would make you weep—"

"*Perrin.*"

He stopped. Gideon took his hand — his human hand, which felt strange and small and warm — and squeezed it once.

"You're coming with us," she said. "That wasn't a question, by the way. I'm informing you."

His throat worked. Thirteen moons ago he had tried to steal her map and leave her in the dark, and she had caught him, and instead of rage she had given him a memory freely — his sister, young and laughing, a day he'd thought lost forever. He had never quite forgiven himself for the attempt, and she had never quite let him, because letting him would have meant agreeing that he was the sort of person who abandoned people, and he wasn't. He was the sort of person who tried to and couldn't. In her experience that described nearly everyone worth knowing.

"My sister's buried back there," he said quietly. "Under the willow past the mill. I never even marked it."

"Then we'll mark it," Gideon said. "With something loud. She'd have hated anything tasteful."

A wet laugh escaped him. "She'd have demanded a statue."

"A statue of a magpie, mid-theft."

"Mid-*heist*. She'd insist on the distinction." He wiped his eyes with his sleeve, briskly, as though clearing away weather. "All right. All right. Let's go be impossible."

Adaeze stood between them, dressed in clothes Perrin had bought in a market town — a plain brown dress, a shawl, boots that almost fit. She looked, Gideon thought, like exactly what she was: a girl of perhaps nineteen who had missed nineteen years of practice at being one. But her chin was up. Whatever the court had fed on, it had never managed to eat the part of her that decided things. Gideon recognized it because she had the same part. Twins, after all.

"Together?" Adaeze asked.

"Together," Gideon said.

They walked up the ridge side by side, Perrin circling overhead in black-and-white defiance of the early hour, and the rim rose to meet them, and Gideon felt the moment they crossed it the way you feel a door close behind you — a change in pressure, a hush.

Nothing happened.

That was the first thing. Nothing happened. No flash, no thunderclap, no ward-flare, no voice of ancient law pronouncing judgment. They simply stepped over a line of dark stone and were, abruptly, inside the valley, and the valley received them the way it received everything: indifferently, quietly, without comment.

But Gideon felt it anyway. Felt the silence come down around her like snowfall — the great, famous, magic-eating silence of the valley, the silence she had built her whole trade inside. And in that silence, everything she carried went still. Not gone. *Still.* Adaeze's soul, which had shimmered faintly ever since the vault, dimmed to a banked ember. The last few memories Gideon had chosen to keep — the summer the Ferryman returned, her mother's voice from the salt flats, the lullaby — folded themselves down small and quiet, like birds settling for the night.

And underneath it all, steady as a heartbeat, the part of her that was not magic at all. The choosing part. The part that had signed the name and taken it back. It crossed the border untouched, because it had never belonged to the mountain, or the court, or the ledger, or any power anywhere. It belonged to her.

"It worked," Perrin said, landing beside them and resuming his boy shape with a stumble, because he was watching her face instead of his feet. "You impossible, stubborn, brilliant — it *worked*."

"The valley can't follow what refuses to be followed," Gideon said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "I told you."

"You told me a theory. At dawn. While eating a heel of bread." He gestured wildly at the whole visible world. "You can't just *say* things like that and have them be *true*, Gideon, there are rules—"

"There really aren't," Adaeze said mildly. "I spent twelve years under a court that ran entirely on rules. I can tell you with authority: the rules are just stories powerful people tell each other until they forget they made them up." She looked around at the valley — at the gray-green hills, the smoke rising from Sefu's chimney in the middle distance, the ordinary miraculous nothing of it — and her eyes filled, and she laughed at herself for it, wiping her face with the shawl. "It's so *quiet* here."

"That's the valley's whole personality," Gideon said. "Give it time. It grows on you. Like moss."

"Like moss," Adaeze repeated, tasting the words, and smiled — a real smile, crooked, their mother's smile worn by a different face. "Good. I've had quite enough of things that grow on you like fire."

* * *

Widow Sefu met them halfway down the hill, because of course she did. Gideon would learn later that Perrin had flown ahead at first light two days prior — while they slept, the traitor, the dear devoted traitor — and told her everything, and that she had spent those two days baking as though famine were imminent and joy required fortification.

She came down the path with a basket on her arm and her gray head uncovered, and when she saw them she stopped, and set the basket down very carefully on a flat rock, as though her hands needed to be free for what came next.

Gideon braced for questions. Where, why, how, who is this girl who walks like you and has your mother's frown. She had answers prepared, several of them lies, one of them true.

Sefu bypassed all of them. She walked up to Adaeze, took the girl's face in both her flour-dusted hands, and looked at her for a long moment — the way you look at something you have prayed about without admitting you were praying.

"There you are," she said simply. "One of you got out after all."

Then she pulled her into an embrace that Adaeze, after a startled half-second, fell into like a person falling into water after years in a desert — gracelessly, completely, with her whole body.

Gideon stood to one side and discovered that her eyes were doing something embarrassing. Perrin appeared at her elbow and offered, wordlessly, a handkerchief that smelled of feathers. She took it.

"She baked," he murmured. "For two days. There's a honey loaf in that basket the size of a paving stone. I checked. Twice."

"You're a menace."

"I'm a *guide*," he said with dignity. "Guiding people toward bread is still guiding."

When Sefu finally released Adaeze, she turned to Gideon, and her expression shifted into something more complicated — fondness threaded with the particular exasperation of a woman who had once had her grief stolen from a locked jar and had responded by *baking for the thief*.

"Well," Sefu said. "You went out over that rim with nothing but a dead stranger's map and a bad attitude, and you've come back with a sister, a story, and — unless my eyes deceive me — a magpie boy who's been sleeping in my hayloft and eating my preserves."

"That last one is slander," said Perrin.

"He ate the plum ones."

"That one is accurate," Gideon said.

"And you." Sefu looked at her, and her voice gentled in a way that was somehow harder to bear than shouting. "You're different, child. Smaller in some ways. Bigger in others. Like a house that's had walls taken out."

Gideon considered the many available replies — deflection, wit, the practiced blankness of a professional — and found, to her mild alarm, that she didn't want any of them. That was new. That was possibly the strangest thing the whole journey had done to her: somewhere between the vault and the rim, she had lost her appetite for doors that locked.

"I gave most of myself away," she said. "To balance the ledger. And then I chose what to keep." She paused. "I kept the bread."

Sefu blinked. "The—"

"The memory. Of your kitchen. Of you giving it to me." Gideon shrugged, feeling her ears go hot. "It seemed like a reasonable thing to build a person out of."

For a moment Sefu said nothing at all. Then she picked up her basket, thrust it into Gideon's arms — it weighed approximately as much as a small child — and said, in a voice suspiciously thick:

"You'll both come inside. There's soup. There will be soup for a week, frankly, I may have overestimated. And you—" she jabbed a floury finger at Perrin "—will return my plum preserves or replace them, and don't think I don't know which jar."

* * *

Evening came, and the valley did what the valley always did: it forgot, gently, thoroughly, everything that didn't happen inside its own borders. The mountain's shadow faded from the sky. The court became a story, then a rumor, then the kind of tale told to children to make them behave — *don't sell your dreams, little one, or the mountain will keep the change.*

Gideon sat on Sefu's doorstep after supper with Adaeze beside her, both of them full of soup and honey loaf, watching the first stars come out over hills that asked nothing of anyone.

"Do you feel it?" Adaeze asked quietly. "The quiet?"

"Mm. All my life."

"No — I mean—" Adaeze pressed a hand to her own chest. "Everything I am that was *theirs*, the court's, the contract's — it's gone soft. Like embers under ash. But the rest of me, the parts that are just *me* — the lullaby, the kitchen, the shoes by the door — those are still burning. The valley didn't touch them." She looked over. "Do you think that's what you meant? Refusing to be followed?"

Gideon thought about it honestly, which was another new habit she was trying to cultivate.

"I think," she said, "that the valley only ever exiled what could be taken away. And we stopped being takeable somewhere on the bone stair." She turned the name over in her mind — *Gideon Okafor*, signed away, reclaimed, remade — and found it sat easier tonight than it had yesterday. Less a coat now. Nearly a skin. Hers, because she had written it, letter by letter, out of everything she'd chosen to keep. "Home isn't the border, Addy. I think I had it backwards my whole life. The border was just where I hid. Home is—" she nodded at the lit window behind them, at the sound of Perrin inside arguing cheerfully with Sefu about the proper storage of preserves, at the sister warm against her shoulder "—home is whoever's still sitting next to you when the story ends."

Adaeze was quiet a moment. Then: "The story hasn't ended."

"No?"

"No." She reached into the folds of her shawl and drew out something small that caught the starlight — a shard of pale glass, and within it, faintly, a curl of ink that moved. One of the court's message-vials, pocketed from the vault in the chaos, still holding a single line of ledger-script that had not yet faded. Adaeze had shown it to no one. "There are ten thousand jars still down there, Gideon. The court is broken, but the vault is only *dark*, not empty. And the ledger-script says—" she tilted it toward the starlight "—it says the debts are recorded elsewhere. A second archive. Somewhere north."

Gideon looked at the shard a long time. Behind them, inside, Perrin's voice rose indignantly over something Sefu had said about plums, and the smell of woodsmoke drifted out the open door, and the valley lay silent and safe around them, holding nothing, refusing everything, making room anyway.

"Well," Gideon said at last, and heard herself grin — the old thief's grin, but warmer now, aimed instead of armored. "I suppose we'd better find out who's keeping the books."

Adaeze grinned back — their mother's grin, twice worn — and closed her hand around the light.

Inside, something crashed, followed by Perrin's voice, high and betrayed: "*That was the LAST of the plum!*"
