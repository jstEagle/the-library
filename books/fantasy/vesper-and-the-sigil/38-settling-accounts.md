## 38. Settling Accounts

The map had been in Perrin's coat pocket for so long that his body had learned its shape the way a riverbed learns water. He reached for it out of habit — checking, always checking, the way he'd checked for it every morning since a dying woman pressed it into her brother's hands and said *thirteen turns* with blood on her teeth.

Thirteen turns had come and gone. The moon had finished its arithmetic. And the map was still there, folded into eighths, wax seal cracked, ink long since gone still and ordinary.

He sat on the widow Sefu's doorstep with it spread across his knees, because it seemed wrong to bury a thing indoors, and because Sefu's house was where people in this valley went when they didn't know where else to put themselves.

"You're going to get dirt on that," Sefu said from behind him, though he hadn't started digging yet.

"It's going in the dirt anyway."

"Then at least dig a tidy hole." She set down a cup of something steaming beside him — chicory, by the smell of it, bitter enough to strip paint — and stood over him with her arms crossed, supervising. "My husband buried our savings under the fig tree during the famine year. Dug it like a grave. Said money deserved ceremony or it came back haunted."

"Did it come back haunted?"

"The money? No. The hole filled with rain and drowned a chicken." She nudged his boot with hers. "Dig deeper than you think you need to. Everything worth burying is worth burying properly."

Perrin dug. The valley soil was stubborn, magic-dead, honest — it gave nothing back but what you put into it, which was more than could be said for most places he'd lived. His hands remembered how to work without him watching them. That was the thing about hands; they kept their own accounts.

The map lay in the bottom of the hole, white paper against dark earth, and Perrin looked at it for a long moment before he picked up the first handful of soil.

His sister had stolen this map from the court beneath the mountain. She had carried it through gates and markets and hunters' country, bleeding the whole way, and she had spent her last strength crossing to a valley where no one ever arrived, because she'd heard — everyone in the trade had heard — that there was a thief there who could hold things no one else could hold. A memory-thief who never asked why.

She'd been wrong about Gideon, in the end. Or right about her in a way neither of them had guessed. Both sisters sold, both sisters traded, one soul in a jar and one name in a body, and the map had known it all along — the ink had rearranged itself into scenes Gideon almost remembered, which meant the map had been trying to talk the whole time, and Perrin had spent six moons calling it a *stubborn piece of parchment* and threatening to feed it to a goat.

"I'm sorry," he told the map. "About the goat remark. You were very patient."

Sefu said nothing, which was her way of saying everything.

He covered it with soil, patted it flat, and sat back on his heels. Something in his chest unclenched — not all at once, not like a knot coming free, but like ice going out of a pond in spring, slowly, from the edges in. He'd carried his sister's debt across half the world. He'd led a stranger through towns made of borrowed light and let her pay bridges in childhood summers. He'd tried to steal the map and run, once, in the third moon, and been caught, and been forgiven — forgiven so freely it had rearranged something in him that he still couldn't name without his voice going strange.

The debt was paid. The map was buried. And now —

Now there was a great deal of morning left, and no errand in it, and Perrin discovered that he did not know what a person did with that.

* * *

"You're moulting," Sefu observed, three days later.

"I am *not* moulting." Perrin smoothed his feathers down with as much dignity as a man can manage while standing in a kitchen with a feather stuck to his chin. Magpie-shifters shed. It was seasonal. It was natural. It was also, at present, happening all over Sefu's floor, and she was sweeping it into a pile with an expression usually reserved for weather.

"You're moulting all over my kitchen, and you've eaten everything in my pantry, and you keep looking at the door like it owes you money."

"The door doesn't owe me anything," Perrin said. "That's the problem."

She set down the broom. "Say it plainly. I'm old and I don't have patience for birds who circle."

He circled anyway. He perched on the edge of a chair, then thought better of it and sat like a person, mostly. "The valley's full of jars," he said finally. "You know that. Half the houses here have them. Grief in a jar on the mantel. Joy in a jar under the bed. People paid thieves like — well. Like Gideon used to be. To take the things they couldn't carry, and the thieves took them, and nobody ever gave anything back."

"Gideon's giving things back," Sefu said. "Her and the sister. They've been at it a month."

"They're doing the mountain's ledger. The big thefts. The court-stamped ones." He turned his cup in his hands. "But there are hundreds of jars in this valley that never came from the mountain. Small ones. Local ones. A widow's grief. A farmer's wedding day. Things people sold because they were hungry, or frightened, or because someone told them they'd be lighter without them." He looked up. "Nobody's doing those."

"And you want to be the one who does."

"I want—" He stopped. Started again. "I spent twenty years taking things, Sefu. Favors, memories, whatever was loose. I owed a dead woman a favor and I resented every step of paying it, and then somewhere around the salt flats I stopped resenting it and I don't know when that happened either." The feather on his chin fluttered as he spoke. He plucked it off and set it on the table with great formality, like evidence. "I don't know how to be done owing. I've never been done owing. But if I open a shop — if I return what was taken, free, no charge, no questions — maybe the account balances. Maybe not mine. Maybe just... generally. The world's account."

Sefu looked at him for a long moment. Then she went to the cupboard, took down a jar — a plain clay jar, unglazed, the kind that cost nothing — and set it in front of him.

Inside, faintly, something glowed. Not light exactly. More like the memory of light, the way a room remembers a window after sunset.

"My mother's laugh," Sefu said. "I sold it forty years ago, the winter my children were small and the crops failed. A traveling man gave me three coins and told me I wouldn't miss it." Her mouth twisted. "I haven't laughed properly since. I'd forgotten why, until your friend came through asking questions about jars."

Perrin's throat went tight. "Why are you giving me this?"

"To stock your shop," she said. "And because somebody has to be first, and I'm tired of being the woman whose grief got stolen in chapter one." She pushed the jar across the table. "Open it whenever you find me. Not before. I want to do it properly, sitting down, maybe with bread."

"Sefu—"

"Don't make it a moment," she warned. "Moments are expensive. Just say yes and go find premises."

* * *

The premises he found was a lean-to attached to the old mill, which leaked in two places and smelled of flour and mice, and which Perrin decided was perfect because it had a wide counter, good light, and a door that opened onto the square where everyone would see them come and go. Shame worked best in cellars. Return worked best in daylight.

He built shelves. He swept. He painted a sign — badly, because magpies are thieves and mimics, not artists, and the letters leaned like drunks — that read:

**THE RETURN DESK**
*Bring us what isn't yours. Take back what is.*
*No charge. No questions. Mostly.*

Gideon, when she saw the sign, stood in front of it for a full minute with her arms crossed.

"'Mostly,'" she said.

"There are questions and there are *questions*. If someone brings in a jar that clearly belongs to the court, I'm asking questions."

"The court's ledgers are ash."

"The court's *habits* are everywhere. Trust me. I had some of them." He hopped up to sit on the counter, which he did now without thinking, forgetting sometimes that other people found it odd. "Besides. 'Mostly' makes people curious. Curiosity gets them through the door."

Gideon studied him. She'd gotten better at that since the mountain — or worse, depending on how you liked being seen. "You used to charge one memory of your choosing for everything," she said. "Crossing a road. Sharing a fire. Breathing near you, some days."

"I was saving up for a coffin."

"You were saving up because you thought if you owned enough of other people's lives, you'd accidentally own one yourself."

Perrin opened his beak — his mouth — to argue, and found nothing in it. "...When did you get wise?" he demanded. "You used to be a wall with legs."

"I died a little in a mountain. It does that." She touched the sign, straightening one drunken letter, and something in her face went soft and far away. "Adaeze says I should thank you properly. For the sixth moon. When I went quiet."

"She says a lot of things. She offered me half her memories last week. I told her I'd rather have dinner."

"What did she say?"

"She said I was the stingiest man she'd ever met and then she ate most of my bread." He shrugged, feathers rippling. "We understand each other."

Gideon smiled — the new smile, the one that belonged entirely to whoever she was now, the author of herself — and left, and Perrin sat alone in his shop with his shelves half-empty and his heart, for the first time in living memory, not weighing anything.

* * *

The first customer came at dawn on the fourth day, and she was eight years old, and she marched in like a general.

"I want to give something back," she announced.

Perrin looked up from inventorying — three jars, one chipped teacup that hummed faintly with somebody's wedding song, and a hat he suspected was simply a hat. "All right. What have you got?"

She produced, from a cloth bundle, a small jar with a wax seal shaped like a bird. Inside, something moved like minnows in shallow water. "My brother stole my dream," she said. "The one about flying over the lake. He took it while I slept and he's been having it himself ever since, and he says it's his now because possession is nine-tenths, which is what Papa says about fences." She slammed the jar on the counter. "It's *mine*. I dreamed it first. I want it returned and I want him to be told off."

Perrin examined the seal. Bird-shaped. His own early work, from years ago, when he'd been younger and hungrier and had passed through this valley selling tricks to children too young to know what they were losing. He remembered the job vaguely — a boy, coin from a game, a dream lifted through a window. He had not remembered the girl. He had not, he realized, thought about the girl at all.

"That's mine," he said quietly. "The seal. I took the dream."

"I know," the girl said. "Mama recognized your handwriting on the sign. She says you owe us interest."

Perrin looked at the child — fierce, certain, standing on tiptoe to see over his counter — and felt the whole shape of his new life arrive at once, the way weather arrives over a ridge.

"Interest," he repeated. "What does your mama suggest?"

"She says you should teach me to guard my own dreams, so nobody takes them again. And you should tell my brother that possession is nine-tenths of *nothing*, because Papa made that up and Papa also says the moon is cheese."

"Your papa sounds like a man of range."

"He's an idiot," she agreed comfortably. "Well?"

Perrin broke the seal. The dream rose out of the jar like breath in winter — a shimmer of wings and water and wind that smelled of reeds — and he held it out to her cupped in both hands, careful as a coal.

"This is yours," he said. "It was always yours. I'm sorry I took it."

She took it, and drank it in the way children drink dreams, whole, and her eyes went wide and far, and then she grinned at him with a gap where a tooth had been, and ran out the door shouting for her brother, presumably to gloat.

Perrin sat down on his stool behind the counter and put his face in his hands and laughed until the feathers shook loose again.

* * *

By the time the evening star came up, the shop had seen eleven customers. A farmer reclaimed his wedding day and wept into his hat. An old man brought back a memory he'd bought secondhand and turned out not to want — somebody else's first snowfall, which had never fit him right. Two sisters came in together, argued for ten minutes about whose memory of their grandmother's kitchen it was, and left sharing it, each carrying half, which Perrin privately considered the best transaction he'd ever witnessed and he'd once watched a Ferryman give away a summer for free.

Word traveled. In a valley built on locked jars, word traveled fast.

He was closing up — turning the sign, banking the lamp — when he heard wingbeats on the roof, and then footsteps, and Gideon came around the corner with Adaeze beside her, the two of them so alike now that strangers did double-takes, so different that friends never did.

"We heard there's a desk," Adaeze said, "where things get given back."

"There is. It's closed till morning."

"We're not customers." Gideon set a bundle on the counter — heavy, wrapped in sacking, and even through the cloth Perrin could feel it: the particular cold weight of the mountain's making, jars that had sat in galleries of purchased selves. "These came out of the vault. We've matched most of them to families in the outer villages. But these four—" she unwrapped the top of the bundle, and lamplight caught glass "—these are valley names. Valley people. Sold south decades ago, before any of us knew the court existed. We can't find who they belong to. Nobody remembers them missing."

Perrin looked at the jars. Four small lights, patient in the dark, waiting the way buried seeds wait.

"So we brought them to the only shop in the world," Adaeze said, "that gives things back for free."

Perrin untied the sacking the rest of the way. Four jars. Four names, etched in the glass in the court's thin, legal hand. He read them twice, and on the third reading his breath stopped, because the last name on the last jar was written in a different script — older, hurried, pressed hard into the glass as if the writer had been afraid of running out of time.

He knew that handwriting. He'd have known it anywhere, asleep, drowning, upside down.

It was his sister's.

"Perrin?" Gideon said. "What is it?"

He turned the jar toward the lamp, toward them, his heart going like a trapped bird. "This one," he said. "This one wasn't bought from the valley."
