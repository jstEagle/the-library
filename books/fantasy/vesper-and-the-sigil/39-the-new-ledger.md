## 39. The New Ledger

The valley did not change when we came back to it. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that undid me a little every morning for a month.

The same crooked fences. The same smell of woodsmoke and wet stone. The same elders who had once demanded I burn a map now nodding at me in the lane as though I'd only been away buying flour. The valley was built to forget, and it had forgotten us so thoroughly that our return looked, to it, like nothing more than two women walking home.

Adaeze stopped in the middle of the road on our first evening back and turned a slow circle, her arms out, her face tilted up.

"It's quiet," she said. "In here." She tapped her temple. "I didn't know quiet could feel like this. Like a room after everyone's gone to bed."

"You'll get used to it," I said. "Or you won't, and you'll complain about it for the rest of your life, which is also an option."

"I'm going to complain about it," she decided. "It seems healthy."

She was thinner than she should have been, my sister — the word still fit strangely in my mouth, like a shoe worn on the wrong foot that was nonetheless unmistakably mine. Twelve moons beneath the mountain, fed on slowly by a court of masked accountants, and she came out of it laughing at the valley's silence. I have thought since about where she found that. I think she found it in the same place I found everything: in deciding what to keep.

* * *

Perrin's shop opened on the fourth day, though "opened" is generous. He strung a sign across the door of Widow Sefu's old smokehouse — she had insisted, and nobody argues with Sefu twice — and the sign read:

**STOLEN THINGS RETURNED. NO CHARGE. NO QUESTIONS. DON'T MAKE ME REGRET THIS.**

The last line was in smaller letters, added later, in a different hand. Adaeze swore she hadn't done it. I believed her, mostly because the handwriting was suspiciously neat and Adaeze wrote like a spider falling downstairs.

"Free of charge," I said, reading it over his shoulder while he pretended to fix the hinge. "You? Free of charge?"

"I'm a changed bird." He tested the door. It swung with a satisfied creak. "Also, Sefu threatened to feed me nothing but bread crusts until I agreed, and I've seen what she calls bread."

The first customer came before noon. A farmer named Osei, who stood in the doorway holding his hat like it might bolt, and asked, very quietly, if it was true that things taken from him could be given back.

"What did you lose?" Perrin asked, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before — not the magpie's flippancy, not the guide's wariness. Something gentler. His sister's voice, maybe, living on in him.

"My mother's voice," Osei said. "Singing. A man came through years ago, bought it off me when the winter was bad and my children were hungry. I don't regret selling it. But some nights I can't remember how the song went, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I'd like it back. If it's not too much trouble."

Perrin looked at me. I looked at the ledger on the counter — a new one, blank, its first page still crisp. We had agreed on a system the night before: names in one column, what was owed in the other, and no column for payment, because payment was the whole point of never having any again.

"We'll need to find where it went," I said. "Sold memories move. They get resold, traded up, bottled and rebottled. But they leave traces. And I know the traces."

Osei's face did something complicated — hope and fear wrestling in a small space — and he thanked us four times on his way out the door, forgetting his hat twice.

When he was gone, Perrin leaned against the counter and exhaled through his teeth.

"One down," he said. "Approximately ten thousand to go."

"Twelve thousand," I said. "I counted the galleries."

"Don't say numbers like that to me before lunch."

* * *

Teaching Adaeze the valley took longer than teaching her the mountain ever could have, because the valley keeps its knowledge in people instead of jars.

We walked it together in the weeks after our return, and I showed her the things that mattered. The spring that ran cold even in high summer, where Sefu said you should drink if you'd been crying, though no one knew why and Sefu refused to invent a reason. The hillside where the elders buried their disagreements — literally, in a little ceremony involving a shovel and a great deal of dignity — rather than let them sour above ground. The gap in the eastern fence that every child in the valley used and no adult acknowledged, because acknowledging it would mean fixing it, and fixing it would mean admitting you'd once been young enough to use it.

"And this?" Adaeze asked, standing at the rim itself, the place where the world went magic-dead. She held her hand out past the boundary line the way you'd test bathwater. Nothing happened, of course. That was the point of the valley. Magic pooled up against it like water against glass and found no way in.

"That's the rule," I said. "No magic follows you here. Whatever you were out there, whatever has a claim on you — it stops at the rim."

"But you broke the rule." She said it without accusation. She'd earned the right to say it plainly; we had both spent enough moons being careful with each other. "You carried me across. The court's claim was still on me, half-faded but real, and you brought it over the line anyway."

"I did."

"How?"

I had asked myself the same question on the bone stair, on the mountainside, in the dark of the first week home when I woke at odd hours certain the ledger had followed us. The answer, when I finally let myself hold it, was simple in the way terrible things are simple.

"The valley cannot follow what refuses to be followed," I said. "That's the other half of the rule nobody says out loud. The magic-deadness isn't a wall. It's an agreement. Everything outside agrees to stop at the rim. But an agreement takes two parties. If what you carry refuses to belong to them — truly refuses, all the way down — then there's nothing for the valley to exclude. You're already not theirs."

Adaeze considered this for a long moment, watching the light pool against the invisible boundary like gold against glass.

"So the way to break a rule," she said, "is to stop being the person it applies to."

"Now you understand my entire life."

She laughed — my sister laughed, head back, unguarded, a sound the court had tried for twelve moons to tax out of her and failed — and looped her arm through mine, and we walked back down toward the smokehouse, where Perrin was almost certainly losing an argument with the second customer of the day.

* * *

The work of returning was slower than the work of taking. I had always known that, in the abstract. Theft is efficient; restoration is a craft.

A stolen memory doesn't come back clean. It comes back with edges — the shape of the jar it sat in, the fingerprints of everyone who handled it. When I gave Osei his mother's song, he wept for an hour, and then he frowned, and then he said, "There's something else in here. Something that isn't mine."

He was right. Bottled in alongside the lullaby was a sliver of someone else's memory — a kitchen, a woman's hands kneading dough, a window with rain on it. Some previous owner's fragment, packed in as filler by whatever broker had handled the sale. The mountain did that. The whole trade did that. Nothing stayed pure once it became property.

"Do you want it out?" I asked.

Osei wiped his face and thought about it, really thought about it, the way people in the valley had apparently forgotten how to do with their own pasts.

"No," he said finally. "Let it stay. Whoever she is, she kneads bread the way my mother folded laundry. It feels like company."

So we amended the ledger. There was a third column now, narrow, penciled: *returned with guests.* Perrin called it the strangest accounting he'd ever seen. I called it honest.

Some returns were harder. A woman came wanting back her wedding day, sold during a bad year, and when I found it — three brokers downstream, half its color gone — she took one look at the restored version and asked me to take it away again. "That's not how it happened," she said. "He smiled more than that. He *smiled*, I know he did." And maybe he had, and the bottling had flattened him, or maybe grief had enlarged him, and there was no way left to check. In the end she chose to keep the flattened version, because it was hers, and hers was the only authority that counted. I wrote that lesson into the margin of the ledger in letters big enough that Perrin couldn't pretend not to see it: *What people remember is theirs, even when it's wrong.*

"You're going to make a philosopher yet," he told me.

"I'm going to make you alphabetize this ledger if you keep talking."

* * *

On the night of the new moon — the first true new moon since the thirteenth turn completed, since the mountain's lights went out gallery by gallery behind us like a house closing its eyes — Adaeze and I climbed to the rim with a basket between us.

Not jars. Not anymore. Cuttings.

Sefu's idea, though she'd deny the sentimentality of it. The valley grew a hardy rosemary along the southern slopes, and Sefu had declared that anyone doing penance should plant something, because "you can't undo a taking, but you can start a growing, and the two of you have a lot of starting to do."

We planted along the boundary line, a dozen cuttings spaced like fence posts, and Adaeze pressed the soil down around each one with the seriousness of someone who had spent twelve moons learning exactly what it costs when things are not tended.

"The court kept ledgers," she said quietly, patting the earth flat. "Everything entered, everything balanced. Debts and payments and sureties. I used to hear the clerks through the wall. Quills, all day, all night. Thousands of quills." She sat back on her heels. "This feels like the opposite of that."

"It is a ledger," I said. "Just a different kind. Things written in dirt instead of ink. Debts paid forward instead of collected." I brushed soil off my hands. "My mother kept a ledger too, in her way. Every entry a grief. I'm trying to learn her handwriting and then write something better in it."

Adaeze bumped her shoulder against mine. "You're getting better at saying her name."

"I'm getting better at a lot of things I used to be terrible at." I looked east, where the first thin edge of the moon-to-come wouldn't show for another night yet. "Give me a few more years."

We walked back in the dark, and I did not flinch at the river sound in the distance, because the Ferryman had returned my summer to me months ago and I had spent the whole of it carefully, deliberately, relearning why I loved water: the sound of it under bridges, the cold of it on ankles, the way it carries things downstream without asking them where they're going.

* * *

Winter came early that year, politely, the way valley winters do. Perrin's shop filled up.

Word travels strangely in a valley built to forget — it travels anyway, underground, through bread and gossip and the particular telegraphy of women who have decided something matters. By midwinter there was a queue most mornings, and Perrin, who had once resented owing a single favor to a single dead woman, now owed patience to half the valley and paid it daily without complaint, or with complaint, which amounted to the same thing from him.

They came with their losses laid out like coins. A grandfather who'd sold his first love to pay a debt of honor. A midwife missing the memory of every birth she'd attended — taken as a set, decades ago, by a buyer who liked them "fresh." A boy of fifteen who had sold a single afternoon, one golden ordinary afternoon, for bread, and wanted it back so badly he'd walked eleven miles in the snow.

I found the boy's afternoon in the third hour of searching. It came back to him whole, and he held it the way you hold a bird — gently, astonished, afraid to close your hands.

"Why do you do this?" he asked me. "For free. Everyone says you used to charge."

"I used to take," I said. "Taking and charging are different trades. I'm retired from both." And then, because he was fifteen and deserved honesty: "Because everything I am was given to me by people who didn't have to give it. A stranger's map. A widow's bread. A sister's half of everything she remembers. You pass it forward or you admit it meant nothing."

He nodded solemnly, the way fifteen-year-olds nod, which is to say completely and for about four seconds, and then he was out the door and gone, carrying his afternoon home through the snow.

Perrin appeared at my elbow with two cups of something hot and questionable.

"You know," he said, "my sister stole that map because she believed the court could be beaten. She never got to see it happen. Sometimes I think—" He stopped, blew on his cup, started over. "Sometimes I think the shop is the part she'd like best. Not the mountain coming down. This. The un-taking. One jar at a time."

"She'd like the sign," I said. "*Don't make me regret this.*"

"She'd have written it herself."

Outside, the snow kept falling, softening the valley's crooked fences into something almost gentle, and inside, the ledger lay open on the counter with its three columns and its crowded margins, page after page of names and debts and debts forgiven, and I caught myself thinking — not for the first time — that my mother would have recognized the handwriting.

* * *

Spring found Adaeze strong again, and restless, the way the valley makes newcomers restless once the quiet stops feeling like rest and starts feeling like a question.

She had begun sitting in on the returns. Watching how I traced a memory's trail through the traces it left — the residue of handling, the ghost-edges of old bottles. She learned fast. Of course she did. We were twins; fast was the family inheritance, along with stubbornness and a weakness for lullabies in minor keys.

By the time the plum trees bloomed she was running returns herself, small ones, uncomplicated ones, her hands steady on the jars, her voice gentle with the grieving. She had a gift for it I never had. Where I approached a broken memory like a lock to pick, she approached it like a hurt animal — patient, unhurried, willing to sit beside it for hours until it decided to trust her.

"You could teach this," I told her one evening, watching her coax a soldier's drowned comrades back into his keeping, one careful image at a time.

"So could you."

"I steal better than I teach."

"You *stole* better than anyone," she corrected. "Past tense. You're a different person now. You made yourself one." She glanced up, and there was something in her face — the expression she wore when she was about to say a thing she'd been carrying for days. "Which reminds me. There's something I've been meaning to ask you. About the summer."

"The summer?"

"The one the Ferryman gave back. Your childhood summer, the one you sold at the bridge." She closed the empty jar in front of her and turned to face me fully. "You've never shown it to me. You've shown me the kitchen, the shoes, the lullaby. Everything from the jar Mama sealed. But not the summer."

I hadn't noticed I was avoiding it. That's the trouble with a self you've assembled by hand — the gaps don't announce themselves. You just find yourself walking around a certain room in your own house.

"It's mine," I said. "The only thing I've ever owned outright. Everything else was given or taken or chosen under duress. That summer I bought back with somebody else's grace. I'm allowed one thing that's just mine."

"Of course you are." Adaeze reached across the table and squeezed my wrist. "I wasn't asking to take it. I was asking because—" She hesitated, and my sister, who had faced a masked court and refused eternity, hesitated. "Because I've been remembering something lately. Since I got my strength back. A fragment, from before. Two girls, a river, a summer. And in my version, one of them is afraid of the water and the other one teaches her to swim. And I can't tell which girl I was."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, Perrin was arguing cheerfully with the evening, and the plum blossoms were coming down through the blue dusk like slow snow.

"In mine," I said slowly, "one of them is afraid of the water."

Adaeze's breath caught. "Then we had the same summer."

"From opposite ends of it."

We sat with that a while, the two halves of a season between us, each of us holding an edge of it, neither of us holding the middle. And I understood — the way you understand things late and all at once — that this was the work now. Not the ledger on the counter, not the jars and columns and names. This: two sisters trading pieces of a shared past across a table, stitching a childhood together out of each other's scraps, until between us we had something whole enough to hand forward.

"There will be more," Adaeze said softly. "Fragments. Mine surfacing, yours surfacing. Years of them, probably. Whole seasons we'll have to rebuild together, piece by piece."

"I know."

"And some nights it'll hurt."

"I know that too."

She smiled then, tired and bright, and pushed back her chair. "Good. I'd hate to be bored."

* * *

Later — much later, when Adaeze had gone to bed and Perrin had locked the shop and the valley lay silent under a sky scrubbed clean by wind — I stepped outside alone and looked up.

The moon was a fingernail paring, three nights old, the first moon of the new count. Our moon. Under it, the rosemary cuttings along the rim had taken root, every one, twelve small green declarations growing in a straight line against the dark.

And in the west, low and early, just clearing the ridge — a brighter light. Not a star quite yet. An evening star, arriving ahead of schedule, the way important things do.

I stood in the cold and watched it, and felt something turn over in me, quiet as a page.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I would tell Adaeze about the summer. All of it. Both ends of the river, hers and mine, stitched into one.

But tonight — tonight there was a garden to plan, and a ledger with blank pages left, and an evening star climbing the dark like the first line of a story neither of us had written yet.
