## 40. Vesper

The first jar went into the ground on an evening so mild it felt like an apology from the sky.

Gideon knelt in the dirt behind Widow Sefu's house — *her* house now, though she still caught herself knocking at her own door some mornings, a habit of a life when nothing had been hers to enter — and pressed the little clay vessel down into the earth with both hands. It was heavier than it looked. Grief usually was.

"That's the last of them," Adaeze said behind her. She was sitting on the upturned wheelbarrow with her ankles crossed, a trowel balanced across her knees like a scepter she hadn't quite decided to claim. "Thirty-one jars. I counted twice."

"You counted four times."

"I counted four times," Adaeze conceded, "because the first two times you were hovering and I lost my place."

"I wasn't hovering."

"Sister." Adaeze said the word the way other people said *darling* or *you fool*, depending on the hour. "You have been hovering since the equinox. It's all right. Hovering is one of your better qualities. Right after brooding and right before dramatic entrances."

Gideon smiled despite herself, which was, she had learned over the past season, simply what happened around Adaeze. Her face did things without consulting her. It was disorienting and wonderful, like weather.

She looked down at the jar in her hands before covering it. Through the clay she could feel the faint warmth of what it held — a widow's grief, the same grief she had stolen in another life, back when she'd been a thief with steady hands and no idea whose hands they were. She had spent months returning what the mountain took. This jar was different. This one she had taken herself, fair and square, in the old dishonest way, and returning it felt like paying a debt to a person who had long ago forgiven her for it.

Sefu had forgiven her with bread, actually. That was how forgiveness worked in this valley: flour, water, salt, patience.

"Ready?" Adaeze asked.

Gideon tipped the jar's contents into the hole — not the clay itself; Sefu had asked to keep that, to plant something *in* — just the memory, poured out like dark honey into the earth. For a moment the air above the garden smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's kitchen, a kitchen where a woman had once wept for a husband, and then the scent faded, settling downward, going home.

They covered it with soil. Adaeze patted it flat with the trowel, twice, firmly, as if tucking it in.

"What are we planting over this one?" Gideon asked.

"Rosemary." Adaeze didn't hesitate. "For remembrance. Obviously. I know you'll say it's sentimental."

"I was going to say it's perfect."

Adaeze blinked at her. "Who are you and what have you done with my sister?"

"The sister you knew is buried under a mountain," Gideon said mildly, reaching for the rosemary starts. "The one you've got now is entirely new construction. Very shoddy foundations. Complains constantly."

"Complains constantly," Adaeze agreed solemnly, "but has excellent taste in sisters."

* * *

The garden had begun, as most dangerous things do, with Perrin.

He had arrived at the valley in the autumn with his sister's map wrapped in oilcloth and a face like a man attending his own funeral out of politeness. He had buried the map under the rowan tree by the well — Gideon had helped, because some griefs go easier with company — and then he had announced, in the tone of someone daring the world to argue, that he was staying.

"You could open a shop," Sefu had suggested, in the gentle way she suggested things, which meant they were already decided.

"A shop," Perrin had repeated flatly.

"Returning stolen memories. Free of charge."

"I'm a magpie, madam. Magpies don't give things back. It's practically anatomical."

But he had opened the shop anyway, in the front room of Sefu's house, with a sign Gideon painted for him that read **FOUND THINGS RETURNED** in letters she'd made deliberately crooked, because Perrin claimed straight letters were "a form of lying." People came from three valleys over now, walking the rim road with their empty hands and their hollow seasons, and Perrin would sit behind the counter with his black-and-white feathers ruffled into something almost respectable, matching faces to jars, handing people back their weddings, their childhoods, their dead.

He complained about it every single day.

He had not missed a single day.

*It suits you,* Gideon had told him once, watching him press a jar of a fisherman's drowned brother into the man's hands with unexpected tenderness.

*I am being slowly devoured by respectability,* he'd replied. *When I die, bury me face down so the other magpies can't see what I became.*

And then, one gray morning in late winter, he had come stomping into the kitchen with dirt on his feathers — actual dirt, which for Perrin constituted a personal crisis — and dropped a burlap sack of bulbs on the table.

"There's a field behind the house," he said. "Full of holes where you two dug up the court's jars to return them. The land looks chewed. It's depressing. I refuse to look at it daily."

"So fix it," Adaeze had said, not looking up from her tea.

"With *what?*"

"Gardens need seeds," Gideon had said slowly, an idea arriving the way ideas always arrived for her now — not stolen, not bartered, but grown, quietly, from compost of everything she'd chosen to keep. "And we happen to be sitting on thirty years of returned memories. Every jar that comes through your shop, Perrin. Every grief, every joy, every summer somebody sold to eat. They're all going back into the valley. What do you suppose grows in soil that rich?"

Perrin had stared at her. Then at the sack of bulbs. Then at her again, with the expression of a bird realizing the shiny thing it had been eyeing was actually a mirror.

"You want to plant a garden on top of a graveyard of memories."

"I want to plant a garden *out of* one."

He had grumbled for six days. He had also, Gideon noticed, already drawn up plans for the irrigation, because magpies complain about everything except the things they love, and those they simply build.

* * *

Now it was nearly midsummer, and the evening star was about to make its first appearance of the season, and the whole valley knew it, because the whole valley had been invited.

That had been Adaeze's doing. Gideon would have kept it small — a handful of people, a quiet planting, the kind of ceremony you could hold without anyone looking at you. But Adaeze had spent thirteen moons in a vault beneath a mountain, fed upon by masked creatures who owned her soul and never once learned her name, and when she had finally walked free into the light, she had developed firm opinions about small ceremonies.

"People," she had declared, "should witness good things. Loudly. With food. Otherwise the bad things win on attendance alone."

So the valley came. Sefu came, carrying bread in quantities that suggested she expected either a feast or a famine and had prepared for both. The elders came, stiff and skeptical, and stayed, soft and skeptical, which for elders was a kind of conversion. Families whose memories had passed through Perrin's shop came, and stood awkwardly near Gideon, and some of them — not all, but some — thanked her. One old man took her hand in both of his and said, "You gave me back my daughter's laugh," and Gideon had to pretend there was dust in her eye, and Adaeze had to pretend not to notice, and both pretenses fooled no one.

Even a few strangers from beyond the rim had come. Word traveled strangely these days. The mountain's lights had gone out on the thirteenth moon, and the market towns had felt it like a tooth falling out, and stories had spread of the thief who signed away her own name to balance a ledger written in souls. Some of the stories were even true. Gideon had stopped correcting them. There was something freeing about being, in the world's telling, several different women — and knowing, privately, exactly which one she was.

Which was, admittedly, a work in progress.

* * *

They planted until the sun went low.

Gideon dug; Adaeze placed; Perrin supervised with the air of a foreman who trusted no one's trench-work, and Sefu moved among the rows like a blessing, pressing seeds into children's palms and showing them how deep to push. Over the jar of the widow's grief: rosemary. Over the soldier's terror, returned to him whole last spring: poppies, because Adaeze insisted that fear deserved flowers too, "so it learns what it's for." Over the baker's joy — a wedding day, sold in a famine year and bought back by his grandchildren — bee balm, which the bees loved and which smelled, faintly and forever, of celebration.

Row by row, the chewed field behind the house became a garden. And underneath it, layer by layer, the valley's returned past settled into the earth like sediment becoming stone.

Gideon paused at one particular spot, near the eastern fence, where the soil was darkest.

She knew what was under it. A jar with no label. She had buried it herself, weeks ago, alone, at dusk — the copy the court had kept of her, the jar bearing her own name, the self she had been before she chose to stop being it. She could have destroyed it. Perrin had offered, cheerfully, to drop it off a cliff. Adaeze had offered, less cheerfully, to burn it. Instead Gideon had carried it home across three hundred miles and planted it, because she had decided — in one of those decisions that felt less like thinking than like setting down something heavy — that the girl she used to be deserved burial, not erasure.

You didn't get to choose what you were made of. That was the lesson of the mountain, of the contract, of her mother's terrible loving arithmetic. But you got to choose what you grew from it.

Something was sprouting over that unlabeled jar now. Small green shoots, stubborn and anonymous. Gideon crouched and touched one and couldn't name the plant, and found she didn't mind. Let it be whatever it was. She knew the feeling.

"Gideon." Adaeze stood at the garden's edge, pointing west. "Look."

Above the rim of the valley, where the mountains went blue and then violet and then gave way entirely to the coming night, the first star of evening had appeared — Vesper, the herald, the one the old songs called the opener of doors. The valley had no magic; that was its oldest law and, lately, its least relevant. But everyone looked up anyway, because some things didn't need magic, only attention.

One by one, the planting stopped. The talking stopped. Thirty-odd people stood in a half-made garden full of buried memories and watched a star arrive.

"It's funny," Adaeze said quietly, beside her. "I spent thirteen moons underground counting moons. Thirteen turns, the stranger said, and every one of them was a coin spent out of me. And now I count stars instead, and every one is—" She stopped, searching. "Free. Every one is free."

Gideon thought about the woman she had been a year ago. Mid-heist, quick-fingered, excellent at taking what others couldn't hold. She had believed she was empty — that the valley's silence had hollowed her, that the missing years were a wound. She had built a whole self out of competence and distance and called it a life.

It turned out she hadn't been empty. She'd been *seeded*. Everything she needed had been in her all along, sealed and waiting — her mother's apology, her sister's lullaby, a stranger's map, a magpie's loyalty, bread from a widow who should have hated her. You couldn't steal any of it. That was the thing she finally understood, kneeling in the warm dirt with the evening star climbing: none of it could be taken, because none of it was ever anyone's possession. It only grew, or it didn't. And what you tended, you kept.

"Penny for them," Perrin said, materializing at her elbow with the unerring timing of a bird who had learned that sentiment was easiest to interrupt mid-bloom.

"They're not for sale," Gideon said.

His feathers rippled with delight. "Look at you. A year ago you'd have named a price."

"A year ago I was an idiot with excellent lockpicks."

"We all were, darling. Some of us just had better tailoring." He surveyed the garden — the rows, the mounded earth, the children with dirt to their elbows — and his voice, when it came again, had lost its polish. "My sister would have liked this. She stole a map to save one girl. You turned it into—" he gestured at all of it, the whole improbable green arithmetic of the place, "—*this*. I keep waiting to feel jealous and it hasn't arrived. Deeply suspicious."

"She'd have liked the shop best," Gideon said. "All those shiny things going back where they belong. It's the most magpie-rebellious thing I've ever seen."

"Don't tell anyone," Perrin said. "I have a reputation." And he stalked off toward the bread, muttering, but his feathers were smooth and settled, and Gideon saw him pause at the rowan tree by the well — just a moment, just a touch of his wing against the trunk, where the map slept — before he let himself be absorbed into the crowd.

* * *

Full dark came gently. Lanterns bloomed along the garden rows. Sefu's bread made its rounds, and someone produced a fiddle, and the evening loosened into music and low laughter, the particular sound of people who had all, in one way or another, gotten something back.

Gideon and Adaeze ended up sitting side by side on the low wall at the garden's edge, shoulder to shoulder, sharing the last heel of bread the way they shared most things now — without ceremony, without accounting, each keeping track of nothing.

"Do you remember the lullaby?" Adaeze asked. "Our mother's?"

"Some of it. The middle verse is gone. It went with the name, I think." Gideon said it without flinching, which surprised her. Losses had edges now instead of teeth. "Teach it to me?"

"You'll have to sing it badly first. That's the traditional method."

"I'm aware. I've heard you cook."

Adaeze laughed — the real one, the unguarded one, the sound the court had tried to eat and failed — and began to hum, low, under the fiddle. Gideon listened, and then joined in, badly, exactly as instructed, and the middle verse came back between them line by line, assembled out of two imperfect memories, neither one sufficient alone, together somehow whole.

*That's how it works,* Gideon thought, watching moths rise silver through the lantern light. *That's how anything works.*

Below them, the garden breathed. Somewhere under the rosemary, a widow's grief was becoming fragrance. Under the poppies, a soldier's fear was learning its purpose. Under the eastern fence, the unnamed green shoots pushed patiently upward out of the buried girl Gideon used to be, feeding on her, honoring her, becoming whatever they chose.

A child ran past — one of the miller's girls, dirt-legged, radiant — and skidded to a stop in front of them, holding up a trowel like a trophy.

"I planted one! Widow Sefu says it's marigolds and they'll be gold by harvest!"

"Then you'd better come back at harvest," Adaeze said gravely, "to check its work."

"I will!" the girl promised, and thundered off, and Gideon watched her go and felt something settle in her chest that had no name yet and didn't need one. Not everything had to be labeled. She of all people knew what the labels cost.

The evening star climbed higher. The music played. Tomorrow there would be jars to return, and letters to answer from towns wanting shops of their own, and Perrin would complain about all of it, and the valley would stay stubbornly, blessedly silent of magic while filling, year by year, with something better.

Gideon broke the last of the bread and handed half to her sister.

She had spent her whole first life learning to take. She intended to spend this one learning what she could grow.

And in the dark soil beneath her feet, patient as a promise, the garden went on remembering for them all.
