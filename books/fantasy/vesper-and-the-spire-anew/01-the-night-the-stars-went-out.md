## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

Petra Marchetti had been counting other people's sins for eleven years, and they had opinions about the filing system.

"Third shelf," they said, not looking up from the ledger. "Bottled weather goes with bottled weather. You cannot shelve a monsoon next to a grief, Sefa, no matter how much you argue they're the same thing in the end."

"They *are* the same thing," said Sefa from atop the ladder, dripping rain onto the floorboards. The girl was seventeen, all elbows and conviction, holding a jar of storm that kept trying to hail inside its glass. "One falls on you when you least expect it and ruins your whole week."

"One is inventory. One is a metaphor. Metaphors don't get logged at cost."

"You log everything at cost. You once tried to put a price on a second chance."

"And I stand by that valuation." Petra dipped their pen. "Depreciates fast if unused."

It was audit night at the Vesper Guild, which meant the lamps burned late and everyone pretended this was normal work instead of what it actually was: an annual reckoning with every impossible thing the guild had ever bought, sold, stolen back, or quietly refused to sell to someone who clearly shouldn't have it. Stolen names, filed under N. Bottled weather, filed under W, against Sefa's ongoing campaign to reclassify it as emotional damage. Second chances, filed under S, though half of them had been redeemed so long ago that Petra only kept the entries out of sentiment, and sentiment, Petra would tell you, was the one thing in the building that didn't depreciate.

The guildhouse itself sat in the crook of the Vesper river like a cat that had decided this spot was fine, actually. Three floors of crooked shelves, a cellar full of things that hummed when the moon was wrong, and above it all the old border wards — thin silver threads stitched through the roof beams by some founder whose name even the ledgers only half-remembered. On quiet nights you could hear them sing. Petra had stopped hearing it years ago, the way you stop hearing a clock.

They were on page four hundred and twelve — *one (1) name, 'Marisol,' returned to owner, condition: slightly worn* — when the singing stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Like a hand clapped over a bell.

Petra's pen dragged a line of ink across the page before they caught themselves. "Sefa. Did you feel that?"

"Feel wh—"

The second thing happened before she could finish the word, which was fortunate for her sentence but unfortunate for everything else.

Every star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out*, all of them, in the same instant, as if the sky were a lamp and someone had found the switch. The dark beyond the windows went absolute and close, pressing against the glass like something curious.

Sefa came down the ladder faster than any ladder should legally permit. "That's — okay. That's not weather. That's not anything we stock."

"No," Petra said. Their voice came out steadier than their hands felt. "No, it is not."

Then the wards shattered. All of them, at once, a sound like a thousand small bells breaking underwater, and the silver threads in the beams unraveled into nothing — not falling, not burning, just *unwriting themselves*, thread by thread, until there was no evidence they'd ever been there at all except the faint smell of ozone and the way the air tasted afterward, like the moment after lightning.

And then came the third thing. The worst thing. Though it took them until dawn to understand how bad it was.

* * *

The messenger arrived at the door near midnight, soaked, wild-eyed, and speaking too fast. A river-warden named Oskar who traded the guild honest news for honest whiskey and had never once lied to them, which in Petra's professional experience made him nearly unique among government men.

"The maps are wrong," he said. "All of them. I came past the surveyor's office and the new charts — the ones printed last month — the country's gone. Not redrawn. *Gone*. There's just river, and then empire, and then more empire, and where our capital should be there's a note in the margin that says *uncharted — marshland*."

"That's absurd," said Petra. "We're standing in it."

"I know! I told them! They looked at me like I was mad, Petra. They looked at me the way you look at a man who insists his house is haunted because he's standing in the house." He gripped the doorframe. "And the letters — the post riders came back with everything returned. Every letter sent out in the last week, returned unopened, stamped *no such address*. And the stamps—" His voice cracked. "The stamps are wrong. The seal on the wax has changed. It's always been the twin rivers. Now it's just... a blank circle. Like someone forgot to finish drawing us."

Petra felt something tilt beneath them, some floorboard of the world giving way, and did what they always did with feelings that couldn't be shelved: set them aside for later and reached for the ledger.

"Oskar. Look at me. We are here. You are wet, which is proof of local weather. Say the country's name."

Oskar opened his mouth.

And nothing came out.

He stood there working his jaw like a man chewing on a word that had been stolen off his tongue mid-bite. His eyes went wide and then wider. "I — it's — there was a name. There was definitely a name. I've said it ten thousand times. I swore an oath on it. I can't — Petra, I can't find it."

The vertigo hit Petra then, properly, the way the plan had promised: not fear exactly, but the ground forgetting which direction down was. Because Petra reached for the name too, the way you reach for your own street, your own mother's face, the shape of a thing so familiar it doesn't need remembering — and found the same hole. Smooth-sided. Recent. A socket where a tooth used to be.

Outside, the night was starless and getting worse. Down the lane, lights were coming on in windows, and voices were rising, and somewhere a woman was screaming a question over and over that none of her neighbors could answer, because none of them could answer it either.

But the ledger lay open on the desk. Page four hundred and twelve. *One (1) name, 'Marisol,' returned to owner.* And above it, three hundred pages back, in Petra's own stubborn handwriting, in iron-gall ink that had outlasted two floods and one fire:

*Received into inventory, spring audit: the borders of Vesper, warded and singing.*

The name was right there. In their own hand. Whole. Unbroken. Real.

Petra read it aloud, slowly, twice, and watched Oskar's face come back from wherever it had gone — watched him mouth it, catch it, hold it like a man catching water in cupped hands. "Yes," he whispered. "*Yes.* That's it. That's — how do you still have it?"

"Ink," Petra said. "On paper. Kept by someone tedious."

* * *

By the time the bells rang the hour after midnight — the bells still worked; whatever was eating the country hadn't gotten around to bronze yet — the whole guild was awake and crowded into the counting room, and Petra stood on a stool because dignity was a luxury and being seen was not.

There was Maren, who ran acquisitions and could talk a duke out of his shadow, currently holding her own shadow's hand like it might be next. There was old Teodor, the appraiser, who had gone deaf in one ear decades ago and claimed it let him hear prices better. There was Juno the locksmith, asleep upright against a shelf of bottled fogs, because Juno treated catastrophes the way other people treated Tuesdays. And there was Sefa, front row, jaw set, eyes red-rimmed, gripping a jar of rain like a weapon.

"Here's what we know," Petra said. "The stars are out. The wards are dust. Our name has been pulled out of every map, letter, seal, and memory beyond the border — and, apparently, most within it. The empire will tell us tomorrow, very reasonably, that we were never here, and that anyone insisting otherwise is unregistered, and that unregistered persons go somewhere I don't intend to find out about."

Silence. Somewhere below, the cellar hummed, off-key.

"Now here's what else we know." Petra lifted the ledger overhead, open, like a relic or a warrant. "This book remembers. Every entry. Eleven years of my tedious, exact, insufferable handwriting, and forty years of Teodor's before me, and further back than that in hands I can't even read. The empire can burn the maps. It can't burn what it can't find, and it can't find what's written in a book it doesn't believe exists. As far as I'm concerned, this room is now the only place on earth where our country is still true."

Maren started laughing — the unhinged kind, the good kind. "So we're a country of one ledger."

"We are a country of *records*," Petra corrected. "Which means tonight we make copies. Every hand in this house writes until sunrise. Names, debts, birthdays, grievances, the recipe for Juno's pickled fog — everything. If they erase us, they'll have to do it page by page, and I promise you, I have hidden copies in places that would embarrass a saint."

Teodor raised his glass. "To tediousness."

"To tediousness," the room echoed, and someone actually cheered, and for one warm minute it almost felt survivable.

Then Sefa tugged Petra's sleeve, and her face had gone white in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Petra. My family lives across the border. Two days east." Her voice was very small. "If the name is gone from memories outside — my mother won't remember my face, will she? She won't remember *me*?"

Petra looked down at her, at the youngest runner of the Vesper Guild, and felt the floor tilt again, worse this time, because this time it wasn't their footing at stake.

"No," Petra said honestly, because Sefa deserved honesty more than comfort. "She won't. Not yet. Which means we have work to do, and not much of it can wait for sunrise."

The ledger fell open under their hands to a fresh page. At the top, in ink that suddenly mattered more than any ink ever had, Petra wrote:

*Debts owed to no one — outstanding.*
