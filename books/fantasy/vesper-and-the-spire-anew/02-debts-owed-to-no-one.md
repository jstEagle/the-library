## 2. Debts Owed to No One

Petra had kept the Vesper Guild's ledgers for eleven years, and in that time they had learned one iron law of impossible commerce: everything sells, eventually, to someone.

The question at dawn — or what passed for dawn now, under a sky like a closed eyelid — was not whether the inventory would sell, but how fast, and for what.

They laid it all out on the long table in the counting room while the lamps burned low and the guild gathered around like mourners at a very strange funeral. Bottled weather first: a jar of the last honest thunderstorm anyone had recorded, a vial of sea fog off a coast that no longer existed on any map but theirs, and the small stoppered flask labeled *Tuesday's rain, regrettable* that nobody had ever wanted and nobody would ever throw away.

Then the harder things. A stolen name, folded small, belonging to a man who'd sold it to pay a gambling debt and never came back for it. A second chance, slightly used, still warm. A key that opened any door exactly once, which was worse than useless now, when every door might be the last one.

"We are not selling the key," said Marisol, who ran acquisitions and had once fought a customs officer over a shipment of bottled dusk.

"We're selling *everything*," Petra said. "That's the point."

"The key is sentimental."

"Marisol. The stars went out. Sentiment is a luxury we can invoice later."

Marisol muttered something about accountants having no poetry, which Petra chose to take as a compliment, because they had, in fact, written a poem once, and it had been about depreciation schedules.

By midmorning the buyers came. Word traveled strangely now — slower, furtive, passed mouth to mouth by people who shouldn't have remembered this country existed but did, stubbornly, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. A cartographer's agent paid an obscene sum for the fog off the vanished coast, because he wanted proof he hadn't dreamed it. A widow bought the thunderstorm and held the jar against her chest like a child. The used second chance went to a young man who didn't ask what it had been spent on before, and Petra didn't tell him, and wrote the sale in the ledger with a hand that only shook a little.

The stolen name, though. The stolen name sat there all day, unsold.

"Someone will want it," Sefa said, appearing at Petra's elbow the way she did, soundless as dust.

"It's not ours to profit from," Petra said. "It's evidence. Proof that names can be taken and survive the taking." They looked at her, and she looked back, steady, and Petra understood without either of them saying it whose face that name might someday stand in for. "We keep it. Mark it *not for sale*. Some entries aren't transactions, Sefa. They're promises."

Sefa nodded and went back to hauling crates, and Petra turned to the ledger, and beneath the morning's tallies wrote the day's true balance:

*Inventory liquidated: enough. Not enough. Enough.*

* * *

Sefa had run messages for the guild since she was nine, and in six years she had learned every alley, every rooftop gap, every door that stuck in winter. She had also learned, three days ago, standing in her mother's doorway with rain in her boots, that her mother no longer knew her face.

Her mother had been kind about it. That was the worst part. She'd offered soup to a stranger, apologized for the confusion, said her daughter was away at school, wasn't she? Such a clever girl. And Sefa had stood there holding her own name behind her teeth like a stone, because she understood even then that if she said it aloud — *Mama, it's me, it's Sefa* — the word would slide off her mother like water off glass, and then it would be gone from both of them forever.

So she'd eaten the soup. It was good soup. She'd complimented it, and her mother had beamed, and Sefa had left before the crying started, and she had not cried on the rooftops, because runners don't cry on rooftops, it fogs the vision and ruins the landing.

Now she ran again, through streets where the lamplighters worked by habit alone, carrying the guild's summons to the scattered members: *Come in. All hands. We're leaving.*

She found Old Tam mending nets that caught nothing anymore, since the river had forgotten its fish. She found the twins, Brannoc and Bell, who forged documents so good that the empire's own wardens had once accepted them, sitting in their shop with the shutters open and their finest forgery half-finished on the desk: a set of imperial writs declaring the two of them registered persons, born elsewhere, owed elsewhere.

"You're coming," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. Runners deliver facts.

Bell looked up. "We could stay," she said. "These papers would hold. Probably. For a while."

"And live as people we invented," Brannoc said, "in a country that doesn't exist, working for an empire that erased us." He tapped the forgery once, neatly, and began feeding it into the stove. "I've done better work. I refuse to be my own masterpiece."

Sefa grinned despite everything. "Petra says bring your seals. Real ones."

* * *

The wardens arrived at dusk, which Petra considered rude, since dusk was when the tea got good.

There were six of them at the guild door, gray-cloaked, faces smooth as unwritten paper, and the one in front carried a scroll that unrolled itself with a sound like a sigh. Behind them, in the street, a wagon waited with a cage built for cargo, not people.

"By authority of the Registry," the warden read, "all unregistered persons within this structure are remanded for processing. Registered persons may depart freely."

Petra leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, and let the silence do some work first. Eleven years of negotiating with people who wanted impossible things had taught them that most power was bluff wearing a uniform.

"Registered according to whom?" Petra asked pleasantly. "Your empire's maps burned our country off them. Your registries forgot our street addresses. By your own paperwork, warden, none of us exist — which means you have no jurisdiction over ghosts." They smiled. "We're happy to haunt you, though. Free of charge."

The warden's smooth face did something almost like a frown. "Compliance will be noted."

"Noted where?" said Marisol, appearing at Petra's shoulder with a jar of Tuesday's regrettable rain held casually, threateningly, at hip height. "In what ledger? Written by whom?" She shook the jar gently. It sloshed with a sound like a small annoyed sea. "You people should think harder about who keeps the records."

For one strange moment the wardens simply stood there, six blank faces confronting the fact that you cannot confiscate what you have already declared imaginary. Then the lead warden rolled up the scroll, and the wagon creaked away into the dark, and Sefa — watching from the upstairs window with the twins' spyglass — reported that the cage was empty anyway, that it had always been empty, that perhaps the empire had come less to arrest them than to check whether they were still there.

"They'll be back with more than six," Petra said quietly, when Sefa relayed it.

"Then we'd better not be here," Sefa said, "when they're more."

And that, Petra thought, was the whole expedition budgeted in a single sentence.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when Petra went down to the cellar where the altar stood.

The god of oaths had been silent for years — longer, if Petra was honest, which they tried to be, professionally. Mercenaries bound by oath to a silent god: the joke had followed Petra through two wars and one bankruptcy. But the altar remained, a plain block of river stone worn smooth by eleven years of Petra's palm, and above it the old sign of the covenant, and beneath it the offering niche where coins had gone, once, when coins meant something.

The passage north cost more than the inventory had raised. The ferryman beyond the winter pass didn't take coin anymore; he took weight, he said, real weight, things that had carried prayers.

Petra stood before the altar a long time. Then they took up the chisel they'd brought, and set the edge against the stone, and hesitated only as long as anyone hesitates before breaking their own heart on purpose.

"I know you're not listening," Petra said. "You haven't listened in years. Maybe you never did. Maybe I made you out of loneliness and a good contract." The chisel bit. Stone dust rose like breath. "But if there's anything of you in this rock, then go wherever we're going. Because I'm not leaving my family behind, and I'm not leaving you either, damn you. That's the oath now. Amended. Witnessed by me."

The altar came apart in pieces no bigger than bread loaves, each one heavy with old prayer, and Petra wrapped them in cloth and packed them like cargo, and climbed the stairs with dust on their hands and their eyes burning, and found the whole guild waiting in the counting room, packs already shouldered, nobody having slept, everybody having decided.

Sefa stepped forward and put a scrap of paper in Petra's hand. On it, in her runner's quick hand: *Debts owed to no one — paid in full. New account opened: everyone, to everyone.*

"Well," Petra said, and had to clear their throat, and pretended the dust had gotten upstairs too. "Ledger's balanced. Let's go find a door."

Outside, under the starless sky, the road north waited — and far off along it, faint as frost forming, the first of the wardens' lanterns was already moving.
