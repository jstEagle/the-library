## 3. What the Door Demands

The snow came down like ash from a fire nobody had lit.

Petra Marchetti walked at the head of the column with the ledger strapped to their chest under their coat, because they had learned in the first week that if you let anyone else carry it, even for a moment, even out of kindness, you spent the next hour checking. Nine of them now, strung along a road that had stopped appearing on maps two hundred miles back. The road itself still existed — that was the strange mercy of it. Only the country had been subtracted. The stones remembered where they were supposed to be. It was everything written about them that had gone soft.

"Petra." Sefa's voice, thin and bright as a wire. She'd taken to walking beside them every morning since her family's last letter arrived addressed to *occupant*. "Tell me the inventory again."

"You have it memorized."

"I know I do. Say it anyway."

Petra sighed the sigh of an accountant who knows exactly what is being asked and why. "One jar of bottled thunderstorm, slightly shaken. Three stolen names, provenance disputed. A second chance, expired but renewable. The weather over the harbor town of Vell, sold twice by accident, which is why we no longer sell weather on consecutive days. And one guild, nine souls, all debts current."

"All debts current," Sefa repeated, satisfied, and tucked her hands back into her sleeves.

That was the ritual. Every morning since the stars went out, since the wards shattered with a sound like the sky clearing its throat and then forgetting how to sing. Every morning, the ledger read aloud, because ink was the only country they had left.

Behind them, Old Maren walked with her cane clicking a rhythm against the frozen ruts, and behind her the twins carried the strongbox between them like pallbearers who intended to argue with death about the schedule. Doss brought up the rear, as always, watching the road behind them for wardens. He hadn't slept properly in eleven days. He said he was fine. He said it in the tone of a man reading a line off a receipt he did not agree with.

The fading people were worse than the cold.

They passed a farmhouse on the fourth day where a woman stood in the doorway, waving at them, and by the time they reached her gate she had become a woman-shaped absence in the doorway, still waving, or perhaps the wind moving a curtain. Nobody said anything for half a mile. Then Maren said, quietly, "She waved," and everyone nodded, and that was the funeral.

"It works from the edges in," Petra explained that night, drawing in the frost on the inn window with one finger. They were sleeping in barns and burned-out waystations now; the empire's wardens had confiscated every registered bed in three counties. "The border went first. Then the outer villages. It eats outward — or inward, depending on how you look at it. A person fades when the last thing tying them to the record goes. Letters. Deeds. Memories across the border." They tapped the ledger through their coat. "As long as your name is in here, you're anchored. Which means I am officially the most important person in this country, and I want it noted that I always suspected as much."

Doss snorted from his corner. "You want it *entered into the record*."

"I want it entered in triplicate."

But Petra stayed up after the others slept, as they did every night, and checked each name against each breath, and hated the god who wasn't answering. The altar-breaking was done, the coin spent, the silence total. Fine, Petra told the dark. Be silent. I kept my books when you wouldn't keep yours. See who lasts longer.

* * *

They saw the Spire Anew on the twelfth day, rising out of a valley full of standing snow like a needle someone had pushed through the world's fabric to hold it together.

It was not beautiful, exactly. It was *correct*, the way a well-balanced ledger is correct — every line meeting every other line, stone and light agreeing with each other down to the last decimal. At its base stood a door. Just a door, oak and iron, absurdly ordinary, set into nothing, framed by nothing, opening onto a darkness that was not empty so much as *unwritten*.

"Well," said Maren. "It's smaller than the stories."

"The stories were written by people who needed it to be big," Petra said. "Desperation inflates everything. Look at our expense reports."

The door spoke before any of them could knock. Not aloud — it simply placed the knowledge in them, the way you suddenly remember a debt on the first of the month: *One true name per soul. Freely surrendered. Taken names will not turn the lock.*

"A true name," Maren repeated. "Not the name your mother used. Not the name on the tax rolls. The one underneath."

"The one you'd answer to if everything else burned," Petra said slowly. They knew theirs. They had known it since the night the god went silent, since the last prayer that got no reply — the small, unglamorous truth of themselves that they had never once put in writing. Accountants know what not to write down.

Sefa looked frightened. "What happens to you? After?"

"I don't know," Petra admitted. "I suspect the honest answer is: less of you. But the door says *freely*, and I've learned to respect terms and conditions."

Then Doss, from the ridge above the valley, in a voice gone flat and hard: "Wardens."

There were forty of them at least, descending the far slope in grey columns, lanterns burning a color lanterns should not burn. And at their head rode a figure Petra knew better than their own handwriting — Commander Ilse Varga, straight-backed, silver at the temples, the woman who had taught Petra that a contract was a promise with teeth.

Varga alone came forward under a white banner. She stopped fifty paces out, and her voice carried the way it always had, across parade grounds and across years.

"Marchetti. You look terrible."

"You look employed."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth — there, then disciplined away. "The empire offers amnesty. Full restoration. Your names restored to the registers, your property returned, your little guild licensed retroactively. All of it. In exchange for the ledger." Her eyes went to Petra's chest, to the shape beneath the coat. "One book, Marchetti. That's all it wants. Give it the book and everyone walks home."

"And everyone forgets," Petra said. "Everyone outside. Everyone faded stays faded. The dead stay unrecorded. We go back to being *permitted* instead of *real*." They shook their head. "You taught me to read the fine print, Commander. This offer has a graveyard in it."

Varga's jaw tightened. Behind her, the warden lines began to move.

"Then I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, which was the worst part.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way catastrophes and reconciliations do.

The wardens came down the slope. The guild formed up around the door — nine souls, one strongbox, one cane, and absolutely no tactical sense whatsoever. Maren raised her cane like a saber. One of the twins threw the strongbox lid open and hurled handfuls of impossible inventory at the advancing line: the bottled thunderstorm burst against the snow and turned the whole valley briefly, gloriously, into July; the expired second chance landed at a young warden's feet and made him weep for a life he hadn't ruined yet.

And Petra stood at the door, hand on the iron ring, and understood — with the terrible clarity of a balanced column — what the arithmetic required.

Forty wardens. Nine guild members. One door whose price was a name freely given, and which would not open for anything taken.

"Petra—" Sefa's voice, cracking.

"Ledger's in my coat," Petra said, already shrugging it off, pressing it into her arms. "Everything's current. Don't let Doss cook. He'll tell you he can cook. He cannot cook."

"Petra, *what are you doing*—"

"What any sensible accountant does when the accounts won't balance." Petra turned to the door, and laid their palm flat against the oak, and gave it the name underneath — the small, unglamorous, burning truth of themselves, the one no god had ever answered, given freely, held out like a lamp.

The door opened onto a darkness full of distant, patient stars.

"Through!" Petra shouted, holding the door wide with both hands, feeling it pull at them like tide pulls at sand. "All of you, *through*, now — Maren, take Sefa's arm — twins, stop looting the wardens, we talked about this —"

They went. Doss went swearing. Maren went laughing. Sefa went last of all, clutching the ledger, sobbing, looking back, and Petra smiled at her — the smile of a person mid-sentence —

"— and tell whoever keeps the books over there that the account isn't closed, it's only *transferred*, and a good accountant always —"

The word *always* finished without them. The door swung shut on a valley of melting snow, and on the snow lay a single line of frost that no one would ever read:

*Paid in full.*
