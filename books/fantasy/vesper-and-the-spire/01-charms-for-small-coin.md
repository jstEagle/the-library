## 1. Charms for Small Coin

The night market in Vesper began the way all good crimes begin: quietly, and with someone pretending to be doing something else.

August Coldharbour had been setting up her stall in Tanner's Row for eleven nights now, which was ten more than she liked. She'd told herself she would move on after three — that was the rule, one of her rules, and rules were the only family she had left — but the corner was good, tucked between a pie-wagon whose owner asked no questions and a shuttered chandlery whose owner, as far as anyone knew, had gone to sea or to prison or possibly both. The lantern light pooled there instead of scattering. And luck-knots sold better where people could see what they were buying.

She worked by feel, mostly. Her hands knew the knots: three strands of dyed cord, red for fever, blue for safe passage over water, green for cows — because even in a city that had outlawed magic, people still worried about their cows, and the Wardens had never once raided anyone over livestock.

That was the trick of it, if there was a trick. Never magic anyone could trace. A knot was just a knot until you decided otherwise, and deciding otherwise happened somewhere no Warden's instruments could follow — not in the cord, not in the fingers, but in the small quiet place between a seller's intention and a buyer's hope. August had spent ten years learning exactly how much of herself she could pour into a thing before it started to glow, metaphorically speaking. The answer was: less than you'd think, and more than most hedge-witches ever managed, which was why she was still free and most of them were not.

"Three coppers," she said to the woman currently turning a fever-charm over in her hands like it might bite.

"It's wool."

"It's wool *and* it's three coppers."

The woman snorted, paid, and pocketed the charm with the furtive tenderness people always used for things they half-believed in. August watched her go and felt the familiar nothing where satisfaction should have been. Eleven nights. Ten too many. She began mentally packing the stall: cord into the basket, awl into the satchel, the little tin sign reading LUCK KNOTS — FEVER CHARMS — NO REFUNDS into the fire when she next made one.

The sign had been Bram Tallow's idea, actually, back when she'd shared a stall with him down at Gullmarket, before his debts got complicated and her caution got expensive. *No refunds,* he'd said, laughing so hard he'd had to sit on a crate. *Because they'll come back, Aug. They always come back. And some of them will be angry.* He'd been right about that, as he'd been right about most things except boats, money, and women.

She did not let herself think about Bram. Thinking about people was how you stayed somewhere, and staying somewhere was how you ended up in a Warden wagon with your wrists bound in cold-iron thread, listening to a man read out your name from a ledger you'd never been allowed to have.

*Never stay anywhere long.* Rule two. Rule one was older and simpler and had been taught to her at nine years old by a grandmother who smelled of woodsmoke and panic: *never magic anyone can trace.*

There were other rules, unwritten ones, that she kept anyway. Don't charm anything for a person who looks at you too long. Don't take coin from anyone wearing grey. Don't learn customers' names, no matter how much they want to give them, because a name given is a thread tied, and threads pull.

It was a lonely way to live. She had gotten very good at calling it something else. Professionalism, say. Discipline. She wore her solitude the way other merchants wore aprons — buttoned up, wiped clean, strictly for work.

* * *

The market thickened around nine bells, when the taverns let out their first shift of drinkers and the fog came off the river smelling of tar and onions. August sold a blue passage-knot to a fishwife whose husband ran the night barge to Lowmarsh; sold a green cow-knot to a man who did not own a cow but "knew someone who might"; sold nothing at all for a long stretch to a drunk who wanted to argue about whether luck could be knotted or had to be woven, and who eventually wandered off mid-sentence, which she considered a professional victory.

Then the lanterns went out.

Not hers — hers was shielded, hooded, warded six ways from Sunday — but every open flame along Tanner's Row guttered at once, the way candles do when a door opens somewhere far away, except no doors had opened. The fog seemed to lean in. Conversations died in the middle of words.

Wardens.

They came up the row four abreast, grey coats, grey gloves, grey lanterns that burned a flame with no color in it at all. Coldfire. It didn't warm anything; it only showed things, which August had always thought was worse. At their head walked a captain she didn't know by name — tall, young-ish, with the particular stillness of a man who has learned that moving fast gets people hurt — sweeping a thin brass instrument across stall-fronts as he went. The instrument looked like a tuning fork that had been disappointed by life.

August lowered her eyes and became furniture.

This was rule three, the one nobody had taught her because nobody had survived long enough to write it down: *when the Wardens come, be boring.* Not invisible — invisibility took magic, and magic under a coldfire lantern lit up like a struck match. Boring. A tired woman selling string. Nothing here but wool and coppers and mild resentment about the weather.

The fork hummed as it passed the pie-wagon. Passed the shuttered chandlery. Came toward her stall.

Her hands, resting flat on the table, were perfectly still. Her heart was not, but hearts were allowed their opinions as long as they kept them quiet.

The fork passed over her basket of cords and gave a single faint note, like a wineglass touched by a wet finger.

August did not breathe.

The captain paused. Looked at the basket. Looked at her. Up close he had tired eyes and a jaw that looked like it had been arguing with itself for years and losing.

"Cord," she said, before he could ask, in the flat voice of a woman who has answered this question many times and resents each occasion. "Dyed. For knots. That's the whole trade." She held up a finished luck-knot between two fingers, dangling it like evidence. "You're welcome to check it for sorcery, Captain, but I'll warn you the last man who did sneezed for an hour, and I don't know if that was the wool or the magic, and frankly neither does he."

Something flickered at the corner of the captain's mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement, or indigestion; with Wardens it was impossible to say.

"The dye," he said. "Where's it from?"

"A woman off Salt Lane. Red madder, woad for the blue. You can smell it." This was true, which was the best kind of lie to build on.

He turned the fork toward the cords again. The note came again, fainter this time, and faded. Whatever the instrument heard in dyed wool, it decided it wasn't worth a report. Or perhaps the captain decided; there was a moment, brief as a moth's wingbeat, where he looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a locked door you've decided not to open tonight — and then moved on.

The lanterns relit behind them, one by one, like the street remembering how to be a street.

August exhaled slowly and discovered her hands were shaking. She folded them in her lap and waited for them to stop, and thought, with the clarity that only arrives after nearly being arrested: *eleven nights is twelve too many.*

She would move tomorrow. Upstream quarter, maybe, or across the river entirely. New name — she had a rotation of them, all dull, all forgettable: Maren Cooper, Tess Aldery, Bethan Roe. New sign, new pitch, new corner of the city to be professionally lonely in.

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the same plan she had made eleven times before, and she was already aware of its fatal flaw, which was that plans require leaving, and leaving required not looking back, and she had never once in her life managed not to look back.

* * *

Curfew bell rang at midnight, as it always did, and the market folded itself away like a card table. August packed fast and well-practiced: basket, satchel, awl, the tin sign (she'd decided against burning it; sentiment was a vice but she allowed herself small ones). The pie-wagon man tipped his hat to her without asking where she'd go, which was why she liked him.

Her room was three streets east, above a cooper's shop, reached by an outside stair that groaned in fourteen distinct dialects of complaint. She'd taken it eight days ago under the name Bethan Roe, paying a month up front, which violated her own policy of never paying more than a week anywhere, and she was already annoyed at herself for the extravagance. Rooms were not homes. Rooms were places you kept your boots.

Inside, she lit a candle — a real one, beeswax, bought honestly — and set her wards humming at the edges of the window and door. Not grand wards. Grand wards glowed, and glowing got hedge-witches hanged. These were the kind her grandmother had specialized in: wards that were mostly suggestion, a little superstition, and one thread of genuine working buried so deep in the weave that even August sometimes forgot it was there. If a Warden's fork touched that thread, it would sing. But forks didn't touch windowsills. Usually.

She ate bread and hard cheese standing up, because sitting down felt like settling in, and settling in was a gateway drug to staying. Through the window, Vesper lay under its fog with the Spire rising out of the middle of it, pale stone going gold where the coldfire lamps caught it, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful. Everyone in Vesper loved the Spire. It was on the coins, the seals, the children's primers. *Built by the Founders,* the primers said, *on empty ground, from nothing, by nothing, for everyone.*

August had grown up outside Vesper, in country where people muttered differently about the Spire, and her grandmother had never once looked at it without spitting, though she'd never said why, and then there had been no time left to ask.

Ten years in this city, and she still couldn't look at the thing without feeling like a guest who'd arrived after the argument started.

She banked the candle. She lay down in her clothes, boots by the door facing outward, because that was rule four, self-invented: *always be able to leave in the dark.*

Sleep came for her the way it always did — reluctantly, and from behind.

* * *

The knock woke her from a dream she wouldn't remember, and she was on her feet before the second knock landed, candle lit, awl in her fist like the world's least intimidating dagger.

Three streets away a clock she couldn't see was striking two. Past curfew. Nobody knocked past curfew except Wardens, and Wardens didn't knock — Wardens opened doors the way weather opened doors, with total indifference to the architecture.

But this was a knock. Small. Knuckles, not a boot. And — she tilted her head — oddly high up? No. Oddly *low*. As though whoever stood on her landing was shorter than the door expected.

August crossed the room without letting the floorboards speak and put her eye to the peephole her landlady believed was a knot in the wood.

A child stood on her stair. A boy, perhaps seven or eight, mud to the eyebrows, hair plastered flat by river-fog, wearing clothes that had been good ones maybe a year ago and had since lost an argument with the whole city. He was shivering. He was alone, at two bells past midnight, past curfew, on the outside stair of a stranger's room, and he was looking directly at the peephole as though he knew precisely where her eye was.

And her wards — her careful, humble, invisible wards — were flaring. Not singing. *Flaring*, the thread of true working in the weave pulled taut and bright as a harp string, reacting to the boy on her step the way iron reacts to a lodestone, and August felt the flare in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes, in the old scar on her palm that had never ached once in ten years and was aching now.

"No," she whispered to the room, to the boy, to the universe generally. "No, thank you. Whatever this is, I'm closed."

The boy raised his hand and knocked again — softly, politely, the way you knock on the door of someone you have traveled a very long way to find — and spoke through the wood in a language August had never heard in her life and understood anyway, the way you understand a voice heard through water, the way you understand a word from a dream:

Words that meant, or seemed to mean, or *felt* like they meant: *I knocked everywhere else. You're the last door.*

Below, in the fog-bound street, a Warden patrol turned the corner, coldfire lanterns swinging, and the boy turned his head to watch them pass with an expression no seven-year-old should own — calm, ancient, faintly sorry for them.

Their lanterns went out. All three. Pinched, like wicks between wet fingers.

The boy looked back up at the peephole, and waited.

August Coldharbour stood in the dark with her heart hammering out rule after rule after rule, and reached for the latch.
