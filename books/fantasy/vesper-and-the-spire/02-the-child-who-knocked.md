## 2. The Child Who Knocked

August Coldharbour had three rules for living in Vesper, and she recited them to herself the way other people said their prayers: never magic anyone can trace, never stay anywhere longer than a season, and never — *never* — open the door after curfew.

She was breaking the third rule before she'd even finished thinking it.

The knock had come again. Not the polite double-rap of a neighbor, not the heavy fist of the Watch. It was small, and stubborn, and repeated at intervals with the patience of someone who had nowhere else in the world to be. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

"Go away," August whispered through the door, which was, she acknowledged, an absurd thing to whisper at someone who was knocking. But curfew in the Lantern District began when the wardens' bells rang the ninth hour, and anyone on the street past it was either a warden, a criminal, or a fool, and she wanted no part of any of those categories.

Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

She pressed her eye to the gap in the shutters. The street was the color of old pewter, lit by the single lamp the city allowed at the corner — Vesper rationed its light the way misers rationed coin — and standing on her step, small as a bundle of wet laundry left behind by mistake, was a boy.

He couldn't have been more than eight. He was mud to the eyebrows. His hair was the brown of river silt and stood up in tufts as though he'd been dragged backward through a hedge, which, given the state of him, seemed likely. He looked up at the shutter as if he knew exactly where her eye was, and knocked again.

"No," August told him firmly.

He knocked again.

"I'm not a charity house. I'm not an orphanage. I'm not even a very good witch." That last part was true in ways he couldn't appreciate; hedge-witchery was mostly fevers, chilblains, and luck that worked about as often as a fair coin. "Go home."

Something flickered across his face — not sadness exactly. A kind of patient sorrow, like he'd heard that word, *home*, and found it a foreign coin he didn't know how to spend.

And then he spoke.

It was one word, maybe two, low and round and strange, and every ward August had laid on that house in ten careful years of careful living went off like a struck bell.

Not audibly — wards that made noise were wards that got you hanged. But she felt them flare along her skin, a heat crawling up from her ankles to her jaw, the protective knots she'd tied into the doorframe tightening so hard the wood creaked. Her salt line hissed. Her iron nail, driven into the threshold stone years ago for reasons she'd half forgotten, grew hot enough that she felt it through the floorboards.

The boy's word hadn't been loud. It hadn't even been directed at anything. And yet her wards reacted as though he'd shouted a name of power into the heart of the house.

August stepped back from the door and stood in her dark kitchen with her heart going like a rabbit's.

*No living person speaks that language.*

She knew it the way you know a face from a dream — not the meaning, but the shape of it. Her grandmother's glossaries, the ones that lived wrapped in oilcloth under her floorboards, were full of that script: tall letters leaning like reeds, words that curled at the ends. Cael Sarni. The tongue of a country that did not exist. Had never existed, according to every map, every record, every schoolmaster in Vesper. A country erased so thoroughly that most people had never heard its name, and those who had were wise enough not to say it.

Her grandmother had taught it to her in fragments, in whispers, the way you teach a child to swim by holding them over deep water. *If you ever hear this spoken aloud, little August, you run.*

The boy knocked again.

"You're supposed to be dead," she informed him through the wood. "Your whole country is supposed to be dead. You are a walking violation of everything I know."

Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

He was shivering. She could hear it in the rhythm now, the knocks losing their steadiness, and the night beyond the shutters had the wet bite of early autumn, and he was eight years old and covered in mud and standing alone on her step past curfew.

"Oh, hang it," August said, to herself, to her grandmother's ghost, to the three rules she was about to break two of. She unbarred the door.

---

He came in like water finding a crack — quick, silent, and immediately everywhere. One moment he was on the step; the next he was inside, and she still wasn't entirely sure she'd seen him cross the threshold. He stopped in the middle of her kitchen, dripping, and looked around with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the careful cataloguing of exits.

"All right," she said, kneeling to his level, keeping her voice level too, because frightened children and frightened animals could both smell a lie in your tone. "You're safe. For tonight. Then we'll sort out where you actually belong."

He answered her in that language. A full sentence this time, urgent, the reed-lean sounds flowing out of him like something he'd been holding back all night. Up close, the words raised the hair on her arms. They weren't just old. They were *weighty*. Saying them felt like dropping stones into a well and hearing no bottom.

"I don't understand you," she said gently. "I know a few words. Very few. *Sarneth?*" She tried the old word for *home*, the only one that had stuck from her grandmother's lessons. "*Sarneth*?"

The boy went absolutely still.

Then his eyes filled, all at once, without warning, the way children's eyes do, and he nodded so hard his muddy hair flopped, and he said *sarneth* back to her — and this time the word landed in the room like a dropped lantern. Every candle stub on her shelf flared upright, flames standing tall and steady as soldiers, and the fire in her grate leapt a handspan high and burned, briefly, a color she had no name for. Gold shot through with green, like sunlight through river water.

August sat down hard on her own stool.

"That's not possible," she told the room. The room declined to comment. The fire settled back to orange, smugly.

The boy watched her with his head tilted, and there was something in his watching that unsettled her more than the fire had. He wasn't surprised. Whatever he'd expected from her little house, candles behaving strangely hadn't been on the list of wonders. He wiped his nose on his sleeve — a sleeve that had once, she thought, been good wool, now stiff with river mud — and pointed at the fire, then at himself, then made a shivering motion.

"Yes, fine, warm yourself up, but don't touch anything."

He touched everything. Not rudely — reverently. He drifted along her shelves trailing a finger an inch above the jars of dried nettle and yarrow, above the bundles of luck-knots waiting for tomorrow's market, above the chipped blue teapot she'd owned since she was nineteen, and wherever his finger passed, things *brightened*. The nettles greened. The knots' frayed ends tightened themselves neat. The teapot's chip smoothed over like a scar fading.

"Stop — stop that—" August caught his wrist, gently. Under the mud and grime his skin was cold as a fish. "You can't do that. Little magics, invisible magics, that's how I've stayed alive here. You're doing the opposite of invisible. You're doing *illuminated manuscript*."

He looked at her hand on his wrist, then up at her face, and said something soft and questioning in that impossible language. And because she was tired, and lonely in the way she usually wore like a coat rather than admitted to, she heard herself answer honestly:

"I don't know what you're saying. But I wish I did."

---

She fed him bread and honey and the last of the stew, and he ate like a small polite wolf, and while he ate she studied him. Mud-streaked, yes, but the mud was river mud — she knew its particular gray-brown from years of gathering herbs along the banks. His boots were wrong for a beggar: well-made, patched with care, sized for a grown man. Someone had put this child in adult boots and sent him walking. His clothes were the same story — good cloth, cut down badly, as if they'd been someone else's first.

And around his neck, on a cord, hung a pendant he kept palming closed in his fist whenever he noticed her looking. When his hand fell away once, briefly, she saw it: a flat disc of dark stone carved with a single tall letter. A reed-lean letter, curling at the end.

Cael Sarni script. On a living child. In her kitchen.

Her grandmother's voice, thirty years dead, murmured in her memory: *Run, little August.*

Instead she asked, "What's your name?"

He touched his own chest. "Wren," he said — and that word was Common, or near enough, though he pronounced it strangely, *Wrenn*, with a weight on the second syllable, as if it were borrowed clothing he hadn't quite learned to wear.

"Wren. I'm August." She tapped her collarbone. "August."

"*Augusst,*" he echoed, and smiled, and it was such a sudden, whole-hearted smile that something in her chest that had been locked for a decade rattled on its hinges.

*Don't,* she told herself sternly. *You've known him an hour. He glows. He speaks a dead language. He is quite possibly the most dangerous thing to knock on your door since you started keeping count.* 

He yawned enormously, mid-catastrophe, and swayed where he sat.

*Oh, hang it all.*

She made up the cot in the corner — the one she kept for nights when the market ran late, though she'd never once had a guest to use it — and Wren lay down in his boots until she made him take them off, and he was asleep between one breath and the next, mud and all, one fist still curled around the stone pendant.

August sat by the banked fire and watched him breathe and did not sleep at all.

---

It was near the ninth hour past midnight when she heard the bells change and the boots begin.

Warden patrols came through the Lantern District twice in the small hours, standard practice, checking curfew-breakers and unlicensed lamps. August knew their rhythm the way sailors knew tides. This patrol was running late and walking fast, six men at least by the sound, boots striking cobbles in the crisp unison of people who enjoyed their authority.

She was moving before she'd finished thinking, snuffing her candles, pulling the blanket over Wren's sleeping form. Through the shutter gap she watched the patrol come around the corner into her street, lanterns swinging, halberds catching the corner-lamp's thin light.

They would pass. They always passed. Her house was registered, her papers were Odile's finest work, her life was a gray little box designed to be overlooked—

Wren stirred in his sleep and murmured something in the old tongue.

Every lantern on the patrol went out.

All six. Simultaneously. Not blown out — there was no wind, not a leaf moved on her street — but pinched, as if enormous unseen fingers had closed around each flame and crushed it. The corner-lamp guttered too, the city's own sanctioned flame shrinking to a blue bead and then nothing, and the street plunged into a darkness so complete that August's night-adjusted eyes found nothing to adjust to.

The patrol stopped.

"Whose light was that?" a sergeant's voice demanded of the darkness. "Who's dousing lamps? That's a fine. That's a *fine and a flogging*—"

"It weren't us, sir—"

"The corner post's out. Corner posts don't go out. Fetch the lamplighter's ledger, I want names—"

Boots splashed and stumbled in the dark. Somewhere a man swore as he walked into the water trough. The patrol's discipline dissolved into the particular chaos of armed men suddenly blind, and through it all Wren slept on, breathing slow, one small hand open on the blanket, and the darkness bent around him like water around a stone.

August stood in her black kitchen with her hand pressed over her mouth.

*This is what my grandmother meant.* Not the language itself. The *presence*. A child carrying a whole unwritten country inside him like a coal carried in cupped hands, and everything built on top of that country — its laws, its lamps, its careful erasures — flickering when he breathed.

The wardens found their flint and steel eventually. By the time the corner-lamp struggled back to life, the patrol had retreated to the main road, arguing, and the street was empty again.

August let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for ten years.

Behind her, Wren turned over in his sleep and spoke again — a longer phrase this time, dream-soft, and though she couldn't translate it, one word rang clear as a bell through the strangeness, a word her grandmother had whispered only once, with the shutters barred and the fire low:

*Spiros.* The Spire. The white tower at the heart of Vesper, the pride of the city, the seat of the Wardens themselves.

A sleeping child had just named it in the language of a country that had never existed.

August looked at the boy, and at her dark window, and at the three rules lying broken around her feet like snapped thread.

"Well," she said quietly, to nobody, to the beginning of everything. "I suppose I'll need Odile."
