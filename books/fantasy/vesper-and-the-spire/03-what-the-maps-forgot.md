## 3. What the Maps Forgot

The morning after the boy arrived, August Coldharbour did what she always did when the world misbehaved: she made tea, swept her step, and pretended very hard that everything was ordinary.

It was not ordinary. The boy — Wren, because he had pointed at a wren on her fence post and looked so pleased when she said the word that she'd adopted it for him on the spot — sat at her table eating porridge with the focused intensity of someone who had not eaten in some time. He was maybe eight years old, small for it, with mud still dried in the creases of his ears and eyes the grey-green of river glass. He watched her move around the kitchen the way cats watch birds: patiently, hungrily, without any intention of leaving.

And when he spoke — which was rarely, and only to himself, a low murmuring under his breath like water under ice — he spoke in a language that made the charms hanging over her doorway hum.

August had checked them twice that morning. The fever-charm over the window. The luck-knot braided into the doorframe. The little woven star above the hearth that was supposed to do nothing at all except look pretty, which was its own kind of protection, if you thought about it. All of them were humming. Not alarming, exactly. More like tuning forks held near a struck bell.

"Right," she said to Wren, who cocked his head at her tone if not her words. "We're going to see a friend of mine. She's going to tell me I'm imagining things, and then she's going to tell me I'm not, and then we're both going to have a bad afternoon."

Wren considered this and offered her, solemnly, his empty bowl.

* * *

Odile Ferrant kept a shop in Tanners' Row between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, which August had always felt was the most Odile location in all of Vesper: wedged tight, unremarkable, and full of things that could get you hanged.

The sign above the door read FERRANT & SONS, SCRIVENERY. There were no sons. There never had been sons. Odile had explained once, over cheap wine, that a scrivener with sons sounded established, and established meant trusted, and trusted meant nobody asked too many questions about why a woman of sixty-odd kept her ink locked up like silver.

August left Wren outside with two coppers and instructions to buy a honey-bun from the cart down the street and *not to speak to anyone*, which she realized halfway through the door was like instructing the tide not to come in. But the honey-bun cart was right there, and Wren was looking at it with an expression of profound diplomatic interest, and it would have to do.

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and vinegar and old paper — the particular smell of paper that has been aged on purpose, quickly, in ways August chose not to think about too closely. Odile sat behind the counter doing something finicky to a deed of ownership with a brush no thicker than an eyelash. She did not look up.

"We're closed," she said.

"It's August."

"I know it's August. That's why I said we're closed." She finished her stroke, set down the brush, and peered over her spectacles. Odile Ferrant had a face like a folded map — all creases and hidden territory — and hair the color of ash that she wore pinned up with whatever was nearest, today a pair of bone styluses. "You've brought trouble in with you. I can smell it. Trouble smells like wet wool and fear-sweat, and you, my girl, have been marinating."

"I need you to listen to something."

"That's worse. Listening is how it starts." But she was already coming around the counter, wiping her hands on her apron, because underneath the grousing Odile had the one quality August valued above all others in this city: curiosity stronger than self-preservation. "Well? Out with it."

So August recited it. She'd spent half the night writing down the sounds Wren made, phonetically, in her private shorthand — the shorthand she used for charm-work, the one that would read as nonsense recipes to anyone who found it. She gave Odile the opening phrase, the one Wren had said on her doorstep, the one that had made her wards flare like startled birds.

She watched Odile's face while she spoke. It went through several distinct weather systems: boredom, professional interest, puzzlement, and then something August had never seen there before, which frightened her more than anything else that had happened in the past two days.

Odile Ferrant went still. Completely still. A woman who talked with her hands stopped talking with her hands.

"Say it again," Odile said quietly.

August said it again.

"Where." Not a question. A demand wearing a question's coat.

"A boy. He turned up at my door last night, past curfew, covered in mud. He can't or won't speak Common. He says—" August gestured vaguely at her own mouth, helpless. "That. He says that."

"How old?"

"Eight. Maybe younger. Underfed."

"Where did he come from?"

"The moor north of the city, as far as I can guess. He was walking out of it when I found him. Odile, what is it? You look like you've seen a ghost, and I'll remind you I'm the one who deals in ghosts professionally."

Odile took off her spectacles. She polished them on her apron with great thoroughness, which August recognized as a stalling tactic, because the spectacles had not been dirty. Then she put them back on and said, in the voice she presumably used for clients who asked dangerous questions:

"Come into the back. And bring your boy."

* * *

The back room of Ferrant & Sons was where the honest work happened, by which August meant the illegal work, though standing inside it now, surrounded by shelves of ledgers and folios and boxes labeled in Odile's cramped hand, August understood that some of this work was neither honest nor dishonest but simply *old*. Older than forgery. Older than Vesper.

Wren sat on a stool, sticky-handed from the honey-bun, turning a brass magnifying lens over and over in his fingers with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Odile had pulled down three boxes and was moving through them with an urgency that made August's stomach tighten.

"You knew the language," August said. "When I said it. You recognized it."

"I recognized the *shape* of it." Odile didn't look up. "There's a difference. I heard it once, thirty years ago, from a man whose job it was to make sure I never heard it again. He was reading aloud from a seized document before feeding it to a brazier. Checking his work, he said." She pulled out a folio, flipped through it, shoved it back. "I was a junior clerk. Royal Archive, third sub-vault. My whole world was cataloguing. And this man stood there reading words in a tongue that wasn't on any register, any index, any list of living or dead languages I had ever been taught, and when I asked him what it was, he looked at me the way you look at a child who's asked where babies come from, and he said: *a mistake, clerk. An error being corrected.*"

"And?"

"And I did what clever young clerks do. I went looking." Odile finally turned around. Her hands had resumed talking; they were wringing each other now. "I want you to understand something about archives, August. People think an archive is a record of what happened. It isn't. It's a record of what somebody *decided* happened. Every kingdom keeps two histories — the one in the books and the one in the burning. Vesper's founders were very thorough about the second one."

"What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you that when I went looking, I found gaps. Not missing books — gaps. Catalogue entries with the descriptions scraped off. Shelf-marks assigned to nothing. Whole years of accession records where the ink changed hand mid-page, as if someone had taken over the ledger specifically to unwrite it." She tapped a box. "I started copying what I could find. Scraps. Marginalia. A shipping manifest here, a court record there, things too dull for the burners to notice. Names. Place-names. I have been collecting the ashes of a country for thirty years, my girl, and until five minutes ago I thought I was mad."

"Cael Sarn," August said.

The name came out of her before she knew she was going to say it — and she knew, even as she said it, that she shouldn't know it. It wasn't in any book she'd read. It wasn't on any map. It lived in her the way a scar lives in you: somewhere below knowing, somewhere the body remembers what the mind was told to forget. Her grandmother had hummed a lullaby once, a strange minor-key thing, and when little August had asked what language it was, her grandmother had gone white as flour and said *nonsense, child, it's just sounds*, and had never sung again.

Odile stared at her. "Where did you hear that name?"

"My grandmother. Once. She denied it immediately."

"They all denied it." Odile's voice had gone soft and terrible. "That was the design. You don't burn a country out of the world by burning its books alone. Books are only paper. You burn it out of *people*. You make saying the name itself feel like stepping off a cliff. You make mothers afraid to sing." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered them, she was brisk again, and August understood that the briskness was armor, and that Odile had been wearing it for thirty years. "Cael Sarn. Yes. The oldest entries I ever recovered used the name. Before the Founding, before the Charter, before Vesper was Vesper — there was a kingdom there. In the valley. Where the city stands."

"That's impossible. The valley was empty. The first settlers found empty land—"

"—found empty land, yes, that's what the Charter says, that's what every schoolbook says, that's what's carved on the Spire itself." Odile laughed, and it was not a nice laugh. "*A city raised on silence,* it says. Do you know what I think that inscription means, August? I think it's a confession. I think they wrote the truth on their own monument and dared anyone to read it correctly."

Wren had stopped playing with the magnifying lens. He was watching Odile with his head tilted, and when she said *Cael Sarn* the second time, his lips moved — repeating it, silently, in the shape of someone greeting a friend.

Then he said something. A short phrase, gentle, addressed to Odile directly.

Odile made a sound like she'd been struck. "What did he say? What did he *say*?" She rounded on August. "Translate! Can you translate?"

"No. Can you?"

"No! I have fragments, scraps, a vocabulary the size of a teacup—" But she was already hauling boxes down, tearing through folios, muttering. "The sound of it. The vowels. Thirty years and I only ever had it dead on paper, and here it is *alive*, sitting on my stool, eating a honey-bun—"

She found what she was looking for: a slim notebook, its cover soft with handling, its pages dense with her cramped script. She ran her finger down a column, cross-referencing, her lips moving. Then she stopped.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly.

"What?"

"He said—" Odile cleared her throat. Tried again. "Based on the grammar, based on the roots, he said: *you kept our names.* Something like that. *You kept our names safe.*" She looked up at August, and her eyes behind the spectacles were bright and appalled. "He thanked me. This child walked out of nowhere speaking a murdered language, and he looked at my thirty years of stolen scraps, and he *thanked* me."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere out front, the button-shop bell jangled; someone laughed; a cart rattled past on Tanners' Row. The ordinary city, going about its ordinary morning, on top of a lie the size of a kingdom.

"Odile." August sat down slowly on a crate, because her legs had opinions about standing. "The Spire. Everyone knows the Spire was built by the founders on empty moor. First act of the Charter, etcetera, etcetera. If there was a kingdom in the valley—"

"Then the Spire wasn't built on nothing." Odile had gone pale. "No. Worse. Think about the masonry, August. Nobody builds a tower that size in a decade, and the Spire rose in a decade, the chronicles brag about it, *raised as if grown rather than built.* Because it wasn't built. It was *quarried*. They cut it out of something that was already there." She was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a woman trying to outrun her own conclusion. "Every founding needs a foundation. And the founders of Vesper—"

The wards. August's thoughts snagged on it, the way a sleeve snags on a nail. Last night, on her doorstep, Wren's voice had made her charms flare. And the Wardens' lanterns — the lanterns had gone out like pinched wicks when the boy got frightened, all four of them, guttered in a heartbeat—

Magic answered magic. That was the first law of hedge-craft, older than any ban. Old power woke near old power. Which meant the boy wasn't just speaking a dead language.

He was speaking to something that could hear him.

"August?" Odile had stopped pacing. "You've gone the color of tallow. What is it?"

"Wren's presence makes magic flare. Old magic. Mine, and the Wardens' lantern-charms, and—" She swallowed. "If the Spire is built out of Cael Sarn, out of *quarried* Cael Sarn, then it's the biggest working in the valley. Maybe the biggest working there's ever been. And a child carrying that language just walked into the city."

They looked at each other across the cluttered room, two women who had built their lives on keeping small — small charms, small forgeries, small footprints — and who had just heard, in the space of one morning, how large the smallness really was.

Outside, the honey-bun cart's bell rang cheerfully. Then it rang again. Then it began ringing in the rapid, arrhythmic way that meant the vendor was ringing it to raise alarm rather than appetite, and beneath it, thin and clear, came the whistle of the Watch.

Odile moved first. She always would, August suspected; thirty years of guilt made a person fast. She swept her notebooks into a satchel, blew out the lamp, and said:

"Back door. Now. And August — whatever that boy is, whoever sent him—" She paused at the curtain, silhouetted against the gray light of the alley. "I burned pages for those people once, when I was young and believed in the Charter. I am not going to stand in a doorway and let them take the last living page."

From the front of the shop came the rattle of the door handle, and a voice, official and cold, announcing an inspection under the Curfew Statutes.

Wren took August's hand. His grip was small and astonishingly strong, and when she glanced down at him, he was looking at the curtain toward the street with an expression far too old for his face — the expression, she thought, of someone who had already run from these men before, and knew exactly how it ended.

"Stay close to me," she whispered, in Common, and then — feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway — she repeated it in the few sounds of his language she'd managed to write down in the night.

His eyes came up to hers, wide and startled and, briefly, lit from within.

He understood her.
