## 4. Wardens at the Window

The knock had come past curfew, and the boy was still asleep on her floor when the second knock came — this one at the window.

August knew the difference between knocks the way sailors knew weather. A neighbor's knock was apologetic, a little rhythm of knuckles that said *forgive me*. The knock at her door two nights ago had been small and desperate and had changed her life. But a knock at a second-floor window was no knock at all. It was a pebble of glass tapping glass, deliberate, patient, and it meant someone had climbed her drainpipe with a lantern hooded against their own chest.

She lay still in her bed and counted. One tap. Two. Then a voice, low and official as a tax notice: "Open in the name of the Wardens of Vesper."

They weren't asking the window. They were announcing themselves to the street below, so that anyone watching would know the procedure had been followed. That was the thing about Wardens — they were scrupulous about ceremony and careless about everything else. August had built ten years of survival on that carelessness.

She rose without lighting a lamp, dressed in the dark by touch, and padded barefoot across the cold boards to where Wren slept in the alcove behind her pantry shelves. He'd dragged her spare blanket into a nest and curled into it like a comma, and even asleep he made a faint sound she had come to think of as *humming in a dead language* — soft syllables rising and falling, a tide going out from a shore that no longer appeared on maps.

"Wren," she whispered.

His eyes opened all at once, no drowsiness in them at all, which she was learning was simply how he woke: like a bird, like something that had once needed to be ready.

"Listen carefully," she said. "Men are coming. They wear grey coats and brass buttons and they will smile at you, and you must not let them hear your voice. Can you do that? Not one word."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he pressed his lips together so hard they went white, and nodded, and August thought: *good lad*, and also: *what am I doing, hiding a miracle in a pantry.*

The knocking moved downstairs to her door proper, three measured blows. She took a breath, arranged her face into the mild irritation of a woman woken for nothing, and opened it on the chain.

Two Wardens stood in the stairwell with a hooded lantern between them. Behind them, filling most of the landing, stood a third man who was not smiling, which marked him immediately as the one in charge. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that looked like it had been assembled from spare parts of other, more handsome faces — a strong jaw, an unremarkable nose, eyes the color of dishwater that nonetheless missed nothing. His coat was better cut than his subordinates'. His boots were older than his coat. August noted both facts and filed them away; a man who kept old boots under a new coat cared about appearances less than he cared about comfort, and men who valued comfort over appearance could be reasoned with.

"Mistress Coldharbour?" he said.

"It's half past midnight," she said. "Whatever it is, it can be half past seven."

"Lantern failure," said the younger Warden, producing a slate covered in official-looking figures. "Corner of Tallow Lane and the Ropewalk, night before last. Four patrol lanterns extinguished simultaneously. Cause undetermined. We're canvassing the street."

"Four lanterns," August said. "In this wind? I'm astonished you keep any lit at all."

"The wind," said the older Warden, "does not discriminate."

"No," August agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't. Which is rather my point."

The big man hadn't spoken again. He was looking past her shoulder into the dark of her rooms with the unhurried attention of a man reading a ledger, and August felt the look travel along her spine like a finger drawn down it. She let him look. There was nothing to see but a poor woman's clutter: dried herbs hanging from beams, bundles of yarn, a chipped basin, the ordinary archaeology of a hedge-witch pretending very hard to be only a seamstress.

"You sell charms," he said at last. It wasn't quite a question.

"I sell *knots*," August said. "Pretty ones. Ladies buy them for their wrists and gentlemen for their sweethearts, and if a gentleman believes a red thread will keep him faithful, that is his own superstition and none of mine. Superstition is legal, Captain. You'd know that better than I."

"Do I have the honor of speaking to a captain?" she added, because flattery cost nothing.

"Hesk," he said. "Captain Hesk." And then, without any change of tone whatsoever: "May we come in?"

There it was. The whole game in four words. If she refused, refusal was itself a reportable oddity, and oddities accumulated. If she consented, there was a boy asleep behind her pantry wall whose presence snuffed flame like pinched wicks.

"I'm in my nightclothes," August said, "and my landlady is a widow of strict reputation. Come back at nine and I'll make you tea, and you can count my knots to your heart's content."

Hesk considered her. The lantern between his men guttered — just slightly, just once, the way a candle does in a draft — and August watched him watch it. His eyes went from the flame to her face and back to the flame, and something behind those dishwater eyes turned over, slow, like a stone in a streambed.

"Nine o'clock," he said. "Thank you for your time, mistress."

The door closed. Boots descended the stairs. August stood with her forehead against the wood until she heard the street door go, and then she did not move for a full minute longer, because she had learned that Wardens sometimes counted to sixty outside doors too.

* * *

Odile Ferrant arrived at eight, which for Odile constituted dawn punctuality, and surveyed the situation with the air of a woman who had been expecting worse news since roughly 1793.

"They'll be back," she said, helping herself to August's tea without asking. "Hesk doesn't do courtesy calls. I knew him, before. He was a clerk in the Provost's office — decent handwriting, no imagination. They gave him a coat and a patrol and he treated law enforcement like bookkeeping. Every anomaly gets entered. Every entry gets cross-referenced. He never solves anything dramatic, August. He just *adds up*."

"Then we need fewer things to add up," August said. "Starting with a boy who puts out lanterns by existing."

"He's not doing it on purpose?"

"He's nine years old and he snores in a language that predates the kingdom. I doubt he's doing anything on purpose."

Wren sat on the floor between them, mending — actually mending, with clumsy competence — a tear in his sleeve, and looked up at the sound of his name. Odile had brought him a honey bun, which he was eating in the methodical way of children who have known hunger professionally, and August felt the familiar twist in her chest that she had decided, for professional reasons, to ignore.

"So what do we do?" Odile asked. "I can forge papers, but forged papers take days, and you don't have days. Hesk will knock at nine sharp. He's a nine-o'clock-sharp sort of man."

August looked at the boy. The boy looked back, and then — deliberately, she was almost sure of it — he pointed at the window, toward the river.

"The cart," she said slowly. "Bram Tallow's cart. The one with the false bottom that smells like herring and illegal brandy."

"You know Bram Tallow?"

"I know *of* him. Everyone on the Ropewalk knows of him. He moves things up and down the river that prefer not to be declared, and he owes money to people whose names you don't say aloud, and twice a week his cart comes through this street at exactly this hour because the checkpoint wardens take bribes and he takes routes around the ones who don't." She was already moving, pulling on her coat. "If we're lucky, he's hungover and greedy. Those are his best qualities."

"And if he's neither?"

"Then I improvise," August said, "which is what I've been doing for ten years, and I would thank the universe to stop making me practice."

* * *

Bram Tallow turned out to be a broad, rumpled man of forty-odd years with a beard that seemed to be losing an argument with his face, sitting on the tailboard of his cart outside the Broken Keel and wincing every time he shifted his weight. When August named her business, he laughed until the wincing stopped him.

"A passenger? In *my* cart? Mistress, the false bottom's for casks of the good stuff, not for — " he lowered his voice with theatrical delicacy — "persons of interest."

"He's a child," August said. "He needs to be across town by noon and invisible until then. I'll pay."

"How much?"

"Three silver."

"Five."

"Three, and I'll throw in this." She took his hand — he let her, curious more than wary — and pressed her thumb into the meat of his palm, where she could feel the deep wrongness she'd already guessed at: old damage, badly set, the kind of ache that made a man wince when he shifted his weight and drink more than he should and snap at people who didn't deserve it. She worked quickly, quietly, a warmth running down her arm and into his wrist like water finding a crack. Ten years since she'd done open magic on another person. It felt like unbuckling a belt after a long meal.

Bram stared at his own hand. He flexed it. He flexed it again, and his face did something complicated — relief and grief and suspicion fighting for the same real estate.

"That's..." He swallowed. "That's the first morning in six years it hasn't hurt before noon."

"Charms for small coin," August said. "It's what I do."

"That's not small coin, mistress. That's — " He looked at her properly then, and whatever he saw made him decide something. "Get in the cart. Both of you. And if anyone asks, you're my cousin from upriver and the boy's yours, and I've never seen either of you before in my life."

* * *

The search began at nine o'clock precisely, because Hesk was a nine-o'clock-precise sort of man, and by quarter past nine it had become a spectacle.

There were six of them this time, plus Hesk, plus a clerk with a portable writing board, and they knocked on every door of Tallow Lane with the tireless courtesy of men collecting a debt the whole street owed. August watched from the window of Odile's lodgings across the lane, Wren beside her with his face pressed to the glass, and marveled at how thoroughly her small life was being taken apart in public.

At her own door, Odile waited, dressed in her best shawl and armed with a document case, playing the role of August's elderly landlady with the relish of a woman who had spent thirty years being dull on purpose and found retirement intolerable.

"Miss Coldharbour has gone to her sister's in Marrowfield," August heard her say through the open window, in a voice pitched to carry. "Left Tuesday. Very sudden. Family trouble — you know how sisters are. She left rent through the month, which is more than some do, though I'll thank you not to write that down, young man, I have a reputation—"

"Did she take luggage?" Hesk asked.

"One bag. A small one. She travels light, that girl. Always has." Odile sighed gustily. "Is this about the lanterns? Because I told the last officer, wind off the river comes up that lane like it's owed money—"

"Thank you, madam. That will be all."

But Hesk didn't leave. He stood in the middle of the lane while his men worked door to door, and he looked up — not at Odile's window, but at August's, at the dark panes, at the drainpipe, at the roofline. Reading the building the way he read ledgers. Adding up.

Inside the room, Wren had gone very still. The candle on Odile's table bent its flame sideways, though there was no draft, and then straightened, and bent again, keeping time with something only the boy could hear.

"Wren." August crouched to his level. "Look at me. Breathe slow. Nothing in this room is going to hurt you, but if you're frightened, the fire gets frightened too. Can you breathe with me? In... and out..."

He matched her, gulp by gulp, and the candle steadied, and downstairs Odile was saying something about her late husband and the quality of Vesper gutters, and the moment passed the way storms pass — leaving everything looking the same and nothing feeling the same.

* * *

Hesk's report, written that evening in his small exact hand, ran to four pages, and he hated every one of them.

*Subject's residence secured 0915. Landlady (Ferrant, O., per lease records) states subject departed for Marrowfield Tuesday last. No witnesses to departure. No carrier manifest matching. No coach ticket purchased under subject's name at any of the three stations.* He wrote *no* eleven times in four pages, and each *no* sat on the page like a stone in a shoe.

The lantern incident itself troubled him more. Four patrol-lanterns, military issue, sealed against weather, extinguished within the same three seconds. His men blamed wind. Wind did not reach inside oiled glass. And when he had stood in that woman's doorway, her candle — he had seen it through the gap in her door, before she closed it — had dipped. Once. At the same moment his own lantern flickered on the landing.

Coincidence. He wrote the word down and underlined it, because Clerk-Hesk, dead these twenty years, had taught him that anything needing underlining was anything you didn't believe.

He pulled the street file again. August Coldharbour, seamstress, resident five years, taxes paid, no associations of record, no Warden file, nothing. Five years of perfect blankness. People were not blank. Even the dull ones left smudges — debts, quarrels, sweethearts, sins. A woman with no history was a woman who had swept very carefully behind her, and in Hesk's long experience of ledgers, the entries that were too clean were the ones worth pulling apart thread by thread.

And there was the other matter. The thing he had put in no report at all.

When he'd asked to enter her rooms, she had refused — smoothly, legally, with a landlady's virtue as her shield. Fine. Common. But she had refused *one heartbeat too late*, the pause of a person calculating rather than deciding, and Hesk had spent enough years among liars to know the difference between a lie and a prepared truth. Her truths were prepared. All of them. Neat as knotwork.

He sat back in his chair in the Wardenhouse, lamplit, the Spire visible through his window above the rooftops, black against blacker sky, and he allowed himself the question he was not permitted:

*What are you, August Coldharbour?*

Somewhere across the city, in a smuggler's cart smelling of herring and contraband brandy, a boy sneezed, and every lantern on Candlemaker's Row went out at once, and three separate patrols filed reports that would land on Captain Hesk's desk by morning.

He would add them up. It was what he did.

But for the first time in his career, Captain Hesk suspected that the sum might be larger than the column — that somewhere beneath the tidy arithmetic of lantern reports and lease records there was a figure he couldn't see yet, something old and enormous, waiting in the margin of the page.

He reached for a fresh sheet of paper, and began, in his small exact hand, a private file.

He titled it: *Coldharbour, A. — continued.*
