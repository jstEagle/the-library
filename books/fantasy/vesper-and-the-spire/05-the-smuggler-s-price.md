## 5. The Smuggler's Price

The cart smelled of tar, wet rope, and a man who had given up on pretending otherwise.

Bram Tallow sat on the upturned crate that served as his porch, his office, and — judging by the grease stains — his dinner table, in the yard behind the Rope & Anchor where the river-smugglers of Vesper kept their boats and their grudges. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had once been red and was now the color of old rust, and he watched August approach with the flat, unimpressed stare of someone who had been lied to by professionals.

"You're the one Odile sent," he said.

"I'm the one Odile sent."

"Odile Ferrant." He said the name like it was a debt he'd already counted twice. "That woman has cost me more money than the river ever did."

"She mentioned you might say something like that."

"Did she mention she owes me eleven crowns?"

"She mentioned you'd bring it up."

Bram grunted, which August was beginning to understand was his native language, with Common spoken only as a second tongue. Behind him, the false-bottomed cart stood in the gray morning light, its canvas cover beaded with last night's rain. Somewhere under that floor, curled around Odile's forged papers like a kitten around a warm brick, Wren was sleeping. He had slept most of the way here, in fits, one hand always finding August's sleeve when the cart jolted — as if checking she hadn't been unwritten while he wasn't looking.

"The boy," Bram said. Not a question. Word traveled fast among people who lived off what other people didn't see.

"The boy," August agreed. "And I need to move him upriver. Quietly. To the moor country past Grayford Bend."

"That's Gull Court water."

"I know."

"Everything past the third lock is Gull Court water, and everything before it is Gull Court water wearing a different hat." He scratched under his beard. "You know what I owe them?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the trick of it. They keep the number themselves and tell you pieces. Last piece was four hundred crowns. Before that it was three-fifty. The number goes up when they're bored and down when they want you grateful." He spat, expertly, into the mud. "I haven't been grateful in two years."

August had prepared for this conversation the way she prepared for everything: with three plans, two lies, and one truth held in reserve. She had expected to bargain with coin she didn't have, or documents Odile could forge, or threats neither of them would enjoy making. She had not expected the way Bram shifted on his crate as he spoke, his left hand pressed flat against his hip like a man holding a door shut against weather.

She had not expected the pain. But now that she looked — really looked, the way her mother had taught her to look, past the surface of things to the small crooked truths underneath — she couldn't stop seeing it.

It sat in him like a stone in a shoe. Old damage, deep in the left hip and down the leg, the kind that came from a bad break badly set, or a boat's boom swung low, or ten thousand nights sleeping on cold decks. It flared when he moved and ached when he didn't, and he had built his whole posture around it the way a tree builds around a fence wire. He was forty, maybe. He carried himself like sixty.

"How long?" she asked.

He blinked. "How long what?"

"The leg. Or hip. Whichever it is."

Something flickered across his face — surprise first, then the quick calculation of a man deciding whether to be offended, then, unexpectedly, a tired honesty. "Fifteen years," he said. "Cargo crane on the *Kestrel* took a notion to swing. Doctor wanted to take the leg off above the knee. I told him I'd take his fingers off instead if he came near me with a saw." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; she saw him pay for it. "Been carrying it since. Some days it's a whisper. Some days it sings."

"What do you take for it?"

"Poppy syrup, when the Gull Court leaves me money for poppy syrup. River willow bark, mostly. Prayers, occasionally, though I've never met a god who dealt in hips."

August stood very still. Ten years of rules sat on her shoulders like a yoke. *Never magic anyone can trace. Never magic anyone can name. A charm sold is a thread leading back to your door.* She had lived by those rules through curfews and searches and winters so lean she'd eaten candle wax stewed with nettles, and she had told herself the rules were wisdom, when part of her knew they were fear wearing wisdom's coat.

But this man was going to decide in the next five minutes whether to sell her to the Gull Court or hide her from the Wardens, and he was going to make that decision while pain sang in his leg like a struck bell.

There were bargains, and there were bargains.

"Bram," she said. "What would you say if I told you I could make it stop?"

His eyes narrowed. "Make what stop."

"The singing."

The yard went quiet around them. Somewhere on the river a gull screamed its one opinion about everything. Bram looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him arrive, slowly, at the thing she was offering — watched him refuse to arrive at it, actually, because hope was expensive and smugglers knew better than anyone to check the price tag first.

"That's a dangerous joke," he said quietly.

"It's not a joke."

"People hang for less than what you're saying."

"Yes," August said. "They do."

She made him carry her inside first. Not out of drama — although she would admit, later, to enjoying the theater of it — but because wards needed walls, and walls needed to be hers. The back room of the Rope & Anchor smelled of hemp and lamp oil; Bram cleared a space on the floor with a sweep of his boot and watched her chalk the circle with the expression of a man watching someone defuse a bomb he personally owned.

"If anyone asks," he said, "you're a midwife."

"I'm a midwife."

"With herbs."

"Very strong herbs."

"From the south."

"Everyone's herbs are from the south. Nobody questions southern herbs. It's the one advantage of southerners being mythical."

He almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, quickly suppressed, but she caught it, and catching it felt like picking a lock.

Wren appeared in the doorway while she worked — she'd felt him wake, a small bright tug at the edge of her awareness, like a fish testing a line. He padded in barefoot, took in the chalked circle, the candles, Bram sitting stiffly in the middle of it with his hands on his knees like a schoolboy, and gave August a look of grave disapproval, as though she were doing magic wrong.

"He can stay," Bram said, nodding at the boy. "If we're all hanged, we'll hang friendly."

Wren crossed the room and sat down beside Bram without asking, the way children sit beside large animals they have decided are safe. Then, after a moment's consideration, he reached out and patted Bram's knee — the bad one — twice, gently, the way you'd pat a horse.

Bram looked down at him. "What's he doing?"

"I don't know," August said honestly. "But my grandmother used to say animals and children can smell where it hurts. Maybe it's that."

"Mm." Bram studied the boy. Something in his face had gone softer and more puzzled. "He doesn't talk?"

"Not in words we know."

"Odile says he talks in words nobody knows. Words that got burned." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light. Everyone in Vesper knew the shape of that story — the dead kingdom, the erased maps, the great forgetting that every child learned to recite without learning why. You didn't say *Cael Sarn* aloud in daylight. You certainly didn't say it over a chalk circle with a hedge-witch kneeling at its edge.

August lit the last candle. "Hold still," she said. "This is the part where you trust me."

"That's the part I'm worst at."

"I noticed."

She put her hands on his hip through the cloth of his trousers, found the hurt beneath — found it easily, horribly easily, a knot of old screaming wrapped in scar — and did the thing she had spent ten years not doing.

Magic is not lightning. That's what the ballads get wrong, and what gets hedge-witches caught: they go looking for lightning, for spectacle, for the flare and crackle that draws Warden lanterns from three streets away. Real magic is quieter. It is persuasion. It is telling a body, in the language bodies dream in, that the war is over, that the wall can come down, that it is allowed — *allowed*, after all these years — to rest.

August told Bram Tallow's leg that it was allowed to rest.

The change came slowly, and then all at once. She felt it leave her hands like warmth leaving bread. Bram's breath caught. His whole body, which had been braced the way a man braces for a blow, unbraced — muscle by muscle, year by year, fifteen years of guardedness letting go in the space of six heartbeats. He made a sound. It was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else, and he covered it by coughing, badly.

"Well," he managed.

"Well," August agreed.

He flexed the leg. Moved it. Moved it again, faster, disbelieving, like a man poking a tooth that had stopped aching. Then he stood up — stood up *straight*, no list to starboard, no careful arithmetic of weight — and walked the length of the room and back, and Wren clapped his hands once, delighted, and Bram turned around with an expression August suspected no one had seen on his face in fifteen years.

It was, roughly, the expression of a man who had been robbed and couldn't find the thief.

"What did you do," he said. Not a question. An accusation shaped like a question.

"Eased it. Not cured — the break is the break, bone remembers. But the singing's done. It'll hold weeks. Months, if you're lucky. When it comes back—" she pulled a twist of paper from her pocket and set it in his palm, "—crumble half of this into hot wine. The other half when that runs out. After that, you'll have to come find me."

"And where will you be?"

"That," said August, "depends on you."

They negotiated on the crate outside afterward, because Bram refused to conduct business standing up straight — "feels dishonest," he said, "like I'm showing off" — and because Wren had discovered the yard's cat, a scarred gray tomcat named Admiral, and the negotiation required a certain privacy. Though in fact Wren and the Admiral simply sat side by side in the middle of the yard, staring at each other with identical expressions of mutual assessment, and August doubted either of them missed a word.

"Upriver to Grayford Bend is three days' run," Bram said. "Four, if the Gull Court's toll-boats are out, which they will be, because they're always out when I'm finally having a good week." He rubbed his hip absently, an old habit meeting new silence, his hand returning to find nothing there. He seemed almost annoyed about it. "Past the bend there's an old landing no one uses. From there it's moor country. Empty, according to every map."

"According to every map," August agreed carefully.

"Right." He looked at her. "And that's the part you're not saying out loud, isn't it. That the maps are wrong."

August said nothing, which was itself an answer, and Bram nodded slowly, the way men nod when a suspicion they've been feeding scraps to for years finally sits up and speaks.

"My grandfather," he said, "used to sing songs nobody taught him. River songs, he called them, but they weren't river songs. Words in them I never heard anywhere else. My mother told him to stop singing them where folk could hear. He'd just laugh and say, *the words remember even if the king's men don't.*" He shrugged. "I thought he was drunk. He usually was. But lately—" he glanced toward the yard, toward the small dark-haired boy communing silently with a cat, "—lately I've been wondering whose words they were."

"Cael Sarn's," August said quietly. It was the first time she had said the name aloud in ten years, and it landed in the yard like a stone dropped down a well. Even the Admiral's ears went back.

Bram exhaled through his nose. "So. The Wardens want the boy. The Gull Court wants whatever the Wardens want, times whatever price they can charge. And you want passage upriver for a mute child who speaks a dead language, a disgraced archivist, and yourself, a woman who just performed open sorcery in the back room of a smuggler's tavern." He ticked them off on thick fingers. "Have I got the shape of it?"

"You've got the shape of it."

"And what's in it for me besides a charm and a hanging?"

August had her answer ready. She'd had it ready since the circle, since she'd felt how much of him was still whole underneath the damage. But looking at him now — at the rust-colored beard and the straightened spine and the cat-quiet boy in the yard — she found herself setting the rehearsed answer down and picking up a true one instead.

"In it for you," she said, "is the chance to be the kind of man your grandfather sang about. And also," she added, because sentiment alone had never paid a toll, "I'll mend the *Kestrel*'s hull seam the same way I mended your leg. You've been bailing her since spring. Don't argue, I could hear it from the street."

Bram stared at her. "You could hear my boat leaking."

"Your boat leaks like a gossip."

A pause. Then Bram Tallow laughed — a real one, big and rusty, startling the gulls off the pilings — and August understood that somewhere in the last hour, without either of them signing anything, the transaction had become something else. Trust rarely announces itself. It just arrives, the way dawn does, while everyone's still arguing about the dark.

"Three days to Grayford Bend," he said, wiping his eyes. "We load tonight, run on the night tide. Odile rides in the cart with the boy — she snores, warn him now. You're crew. Can you handle a tiller?"

"I grew up on the Fenwater."

"Fenwater." He shook his head mournfully. "Puddles with ambitions. Well. You'll learn."

He stuck out his hand, and she took it, and his grip was warm and whole and steady, and she realized she was grinning like a fool and couldn't stop.

"One condition," Bram said, as Wren wandered over to stand at August's elbow, the Admiral trailing him like a small furry shadow. The boy looked up at them both, then pointed one small finger at Bram's hip, then at himself, then made a gesture — cupping his hands, pouring something invisible from one to the other.

August frowned. "What's he—"

"I think," Bram said slowly, "he's asking if some of what you gave me can be poured into him. Into the quiet place." He looked down at the boy, and his voice went gentle in a way that clearly embarrassed them both. "Whatever's hurting you, lad, I don't think it's the kind that comes out through the leg."

Wren considered this. Then he nodded once, gravely, and leaned his head briefly against Bram's arm — a transaction of his own, concluded — and went back to the cat.

Bram watched him go. When he spoke again, his voice was lower.

"There's a stretch past Grayford Bend," he said, "where the river runs strange. Old stones in the water, older than any bridge. Boatmen won't pole there after dark. Say the river hums." He met her eyes. "My grandfather used to sing along with it. In those words nobody taught him."

August felt the ground tilt, gently, the way it had been tilting since a knock came at her door past curfew.

"Then we'll want to be on the water at dusk," she said, "and listening."

Bram nodded, and went to ready his boat, humming something under his breath — something old, something the river would know.
