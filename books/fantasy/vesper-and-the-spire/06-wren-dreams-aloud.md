## 6. Wren Dreams Aloud

The river had opinions about their journey, and it shared them freely.

August learned this on the second night aboard Bram Tallow's boat, the *Merry Widow*, which was neither merry nor — as far as she could tell — ever a widow. It was a flat-bottomed barge with a patched sail and a hold that smelled of eel oil and old secrets, and it moved upriver at the pace of a tired man walking home. The current fought them. The wind changed its mind hourly. And the boat creaked in a language of its own that August was beginning to suspect was related to Wren's.

She sat in the stern with her back against a coil of rope, mending a tear in her cloak by lantern-light — a real lantern, lit with flint and tallow like an honest person's, because after what had happened to the Wardens' lamps on her street she did not trust anything with a flame near the boy when he slept.

Wren slept a great deal. He ate like a stevedore and then slept like one too, curled in the crook between two cargo crates with Bram's spare coat dragged over him, and he dreamed loudly.

Not nightmares, exactly. Not always. He made sounds — small, urgent sounds, words in that language that made the air feel thinner, and sometimes his hands moved, tracing shapes against the crate wood: walls, windows, something tall.

"Does he do this every night?" Bram asked, lowering himself onto the bench across from her with the careful choreography of a man whose back had filed formal complaints years ago and never received a satisfactory answer.

"Every night since I've known him," August said. "Which is four nights."

"Four nights." Bram shook his head. "You've known the lad four days and you're already fleeing the capital with him on my boat. My mother said I'd end up mixed up with fast women. She never mentioned the slow ones would be worse."

"I'm not fast," August said, biting her thread. "I'm thorough."

"That's what worries me."

* * *

Here is what August had not told either of them: she could understand pieces of what Wren said in his sleep.

Her apprenticeship had been unconventional even by hedge-witch standards. Her teacher, a woman named Sorrel who had lived at the ragged edge of three parishes and belonged officially to none of them, had insisted that magic was mostly a matter of translation — of hearing what the world said underneath its ordinary noise. *A charm is just a sentence the world agrees to believe,* Sorrel used to say, and then she'd made August copy out glossaries of dead tongues until her fingers cramped, because half the oldest charms were built on languages nobody spoke anymore, and a witch who couldn't parse them was a cook who couldn't read recipes.

One of those dead tongues had no name in any glossary Sorrel owned. It appeared only in the margins of a single water-damaged primer, annotated in another hand: *the speech of the old country, source unknown, do not use for binding work.* August had copied it out faithfully at fourteen, found it beautiful and useless, and forgotten it for ten years.

Then a mud-streaked child had knocked on her door speaking it fluently, and every ward on her house had stood up and screamed.

Now, listening to Wren dream, the old vocabulary came back to her the way a song comes back — badly at first, then in whole verses. She kept a scrap of paper in her pocket and, when the boy murmured something she recognized, she scratched the Common equivalent beside it in pencil, hunched over the lantern like a woman doing sums.

*Tower,* she wrote. He said it often. *Fall. Fall again. Door.*

And once, very clearly, in a voice nothing like a child's sleep-voice — older, deliberate, almost annoyed, as though repeating himself to a slow student:

*The door remembers names.*

She looked up. Wren was still asleep. His eyes moved beneath his lids. His hands had gone still.

Bram was watching her from across the deck. "You understood that," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Some of it." August folded the paper into quarters, then eighths, which accomplished nothing except giving her hands something to do. "It's an old language. Very old. There are fragments in charm-books — recipes, basically. Nobody speaks it. Nobody *should* speak it."

"And yet."

"And yet."

Bram nodded slowly, the way he seemed to nod at everything, as if the world were a series of mildly disappointing business proposals. "My grandmother used to say there were words you shouldn't say on open water. Storm-words. My da said she was superstitioned out of her mind." He glanced at the sleeping boy. "Da also said the Gull Court was honest businessmen, so his record's spotty."

"Your debt," August said carefully. "To them. Is it —"

"Large enough that we're going upriver instead of down, where they'd look first." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to his side, to the place beneath his ribs where August knew the pain lived — she'd seen him press it twice today when he thought no one was watching, seen the way his breath went shallow around it. "Don't fret about me, witch. I've been dodging creditors since before you could tie a luck-knot."

"Charm-knots," she corrected automatically. "Luck-knots are a different pattern entirely. Sloppier. Anyone can tie a luck-knot; that's why they don't work."

"Is that so." Something warmed in his face, brief as sun through cloud. "What do proper ones do?"

"Proper ones?" She hesitated. Ten years of rules sat on her tongue like a stone. *Never magic anyone can trace. Never stay anywhere long. Never, never let them see what you are.* But the Wardens already knew her street. Her small life was already gone; she'd watched it burn down politely, without flames, over four days. "Proper ones remind your body what it felt like before it hurt. That's all. Magic can't fix anything. It can only remind."

"Remind," Bram repeated. He looked out at the black water sliding past. "Huh."

* * *

On the third night the wind died completely, and Bram anchored in the lee of a willow-choked bank because, as he put it, "a sail full of nothing is just laundry." They ate smoked fish and hard bread and a jar of pickled things Bram refused to explain the provenance of. Wren fell asleep mid-bite, a piece of bread still in his fist, and August eased him down among the crates and covered him with the coat.

That night he dreamed aloud more than ever.

She woke to it — or perhaps she hadn't been fully asleep; she'd been drifting in that shallow watchfulness that had followed her since the knock at her door, the watchfulness of a woman whose life had developed a leak. His voice came low and running, syllables tumbling, and the lantern flame leaned toward him though there was no draft.

August sat up. Took out her paper. Listened.

*Tower falling,* she translated silently, word by word, dredging the margin-glosses up from fourteen-year-old memory. *Not broken — falling the way fruit falls. Falling on purpose. And under it — under it a door, and the door is —*

She frowned. One word recurred, a long liquid word she'd heard him say awake, once, pointing at the Spire's distant crown above Vesper's rooftops. In her glossary scraps it wasn't a noun at all. It was a verb. To remember. To keep. To hold something so firmly inside yourself that it cannot be taken.

He was using it as a name.

"The door keeps-names," she whispered, testing it. "The door remembers names."

Wren's voice climbed. His hands worked against the coat, pressing, pushing at something only he could see. And then he said a new word — short, sharp, doubled — and August's blood went cold, because she knew that one too. It was in the primer's very first lesson, the old tongue's word for a thing every charm-witch learned early, because it was the thing all their work bent around.

*Stone.*

But he wasn't saying *stone*. He was saying *stones-that-were-something-else*. The grammar did something she'd never seen in the glossary — a construction the dead language had for material that remembered its previous shape. Timber that had been a living tree. Cloth that had been a sheep's warmth. Stone that had been —

She didn't have the word for what the stones had been. The boy supplied it himself, a moment later, in a whisper so clear and grief-stricken that Bram sat up across the deck.

Wren said the name of a city.

August had never heard it before. She was certain of that, and simultaneously certain she had — because the moment it left his mouth, every charm she had ever learned stirred at once, the way birds go quiet before weather. The name fit somewhere in the world, some hollow exactly its shape, and the world had been carrying the hollow around for a hundred years pretending it wasn't there.

"He's dreaming about the Spire," Bram said quietly. "Isn't he. The tower in his head. It's the Spire."

August looked at the boy. At the shape his hands made against the dark — tall, tapering, crowned. She had seen that silhouette every day of her life from a dozen streets of Vesper. Every citizen had. You could navigate the whole city by it; the Wardens' proclamations hung beneath it; it was on the coinage, stamped proud and featureless, a tower with no history, as if it had been assembled from raw sky.

"It's the Spire," she agreed. "Exactly the Spire. Down to the —" she stopped.

"Down to the what?"

"There's a kink in it," she said slowly. "Near the top. A bend, like the tower flinched. Everyone says it's the wind, or settling, or bad old engineering. It's in his dream. The tower in his dream flinches."

The river lapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, got no answer, called again, and gave up.

"Witch," Bram said. "What's he dreaming *about*?"

"I don't know yet." She smoothed the crumpled paper on her knee. "But I'm going to find out. Go back to sleep, Bram."

"You first."

They glared at each other with the mutual stubbornness of two people who had each survived alone for a long time and were out of practice being looked after. Then Wren murmured something else — soft, questioning, a child's voice again — and both of them turned to look, and the argument dissolved unspoken.

August lay back against the rope coil and did not sleep. Above the eastern hills, hours later, the sky went the grey of old linen, and the boy woke, and looked at her, and pointed at her paper with bread-sticky fingers.

He said a word. Then he tapped the paper. Then he looked at her with an expression of terrible patience, the patience of someone waiting for a slow companion to catch up on a long road.

"What?" August said. "What is it?"

He tapped the paper again, harder, frustrated, and then — with the sudden decisive fury of a child denied — he snatched the pencil from her hand.

And drew.

* * *

He drew the way other children scribbled, fast and graceless, tongue between his teeth. But what he drew was no scribble. A tower, tapering, with the flinch near its crown. Beneath it, lines — roots, August thought, or foundations, spreading wider than any building needed. And at the bottom of the page, a plain arch, drawn over and over until the paper wore thin, with small marks inside it that were not decoration.

Words. He had drawn words, in that old tongue, stacked inside the arch like stones in a wall.

Wren held the page out to her. His eyes were steady and afraid and certain, all three at once, which August had not previously known a face could manage.

"It's real," she said. "Whatever this is. It's not a dream, is it. It's a memory. Or —" she stopped, because the next word arrived in her head in his language, unbidden, translated by whatever channel had opened between the boy's sleeping mouth and her waking ear.

*A message.*

Bram leaned over her shoulder, squinting at the arch. "Door," he said. "That's a door. Why's a door at the bottom of everything?"

Neither of them answered him. Upstream, faint through the morning mist, the Spire itself was not visible — but August felt it anyway, the way you feel a tooth you've been told not to touch. A tower with a flinch in it. Stones that remembered being something else. A door that kept names.

And beneath the willow bank, moored in the reeds with its patched sail furled, the *Merry Widow* rocked gently, carrying them — one witch, one smuggler, one impossible child — straight toward the heart of a country that had never existed.

"We'll need more paper," August said.
