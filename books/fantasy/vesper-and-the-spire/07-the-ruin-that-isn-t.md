## 7. The Ruin That Isn't

The map said *moor*.

August had checked it three times before they left the river — Bram's battered survey chart, the one he'd won at cards from a toll-keeper and never bothered to replace. The valley where Wren had been found was rendered in the cartographer's flat, honest ink: empty upland, good for nothing but wind and sheep that were too clever to stay. No road. No settlement. No reason for anyone to be there.

The valley disagreed.

They crested the ridge at midmorning, Wren riding pillion behind August with his arms locked around her waist and his chin tucked between her shoulder blades, and August heard Bram rein in his horse ahead of her with a sound like a man who'd just been handed a bill he couldn't pay.

"Well," Bram said. "That's not moor."

It was not moor.

Below them, the land folded into a green bowl perhaps half a mile across, and the bowl was full of *shapes*. Foundations — pale rectangles of stone showing through the grass like bones through skin, laid out in streets, in lanes, in the neat geometry of people who had expected to stay. Orchard rows marched up the southern slope, gone wild now, their trees grown tall and leggy and tangled together, but unmistakably planted, row upon row upon row, hundreds of them, all blooming out of season with small white flowers that filled the air with a sweetness so thick it was almost rude.

And at the center of it all, ringed by a low wall of fitted stone, a well.

"Odile," August said carefully, "your codex."

Odile Ferrant sat her mule like a woman who had lost an argument with it years ago and had simply stopped contesting the terms. She had the codex open on her knees already; she'd had it open since the ridge, August realized, because of course she had. Odile read maps the way other people read weather.

"There's nothing," Odile said. Her voice had the particular flatness she got when she was frightened and refusing to advertise it. "Not in the codex, not in any gazetteer I ever handled. I catalogued every settlement survey in the northern reaches for eleven years. This valley should have a name. It should have a *tithing record*, a mill levy, a parish boundary. There is no gap in the records here, August. That's what frightens me. A gap you could explain. This is—" She paused, hunting for the word, and when she found it she said it very quietly, as if it might bite. "This is *absence*. Deliberate. Seamless."

Wren slid down off the horse without waiting for help.

He'd been quiet for two days — quieter than even his usual silence, which had a quality August had come to think of as *attentive*, the silence of a child watching the world for signs. But this was different. As his feet touched the grass he made a sound, small and involuntary, halfway between a breath and a word, and then he was running — down the slope, through the wild orchard rows, petals scattering around him like startled birds — and August was scrambling after him before she'd decided to move, because that was apparently what her life was now: chasing a mute child into impossible valleys while a smuggler shouted something behind her about *not splitting up, are you both mad—*

Wren stopped at the well.

He put both hands on the low stone wall and leaned over it, and August caught up just in time to see him look down into the dark, and then up at her, and his face —

His face did something August had no training for.

She had seen him frightened. She had seen him wary, exhausted, stubbornly, ferociously patient. She had seen him in the grip of dreams that made him speak a dead language in a voice too old for him. But she had never seen him *recognize* anything. And now he stood at this well in a valley that officially did not exist, and his whole face opened like a door, and he looked at her with an expression of such naked, uncomplicated *homecoming* that August had to turn away and pretend to examine the orchard, because there were things a person could not witness without being changed by them, and she wasn't sure she could afford the change.

Bram arrived at a trot, breathing hard, leading the horses. He looked at the foundations. He looked at the well. He looked at Wren, who was now patting the well-wall with both palms, gently, the way you'd greet an old dog.

"Right," Bram said. "Somebody explain to me how a valley full of houses isn't on any map, because I've run cargo past this moor maybe forty times and I swear on my mother's grave there was *nothing here*."

"Your mother's alive," Odile said, dismounting with a wince.

"She's alive and she'd still want me to swear on it. Explain."

"I can't." Odile had come to stand beside August, and her eyes moved over the valley with the terrible professional hunger of an archivist confronting a book she'd been told didn't exist. "But I can tell you what it *isn't*. It isn't ruined. Look." She pointed with one gloved finger. "Ruins burn. Ruins collapse. Ruins get pulled apart by neighbors needing stone. These walls are standing. The roofs are gone, yes, but the walls are *whole*, and the glass—" She crouched beside the nearest foundation and brushed the grass aside, and came up holding a shard of window-glass, thick and greenish, unbroken at the edges. "The glass is whole. Nothing broke this place. Nothing *happened* to it."

"Then where's everybody?" Bram asked.

"That," said Odile, "is the question I've been afraid of since the ridge."

* * *

They spent the afternoon walking the valley, and the valley let them.

That was the only way August could describe it. Places abandoned for decades — even places merely neglected — had a texture she knew from her years of moving through them: the sullenness of a house nobody wanted, the way brambles took doors off hinges and frost heaved hearthstones apart. This place had none of that. The grass grew soft and even over the foundations, as if the ground were keeping the shapes safe rather than swallowing them. The orchard trees bore fruit out of season — small hard apples, green-gold, sweet enough to make Bram close his eyes when he bit one. The lane stones sat snug in their beds. And the well—

The well was sweet.

August drew the bucket herself, mostly to have something to do with her hands. The water came up cold and clear and tasting faintly of stone and apple blossom, and it was the best water she had ever drunk, and she found herself standing there with the cup in both hands, unable to stop thinking about the fever-charms she sold in the night market for two coppers apiece, brewed from ditchwater boiled twice because clean water was a luxury and luck was cheaper than health.

Somewhere under her feet, someone had built a village with a sweet well and six hundred fruit trees and lanes laid straight and true, and someone had decided that village had never existed, and the decision had *held*.

"Wren says there was a school," Odile said.

August nearly dropped the cup. "What?"

Odile was sitting on the low wall of what must once have been a square, the codex open on her knees, a stub of pencil in her hand. Wren stood beside her, pointing at things, and Odile was writing — writing fast, her handwriting degenerating from its usual beautiful clerk's script into something urgent and jagged.

"He's showing me. He can't say the words in Common — most of them don't exist in Common, that's rather the point — but he points, and I ask, and some of it I can follow from context." Her pencil scratched. "There was a school there. And a mill — no, wait, not a mill, a *fulling shed*, they processed wool, that's why the orchard, the tannins—" She stopped. She looked up at August, and August saw that her eyes were wet, which Odile would deny under torture. "August. He's naming families. He's pointing at foundations and naming the families that lived in them. The Ashlows. The Verrin family. Someone called Old Tam who kept bees." Her voice cracked on *bees*, of all things. "He knows the bees' names."

August set the water cup down very carefully on the well-wall, because her hands were not steady.

"How old is he?" she asked. It came out rougher than she meant. "Odile, he's seven. Eight. Whatever happened here happened long enough ago for the orchard to go wild, for the maps to be rewritten, for the whole thing to be scrubbed from every record in the country. He can't remember it. Children don't remember things they were too young — "

"No," Odile agreed quietly. "They don't."

They looked at each other, and neither of them said the next part, because saying it would make it real, and it was already too real: whatever lived inside Wren, whatever spoke through his sleep in the tongue of Cael Sarn, it was not memory the way August understood memory. It was something older, and deeper, and it had been carried to her doorstep like a message that refused to be undelivered.

Across the square, Wren had stopped pointing.

He was standing very still in front of one foundation — smaller than the others, set back from the lane, with a low step-stone worn smooth in the middle. He crouched. He touched the step-stone with one finger, tracing a groove in it, and then he did something August had never seen him do.

He cried.

Not loudly. Not the way children cry when they want to be comforted. He cried the way adults cry when they have finally stopped hoping someone will come — silently, shoulders shaking, face turned down, one hand pressed flat against the stone as if holding it in place. Holding *it* in place. Holding the whole invisible village in place with one small hand, because if he let go, who else was there?

August crossed the square and knelt beside him, and she didn't touch him — she had learned that much, in their weeks together, that Wren came to you or he didn't — but she knelt where he could see her, and she waited.

After a long time, he leaned sideways until his shoulder rested against hers.

She put her arm around him and looked out over the valley — the pale foundations, the blooming orchards, the well with its bucket swaying gently on its rope — and she thought about what Odile had said, *nothing happened to it*, and she thought about the founders of Vesper burning page after page after page, and she understood, suddenly and completely, in the place where understanding hurts:

They hadn't destroyed this place. Destruction leaves wreckage, and wreckage leaves grief, and grief leaves *stories*, and stories are stubborn things that grow back like weeds. You couldn't kill a village with fire. So they had done something worse. They had unwritten it. They had reached into the world and taken out the sentence that said *this place exists*, and stitched the edges closed so neatly that even the sheep had moved on, even the roads forgot to come here, even the maps lay smooth and innocent as a healed scar.

No ruins. Because ruins mourn. And mourning was exactly what Vesper had been built to prevent.

"They weren't allowed to be grieved," August said aloud. She hadn't meant to say it. Her voice sounded strange in the flowering air. "Nobody was allowed to miss them. That was the whole point. You can't outlaw sorrow for something that never was."

Beside her, Wren lifted his head. He looked at her with red-rimmed eyes, and then he said something — a short phrase, soft, in the old tongue — and though August knew perhaps thirty words of Cael Sarn and this phrase contained almost none of them, she understood it anyway, the way you understand a tune you've never heard.

*You may grieve. Somebody has to.*

"All right," she said, and her voice broke on it, and she let it. "All right, little one. We'll grieve. Both of us. Somebody has to."

They stayed there until the light went long and gold, and Bram — who pretended harder than anyone August had ever met that he felt nothing — quietly built a fire in the lee of a foundation wall and set out apples and hard cheese and the last of the river-bought bread, and none of them remarked on the fact that he'd set four portions, or that one of them sat untouched at the edge of the firelight, the way you'd set a plate for someone expected home.

* * *

It was full dark when Odile screamed.

August came awake with a knife in her hand and her wards flaring blue along her wrists — and stopped, because Odile was not in danger. Odile was sitting bolt upright by the embers with the codex clutched to her chest, staring at its pages as though they'd begun speaking to her, which, given the week August was having, seemed entirely possible.

"What," August said. "What is it."

"The paper." Odile's voice shook. She held the codex out with both hands, and August saw it and felt the ground tilt beneath her for the second time in two days. "August. Look at the paper."

The codex was Odile's life's work — thirty years of secret reconstruction, copied onto whatever paper she could buy without questions. August had seen its pages a hundred times: cheap rag stock, yellowed, some of it water-stained, all of it ordinary.

Every page was blank.

No — not blank. August snatched the codex closer, heart hammering, and looked again, and saw that the pages were not empty. They were *waiting*. Faint lines ghosted beneath the surface of the paper, like writing sunk deep underwater, like words trying to rise. And as she watched, holding the book over the coals' dim light, one line near the top of the first page grew slowly, steadily darker — a single sentence surfacing letter by letter, in a hand none of them recognized, in a language August now knew better than she wished she did.

Odile translated in a whisper, her finger trembling under each word as it appeared.

"*The valley remembers. The valley has always remembered. Bring the key to the door beneath the tower, keeper, before the ones who unwrote us finish the work.*"

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the orchard trees went on blooming, softly, impossibly, filling the night with the scent of a spring that had ended a hundred years ago.

August looked up from the page and found Wren standing at the edge of the firelight, watching her, wide awake, his eyes reflecting the coals like two small lamps.

"Beneath the tower," she said slowly. "Wren. Is there a tower?"

The boy nodded once.

"And is it," August said, and her mouth had gone dry, "by any chance, in the middle of a city? A city called Vesper?"

Wren nodded again.

Behind her, Bram swore with feeling and at length, and Odile began, very quietly, to laugh — the slightly unhinged laugh of an archivist whose lifetime of stolen work had just started writing itself — and August sat holding the codex in her lap while a dead village dictated its first new sentence in a hundred years, and understood that the valley had not brought them here to mourn.

It had brought them here to give them orders.
