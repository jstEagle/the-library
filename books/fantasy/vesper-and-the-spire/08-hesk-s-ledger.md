## 8. Hesk's Ledger

Captain Edran Hesk had a theory about paperwork, and it was this: every lie in Vesper eventually had to be written down, and everything written down could be found again by a man patient enough to look.

He was, by his own modest accounting, the most patient man in the Warden service. He did not say so aloud. He simply kept a small leather notebook — not an official one, nothing with a serial number or a chain of custody, just a private ledger he'd bought for two coppers from a stationer who assumed he was a schoolmaster — and into that ledger went everything the official reports left out. Dates that didn't align. Witnesses whose stories matched too neatly. Lanterns that failed on Coppersmith Row at the precise hour a mud-streaked child had been seen running from a hedge-witch's door.

The lantern report bothered him. Not because lanterns failed — lanterns failed all the time in Vesper, cheap tallow and cheaper wicks — but because they had failed *together*. Four of them, four different patrolmen, in the space of six seconds. He had interviewed each man separately and gotten four versions of the same shrug.

"Windy," said Patrolman Corr.

"Damp," said Patrolman Vess.

"Bad batch of oil," said Patrolman Osk, who then admitted he'd filled his own lantern from his own jug that morning.

And Patrolman Dree, the youngest, had looked at his boots and said, "It felt like something *pinched* them, sir," and then gone pink as though he'd confessed to wetting the bed.

Hesk wrote *pinched* in his ledger and underlined it twice.

Now, on a grey morning that smelled of rain deciding whether to commit, he sat in the sub-basement of the Hall of Records with a lamp, a pot of cold tea, and three crates of confiscated material that had no business being in his custody at all. They had arrived the week before with a chit signed by someone in the Spire's administration — no name, just a seal — and a note instructing him to review them *in connection with ongoing matters on Coppersmith Row*.

Ongoing matters. He turned the phrase over the way he turned over everything: looking for the false bottom.

The crates were marked ARCHIVE RECOVERY, which was a strange phrase. Archives weren't recovered like lost dogs. And the contents were odder still — bundles of paper that had been burned and then, inexplicably, *saved*. Charred page-fragments sorted into envelopes. Ash pressed between sheets of glass. A ledger whose first hundred pages had been razored out so cleanly it looked surgical, leaving only the stubs and, here and there, the ghost of ink pressed through from the missing leaves.

Hesk put on his reading spectacles, which made him feel seventy instead of fifty-one, and began.

* * *

By midday he had learned three things.

First: whoever had destroyed these documents had done it carefully, methodically, and long ago — the burn patterns were old, the paper brittle as autumn leaf. This wasn't evidence of a recent crime. It was evidence of an old one, kept the way one keeps a trophy or a threat.

Second: the fragments that survived were written in a script he didn't recognize, dense and angular, with little marks above the vowels that looked like birds resting on a fence.

Third: somebody, at some point, had tried to salvage what they could. Tucked inside the third crate, beneath a false lining that Hesk found only because he always checked for false linings — it was, he felt, the professional courtesy one liar owed another — was a thin sheaf of modern paper. Common script. Recent ink. Someone's private notes, copying out what they could remember or reconstruct.

The handwriting was small, precise, and increasingly hurried as the pages went on, as if the writer feared being interrupted. At the top of the first sheet, a single line:

*They didn't lose the records. They fed them to the fire one page at a time and called it founding.*

Hesk read that sentence four times. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and read it a fifth time, because the first four readings had each produced a slightly different sensation in his chest and he wanted to be sure which one was accurate.

It was anger. He was surprised to find it was anger.

He was a Warden captain. He had spent twenty-six years enforcing the Concord — the great compact on which Vesper stood: no magic, no old kingdoms, no looking backward. Magic was chaos; the old kingdoms were chaos wearing a crown; nostalgia was chaos with better manners. He believed this the way he believed in load-bearing walls. You didn't examine it daily. You simply trusted the building to stand.

But he was also a man with a ledger, and ledgers have a way of asking questions.

He worked until the lamp guttered and the tea went fully cold, matching fragments to notes, notes to fragments. The salvaged sheets named places. Not Vesper's places — older ones. A river called the Sarn, which he strongly suspected was now called the Vesper River, because the city had a habit of renaming things the way a thief renames stolen horses. A district called the Weavers' Terraces, which sounded suspiciously like the Weft, where August Coldharbour sold her luck-knots. And rolls — rolls of names. Households. Guilds. Parishes. Thousands of names, most of them burned beyond recovery, but some preserved in the salvager's careful hand.

He was copying out a list of names from a parish roll — the parish of Saint Aldhelm's-in-the-Reeds, a name that meant nothing to him and yet snagged on something, the way a loose thread snags on a ring — when he stopped breathing.

Halfway down the column, in the salvager's neat hand:

*Hesk, Maren. Hesk, Odger. Hesk, the widow Tam and her sons Edran and Pell.*

Edran.

His own name. His father's name — Odger, dead thirty years, a customs clerk who had never once spoken of his childhood except to say, once, drunk on festival wine, *"We lived near water before we lived near walls."* Hesk had assumed he meant the docks.

And below the names, in the salvager's smaller annotation: *Collaborators' register. These households purchased continuance. See terms, folio 41 (burned).*

Purchased continuance.

He sat very still in the cold room while the rain finally committed outside, drumming on the high slit window. Somewhere above him, clerks stamped forms. Somewhere across the city, the Spire stood in its square of pale stone, beautiful and blameless, the pride of Vesper.

*These households purchased continuance.*

His family hadn't been erased. His family had helped hold the eraser.

* * *

He told himself, walking home through the wet evening, that it proved nothing. Names repeated. Half of Vesper was named after saints and the other half after occupations; somewhere in the city there were doubtless forty men named Edran Hesk, most of them probably bakers, none of them haunted.

But his father had never spoken of his childhood. His father had flinched — actually flinched, Hesk remembered it now with terrible clarity — whenever the Spire appeared in conversation, the way other men flinched at debt collectors. And his grandmother, the widow Tam of the roll, had died when Hesk was nine, and at her funeral his father had wept not like a man mourning his mother but like a man apologizing to her.

Hesk climbed the stairs to his rooms, lit his lamp, and took down the shoebox from the top of his wardrobe where he kept the family things nobody wanted: his mother's thimble, his commission letter, a bundle of his father's letters. He had read them all before, many times, hunting for warmth. Tonight he hunted for something else.

In the fourth letter — a dull, sweet letter about the price of coal — his father had written: *Your grandmother always said we should keep the garden gate oiled even in winter. She never said why. I think she was afraid of needing to leave quickly.*

Keep the gate oiled. In a city with no enemies. In a kingdom that had never existed.

Hesk sat with the letter until the candle burned low, and then he opened his private ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in his smallest, most careful hand:

*Question the first: if the law exists to protect the people, whom was it protecting when it burned them?*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. It was insubordinate. It was possibly treasonous. It was certainly true, and truth, he had learned, was the one thing in Vesper that required a permit.

He did not cross it out.

* * *

The next morning he requested a transfer.

He did it properly — the correct form, the correct queue, the correct tone of bureaucratic regret. *Personal circumstances. Family matters. Nothing affecting my fitness for duty.* All technically true. His personal circumstance was that he had found his family in a confiscated archive. His family matter was that they had been collaborators, and he was beginning to suspect the entire Warden service was the continuation of their crime, staffed generation after generation by the descendants of men who had bought their continuance and paid for it in obedience.

The clerk behind the counter took his form, glanced at the case number, and frowned.

"Coldharbour matter?"

"Ongoing matters on Coppersmith Row."

"That's flagged." The clerk's frown deepened. "Flagged means it doesn't transfer. Flagged means it comes back to you."

"I'm requesting—"

"I can read, Captain." The clerk stamped something anyway, the way clerks do, to make the machinery groan audibly. "It'll be denied. Probably today."

It was denied within the hour, and the denial came with a summons.

* * *

The office of Deputy Warden-General Corvane occupied the top floor of the Hall of Records, which Hesk had always thought was either a joke or a warning: the man who commanded the archives sat literally above them. Corvane himself was a neat, silvering man with the kind of face that seemed designed for coins, and he received Hesk standing at his window, looking out at the Spire.

"Captain Hesk." He didn't turn around. "Twenty-six years of service. Commendations for thoroughness. Known throughout the service as a man who finds things." Now he turned, smiling faintly. "That's a compliment, before you take it otherwise. Finding things is your gift. So let me ask you plainly: what did you find?"

Hesk had prepared three answers, ranked from safest to most honest. Standing in the warm lamplight with the Spire gleaming behind Corvane's shoulder, he discovered he couldn't remember any of them.

"Old paper," he said. "Burned old paper, kept for reasons no one has explained to me."

"And your request to leave the case?"

"Family matters, sir."

"Yes." Corvane returned to his desk and tapped a folder lying upon it. Hesk recognized the shape of it with a lurch: his transfer request, already denied, already annotated. "Your family is from the Reeds district originally. Old river family." Corvane said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man mentions weather, and Hesk felt the floor tilt a degree. "Good stock. Service stock, you might say. A great many Warden families come from the river districts. There's a reason for that, and the reason is loyalty, Captain, and loyalty is the whole of the law. Everything else is procedure."

"Sir, I only asked—"

"You asked to step away from the most sensitive matter this office has handled in a decade, citing reasons you declined to specify, one week after receiving materials you were specifically instructed to review." Corvane sat down at last and folded his hands. "Do you know what that looks like from where I sit?"

"It looks like a tired man asking for a quieter post."

"Mm. From where I sit it looks like a man finding things." The smile again, thinner. "Which is your gift. Which is why you'll keep the case."

Hesk waited. There was more. With men like Corvane there was always more, delivered like the second blade of a folding knife.

"You'll be assigned a partner," Corvane said. "Effective immediately. Warden-Sergeant Ilse Vair. Capable young officer. Excellent instincts." He slid a second folder across the desk. "She reports directly to this office. Weekly. In writing."

The room was very quiet. Rain ticked against the window. Downstairs, the archives held their breath around their burned secrets.

"You're pairing me with my own shadow," Hesk said.

"I'm pairing you with *oversight*. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you give me reason to need the other kind." Corvane stood, signaling an end, and offered his hand. Hesk shook it. The grip was dry and cool and entirely without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Find your witch, Captain. Find whatever the boy is. Close the matter cleanly. That's all anyone wants. Cleanly."

* * *

Warden-Sergeant Ilse Vair was waiting for him in the corridor, and Hesk knew her at once, though they'd never met — knew her the way you know a particular kind of weather coming. Young, maybe twenty-eight, uniform pressed to architectural standards, eyes that catalogued him in the first second and filed him somewhere. She fell into step beside him without being asked.

"Captain."

"Sergeant."

"I've read your file. Very impressive. The lantern incident on Coppersmith Row — your interviews are in the training materials now. *How to hear what a witness isn't saying.*" She smiled, and it was a genuinely pleasant smile, which unsettled him more than a hostile one would have. "I'm looking forward to working with you, sir. I understand you keep excellent notes."

They descended the stairs together, past the archive floors, past the sealed doors, past the smell of ash and dust that Hesk now understood was not dust at all but the residue of a country, still hanging in the air of its own tomb.

"Excellent notes," Vair repeated pleasantly, at the bottom of the stairs. "Where do you keep them, sir? For coordination purposes."

"My head," said Hesk, "like everyone else's."

She laughed as though he'd said something charming, and wrote something small in a notebook of her own, and Hesk walked out into the rain with his collar up and his ledger against his ribs like a second heart, thinking:

*They've given me a watcher. Which means they believe there's something worth watching. Which means the witch, the boy, the burned country — it's all real, and they know it, and they sent me to close the matter cleanly.*

He turned toward Coppersmith Row, and the rain, and the small crooked street where a hedge-witch sold luck to people who couldn't afford it, and he found — to his considerable professional alarm — that he was no longer certain which side of the hunt he was walking toward.
