## 9. The Gull Court Collects

The river had been kind to them for three days, which August should have known was only the river lulling them before it did something terrible.

She'd grown up on canals, not rivers — Vesper's waterways were narrow, cobbled, and smelled of cabbage and tanneries — but she was learning the difference quickly. A canal was a servant. A river was an animal with opinions. The Sarnwater ran wide and brown-green between reed banks, carrying them north through country that grew emptier by the mile, and Bram Tallow piloted his flat-bottomed boat, the *Kindly Grist*, with the easy competence of a man who had spent more of his life on water than on land.

"Named her after my grandmother," he'd said on the first morning, catching August's look at the name. "Granny Tallow. Meanest woman in three parishes. Ground wheat like it owed her money." He'd grinned. "Boat suits her."

August was beginning to like Bram, which worried her. In her experience, liking people was how you got them hurt.

Wren, meanwhile, had discovered the boat's roof. He spent his days up there among the coiled rope and the tarred canvas, watching the banks slide past, and at night he came down smelling of river wind and slept like a stone that had finally been allowed to stop rolling. His dreams still talked, though. August had taken to keeping a stub of pencil and Odile's spare paper by her bedroll, because twice now Wren had murmured something in the old tongue that matched a phrase from the codex, and she wanted it written down before sleep let go of it.

*The tower leans,* one fragment said. *The tower leans and the door remembers.*

Odile sat across from her now in the boat's low cabin, mending a tear in her sleeve with the fierce concentration of a woman who considered needlework beneath her and intended to win anyway. She was fifty-something and built like a filing cabinet, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles she polished so often they were more scratch than glass.

"You're doing it again," Odile said without looking up.

"Doing what?"

"Brooding. You get a line right here—" she tapped the space between her own brows— "like a ledger that won't balance."

"I'm thinking about Hesk," August admitted. The Warden captain had let them go — or failed to catch them; the distinction felt thinner every day. Somewhere behind them, downriver, a man was reading confiscated archives and finding his own family name in them. She hoped it was breaking him open in a useful way. She wasn't sure hope worked like that.

"Bram says we'll reach the moor country tomorrow evening," Odile said. "If the weather holds."

"If the weather holds," August agreed, "and if nothing else goes wrong."

Odile snorted. "You said that out loud. On a river. Never say that out loud on a river."

* * *

They heard the other boat before they saw it — or rather, Wren heard it. He came sliding down from the roof so fast he nearly took the ladder off its hooks, and pressed himself against August's side, saying one word over and over in the old tongue. She didn't need the codex for this one. Some words translate themselves.

*Gulls.*

Bram went still at the tiller in a way that made August's stomach drop. He tilted his head, listening, and she watched ten years of easy humor drain out of his face like water through a cracked hull.

"That's the *Marrow Eel*," he said quietly. "That's Corvo Salt's boat."

"Friend of yours?"

"Corvo Salt is not anybody's friend. Corvo Salt is what the Gull Court sends when they've stopped sending letters." He was already moving, kicking open a hatch, hauling out a canvas sack. "Listen to me. They'll want coin. I owe them coin — more than I have, which is why we're on this river instead of in a tavern. Give them what's in the strongbox, be polite, keep the boy behind you and keep him quiet, and with luck they'll take my money and my dignity and leave us the boat."

"And if they don't?"

Bram looked at her, and for once there was no grin anywhere on him. "Then I've been polite for nothing."

The *Marrow Eel* came around the bend like it owned the bend. It was longer than the *Kindly Grist*, black-hulled, with a figurehead of a gull that someone had thoughtfully given brass eyes, so that even at distance it seemed to be watching you specifically. Six figures stood along its rail. Not one of them was holding a rope.

"They're not working," Odile observed. "On a boat that size, nobody stands idle unless they want to look like they could."

"Thank you for the tactical analysis," Bram muttered. "Very comforting."

The *Marrow Eel* swung alongside with a bump that shivered the deck plates, and grappling hooks came over the rail — three of them, casual as a handshake. A gangplank followed. And then Corvo Salt himself stepped aboard the *Kindly Grist* as though he were arriving at a dinner party slightly early.

He was smaller than August expected. That was somehow worse. Small men who did this work had stopped needing size years ago. He wore a good coat, river-damp at the hem, and a smile that reached all the way to his eyes, which was the most frightening thing about him. Behind him came two of his crew: a huge woman with a boathook held like a walking stick, and a thin man whose job, judging by the ledger under his arm, was arithmetic.

"Bram Tallow," Corvo said warmly. "Alive. You know, there was a pool."

"A pool?"

"On whether we'd find you floating. I had two crowns on yes, personally, so this is genuinely disappointing." He spread his hands. "But look at me, complaining, when I should be welcoming you home. Where are my manners? Where's my money?"

Bram produced the strongbox. "There's forty crowns in there. It's most of what I have. I need another month for the rest, Corvo, and I swear on Granny Tallow's grave—"

"Don't swear on graves, Bram, it's morbid and it never holds up in court." Corvo nodded to the thin man, who set the ledger on a crate and opened it with the reverence of scripture. "Now. Let's see. Forty crowns. Against a debt of..." The thin man turned the ledger around and tapped a number with one finger, apologetically, as if the number had done something embarrassing in public.

August didn't know much about money, having deliberately kept as little of it as possible for a decade, but she knew enough to understand that the debt had grown while they'd been on the river. Interest, Bram had called it. The Gull Court called it, apparently, whatever the ledger said it was.

"That's not—" Bram started.

"It's exactly," Corvo said pleasantly. "It's precisely. That's the whole beauty of it. Numbers don't have moods, Bram. Numbers don't have bad days. You borrowed on a Tuesday, and every day since has been a small Tuesday, forever." He sighed, almost fondly. "Forty crowns is a lovely gesture. It buys you... let me see..." He glanced at the thin man. "Four days."

"Four days," Bram repeated.

"Of continued breathing. It's a start! I believe in starts." Corvo clasped his hands. "Now, about the rest. Because here's my problem, Bram, and I want you to hear it as the friendly problem it is. Upstairs — and when I say upstairs I mean *upstairs*, the top floor, the gulls' nest itself — somebody has been asking questions. Somebody with better boots than mine. And those questions aren't about you."

The cabin had gone very quiet. August became aware of Wren beside her, small and silent, and moved — casually, she hoped — half a step in front of him.

"The questions are about a boy," Corvo said. "A boy found on the moor. Mute, or playing at it. Speaks a language nobody speaks." His smile widened by exactly one tooth. "And there's a price on finding him. A price, Bram, that makes your debt look like a rounding error. A price that would clear every name in that ledger and buy the ledger a nicer cover."

Every instinct August had honed in ten years of hiding screamed at once. *Someone knows.* Not the Wardens — or not only the Wardens. Someone above the syndicate, someone with reach into both worlds, had posted a bounty on a child who spoke a dead language, and the ordinary criminal world had folded itself around that knowledge like paper around a blade.

"We haven't seen any boy," Bram said.

"Oh, Bram." Corvo looked genuinely hurt. "I boarded your boat. Do you think I board boats for conversation? Nix." He snapped his fingers, and the huge woman with the boathook moved — fast, so much faster than a person that size had any right to move — and hauled open the hatch to the sleeping cabin.

Empty. August's heart performed a complicated maneuver. Then she remembered: Wren. Clever, river-raised-in-nothing, feral little Wren, who had spent three days learning every shadow on this boat. He wasn't in the cabin. He was somewhere in the dark under the cargo boards, curled up smaller than a cat, being nothing at all.

"No boy," the huge woman said.

"Hm." Corvo's eyes swept the cabin, unhurried, and August watched them pass over the loose floorboard near the ballast and keep moving. Either he hadn't seen it, or he was saving it. With men like this you rarely got to know which until it was too late. "Well. Perhaps the stories are wrong. Stories usually are. But Bram — four days. And if a boy happens to wash up against your hull in the meantime, remember that upstairs pays finders, and the Gull Court pays finders *better*."

He turned to leave. And that was when Wren, in the dark under the floor, sneezed.

* * *

Later, August would try to reconstruct those next ninety seconds and fail. Memory gave her pieces: the boathook slamming down through the floorboards; splinters like white teeth; Wren bursting up out of the dark with his hair full of dust and his eyes full of something old and furious; the lanterns — all the lanterns, on both boats — guttering at once, as if the river had pinched them out between finger and thumb.

In the sudden dark, everything happened by sound. Bram roaring — actually roaring, the mild smuggler gone, some older Bram in his place. Odile's voice cracking out sharp as a whip-crack: "*Down!*". The crack of the gangplank parting under someone's boot. Wren's voice, high and ringing, speaking words August didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand thunder — and the *Marrow Eel*'s brass-eyed gull figurehead groaning, and the black hull shudding away from theirs as if shoved by a hand the size of the sky.

Then light again — someone had relit a storm-lamp — and chaos resolving into aftermath.

The *Marrow Eel* was fifteen feet downstream and drifting, its crew scrambling, its grapples torn loose. Corvo Salt stood at the *Kindly Grist*'s rail no longer, because the rail he'd been standing beside was now occupied by Bram, who had him by the collar with one hand and the strongbox lid in the other, and was holding him over the water with the tenderness of a man deciding.

"New arithmetic," Bram said. "You're going to tell whoever wears the best boots upstairs that you found nothing. No boy. No witch. Nothing but a poor debtor and his grandmother's boat. And in exchange, I'm going to forget the pool."

"You wouldn't dare," Corvo said, and then, because the river was right there under his excellent coat, "Bram. *Bram.*"

"Numbers don't have moods, Corvo." Bram dropped him — into the shallows beside the *Marrow Eel*, where his crew hauled him out dripping and furious and, crucially, alive. "Four days!" Bram called cheerfully after him. "I believe in starts!"

The *Marrow Eel* backed water and slid away around the bend, and the river closed over the whole encounter like a page turning.

* * *

They took stock in the grey of early evening, and the stock was grim.

The boathook had gone through the fresh-water cask. Two crates of Bram's legitimate cargo — tinware, of all things — had gone overboard during the fight, and one of the *Marrow Eel*'s parting grapples had torn a gash in the hull above the waterline that was above the waterline *now*, in summer, and would not stay that way in weather.

More than that: the *Kindly Grist* was known now. Her name was in Corvo Salt's ledger and in his mouth, and by tomorrow it would be in every syndicate ear from the delta to the moors. Bram sat on a crate amid the mess, turning his grandmother's boat's name over and over with his thumb where it was painted on the tiller housing, and August watched him do the sums he didn't want to do.

"She won't make the moor country," he said at last. "Not with that gash and not with every gull on the river looking for her. I'd be sailing a bell with my name on it." He laughed, and it was a real laugh, which somehow made it worse. "Ten years I've kept her. You know what the funny thing is? I always figured the Wardens would be what sank me. Or the debt. Never figured on a mute child sneezing."

"He saved us," August said. "Before the sneeze, he was hidden. After it, he put their boat in the water. That's not nothing, Bram."

"I know it's not nothing." He rubbed his face. "That's what frightens me. Boy did with a word what I couldn't do with twenty years of knowing this river. What *is* he, August? Truly. Not the story version. The true version."

"I don't know yet," she said. It was the honest answer, and it cost her. "But I know people want him badly enough to pay a syndicate's debts for him, and that means whatever he is, it's bigger than hiding. We stopped being able to hide the moment Corvo Salt smiled at us."

Bram was quiet a long time. The river muttered against the hull. Up on the roof, Wren sat with Odile, who was showing him — of all things — how to mend the torn sail, guiding his small hands through the stitches, and the sight of it did something to August's chest that she chose not to examine.

"There's a slough, two miles up," Bram said finally. "Reed-choked, unmarked, the kind of hole maps apologize for. I can beach her there, patch her proper, and come spring—" He stopped. Started again. "No. Come spring she's collateral. You know that. First port with a Gull Court clerk, she's seized." He patted the tiller housing once, hard, like a man closing a coffin. "So. We beach her, we strip what's worth carrying, and the *Kindly Grist* retires undefeated. And we walk."

"Walk where?"

"Upstream, witch. To your valley that isn't on any map." He stood, wincing at his ribs — he'd taken a boathook butt across them during the fight and refused, absolutely refused, to let her charm it. "Because if the Gull Court knows about the boy, and the Wardens know about the boy, and *somebody upstairs* knows about the boy, then the only place left that doesn't know anything is the place nobody remembers. And I want to see it before everyone else does."

August looked at the darkening river, at the wake the *Marrow Eel* had left, already smoothed away as though no black boat had ever come. The ordinary world — debts and ledgers and pools on whether a man floated — had reached for them today, and it had reached with someone else's orders in its fist. Whatever waited upstream, it was no longer just Wardens they were running from. Something older and better dressed had joined the hunt.

"Wren," she called softly, up to the roof.

The boy looked down at her, needle in hand, a smudge of sail-thread on his chin. When she beckoned, he came, and she took his small hand in hers and asked him, in the careful syllables of the old tongue she was only beginning to own:

*Are you afraid?*

Wren considered the question with the seriousness he brought to everything. Then he shook his head, and answered in a whisper that was almost steady:

*The door remembers names. Soon it will remember yours.*

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, Odile had gone very still over her mending — still in the particular way of a woman hearing something she had read once, thirty years ago, in a book that was supposed to have burned.
