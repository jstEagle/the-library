## 10. Odile's Confession

The fire was Bram's idea.

"We're not building a fire," August had said, when he'd dragged the boat into a reed-choked inlet a mile short of the ruins and announced they were making camp. "Smoke is visible. Smoke is *evidence*."

"Smoke is also warm," Bram said, already stacking driftwood with the cheerful incompetence of a man who had never once in his life been cold on purpose. "And I've got a hole in my hull the size of a dinner plate, no payment coming from anyone until we find whatever it is we're finding, and a knee that sings hymns when it rains. We're stopping."

It was raining, as it happened — a thin, needling drizzle that came off the river in veils. Wren stood at the waterline with his sleeves pushed up, watching the rain land on the surface, and when August glanced over she saw that where the drops struck near him, they landed in little rings of stillness, as though the water were listening to him.

She decided not to think about that. She was getting quite good at deciding not to think about things; it was practically her profession.

The fire, once lit, turned out to be small and well-behaved, tucked into a hollow beneath an overhang of clay bank, its smoke crawling low along the ground instead of climbing. Bram swore this was a smuggler's trick. Odile, settling onto an upturned crate with the groan of a woman whose bones had opinions, said it was actually a trick she'd invented for him three years ago, but she was willing to let him have it.

They ate river fish scorched on sticks, hard bread softened in the pot, and tea brewed from pine needles because that was all there was. Wren ate like a small wolf and then sat close to the fire with his knees drawn up, turning his hands palm-up toward the heat, and hummed something under his breath — two notes, rising, falling — that made the flames lean very slightly toward him before August caught his eye and he stopped, abashed, the way a child stops whistling in church.

"Don't scold him," Odile said quietly. "It's only a hearth-song. They used to sing it to bank fires for the night." She said it absently, staring into the flames, and then her own words seemed to catch up with her, and August watched her go still the way a clock goes still — everything moving right up to a certain moment and then nothing.

"You knew that," August said. "You knew what it was."

Odile did not answer. She was a narrow woman, dry as a ledger spine, with grey hair pinned in a knot so severe it looked load-bearing, and she had spent the entire journey upriver being either brisk or asleep, with nothing in between. Now she sat with her teacup balanced on her knee and looked into the fire for a long time.

"I knew," she said finally, "because I helped burn them."

Bram stopped chewing. Even the rain seemed to hold off a beat, drumming once and pausing.

"The songs," Odile said. "The archives. All of it." She turned the cup slowly on her knee. "I was nineteen years old. Junior clerk, Third Rank, Office of Records and Reconciliation. Do you know what 'reconciliation' meant, in those offices? It meant reconciling the country with its past by removing the past. There were four hundred of us. Four hundred clerks in grey coats, and we worked in shifts around the clock for eleven months, and we burned every page of Cael Sarn that could be found, and we were given medals."

August felt the ground do the thing it had been doing lately — that slow tilt, as if the whole world were a table someone was patiently lifting one leg of. She'd known, since Odile had identified Wren's language, that the records hadn't been lost. She'd known they'd been destroyed. But knowing a thing in the abstract and hearing it from a person who had held the pages in her own hands were different animals entirely.

"Eleven months," Bram said. "That's — how much paper is that?"

"Nine hundred thousand documents," Odile said. "Give or take. Charters, tax rolls, letters, recipes, children's copybooks, love poems, court transcripts, seed inventories, funeral records. A country is not a king and a flag. A country is nine hundred thousand small pieces of paper that say *we were here, we mattered, we owed each other things.*" She smiled, and it was not a nice smile; it was the smile of a woman doing arithmetic with knives. "We fed all of it into the furnaces under the Hall of Records. The ash came down on the city for a week. People complained about their washing."

Wren had gone very still. He was looking at Odile with an expression August couldn't read — not anger, not fear. Something older than both.

"I believed in it," Odile went on, and her voice didn't waver, which somehow made it worse. "That's the part you should understand, August, because you keep looking at me like I'm going to confess to having been a monster, and I wasn't. I was *right*. That's what I was. The old kingdom had torn itself apart — you've seen what the Spire sits on, or you will. The last war of Cael Sarn killed one in three people alive. The founders of Vesper looked at that and said: never again, and the surest way to make sure of never again is to make sure there is no *again* to return to. No maps. No names. No door." She lifted the cup and drank her terrible pine-needle tea like it was a sacrament. "Peace needs a foundation. We gave it one. We just happened to build it out of other people."

"And now?" August asked. "What changed?"

Odile set the cup down. She reached into her coat — that heavy, shapeless coat she wore even in summer, the one August had privately catalogued as *archivist, refuses to dress appropriately* — and drew out a book.

Not a large book. Octavo, bound in grey leather gone soft and dark at the corners, its spine hand-stitched, its edges worn round with handling. It had no title. It didn't need one. August knew what it was the way you know a face in a crowd you've been dreading or hoping to see.

"Thirty years ago," Odile said, "I stood in front of the last furnace on the last night, and I was given the honor of burning the final crate myself. And in that crate I found a letter. Just a letter. A woman writing to her sister about an orchard, and a lame horse, and whether the girl should marry the cooper's son. Nothing important. Nothing seditious. It was the most ordinary page in the entire archive." Her thumb moved along the book's cover, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, like touching a scar. "And I thought: I am the last person alive who will ever read this sentence. When it burns, it will be as if she never wrote it, as if the sister never got it, as if the orchard never grew. And I couldn't."

"So you kept it."

"So I kept it. One page. Then it was ten pages, because keeping one page is a sickness and it spreads. Then it was that I couldn't sleep, because I realized I was forgetting — that I'd handled nine hundred thousand documents and my memory was letting them go, dozens every night, whole drawers of lives evaporating while I lay there. So I started writing. What I remembered. Everything I remembered. At first it was fragments — a name here, a trade route there. But memory is a muscle, August, and I had spent my whole life training it. The more I wrote, the more came back. I requested transfers to provincial offices so I could visit places that had been Cael Sarn and walk the roads and match what I saw against what I recalled. I interviewed old people who pretended not to remember and then, after enough wine, remembered everything." She looked up, and firelight caught the lines of her face and filled them. "Thirty years. This codex is the only complete record of Cael Sarn in existence. I am the archive now. I burned it, and then I became it. There's a symmetry there that I'm sure some poet would find meaningful, and I find it mostly exhausting."

Bram let out a long breath. "How big is it?"

"Six hundred and forty pages. And an index." She said *and an index* the way other people said *and a firstborn child.*

August leaned forward. "The Door of Return. You mentioned it before — beneath the Spire. Is that in here?"

For the first time all evening, Odile hesitated.

"It's in here," she said. "But I want you to understand what you're asking for before I show you. Because I've had thirty years to think about why the founders sealed the vaults instead of emptying them, and why they built the Spire on top, and why the Door of Return appears in exactly three documents in my codex and none of them say plainly what it does." She turned the book over in her hands. "When you burn a country, August, you don't burn the people. Not all of them. Some surrendered. Some collaborated — bought their survival with names, with information, with silence. And some refused. Refused to be unwritten. And the founders discovered that you cannot erase a person who refuses. You can unmake their village, strike their language from every record, teach their grandchildren to flinch at their own lullabies — but the ones who refuse, the ones who hold on, they stay *written*. Somewhere. In whatever place a thing goes when everyone agrees it's gone."

"The vaults," August said quietly. Beneath the Spire. Beneath the grandeur. She thought of Wren's dreams — the tower falling, the door that remembers names — and felt cold despite the fire.

"The vaults," Odile agreed. "The Spire isn't a monument. It's a lid. And the Door of Return is the one thing the founders couldn't destroy, because — and here is the joke, the enormous, ugly joke — because the Door doesn't belong to Vesper. It belongs to Cael Sarn. It was theirs before the founders ever laid a stone, and it is the one piece of the old country that would not burn, would not break, would not forget. So they built over it. Buried it. Put their shining tower on top of it and called the tower a triumph." She opened the codex at last, carefully, to a ribbon-marked page, and turned it toward the firelight. "Here. Transcript of a founder's private memorandum, year one of the Interregnum. '*The arch below the foundations resists all demolition. Recommendation: entombment and perpetual watch. Let no man speak of it. What is buried and unnamed cannot be opened.*'"

"*Cannot be opened,*" August repeated. "Or *will not*?"

"That," said Odile, "is the question I have been unable to answer for thirty years, and it is the reason I am sitting in the rain eating burnt fish with a smuggler, a hedge-witch, and a child who snuffs lanterns." She closed the codex gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room. "Because there are references. Fragments. A line in a founder's diary: '*the Door asks.*' A marginal note in a surveyor's report: '*it wants a voice.*' Something in the Door's nature requires more than keys. And I believe — I have come, over three decades, reluctantly, to believe — that Wren was not lost, and Wren was not sent by accident. I believe the boy is a message. And I believe the message says the Door has finished waiting."

The fire popped. Out on the black river, rain needled the water, and somewhere far downstream a night bird called three notes, rising, falling — almost, but not quite, the hearth-song.

Wren moved. He crossed the space between his place and Odile's and knelt beside her, and he put one small mud-browned hand flat on the cover of the codex, the way you'd rest your hand on a sleeping animal, or a grave. Then he looked up at Odile and spoke — a short phrase, soft, in the language that made wards flare and lanterns die.

Odile's eyes filled. She didn't let them spill; thirty years of practice, probably. "He says," she translated, and her voice was rough, "*thank you for keeping us.*"

Nobody said anything for a moment. Bram blew his nose loudly and blamed the smoke.

Then Odile straightened, and August watched the transformation happen — watched guilt finish its long, slow work and become something else, something with edges and direction. The archivist squared her shoulders like a soldier taking up a standard.

"So here is what we know, and here is what we must decide," Odile said, all business now, tapping the codex. "The Door lies beneath the Spire, reachable — I believe — through the old service ways, the undercity, the tunnels the founders used to haul stone and later to hide what they'd stolen. My codex contains three separate accounts of the approaches, though all are a century stale. The Wardens are hunting us, the Gull Court is hunting Bram, and the Spire itself is failing, which means whatever balance the founders built is coming undone with or without our help." She looked around the fire, at each of them in turn, and finished dryly: "In summary, our plan is to sneak into the most heavily guarded structure in the country through its sewage, guided by a book written from memory by a traitor, accompanied by a child who may or may not be a key. Questions?"

"Yes," said Bram. "Whose fault is it when we die? Asking for inheritance purposes."

"Yours, obviously. You're driving the boat."

August laughed — surprised by it, the sound escaping before she could file it properly — and Wren laughed too, a bright startled bark of a laugh, and for one moment the firelit hollow felt less like a hiding place and more like a kitchen, like the kind of room where people belonged.

Then Wren went quiet. His head tilted, listening, the way he did — and August saw the color leave his face before she heard anything herself.

Downriver, faint but closing, came the sound of oars. Many oars. Rowed in rhythm, by people who did not care who heard them coming.

Odile closed the codex and slid it into her coat. "Change of plans," she said.
