## 11. Into the Undercity

The tunnel mouth opened behind a tannery on the Saltwash, which was Bram's way of saying *nobody will look for us here* and also his way of apologizing for the smell.

"Mind the first hundred paces," he said, holding the lantern low. "The floor's honest enough, but the walls like to shed."

The walls did indeed like to shed. August kept one hand on the damp brick and one on Wren's shoulder as they descended a ramp that had been a road once — she could tell by the wheel-ruts worn into the stone, deep as finger-grooves in soft cheese. Above them, muffled through earth and brick and the tannery's unfortunate business, Vesper went about its evening: cart wheels, a drunk singing something patriotic and off-key, the far bell of the curfew.

Below it all, the city had bones. August had lived ten years in Vesper without ever once thinking of what lay under her boots. She suspected most people managed the same trick. It was easier to love a city if you didn't ask what it stood on.

"Odile," she said. "How far to the first marker?"

Odile Ferrant held her codex close to the lantern light. The book was a homely thing — rebound twice, its pages sewn from three different eras of paper, some of it water-stained, some of it written in ink so old it had gone brown as dried blood. Thirty years of a guilty woman's memory, copied out by candlelight. "There should be a junction with a carved keystone. A sunburst with seven rays." She paused. "If my memory is accurate."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we are lost beneath a city that wants us dead, and I shall feel very silly about it." Odile adjusted her spectacles. "I was a clerk for eleven years before they let me touch anything important. Accuracy became a matter of survival. My memory is accurate."

Bram snorted. "That's the most frightening sentence anyone's said all week."

They walked. The ramp became stairs, the stairs became a corridor tall enough for two men standing on each other's shoulders, and the air changed — cooler, sweeter, with a mineral tang like the inside of a cave. August noticed it first as a prickling along her forearms, the place where her magic lived closest to the skin. The wards she'd woven into her own coat hem years ago, small defensive things no bigger than cobwebs, began to hum.

Not warning-hum. Something else. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite.

"Wren?" she said softly.

The boy had gone still. In the lantern light his face looked older than its years, which was a thing August was learning happened to him whenever the old language came near — as though remembering added weight to him, grain by grain. His lips moved. No sound came out, but she caught the shape of it, the rounded vowels she'd begun to know in fragments on the river: *aereth*, *sarn*, the word Maren's people used for *home* that had no Common translation because home, for them, had been a country and not a house.

"He can feel it," Odile murmured. "The Spire's roots run deeper than anyone admits. We're walking into the edge of its shadow already."

"Is that dangerous?" Bram asked. He was trying to sound casual and mostly achieving *hungry wolf pretending to be a dog*.

"For him? No." Odile watched Wren with an expression August couldn't quite read — tenderness braided with guilt, the face of a woman looking at something she'd helped bury. "For us, possibly. For the city above—" She closed the codex gently. "Ask me again when we're under the Spire itself."

* * *

The keystone was there, exactly where thirty-year-old memory said it would be: a sunburst with seven rays, carved into the junction arch, worn nearly smooth. Below it, someone had scratched newer graffiti — a crude drawing of the Spire with a crown on top, and beneath it, in blocky Common letters: **THE SPIRE WATCHES**.

"Charming neighborhood," Bram said.

Four tunnels branched away from the junction. Three were choked with rubble or worse; the fourth ran straight and dry, its floor swept clean in a way that made August's neck prickle. Tunnels didn't sweep themselves.

"People use this," she said.

"Smugglers," Bram agreed. "Gull Court runners, mostly. There's a whole economy down here nobody upstairs knows about. Or pretends not to." He rubbed the back of his neck, where a fading bruise from the syndicate enforcers' visit had gone yellow-green. "Which is why we keep moving and don't chat with anyone. I owe too many people money for chatting to be safe."

August looked at the four mouths of the tunnels, then back the way they'd come. The ramp up to the tannery. The night market. Her little rented rooms with their crooked chimney and the loose third stair she'd never fixed because fixing things meant staying.

Every exit was still open. That was the thing she kept noticing, the way you notice a door left ajar in winter. They could turn around. Go north over the moor, take ship from some harbor where nobody asked names, find a village small enough that a hedge-witch's charms would be welcome coin and a mute boy would be nobody's miracle. She could teach Wren Common. He'd grow up. She'd grow old. The Spire would go on standing over Vesper like a needle through the heart of a lie, and she would have chosen the small warm life, and no one — not even she herself — could call it wrong.

She thought about the well at the unwritten valley, still sweet after however many years. Somebody had tended that orchard once. Somebody had sung children to sleep in a language that now existed only in one boy's mouth and one old woman's codex.

"August?" Odile said. "You've stopped."

"I'm deciding," August said.

"Ah." Odile waited. She was good at waiting; thirty years of secret copying taught a person patience the way rivers teach stones.

"The thing is," August said, "I spent ten years being careful. Small charms, small coins, small life. Never magic anyone could trace, never stay anywhere long. And it worked. I'm alive. That's the whole achievement — I'm alive, and I'm careful, and I am *so tired*." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and pressed on before pity could arrive. "Wren knocked on my door and every careful thing I built fell over like a stack of plates. And the strange part is I don't miss it. The carefulness. It turns out it was just loneliness wearing an apron."

Bram shifted his weight. "For what it's worth," he said, "I'd have taken the ship north. Debt's easier to outrun than Wardens." Then he looked at Wren — really looked, the way he rarely let himself look at anything — and sighed from somewhere near his boots. "But I've been running debts for six years and all it's bought me is a faster walk. Maybe there's a difference between running and going somewhere." He lifted the lantern. "Straight tunnel it is."

August took Wren's hand. His fingers curled around hers with surprising strength, and she felt it again — that hum, that pull, faint as a plucked string two streets away. The boy wasn't afraid. That frightened her more than fear would have. He was walking toward the Spire's roots the way water finds its level, without hesitation, without choice, or perhaps with the only kind of choice that mattered.

"Onward, then," she said, and stepped into the straight tunnel, and felt — distinctly, unmistakably — the exits closing behind her. Not physically. Nothing so dramatic. Just the sense that the woman who might have turned around had stayed behind at the junction, a small careful ghost in a small careful life, waving goodbye.

* * *

An hour down, they found the first of the old doors.

It was set into the tunnel wall where no door had any business being: oak, iron-banded, its wood black with age, and across its lintel ran a line of carving that made Odile stop so suddenly Bram walked into her.

"What is it?"

"That's not decoration." Odile's voice had gone thin. She raised the codex, compared, and August watched thirty years of guilt rearrange themselves around new evidence. "It's a street name. This was a doorway in Cael Sarn. Someone *moved it here*."

"Moved a door," Bram repeated flatly.

"When they built Vesper's foundations. When they—" Odile's jaw worked. "When they took the capital apart for materials. Doors, lintels, keystones, hearthstones. You don't quarry a living city, you *dismantle* it. And the pieces came down here, into the dark, and got built into walls, and everyone upstairs agreed to forget what they were looking at." She touched the carved letters with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "*Caelmar Row.* This was a street where people lived. There were probably flowerpots on these steps."

August felt Wren's hand tighten in hers. The boy had drifted toward the door and laid his palm flat against the oak, and where he touched it, August saw — or thought she saw, or refused to disbelieve — a faint warmth spreading through the black wood, like breath fogging a cold window. And from behind the door, from whatever bricked-up room it now opened onto, came a sound.

A hum. Low, cracked, out of practice. A voice, singing half a lullaby it only half remembered, in the old tongue.

Wren sang the other half.

His voice was small and clear and it filled the tunnel the way light fills a jar. The answering voice behind the door faltered, then joined, and for eight bars — August counted them, she would count them for the rest of her life — something that had been alone in the dark for longer than Wren had been alive was not alone.

Then the song ended. Silence pooled. Wren took his hand off the door and stepped back to August's side, and his eyes when he looked up at her were wet and calm, and she understood that he hadn't done it for the prisoner behind the door. He'd done it for himself. You cannot carry a language alone. Even a river needs banks.

"We keep moving," Odile whispered, and her voice was wrecked. "There will be more doors. If we stop at every one—"

"There'll be more doors," August agreed quietly. "And we'll stop at none of them, and I will hate myself efficiently later." She squeezed Wren's hand. "Come on, little wren. Sing them a promise instead. Tell them we're coming."

He did. One line, repeated as they walked away, growing fainter behind them but never quite fading — August chose to believe it carried, down every bricked-in passage, to every ear pressed against every stolen door:

*We remember. Hold on. We remember.*

* * *

The last stretch before the roots was guarded, in the way that everything in Vesper was guarded: cheaply and by fear.

They smelled the checkpoint before they saw it — tallow smoke and wet wool. Around a bend, the tunnel widened into a chamber where smuggler traffic funneled past a plank table lit by two storm-lanterns. Three men sat behind it. Two had cudgels. The third had a ledger and the air of a man who had once dreamed of owning a shop and had settled for taxing people in a hole in the ground.

"Toll," said the ledger man, without looking up. "Two bits a head, five for the big one." He nodded at Bram, who was indeed the biggest thing in the tunnel including the architecture.

Bram opened his mouth — August could see the bravado loading — and she stepped forward first.

"Friend," she said pleasantly, "we're expected. The Gull Court sent us down to check on your collections." She let a small charm rise, nothing visible, nothing traceable — just warmth, just the suggestion of authority, the same trick that made luck-knots sell better and fever break faster. Confidence, bottled and uncorked. "There's been talk of an audit."

The ledger man looked up. Looked at her plain gray traveling cloak, her unremarkable face, her utter absence of weapons. Looked at Odile's spectacles, at Wren, at Bram's bruised neck. Whatever the charm showed him, it wasn't any of that.

"Audit," he said, in the voice of a man tasting a word he disliked.

"Routine," August said. "You wouldn't even be mentioned. Unless there's a problem."

"There's no problem," said one of the cudgel men quickly, because men who sit in holes collecting tolls have finely tuned instincts about problems, and the word *audit* had landed in his stomach like a swallowed hook.

"Good." August smiled the smile she used on customers who haggled over fever-charms while their children coughed. "Carry on."

They carried on. It was only fifty paces past the table, when the checkpoint was safely behind them, that Bram leaned down and muttered, "That was the single most terrifying thing I've ever seen you do, and I once watched you set a man's tooth with a fishing line and a prayer."

"It wasn't a prayer," August said. "It was a suggestion. Prayers are for people with someone to pray to." She glanced sideways at him. "Besides, you were about to fight three men over two bits apiece."

"I was about to *negotiate*—"

"You were absolutely going to fight them."

"I might have fought them," Bram admitted. "A little."

Ahead, the tunnel began to change. The brick gave way to older work — great blocks of pale stone fitted without mortar, each one carved, though the carvings had been chiseled flat in long deliberate gouges, as if someone had gone down the length of the passage erasing faces. Erasing names. August ran her fingers over one defaced panel and felt the ghost of grooves beneath the gouges: a border of vines, and where a figure should have stood, a smooth wound in the stone.

"They scraped them," Odile said behind her. "Even down here. Even where no one would see." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, bright and broken. "Of course they did. That's the whole point of erasure. You don't leave it where visitors might stumble on it. You dig it out of the *cellars*."

Wren had stopped walking.

He stood in the center of the passage with his head tipped back, and August followed his gaze upward and forgot how to breathe. The ceiling rose here — the tunnel becoming the base of some vast shaft, climbing into darkness beyond the lantern's reach. And the shaft was lined with the pale blocks, hundreds of them, thousands, and they were not defaced. They were too high to reach. High overhead, untouched by any chisel, carved figures danced and harvested and embraced around the rim of the dark: a frieze a quarter-mile long, a whole city's worth of life, preserved the way flies are preserved in amber — perfectly, helplessly, upside down over the heads of everyone who had forgotten them.

The Spire's roots. They were standing inside the Spire's roots, and the Spire was not built on Cael Sarn.

It was built *of* it.

"Maren said the vaults hold the ones who refused," Odile whispered. "But I don't think—" She steadied herself against the wall. "I don't think I understood. It's not a prison *beneath* the Spire. The prison is the building. Every stone above our heads is a stone that remembers."

Wren turned to August. In the lantern light his face was shining with something that was not tears, or not only tears, and when he spoke it was barely a whisper, and it was in Common — his second sentence ever, hoarse and careful, as if the words were heavy cargo he was learning to lift:

"They're so close. All of them. Right above us." His hand found hers again, gripping hard. "August. They know we're here. I can hear them waking up."

And from somewhere far above — through stone and silence and a century of enforced forgetting — came a sound like a great bell struck once, very softly, underground.

The Spire, humming back.
