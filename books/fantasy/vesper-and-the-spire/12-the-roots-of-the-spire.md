## 12. The Roots of the Spire

The tunnel narrowed until August could touch both walls at once, and the walls were warm.

That was the first wrong thing. Stone under Vesper should have been cold — she had spent ten years learning the temperature of every cellar and culvert in the city, the way other people learned street names. Cellars ran cold in winter and cool in summer, always. But this stone held a heat like a sleeping animal's flank, faint and steady, and when August pressed her palm flat against it she felt something that was not quite a pulse.

"Odile," she said quietly. "Your codex says anything about warm rock?"

"Nothing." Odile's voice came from behind her, thin with effort. She was sixty-one years old and had insisted on carrying the codex herself, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart. "Though I confess most of what I remember about the Spire's foundations comes from a footnote. Footnote forty-seven. The archivist who wrote it was executed for it."

"A whole life for a footnote," Bram said from the rear, where he'd appointed himself rearguard on the grounds that he was "the only one of you with any sense of self-preservation." He carried a storm-lantern with its shutter turned down to a knife-slit of light. "Comforting heritage you've got, Odile."

"I never claimed it," Odile said. "I merely catalogued it."

Wren walked between them all, and Wren was *glowing*.

Not literally — though August had checked twice, because with this child one stopped assuming. But since they'd entered the tunnels beneath the city his whole manner had changed. In the ruins of the unwritten valley he had been quiet, heavy, a boy walking through a dream he couldn't wake from. Here he moved like water running downhill toward the sea. His steps were quick and certain. Twice he had gone ahead around corners before anyone could stop him, and both times there had been nothing to find but more tunnel, and both times he'd looked back at them with an expression of such naked impatience that Bram had muttered, "He knows where he's going better than we do, then."

"He knows where he's going better than anyone alive," Odile said.

August kept her hand on the wall as they walked. The warmth grew. And beneath the warmth — she didn't want to name it, so she made herself name it — beneath the warmth was a sound. Very low. Below hearing, almost, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than the ears. A murmur. Like a crowd heard through three closed doors.

"Does anyone else hear that?" she asked.

Bram raised his lantern slightly. "Hear what?"

"Feel it, then."

They all stopped. Even Wren. The boy tilted his head, listening with his whole body, and his face did something complicated — hope and fear fighting for the same ground.

"It's voices," August said. Her mouth had gone dry. "It sounds like — a market day. Like people talking."

"The vaults are sealed," Odile said slowly. "Sealed for two hundred years. Whatever you're hearing, it can't be—"

She didn't finish. None of them wanted her to finish.

* * *

The tunnel ended at a wall that was not a wall.

It was a door — or had been, once. Now it was a heap of broken masonry, and the masonry was carved. August got her hands into the rubble and lifted out a fragment the size of a loaf, and her breath caught.

It was a window frame. A miniature one, or a piece of a full-sized one, cut from pale grey stone shot through with veins of white quartz. Along its edge ran a line of lettering in the old tongue, worn shallow by weather that should have been impossible this far underground.

"That's not building stone," she said. "This is *dressed* stone. This is a wall from somewhere. From a house."

"Give it here." Odile took the fragment and turned it in the lantern light. Her fingers, usually so steady over documents, trembled. "Quartz-veined granite. There's only one quarry in the old kingdom that produced this — the Whitefell seam, north of the capital. It was used for—" She stopped.

"For what?"

"For everything," Odile said. "Temples. The law courts. The palace of the last king. It was the signature stone of Cael Sarn. You could tell where you were in the country just by looking down at the paving." She looked up, and in the slit of lantern light her face had gone the colour of old paper. "August. The Spire isn't built *on* the old capital. The histories say the Spire's foundations were quarried locally. They weren't quarried at all."

"They were taken," August said.

"They were *torn out*," Odile said. "Street by street. Wall by wall. The founders didn't erase Cael Sarn from the maps and leave the stones lying about to contradict them. They built their monument out of the corpse." She set the fragment down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might still be alive. "Every block of dressed granite in the Spire above us came from a home. A temple. A court of law. We have been standing inside a stolen country our entire lives and calling it grandeur."

Nobody said anything for a while. The murmur went on beneath them, patient as tide.

Then Wren stepped forward and put both small hands against the broken door, and pushed, and the rubble — which had stood for two centuries — sighed apart like ash.

* * *

Beyond was a stair, wide enough for six abreast, descending in long shallow flights. The architecture changed as they went down. The rough-hewn smuggler's work gave way to the quartz-veined granite, and the granite was not merely reused — it was *arranged*. August began to see it in the lantern light: corbels that had once held balconies now braced the ceiling. A lintel carved with oak leaves and acorns sat above a doorway leading nowhere. A stretch of wall bore the ghost of painted plaster, and on the plaster, faded almost to nothing, someone had drawn a row of sunflowers, child-height, the way a child draws them — enormous heads, spindly stems.

Someone's kitchen wall. Someone's garden gate. Carried down here in the dark and stacked into a prison.

"Oh," said Bram, very quietly. He had gone still beside the sunflower plaster, and when August glanced at him his usual flippancy had drained out of his face entirely. "My gran used to say the Spire was raised in a single night by holy men. That's what they teach dockside, anyway. Holy men, one night, no mortar." He touched the sunflower with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping cat. "No mortar because the stones already knew each other, is that it? They just — remembered how to fit?"

"Something like that," August said.

"And everyone up top walks past it every day. Bows their head to it. Puts coins in the collection boxes at its feet." He shook his head slowly. "We've been tithing to a graveyard and calling it luck."

"You didn't know," Odile said.

"No," Bram agreed. "But I knew *something*. Everyone knows something. You grow up next to a thing that big and it hums at night, and they tell you it's the wind, and you believe them because believing is easier." He looked back down the stair, up toward the city that slept overhead, unaware. "I owe money to some genuinely terrible people, and even they're honest compared to this."

Wren had stopped three steps below them.

He was standing with his head tipped back, eyes closed, and his lips were moving. Sound came out of him — the old language, flowing soft and continuous, and August realized with a lurch that he wasn't speaking *to* them. He was answering.

"Wren?" she whispered.

His eyes opened. He looked at her, and pointed down, and said one word in Common — one of the perhaps twenty words he ever offered them:

"*Home.*"

Then he took the stairs at a run.

* * *

The vault opened without warning. One moment the stair, the next a cavern so vast the lantern light simply gave up, swallowed by distance. Bram cranked the shutter wide and the beam leapt out and found — walls. Walls going up beyond sight, faced in granite, and set into those walls—

Doors.

Hundreds of doors. Small ones, human-sized, iron-banded, each set flush into the ancient stone, each bearing a lintel carved with letters. They receded into the dark on every side, tier upon tier, a honeycomb of cells built into the bones of the stolen city.

And from behind the doors came the murmur. Not a murmur anymore, now that they stood inside it. Voices. Many voices. Low, unhurried, overlapping — the sound of people talking in a large room, the sound August had heard through three closed doors and named *market day*, and she had been right, God help her, she had been exactly right.

"They're alive," August said. The words came out flat with shock. "Odile. The records said the prisoners were executed. The ones who wouldn't sign the Oath of Forgetting. They said—"

"The records said what the founders needed them to say." Odile's voice had dropped to almost nothing. She was staring at the doors, counting, her lips moving. "Two hundred years. No one can survive two hundred years in a cell. Unless—" She stopped. Started again. "Unless the Spire itself sustained them. All that stolen magic, all that power pulled out of a living country and harnessed into a prison — maybe the prison fed them. Maybe it couldn't help it. The stones remember being homes. Homes keep people alive. It might be the only instinct the Spire has left."

"So they've been down here," Bram said, "the whole time. Under the city. While we—"

"While we sold charms," August said, "and forged deeds, and ran contraband, and lived our little lives on top of them."

A voice answered her.

It came from one of the nearest doors — a woman's voice, cracked with disuse but unmistakably amused, and speaking Common with an accent August had never heard and somehow recognized instantly, the way you recognize a song your mother sang before you could talk.

"*Charms,* is it? What kind? My mother sold charms. Love-charms, mostly, and lies about love-charms."

August's whole body went cold and hot at once. She crossed to the door. Iron bands, rusted to lace. Through the gap where the wood had shrunk: darkness, and within the darkness, close, a gleam that might have been eyes.

"Who are you?" August asked. Her voice shook. She let it.

There was a pause. Then, wonderingly: "You don't know me. You *don't know me.* Two hundred years, and they send down a stranger who doesn't know my family, my street, my name." A dry sound that might have been laughter or weeping. "Oh, that's freedom of a kind, girl. Ask me anything. Ask me my name. I haven't said it aloud since before your grandmother's grandmother was a rumour."

"Tell me," August said.

"Maren," said the voice. "Maren of the Lanternmakers' Row, which was where the bridge met the third terrace, which was burned into the foundation of your pretty tower along with everything else. Maren, who refused the Oath. Maren, who has been half-alive and half-remembering so long she sometimes can't tell which half is which." The gleam of eyes shifted. "And who did you bring with you, keeper of charms? Because there's someone behind you who smells like—"

Wren stepped past August.

He put his face to the gap in the door, and from inside came a sound August would hear for the rest of her life: a sharp intake of breath, and then the woman's voice saying, in the old tongue, a single word — a word August had found in Odile's glosses and never once heard spoken, a word whose meaning she knew the way you know the meaning of *mother*.

*Grandson.*

"No," Maren said, in Common now, rapid and ragged. "No, no — whose boy is this? Whose? He has Sefa's chin. He has — boy, boy, come to the light, let me—"

Small fingers found the gaps between August's own. Wren pressed close against her side, trembling, and spoke into the dark in the old tongue, a flood of it, stumbling and urgent, and August understood almost none of it and understood all of it: *I was sent. I was sent to find someone. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I took so long.*

From behind the door, Maren began to laugh and cry in the same breath, and the sound carried, and other doors began to answer. Voices waking all down the tiers of the vault, calling out in the old language, asking, answering, a market day two hundred years late opening all at once in the roots of the Spire. Names, August thought, dazed. They're saying names. Whole genealogies unfolding in the dark. Streets. Seasons. Everything the Oath tried to burn.

Above them, faint but distinct, a bell began to ring.

Bram's head came up like a dog's. "That's not the hour bell," he said. "That's the alarm. Someone's found the tunnels."

Maren's voice cut through the rising chorus, suddenly clear and hard as a blade: "Girl. Charmseller. Whoever you are. If you've come this far, you've come for the Door. So listen fast, because I think your time upstairs just ran out."
