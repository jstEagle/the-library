## 13. Voices in the Vault

The dark beneath the Spire was not empty.

August had expected emptiness. She had braced for it the way one braces for a cold bath — a quick shock of nothing, stone and silence and the small animal comfort of her own breathing. Instead, as they descended the last of the stairs that Odile's codex called *the Throat*, she heard singing.

It was very far away and very old, a thread of melody worn thin by centuries, but it was unmistakably a lullaby. August knew it. Not from her apprenticeship, not from any glossary or burned book — she knew it the way you know a song your mother hummed while stirring a pot, without knowing its name or where it came from. A hedge-witch's inheritance, passed hand to hand down generations of women who had never been told what they were carrying.

"Odile," she whispered. "The song."

Odile stopped two steps below her, lantern raised. The old archivist's face had gone the color of the parchment she spent her life forging. "I know it," she said. "I've known it all my life. I thought it was Vesperine." Her laugh came out cracked. "Thirty years copying dead words into a private codex, and I never once asked myself why my grandmother sang me to sleep in a language that officially doesn't exist."

Bram shifted behind them, Wren's weight on his shoulders. The boy had grown heavier as they'd gone deeper — not in body, August thought, but in presence, the way a bell grows heavier the closer you stand to it. His eyes were open now, wide and luminous in the lantern light, fixed on the darkness below.

"They're alive," he said.

Not in Common. In the old tongue, the language that made August's wards itch under her skin like wool on a fever day. But she understood him. Somewhere between the river and this stair, understanding had stopped being translation and started being recognition, the way you stop reading a letter and start simply hearing the voice of the person who wrote it.

"You're sure?" Bram asked, though he must have felt it too. The whole stairwell felt it — a warmth rising from below like bread-heat from an oven, faintly sweet, faintly sad.

Wren only looked at him. Then, with the terrible patience of children delivering bad news: "They have always been alive. That is the problem."

* * *

The vault opened out of the Throat like a cathedral opening out of a keyhole.

One moment they were walking single-file through stone barely wider than Bram's shoulders; the next, the ceiling soared away into darkness and the lantern light fell across a floor of pale flagstones — and August's breath caught, because those were not flagstones. They were cobbles. Rounded by feet, guttered for rain, laid in the herringbone pattern of an old market street. A street, buried whole, roofed over with the bones of the city that had eaten it.

And the street was full of people.

They sat along the walls where shopfronts had been, dozens of them, scores — wrapped in blankets gone gray with age, some sleeping, some staring at the lanterns with eyes that took a long time to focus. Their faces were wrong in a way August couldn't at first name, and then could: they were unfinished. Like portraits someone had abandoned halfway through, the eyes vivid and the mouths smudged, as if memory itself had run out of paint. They were sustained — she could feel it now, the great slow pulse of the Spire above them, magic trickling down through the stones like water through a cracked wall — but sustained was not the same as kept. They had been stored, not saved.

A woman near the front rose to her feet. She was small and straight-backed, white hair braided in a crown, and she moved with the particular authority of someone who has been the eldest in a room for a very long time. Her eyes went past August, past Odile, past Bram — and found Wren.

The change in her face was like watching ice become water.

"*Sarneth*," she said, and the word rang off the buried street like a bell struck in church. "*Sarneth ven hallo.*"

Every person in the vault who could stand, stood. The sound they made was not a cheer. It was quieter than that and much more frightening — a long collective exhale, decades held and released at once, a sound like a country remembering it had lungs.

Wren slid down from Bram's back. He walked forward into the sea of reaching hands, and August watched the smallest member of their ragged company be gathered up by people who had been waiting for him since before he was born, and something in her chest pulled tight and would not loosen.

"He's just a boy," Bram murmured beside her. There was an edge of alarm in his voice. "He's eight, maybe. Whatever they think he is—"

"He knows," Odile said softly. "Look at him. He knows exactly what they think he is."

She was right. Wren stood in the center of the crowd with his chin lifted, accepting their hands on his hair and cheeks, and there was nothing confused or frightened in him. He let them believe. And when the elder woman — Maren, others were calling her, *Maren, Maren* — knelt before him and spoke a formal question in the rolling cadence of ritual, Wren answered in the same tongue, clear and grave, and August understood perhaps one word in ten but understood the shape of it completely.

*I am not the herald,* he said. *But I brought one.*

Then he turned, and pointed at August.

* * *

Later — much later, after the blankets and the bitter tea brewed on a brazier that smelled of juniper, after Bram had been adopted by three grandmothers simultaneously and could be heard protesting that no, really, he wasn't hungry, yes, really, all right, one bowl — August sat with Maren on the step of what had once been a baker's shop, its stone oven still faintly warm after two hundred years, which told August more about the nature of the Spire's magic than any archive could.

Maren studied her for a long time before speaking. When she did, it was in Common, accented but precise, each word set down like a coin counted out.

"Wren says you are a witch of the hedges. That you hid for ten years in the new city. That you are not one of us." A pause. "Is this true?"

"All of it."

"Good." Maren nodded, satisfied. "Then you will understand what I say next, because you will not flatter yourself. The children hoped. The children always hope. They see a stranger come down the stairs and they think: *now*. Now the door opens. Now we go home." She folded her hands. "I am too old for hope. I will tell you the truth instead, and you may carry it back up the stairs if it is too heavy."

"I'm stronger than I look," August said.

"So was Cael Sarn." For the first time, something moved behind the old woman's eyes — grief so old it had polished itself smooth, like a pebble worried by a river. "You have seen the Door?"

"Odile's codex describes it. Beneath the deepest vault. An arch of plain stone."

"Mm. Plain." Maren smiled without humor. "The founders of Vesper built towers of gold and glass to prove they were real. We built our Door plain, because we knew we were. Stone is honest. It does not need to shout." She turned to face August fully. "You want to know how it opens. Everyone wants to know how it opens. Listen, then, witch, and count the cost before you spend anything.

"The Door does not open with keys. It does not open with blood, though we tried that, God forgive us, in the first years. It does not open with magic — your kind has always mistaken magic for the thing itself, when it is only the shadow the thing casts." She said it gently, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The Door opens for belief. Not the small belief of hoping. The large kind. Someone must stand before it and claim this country — aloud, wholly, with every part of themselves — as their own. *Mine. My home. My people. My grief.* The Door listens the way stone listens, which is to say forever, and it cannot be lied to. If there is one corner of the heart still keeping an exit open, one *perhaps*, one *but* — the arch stays shut."

"And the cost?" August asked quietly. Because there was always a cost; her whole life had taught her that charms for small coin were the only honest ones.

Maren was silent a moment. Above them, impossibly far away, the Spire groaned — a long settling sound, like a giant turning in uneasy sleep. Dust sifted from the buried eaves of the baker's shop.

"You noticed the city is failing," Maren said. "The magic that keeps us — keeps our bodies, our memories, whatever is left of them — it comes down from the Spire in thinner threads every year. When it fails entirely, we fail with it. All of us. Every soul in this street." She said it the way she might report the weather. "The Door can carry us out. All of us, into whatever waits on the other side — I do not pretend to know what waits. But the Door is also a hinge, and a hinge must have a pin. Whoever claims Cael Sarn becomes the pin. The keeper. Bound to the threshold until the last of us has passed through — and bound after, I think, though even I do not know how long after. You cannot claim a country and leave it. That is the entire lesson of the last two hundred years. That is the lesson *they* never learned, the ones who built their golden tower on our graves." She reached out and took August's hand, and her grip was strong as a root. "So. Witch of the hedges. Wren says you are the one who chooses things. Is he right?"

August looked down the buried street. Lamplight moved among the blankets; somewhere a child — an actual child, impossibly preserved, impossibly tired — was being handed a bowl of tea by Bram Tallow, smuggler and debtor, who was making faces at her until she smiled. Odile sat against a wall with her codex open on her knees, writing by lantern-light with the fierce speed of a woman racing her own guilt, while an old man dictated names to her, name after name after name, a litany of the unwritten.

Hope had come down these stairs tonight wearing a bill, and the bill was everything.

"I don't know yet," August said honestly. "Ask me again tomorrow."

Maren laughed — a dry, warm, startling sound. "Tomorrow," she agreed. "We are very good at tomorrow. We have had two hundred years of practice." She squeezed August's hand once and let go. "But witch — do not take too many tomorrows. The Spire has fewer than we do."

As if to agree, the stones overhead shuddered, and far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stolen city, a bell began to ring.

Not the evening bell. The alarm.

And running figures appeared at the mouth of the Throat, lantern-light and steel, and a voice August knew — rough, reluctant, familiar — shouting her name down into the dark.

"Hesk," she breathed.

Maren rose slowly to her feet, watching the lights descend, and her face was unreadable as plain stone.

"Well then," said the elder of Cael Sarn. "Perhaps you won't need tomorrow after all."
