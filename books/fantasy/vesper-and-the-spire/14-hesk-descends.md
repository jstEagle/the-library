## 14. Hesk Descends

The order came down at the ninth bell, written in the Spire's own hand — which is to say, in no hand at all, but pressed into the paper by a seal that left no wax, only a faint depression like a thumbprint in bread dough.

*Vaults beneath the Spire. Seal permanently. No record of contents. No survivors of record.*

Captain Aldous Hesk read it three times by lamplight, and each time the words seemed less like instructions and more like an apology someone had forgotten to finish.

He had been a Warden for twenty-two years. He knew what *no survivors of record* meant. He had written it himself, twice, in reports he did not like to reread, and both times he had told himself that the law was a wall, and walls do not ask whether the people on the other side deserve shelter.

But that was before he had found his own name in a dead woman's codex.

Not his name, precisely. His grandfather's grandfather's — *Hesk, Aldous, miller's son, entered the rolls of Vesper upon the Unwriting, sworn and signed.* Sworn and signed. His family had not been erased. They had held the pen.

He sat with the codex open on his desk for a long while, the page gone soft at the corners from Odile Ferrant's thirty years of secret copying, and then he stood, put on his coat, took the sealing charges from the quartermaster without signing for them, and went down.

His new partner — the one who reported directly to the Spire, whose name Hesk had already forgotten on purpose, whose eyes had the flat shine of a man who enjoyed paperwork the way some men enjoyed blood sports — was waiting at the top of the undercity stairs.

"Captain," the partner said. "The order specifies two."

"The order specifies sealing," Hesk said. "It doesn't specify conversation. Wait here."

"It's not a request I can—"

"I know exactly what kind of request it is." Hesk stepped past him onto the first stair. "If I'm not back by morning, write your report. You'll enjoy it. You get to use the word *regrettably*."

The dark below swallowed him one step at a time.

* * *

The tunnels under Vesper were older than the tunnels were supposed to be. That was the thing nobody said aloud: the city's foundations predated the city, and every surveyor who noticed had been promoted to somewhere far away until they stopped noticing things.

Hesk followed the sounds instead of the map. Somewhere ahead there was water dripping in a rhythm too regular to be water, and voices — many voices, murmuring, overlapping, the way a congregation sounds through a chapel wall. And threading through it all, thin and bright as wire, a child's voice speaking a language Hesk did not know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a song your grandmother hummed when she thought no one was listening.

He found them at the edge of a vault antechamber, lit by Bram Tallow's smuggler's lantern and the strange pale glow that came off the stones themselves, as though the walls remembered firelight and were embarrassed to admit it.

August Coldharbour saw him first. Of course she did. She had the look of a woman who had spent ten years cataloguing exits, and he watched her find all four of them in the space of a breath before her hand even moved.

"Warden," she said.

"Captain," Wren corrected, gravely, in Common, and Odile Ferrant made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Hesk raised both hands, empty, the sealing charges still slung over his shoulder where everyone could see them. "I'm going to set these down," he said. "Slowly. Because if I hold them any longer I'm going to do something stupid with them, and I'd rather do something stupid *away* from all of you."

He knelt and laid the charges against the wall, and stepped back, and the silence that followed had weight.

"You're supposed to seal this place," August said. It wasn't a question. She had heard the orders too, or guessed them; she had that kind of mind.

"I'm supposed to do a great many things." He looked around the antechamber — at the old woman Maren standing like a gatepost between the child and the world, at Odile clutching her codex to her chest like a shield made of paper, at Bram Tallow positioned casually in front of the tunnel mouth with the studied looseness of a man who intended to be very fast very soon. "Thirty years ago my family bought their place in Vesper with a signature. I've spent my life enforcing the purchase."

Odile's arms tightened around the codex. "You've read it, then."

"I've read it." He met August's eyes. "My people weren't unwritten, Mistress Coldharbour. They did the writing. Or helped. Signed the rolls, took the amnesty, and raised their children never to say the word Cael Sarn above a whisper, and eventually not even below one. And now I stand here holding enough blasting oil to bury whatever's behind that wall, and I find I don't know—" His voice caught on something. He pushed through it. "I don't know which country I owe."

The question hung in the vault air, among the murmuring voices, and Hesk realized with distant surprise that he had never asked it aloud before, and that saying it had not killed him, which the Wardens' training had always implied it would.

August came closer. Not touching distance — she was careful, always careful — but near enough that he could see the lamplight move in her eyes.

"You owe the country that tells the truth," she said. "That's all a country is. Everything else is furniture."

"That's a very tidy answer from a woman who hid for ten years."

"It's a very tidy answer from a woman who hid for ten years *badly*, because hiding is lying with better posture." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might take root. "You came down here instead of waiting up top. That's your answer, Captain. You just haven't signed it yet."

From deeper in the vaults, the murmuring swelled and subsided, like a tide deciding something. Wren had gone still, head tilted, listening. Then the boy looked at Hesk — really looked, the way children look, without the mercy of politeness — and said something in the old tongue, soft and questioning.

Maren translated, her voice dry as old vellum. "He asks if you are the one who comes to break the door or open it. The words are nearly the same in our language. Only the hands differ."

Everyone looked at Hesk's hands.

They were steady. He was mildly astonished to find they were steady.

"My grandfather signed a roll," Hesk said slowly, "and it kept my family fed and safe and ignorant for five generations, and I have spent twenty-two years punishing people for the crime of remembering what mine chose to forget." He turned, deliberately, and picked the sealing charges back up — and carried them to the far wall of the antechamber, away from the vault door, and began unpacking them with a craftsman's care.

"What are you doing?" Odile whispered.

"There's a secondary passage behind us — the one your smuggler friend came up by. When the Spire's people come down, and they will come down, they'll come that way." He worked as he talked, packing charge and fuse with the economy of long habit. "I'm going to collapse it. Behind us. Which means when I do, Captain, I'll be on the wrong side of it, same as all of you."

Bram Tallow let out a low breath. "That's either the bravest or stupidest thing I've seen a Warden do, and I once watched a man fight a swan over a pie."

"Both, probably." Hesk tamped the last charge home and looked back at August. In the pale stone-light his face had lost the wall of it, the official blankness, and underneath was just a tired man of fifty-odd years holding a question he could no longer put down. "I can't give you back what my family helped take. But I can hold a corridor. It's not much of a country, but it's the one I'm choosing."

August studied him a moment longer. Then she extended her hand — hedge-witch's hand, charm-scarred, unafraid.

"Aldous Hesk," she said, "welcome to the country that tells the truth. Membership is free. The dues are terrible."

He shook it. His grip was careful, as if afraid the honesty might bruise.

And that was when the lamps guttered — all of them at once, Bram's lantern, the stone-glow itself dimming like a held breath — and from far above, muffled through a hundred feet of stolen rock, came a sound none of them needed explained.

Boots. Many boots. And a voice, flat and official, reading orders aloud in the dark:

*"Seal the vaults. No survivors of record."*

Wren's hand found August's sleeve. Maren drew herself up like a door closing. And Hesk touched the fuse at his lips, ready to light the only way out — the wrong way out — and said quietly, to no one and everyone:

"Well. I suppose we'd best go meet the country."
