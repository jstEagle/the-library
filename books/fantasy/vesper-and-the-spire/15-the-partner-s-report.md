## 15. The Partner's Report

The report reached Captain Hesk at the fourth bell of the morning watch, delivered by a runner who had clearly sprinted the whole way from the Spire and was now paying for it in visible regret.

"Sir. The partner's arrived."

Hesk set down his pen. He had been writing — or pretending to write — a summary of the Ferrant woman's confiscated papers, a document that grew no closer to completion no matter how many hours he bent over it, because every time he tried to reduce Cael Sarn to an official sentence, the sentence came out wrong. *Subject matter: nonexistent nation, records deliberately destroyed.* It read like madness on paper. It read like madness because it was true.

"Arrived," Hesk said. "From where?"

"The Spire, sir. She came down through the upper galleries. Warden-Captain Vess." The runner swallowed. "She's asking for you by name. She has forty reinforcements with her, sir, and writs for full vault access."

Forty. Hesk had spent nineteen years learning how many men it took to do things in Vesper. Forty was not a search party. Forty was an erasure.

He stood, straightened his coat, and thought — briefly, treacherously — about the ledger in his breast pocket. His family name, inked in a clerk's careful hand three hundred years dead: *Tallisker, Hesk-line. Compensated. Retained.* Collaborators, the word meant, though the codex dressed it politely. Men who had helped unwrite a country so that their grandchildren could grow up believing there had never been anything under their feet but honest stone.

And now those grandchildren were being sent down to finish the job.

"Lead on," he said.

* * *

Warden-Captain Vess was waiting for him at the head of the descent stairs, and she was nothing like what Hesk had expected, which was perhaps the most frightening thing about her. He had imagined someone brutal. Instead she was small, gray-eyed, and pleasant, with the manner of a schoolmistress about to hand out sweets, and she spoke to him as though they were old colleagues catching up over tea.

"Captain Hesk. You've been thorough. I'll say that for you." She smiled. "Thoroughness is why you were chosen for this file."

"I wasn't aware I'd been chosen," Hesk said. "I was assigned."

"A distinction without a difference, surely." She gestured down the stairs, into the dark where the alarms were still ringing — a low, grinding bell-tone that seemed to come out of the walls themselves, out of the stones, as though the building were clearing its throat after centuries of silence. "The Spire has begun making noises it should not make. The upper vaults are responding. Someone is below, doing something to the roots of this city, and my orders are to seal every access permanently once the intruders are contained."

"Contained," Hesk repeated. "Not arrested?"

Vess looked at him then, and the pleasantness did not leave her face, which was somehow worse. "Captain. Do you know what's in those lower vaults? Officially?"

"Nothing," Hesk said. "Officially, they're structural. Foundation chambers. Sealed since the founding."

"Mm." She turned toward the stairs. "And unofficially, you've been reading Ferrant's papers, so we both know better, don't we? Unofficially, there are people down there who were never supposed to have survived the founding. And unofficially, if they walk out of this city and start telling everyone what they are, then everything above us — the law, the ban, the peace, all of it — comes down like wet plaster." She began to descend, her boots ringing on the steps, forty Wardens falling in behind her like a tide deciding which way to flow. "So. Contained. Come along, Captain. You know these tunnels better than anyone. That's why you're here."

*That's why you're here.* Hesk followed her down into the dark and understood, with a cold clarity, exactly what kind of tool he had been all along: the one who could find the door, because he was descended from the men who had built it shut.

* * *

Bram Tallow heard the alarms before he saw anything, and his first thought — genuinely, sincerely — was *oh good, they've found the other tunnel too.*

His second thought was more practical: the chokepoint.

The undercity had been built by smugglers, priests, and cowards over four hundred years, and it showed. Corridors branched and doubled back; staircases went up to rooms that no longer existed; and here and there the old builders had left narrow places where the roof dipped low and the walls pinched together, because apparently even cowards four hundred years ago had understood that someday you might need somewhere to make a last stand.

This one was perfect. A corridor barely wide enough for two men abreast, ceiling sloping down to shoulder height, with rubble from some ancient collapse piled conveniently at either end. Behind him, the passage ran back toward the Door chamber, where August and Odile and Maren and Wren were even now walking toward whatever waited at the arch. Ahead of him, faint but growing, came lantern-light and boot-steps and the particular sound of forty people trying to move quietly, which is to say, the sound of forty people failing to move quietly.

Bram leaned his shoulder against the wall, tested the grip of the boat-hook he'd carried since the Gull Court burned his ship — it had been a boarding hook once, back when he'd been on the respectable side of such transactions — and did the arithmetic.

August would say no. He knew that already. He could hear her saying it, actually, in that crisp market-voice of hers: *Bram Tallow, if you think for one moment—* But August was half a mile back by now, and the net was closing from above, and somebody had to be the hinge.

Besides. He owed debts. He'd spent ten years owing debts — to the Gull Court, to river-masters, to luck itself — and this was the first one he'd ever been glad to pay.

"Well," he said aloud, to nobody, settling himself in the narrow place. "Could be worse. Could be rain."

The first lanterns rounded the corner.

* * *

They were led by a young Warden with a nervous voice and a very official-sounding proclamation, which he began to deliver — *"In the name of the Founding Charter, you are commanded to—"*

"Oh, sit on it," said Bram, and swung the boat-hook.

What followed was not, by any honest measure, a battle. It was a bottleneck with one badger in it. The corridor was too narrow for swords to be used properly, too low for pikes, too dark for numbers to matter, and Bram Tallow had spent his whole life fighting in spaces exactly this size — cargo holds, bilges, the alley behind the Drowned Gull when the Gull Court's collectors got creative. He knew how to fight in a place where only one man could reach him at a time, and he knew, with a professional's calm, that every minute he stood here was a minute the others didn't have to spend running.

"You know what's funny?" he called out, parrying a blade off the wall and shoving its owner backward into his fellows. "I used to be on your side of this! River work! Very respectable! Well — respectably paid, anyway!"

"Stand down!" someone shouted. "You're outnumbered forty to one!"

"That's not fair to the rest of you," Bram said, cracking a lantern with the hook's butt-end. Darkness bloomed. "Some of you are useless!"

A hand grabbed his ankle. He kicked free. Something scored a line across his forearm, hot and bright, and he laughed — actually laughed, high and wild, because the pain felt honest in a way nothing else in his life had been for years. He thought about his boat going down in the harbor with the Gull Court's fire eating through her hull. He thought about August pressing her palm to his ruined knee in a smoky cabin and taking ten years of ache out of it like drawing a splinter, the first kindness anyone had done him without a price tag attached. He thought about Wren asleep against the mast, dreaming in a language older than the city trying to kill them all.

"Tell you what," he shouted into the dark, backing them up again, buying another yard, another minute. "First man to land a hit gets my recipe for pickled eel! Second man gets to explain to the Gull Court why I died owing them money, because believe me, that conversation is worse than this one!"

Boots scuffled. Steel rang off stone. Somewhere in the press, a voice — younger, frightened — yelled, "There's only one of him! Why won't he go *down?*"

"Because," Bram said, spitting blood, swinging again, "I'm a Tallow, mate. We float."

* * *

Above, in the upper galleries, Hesk stopped walking.

Vess noticed immediately — of course she did; she missed nothing — and turned with that schoolmistress smile. "Captain?"

"Listen," Hesk said.

Below them, muffled through stone, came the sounds of a fight. One man's fight, by the rhythm of it — a single voice shouting jokes at forty soldiers, and the strange, stubborn persistence of resistance that refused to collapse. Hesk had heard sieges break. He had heard barricades fall in minutes. This was not that. This was one man standing in a doorway of history and refusing to let it close.

"Bram Tallow," Hesk said quietly. "The smuggler. He's holding the chokepoint tunnel."

"He's delaying the inevitable," Vess said. "As will you, if you continue to develop opinions. Walk, Captain."

Hesk walked. But he counted the seconds as he did, and he knew what he was counting: the distance between himself and the Door, measured against how long one loyal, foolish, debt-ridden smuggler could keep forty Wardens entertained in the dark.

It wasn't enough. It wouldn't be enough. Unless—

"Vess," he said. "The lower galleries flood. Old cistern channels. If your men take the eastern branch, they'll be wading to their knees within two hundred yards, and their powder will be soaked before they ever see the Door. Take the western route. I know it. I've mapped it."

Vess studied him for a long moment, gray eyes pleasant and bottomless. "You're recommending a slower route."

"I'm recommending a dry one."

"Mm." She waved a hand. "Western route. Lead us, Captain. And Hesk—" the smile widened a fraction, "—do try to remember whose side of the water you're standing on."

He led them west. Slowly. As slowly as a man could lead forty soldiers through a maze while appearing eager, which turned out to be quite slowly indeed, especially once he began consulting his map at every junction with the furrowed brow of a scholar.

Behind him, far below, the sound of one man's stubborn laughter rang up through the stones, and Hesk walked a little slower still.

* * *

Bram heard them change direction — the boot-fade receding westward, the pressure easing — and allowed himself one second of pure, undiluted smugness.

Then the ones he'd been fighting decided they were done being polite.

They came in with shields now, locked shoulder to shoulder, using the corridor's narrowness against him instead of letting him use it against them, and Bram's world contracted to impact and breath and the boat-hook growing heavier in his hands. Something broke in his side — a rib, probably, or possibly several ribs holding a meeting. A blade opened his thigh. He went down to one knee, got up, went down again.

"Still floating," he told them, from the floor, and swung anyway.

He bought them eleven minutes. He would learn later that it was eleven minutes — Odile counted, because Odile counted everything — and that eleven minutes was exactly the width of the gap between failure and the Door. He bought it with a broken boat-hook, six cracked ribs, a thigh wound that would scar like a river delta, and a final act of theatrical defiance that the young Warden who survived it would describe, years afterward, in a resignation letter of his own: *he was laughing, sir. Right up until the end of it, he was laughing.*

When they finally dragged the chokepoint clear and poured down the passage toward the Door, Bram Tallow lay in the dark among the wreckage of lanterns, breathing shallowly, alive by the narrowest margin the river had ever granted one of its own — and smiling, because somewhere ahead of him, muffled by stone and distance, he could hear the old tongue rising like water finding its level.

"Go on, then," he whispered to nobody. "Door's open. Tell them Bram sent you."

* * *

At the arch, August Coldharbour heard the fighting stop, and her heart stopped with it.

"They'll be here within minutes," Maren said, calm as tide. "The Door is ready. The question, keeper, is whether you are."

Wren's hand found hers, small and fierce. Beyond the arch, light was already gathering — old light, patient light, the color of a country remembering itself.

And down the western gallery, boots. Many boots. Coming fast.

August drew one breath, held it, and stepped forward to speak the name that would cost her everything.
