## 16. What the Door Asks

The tunnel ended the way all true endings do: without ceremony.

One moment August was walking bent-double through a passage that smelled of river silt and old candle smoke, Bram's lamp guttering ahead of her, Wren's small hand trustingly tucked in hers. The next, the ceiling simply opened upward like a held breath released, and they stood blinking in a vaulted chamber so vast that the lamplight gave up before it found the walls.

And there — at the far end of a floor of pale stone worn smooth as a chapel's — stood an arch.

It was not what August had expected. She had expected grandeur. She had expected, after everything, something to match the Spire above them, all that stolen magnificence stacked into the sky like a monument to theft. But the Door of Return was plain. Grey stone, uncarved, no taller than two men standing on each other's shoulders. It framed nothing but darkness, and the darkness did not look like absence. It looked like patience.

"It's humming," Wren said.

He said it in Common — his second word of it since the vaults, hoarded like a coin he wasn't sure he wanted to spend — and August felt Odile go still beside her.

"He's right," Odile whispered. She had her codex clutched to her chest with both arms, the way she'd carried it for thirty years, presumably, the way one carries a thing that is both salvation and sin. "I can feel it in my teeth."

August could feel it too. Not sound exactly — more like the memory of sound, a note sustained so long that the air itself had learned it. And threaded through it, faint as writing under water, were words. Old words. The language that had come out of a mud-streaked child and turned her whole life inside out.

Maren walked forward first.

The elder of the unwritten moved slowly — she had been half-remembered for longer than August had been alive, and it showed in her joints, in the careful way she placed her feet as though the floor might disagree with her weight. But she did not hesitate. When she reached the arch, she laid one hand flat against the stone, closed her eyes, and listened the way other people pray.

Then she began to translate.

* * *

"The Door does not open," Maren said, when she finally turned around. Her voice carried strangely in the vastness, not echoing so much as arriving, fully formed, at every ear at once. "I want you to understand that first. We have spent — I don't know how long. Time moves strangely down here. We have spent all of it believing there would be a mechanism. A key. A word spoken correctly. A sacrifice." Her mouth twisted. "There is always talk of sacrifice among people who have already lost everything. It makes the losing feel chosen."

Bram shifted his weight against the tunnel wall, where he'd planted himself with his arms crossed and his bad leg straight, the posture of a man who intended to be useful and refused to be sentimental about it. "So what is it, then? A riddle?"

"A question," Maren said. "The Door asks a question. And it only opens for the answer it wants."

She looked at August when she said it. August noticed this the way you notice a cold draft — peripherally first, then all at once, everywhere.

"Ask it," Odile said. "Translate it properly. All of it. I'll take it down." She was already fumbling for her pencil, and her hands were shaking, and August loved her fiercely for the shaking and the writing anyway.

Maren nodded once and turned back to the arch. She pressed her palm to the stone again, and this time she spoke aloud, her voice dropping into the old tongue, low and rolling, words that made the wards August kept stitched into her coat hem flare warm against her collarbone — not warning-warm. Listening-warm.

When Maren finished, the humming deepened by a single degree, and August felt it settle into her chest like a hand resting there.

"She says," Maren translated, and her voice had gone rough, "'This door opens from one side only. It opens for one who claims the lost country as their own — not by blood, which cannot be helped, nor by grief, which fades, but by choice, which does not. Let such a one speak its name in the old tongue and call it theirs, knowing what the calling costs.'"

"And the cost?" Odile's pencil had stopped moving. "What cost? Say it plainly, Maren. I've spent thirty years being lied to by omission. I won't have it from a friend."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then at Wren. Then, last and longest, at August.

"The claiming binds," she said. "Whoever claims Cael Sarn becomes its keeper. They hold the Door open from this side while the people pass through. Every soul. Until the last survivor crosses." She paused, and August watched her choose honesty over mercy, and understood that this too was a kind of love. "And the last survivor will be a child. Children are slow to leave home, even a broken one. Even a borrowed one."

The silence that followed was the kind that has weight. August felt it press down on her shoulders along with the arch's humming, and somewhere beneath both, quieter than either, she felt something else — something that had been waiting in her all along, patient as the dark beyond the Door.

"No," said Wren.

* * *

He stepped forward out of the lamplight, and August's heart did the thing it had been doing since the night he knocked — that lurching, unwilling fall that she had stopped pretending wasn't love about four rivers ago.

"No," he said again, in Common, clumsy and fierce. "No. You don't —" He ran out of words, which happened to him often in this language, and switched to the old tongue, rapid and desperate, and Maren's face went soft and sorrowful as she listened.

"What's he saying?" Bram asked quietly.

"He says it isn't fair." Maren's voice wavered. "He says — he says the ones who remembered Cael Sarn loved it too much. That's why none of us could do it. He says we loved it like a wound, and a wound can't hold a door." The boy's voice cracked on something, and he started again, louder, and this time he didn't wait for translation because he didn't need it, because some things cross languages without papers:

"You found me. You came back for me when the Gull Court — you carried me when my leg — you learned my words. You're the only one who *chose*—" He stopped. His fists were shaking at his sides. "You can't give it away. You just got here."

August crouched down to his level, which took longer than it used to, her knees announcing their objections the way knees do after tunnels and terror and ten years of sleeping badly.

"Wren," she said. "Look at me."

He looked at her. Mud-streaked, months ago now; clean-faced tonight; eyes that had snuffed Warden lanterns like pinched wicks and were currently doing their best to snuff her resolve, and very nearly succeeding.

"I'm not giving anything away," she said gently. "Nothing's decided yet. Nobody's decided anything. We're going to sit down, and Odile is going to write everything down twice, because that's how she loves people, and Bram is going to complain about his leg, and we are going to think. All right?"

"All right," he said, in the tone of a child who has agreed to nothing forever.

They sat down. It was, August thought later, the strangest council ever held beneath a city that didn't remember what it was built on: a disgraced archivist, a smuggler, a mute-who-wasn't, and the elder of an unwritten people, arranged on the smooth pale stones before a humming arch, sharing the last of Bram's river-bread and passing a water skin around like communion.

Odile wrote. Her pencil scratched steadily through the quiet, and every so often she read a line aloud in her dry, precise voice, and the reading-out made the thing realer, which was a cruelty and a kindness at once.

"The binding," she said at one point, tapping the page. "Is it — forgive me, Maren, I must ask — is it a cage? Is this another prison? Because if the Door is asking for a new prisoner, I want that written plainly, and I want everyone to see me refuse to help."

Maren considered this with the unhurried gravity of someone who had decades of half-memory to consult.

"When the Spire holds someone," she said, "it forgets them. That is how it keeps them. Erasure is its chain." She touched the arch again, lightly, the way you'd touch a horse you were learning to trust. "The Door is the opposite of the Spire. It doesn't forget. I think — I think whoever keeps it will be the most remembered person in either country. Every name that passes will pass *through* them."

"That's not comforting," Bram said. "That's terrifying."

"Yes," Maren agreed. "Most true things are both."

* * *

Later — much later, when Odile had fallen asleep mid-sentence with her cheek on the codex and Bram was pretending not to keep watch and Wren was asleep too, curled against August's side with the boneless totality of the young — August got up and walked to the arch alone.

Up close, the stone was warm. She hadn't expected that. It hummed against her palm, and the old words moved under the surface like fish under ice, and she found, to her complete lack of surprise, that she could almost read them now. Months of Wren's sleep-talk, of Odile's glosses, of listening in the dark vaults — the language had been seeping into her the way river water seeps into anything that stands in it long enough.

*Open for one who chooses,* the stone murmured. *Bind the keeper. Hold until the last.*

Her small life rose up in front of her, unbidden. The night market stall. The luck-knots and fever-charms sold for small coin. The rules: never magic anyone could trace, never stay anywhere long. Ten years of professionalism worn like armor over loneliness, and she had told herself it was safety, and it had been, a little, the way a locked empty room is safe.

But she had knocked on doors her whole life hoping someone would answer. And now a door was asking.

"It's not even a hard question," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange and wet. "That's the awful part. Everyone's waiting for me to weigh it like a merchant. My freedom against their lives. As if it's close."

"It isn't close," said Maren, from the darkness behind her. August hadn't heard her approach; perhaps the unwritten didn't make sound unless they chose to. "It was never going to be close. The Door knows that. That's why it asks the way it asks — not *will you*, but *do you*. There's a difference."

August wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she did everything she didn't want witnessed.

"There's a boy asleep over there who asked me not to," she said.

"Yes."

"And I'm going to do it anyway."

"Yes," Maren said again, softer. "Because he asked you not to *leave him*, underneath. And you're not leaving him. You're holding the door he walks through. Do you see? Keepers don't stay behind, August. Keepers stay *with*. There has never been a lonelier post, and never a less lonely one."

The arch hummed. Beyond it, the dark waited with the patience of a country that had been unwritten and had never once doubted it would be written again.

August stood before it a long time, one hand on the warm stone, feeling the old tongue move beneath her fingers like a pulse.

Then, behind her, from across the chamber, small and clear and terrified:

"Don't say it yet."

Wren was awake. He was sitting up in the lamplight with his fists clenched, and his eyes were bright, and he spoke in Common, carefully, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"Not yet. First — first I have to tell you something. Something I was sent to say. And I've been — I've been afraid of it the whole way here."

August turned from the Door. Whatever it asked, whatever it cost, it would keep a few minutes more.

"Then tell me," she said.
