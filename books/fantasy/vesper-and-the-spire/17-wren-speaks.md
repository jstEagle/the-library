## 17. Wren Speaks

The Door of Return hummed.

That was the only word August could find for it, though she had spent the better part of an hour trying to improve on *hummed*. It didn't sing, exactly, and it didn't whisper, and it certainly didn't speak — not in any language she knew, which by now was a shorter list than it had been a month ago. It made a sound like a held breath, like a kettle just before boiling, like the moment before someone says your name across a crowded room and you turn before you hear it.

She sat on the cold stone floor with her back against the arch's plinth and watched it do nothing at all, magnificently, for a long time.

Behind her, deeper in the vaults, Maren's people were singing. It wasn't a song so much as a practice — the elder had explained, with the patience of someone who had been explaining things to frightened people for longer than August had been alive, that names needed exercising or they went stiff. So they sang their own names, over and over, softly, like lullabies addressed to themselves. *I am Maren of the Ash Row. I am Maren of the Ash Row.* Half-remembered surnames surfaced and sank. Somewhere in the dark a man wept while he sang his, which apparently was also correct.

August should have been sleeping. Tomorrow — or what passed for tomorrow down here, where the Spire's failing light came through the stones in slow amber pulses like something breathing — she intended to stand up, walk to that humming arch, and unmake her life.

Instead she was counting the cracks in the floor.

"You'll wear a groove," said Odile, settling beside her with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. She pressed a tin cup into August's hands. Bram's tea, brewed from leaves he swore were tea and which tasted like a barn roof. "Drink. If you're going to be heroic, be hydrated about it."

"I'm not being heroic."

"No," Odile agreed mildly. "You're sitting on a dungeon floor at what I believe is dawn, staring at a door that eats keepers, drinking barn-roof water. Heroism is much tidier."

August almost smiled. Almost. The cup warmed her palms; the warmth felt borrowed. "Odile. The condition. What Maren said — *binds the claimer to remain*. Do you think she meant—"

"I think she meant every word, and chose them carefully, because she has spent decades learning how to say terrible things gently." Odile looked at the arch with an expression August couldn't read — thirty years of guilt folded very small. "I also think you've already decided, and you're hoping one of us will talk you out of it so you don't have to carry the decision alone."

"Is that so terrible?"

"It's the most human thing you've done since I met you." Odile patted her knee, rose with another groan, and left her there.

* * *

Bram found her next, which surprised her less than it should have. He moved quietly for a large man, but he always announced himself anyway — some old smuggler's courtesy, she suspected, learned from years of approaching people who might shoot first.

"Brought you bread." He lowered himself down beside her with considerably more grace than Odile had managed and considerably less dignity. "Well. Brought myself bread. Brought you half."

They ate in companionable silence. Above them, faintly, the Spire groaned — a long stone-on-stone complaint, like a ship working against its moorings. Both of them glanced up. Neither commented. There was a whole vocabulary of things none of them commented on anymore.

"Held the tunnel fine," Bram said at last, which was his way of saying *the handler's scouts came within forty feet of us today and I stood in the dark with a boat-hook and lied to them about tide schedules until they left.* "Told 'em the lower vaults flooded in spring. They believed me. Wardens never learn rivers."

"They'll send someone who does."

"Aye. Probably." He brushed crumbs from his coat. "You know I'd hold it again. Longer, if it came to it."

"I know."

"And you know I think this is a mad plan."

"I know that too."

He nodded, satisfied, as if the exchange had been a contract signed in duplicate. Then, quieter: "The boy's been looking for you. Been circling like a gull round a fish cart. Won't come near. Thought you'd want to know."

August's chest did something complicated. "Where is he?"

"Where he always is. Near enough to watch you. Far enough that watching counts as not-watching." Bram stood, stretched until something popped, and added, with the gentleness he hid under all that bluster like contraband under a false deck: "Talk to him, August. Whatever you're carrying, he's carrying it too, and he's smaller."

* * *

She found him at the edge of the survivors' firelight, sitting with his knees drawn up, watching the singers the way children watch weather.

He didn't run when she approached. That was new. For weeks he had orbited her — close enough to touch when she slept, gone by morning, always near, never near. Since the Door, since Maren's translation of its condition, he had watched her with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it herself once, at fourteen, listening through a door to adults deciding her future.

"Wren," she said, and sat down beside him, leaving a careful handspan of space.

He looked at her. In the firelight his eyes were the color of the well water in the unwritten valley — sweet, deep, older than a child's eyes had any right to be. He opened his mouth.

And closed it.

And then, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, cracked and small and unmistakably *Common*:

"Don't do it."

August stopped breathing.

The words hung between them. Around them the singing continued — *I am Pell of the Mill Road, I am Pell of the Mill Road* — and the Door hummed its held breath, and Wren, the mute boy who had knocked on her door speaking a dead language, who had not spoken a single word of Common in all the weeks she had known him, said again:

"Don't do it. Please."

"Wren—" Her voice came out strange. "You can talk."

"I could always talk." He said it flatly, the way children say devastating things. "In both. Mam taught me both. She said—" His face crumpled and reassembled itself, quick as weather. "She said a tongue is a door too. You keep it oiled even if nobody comes."

"Why now? Why tonight?"

"Because tomorrow you'll go through the arch and the Door will take you and everyone will be happy and I'll hate them." The words tumbled now, dammed too long. "Everyone who remembers loves Cael Sarn too much. That's why they sent me. Did you know that? Did Maren tell you?"

August shook her head slowly.

"They couldn't do it themselves." He stared into the fire. "Any of them. They tried — Maren told me, before we came down here, she tried three times to claim it and each time she got to the part where she says goodbye and her mouth won't make the words. Because to open the Door you have to give it away. You have to say *this country is mine and I stay behind so it can live.* And everyone down here loves it too much to leave it. Even to save it. Even knowing staying means the vault, the dark, the forgetting." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "Love is the lock. Isn't that funny?"

It was not funny. August found she could not speak at all.

"So they waited," Wren went on. "Years and years, and the Spire getting sicker above us, and the stones forgetting faster. And then the elders — the ones who still remembered the prophecy-rhymes, the ones about a keeper from outside — they started looking. Not for someone strong. Not for someone clever. For someone *willing*. Someone who would choose us without owing us anything. Someone whose love would be a gift instead of a wound." He turned and looked at her fully, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Someone like you."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, the Spire groaned again, longer this time.

"They picked me because I'm small and I don't look like anything," he said. "Because a child at a door gets let in. Mam carried me to the edge of the unwritten valley and put me on the road and told me to walk until I found a witch who sold luck-knots, because the rhymes said *a hedge-witch's hand, unbought and unbowed.*" He recited it in the old tongue first, soft, then in Common, and August heard her own hidden life described back to her in words older than Vesper. "I walked a long time. I knocked on a lot of doors. Most people called the Watch."

"I know. I saw the bruises."

"You took me in anyway." His chin trembled. "You fed me soup with too much pepper in it and you complained about your landlord and you mended my coat sleeve without being asked, and I was supposed to just — observe you. Report back if you were suitable. But you were *kind*, and kindness is worse, kindness is the worst thing, because now I know you, and I know how you laugh at Bram's terrible jokes even when they're terrible, and how you talk to your plants like they're tenants, and—" He was crying now, furiously, wiping his face with his sleeve like the tears were an inconvenience. "If you do it, I lose you. And I only just found you."

August reached out and pulled him against her side, and he came, folding into her like he'd been doing it for years, his fists knotted in her coat.

"I'm sorry," he whispered into the wool. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I was sent to find you and then I loved you and now I'm asking you to fail. Everyone will get free except the one person who made it happen. Don't do it. Find another way. There's always another way, isn't there? You always say there's another way."

She rested her chin on his hair. He smelled of woodsmoke and river and the sweet well-water of a country that had never officially existed.

"Wren," she said carefully, "can I tell you something true?"

He nodded against her shoulder.

"For ten years I kept a rule. Never magic anyone can trace. Never stay anywhere long. Never belong anywhere, because belonging is how they find you." She felt him go still. "I thought the rule was keeping me safe. It wasn't. It was just keeping me *small*. Every room I left early, every friend I didn't make, every winter I spent alone with my wards and my luck-knots — that wasn't safety. That was a smaller prison than this one, and I built it myself, and nobody even made me."

"That's different—"

"It's not. Listen." She tilted his chin up so he had to look at her. "Tomorrow I get to choose to belong somewhere. Really belong — not hiding, not pretending, not leaving before dawn. Yes, it costs. Everything worth belonging to costs. But no one has ever needed me *without disguise*, Wren. No one has ever said: stay, be exactly what you are, we will build our freedom on you. The Wardens hunted what I am for ten years. This Door is asking for it." Her throat tightened. "That's not a cage. That's the first honest offer I've ever had."

"But I'll be on the other side of it." Barely a whisper. "Everyone I love will be on the other side of it."

"Yes," August said, because lying to him now would be the one unforgivable thing. "And I will miss you so much it will feel like weather. Like a season that doesn't end." She smoothed his hair back. "But you'll walk through into Cael Sarn — real Cael Sarn, or its honest ghost, whichever it turns out to be — and you'll grow up with your mother's language all around you, and orchards, and a sky that isn't a prison ceiling. And someday, maybe, someone will come to my door. Knocking. Speaking two tongues."

"That's a story," he said accusingly. "You're making it a story so it hurts less."

"Of course I am. I'm a professional." She smiled, and after a long, wet moment, wobbling and reluctant, he smiled back. "Stories are how we carry things too heavy to lift."

They sat like that while the fire burned low and the singers' voices thinned toward sleep. Eventually his breathing slowed and evened, and August eased him down with his head in her lap, and sat watching the arch hum its held breath, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

It was the change in the sound that woke the vault.

The Door's hum — that patient, kettle-tight holding of breath — stuttered. Broke. Resumed a half-tone higher, urgent now, and every ward August had ever woven along her own skin flared cold at once.

Above them, iron rang. Not the Spire's groaning — boots. Many boots, on stairs that had been sealed for a century, descending.

Maren was on her feet in the firelight, head cocked, ancient and straight-backed. "They've found the upper gallery," she said calmly, terribly. "The seal-stones are breaking."

Bram appeared out of the dark with his boat-hook already in his fist and blood on his knuckles from a shift he'd taken hours ago. "Chokepoint tunnel's got company. Lots of it. Someone talked."

Odile clutched her codex to her chest like a shield. "How long?"

"Long enough," Bram said, and grinned the grin he wore going into bad weather, "if nobody wastes time."

Wren was awake, on his feet, gripping August's hand hard enough to hurt. He looked up at her, and though the vault filled with shouting and the ring of iron and the Spire screaming stone above them all, his voice came clear:

"August. *Now.*"

The Door of Return stopped humming, and began, softly, to listen.
