## 21. Vesper Remembers

The trouble began, as most trouble in Vesper did, with a song.

Hesk heard it first from a fishwife on the Salt Stairs, a broad woman with forearms like mooring rope, who was singing while she gutted herring in the grey light before dawn. The tune was nothing — a work-song rhythm, the kind that had been passed mouth to mouth since before anyone could say who wrote it. But the words had changed.

*Under stone and under salt,*
*under everything we built,*
*there's a name beneath our feet—*

She got that far and stopped, frowning at her own hands as though they'd betrayed her. Hesk stood in the shadow of the stairwell with his warrant book under his arm and waited for her to notice him, because noticing him was his job, and for eleven years it had been a job he was good at.

"Morning, Captain," she said, not looking up. "Lovely weather."

"It's raining."

"Lovely raining, then." She went back to the herring. "You want something, or are you just standing there being official?"

"The song," Hesk said. "Where did you learn it?"

She looked up then, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen from a citizen of Vesper in a long time — not fear, and not defiance either, but a kind of puzzled honesty, like a woman trying to remember where she'd left her keys.

"That's the strange thing," she said. "I don't know. Woke up with it Tuesday. Like it was always there and I'd only just now thought to open my mouth." She shrugged, a mountain range shifting. "You know how it is."

He didn't. That was the whole problem. He didn't know how it was anymore, and hadn't since the night he'd stood in a vault beneath the Spire holding a lantern over a page of Odile Ferrant's codex and read his own family name written in a hand three hundred years dead: *Hesk of the Marchlands, sworn to Cael Sarn, pardoned by the founders for services rendered.* Services rendered. His great-great-great-grandmother had sold a country to buy a life, and every Hesk since had grown up believing the law they enforced was the same thing as the truth.

"Right," he said, because there was nothing else to say. He touched his hat brim. "Carry on."

"Always do," said the fishwife, and resumed singing, louder than before, which he suspected was deliberate.

* * *

By midsummer, the songs were everywhere.

They came up out of the ground, people said later, though what they meant was that nobody remembered learning them. A dockhand would start humming a melody about a tower made of stolen stones, and by evening three taverns were singing it, and by morning the words had shifted again, growing older, growing stranger, growing *truer*, as if the city itself were clearing its throat after three centuries of enforced silence.

The Wardens confiscated broadsheets. They arrested two ballad-sellers on Cordwainer Row, and both arrests collapsed when the prisoners, questioned separately, sang their confessions instead of speaking them — in a language no living witness could identify, except that everyone somehow understood it anyway. The transcripts of those interviews circulated through the Warden barracks like contraband, which, technically, they were.

Sergeant Ivo Pell, who had been Hesk's second back when Hesk still believed in warrants, cornered him in the records room one afternoon.

"You've heard them," Pell said. It wasn't a question. "The new verses. There's one about a keeper. A woman at a door." He lowered his voice. "There's one about *you*, Captain. About a Warden who held a corridor against his own."

"I've heard it."

"They're calling you the Turncoat of the Undercity."

"I'd have preferred something with better meter."

Pell almost smiled, then didn't. "Commander Vayle wants a report on the singing. By Friday. She wants names."

Hesk looked down at the ledger open on the table before him — one of forty he'd been quietly requisitioning all spring, this one a tax roll from the reign of the last king anyone admitted to, its margins annotated in a modern clerk's careful hand. Odile's hand. She'd been leaving them for him like breadcrumbs, or like an apology, each volume carrying more recovered names out of her codex and into the official record where they could not be unwritten twice.

"Pell," he said, "how many names do you think are in these books?"

"Sir?"

"Names that were taken off people. Struck from rolls, burned out of registers, so thoroughly that their own grandchildren couldn't tell you what family they came from." He turned the ledger around. "How many do you think live in this city right now, walking around with holes where their history should be?"

Pell was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "And you want me to put that in a report to Commander Vayle?"

"No," Hesk said. "I want you to help me count them."

* * *

It was the bones that broke the ban.

Not literally — although there was a story going round about a mason in the Tanners' Quarter who'd cut into an old foundation wall and found a skeleton wearing a signet ring with a crest nobody recognized, except that everybody recognized it, the way you recognize a word on the tip of your tongue. The mason took the ring home. His daughter put it on as a joke and burst into tears without knowing why, and by supper she could draw the crest perfectly and told her father, in a small frightened voice, that the name attached to it was *Aldery*, and that it had been hers.

No, the real breaking was quieter than that. It happened in kitchens and counting-houses, in the space between one ordinary sentence and the next. A grandmother would pause over a pot of stew and say a word she'd never been taught — *vesperine*, meaning the last light before dark, meaning also, apparently, a kind of bread — and her granddaughter would write it down. A merchant signing a bill of lading would find his pen adding a flourish he'd never learned, an old glyph for *held in trust*, and stare at it, and not cross it out.

Magic, technically, was still illegal. The statute sat in the lawbooks where it had always sat, and Warden patrols still walked their routes with their lanterns lit, and every few weeks some earnest young officer would file a report about a hedge-charm or a luck-knot or a child skipping rope to a rhyme that made the pavement glow faintly gold at dusk.

But enforcement had become a kind of theater that everyone had stopped believing in, including the actors. When Officer Brandt cited a widow for selling fever-charms at the Lamplight Market, the crowd that gathered didn't jeer him — they simply watched, with polite interest, until the widow finished her transaction, thanked him for his patience, and packed up her stall. His report that week contained the sentence *"the populace appears to regard the statute as historical,"* and someone in the clerical pool had underlined *historical* twice and added in the margin: *isn't that the whole problem?*

August Coldharbour, had anyone thought to ask her, would have found the whole business deeply funny. She'd spent ten years perfecting magic that couldn't be traced, and now the city was doing it for her, wholesale, untraceably, in street songs and bread-words and children's games. Luck-knots sold openly at the market now, tied in patterns nobody could source but everybody trusted. Somebody had even set up a stall selling *authentic pre-Erasure charms, guaranteed old*, and August, passing through in her keeper's grey cloak, had examined the wares and concluded they were about as authentic as the maps that showed the valley as empty moor. She'd bought one anyway. Sentiment.

* * *

Hesk resigned on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays felt less dramatic than Fridays and he wanted, for once in his life, to do something undramatic well.

He wrote the letter himself, in plain language, and delivered it to Commander Vayle's office in person. She read it standing at her window with her back to him, and when she finally spoke, her voice was flat as slate.

"You understand I'm required to open an inquiry."

"I'd expect nothing less."

"A Warden captain resigning rather than enforce a lawful statute. There will be questions about your conduct during the Spire incident. About your association with the archivist Ferrant. About—" she paused, "—the tunnel."

"Yes," Hesk said. "All true. I'll answer whatever's asked."

Vayle turned. She was not, he reflected, a bad woman — that was perhaps the saddest discovery of his year, that very few of the people enforcing the erasure were bad. They were tired, and loyal, and afraid, and they had been handed a law so old that questioning it felt like questioning the ground.

"My grandfather," she said abruptly, "was a cooper. Barrel-maker. Family trade, four generations." Her jaw worked. "Last month my mother started humming a song about coopers. About a cooper's guild in a city that doesn't exist, and a motto she says has been in her head her whole life and she just never noticed it before." She looked at him. "*Oak remembers,* Captain. That's the motto. What am I supposed to do with that?"

"Nothing," Hesk said gently. "That's rather the point. Nobody has to do anything. It's coming back whether we enforce anything or not. You can't arrest a city for remembering."

Vayle stared at him for a long moment. Then she stamped his resignation — the sound of it very loud in the quiet office — and said, in a voice that was almost steady:

"For what it's worth, I never sent anyone to seal the vaults. The order came from above me. From the Spire itself, they said." She met his eyes. "I've wondered, ever since, who was up there giving orders to a building that isn't supposed to be anything but stone."

"So have I," said Hesk, who had wondered exactly that, and had begun, in his off hours, to wonder it on paper.

* * *

His quiet work, as he came to think of it, occupied a rented room above a chandler's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, two tables, forty-one ledgers, and a growing pile of correspondence that arrived by increasingly inventive means. Odile visited twice a week, ostensibly to supervise, actually to argue.

"The roll of the Weavers' Guild goes *after* the parish registers," she insisted, tapping the table with her cane. "Chronology matters, Hesk. If you interfile them, future scholars will think the guild predates the parishes, and then some fool will build an entire theory of Cael Sarni social structure on your filing error, and I will *haunt you*."

"You're seventy-one. You plan to haunt me for a filing error."

"I plan to haunt you regardless. The filing error merely gives me cause."

Bram Tallow came by too, when his boat was in port — a different boat now, smaller, with a patched sail and a figurehead he claimed was lucky. He ferried things across the threshold that wasn't supposed to exist: letters mostly, and once a crate of apples from a country that officially had never grown any, which Bram swore were the best he'd ever tasted and refused to discuss further. He always left a few apples on Hesk's table without asking, the way you leave payment for a friend.

Wren's letters came through Bram too, short ones, in a looping careful hand, switching between Common and the old tongue mid-sentence as if the two languages were simply rooms in the same house. *The orchards are real again. Maren says the well water tastes like memory. Tell the archivist the codex was right about the eastern gate.* And always, at the bottom, in Common: *Tell August I'm practicing.*

Practicing what, he never said. August, when Hesk relayed it, only smiled in a way that suggested she knew, and that knowing hurt her pleasantly, the way a healing wound does.

* * *

Autumn came, and with it the festival that nobody organized.

It started, as far as anyone could determine, with the lamplighters, who had always sung to keep their rhythm on the long rounds, and whose songs that season had gone entirely native. On the anniversary of the night the Spire had begun to remember itself, half the city turned out with candles, and the other half turned out to watch, and the Spire itself — pale stone climbing into the dark — caught the candlelight and gave it back warmer, the way a face warms when it hears its own name spoken kindly.

Hesk stood in the crowd with Odile and Bram and watched. Children ran between the legs of adults chasing sparks. Somewhere near the fountain, the fishwife from the Salt Stairs was leading a verse that everyone seemed to know, including, impossibly, Hesk himself, whose voice joined the chorus before his mind could object.

*Under stone and under salt,*
*under everything we built,*
*there's a name beneath our feet,*
*and the name is ours to keep.*

Odile dabbed her eyes with a handkerchief and pretended she wasn't. "Sentimental nonsense," she said thickly. "Historically unsound. Half those details are wrong."

"Which half?" Bram asked.

"All the best ones," she said, and laughed, and cried a little more.

Later, walking home alone past the darkened Warden barracks — he still took the long way, out of habit or penance, he wasn't sure — Hesk noticed a light burning in August's old window above the chandlery. She kept the rooms, though she lived at the Door; she said a keeper ought to have somewhere ordinary to be ordinary in. And as he paused on the cobbles below, he saw her silhouette cross the glass, unhurried, and stop, and turn toward the door as if she'd heard something.

A knock, he thought. At this hour.

He stood a moment longer, watching the window, watching the light, and found he was smiling without quite knowing why — the way the whole city smiled lately, hearing words it had never been taught and somehow had always known.

Whatever — whoever — waited on the other side of that door, Hesk had the sudden, certain feeling that the remembering was not finished with Vesper yet.
