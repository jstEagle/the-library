## 2. Ink That Dries Backward

The trouble began, as most troubles in the Guild of Impossible Things did, with a cataloguing error.

Juniper Quintana noticed it at half past nine in the evening, alone in the Vault of Small Catastrophes, where the guild kept the objects too strange to sell and too dangerous to destroy. He had been there since sundown, working through a backlog of acquisitions by lamplight, because Master Corvane believed that debt was best paid in hours and Juniper owed enough hours to fill three lifetimes. His fingers were gray with handling-dust. His back ached in the particular way that came from leaning over ledgers for eleven years, and his supper — a heel of bread and a pickle, eaten standing — sat in him like an apology.

The book in question was shelf-marked *P-114*, and it should not have been on his list at all.

It was a fat quarto bound in vellum gone the color of old teeth, its covers clasped shut with two iron hasps that had been soldered over — not locked, but *sealed*, the metal poured hot into the fittings a century ago and left to cool into permanent refusal. The spine read, in faded gilt: **PROPHECIES CONCERNING NATIONS NOW UNRECORDED.** A paper tag tied to the clasp said, in Corvane's cramped hand: *Sealed by order of the Prophecy Office, Year 312. Do not open. Do not catalogue. Do not touch.*

And yet it was on the intake ledger for tonight, in Juniper's own handwriting, which he had no memory of writing.

He stood very still in the lamplight and looked at the entry. *One (1) book, vellum, sealed, provenance unknown, received from the night-cart.* The night-cart brought things in after dark — drowned letters, keys to houses that had burned down, the occasional grief someone could no longer carry — but the night-cart had come and gone at eight, and Juniper had signed for everything on it. There had been no book.

Unless he had written the entry before the book arrived. Which would be impossible. Which would, he supposed, make it exactly the sort of thing this guild existed to handle.

"Right," he said aloud, because the vault was empty and saying things aloud kept the small hairs on his arms from standing up quite so high. "You're a clerical error. I've made hundreds of you."

The book said nothing, being a book. But the lamplight shifted across its cover as he approached, and he saw — or thought he saw — a faint tremor under the vellum, like a pulse under skin.

He told himself it was the flame guttering. He picked up the book anyway, because curiosity was the one appetite his debt had never managed to starve, and carried it to his desk at the far end of the vault, beneath the lamp, where the light was honest.

* * *

The first thing he noticed, turning it over in his hands, was the weight of it. Books of prophecy were usually heavy with pretension — gilt edges, silk markers, the pomp of certainty. This one was heavy the way a stone is heavy: dense, indifferent, older than anyone's opinion of it. The vellum cover was scarred with fine scratches, as though something inside had spent a hundred years trying to claw its way out.

The second thing he noticed was the wax.

Around each iron hasp, where the seal-metal met the cover, there had once been a ring of black sealing wax stamped with the Prophecy Office's sigil — a closed eye. He knew the stamp; every document Juniper had ever filed for them bore it, that patient blind eye that saw everything anyway. But on the upper hasp, the wax ring was broken. Not cracked. *Broken*, cleanly, from the inside out, the fragments pushed outward as if something had been taken out rather than put in.

And the broken edge was glossy. Wet-looking. Fresh.

Wax did not stay wet for a hundred years. Wax did not stay wet for a hundred minutes, unless—

Juniper set the book down, wiped his palms on his apron, and made himself do what he was trained to do. He was a scribe. Scribes observed before they concluded. He fetched his magnifying glass, his tweezers, his blotting papers, and his little tin of reference powders, and he examined the seal like a man defusing something.

The wax was warm. Slightly, faintly warm, the way a coin is warm when it has been held in a fist. The break was recent — within the day, perhaps within the hour. And along the inner edge of the lower hasp, where the metal met the vellum, there was a thread of ink. Black ink, still shining, as if a pen had been drawn through the gap and left a hairline of itself behind.

Ink that had gotten *out* of a sealed book.

Juniper sat down slowly on his stool. Around him the vault ticked and murmured in its usual way — the bottled thunderstorm rolling over in its sleep on Shelf Nine, the widow's second shadow rustling its tissue paper, the tax collector's conscience ticking like a dying clock in its cedar box. He had worked among impossible things for eleven years and had developed a professional calm about most of them. But those things were all *finished*. They were impossibilities that had already happened, captured and shelved, their strangeness safely in the past tense.

This book was doing something. Now. Tonight. In his vault, on his shift, against his ledger.

He should wake Corvane. That was procedure. Unexplained phenomena went up the chain; the chain ended at the Guildmaster's door; the Guildmaster decided whether a thing was inventory or emergency. It was, in fact, the single clearest rule of his employment, and Juniper's employment was the only thing standing between him and the debtor's wing of the Counting House, where men who failed to pay their hours went to have the failing taken out of them.

He looked at the book. The book lay there, warm and sealed and quietly wrong.

He did not go wake Corvane.

Later — much later, in a country that no longer existed, sitting beside a smuggler who had taught him that rules were just debts other people wanted you to owe — he would try to explain why. Why he, cautious Juniper Quintana, eleven years obedient, had picked up the tweezers and slipped them into the gap of the broken hasp instead of running for his master. The truest answer was also the simplest and the saddest: because the entry was in his own handwriting, and some part of him, starved and stubborn, needed to know what else he might have written without knowing it. Because a man who belongs to a ledger will risk anything to catch a glimpse of the page where his own name appears.

But that night he only told himself it was diligence. Diligence sounded better.

* * *

The hasp gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the smell that came out of the book stopped his heart for one full beat.

It smelled of rain on hot stone. Of cut grass and woodsmoke and river water and bread — a whole landscape of smells folded together, none of them possible in a cellar vault under the city of Graywater, all of them so vividly *specific* that Juniper's eyes stung with sudden homesickness for a place he had never been. It was the smell of somewhere. Of a real somewhere, loved by someone, long ago.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you're not a forgery."

Forgeries smelled of nothing, or of the forger's hurry. This book smelled of a country.

He opened the front cover. The endpaper was marbled in green and silver, and across it, in a hand so old the ink had gone brown, someone had written: *Given into keeping that it may not be read until reading can do no harm. If the seals hold, the words are sleeping. Wake nothing.*

Below that, a later hand — Corvane's, Juniper realized with a chill, Corvane had held this book, had touched these pages, and had never breathed a word of it — had added: *Received and resealed per agreement. The Office pays well for silence. C.*

Corvane had known. Corvane had taken the Prophecy Office's money and put this book in his own vault and let it sit for — how long? The date beside the note was thirty-one years old. Juniper had been six years old when his master pocketed his first payment for this silence.

He turned past the endpaper, and the pages began.

They were dense, close-written, in dozens of hands across what must have been decades — prophecies concerning nations Juniper had never heard of, and then nations he *had* heard of, and then, with a slow gathering horror, nations whose names rang bells in the deep drawers of his memory: places he had seen in old documents, in treaty annexes, in the margins of atlases, always with the same cold annotation. *Dissolved by accord. Borders unwritten. To be regarded as never having been.*

There was a page given entirely to a nation called Sarnholt, whose rivers were promised to forget their own courses. A page for the Meridian Isles, whose language was prophesied to die in the mouths of its last forty speakers on a specific Tuesday. Page after page of countries being unwritten, each entry ending in the same formula, the same closing line repeated like a liturgy:

*And it shall be as if it had never been, and the ledgers shall agree, and no one shall grieve, for grief requires a name.*

Juniper's mouth had gone dry somewhere around Sarnholt. He turned the pages faster now, past entries dated fifty years back, thirty, twenty, ten — the hands growing fewer, the intervals between prophecies stretching, until he reached the last written page and found it dated ninety-eight years ago. After that, blankness. Ninety-eight years of blankness. The book had fallen silent, as books do, and the world had presumably congratulated itself.

Except.

Except that the blankness did not extend all the way to the edge of the quire. There were perhaps twenty more leaves, and they were not blank anymore.

The newest entry began halfway down a leaf, in ink so fresh it still had a faint sheen under the lamp, and it was dated *tonight*.

Juniper did not read it at first. He looked at it the way one looks at a snake on a path — taking in the fact of it while refusing, for one merciful moment, its meaning. Then he made himself read.

*A cart comes to the guild door after dark, and the debt-bound scribe signs for what is not upon it, and does not know his own hand.*

His eyes flicked up, involuntarily, to the intake ledger lying open on the corner of his desk. To the entry in his own handwriting. *Received from the night-cart.* Signed for what was not upon it.

The room got very quiet. Even the bottled thunderstorm seemed to hold its breath.

He read on.

*The scribe breaks a seal that was bought and paid for, and tells himself it is diligence, and knows it is hunger. The book wakes under his hands, for it has been waiting ninety-eight years for a reader with ink on his fingers and a debt around his throat.*

Juniper became aware that he was holding his breath, and let it out, and discovered his hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the desk. Think, he ordered himself. Think like a scribe. This proved nothing. Anyone could have written this — someone who watched him, someone who knew the vault, someone playing a trick, the Prophecy Office testing Corvane's silence, a rival guild, a ghost, a joke. Fresh ink proved a recent hand, not a foreseeing one. There were a hundred explanations and ninety-nine of them were survivable.

Then he turned the leaf, and the hundredth explanation was waiting for him, and it was not survivable at all.

*This same night,* the next entry read, *the scribe will find his own name in this book, written beside tomorrow's date, and beside his name a word he does not yet know to fear: Vesper.*

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Juniper sat without moving for a long time. Somewhere above him, through layers of floor and filing, the guild slept — apprentices in their bunks, Corvane behind his locked door counting money or sleep or both. Outside, Graywater went on being Graywater: watchmen's boots, a drunk singing three streets over, the harbor bells marking the quarter hour with their usual indifference. The ordinary world, humming along, unaware that here in the Vault of Small Catastrophes a hundred-year-old book had just described a man's evening with the accuracy of a witness and the tenderness of a trap.

*Vesper.*

He knew the word. That was the worst of it — he *knew* it, the way you know a face glimpsed once in a crowd years ago. It sat in him with a weight out of proportion to its two syllables, and when he reached for its meaning, meaning slid away like water. A name of a place? A person? Something from a document he'd filed, a treaty, an atlas margin, a dream? He had handled thousands of erased things in this vault and never once felt one reach back for him.

He looked down at the fresh entry again, and this time he noticed what he should have noticed at once, what any competent scribe would have noticed, and the noticing went through him like cold iron.

The ink was drying backward.

He had seen it happen twice before in his career — once with a forged deed, once with a letter written by a dying man — ink that refused to oxidize, that stayed wet and shining minute after minute, because whatever wrote it was still, in some sense, writing. He watched now, magnifying glass raised, as the final word of the entry — *Vesper* — gleamed wetly under the lamp. And as he watched, a bead of black gathered at the tail of the final letter, swelled, and drew itself out into a new stroke. A hairline curve. The beginning of another character.

The book was not finished. The book was writing *now*. It had been writing all evening — the entry about the cart, the entry about the seal, the entry about tomorrow — each line appearing not in the past but in the present tense of its own making, and Juniper had simply arrived early enough to catch it mid-sentence, a man walking in on a confession.

The new stroke extended. Curved. Closed.

An *S*.

*Vesper* was becoming *Vespers* — or something else, something longer, and Juniper found he could not bear to watch it finish. He slammed the book shut. The iron hasps, broken, did not hold it closed, and it fell open again at once, patiently, the way a door falls open when the house wants you to look inside.

On the fresh page, below the unfinished word, new lines were surfacing even as he stared — ink rising through the vellum like bruises coming up through skin, letters assembling themselves out of nothing, and Juniper, scribe, eleven years in the Guild of Impossible Things, recognized the emerging hand with a horror that started in his scalp and ended in his knees.

It was his handwriting. Not similar to his handwriting. *His* — the slight forward slant, the idiosyncratic crossed sevens, the way his lowercase *d* never quite closed its bowl. No forger alive could take it that perfectly, because no forger alive had spent eleven years being corrected into it by Master Corvane's red pencil.

The book was writing in his hand. The book was writing *as* him. And the newest line, still wet, still forming, said:

*Tonight he will turn to the last written page, and find the date that has not happened, and read the name that is his own.*

Juniper's stool scraped back as he stood. His heart was going like a hammer on a nail. Every sane instinct he owned — and eleven years of debt had bred sanity in him the way captivity breeds it in anything — screamed at him to close the book, shelve it, wake Corvane, confess everything, pay whatever obedience cost, and never think the word *Vesper* again.

Instead he heard himself say, in a voice that did not sound entirely like his own:

"What date?"

The book's pages stirred, though the air in the vault was dead still. The wet ink at the bottom of the page gathered, trembled, and began — slowly, deliberately, with the patience of a thing that had waited ninety-eight years and could certainly wait a few moments more — to write the answer.

And Juniper Quintana, debt-bound, exhausted, alone in the lamplight with a country's smell rising off the pages like breath, turned to the last written page of the book, because the book had known he would, because the book had written that he would, and because some starving, stubborn part of him needed, more than safety, more than freedom, more than air —

— to see his own name.
