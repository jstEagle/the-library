## 3. The Date That Hasn't Happened

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Juniper found his own name.

He would remember that afterward — the candle, the way its light guttered in the draft from the sealed window, the little shadow it threw of his own hand across the page. He would remember it because memory was the only thing he could trust after that night, and even memory he would come to doubt. But first there was the candle, and the cold, and the book lying open on the reading desk of the Guild of Impossible Things at an hour when no scribe was supposed to be anywhere near it.

*Juniper Quintana.*

His name. In a book that had been locked behind glass for a hundred years. Beside a date that had not yet arrived.

He did not scream. He wanted to; some animal part of him pressed up against the back of his teeth and demanded it. But twelve years in the Guild's service teaches a body certain disciplines, and the first of them is this: *when you see something impossible, do not react. Catalogue it.* So Juniper Quintana sat very still in his chair with his hands flat on the desk, the way Master Corvane had taught him to sit during inventory, and he looked at the entry, and he breathed.

The prophecy book — no one called it anything grander than *the book*, though its formal title, stamped in flaking gilt on the spine, was *A True and Complete Account of Things to Come, Being the Prophecies of the Sealed Century* — lay open to the final quires. The last dated entries. The ones that were supposed to be blank.

They were not blank anymore.

He had noticed it two nights ago, or thought he had: a smudge of darkness at the bottom of a page that should have been empty, like a fly pressed between leaves. He had told himself it was foxing, or ink bleed-through from the verso, or his eyes tired from cataloguing the widow's second shadow, which had taken four hours because shadows are argumentative things to describe. Tonight he had come back with the key he was not supposed to have copied and the candle he was not supposed to be burning, and he had opened the book past the point where the handwriting of the long-dead prophet stopped, past the point where the paper should have been clean.

And there, in ink so fresh it still held a faint wet gleam where the candlelight caught it, was tomorrow's date.

*The 14th of the Frost Month.*

And beneath it, in a hand that made his stomach drop through the floor of the world because he knew it — he knew it the way you know your own reflection, the way you know the particular drag of your own nib on cheap guild paper —

*Juniper Quintana.*

And beneath that, one word more:

*Vesper.*

* * *

Denial came first, as it always does, dressed sensibly for the occasion.

*It isn't my name,* he told himself. *It's a common enough name. Juniper. There must be a dozen Junipers in the city. Quintana less so, but — scribes copy names. Someone copying this book might have written mine as an exercise. A marginal note. A test of the pen.*

He leaned closer. The candle flame bent toward the page as if it too wanted to read.

It was not a marginal note. It was set into the text itself, ruled to the same margins, spaced with the same careful hand as every other prophecy in the book — the fall of the bridge at Marrow's Crossing, the drowning of the year, the death of kings whose kingdoms no longer appeared on any map Juniper had ever catalogued. The same hand. The same iron-gall ink, gone brown at the edges of the older letters, black and glistening at the center of the new ones.

But it wasn't the hand of the old prophet. That was the thing his mind kept circling and refusing to land on. The old entries were written in a script Juniper recognized from the Guild's oldest holdings — a formal, spidery antiquarian hand, all ascenders like thorns. This new writing was rounder. Softer. Familiar in a way that made the skin of his arms prickle.

He knew that hand because he practiced it every morning before work, ten lines of it, the way other men did scales. It was his own. Not similar. Not close. *His* — down to the way his 'Q' always came out slightly drunk, tail curling the wrong way, a flaw three different writing masters had failed to beat out of him.

"No," he said aloud, and the word fell flat in the sealed room, absorbed instantly by the shelves of bottled weather and boxed regrets. "No, that's not — that can't be."

Because here was the arithmetic of it, and Juniper trusted arithmetic the way drowning men trust rope. The book was sealed. He had seen the seal himself, wax the color of dried blood, stamped with the Guild's sigil — the impossible knot, the loop that has no beginning. The seal had been unbroken for a hundred years; the Guild's own records said so; Master Corvane said so, and Corvane was many unpleasant things but never careless about property. Ink does not write itself through wax and glass and a century of dust.

Therefore the entry was not new.

Therefore it had always been there, and he had simply never seen it, and pages do not grow words the way walls grow damp, and therefore —

Therefore something was wrong with the world, or wrong with him, and Juniper genuinely could not decide which possibility frightened him more.

* * *

What he did next, he did because it was the only thing he knew how to do. He verified.

He fetched the good lamp from his cell — his one luxury, bought secondhand with three months of saved coppers, brass, dented, burning whale oil he could barely afford — and he brought it back and set it beside the candle, and he turned the page to the colophon at the front of the book where the binding records lived. Every guild book carried them: date of binding, name of binder, number of leaves, record of resealings. He read it twice. Bound in the Year of the Salt Peace. One hundred and six years ago. Resealed once, forty years past, after the incident with the fire — everyone knew about the fire, it was the sort of story apprentices told each other in the dark, how a junior scribe had tried to burn the prophecies and the flames had gone *backward*, licking down into the wick instead of up, until the boy had thrown the whole candlestick out the window rather than watch his own hand age in reverse.

One hundred and six years. And the entry was dated tomorrow.

He went back to the page. He read the full entry for the first time instead of only staring at his name, and reading it was worse than staring, the way falling is worse than standing at the edge.

*On the 14th of the Frost Month, the scribe Juniper Quintana shall look upon the name of Vesper, and Vesper shall look upon him, and the country that was unwritten shall begin again to be written, and the price of the writing shall be paid at the Thorn Door, and the ledger shall balance, and the last memory shall remain.*

There was more, but his eyes kept snagging on the same phrases, going back over them like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. *Shall begin again to be written.* *The Thorn Door.* *The last memory shall remain.*

Vesper.

He knew the word the way you know a word from a dream — present in the mind, absent from every source. He sat back and interrogated his own memory, item by item, the way he'd been trained to catalogue. Had he read it somewhere? In what? He could bring up no book, no deed, no shipping manifest, no gravestone. And yet the word sat in him with weight, with *texture*, warm on one side as if it had been held near a fire. Somewhere in him a bell he had never heard was finished ringing, and the hum of it was still in his bones.

He tried the index. Old books like this one had indexes, laboriously compiled, and the index of the prophecy book was famous among the few scribes allowed near it — a great alphabetical catalogue of every doom the century contained. He turned to V.

Vellum, scarcity of. Vermin, prophesied plagues of. Vestments, mourning.

No Vesper.

He checked twice. Then a third time, running his finger down the column slowly, touching each entry as if the physical contact might shake loose what his eyes refused. Nothing. The word that anchored the most important prophecy in the book — the word written directly beneath his own name — did not appear in the book's own index.

Which meant one of two things. Either the indexer had missed it — impossible; the index was complete, obsessively complete, it cross-referenced itself — or the entry had not existed when the index was made.

The lamp hissed. Outside, somewhere over the rooftops of the city, thunder muttered — and Juniper's head came up sharply, because the bottled storm on Shelf Nine, the one he had catalogued just days ago, had begun, faintly, to rattle against its glass.

* * *

He did what any sensible man would have done. He decided it was a forgery, and he set out to prove it.

This was the compulsive part, though he would not have used that word. He told himself it was diligence. He told himself a scribe's first duty is authentication, and that he was simply doing his duty at an unusual hour. But his hands were shaking as he gathered his tools, and he knew — some quiet honest room deep inside him knew — that he was not trying to disprove the entry. He was trying to survive it. If it was forged, then someone had done a cruel and brilliant thing, and cruelty could be reported, and reported things could be escaped. If it was real, then there was nowhere in the world to report it to.

First: the paper. He held the leaf up to the lamp, tilting it, looking for the telltales. Forged insertions show themselves in the sheet — a slightly different rag content, a watermark interrupted, a thickness where a newer leaf has been sewn in. The page was uniform. Same weight, same faint grain, same waterline ghost of the binder's mark — a thorned rose, he noticed for the first time, which struck him as odd for a prophecy book but he filed it away — running uninterrupted beneath both the ancient text and the new. Whatever had been written on that page had been written on that page's paper. You cannot forge onto paper that did not exist.

Second: the ink. He had a loupe, cheap, cracked, but serviceable. Under magnification the old entries showed their century honestly: iron-gall gone sepia, micro-cracked along the stroke-lines, eaten faintly into the fibers where the acid had bitten. The new entry showed none of that. It sat *on* the page like a living thing resting on a stone. Fresh. Hours old at most. Written with a nib he recognized — medium flex, slight rightward bias, the kind sold in the Guild storeroom by the gross.

Written, in other words, with a pen from his own storeroom. Possibly with *his* pen, which was sitting in his breast pocket at that moment, and which he suddenly wanted very much to throw out the window.

Third — and this was the test he dreaded, because it cut both ways — the seal. He took the lamp down into the vault corridor where the book normally lived, its chain cold under his fingers, and examined the wax sigil with the loupe pressed so hard to his eye socket it left a bruise. Unbroken. Unbroken, uncracked, undisturbed, the impossible knot perfect in its blood-colored wax, exactly as it had been for forty years since the last resealing.

The seal was intact. The entry was fresh. The paper was original. The hand was his own.

Three facts, each one simple, each one true, and together they formed a shape that no logic could hold — a knot with no beginning, like the Guild's own sigil. Juniper stood in the cold corridor holding a lamp, and felt the floor of his ordinary life tilt gently, the way a ship tilts, and understood that he was now a man who believed in something impossible, because the alternative was madness, and madness did not run in his family. Debt ran in his family. Debt was the family trade.

*The 14th of the Frost Month.* Tomorrow.

*The scribe Juniper Quintana.*

Him. Tomorrow. And then Vesper — whatever Vesper was — and a door made of thorns, and a price, and a memory left behind like a coat at a funeral.

* * *

He climbed back to the reading room and sat down across from the open book, and for a long time he simply looked at it, the way you look at a dog that has suddenly spoken.

The candle had died. Only the good lamp lit the room now, and in its steadier light the prophecy book looked older and more patient, if a book can look patient — and this one could, he realized; it had the patience of a trap, of a snare set long ago and long forgotten, waiting without effort for the exact foot.

Because that was the thought that finally arrived, the one he had been walking around all night the way you walk around a hole in the dark: *the book was waiting for me.* Not for anyone. The seal had held for a hundred years against fire and theft and curiosity, and it had opened — or the ink had come through it, or however such a thing was done — for him. On this week. With his name. His flawed, drunken Q. His storeroom nib.

Prophecies, in Juniper's experience — and his experience was considerable; he had catalogued four hundred and eleven items of the impossible, and three of them had been prophetic in nature — prophecies did not behave like sentences. They behaved like debts. They accrued interest. They found you.

And he was, he thought with a laugh that came out as almost nothing, extremely easy to find. He was bound. His debt-bond was lodged in Master Corvane's strongbox; his labor belonged to the Guild until the improbable sum of nine hundred crowns was discharged, which at his current wage would take roughly the rest of his natural life plus eleven years. He could not flee. He could not refuse. Whatever the book wanted with Juniper Quintana, it wanted a man who could not say no.

That was when he heard the footsteps.

Not Corvane's — he knew the Guildmaster's tread, heavy and deliberate, a man who wanted to be heard coming. These were lighter, quicker, moving along the gallery above the reading room with the particular rhythm of someone who had walked this building many times and preferred not to be announced. An intruder, then, at the same impossible hour as himself. Or a collector. Or — his blood went cold and stayed there — someone sent to see whether the scribe had found his name.

Juniper closed the book. His hands moved on their own, trained hands, and did the three things that mattered: he returned it to its shelf-niche behind the false panel, he wiped the desk of every trace of lamp oil and dust-disturbance, and he pocketed the loose scrap of blotting paper on which, without quite deciding to, he had copied the entire entry in his own hand — his own hand, which was also the book's hand, which was a thought he firmly shelved for daylight.

The footsteps reached the stair.

He pinched out the lamp and stood in the dark with his heart going like a hammer in a bottle, and through the doorway he watched a figure descend into the reading room — a stranger, a woman by her outline, hooded, mud on her boots, carrying under one arm a wrapped bundle that glinted brass at one corner. She paused at the threshold. She sniffed the air, once, like a bird.

"Burning whale oil at this hour," she said to the dark room, conversationally. "Somebody's been reading."

Juniper did not move. Did not breathe. The blotting-paper copy of his own death-date pressed against his chest like a second heartbeat.

The woman turned her head slowly toward the shadows where he stood, and he saw, even in the gloom, that her eyes had already found him — and that she was smiling the way people smile when they've been looking for something for a very long time and have just, at last, seen it.

"You're the scribe," she said. "Aren't you."
