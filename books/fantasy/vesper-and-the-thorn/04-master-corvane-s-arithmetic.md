## 4. Master Corvane's Arithmetic

The summons came at half past nine, delivered by a boy of perhaps ten who smelled of lamp oil and fear.

"Guildmaster wants you in the Counting Room," the boy said, not meeting Juniper's eyes. "Now."

Juniper set down his pen mid-stroke. The ink pooled where it fell, a small black bloom spreading across the ledger page he'd been balancing — three farthings owed to a client whose dead husband's letters had been un-burned and re-burned twice, and who disputed the second fee. He watched the ink spread and thought, with a detachment that frightened him a little, *that's what my name looked like in the prophecy book. A bloom. A stain. Something that spreads.*

"Tell him I'm coming," he said.

The boy was already gone, running, as though proximity to Juniper had become a kind of contagion overnight. Which meant Corvane knew. Of course Corvane knew. The Guild of Impossible Things had eyes in every corridor and ears behind every shelf, and Juniper had spent two hours past midnight with a sealed book open under lamplight, copying entries that should not have existed into his own private notebook that most certainly did exist, and if he could see the shape of that transgression written plain across his own face, then Master Corvane — who had made a life's study of reading men the way Juniper read ledgers — had seen it before Juniper had even closed the cover.

He washed his hands. It was an old habit, scribe's superstition: clean hands before difficult pages. Then he took the stairs down from the scriptorium, past the Cabinet of Bottled Weather, past the alcove where the widow Alba's second shadow slept in its velvet-lined case like a sleeping cat, down and down, to the Counting Room at the bottom of the guildhouse, where all debts were weighed.

* * *

The Counting Room had no windows. This was not an architectural accident. Master Corvane maintained that debt was best conducted in a place where the debtor could not see the sky, because the sky suggested possibilities, and possibilities were the natural enemy of obligation.

The room was long and low, its walls lined floor to ceiling with drawers — thousands of them, each labeled in Corvane's own cramped hand, each containing some fragment of someone's indebtedness. A lock of hair here. A confession there. In one drawer, so the apprentices whispered, was the last word a man ever said to his daughter, confiscated as collateral against a gambling debt, and the man had come back every year for eleven years trying to buy it back, and every year the price went up.

Corvane sat at the center of it all behind a desk of black oak, a small man made smaller by stillness. He was sixty-some years old and built like a folded letter, all creases and corners, with pale eyes that did the work other men needed hands for. He was not writing when Juniper entered. He was simply sitting, hands flat on the desk, watching the door, which meant he had been sitting that way for some time, which meant this audience had been arranged in his mind long before the boy was sent.

"You opened the Vellum Case," Corvane said. No greeting. No preamble. His voice was dry as ledger paper.

Juniper stood just inside the door, because Corvane had never once invited him to sit, in nine years of service, and asking permission now would feel like losing something he couldn't name. "I catalogued it," he said. "It was on my list."

"It was on your list to catalogue its *condition*. Dust, damp, integrity of the seals." Corvane lifted one hand and turned it over, examining his own palm as though checking for ink stains. "Not to read it."

"The seals were broken."

"The seals were broken by me, thirty-one years ago, for reasons that are mine." The pale eyes came up. "You read it."

There was no point lying. Corvane could smell a lie the way other men smelled smoke, and besides, Juniper had learned long ago that the only currency worth anything in this room was accuracy. "I read part of it."

"Which part?"

"The end. The new entries."

Something moved behind Corvane's face — not surprise, nothing so generous as surprise. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man confirming an entry already posted. "And what did you find there?"

Juniper's mouth was dry. He considered, briefly, the enormous relief of telling the truth — of setting it all down before this man who understood accounts better than God, of saying *my name is in the book, Master, written in ink older than my grandfather, beside tomorrow's date, followed by the name of a country that doesn't exist* — and watched the truth die in his throat. Because he understood, with the particular clarity of the deeply indebted, that whatever Corvane did with that information, it would be arithmetic. It would be weighed, priced, and entered against him. And Juniper could not afford one more line in his column.

"Prophecies," he said. "Old ones. And some new ones in a different hand."

"Hm." Corvane rose. It was an event, Corvane rising; he did it rarely, and when he did, the whole room seemed to attend to it. He crossed to the wall of drawers and ran one finger along the labels without stopping at any of them, a gesture like a priest touching relics he no longer believed in. "Do you know how you came to this guild, Quintana?"

"I know what it cost."

"Tell me anyway. I find repetition instructive."

Juniper swallowed. "My father borrowed against the family trade-name. When he died, the debt passed to me. Four hundred crowns, plus interest compounded quarterly, plus fees for the appraisal of collateral, plus—" He stopped.

"Plus," Corvane agreed pleasantly, "the fee for the fee. Yes. You were fourteen. You had a fine hand and no prospects, and I bought your bond off the moneylender at a discount, because I could see what you'd become." He turned back around. "A very good scribe. Perhaps the best I've trained. You have a gift, Quintana, for making impossible things look orderly. That is the entire art of this guild, and you have it in your fingers."

"Thank you, Master."

"Don't thank me yet." Corvane returned to his chair but did not sit. He braced both hands on the black oak and leaned across it, and for a moment the lamplight caught his face at an angle that made him look, briefly, genuinely old — old and tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "You read a book that was sealed. That book belongs to the Prophecy Office. Do you understand what that means? Not metaphorically. *Legally.* Every page in that volume is the property of an institution that employs forty auditors and does not believe in mercy, only in accuracy. If they learn a bonded scribe of mine handled their property without authorization—"

"They won't learn it from me."

"They will learn it from *you*, Quintana, because you have the face of a man carrying a secret, and I have watched you carry it badly since dawn. You flinched when Tomas sneezed. You re-inked the same heading four times." Corvane straightened. "You are not built for secrets. You are built for order. It is why you are valuable and why you are, at present, a liability."

The words landed like weights on a scale, each one measured, each one placed precisely where it would press hardest. Juniper felt the familiar sensation of the Counting Room closing in — the windowless dark, the thousand drawers, the sense that the air itself was itemized and he was in arrears on all of it.

"What do you want me to do?" he asked quietly.

"Three things." Corvane held up fingers, folding them down as he spoke. "First: you will forget what you read. Not pretend to forget — I'll know the difference — but perform the forgetting so thoroughly that even you believe it. Second: you will say nothing of the book to anyone. Not the widow Alba, not the apprentices, not the woman you drink with at the Drowned Bell. Third—" the last finger folded, and Corvane's voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse than his cruelty, "—you will bring me anything else you copied out of it. Tonight. All of it."

Juniper's heart performed a single, terrible maneuver. "I didn't copy anything."

"Quintana." Corvane sighed, and it was the sigh of a man doing sums he found tedious. "You are a scribe. You found fresh entries in a century-old book describing events that haven't happened. You are telling me that a man whose entire soul is organized like a ledger looked at an anomaly of that magnitude and did not reach for his notebook?" He shook his head slowly. "I taught you better than to insult us both."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, faint through stone and timber, Juniper could hear the ordinary sounds of the guild going about its evening — the clink of the bottled storm being dusted, someone laughing in the kitchen, life continuing at its small scale while beneath it the Counting Room conducted its larger weather.

"I'll fetch it," Juniper said finally. His voice sounded distant to himself, as though someone else were speaking through him. "The notes. They're in my room."

"I know where they are. I've known since you wrote them." Corvane finally sat, folding himself back into his stillness. "Bring them to me, and we will consider this matter a rounding error. Refuse, and I will be obliged to report an unauthorized handling to the Prophecy Office myself — because if they discover it independently, they will not stop at you, Quintana. They will audit the entire guild. Every drawer. Every collateral. Everything we hold for everyone." He let that settle. "You would not merely be ruining yourself. You would be foreclosing on forty people's secrets. Can you do that arithmetic?"

He could. That was the horror of it — he could do it instantly, cleanly, the way Corvane had trained him to. Forty people. Their last words, their confessions, their shadows and sins, all of it dragged into the light because one scribe had been unable to leave a sealed book alone. And underneath the fear, colder and quieter, ran the thought he could not stop thinking: *the book described tonight. It described me. And the Guildmaster has known about those entries for thirty-one years.*

"Why did you break the seals, Master?" The question escaped before discipline could catch it.

For a long moment he thought Corvane wouldn't answer. Then the old man looked at him with those pale, weighing eyes, and said: "Because thirty-one years ago, I read the same entries you read, and I spent a great deal of money ensuring they would concern no one of consequence to this guild." A pause, thin as a knife. "You were seven years old at the time, Quintana. Whatever the book thinks it knows about you, remember that books are also debts — and I am very good at renegotiating."

* * *

His room was under the eaves, six feet by nine, containing a cot, a desk, a candle stub, and everything he owned in the world, which fit into one satchel and had always struck him as either poverty or freedom depending on the hour. Tonight it felt like neither. Tonight it felt like a cell with excellent lighting.

The notebook was where he'd left it, wedged beneath a loose floorboard along with his mother's ring and the first pen he'd ever been paid for. He knelt and drew it out, and his hands were steady until he opened it, and then they were not.

Seventeen pages. He had copied seventeen pages between midnight and the grey hour, his fine hand marching across them in its neat columns: dates, names, events, the strange recurring word — *Vesper, Vesper, Vesper* — and beside the date of tomorrow, in ink that had dried backward into the century-old vellum, his own name. *Juniper Quintana.* Followed by nothing else. Just the country's name, hanging after his like a sentence awaiting its verb.

He sat on the edge of the cot with the notebook open on his knees and tried to do what Corvane had ordered: to forget. To fold the knowledge away like a document into a drawer, label it, shelve it, lose the key. It was a skill, forgetting; he'd seen Corvane practice it on whole decades. But the entries kept surfacing, patient as water. *The scribe shall stand at the door grown of thorn.* *The country remembers what the world was paid to forget.* *On the last night, the ink runs backward, and the account comes due.*

*The account comes due.*

Everything in his life, reduced at last to a single line. He laughed — one dry note, with no humor anywhere in it — and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

He thought about running. The thought arrived honestly, the way such thoughts do: not as a plan but as a temperature, a sudden heat in the blood. There was a river. There were barges. There was a whole world south of the city where nobody had heard of the Guild of Impossible Things or its arithmetic. He had nine crowns hidden in the satchel lining and a fine hand that could forge any bill of lading in the province.

But the bond. The bond lived in Corvane's strongbox, and a runaway bond turned a man into salvage — any magistrate, any bounty house, any guild collector could claim him, and they would, because a trained scribe was worth more than a horse and easier to break. Nine years he had served. Eleven more remained. He had done the subtraction so many times the number had worn a groove in him: *eleven years, four months.* He was twenty-three years old and owned until thirty-four, and the prophecy book, with its century-old ink, seemed to suggest he wouldn't need to worry about the back half of the term.

He blew out the candle and sat in the dark and held the notebook, and understood that he was going to obey. That was the terrible clarity of the Counting Room: it didn't threaten you with pain. It threatened you with *arithmetic*, and arithmetic always won, because arithmetic was true. He would carry the notebook downstairs. He would place it in Corvane's hands. He would watch it go into a drawer with a label in that cramped hand, and it would become another thing in the guild's possession, another piece of himself held as collateral, and he would go back up to the scriptorium tomorrow and balance accounts for people who could pay, and he would try, with all his considerable discipline, to forget.

He was almost at the door when the knock came.

Three raps, quick and irregular — not the guild's formal knock, not the collectors' knock, nothing with a pattern he recognized. Juniper froze, notebook against his chest, and listened. Through the thin wall he heard the house settling, the storm in its bottle muttering to itself, and below, faintly, voices in the front hall — and one of them was female, and one of them was saying words that did not belong in the Guild of Impossible Things at this hour or any hour:

"—told you I've got a compass that points at places that don't exist, and I want to know what your guild pays for things like that. And while we're haggling—" a pause, and then, lighter, sharper, like a blade being drawn an inch, "—maybe your scribe can tell me why the name *Vesper* is written in his little black book. Because I saw it when he dropped it on the stairs just now, and I haven't heard that name spoken aloud in twelve years."

Juniper looked down. The notebook was not against his chest. The notebook was on the floor by the door, where it had slipped from his hands — fallen open, fallen face-up, its seventeen pages of stolen prophecy spread out on the boards like a hand of cards he'd been caught holding.

He hadn't taken it downstairs yet. He hadn't left his room. And somewhere below, in the hall of the guild that owned him, a stranger was reading his handwriting by candlelight, saying the one word the whole world had been paid to forget.
