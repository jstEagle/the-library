## 6. What the Maps Refuse

Wren had a rule about names: never say one out loud unless you were prepared to carry it. She'd learned that rule the hard way, at nine years old, in a customs house that smelled of lamp oil and official ink, when a man with a brass armband told her mother that Vesper no longer existed and therefore neither, technically, did they.

Her mother had wept. Wren had done arithmetic. If a word could unmake a country, then words were weapons, and weapons could be stolen.

She was thinking about all of this now because the scribe—Quintana, Juniper Quintana, a name like a prayer someone had tripped over—was staring at her across the back room of the Drowned Bell as if she'd just told him the moon was made of ledger paper. Which, in fairness, she had not yet. She hadn't told him anything yet. She'd only said the word *Vesper*, and he'd gone white in the particular way of a man who has found his own handwriting somewhere it should never have been.

"You know the name," he said. Not a question. Scribes, she was learning, asked questions that weren't questions the way other men breathed.

"I know lots of names." She turned her cup on the table, watching the tannin ring it left. "Doesn't mean I hand them out for free."

"Your compass pointed at it."

"My compass points at everything that doesn't exist. It's very busy these days." That was true, and it was also a lie, and the difference between those two things was a country wide. The compass didn't point at *everything*. It pointed at one thing, always, no matter how she spun it, no matter which city she stood in, no matter how many times she told herself she'd sell the wretched thing and be rid of it. It pointed south-southwest, into the hills where the maps went blank, and it had been pointing there since the night she stole it off a dead smuggler's neck outside a border that wasn't a border anymore.

The scribe's hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle. Guild hands. Debt-bonded, if she guessed right—and she was rarely wrong about debt; you could smell it on people, a faint sourness under the skin, like milk turning. He had the look of a man who had counted his remaining years in entries catalogued and found the sum wanting.

"Tell me what it is," he said. "Vesper."

"Why?"

"Because it's written next to my name in a book that's been sealed for a hundred years, and the entry describes tonight, and I would very much like to stop feeling like I'm standing at the edge of something."

Wren considered him. Outside, the Drowned Bell's common room roared with the ordinary music of the living—dice, argument, a fiddle slightly flat—and in here it was just the two of them and a candle burning down toward its own funeral. She could walk away. She'd walked away from better men than this, and worse ones too, and the walking away had never once cost her anything except the things she refused to count.

But he'd said *tonight*, and his hands were shaking, and she knew what it was to find your name in a place names shouldn't go.

"All right," she said. "But you're buying the next bottle, and you're going to want to."

* * *

She started the way she always started: with the treaty.

"The Concord of Ashes," she said. "Signed eleven years ago by nine governments, none of them Vesperan. You can read it yourself—it's public record, which is the funniest thing about it. Article One: the nation of Vesper is dissolved. Article Two: its territories are apportioned among the signatories. Article Three—" and here she made her voice go flat and official, the voice of clerks and hangmen, "—all cartographic, documentary, and historical reference to Vesper shall be unwritten, such that the nation shall pass from memory as it has passed from law."

"Unwritten," the scribe repeated. His fingers moved against the table, tracing letters that weren't there. A scribe's tic. She'd seen it before in men who worked with their hands in ink—they touched the world like it might need correcting.

"That's the actual legal term. Not erased. Not forbidden. *Unwritten.* Like the country was a rough draft somebody got paid to clean up." She took a drink. The wine was bad, which meant it was honest. "And here's the part they don't put in the histories, mostly because there aren't any histories anymore. Vesper didn't lose a war. There was no invasion, no surrender, no last stand with banners and blood. They just—signed. The old king was dead, the treasury was hollow, the neighbors were hungry, and so a room full of tired men agreed that a thousand years of the place would be tidied away. Nine signatures. That's all it takes. Nine."

"And the people?"

"Scattered." The word came out of her like a splinter. She let it. "Resettlement programs, they called them. Families split across three borders so no village of exiles could form. Children sent to foster households in countries whose languages they didn't speak. And anyone who kept saying the name—" she shrugged, a small cruel motion, "—there are laws about sedition, and after Article Three, saying *Vesper* in public was sedition. You couldn't conspire to restore a nation that never existed. Legally elegant. You have to admire it, if you're the kind of monster who admires things."

The candle guttered. Somewhere beyond the wall, the fiddle found a key it liked better.

"So the maps," the scribe said slowly. "When you said the compass points at places that don't exist—"

"The maps refuse, Quintana. That's the phrase my mother used, and she was a schoolteacher before the schools stopped teaching it, so listen, because she chose her words like a woman laying stones. The maps don't lie. Lying would be easy. Lying you could catch—the coastline would be wrong, the rivers bent, some clerk's error you could sue over. No. What the treaty bought was refusal. Every map printed since the Concord simply *ends* along the old borders. Not blank—blank would be an admission that something was there to leave blank. The paper itself stops. Rivers run to the edge of the page and dry up mid-ink. Roads arrive at nothing and the cartographer's hand, without knowing why, turns aside and draws a forest instead. Surveyors who go in come out with instruments that disagree with each other and memories that won't hold still, and after the first few went mad or quiet or both, nobody goes in anymore. Officially, the region is 'administratively consolidated.' Unofficially—" she tapped the table, once, twice, "—it's a hole shaped like a country."

"And your compass doesn't refuse."

"No. My compass is a rude little instrument with no respect for treaties. It points at the hole. It has always pointed at the hole. I've had offers for it from people who very much wanted it to stop doing that." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere useful. "I declined. Politely. Mostly politely."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into something careful, the way men speak when they're carrying something fragile up a staircase.

"The book. The prophecy book in our archive. It mentions Vesper. Tonight. Me. If the country was unwritten—if every reference was purged—how does a century-old book still have it in there?"

"Ah." Wren set down her cup. This was the part she hated, the part she'd been circling all evening like a moth around a window. "That's the question, isn't it. Here's what I know, and I'll thank you not to interrupt, because I've only ever told this story once before and it went badly."

* * *

She told him about her mother first, though she'd sworn she wouldn't. That was the trouble with wine and candles and men with shaking hands; they pried the lids off things.

Her mother, Maren, had been a teacher in a town called Lowmark, which had been a river town in southern Vesper, which was now, according to every document in the world, a market village in the province of Callowmere, and had always been, and you would be fined for suggesting otherwise. Maren had kept books. Illegal ones—not smuggling ledgers, worse: a grammar of Vesperan, a book of children's songs, a list of the names of everyone in Lowmark with the old spellings, the ones that predated the Concord's standardized registers. She taught them to Wren at night, behind a shuttered window, in a whisper, like prayers. *This is how you write home,* she'd say. *This is the word for hearth. This is the word for the space between the fields where the wind comes through. This is your grandmother's name, and you will not forget it even if they take the paper.*

"They came for her in the fourth year," Wren said. "Not soldiers. Auditors. Men with brass armbands and portable presses, and they didn't arrest her, that's the thing everyone gets wrong about the unwriting. Arresting proves you matter. They just—visited. Sat in our kitchen. Asked her questions in soft voices while another man went through her books with a razor and a pot of paste, removing pages so cleanly you'd never know pages had been there. And at the end, the head auditor thanked her for her cooperation, and my mother thanked him back, and I sat on the stairs hating her for thanking him, and three weeks later she'd forgotten the word for hearth. Just that one word. Gone. She'd look at the fireplace and her mouth would do a little searching thing, like a hand patting pockets. Everything else stayed. Cooking, songs in the new language, my name. But that word was lifted out of her like a rotten tooth, and whatever else they took, they took it so gently she never knew she'd been robbed."

The scribe had gone very still. "The Prophecy Office," he said. "Auditors. Brass armbands."

"You know them."

"I work adjacent to them. Everyone in the Guild does. I thought they were—clerks. Archivists. Men who file things." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, brief and joyless. "I broke into their reading room tonight. Well. My credentials opened the door. I saw shelves—"

"Shelves of nations," Wren said. "I know. Gray bindings, no titles, arranged by date of dissolution. My mother's auditor carried one under his arm the whole visit, like a hymnal. I've spent eleven years learning what those shelves are. Every erased thing ends up filed somewhere, Quintana. That's the dirty secret of unwriting. You can't destroy a country. There's too much of it. Too many roads worn into the earth, too many graves, too many words soaked into doorframes. So instead they *archive* it. They move the whole nation indoors. Lock it in the dark, in a building with no windows, and call the lock peace." She spread her hands. "The prophecy book isn't a contradiction. It's inventory. Somewhere in that office is a shelf where Vesper lives now, between two other dead countries, and your sealed book is either a leak in the filing system or a receipt."

"A receipt," he echoed.

"For the purchase. Eleven years ago, nine governments bought themselves a forgetting. Somebody wrote down the terms. Somebody always writes down the terms—that's the first thing they teach you in any guild worth the name, isn't it? Never trust a deal that isn't in the ledger." She leaned forward. "And here's the part that keeps me up at night, scribe, the part I'll deny telling you if you ever repeat it. My compass points at the hole in the map. Fine. But lately—this past year—it's started doing something else. Some nights, near dawn, it swings. Chases its own tail like a dog after a scent. And the nights it does that are the same nights, I'd stake my life, that the exiles dream loud. There's a woman in the Saltmarket, old Veska, she was a girl in Lowmark before I was born, and when I told her about the swinging she crossed herself with the wrong hand—an old-country gesture, my mother did it too—and she said, *the country is dreaming of itself, child, and one night it will wake up, and God help whoever's standing nearby.*"

The candle died. Neither of them moved to light another. In the dark, the sounds of the Drowned Bell went on—laughter, dice, the flat fiddle—as if the world outside this room had no idea what was being said inside it, which, Wren reflected, was the condition of the entire world regarding Vesper, and had been for eleven years.

"Your book says tonight," the scribe said at last. His voice had changed. Steadier now, or pretending to be. "My name. And then Vesper."

"Yes."

"What happens tonight?"

"How would I know? I'm a smuggler, not a prophet." But her hand had gone, without her permission, to the compass at her throat, and she felt it through her shirt, that faint insistent tug, south-southwest, patient as a debt. "What I know is this. Countries don't die of signatures. They die of forgetting, and forgetting is slow, and it can be fought—I've watched my mother fight it with a razor at her throat and lost, but I've watched others win, little wins, a song here, a name there. And I know that a sealed book that updates itself, and a compass that's gone restless in the same season, and a debt-bonded scribe whose name shows up next to a dead country's name on the eve of—of *whatever tonight is*—those aren't three coincidences. Those are threads. And I have spent eleven years being pulled around by other people's threads, and I am *done* with it."

She stood, because sitting still had become impossible, and went to the window. Below, the street ran with lamplight and late traffic, the great indifferent city, capital of a league of nations that had signed its name to a tidy murder and slept soundly after.

"There's someone you need to meet," she said, not turning around. "Before tonight does whatever it's going to do. A cartographer. Odile Marchetti. Best mapmaker of her generation, until she drew a road network that officially has never existed—from memory, every ford and switchback, accurate to the pace. They stripped her license, seized her atlases, and locked her in the Spindlehouse, where they keep people who insist on remembering. She's been in there four years. She's the only person alive who could get us across the hole in the map, if it comes to that. And given that your name is in a death-book next to a country that doesn't exist, scribe, I am increasingly of the opinion that it comes to that."

Behind her, silence. Then, quietly: "You'd break a stranger out of prison. For a country you won't even say you're from."

Wren watched her own reflection in the dark glass, and hated how legible it was.

"I never said I'm from anywhere," she said. "That's the trick of it. You can't unwrite a person who refuses to be written down in the first place." She turned, and let him see, just for a moment, the thing under the cynicism, the thing she wore grief like—a coat, a disguise, a country she carried folded up small against her heart. Then she shuttered it again. "Meet me at the Spindlehouse gates an hour past dawn. Bring your guild credentials and whatever courage you've got left after tonight. And Quintana—"

"Yes?"

"If the book gives you any more instructions, you tell me first. Before Corvane, before the Office, before God. Because I've read enough ledgers to know that whoever holds the pen holds the power, and I am very tired of other people holding the pen."

He nodded. And in the dark, against her breastbone, the compass gave a single sharp swing—south-southwest, toward the hole, toward the hills, toward home—and settled again, like a heart deciding to keep beating.
