## 7. The Cartographer Who Remembers

Wren had been in a great many prisons, but never one that smelled of lavender.

That was the first thing wrong with the House of Correction at Saint Verre. The second was the garden. Convicts in grey smocks knelt among rows of rosemary and chamomile, their wrists chained to iron stakes driven into the soil like the stakes were plants themselves, something the ground had grown overnight. A matron with a face like folded linen walked the rows with shears, snipping dead blossoms and, occasionally, glancing at the prisoners as though deciding which of them had begun to wilt.

"They call it rehabilitation," Wren said, arms crossed, watching through the gate.

Juniper stood beside her with his scribe's credentials clutched in both hands like a talisman he wasn't sure still worked. "It's a prison."

"It's a prison that wants you to *thank* it." She spat neatly into the gutter. "Those are the worst kind."

The plan was simple, because Wren only ever made simple plans: Juniper would go in under his Guild credentials — the Guild of Impossible Things had standing arrangements with half the correctional houses in the city, since half the inmates were there for impossible things — and request access to a prisoner for document transcription. Scribes copied confessions, wills, last statements. It was dull work, invisible work, exactly the kind of work nobody questioned.

And Odile Marchetti had been copying documents for six years. That was her sentence. Not for theft, not for sedition, not for any of the crimes that got ordinary people chained to rosebushes. For *drawing*.

"Remember," Wren murmured, adjusting the strap of the compass case across her chest, "you're bored. Bored scribes don't get remembered. Ask for the woman, complain about her handwriting, get your ink on her paper, and walk out."

"And if she won't come?"

"She'll come." Wren said it with more certainty than she felt. "People who've been locked up six years for a country that officially never existed — they're either mad or they're waiting. Mad doesn't help us. Waiting does."

Juniper looked at her sideways. "You've met her before."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't pretend it was. "Once. Years ago. Before." She let the word do its work; *before* was a door she kept locked, and Juniper, to his credit, didn't rattle the handle. "She drew me a map once. Free of charge. Said maps should be free the way water should be free, and then she got arrested for saying so about the wrong map."

"The wrong map being—"

"Vesper." The word came out flat, worn smooth from handling. "Come on. Your credentials are burning a hole in your pocket and my patience is already spent."

* * *

The interior of Saint Verre smelled of lavender too, but underneath it was the true smell of every institution Wren had ever slipped out of: lye, damp stone, and fear kept politely out of sight. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs examined Juniper's papers twice, then a third time, running his thumb along the Guild seal as though checking whether it might peel off.

"Transcription detail," the clerk said. "Who authorized?"

"Article Nine," Juniper said, and Wren nearly smiled. He'd learned that trick somewhere — say a number with enough confidence and people assume the number exists. "Standard arrangement. I'll need the prisoner at a table, good light, and no interruptions. The Guild is billed by the hour, so if you'd like to stand here discussing it—"

The clerk did not want to discuss it. Nobody ever wanted to be billed by the hour.

They were led down a corridor where the lavender gave way entirely, past cells whose doors had observation slits like half-closed eyes, to a workroom at the end: a long table, two chairs, a barred window admitting a slab of afternoon light. Waiting inside, hands folded, sat a woman who could have been anywhere between fifty and seventy, with iron-grey hair cropped short and forearms mapped with old scars — burn scars, Wren knew, from a life spent hunched over candle flames and hot pressing-irons, sealing ink into vellum before it could smear or lie.

Odile Marchetti looked up, and her eyes went first not to Juniper but to Wren, standing just inside the doorway, and something moved behind them like a fish under ice.

"Well," Odile said. Her voice was dry and precise, a voice used to being read aloud from. "They've sent me a scribe and a smuggler. Either the Guild has gotten strange, or this isn't transcription."

"It's transcription," Juniper began, laying out his papers with the careful movements of a man building a wall between himself and the truth.

"It's not." Odile turned her folded hands over. Her nails were trimmed blunt, practical, stained faintly blue at the edges — thorn-ink, Wren thought, and her heart did something complicated. Six years in a lavender prison and the woman still had thorn-ink under her nails. You couldn't wash that out. It got into the skin the way grief did. "Nobody sends a smuggler for transcription. Sit down, boy. Both of you. And tell me what's happened to Vesper now, because something has. I can smell it. Something has always just happened to Vesper; that's how they keep it dead. Funerals every week so nobody notices there's no body."

Wren pulled out a chair and sat, straddling it backwards, arms folded on the backrest. "Hello, Marchetti."

"Hello, little bird. Still running things across borders that don't want you?"

"Still drawing countries that don't want to exist?"

"They exist," Odile said quietly. "That's the whole crime, isn't it? They exist, and I said so on paper."

* * *

It took twenty minutes to tell her everything, and Wren watched Odile the way she watched unfamiliar waters — for currents, for rocks, for the moment the surface lied. She told it plainly: the prophecy book, the fresh entries, Juniper's name beside tomorrow's date beside the word *Vesper*. The compass that pointed at places that weren't. The Prophecy Office, Auditor Hale, the flight that hadn't started yet but was coming for them all the same.

Odile listened without interrupting, which Wren recognized as a professional habit. Mapmakers listened the way sailors did — cataloging, correcting, waiting for the detail that didn't fit the chart.

When it was done, Odile was silent for a long moment. Then she asked one question.

"The entries in the book. The new ones. What's the hand like?"

Juniper blinked. "I — what?"

"The handwriting. Describe it."

"Old-fashioned. Heavy downstrokes. The letters lean left, against the direction of reading, like—" He stopped.

"Like they're trying to leave the page," Odile finished. "Thorn-ink script. There was a school of it, once, in the eastern valleys. Prophecy was a trade there, before it became an office. They wrote against the reading so that the future would have to fight its way forward." Her eyes had gone distant, fixed on the barred window and the slab of light. "I copied a page of it when I was an apprentice. Just one page. My master burned it and beat me and burned it again, and I have spent fifty years being glad and sorry in equal measure."

"You know the book," Wren said.

"I know its *family*." Odile unfolded her hands at last and laid them flat on the table, and Wren saw that they trembled very slightly, the way precision instruments tremble — not weakness, but sensitivity. "There are perhaps nine books of that lineage in the world. Ledgers of what will be. The Office holds most of them. And every one of them has a closing clause, girl. Every prophecy ends the same way: with the thing it describes being finished. Ended. Closed like an account." She looked at Juniper then, really looked, and Wren saw the cartographer take his measure the way she'd once taken the measure of coastlines — patiently, mercilessly, with love. "You're tomorrow's entry."

"So everyone keeps telling me."

"Don't be glib. Glibness is what people use instead of screaming." But there was no cruelty in it. If anything, Wren thought, there was something almost gentle, the gentleness of someone who had screamed herself hoarse years ago and found nothing on the other side but quiet. "Now. Why are you here? You didn't cross this city, forge your way into a correctional house, and bring a smuggler to see an old woman because of handwriting. What do you want?"

Wren leaned forward. This was the part she'd rehearsed on the walk over, and rehearsal hadn't made it easier. "We need out. Not out of the city — out of the *treaty*. Out from under the unwriting. There's a way, if the stories are true. A last road. A door."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren heard the lavender-scented hush of the corridor, the distant snip of the matron's shears, her own heartbeat doing something undignified.

"The Thorn Door," Odile said at last.

"You know it."

"I *drew* it." And now the trembling in her hands stopped, as though the words had anchored them. "Sixty-one roads into Vesper. Fifty-nine closed by treaty, walled or drowned or simply declared never to have existed. One burned the night they signed the Erasure — the Gate of Ash, at Carrow Ford. And one that was never on any treaty map at all, because the treaty men never found it, because it doesn't open onto a road. It opens onto a *condition*. You don't walk to the Thorn Door. You become the kind of person it will admit, and then it's simply there, in front of you, growing out of whatever's nearest." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Blackthorn. Black as tar, blooming out of season. It takes a toll. Everything true takes a toll."

"What toll?" Juniper asked.

"That depends what year you ask." Odile's gaze slid away. "In the old songs, it asks for memory. In the newer ones, worse. But here's what matters, scribe, and listen carefully because I'll only draw this once: nobody alive knows the roads to it. Nobody except the man who surveyed them, and he's forty years dead, and except me." She tapped her own temple. "It's all in here. Every mile. Every ford and switchback and village that no longer appears on any chart printed south of the mountains. Six years they've kept me copying other people's confessions, and every night I close my eyes and walk Vesper end to end, and every morning it's all still there. Do you know what that's like? To be the last copy of an entire country?"

"Yes," Juniper said softly.

And Wren watched the old woman look at him — really look, the way you look at a page you suspect contains your own name — and something passed between them that Wren couldn't chart and didn't try to.

* * *

"But," Odile said.

"There's always a but," Wren muttered.

"I'm in here, and the knowledge is in here with me, and neither of us leaves through the front gate." Odile spread her hands. "You want me to guide you to the Thorn Door, you'll need more than my memory. You'll need my atlas. My master atlas — the one they confiscated at trial. Every survey I ever made, every road I restored from interviews with exiles, from grave-markers, from the way rivers bend around towns that aren't supposed to be there. Two hundred pages. It sits in the evidence vault of the Prophecy Office, filed under *Fiction, Dangerous*."

Wren laughed despite herself. "Of course it does."

"The vault is not a place one walks into," Odile said. "But scribes do. Scribes go everywhere. Ink opens more doors than crowbars ever did." She fixed Juniper with a look. "Well, boy? You wanted a guide. Guides cost. My price is my life's work off their shelf and back in my hands. Get me my atlas, and I'll walk you to the Door myself — through every road they tried to erase, and I'll name each one as we go, so the roads know they're remembered. Roads remember feet, you know. Given half a chance, they remember."

Juniper had gone pale. Wren had watched him go pale twice already today, and she was starting to compile a small taxonomy of his fears: there was pale-because-prophecy, pale-because-debt, and now apparently pale-because-heist. "The Prophecy Office," he said. "Where Hale works. Where the book came from. You want me to walk into the building that owns my death warrant and *borrow* a banned atlas."

"Borrow," Odile agreed pleasantly. "Steal sounds so crude for a man of letters."

"He'll say yes," Wren said.

Both of them looked at her.

"He'll say yes," she repeated, "because he's already decided he's a dead man walking — he read his own name in a book, Marchetti, you don't come back from that unchanged — and a man who's already dead is the most dangerous kind of volunteer. He's got nothing left to protect except other people." She said it lightly, but it landed heavier than she intended, and she saw Juniper hear the weight of it, and she saw him decide not to flinch.

He took a breath. Straightened his ridiculous scribe's collar. "Conditions," he said. "First: we do this tonight, before Hale's agents finish tracing the book's missing pages to me. Second: nobody gets hurt. I've seen what the Office does to people who resist; I'm not adding to it. Third—" he hesitated, and Wren watched him find the third condition the way a man finds a coin in a coat he'd given up on, "—third, when this is done, whatever happens at the Door, the atlas goes with you. Not sold. Not traded. *Yours.*"

Odile Marchetti smiled for the first time, and it transformed her face from a ruin into a map of everything the ruin had survived.

"Scribe," she said, "you may yet be worth the ink."

* * *

They walked out through the lavender garden as the light went long and gold. Behind them, the convicts knelt among their herbs, chained to the earth, tending beautiful things they would never be allowed to keep. Ahead, the city was lighting itself lamp by lamp, and somewhere in that spreading dark the Prophecy Office waited with its shelves of erased nations and its vault full of dangerous fictions.

Wren felt the compass case against her ribs, warm as a sleeping bird, and beneath it her own heartbeat, quicker than she liked.

Desperate hope, her mother used to call it — the kind of hope that comes after you've stopped expecting anything, when wanting something again feels like reopening a wound to check whether it might heal. Wren had crossed borders with forged papers, smuggled relics through checkpoints, talked her way past men with drawn steel. None of it had scared her like this: walking toward a country that had been killed on paper, carrying the wild, stupid belief that paper wasn't the last word.

"Hey," Juniper said quietly, as the gate swung shut behind them. "Thank you. For coming in with me. You didn't have to."

"No," Wren agreed. "I didn't."

"Why did you?"

She looked at him — this debt-ridden, doomed, stubbornly decent scribe who had known her three days and had already started trusting her, which was either wisdom or a symptom — and she told him a piece of the truth, because pieces were all she dealt in.

"Because six years ago a woman drew me a map for free," she said, "and I've been trying to pay it back ever since, and the interest has gotten obscene."

Juniper smiled. Then his face changed, and Wren followed his gaze down the street.

Under the lamp at the corner, a figure in the grey-and-silver of the Prophecy Office stood perfectly still, watching them. Not approaching. Not speaking. Just watching, the way an auditor watches a ledger — noting, totaling, waiting for the column to close.

"Wren," Juniper said. "Is that—"

"I see him." She took his arm, casually, the way lovers do, and turned them both down a side street at an unhurried pace while her mind ran ahead like a cut purse rolling downhill. "Don't run. Running confirms. Walk. And start thinking hard, scribe, about every door in that Office with your credentials on it — because it looks like we're going in sooner than tonight."

Behind them, unhurried, the grey figure began to follow.
