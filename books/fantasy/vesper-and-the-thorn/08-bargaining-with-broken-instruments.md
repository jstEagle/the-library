## 8. Bargaining with Broken Instruments

The house at the end of Coil Street had been a mapmaker's once. You could tell by the windows—each one cut at a different height, as though the builder had wanted to see the world from every elevation at once—and by the door, which bore no number, only a small brass plate engraved with a compass rose whose needle had been pried out long ago, leaving a wound-shaped absence at its center.

Wren knocked the way she did everything: twice, hard, and without apology.

"Nobody lives here," Juniper said, hugging his satchel against the wind that came funneled down the street off the salt flats. The city of Corium was all chimneys and cold this time of year, and Coil Street was its coldest artery, abandoned even by the lamplighters. "You said she was imprisoned."

"She was." Wren blew on her fingers. "Prisons let people out eventually. That's the flaw in them. Everything gets out eventually—it's just a question of what shape it's in when it does."

The door opened before Wren could knock a third time.

The woman standing in the gap was tall and stooped at once, the way a tree is stooped after a century of weather it refused to acknowledge. Her hair was iron-gray and cropped close to the skull, practical as a ledger line. She wore a coat with too many pockets, each one weighted with something that clinked or rustled or shifted, and her eyes—pale, almost colorless, like paper left too long in the sun—moved over them both with the speed and precision of someone measuring distance.

"You're not guild," she said. Her voice was dry, unhurried, the voice of a person who had already decided how much of her attention anything deserved.

"We're not Prophecy Office either," Wren said. "Which is rarer these days."

"That depends entirely on why you're here." The woman did not move aside. "State your business or walk back down my street. I'll know within four words whether you're lying. Everyone lies in fewer than four words. It's the fifth word where they get ambitious."

Juniper felt his mouth go dry. He had rehearsed this—on the walk over, in Wren's rented room, half the night while the candle drowned itself in its own wax—but rehearsal dissolved now, the way ink dissolves when you breathe on it wrong.

"My name is Juniper Quintana," he said. "I'm a scribe with the Guild of Impossible Things. I found my name in a prophecy book that shouldn't have entries in it. Beside tomorrow's date. And next to my name is a word I've never seen written anywhere official, because everywhere official has been ordered to forget it." He swallowed. "Vesper."

Something happened to the woman's face. It was very small—the kind of change a cartographer would mark with a symbol rather than words, a contour line shifting by a single degree—but Juniper saw it, because he spent his life watching faces for the moment they stopped performing.

"The fifth word," she said quietly, "was Vesper."

"Yes."

She stepped back from the doorway. Not an invitation, exactly. More like a door deciding, on its own recognizance, to be open.

"Come in before the street hears you," she said. "Coil Street has ears. Most of them mine, but not all."

* * *

Inside, the house was a country made of paper.

Maps covered every wall—not framed, not preserved, but *lived in*, pinned and layered and annotated until the plaster beneath them had become theoretical. There were maps of coastlines Juniper didn't recognize, mountain ranges drawn in confident strokes that no atlas he'd ever catalogued confirmed, cities rendered in such loving detail that he could almost smell their markets. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, were roads. Roads marked in a fine brown ink, roads that bent around hills no other cartographer had bothered to record, roads labeled in a script that was beautiful and unfamiliar and that made the back of Juniper's neck prickle with recognition he could not account for.

"You drew Vesper," he said. It wasn't a question. The whole house was the question, answered a thousand times over.

"I drew Vesper for forty years," the woman said, clearing two stools off a workbench with the economy of long habit. "I drew it before they unwrote it, and I drew it after, from memory, which is the only way anyone draws anything worth trusting. Memory is the last honest instrument. Everything else can be bribed." She sat down across from them and laced her fingers together. "Odile Marchetti. Formerly Chief Cartographer to the Crown of Vesper, a title that now exists nowhere but in this room and in the heads of three other stubborn old people. You've come about the roads."

"How did you—" Wren began.

"Everyone comes about the roads eventually." Odile waved a hand at the walls. "Smugglers who want routes through territory that officially isn't there. Scholars who want proof. Grief-struck exiles who want a picture of home to hang above a hearth in a country that fined me eleven hundred crowns and two years in Marrowgate Prison for providing exactly that." Her pale eyes settled on Juniper. "But you're none of those. You're a guild scribe with ink under his nails and terror behind his eyes, and you said *prophecy*, which means you're here about the ending, not the roads. So. Tell me the rest, and tell it in order. Cartographers are helpless without order."

So Juniper told it in order. The sealed book in the Guild archive, older than the guild itself, chained shut since before Corvane's grandfather had been born. The entries appearing in fresh ink inside it—entries describing things that had happened hours before they were written, then days, then weeks ahead. His own name beside tomorrow's date. And beside his name, the word *Vesper*, followed by nothing, followed by blankness, followed by a final page that the book would not let him turn past.

He told it flatly, the way he catalogued acquisitions, because that was the only voice he trusted himself with. But somewhere in the middle of it—in the part about the blank space after his name, the white silence where a death should be written—his composure cracked along a seam he hadn't known was there, and Odile watched him crack with the patience of someone reading terrain.

When he finished, the house was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, paper shifted, as if the maps were turning over in their sleep.

"A prophecy book that writes itself forward," Odile said at last. "And it chose a debt-scribe to carry it. That's either an insult or an honor, and with prophecies it's usually both." She leaned forward. "Show me your hands."

Juniper blinked. "My—"

"Hands. Scribes' hands tell truths their mouths won't. Ink-stains in the wrong places mean you write things you're not supposed to. Calluses in the wrong places mean you hold your pen like a weapon." She waited, and feeling foolish, Juniper extended his hands palms-up across the workbench.

Odile studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy some private standard, because she nodded once, sharply, as though confirming a coordinate.

"You grip your pen like a man holding onto a ledge," she said. "Good. Ledge-grippers don't let go. Now—the important question, and answer it honestly, because I will build the next month of my life on your answer." She fixed him with those sun-bleached eyes. "Do you want to survive this prophecy, or do you want to understand it? They are not the same desire. They lead to different doors. And I only travel with people who know which one they're walking toward."

Juniper looked down at his own hands, still open on the bench between them, ink-stained, ledge-gripping, ordinary.

"I want both," he said finally. "I know that's greedy. But I've spent six years paying off a debt by writing down other people's impossible things, and I've never once been allowed to ask whether any of them were *true*. If I'm going to die in a book, I want to at least know why the book picked me. And if there's a way through it—" his voice caught, steadied— "then yes. I want to live. I'm not noble enough to pretend otherwise."

For the first time, Odile smiled. It was a thin thing, mostly corners, but genuine.

"Honest greed," she said. "Rarer than honest courage. All right, scribe. Here is my price, and it is not negotiable, so save your breath."

She rose and crossed to the far wall, where one map hung apart from all the others—larger, older, its edges darkened with age and handling. Even from across the room, Juniper could feel the difference in it. The other maps were drawings. This one was a *testimony*.

"In Marrowgate they took everything," Odile said, her back to them. "My instruments. My drafts. My letters. Eleven hundred crowns I did not have, so they took years instead. But there was one thing they wanted more than all the rest, and they didn't burn it, and do you know why?" She turned. "Because burning it would have been an admission. You cannot destroy a document and call it fiction in the same breath. So they locked it away instead, in the deepest shelf of the Prophecy Office vault, filed under erasures, where it sits to this day gathering dust and proving, by its continued existence, that Vesper was real enough to need forgetting."

"The master atlas," Wren breathed. "Your original survey. Every road."

"Every road, every bridge, every ford and ferry crossing and shepherd's path, surveyed by my own feet over forty years." Odile's voice stayed level, but her hands, Juniper noticed, had closed into fists at her sides. "It is the only complete map of Vesper in existence. Without it, I can guide you by memory, and memory drifts—it loses a mile here, misplaces a river there, and in the borderlands a misplaced river will kill you. With it—" she tapped the testimony-map on the wall— "with it, I can take you to the Thorn Door itself, if it exists, if it still stands, if half the stories are true. Which they aren't. Stories never are. Maps are worse."

"And the price," Juniper said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water, "is that we break into the Prophecy Office."

"The vault beneath it, specifically. Where the Auditor Hale you mentioned keeps his shelves of unwritten nations." Odile sat back down, folding her hands, the bargain struck in her posture before it was struck aloud. "You have guild credentials. A scribe can walk into that building on paperwork alone—they respect paper there the way priests respect relics. I am a convicted forger of geography; I cannot cross that threshold without being arrested on sight. The smuggler—" she glanced at Wren— "has a face that appears on at least four watch lists, if my information is current."

"It's current," Wren said cheerfully. "I keep them updated."

"So it falls to you, Juniper Quintana." Odile's gaze came back to him and stayed there, patient and pitiless. "Recover my atlas, and I will guide you to the Door. Fail, and we are all three of us finished—you'll be arrested, the smuggler will be arrested, and I will burn this house and every map in it and disappear so thoroughly that even I won't remember where I went. Those are the terms. Take them or leave them."

* * *

Later—much later, after Wren had gone to arrange passage and Odile had retreated into the back rooms to argue with her instruments—Juniper stood alone in the map-lined front room with a cup of tea going cold in his hands, doing the arithmetic that scribes do.

He was good at arithmetic. It was most of what the Guild had ever taught him: the cost of things weighed against the cost of other things, debts balanced against obligations, every choice a column of figures that always, somehow, summed to what Master Corvane wanted. He had thought himself free of that arithmetic for one night—one wild, impossible night of running through salt fields with a smuggler's compass pulling them toward a country that didn't exist.

But here it was again, the same cold ledger, opened in a different hand.

*Cost:* breaking into the Prophecy Office. Discovery meant arrest, and arrest meant Hale, and Hale had read the book cover to cover and knew Juniper's name was in it. Hale would not need to interrogate him. Hale would simply open the prophecy to tomorrow's date and wait for the ink to prove itself.

*Cost:* if he succeeded, he would be handing Odile Marchetti the one document that could lead them all to the Thorn Door—which was, according to the book, the place where he died.

*Gain:* a guide. A road. A chance, however thin, however mad, of reaching the ending of his own story while it was still, in some small and stubborn way, his.

He set down the tea and looked at the walls. At the roads drawn in brown ink, forty years of a woman's feet translated into lines. At the little brass compass rose on the front door, hollow where its needle should be.

*Everything else can be bribed,* Odile had said. *Memory is the last honest instrument.*

His fear was still there—it would always be there, he understood that now, fear was simply the interest his life accrued—but beneath it, for the first time, something else had begun to accrue. Not courage exactly. Courage sounded like a thing you owned. This was smaller and more durable: a decision, made quietly, the way entries get made in ledgers, one line at a time, until the sum is unavoidable.

He would go to the Prophecy Office. He would use the credentials that had bound him like chains and walk through doors they were built to open. He would find the master atlas of a country the world had agreed to forget, and he would steal it, and he would pay whatever the stealing cost, because the alternative was to stand still and let the book finish its sentence with his name in it.

"Fear accepted as cost," he said aloud to the empty room, testing how it sounded.

From the back of the house, faintly, came the sound of Odile Marchetti laughing at something—or perhaps at nothing, or perhaps at him. With cartographers, Juniper was learning, it was difficult to tell the difference.

He drained the cold tea, squared his satchel on his shoulder, and went to find Wren, because tomorrow's date was coming whether he was ready for it or not—and the Prophecy Office opened its doors at dawn.
