## 9. The Prophecy Office

The Prophecy Office did not look like a place where nations went to die.

It looked like a customs house. That was the first obscenity of it—the plainness. A gray limestone façade on Candlewick Row, wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold mourning veils, with brass letters over the door reading *Bureau of Records Reconciliation* in a font chosen by someone who had wanted to be forgotten themselves. Juniper had walked past it a hundred times in his life without a second glance. Everyone in Corvane's Crossing had. That was rather the point.

He stood across the street now, in the shadow of the chandler's awning, and made himself look at it properly for the first time. The windows were tall and narrow and dark as well-bores. Smoke rose from a chimney at the back—ordinary smoke, hearth-colored—and somewhere inside, he knew, clerks were sharpening pencils and arguing about lunch, the way clerks did everywhere, while on their shelves sat the unmade graves of countries.

*You're stalling,* said the part of his mind that sounded like Master Corvane. *Stalling is a form of debt.*

He adjusted the satchel on his shoulder. Inside it: his guild credentials, stamped and countersigned; three blank requisition forms he had forged that morning with a steadiness that frightened him a little; a stub of graphite; and, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs where it could not be seen, the page he had torn from the prophecy book. His own name. Tomorrow's date. *Vesper.*

Wren had gone to ground with Odile Marchetti in the smuggler's warren under the tanneries, and before she'd left she had gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, "If you're not back by the last bell, I'm coming in after you, and I'll be loud about it."

"That's the opposite of what you should do," Juniper had told her.

"I know," she'd said, and smiled, and he had understood that she meant every word of it, and that this was somehow the most frightening thing that had happened to him all week.

Now the bells of Third Hour rang, and the clerks' entrance on the side alley opened to admit the afternoon shift, and Juniper Quintana, debt-bound journeyman scribe of the Guild of Impossible Things, crossed the street with his ledger held just so—slightly open, slightly worn, the posture of a man whose business was boring and official and absolutely none of yours.

* * *

The guard at the records door was a woman built like a filing cabinet, with a face that suggested she had personally read everything in the building and been disappointed by all of it.

"Business?"

"Cross-verification." Juniper let the guild accent come through, the flat vowels of a man who spent his days bent over other people's paperwork. "Guild of Impossible Things. We've got an item in inventory—a sealed codex, provenance disputed—and the Bureau holds the parent registry. Standard reconciliation. You know how it is."

He did not know if she knew how it was. He was gambling that no one ever asked her, and that the question itself would make her tired.

It did. Her eyes went briefly, profoundly vacant. "Sign here. Leave anything with an edge at the desk. Third floor, Reading Room B. If you touch the stacks without a red slip, you'll be escorted out and your guild will hear about it."

"I wouldn't dream of touching the stacks," Juniper said, which was true in the way that most useful lies are true: he intended to do far worse than touch them.

The knife at his ankle went into the tray. The oilcloth-wrapped page stayed against his ribs, because it was not a weapon, he told himself, it was evidence, and evidence was practically paperwork.

The stairs smelled of dust and lamp oil and something else underneath, faint but unmistakable once he noticed it: ink. Not fresh ink. Old ink, decades of it, centuries of it, soaked so deep into the building that the walls themselves had become a kind of palimpsest. He put his hand flat against the banister as he climbed and felt—this was impossible, he knew it was impossible, and he felt it anyway—a faint tremor, like a heartbeat heard through a door.

* * *

Reading Room B was exactly what it promised: green-shaded lamps, long tables, two elderly scholars squabbling in whispers over a genealogy. Juniper signed the day-book, took a seat, requested a volume of shipping manifests through the dumbwaiter, and spent forty minutes being genuinely bored by grain tariffs out of Harrowmouth while he mapped the room in his peripheral vision.

There was a door behind the reference desk marked **STACKS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL**. There was a clerk, a young man with ink-stained cuffs who kept falling asleep over his sorting. And there was a pneumatic tube system in the corner, brass and leather, with a schedule board beside it listing internal departments:

*Acquisition. Verification. Redaction.*

Redaction. Not storage, not archiving. *Redaction.* As though erasure were an ongoing process, a chore, something that required staffing and budget lines and someone down in the basement with a very steady hand.

Juniper waited for the scholars to begin a new argument, waited for the sleeping clerk's chin to drop another inch, and then walked—not hurriedly, never hurriedly—through the authorized door as though he had done it every day of his employed life.

* * *

The stacks went down.

He had expected up. Archives climbed; that was their nature. But the stairwell behind the door descended, flight after flight, the gas brackets giving way to cold electric globes, the air cooling and thickening until his breath fogged faintly. The shelves began simple enough—deeds, censuses, tax rolls, the sediment of ordinary governance—but by the fourth landing the labels changed character entirely.

**DISCONTINUED SURNAMES, 1802–1844.**

**UNANSWERED PRAYERS (WESTERN RITE) — PARTIAL HOLDINGS.**

**LANGUAGES WITH NO LIVING SPEAKERS: SEE CROSS-INDEX.**

He stopped at that one. The shelf behind the label ran thirty feet and was full—full—of ledgers bound in gray buckram, each spine lettered with a word he did not know and could not pronounce and felt, obscurely, that he ought to mourn. He pulled one at random. Inside, in columns of immaculate clerk-hand, was a grammar. Conjugations. A vocabulary of four thousand words, each one followed by a date and a single word in red: *last spoken,* and then a name, and then nothing. Page after page. An entire way of saying *I am cold, I love you, come home,* reduced to an entry, closed out like an account.

Juniper put it back with hands that were not quite steady.

He thought of Wren saying *Vesper* the way you'd spit out a seed. He thought of Odile Marchetti in her cell of a workshop, drawing roads from memory because the paper ones had been declared never to have existed. He had understood, intellectually, what the treaty had done. He understood it differently now. Erasure wasn't a single act. It was a department. It had a *schedule board.*

* * *

The fifth sublevel was different.

The air changed first—drier, colder, with the particular stillness of a place where nothing was permitted to decay. Then the light: the electric globes gave way to lamps burning behind frosted glass, and their glow was the color of old ivory. And then the shelves themselves, which were not wood but black iron, floor to unseen ceiling, arranged in radiating aisles like the spokes of some vast buried wheel.

Each aisle was labeled with a nation.

Not a region. Not a province. A *nation*—a word, a flag described in text, a span of dates. Juniper walked the central aisle slowly, reading, and with each label he felt something in his chest tighten another degree.

Some names he half-knew from ballads and grandmothers' curses: **KARST-MAREN**, **THE FREE MARCHES OF OLEV**, **SULTANATE OF THE TWO REEDS**. Some meant nothing at all, and that was worse, because he could see by the thickness of the ledgers that they had meant everything to someone, once, for a long time. Whole histories in gray buckram. Census rolls. Border surveys. Birth registers with tens of thousands of names, each name struck through in red—not crossed out carelessly, but *struck*, with a single clean line, the way a scribe strikes a paid debt.

At the end of the central aisle stood a lectern, and on the lectern lay a single volume, open, its pages blank except for a heading in fresh ink:

**VESPER — DISSOLUTION IN PROGRESS. DO NOT FILE.**

*In progress.* Not complete. The red strike-through had not yet fallen on Vesper's names. Somewhere in this iron wheel there were shelves—maybe a whole aisle—with Vesper written on them, waiting, patient as iron, for the last line to be drawn.

Juniper's hand hovered over the open volume. Every instinct he had as a scribe screamed at him not to touch it—an open register, unattended, was sacred in a way even he couldn't fully explain—but his fingers moved anyway, drifting above the page like a diviner's rod, and where they passed, he saw it: a faint ghosting beneath the blank surface, as though the paper remembered what had been written on it and was waiting to be told again. Half-formed letters. A coastline. A word that might have been *home* in a language he did not know and yet, when his eye caught it, his mouth almost shaped.

He snatched his hand back.

"You shouldn't do that," said a voice behind him, mild as milk. "Unfinished entries are sensitive. They take impressions."

* * *

Juniper turned with his heart hammering and his face already arranged into apologetic competence—the scribe's oldest armor.

The man at the end of the aisle was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, dressed not in clerk's black but in the dove-gray coat of an Auditor, with the small silver seal of the Bureau at his collar. He carried a lantern despite the electric lamps, an affectation or a habit, and he looked at Juniper with an expression of polite, bottomless attention, the way a deep lake looks at a dropped stone.

"I'm sorry," Juniper said. "Credentials—" He produced them before they were asked for, which was either the best or worst thing he could have done. "J. Quintana, Guild of Impossible Things, cross-verification, I must have taken a wrong turning on the stairs—"

"The stairs don't have wrong turnings." The Auditor came closer, unhurried. "That's rather the philosophy of the building." He glanced at the credentials, and something flickered across his face—not suspicion, exactly. Recognition of a kind. "Quintana. Scribe. How interesting."

"It's a common name."

"It is," the Auditor agreed pleasantly. "Though I confess I'm less interested in your name than in your handwriting. May I?" He did not wait for permission. He turned Juniper's right hand over, palm up, and examined the ink stains on the middle finger and the callus at the base of the thumb with the tenderness of a physician. "Iron-gall. Left-slant correction habit—you were trained by someone old-fashioned. And you press hard." He released the hand. "You press *very* hard, Mr. Quintana. Men who press hard are usually trying to make something permanent."

"I copy inventories," Juniper said. "Nothing I write needs to be permanent."

"No," the Auditor said softly. "Most things don't. That's rather our whole business." He stepped back and inclined his head, courteous as a host. "Auditor Hale. I'm afraid I'll have to ask you to finish your verification another day—the lower stacks are closed for reconciliation this evening. But do give my regards to Master Corvane. Tell him the Bureau remains grateful for the Guild's... discretion, over the years."

Every hair on Juniper's arms stood up. *Corvane.* The name dropped into the conversation like a coin into a well, and Hale watched it fall, watching Juniper watch it fall.

"Of course," Juniper heard himself say. "Good evening, Auditor."

"Good evening, Mr. Quintana." Hale raised his lantern a fraction, a small salute. "Do be careful on the stairs. And do come back tomorrow, if you like. I have a feeling—" the mildest of smiles— "that we'll be seeing a great deal more of each other."

Juniper climbed. Four flights, five, his lungs burning, the ghost-word still shaping itself silently in his mouth. Only at the top, signing out under the sleepy clerk's indifferent gaze, reclaiming his knife from the tray, did he allow himself to understand what his body had known since the fifth sublevel:

Hale hadn't stopped him. Hale could have summoned six guards with one word. Hale had *let him go*—had sent him home with a message for Corvane, like a note passed between partners.

And tomorrow's date was circled in his own torn page, pressed warm against his ribs, and tomorrow, the Auditor had said, they would be seeing a great deal more of each other.
