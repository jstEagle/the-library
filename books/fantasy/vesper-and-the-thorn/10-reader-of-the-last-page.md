## 10. Reader of the Last Page

The shelves of the Prophecy Office did not creak. That was the first thing Juniper noticed, and it unsettled him more than anything else he had seen that night. Every archive he had ever known—the Guild's own cramped repository of bottled wonders and misfiled miracles, the public records hall where debt-bonds were stamped and stamped again—had a voice. Wood settled. Paper sighed. Dust ticked down through slants of lamplight like slow snow. But here the silence was maintained, deliberate, the way a held breath is deliberate. Thousands of volumes stood in ranks along walls that rose beyond the reach of the lamps, and not one of them made a sound.

He moved between them with his scribe's credentials warm in his breast pocket—a brass token stamped with the Guild's impossible sigil, a door opening onto nothing—and told himself, for the eleventh time since he had slipped past the night clerk, that he belonged here. Scribes were permitted in archives. Archives were where scribes went. The logic was sound. The logic was a rope bridge over a gorge, and every step he took tested another strand.

The shelves were labeled by nation.

Not by subject, as he had expected. Not by century, or by school of prophecy, or by any of the orderly principles the Guild used to tame its chaos. By *nation*. He read the gilt letters as he passed, and each one landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well: **AURELLE**. **THE KESSERIN MARCHES**. **SOLVANE-BY-THE-SEA**. He knew none of them. That was the horror of it—he knew none of them, and yet there they were, whole countries reduced to shelf-space, their names preserved only because someone had decided to keep the record of forgetting.

He stopped at a shelf whose label read **VESPER**, and his mouth went dry.

It was not empty. He had half-expected it to be—Vesper was unwritten, erased, a word that maps refused—but the shelf held perhaps forty volumes, bound in grey leather gone soft at the corners, their spines unlettered. He drew one out with fingers that trembled more than he wanted them to and opened it to a random page.

*The bells of Vesper rang on the morning of the treaty,* said the faded ink, *and no one outside the borders heard them, because by then the borders had been taught to lie.*

Juniper closed the book gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead, and slid it back.

That was when he heard the footsteps.

They came from the deep end of the stacks, unhurried, measured, the tread of a man who had never once needed to hurry in his life. Juniper's hand flew to his pocket—to the credentials, absurdly, as if brass could stop bullets—and he pressed himself against the shelf beside Vesper's grey books, suddenly very interested in the label above them: **THE FREE HOLDINGS OF TARN**, a country he had never heard of, which had apparently also never existed.

The footsteps stopped.

"You're standing in my aisle," said a voice. It was dry and precise, a voice like a ruled line. "I don't mind. It's a good aisle. But you should know that I notice things, and I have already noticed three things about you."

Juniper turned.

The man at the end of the aisle was tall and narrow as a filing cabinet, dressed in the dove-grey uniform of the Office's civil service, with a chain of office that held no medallion—only a small blank ledger, closed with a clasp, hanging at his chest like a heart. His hair was iron-colored and cropped close. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they did not move the way eyes were supposed to move; they went from point to point without traveling between, arriving rather than looking.

"Auditor Hale," Juniper said, before he could stop himself. The name came out of him the way a sneeze comes out—unbidden, involuntary, drawn from some part of him that had been paying attention even while the rest of him panicked. He had seen the man's portrait once, years ago, on a recruitment broadside: *Serve the Record. The Record serves everyone.*

"You know me." Hale sounded pleased, in the way a scale is pleased when it balances. "That's the first thing. The second is that your credentials are genuine but your authorization is not—you are a guild scribe, junior grade, debt-bound, and no guild scribe of junior grade has clearance for the national shelves after dark. And the third—" Hale took a single step closer, and the lamplight caught the blank ledger on his chain and made it gleam, "—is that you have been standing next to the Vesper collection for four minutes and eleven seconds, and in all that time you have not once looked away from it. Most people can't bear to look at it at all."

"I was just—"

"Shelving," Hale suggested. "Returning a volume. Browsing." He shook his head slowly, a teacher disappointed in a promising student. "You may try any of those. I will listen politely. But I would prefer, if it costs you nothing, the truth. I find the truth is cheaper in the long run. Lies accrue interest."

*So do debts*, Juniper thought, and thought of Corvane, and thought of the prophecy book hidden beneath the false bottom of his satchel back at the Guild—the sealed book, the century-old book, the book that had grown fresh entries describing tonight, describing tomorrow, describing *him*. His heart was going so hard he was sure the Auditor could hear it, count it, enter it into some ledger of vital statistics.

"I'm looking for something," Juniper said. It was true, and truth, as Hale had said, was cheap. "A record. A verification."

"Of what?"

"A date."

Hale's colorless eyes arrived at Juniper's face and stayed there. Something shifted in the aisle—not physically, nothing so crude, but the quality of the air changed, the way it changes when a conversation stops being a conversation and becomes an examination. "A date," Hale repeated. "Dates are my specialty. Do you know what I do, scribe? Everyone thinks the Office reads the future. We do not. Anyone can read the future; the future is generous with its drafts. What we do is *audit* it. When a prophecy is fulfilled—or fails—we reconcile the account. We determine what was promised, what was paid, and what remains outstanding. And when a nation is unwritten—" he gestured, one long finger, at the shelves stretching away into the dark, at Aurelle, at Solvane, at all the silent countries, "—we audit that too. We make certain nothing was left over. No stray map. No surviving heir. No *memory* that might compound, like unpaid interest, into restoration."

"And Vesper?" The question escaped Juniper like a bird from a closing hand. "Was anything left over?"

For a moment—just a moment—Hale said nothing, and in that nothing Juniper heard the entire weight of the archive pressing down, ten thousand silenced nations holding their breath.

"That," Hale said quietly, "is the most interesting question anyone has asked me in eleven years. And it brings me to the reason I am pleased you broke into my archive tonight, although I should warn you that *pleased* is not the same as *kind*." He reached up, unclasped the little blank ledger from his chain, and held it in both hands with the reverence of a priest handling relics. "Three weeks ago, a volume surfaced in the private collections. Sealed. Century-old binding, century-old wax, catalogued and forgotten—which is how the dangerous ones always survive. Standard procedure required an audit before disposition. So I read it."

"You read it," Juniper repeated. His voice had gone thin.

"Cover to cover." Hale opened the blank ledger, and Juniper saw—with a lurch that turned his stomach to cold water—that the pages were not blank at all. They were filled with Hale's own narrow, ruled handwriting, dense as scripture, annotated and cross-referenced in three colors of ink. "Do you know what most prophecies are, scribe? Weather. Fog. A hundred vague promises that can be bent to fit any outcome, so that the credulous always believe and the auditors always yawn. But this one—" Hale ran a finger down a page, and Juniper craned despite himself, catching fragments: *the thorn*, *the door*, *the last memory*, *the scribe*— "this one has dates. Precise ones. Names. Proper nouns, scribe. Do you understand what a proper noun does to an auditor's heart? It gives us something to *check*."

"The entries are still appearing," Juniper said. He hadn't meant to say it. It fell out of him like a coin from a torn pocket, and once it was out he couldn't pick it up again.

Something flickered across Hale's face—not surprise. Confirmation. The look of a man whose arithmetic has finally summed.

"Yes," Hale said softly. "They are. Which means the prophecy is not merely old—it is *active*. It is being written even now, entry by entry, and every entry has come true, and every entry that hasn't come true yet is scheduled." He closed the ledger. The clasp clicked like a lock engaging. "And on the page dated for tomorrow night, there is a name. A proper noun. The name of a debt-bound guild scribe, junior grade, who has spent the last four minutes and eleven seconds staring at the Vesper collection as though it owed him money."

The silence that followed was the longest of Juniper's life. Somewhere far off in the archive, a clock he could not see struck the quarter hour, and each stroke seemed to fall on him personally.

"You've read it," Juniper managed. "All of it. You know how it ends."

"I know exactly how it ends." Hale's voice did not change, and that was the terrible thing—there was no triumph in it, no menace, only the flat serenity of a man reading figures off a page. "It ends at a door made of thorn, at the border of a country that no longer exists, with a death that purchases a forgetting. It ends with everything balanced. And I will tell you something else, scribe, because you have earned one truth for your trespass: I have read ten thousand prophecies, and this is the first one I have ever *hoped* would fail its audit."

Juniper blinked. "You—what?"

"Erasure is tidy," Hale said. "Tidiness is my profession, and I am very good at it, and I will tell you what thirty years of tidiness has taught me." He stepped closer, and now, finally, his eyes did something human: they searched. "Every unwritten nation leaves a remainder. A widow's second shadow. A song in the wrong language. A compass that points at places that don't exist—I know about the compass, scribe; the smuggler who carries it has been on three watch-lists since spring. Remainders accumulate. And a remainder the size of an entire country, left to compound for a hundred years—" he tapped the ledger against his palm, once, twice, "—that is not a rounding error. That is a reckoning."

"So let it be reckoned," Juniper said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt, hear it take on something of Wren's defiance, Odile's stubbornness. "If the prophecy says a scribe dies at the Thorn Door—if your precious book has already scheduled it—then why are you telling me? Why not simply let the account settle?"

"Because," said Auditor Hale, "an audit requires verification. And the final entry—the one dated for tomorrow—has not yet been written. There is still ink in the well, so to speak. Still time for the figures to change." He looked at Juniper for a long moment, and then he did something that frightened Juniper more than any threat could have: he extended the ledger toward him, open, offering. "Run, scribe. That is my professional advice, given against my professional interest. Run tonight, run fast, run toward that door or away from it, I no longer care which. Because if you are standing here at dawn, I am obligated to detain you—and if you are detained, the prophecy cannot complete, and if the prophecy cannot complete, the remainder stays outstanding, and I will spend the rest of my career chasing a country that refuses to die." His colorless eyes glinted. "Whereas if you run—if you go to that door and do what the book says—then by this time tomorrow, my ledger balances, Vesper is forgotten forever, and everyone goes home."

"And which do *you* want?" Juniper whispered.

Hale closed the ledger. Clasped it back upon his chain, over his heart, where a medallion should have been.

"I want," he said, "to stop wondering. Now go. You have perhaps ninety seconds before the night shift completes its rounds of this wing, and I am no longer able to pretend I haven't seen you."

Juniper ran.

He ran the way he had never run in his life—past Aurelle, past the Kesserin Marches, past all the shelved and silenced dead, his credentials burning in his pocket like a coal, Hale's words ringing in his skull louder than the unseen clock: *run toward that door or away from it.* Behind him he heard—distinctly, deliberately—the Auditor's voice raised for the benefit of approaching boots: "Wing's clear! Nothing stirring but dust!"

Nothing stirring but dust. Juniper burst through the archive's side exit into the cold breath of the night, into the alley behind the Office where the fog lay thick as wool, and he did not stop running until he reached the corner where Wren had said she'd wait, under the broken lamp, with the impossible compass in her fist and Odile's shadow sharp beside her.

They saw his face and knew.

"They know," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"Hale knows everything," Juniper gasped, bending double, his lungs full of knives. "The book. My name. Tomorrow. He's read it cover to cover—and Wren—" He looked up at her, and the fog beaded on his lashes, and somewhere across the city a bell began to ring the hour, though he could not have said which bell, or whether it belonged to any country at all. "He told me to run. He *wanted* me to run. And I think—I think whatever happens at that door, we have less than a day to get there first."

Wren's hand closed around his wrist, hard, warm, certain. "Then we're done walking," she said.

Down the fog-drowned street, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots—many boots, moving quickly, fanning out through the city in patterns only auditors and hunters could read.
