## 13. Songs in the Wrong Language

The elder's name was Sable Ferrow, and she was the only person in Grayhollow who could still sing the whole of the Lament for the Salt Coast without stopping.

Juniper learned this the way he learned most things in Grayhollow: sideways, by accident, in the gaps between what people said and what they meant. The exiles of that unmapped village did not announce their treasures. They set them out like chipped crockery on a table, ordinary as bread, and waited to see who would notice.

He noticed at dusk on their second day, when the whole town gathered in the square before the well — a square that appeared on no map because no map had ever been permitted to hold it — and an old woman with hands like folded paper climbed onto the mounting block and began to sing.

The language was wrong. That was his first thought, and it shamed him even as it formed, because there was nothing wrong with it at all. It was simply a language he had never heard, full of vowels that opened like doors into dark rooms, consonants that clicked softly at their edges like shutters closing. And yet some traitor part of his mind kept insisting: *wrong*. As if he had once known the right version and was hearing it mangled. As if the song were a room he'd lived in, refurnished badly by strangers.

He stood at the edge of the crowd with Wren's shoulder against his and felt the hair rise along his arms.

"What is it?" Wren murmured. She had gone very still, the way she went still when she smelled trouble or money, though her eyes were not on the singer but on the faces around them — faces lit gold by lamplight, wet-eyed, mouths moving along with words they all knew.

"It's Vesperan," said Odile Marchetti from his other side. Her voice was rough. "The Lament for the Salt Coast. They used to sing it every year on the last night of harvest. I heard it once, when I was nine, from a woman my father bought salt from." The cartographer's jaw tightened. "I've spent forty years trying to remember how it went. She has all of it."

"She's teaching," Juniper said.

And she was. After the song ended — after the silence that followed it, which was less silence than held breath — Sable Ferrow looked out over her people and said something short and practical in that impossible language, and then repeated it in Common: "Again. From the third verse. The children are losing the middle sounds."

* * *

They were losing the middle sounds. That was the truth of Grayhollow, the truth under its stubborn warmth, and everyone in the square knew it and no one said it plainly. A country erased from maps does not die all at once. It dies the way a fire dies in a wet woodpile: at the edges first, then inward, verse by verse, word by word, until one day the youngest generation sings the Lament with holes in it where the language used to be, and hums where their grandparents wept.

Vesperan had no country to live in anymore. It lived only in throats, and throats fail.

"They won't let us teach it in any school," a man named Aldous Pell told them later, in the taproom of the inn that wasn't called anything because naming things drew attention. He was a broad, gray-templed man with a cooper's forearms and a scholar's careful way of speaking. "There are laws. There have been laws since the Treaty of Ashes. You can't write it down — writing makes evidence, and evidence makes arrests. You can't print it. You can't correspond in it. Forty years ago you could be fined for it. Twenty years ago, imprisoned." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Now they mostly don't bother. Why arrest a man for speaking a dying tongue? It's cheaper to wait."

"So they let it starve," Wren said.

"They let it starve," Pell agreed. "Elegantly. With great sympathy. There are historians in the capital who will give lectures about the tragedy of Vesperan while it takes its last breath, and they will mean every sorrowful word, and they will lift not one finger." He smiled without any humor in it. "Erasure works best when the erasers can weep."

Juniper sat with his hands wrapped around a cup of small beer he wasn't drinking and listened to the taproom talk flow around him — half in Common, half in Vesperan, sliding between languages the way water slides between stones. And all evening, that feeling would not leave him. That feeling of standing in a refurnished room.

It got worse when Sable Ferrow began the lesson.

She taught the way, he suspected, teachers had always taught in Vesper: without books, without notes, everything carried in the body. She made the children — and the adults, and to his horror eventually the three visitors — repeat phrases after her, call and response, the old rhythm of oral instruction.

"Say it back," she commanded, and gave them a line:

*"Nal vetha sorren, nal vetha kai."*

The children chorused it. Odile stumbled through it, her accent forty years rusted but her memory evidently sounder than she claimed. Wren butchered it cheerfully, treating the vowels like obstacles to be run over.

And Juniper — Juniper opened his mouth and said it correctly.

Not approximately. Not with a traveler's fumbling approximation. Correctly, with the soft click at the back of *vetha*, with the falling tone on *sorren* that he somehow knew was falling, with the vowel in *kai* held a fraction longer than comfort allowed, exactly as long as it wanted to be held.

Sable Ferrow stopped.

The whole square stopped with her. Thirty faces turned toward him in the lamplight, and Juniper felt his ears go hot and his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"Where did you learn that?" the old woman asked. Her voice was not unkind. It was worse than unkind. It was careful, the way one is careful with a wound or a miracle.

"I didn't," Juniper said. "I've never — I don't speak Vesperan. I've never heard it before tonight."

"Say the next line."

"I don't know the next line."

"Say it anyway."

And the terrible thing — the thing that made the lamplight seem suddenly too bright and the crowd too close — was that he could. Not confidently, not the way he could recite a guild tariff schedule, but the way one remembers a dream: reaching, doubting, and then finding the shape of it already there under his hand.

*"Vessa torum, vessa ilen—"*

He stopped himself, appalled. "*Vessa*? Is that — does that mean—"

"Home," said Sable Ferrow quietly. "Or close enough. *Vessa*, the hearth-country. What we called ourselves before the treaty called us nothing." She came down off the mounting block slowly, joints cracking like green kindling, and walked toward him across the packed earth, and the crowd parted for her the way water parts for a keel. "Boy. What is your name?"

"Juniper Quintana."

"Your mother's name?"

"Maren. Maren Quintana. She was a laundress in Coriander's Cross. She died when I was six. She spoke Common, only Common, I'd swear to it—"

"Her mother's name?"

He blinked. "I — I never knew. She didn't talk about her family. I always thought..." He had always thought it was grief. Now, standing in a square full of exiles in a village that existed on no map, another explanation slid coldly into place beside the first. "I always thought she didn't want to remember."

Sable Ferrow took his face in her paper-dry hands and turned it up to the lamplight, studying him the way Odile studied coastlines. Whatever she saw there made her exhale through her nose, long and slow.

"You have your grandmother's bones," she said. "That jaw. That brow. There were Quintanas in the salt villages, east of the Thornwood. Good singing families." She released him. "When the unwriting came, some fled north and changed their names and buried the language so deep their own children never heard it. But a language doesn't stay buried, boy. It goes into the body. Into the way you hold your mouth. Your mother sang to you, I'd wager. Lullabies she claimed she invented."

Juniper's throat worked. "There was one. About a boat. A little boat going out past the harbor lights. She said she made it up."

The old woman laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound — and several of the exiles laughed with her, and none of them laughed cruelly.

"*Il vessa noru, il vessa dai,*" she sang, two lines, low and sweet, and the bottom fell out of Juniper's entire life.

Because he knew it. He knew the next two lines. He had known them since before he could remember knowing them; they lived in him the way his heartbeat lived in him, wordless and constant, a melody he had hummed all his life without ever once asking himself where it came from or what it meant. His mother had sung it and told him she'd made it up, and he had believed her, because why wouldn't he, because she was his mother and mothers do not lie about lullabies —

Except mothers do lie about lullabies. Mothers bury languages in their own bodies and pass them to their children disguised as inventions, because a lullaby is the one song no law can reach.

"The little boat goes out," Sable Ferrow translated, her voice gentle now, almost tender. "The little boat goes out past the lights, and the water holds it, and the water carries it home. It's a cradle song. Every Vesperan child had it sung to them. We thought it was lost — the full version. The verses after the second." Her eyes glittered. "You're carrying the full version, boy. All eight verses. I can see it in your face. You know there are more."

"There are more," Juniper whispered. His voice sounded far away to him, as if someone else were speaking through his mouth. "I just... never needed them. She died before — I only ever needed the first two."

Around him the square had gone utterly silent. Even the children had stopped fidgeting. Wren's hand found his arm — not gripping, just resting there, warm through his sleeve, and he was distantly grateful for it, because the ground felt unreliable.

"This is impossible," Odile said hoarsely. "Forty years. We've been searching forty years for anyone who remembered the whole cradle song. The Prophecy Office burned the last written copy in the purges. I've interviewed hundreds of exiles, and the best of them had fragments, three verses, four—"

"And here stands a guild scribe from Coriander's Cross," said Aldous Pell softly, "with all eight verses in his mouth and no idea how they got there." He looked at Juniper with an expression Juniper couldn't name — awe braided with something hungrier. "The country isn't only in our memories, friend. Sometimes it writes itself into people who never asked for it. Sometimes it hides in the blood and waits."

* * *

Later — much later, when the square had emptied and the lamps had been snuffed one by one and the strange stars wheeled overhead in constellations Odile kept muttering corrections to — Juniper sat on the steps of the inn with the prophecy book open on his knees.

He hadn't meant to take it out. He had sworn, after Master Corvane's warning, after the Auditor's shadow had fallen across his life in the archive, that he would not touch it again. But his hands had gone looking for it on their own, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

The book lay open to the page with his name. *Juniper Quintana, scribe of the Guild of Impossible Things.* The date. And beneath both, in that same patient century-old ink: *Vesper.*

He had read it a hundred times since the archive. He had told himself it was coincidence, forgery, guild trickery, madness. He had built a whole architecture of denial and lived in it comfortably for days.

But now he sat in a village of exiles, having sung eight verses of a dead language he had never learned, and the architecture groaned.

*The country writes itself into people,* Pell had said. *Sometimes it hides in the blood and waits.*

Waits for what?

He turned pages with a scribe's care, though his fingers were not steady. The prophecy book was organized strangely — not chronologically, not alphabetically, but the way a ledger is organized, which is to say the way debt is organized: cause leading to payment, payment leading to consequence. Entries about the Treaty of Ashes. Entries about the unwriting, described not as politics but as *arithmetic* — balances struck, sums erased, margins cleared. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, a phrase in Vesperan that he now, horribly, could read:

*Nal vetha sorren, nal vetha kai.*

One country forgotten, one country kept.

He stared at it. The lamplight from the inn window fell yellow across the page. Somewhere behind him, faint through the shuttered window, someone in the taproom was humming the cradle song — the little boat going out past the lights — and the melody wound through the night air and settled over him like a coat.

His mother had been Vesperan. Or the granddaughter of Vesperans. She had fled or been fled, changed or been changed, and she had carried a whole country inside her mouth and called it invention, and she had died with it, and passed it to him anyway, unbeknownst, in the dark, in the sweetest possible disguise.

Which meant the prophecy book had not chosen him at random.

Which meant that when it wrote his name beside the death of Vesper, it was not pairing a stranger with a stranger. It was pairing a country with the last unknowing vessel of its own tongue. A key with its lock. An ending with the one man alive who could sing it.

"No," Juniper said aloud, to the book, to the night, to whatever patient intelligence had inked those entries a hundred years ago. "No. I'm not your instrument. I'm a scribe. I know what ink is for. Ink serves whoever holds the pen."

The book, being a book, said nothing. But the wind moved through the square, stirring the cold ashes of the evening's fire, and for one disorienting moment the sound it made through the eaves of Grayhollow was not wind at all but a voice — many voices, layered, distant, singing in the falling tones of a language that had no country left except, perhaps, him.

*Vessa torum, vessa ilen.*

Home, the hearth-country. Waiting.

He closed the book and held it against his chest and sat alone on the steps until the singing behind the window faded and the stars turned, and he did not sleep at all.

Near dawn, Wren found him there. She sat down without asking, handed him a cup of tea gone lukewarm, and said nothing for a long time. That was one of the things he was learning to love about her: she knew when silence was the correct entry in the ledger.

Finally she said, "Odile wants to leave within the hour. Says we've stayed too long already. Says Hale's hounds aren't stupid and Grayhollow's secret won't keep." She paused. "But the exiles want to talk to you first. All of them. Sable Ferrow's been up all night with the elders." Wren turned and looked at him, and in the gray pre-dawn light her face was unreadable and very serious. "They say you might be able to teach them the verses they've lost. The middle sounds. The endings. They say the country sent you."

"The country didn't send me," Juniper said. His voice was raw from sleeplessness. "Nobody sends anybody. That's what Corvane never understood, and what Hale refuses to understand. People choose. That's the whole of it."

"Then choose fast," Wren said, "because there's smoke on the south road, and Odile says it isn't chimney smoke."

Juniper stood. The prophecy book was heavy in his satchel, heavier than paper had any right to be, and somewhere inside it his name waited beside a date that was coming toward him at the speed of all dates. But when he looked south, past the rooftops of the village that did not exist, at the thin gray thread rising against the lightening sky, what he felt was not dread.

It was recognition. Cold and complete and terrible.

He knew that road. He had never seen it in his life, and he knew it — the bend of it, the thorn hedge along its eastern verge, the way it ran toward the borderlands like a sentence running toward its final word.

"Nal vetha sorren," he said quietly, testing the words in the open air, giving them back to the morning. One country forgotten.

He picked up his satchel and went to wake the others.
