## 15. The Debt Comes Due

Wren had stolen things from kings, from temples, from a cardinal who kept his sins in a locked reliquary shaped like a dove. She had lifted a crown off a sleeping man's head in Marrowgate and walked out through two rings of guards by pretending to be the woman who brought the guards their supper. None of it had frightened her the way Grayhollow's quiet did.

The village sat in a fold of hills that no map admitted to, and its people moved through their days like they were afraid of waking something. They remembered Vesper so completely that remembering had become a kind of labor — an old woman reciting street names to her bread oven each morning, a boy who swept a doorstep that led nowhere because his grandmother said the house it belonged to would want sweeping if it ever came back. Wren watched them and felt something she refused to name. It was not homesickness. She had left Vesper when she was six years old; you could not be sick for a home you could not recall. But it was adjacent to homesickness. It lived in the same tenement of the heart, one floor down.

She was sharpening her knives on the inn's back step when the riders came.

She heard them before she saw them — four horses moving with the particular discipline of animals trained not to spook at anything, which meant their riders expected trouble. Wren set down the whetstone without hurry and stood, rolling her shoulders, letting her coat fall open so the knives were visible but not threatening. A language she'd spoken fluently since childhood: *I am armed, I am calm, we can still avoid this.*

They came up the cart road in gray livery, unmarked but for a small brass badge shaped like an open ledger. Guild collectors. Wren's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

There were three kinds of trouble in the world, as far as she'd ever catalogued it: trouble you could fight, trouble you could flee, and trouble that wore paperwork. The third kind was the worst. You couldn't cut paper with a knife. Well — you could, but it grew back somewhere worse, in triplicate.

"Looking for a scribe," said the lead collector, dismounting. He had the face of a man who had been told once, long ago, that he looked trustworthy, and had spent every day since proving the compliment wrong. "Juniper Quintana. Debtor of record, Guild of Impossible Things."

"He's not here."

"I didn't ask where he is." The collector smiled with all the warmth of a bill of sale. "I asked for him. And here is where he sleeps." He turned to the innkeeper's daughter, who had frozen in the doorway with a basket of eggs, and produced a document with a flourish that suggested he enjoyed this part. "We have a writ of seizure. His bond papers are held against outstanding debt. We're taking possession."

"Of his papers."

"Of him."

The word landed in the yard like a stone into a well. Wren heard the splash of it go all the way down.

* * *

They found Juniper at the elder Sable's house, where he'd spent three evenings now learning Vesperan script from a woman who wrote letters on slate because paper was too precious to waste on practice. He came out when Wren whistled — their signal, low and rising, meaning *company, bad kind* — and she watched his face do the arithmetic before anyone said a word. He was quick like that. He'd been poor his whole life; poor people learned to read trouble faster than rich people read books.

"They've come for my bond," he said. Not a question.

"They've come for *you*. Papers first. The rest is procedure." Wren kept her voice flat, which took effort, because what she actually felt was a heat climbing her throat that had nothing to do with anger and everything to do with the specific fury of watching someone you — someone you *needed*, she corrected herself, someone whose hands knew how to read dead languages and whose spine hadn't yet learned to bend — being fitted for a collar. "Corvane sent four riders. Four. That's not collection, Juniper. That's retrieval. That's a man who thinks his property has wandered off."

"I'm not property."

"No," Wren agreed. "You're collateral. It's worse. Property gets fed."

He flinched. She wished immediately that she could take the words back, or at least file the edges off them, but Wren had never in her life developed the talent for softening a sentence after it left her mouth. Her mother — no. Not her mother. There was a blank there, a smooth white space where a face should have been, and it frightened her more than the riders did, some mornings, how much of herself had gone missing without her noticing. She pushed past it. There would be time to grieve things she couldn't name later, assuming there was a later.

The collectors had made camp in the village square rather than the inn, which was a message in itself: *we do not need your hospitality, we need only your compliance.* They had built a small fire and were heating wax. Sealing wax. Wren watched from the shadow of the well as the lead collector — his name, according to the gossip already circulating through Grayhollow at remarkable speed, was Brann — laid out documents on a folding table with the reverence of a priest arranging relics.

The guild-bond papers. Juniper's whole life, reduced to vellum and seals: the debt inherited from a father who had borrowed against a son not yet born, the interest compounded across twenty years, the clauses and sub-clauses that bound him to the Guild of Impossible Things until the ledger balanced or the debtor died, whichever proved cheaper.

"You're staring," said Odile Marchetti, materializing at Wren's elbow with the uncanny silence of a woman who had spent decades making herself invisible to institutions.

"I'm planning."

"Mm. And does your plan involve the part where there are four of them and they're legally untouchable inside any chartered city's reach?"

"My plans usually involve that part."

Odile snorted. Up close, in the firelight, the old cartographer looked like a map of herself — every line earned, every crease marking somewhere she'd been and paid to leave. "Then let me save you the ink. You can't steal those papers, girl. I know the type. I *was* the type, forty years ago, before the Prophecy Office taught me what confiscation really means. You steal a bond, they void it and reissue it with harsher terms. You burn it, they declare the debtor in default and take him bodily. The paper isn't the chain. The paper is just the receipt for the chain."

"Then what's the chain?"

"Arithmetic." Odile's voice went quiet and bitter. "Corvane doesn't own Juniper's body. He owns the *difference* between what Juniper owes and what Juniper is worth. As long as that difference exists, Corvane owns him. Change the sum, and the chain falls off on its own."

Wren stared at the fire, at the wax beginning to soften, at Brann dipping a seal into it with the satisfaction of a man stamping his initials into another person's life. "And how does a smuggler change a sum?"

"She doesn't." Odile tapped her temple. "But she knows a scribe who's spent his whole life learning exactly how much a thing costs, and exactly how debts move, and exactly where the ledger keeps its soft places. The question is whether anyone's bothered to tell him yet."

* * *

They told him in Sable's kitchen, over tea nobody drank.

Juniper listened the way he did everything — completely, silently, his fingers resting on the table as though it were a page he was reading. When Wren finished, he was quiet for a long moment. Then he asked the question she'd been dreading.

"How much do I owe? Exactly. To the copper."

"Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve marks, nine coppers," said Odile, who had apparently memorized the figure out of professional respect. "As of last quarter. It accrues daily."

"And my assessed worth to the guild? My cataloguing, my transcription, my — everything they've had me doing since I was nine years old?"

"Assessed at eleven marks a year against the principal. Less room and board."

Wren did the math and watched Juniper do it faster. Eleven marks a year against a debt growing faster than that. It wasn't a debt. It was a life sentence wearing accounting's clothes. No one could ever pay it. It was designed so no one could ever pay it. The Guild of Impossible Things didn't collect debts; it collected *people*, and dressed the collecting in columns and sums so everyone involved could pretend it was commerce instead of slavery with better stationery.

"That's why Corvane wants me back," Juniper said softly. "It's not the prophecy book. It's not even the compass. It's that I ran, and a debtor who runs devalues the whole principle. If I get away with it, other debtors might—" He stopped. He looked at his own hands, the ink-stained fingers that had catalogued bottled thunderstorms and widows' second shadows and a thousand other impossible things, and Wren saw him arrive at the truth the way you arrive at a wall in the dark: painfully, and all at once. "I'm not a person to them. I'm a precedent."

"Now you see it," Odile said.

"I've always seen it. I just called it something gentler." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "My father signed me away before I was born. Did you know that? I found the original contract when I was fourteen, in the deep files. His mark, not even a signature — he couldn't write. An X, witnessed by two men whose names I've never been able to find. I used to think if I could just work hard enough, balance far enough, the X would stop mattering." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and clear. "The X never stops mattering. That's the whole design."

Wren's chest hurt. She crossed her arms over it, an old habit, armoring the soft places. "So we run further. Salt Fields are behind us, but there's country east—"

"And they follow, and every city honors the writ, and eventually they corner us somewhere with fewer friends than Grayhollow." Juniper shook his head slowly. "No. Odile's right. You don't outrun arithmetic. You amend it."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning I walk into that square tomorrow morning, look Master Corvane's dogs in the eye, and negotiate." He said it simply, the way he catalogued impossible things, as though writing the words down made them manageable. "A bond is a contract, and contracts have terms, and terms can be restructured if both parties consent. So one party will consent. Vigorously."

"You can't trust them—"

"I don't have to trust them. I have to *read* them." For the first time all evening, something like his old dry smile surfaced. "Wren. I have spent twenty years reading contracts written by people who assumed no one would ever read them closely. Every loophole Corvane's lawyers ever built, I've catalogued a cousin of it. Let me do the one thing I'm actually good at."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then you steal me," he said. "Deal?"

It was supposed to be a joke. She could tell by the tilt of his eyebrow, the little shrug. But Wren heard it land somewhere serious inside her, in that white space where her missing memories lived, and she heard herself answer before she'd decided to.

"Deal. But I set the terms of the stealing. And Juniper — if they put a hand on you, the negotiation's over. I don't care about arithmetic. I care about you."

The words hung in the kitchen air, larger than she'd intended, truer than she'd planned. Juniper looked at her for a long moment, and whatever passed between them needed no seal and no wax and no witness, which was more than either of them could say for anything else in their lives.

"All right," he said quietly. "Tomorrow, then."

* * *

Tomorrow came gray and early, and it brought company.

Wren was awake before dawn — smuggler's habit, the body learning to treat sleep as a country you visit but never live in — and so she was on the inn's roof with a view of the cart road when the second party arrived. Not riders this time. A single carriage, black, unmarked, drawn by two horses that moved like they were ashamed of their work. And standing on the carriage's rear step, holding the rail with one gloved hand, a figure Wren recognized from a hundred whispered warnings in a hundred smuggling dens.

Auditor Hale.

Beside her, Odile drew a sharp breath. "That's not possible. Hale doesn't leave the Office. Hale *is* the Office."

"Well," Wren said, watching the carriage roll toward the square where Brann's fires still smoldered, "it seems the Prophecy Office and the Guild of Impossible Things have finally compared notes. Two institutions that erase things for a living. I wonder what took them so long."

Below, the collectors were stirring. Brann emerged from his tent, saw the carriage, and — Wren noted with professional interest — went pale. Whatever hierarchy existed between guild collectors and Prophecy auditors, it apparently flowed sharply downhill toward the brass badges.

They woke Juniper. He came to the window with his hair uncombed and his eyes already fully awake, because Juniper's eyes had been fully awake for weeks now, ever since he'd found his own name in a book that should have been sealed for a century. He looked at the carriage. He looked at Hale. Wren watched him understand, in real time, that the trap had never been four riders and a writ of seizure. The riders were the net. The carriage was the hand drawing it closed.

"They're not here for the debt," he said.

"No," Wren agreed. "The debt's just the handle. They're going to pick you up by it."

Down in the square, Brann was bowing to the carriage. The door opened. Auditor Hale stepped down into the gray light of Grayhollow — a tall, dry, precise figure, dressed in the Prophecy Office's charcoal gray, carrying under one arm a book that Wren would have known anywhere, having seen Juniper clutch it in his sleep like a talisman and wake from dreams about it sweating.

The century-old prophecy book. Sealed. Stolen back, or never lost at all.

Hale stood in the square of a village that did not exist on any map, in a country that officially did not exist at all, and raised one hand — and every collector in the yard straightened like puppets whose strings had been found again.

"Debtor Quintana," Hale called, in a voice like a closing ledger, pitched to carry to every window in the village. "The Guild of Impossible Things has transferred your bond to the Prophecy Office, effective this hour. Your debt is no longer owed to Master Corvane." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "It is owed to history. Come down and settle it — or we will come up, and we will settle it for you. And we will begin with everyone who taught you to sing."

In the window beside him, Wren felt Juniper go very still. Below, in the square, the elders of Grayhollow had begun to gather — old Sable first, then the woman who named streets to her oven, then others, emerging from doorways with the slow, deliberate courage of people who had already lost a country once and were deciding, visibly, whether they could bear to lose a village too.

Wren's hand found her knife. Her mind, traitorously, was already running the theft — routes, distractions, the carriage's wheels, the horses' water — and finding every line of it ending in blood.

"Juniper," she murmured. "Tell me you have a plan."

He was silent a moment, watching Hale lay the prophecy book on Brann's folding table, open it to a page near the end, and wait — patiently, terribly — like a man who had already read the last page and was merely letting the rest of the story catch up.

"Yes," Juniper said at last. "But you're not going to like whose plan it is."
