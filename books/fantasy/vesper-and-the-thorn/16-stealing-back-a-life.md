## 16. Stealing Back a Life

Wren had stolen many things in her life: compasses that pointed at nothing, letters sealed with dying men's names, once—briefly and by accident—a bishop's hat. She had never stolen back something that belonged to someone standing right beside her, and she found she hated it less than she'd expected.

The collector's caravan sat in the yard of the Wayhouse at Cinders Cross like a fat black beetle that had died mid-crawl. Four wagons, lacquered dark, each one stamped with the Guild of Impossible Things' seal: an open hand holding a closed eye. Lanterns burned at the corners of the yard, but the collectors themselves were sprawled inside the wayhouse, drinking on Corvane's coin. Guild men were always generous with money that wasn't theirs. Wren had learned that from the inside.

She lay flat against the cold of the roof ridge, Odile beside her, and watched the wagon with the iron-banded strongbox on its bed. Somewhere under that lock was a sheaf of papers with Juniper Quintana's name written across them in guild ink—the bond that made him property. The debt he'd been born into, sold into, the arithmetic of his whole life reduced to a ledger line.

"You're grinding your teeth," Odile murmured.

"I'm planning."

"Planning loudly."

Wren exhaled through her nose. Below them, the yard was mud and lantern-light and the smell of wet straw. Two guards remained outside, huddled under the near eave, playing dice with the bored intensity of men who'd rather be drinking. Their swords leaned against the wall behind them, which was either luck or stupidity, and Wren had stopped believing in luck around the time her country stopped existing.

"Simple," she said. "I go down the drainpipe, cut the box free, we carry it to the treeline, burn it there. Paper burns fast. Bond's ash before they finish their third bottle."

"And Juniper?"

"Juniper is going to hate this."

Odile's laugh was dry as old vellum. "Then perhaps you should have told him."

"He'd have argued. He argues with his whole face." Wren shifted her weight. "He'd have insisted on paying. On walking up to those collectors and offering them something honest for something that should never have been for sale in the first place. And they'd have taken whatever he offered and kept the bond anyway, because that's what guild men do. That's what *Corvane* does. You don't bargain with a ledger, Marchetti. You balance it or you burn it."

"Spoken like a woman who has burned things."

"Spoken like a woman whose country was erased by people who preferred paperwork to fire." Wren pulled her knife, tested the edge against her thumbnail. "Vesper wasn't conquered. It was *amended*. Treaties, decrees, maps redrawn while everyone slept. Nobody had to fight us because nobody had to. They just wrote us out." Her jaw tightened. "So no. I'm not asking permission from paper tonight. I'm answering it."

Below, one of the guards threw his dice, swore, and turned his back to the yard to relieve himself against the fence post. His partner laughed too hard at nothing.

"Now," Wren breathed, and went over the edge.

* * *

Odile followed more slowly. Her knees had opinions about rooftops these days, and her hands—hands that had once drawn the roads of an entire nation from memory alone, stroke by faithful stroke—now shook when the cold got into them. But she could still read a yard the way she read terrain, and the yard was simple: mud that would hold bootprints, so step in the wheel ruts; lantern at the corner cast a blind spot along the second wagon's flank; the strongbox was chained at two points, both reachable from the shadow side.

She dropped into the ruts and moved.

There was a particular shame in this, she thought as she worked the first chain pin with a pick she'd fashioned from a broken compass needle. She had been a cartographer of the Royal Survey of Vesper. She had been imprisoned for eleven years for the crime of remembering. And now here she crouched in the mud of a foreign road, committing burglary for a scribe she'd known three weeks—a boy who flinched at loud voices and apologized to furniture he bumped into.

And yet.

She thought of the atlas waiting in her pack, recovered from the Prophecy Office vault at the cost of Juniper's terror and Wren's blood and a night none of them would speak of easily. She thought of the door drawn in thorn-ink on its final page, unmarked, unlabeled, patient as a held breath. Everything now depended on whether Juniper Quintana stayed alive long enough to reach it—and Corvane's collectors had come to collect him like a debt of coin.

The first chain gave. The second fought her, rusted in its staple, and she worked it with small furious twists while the dice game rattled on ten feet away and the drunk guard sang something tuneless about a miller's daughter.

"There," Wren whispered at her shoulder, making her jump. "Box free. Take the far end."

They lifted together. The strongbox was heavier than it looked—guild boxes always were, weighted with lead so no thief could run far with one—but between Wren's wiry strength and Odile's stubbornness they got it over the fence rail and into the ditch beyond, then dragged it through wet grass toward the treeline, breath steaming, hearts loud.

At the black margin of the trees, Wren set down her end, drew her knife, and pried at the lid's hasp.

"Wren."

The voice came out of the darkness like a dropped stone.

Juniper stood among the pines, lamplight from the distant yard catching only the edge of him—his coat wrong-buttoned, his hair wild, his face wearing the expression Odile had privately named *the look of a man reading bad news twice*. Behind him, faintly, the road back toward Grayhollow stretched empty. He had run. He must have run the whole way.

"How did you—" Wren began.

"The bond papers are keyed to me. Guild ink. I felt it." He touched his own chest, vaguely, over the heart. "Like a hook pulling. I've felt it all night, actually—I thought it was just dread. It's been getting worse since you left. So I followed it." He stepped closer, and his voice did the thing it always did when he was frightened and determined at once: went quiet and very precise. "Put the knife down, Wren."

"It's a strongbox, Juniper, not a chapel."

"You're stealing my life out of a box in the mud and you want me to be calm about it."

"Yes," said Wren. "That's exactly what I want."

* * *

Juniper looked at the strongbox and felt the hook behind his sternum pull tighter, and understood, with terrible clarity, that everything Wren said made sense and everything she intended to do was wrong.

That was the trouble with the last few weeks. Sense and rightness had stopped living in the same house.

"If you break that open and burn those papers," he said, "the guild doesn't lose the debt. They transfer it. That's how bonds work—you know that. Corvane will simply declare the destruction an act of default and attach the debt to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "To whoever's nearest. To whoever helped. To *you*, probably. Or Odile. Or Grayhollow itself, if he wants to be creative. Corvane is always creative with other people's obligations."

"So we burn it faster than he can write."

"You can't outrun ink, Wren. Believe me. I've tried. I'm *made* of trying." He knelt beside the box, laid his palm flat on its cold lid, and felt the bond hum against his skin like a struck string. His name was in there. Twenty-six years of interest and penalties and clauses written in a hand he recognized because it was the same hand that had written every entry in every guild ledger for a century: Master Corvane's neat, patient, merciless script. "There's another way."

"Which is?"

"I pay it."

The silence that followed had texture. Odile broke it first, her voice thin with disbelief. "With what, child? You have a coat with missing buttons and half a book of prophecy."

"With what the bond says I can pay with." He kept his eyes on the box. "It's in the clauses. I've read them a thousand times—they're the only inheritance I've ever had. Clause nine. A bonded scribe may discharge outstanding debt through *service rendered at cost*, valued by the guild assessor. If I surrender myself to collection—voluntarily, tonight, no force needed—the assessed value of a trained scribe of my grade is applied against the principal. It won't clear it. But it changes the terms. It moves the debt off my person and onto a schedule. It buys time. Legal time. Time Corvane can't argue with, because it's *his own arithmetic*."

"And where do they take you," Wren said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the smuggler's voice, the one that came out when feeling got too big to hold any other shape. "Where does 'surrendering to collection' put you, Juniper? In a guild cell? In irons on a wagon like this one? Delivered back to Corvane, who we *know* wants you dead on schedule because a century-old book says so?"

"The prophecy needs me at the Thorn Door," he said quietly. "Not in a cell. Corvane can't kill me early without breaking his own precious ledger. Which means if I go willingly, I stay valuable. Valuable things get transported carefully. Carefully means slowly. Slowly means you can find me."

"That's not a plan, that's a hostage negotiating with his own ransom."

"It's the only plan that doesn't make anyone else pay for my life!" It came out louder than he meant, cracking in the middle, and somewhere back at the wayhouse a dog started barking. He lowered his voice to a fierce whisper. "You stole an atlas for me. You crossed the salt fields for me. You gave up—" He faltered on the memory of the toll bridge, of what she'd paid, of the hole in her where her mother's face used to be and the promise he carried in its place. "You've paid enough. Everyone's paid enough. This one's mine. It was always mine. Let me carry it the honest way."

"Honest." Wren laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "You know what honest got Vesper? Honest signed the treaty. Honest filed the maps. Honest stood in the archive and let them shelve a whole country like a dead man's letters." She grabbed the front of his coat, not hard, but close, her forehead nearly touching his. "I am not letting them put you in a box, Juniper Quintana. I've seen what happens to things the guild puts in boxes. They get *catalogued*."

For a moment neither of them moved. The dog barked itself hoarse and quit. Odile stood a little apart, hands folded, watching them the way she once watched weather fronts gather over the Vesperan coast—with the professional calm of someone who knew the storm was coming regardless and was merely noting its bearing.

Then Juniper reached up and gently unwrapped Wren's fingers from his collar, one by one, and held her hand a moment longer than the gesture required.

"I'm not asking you to agree," he said. "I'm asking you to let me try it my way first. If the assessor refuses clause nine—if they won't take the surrender—if anything goes wrong at all, then burn it. Burn the box, the wagon, the whole caravan, and I won't say one word against it. But give me until dawn. Give me my one honest act. I've never had one before. My whole life's been other people's entries." His voice steadied. "Let me write one of my own."

Wren stared at him. Odile saw her jaw work, saw the argument die behind her teeth not because it was answered but because it was *outnumbered*—outnumbered by the particular stubborn decency of the man in front of her, which was, Odile had long since concluded, the single most dangerous thing any of them carried.

"Dawn," Wren said at last, savagely. "And I'm watching. And if they put a hand on you, I don't care what clause covers it, I'm burning everything including the horse."

"There's a horse?"

"There's always a horse."

* * *

They buried the strongbox in the soft ground under the pines—Odile insisted; evidence destroyed was one thing, evidence hidden was leverage—and waited out the last hours of dark in the treeline, taking turns pretending to sleep. Juniper didn't pretend. He sat with his back against a pine trunk and rehearsed.

That was what it was, though he'd never have admitted it aloud. He rehearsed the words of clause nine under his breath like a prayer in a language he was only half fluent in, and Odile, waking briefly in the gray hour, heard him and thought: *he sounds like me, thirty years ago, practicing the testimony that would convict him.* She had recited her own maps to a tribunal once, every road and river of Vesper offered up as proof, and they had called it forgery and sedition and erased her name from the Survey rolls. Institutions did not reward honesty. Institutions digested it.

But she said nothing. Some lessons had to be walked into, not warned away from. She had learned that too.

Dawn came up the color of pewter. Mist lay in the yard of Cinders Cross like spilled milk. The collectors emerged from the wayhouse squinting and sour, and Juniper walked out of the treeline with his hands visible and his chin up, and Wren's nails bit four crescents into her own palm.

He stopped ten feet from the senior collector—a wide man with the guild seal tattooed on his neck and the eyes of someone who had long ago stopped seeing people as anything but balances—and spoke clearly enough for the whole yard to hear.

"My name is Juniper Quintana. Bonded scribe, third grade, account number seven-oh-nine-four. I am surrendering myself to collection under clause nine of my bond. I request formal assessment."

The collector looked at him for a long moment. Then, unhurried, he took out a ledger, licked his thumb, and turned pages while the mist curled around all their boots.

"Clause nine," he said at last, and smiled without warmth. "Huh. Nobody's invoked clause nine in forty years." He snapped the ledger shut. "You know why, scribe?"

"No."

"Because forty years ago, the guild amended it." He jerked his thumb at his men. "Bind him. The master will be pleased—he does love a volunteer."

Behind the treeline, silent as ink soaking into paper, Wren was already moving, and Odile's hand closed around the handle of the shovel they'd used to bury the box.
