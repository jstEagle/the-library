## 20. Anchors Against Forgetting

The forgetting did not come all at once. That was the worst of it.

Juniper had expected the border of unwriting to be a wall, a gate, some visible line where the world stopped agreeing to remember Vesper. Instead it was weather. A grayness in the air that thickened by degrees as they walked, so that by the second day he could not say when they had crossed anything at all—only that the light had gone the color of old paper and the road beneath their feet had begun to feel less like stone and more like a sentence someone was slowly erasing.

He noticed it first in his own hands.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen waymarker—a pillar whose inscription had been worn to nothing, or perhaps never written at all—and Juniper took out his ledger to record the day's distance, as he had done every night since his apprenticeship, and found that his pen hesitated over his own name. Not because he had forgotten it. Because the name felt *provisional*. As if it were a word in a language he was losing, still understood but no longer native to his mouth. *Juniper Quintana.* He wrote it. The ink sat on the page looking like it might get up and leave.

"Write it again," Odile said from across the fire.

He looked up. The cartographer was watching him with an expression he had learned, in the weeks since Grayhollow, to read as fear wearing a cartographer's composure.

"What?"

"Your name. Write it again. Then say it aloud." She turned her own wrist toward the firelight, and he saw she had written *Odile Marchetti* on her forearm in map-ink, the good indelible kind, the kind that survived water and time and the particular spite of the Prophecy Office. "I've been doing it every hour. You'll want to start."

"Why would writing my own name—" He stopped. He thought about Wren, two nights ago, going silent in the middle of a sentence about her mother, her face going through expressions like a woman searching pockets for keys she could no longer picture. He thought about how she had finally said, *I know I had one. I know I loved her. I can't see her anymore,* and how none of them had known what to do with that except sit closer to her.

He wrote his name again. Said it aloud. It sounded truer the second time, which frightened him more than if it hadn't.

* * *

By the third day they had developed what Wren called, with smuggler's contempt for sentimentality, "the inventory."

It happened at dawn and dusk, without exception, and it went like this: they sat in a circle, because circles were harder to lose people from than lines, and each of them said their full name, their mother's full name, and one true thing that had happened to them before the border. Not a story. A fact. Facts held better; stories wanted to drift.

"Juniper Quintana," Juniper would say. "Son of Marisol Quintana, who pressed flowers in the back of her recipe book and never told anyone why. True thing: when I was nine I stole a bottle of blue ink from Master Corvane's desk and drank a sip of it to see if I could taste words. I could. They tasted like pennies and rain."

"Wren Ashgrave," Wren would say, and pause, because the next part cost her. "Daughter of—" and here she would press her thumbnail hard into the inside of her wrist, where she had taken up Odile's practice, "—daughter of a woman whose face I can't find anymore, but who sang off-key on purpose so we'd laugh, and I know this is true because I remember knowing it. True thing: I stole my first compass at six years old and it pointed at my father's grave, and I kept it until it rusted, and I never once let it point anywhere else."

The first time she'd managed the whole of it, her voice had cracked on *Ashgrave*, and Juniper had reached across the circle—not thinking, just reaching—and put his hand over hers where it gripped her knee. She hadn't pulled away. That was its own entry in the ledger of things he was afraid to examine too closely.

"Odile Marchetti," Odile would say, crisp as a signature. "Daughter of Teodora Marchetti, who drew coastlines the way other women embroidered. True thing: I drew the road from Vesper's capital to the sea forty-one times from memory before they arrested me, and every time it came out the same, and that sameness is the only proof I have ever needed that memory is a form of measurement."

And then—the part that had been Juniper's idea, though he'd offered it shyly, the way he offered most things—they traded. Each of them told one true thing about another member of the company, something the other might be about to lose.

"You," Wren said to Juniper one gray evening, pointing at him across the fire with a strip of dried fish as if indicting him, "laugh before your own jokes. Not after. Before. Like you're so pleased with them you can't wait. And you hum when you write. Badly. You hum like a man who has heard music described to him."

"I do not—"

"You do. Ask the cartographer."

"He does," Odile confirmed gravely. "It is the worst sound I have ever been imprisoned near."

"And you," Juniper said to Wren, because it was his turn and because he had been saving this one, "talk to locks. When you pick them. You apologize to them first. 'Sorry, friend, nothing personal,' you said to the Prophecy Office vault door, and I have thought about it every day since, because only someone who believes everything has a name would apologize to a lock."

Wren stared at him. Something moved through her face—surprise, then a fierce, blinking tenderness she tried to disguise as irritation. "That's surveillance, scribe."

"That's inventory," he said.

* * *

The rituals multiplied, because fear is inventive.

Odile taught them to draw maps of places they loved from memory, every evening, on whatever scraps of paper remained, and then to compare them against the scraps from previous evenings. If a coastline shifted—if a street vanished from a city that had stood in your mind your whole life—you knew the grayness had gotten into you, and you redrew it, and you said the place's name aloud while you drew, and you made someone else watch you do it. Witnesses, Odile insisted, were everything. *A memory witnessed is a memory anchored.* She said it like a theorem. She had lost enough to speak in theorems.

Wren's ritual was rougher. She carried three stones in her pocket—one for herself, one for her mother, one for the country she'd left as a child—and each morning she counted them aloud, and if there were three, she said, "Still here, all of us," in a voice that dared the borderlands to disagree. Once, horribly, there were two. She had stood very still in the middle of the road, white-faced, patting her pockets, until Juniper found the third stone where it had rolled into the seam of her pack, and he had never seen her look so much like a child, and she had never forgiven him for witnessing it, and she had thanked him twice, quietly, the same night.

Juniper's own ritual was the ledger. Of course it was. He wrote everything now—not just distances and camps but the inventory itself, verbatim, all their names and true things, in duplicate, hiding one copy in his boot and one in the lining of his coat, because a single copy was a single point of failure and he was a scribe, redundancy was his religion. Some nights he wrote until his hand cramped, transcribing the day's small ceremonies: *Wren counted three stones. Odile's map of the harbor lost its lighthouse; she redrew it and named it aloud, the Lantern of Saint Amara, and we all said it together. I remembered that I hate the taste of pennies and rain.*

Because that was the secret he was beginning to understand, down in the marrow of it: the rituals weren't really against forgetting. They were against *unbeing alone*. A name said into silence was a candle in wind. A name said into a circle of people who repeated it back—that was a wall.

* * *

On the fifth night the grayness tested them properly.

Juniper woke past midnight to find Wren sitting up, staring at her own hands in the banked firelight, turning them over as if checking for damage.

"I couldn't remember why we're going to Vesper," she said, without preamble, without looking at him. "Not the big why. The little one. My why. There was a reason I said yes when you asked me to run, and it wasn't money, and it wasn't the compass, and I've been sitting here for an hour and it's just—gone. Like a room in a house I grew up in that turns out not to exist."

His chest did something painful. He sat up beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and he did not say *it will come back*, because he didn't know that it would, and lying inside the border seemed like a dangerous kind of spending.

Instead he opened the ledger to the duplicate copy hidden in the coat lining and read to her, by feel more than light, the entry from the night they'd fled the salt fields:

*"Wren Ashgrave took the wheel of the cart and drove us through Hale's pickets laughing—actually laughing—and afterward she said, 'I haven't run toward something in ten years. Only away.' That's why she's here. She said so. Witnessed: J.Q."*

She was quiet a long moment. "I said that?"

"You said that."

"Toward something," she repeated, tasting the words. And then, softer, almost to the fire: "You wrote me down."

"You were happening," Juniper said. "It's what I do."

She looked at him then, and the look lasted one breath longer than looks between friends are supposed to last, and neither of them repaired it, and eventually she lay back down and said, "Wake me for the inventory," and he sat watching the gray dark and understanding, with terrible clarity, that he would write her every day for the rest of his life if she asked, and that she had not asked, and that maybe she didn't need to.

* * *

Dawn brought the sixth inventory, and with it, Odile's announcement.

"We're close enough now that the border knows we're coming," she said. She had spread her re-drawn fragments of the master atlas across a flat stone, weighted with pebbles, and at the center of them was the thorn-inked door, and around the door, for the first time, faint marginalia in her own younger handwriting that none of them had seen before. "These notes—I don't remember writing them. Or rather, I remember the pen in my hand, thirty years ago, and nothing of the words. Which means I wrote them *knowing* I'd forget. Look."

They leaned in. The script was small and urgent:

*Past this line, carry each other. The unwriting takes what is held alone. Say names in pairs. Sleep touching, if you can bear it. Grief shared stays; grief hoarded goes. — O.M., who learned this the hard way and does not wish to detail how.*

A silence settled over the camp, the specific silence of four people each privately deciding whether they could bear it.

"Well," Wren said at last, into the quiet. "Sleeping arrangements just got political."

But that evening, when they made their circle, they changed the inventory. Names in pairs, as the note instructed—each person speaking their own name and then the name of the person beside them, binding them together in the saying, so that losing one meant losing the witness too, so that the border would have to take them entangled or not at all.

"Juniper Quintana," he said, "and Wren Ashgrave."

"Wren Ashgrave," she said, her shoulder warm against his, "and Juniper Quintana—who hums badly and laughs early and writes me down like I'm worth the ink."

"Odile Marchetti," said Odile, "and the two of you, who are being disgustingly obvious, and I am choosing to find it anchoring."

They laughed, all three, and the laughter rang strangely loud and bright in the gray air, defiant as a struck bell, and Juniper thought: *let the border try to take this. Let it try to unwrite a circle.*

It was Odile who sobered first. She was staring east, past the dead waymarker, to where the grayness gathered thickest along the horizon—and where, threaded through it, unmistakable even at distance, ran a line of blackthorn trees, twisted and dense, marking the land like a scar.

"The Thorn Door," she said quietly. "Two days. Maybe less."

And behind them, far back along the road they had traveled, thin as a scratch of ink, a rider crested the rise—then another—moving with the unhurried certainty of people who knew exactly where the road would deliver everyone on it.

Juniper's hand found his ledger. His other hand found Wren's.

"Hale," she breathed.

"No," Odile said, her eyes still fixed on the blackthorn line, her voice gone strange. "Look at the horses' colors. That's not the Office."

The riders carried no banners. But stitched on their saddlecloths, catching the dead gray light, was a sigil Juniper knew intimately, had copied a thousand times at the head of a thousand contracts: the open ledger and quill of the Guild of Impossible Things.

Corvane had found them.
