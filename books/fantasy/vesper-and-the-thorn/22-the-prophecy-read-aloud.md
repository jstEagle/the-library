## 22. The Prophecy Read Aloud

Wren heard the horses before she saw them—not the drumming gallop of pursuit but the slow, deliberate clop of animals that had nowhere left to chase. That was worse. Men who rode slowly were men who already knew where you were.

She was on the last watch before dawn, crouched in the lee of a fallen boundary stone at the edge of the borderlands, where the grass grew gray as old thread and the stars overhead seemed slightly out of focus, like a painting someone had breathed on. Behind her, in the hollow beneath the stone, Juniper slept with the compass cradled against his chest and Odile's atlas open across his knees, as if even unconscious he meant to keep reading his way home. The exiles of Grayhollow had gone on ahead at first light yesterday, forty-odd souls walking into the unwritten country with songs in their mouths and nothing else. Wren had stayed behind with Juniper and Odile to guard the rear. She was beginning to regret it.

The horses came around the shoulder of the hill: three riders, black-cloaked, and leading them a fourth figure on foot—a tall man in an auditor's gray coat, carrying no weapon that Wren could see except a book bound in cracked green leather.

She knew that book. She had spent eleven days watching Juniper not touch it, not look at it, not think about it, which is to say thinking about nothing else. The Ledger of Vesper's Ending. The prophecy that had started all of this, lifted from the Prophecy Office vault along with half of Wren's future and all of her sleep.

"Auditor Hale," she said, standing, because there was no point pretending to be a rock. Her hand found the knife at her belt. It felt about as useful as a spoon against the sea.

"Wren." Hale stopped ten paces off, close enough that she could see the lamplight-colored circles under his eyes, far enough that courtesy could still be mistaken for confidence. He looked tired in a way that went deeper than the body—tired like a house whose foundations had settled wrong. "I'd like to speak with all three of you. I would prefer not to do it at knifepoint."

"Then stay where you are."

"I intend to." He gestured, and the two riders dismounted and stood by their horses, hands visible, empty. One of them yawned. Wren found that detail more frightening than drawn steel: they were bored. Bored men were men who had done this many times.

Behind her, she heard the scrape of Odile waking, the sharp intake of breath that meant Juniper had surfaced from sleep straight into understanding. By the time she glanced back he was on his feet beside her, hair wild, the compass clutched in one white-knuckled fist.

"He's alone," Juniper said quietly. "No Corvane."

"For now," Wren said.

"No," said Hale, and something in his voice made them all go still. "Not for now. Not ever again, I think. May I read to you? It will take perhaps ten minutes. Afterward, if you still want your knives, you may keep them. I suspect you won't want anything at all."

* * *

They built a small fire because the cold in the borderlands was not ordinary cold—it came up through the soles of the boots, out of ground that had forgotten it was ever warm—and because Odile said, flatly, "If we're going to be destroyed, I'll be destroyed warm," and nobody argued.

Hale sat across the flames with the green book open on his knees. Up close, in firelight, he looked less like an auditor and more like what Wren supposed he had been before the Office got its hooks in him: a scholar, maybe, or a priest of some very patient god. His fingers rested on the pages the way a doctor's fingers rest on a pulse.

"You've read fragments," he began. "Juniper has read more—he was foolish enough, or faithful enough, to keep looking. But none of you have heard it whole. The Office trains its auditors never to read a prophecy piecemeal. A prophecy read in pieces is a riddle. Read whole, it is—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care— "a sentence. And sentences end."

"Just say it," Wren said.

Hale nodded once, almost gratefully, and read.

He read in a voice like dry paper turning, and the prophecy came out of him in the old high Vesperan cadence that made the hairs on Wren's arms stand up, because she hadn't heard that cadence since she was six years old and her mother sang it over the washbasin without knowing she was doing it. The words themselves were archaic, formal, terrible:

*When the thorn blooms backward and the door remembers its hinge,*

*a scribe shall come who owes his name to another's book.*

*He shall carry the compass that points at nothing,*

*and the map drawn by the woman who loved the roads too much,*

*and the bird that flies for coin and stays for love.*

*Three times shall the country try to keep him.*

*Twice it shall fail.*

*At the third keeping—the Door grown of blackthorn,*

*which opens only for consent—*

*the scribe shall give himself,*

*and in giving, become the country's final memory,*

*the last mind to hold Vesper whole.*

*And when his memory closes, so shall the country's,*

*and Vesper shall pass out of all books, all maps, all mouths,*

*unwritten past the power of any pen to find again,*

*and the debt of its existence shall be marked paid in full.*

Hale closed the book.

The fire popped. Somewhere behind the hills, wind moved through grass that had no business being grass, being here, being anything. Wren became aware that she had stopped breathing somewhere around *the scribe shall give himself*, and that her hands were shaking, and that Juniper was looking at the flames with an expression she had seen only once before—the night he'd shown her his name in the ledger, written a century before his birth. It was not fear. It was recognition. It was a man meeting a sentence he had somehow always known would be handed down.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"No." She was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. "That's not—that's Corvane's version. That's the Office's version. Prophecies get edited, everyone knows that, the Guild buys amendments, the Office redacts—"

"The Office redacts," Hale agreed mildly. "Corvane purchases. I have compared this text against three independent copies held in sealed archives in three cities that have never traded with one another. They are identical to the word. Including the word *bird*, which I confess I did not understand until I watched you fly cargo through a customs blockade in a storm two years ago, Mistress Wren, and understood that you flew exactly the way this line describes." He looked at her with something that was not quite pity and not quite apology. "I have been reading this book for nineteen years. I know every clause. There is no amendment. There is no loophole. There is a scribe, a door, and a death, in that order, forever."

"Then your archives are wrong."

"Then every archive in the world is wrong in precisely the same way, which is another word for true."

Odile spoke for the first time, low and rough, from her place by the fire. "And the country? The forgetting? Is that also true?"

Hale was quiet a moment. When he answered, he answered her, only her, as if the others had temporarily ceased to exist. "Yes. That is the part no one tells you, cartographer. The erasure of Vesper was not merely political. It was contractual. The treaty that unwrote the borders required a mechanism of completion—a closing clause. The prophecy is that mechanism. So long as it stands unfulfilled, Vesper lingers. Half-alive. Haunting its own absence. Grayhollow exists because the clause hasn't closed. Every exile who remembers, every road that reshapes itself under believing feet—all of it is the country refusing to die until the terms are met." He turned the green book over in his hands. "The scribe dies, the final memory forms, the memory closes, and Vesper is finished. Gently. Completely. Irrevocably."

"And if the scribe doesn't die?" Wren demanded. "If we just—don't go to the Door? Walk away? Sail somewhere with better weather and worse wine?"

"Then the clause remains open, and the Office sends someone else to fulfill it. There are other scribes in debt to the Guild. There are always other scribes." Hale's mouth twisted. "I should know. I audit their bonds."

"So we run forever."

"You run until the Office grows impatient, and then it stops chasing and starts arranging. You cannot outrun a sentence, Mistress Wren. You can only delay the period at its end."

Wren looked at Juniper. She hated herself for looking, because she knew what she'd see, and she saw it: not despair. Worse than despair. Acceptance, already blooming, quiet as frost. He was doing the arithmetic she could hear ticking inside him—*one life against a nation; my debt against their freedom*—and the arithmetic was coming out the way arithmetic always comes out for people like him, people who had been taught since childhood that they were sums to be settled.

"Don't," she said.

"It's forty thousand people, Wren. Grayhollow alone. And every scattered family from here to the coast who still lights a candle on Vesperan New Year and doesn't know why."

"It's *you*."

"That's the same thing," he said softly, "if you believe the book."

"Well, I don't." She snatched the kettle off the fire and poured tea into four cups with hands that wanted to throw things instead, because her mother had raised her that way—grief goes in the hands or it goes in the heart, and the heart has less room. "You want to know what I hear, Auditor? I hear a book written by men who needed a country to die tidily, so they dressed up murder as fate and called the murderer a prophet. I've smuggled enough contraband to know forged papers when I hear them read aloud."

"The text predates the treaty by sixty years," Hale said.

"Then the treaty was forged to fit the text, which is worse." She thrust a cup at him hard enough to slosh. He took it. Their eyes met over the steam, and she saw, to her fury, that he agreed with her—or some part of him did—and that agreement had done nothing to stop him riding out here, and nothing would.

"Why?" Juniper asked. It was the question underneath all of them, and he asked it gently, the way he asked everything. "Why ride three days to tell us this yourself? You could have let us walk into it ignorant. Ignorant men walk faster."

Hale held the cup without drinking. For a long moment the firelight moved on his face and he seemed to be deciding how much truth he could afford, the way a poor man decides how much bread.

"Because I have read this book cover to cover," he said finally, "nineteen years, every page, every marginal note in every dead auditor's hand. And do you know what I found in the margins, scribe? Doubt. Eleven generations of doubt, written small and struck through, struck through and rewritten. Auditors who noticed that the prophecy never says *who writes the final entry*. Who noticed that the closing clause requires a *memory*, and memories live in minds, and minds are notoriously disobedient things. Who noticed—" his voice dropped, and the firelight caught something wet in his eyes that might have been exhaustion or might have been the thing exhaustion was protecting— "who noticed that the book was bound in green leather from a tannery in Vesper, and that no one at the Office has ever been able to explain why a book foretelling a country's death should be bound in its skin."

Silence. The wind moved. Odile set down her cup with a click like a lock turning.

"You're telling us," Wren said slowly, "that your own office has been arguing with its own prophecy for two hundred years."

"I'm telling you that I burned my notes before I left the archive," Hale said. "I'm telling you that when this is over—if there is an over—I will either be promoted or erased, and I have stopped caring which. I came to tell you the whole sentence because you deserve to hear it whole before you decide what to do with it. That is all. That is everything." He rose, brushed ash from his coat, and stood holding the green book against his chest like a wound he'd learned to carry. "The Door is two days east. Corvane moves faster than I do, and he does not read aloud, and he does not bring tea."

He walked back toward his horse. Then he stopped, half-turned, and looked at Juniper across the dying fire.

"One more thing, scribe. In nineteen years, I have never told anyone this, because it is not in any report and it ended the career of the last man who said it aloud." His voice was very quiet now, nearly lost under the wind. "On the last page of the ledger, beneath the final clause, there is a single line in a different ink. Older ink. Faded almost to nothing. It says: *Amendments permitted. The price is the difference.*"

"Price of what difference?" Wren whispered.

"I don't know," Hale said. "No one has ever dared to find out." He swung up into the saddle. "Perhaps someone will. Perhaps that's the real prophecy, hiding under the official one, the way a country hides under its own erasure. Good luck. You'll need more than I can give."

The hoofbeats faded east, then south, then not at all.

* * *

They sat around the ashes until the sky went the color of a bruise healing. Nobody spoke for a long time. The borderlands hummed their tuneless hum, and far off, faint as a bell underwater, Wren thought she heard singing—Grayhollow's exiles, miles ahead, singing Vesperan songs into a country that was trying to remember its own name.

It was Odile who broke the silence, staring into the coals with her scarred mapmaker's hands folded tight.

"*Amendments permitted,*" she repeated. "The price is the difference." She looked up, and her eyes had the light in them that Wren had learned to fear and love in equal measure—the light of a woman about to commit professional heresy. "A ledger is a ledger. I've kept ledgers. I've *cooked* ledgers. If a prophecy is a ledger—and it is, it balances, that's the whole horror of it, every clause pays for the next—then it can be amended. It's written right there. Permitted."

"At what price?" Juniper asked. "The difference between what and what?"

"Between the sentence as written," Odile said, "and the sentence as amended. Someone dies at that Door in the original text. If the amendment strikes that clause, the ledger must be paid the value of a life. Or a country. Or—" she hesitated, and Wren watched the thought arrive in her face like weather— "or whatever the ledger judges equivalent. Ledgers don't care what you offer. They care what it's worth."

Wren's blood went cold and hot at once. She looked at Juniper—at the thin wrists, the ink-stained fingers, the terrible gentle readiness already settling over him like snow—and she made herself a promise so fierce it felt less like a decision than like a change in her physical nature.

Whatever the price was, whatever the ledger demanded, whatever the difference came to—

She would pay it before he could. She would pickpocket fate itself. She had stolen a compass that pointed at nothing; she could steal a death.

"We move at first light," she said, standing, kicking dirt over the coals. "We beat Corvane to the Door. And Juniper—" she caught his chin, made him look at her, made him see her— "you don't get to do the arithmetic alone anymore. That's an amendment too."

Something moved behind his eyes—fear, gratitude, love, all three wearing the same face. He covered her hand with his.

Above the hills, the false stars flickered, and for one heartbeat—one only—they arranged themselves into a shape Wren recognized from a map she'd memorized as a child and lost as a woman: the constellation Vesperan sailors called the Thorn Crown, which appeared on no chart drawn after the erasure, and which was, tonight, burning steadily over a country that refused to finish dying.

Two days to the Door. Two days to beat a guildmaster, outwit a prophecy, and cheat a ledger older than the language it was written in.

Wren tightened her pack straps and started walking.
