## 36. Out Through the Thorn

The Door was smaller than Wren remembered it being.

That was the strange arithmetic of endings—everything shrank as it approached. The blackthorn gate that had loomed over the ridge like a wound in the world now stood barely taller than a woman, its branches woven so tight that no light passed between them, its thorns each longer than a finger and curved like question marks. It did not look like an exit. It looked like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

Behind it, Vesper was burning in the way that countries burn when they are made of memory: slowly, from the edges inward, with a great deal of light.

Wren stood at the head of the column of exiles and watched the western hills come apart like sugar in warm water. Not collapsing—*unmaking*. The hills went first to outline, then to suggestion, then to a pale gold haze that smelled, impossibly, of bread baking and river water and the particular green of a summer she had been too young to keep. Somewhere in that haze a bell was ringing. She could not have said which belltower. She had never lived here. She had only left here, which was somehow the same thing.

"Stop staring," Odile said gently, at her elbow. "It'll stare back."

"It's already staring back."

"I know." The old cartographer's hands were empty for the first time since Wren had known her. The atlas was gone—the book had taken it, along with everything else—and her fingers kept curling around nothing, a habit of forty years with nowhere to land. "I know it is."

Juniper came down the line of people, and Wren watched him do it, because watching Juniper Quintana move through a crowd had become, without her permission, one of the small permanent pleasures of her life. He touched shoulders as he passed. He said names. He had learned two hundred Vesperan names in three days of walking, and he said each one now like a man paying out coin he could not afford, making sure everyone had some to carry through.

He reached her and stopped, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because there was a whole country dissolving behind them and what was there to say that the bell wasn't already saying?

"The book's quiet," he said finally. He had it slung against his chest in its sling of grayhollow wool, and his hand rested on it the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust. "No new entries. Since dawn, nothing."

"That's either very good or very bad."

"With ledgers," Juniper said, "it's usually both."

Wren huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. Her eyes were stinging and she refused to investigate why. "How many?"

"Two hundred and six walked out of Grayhollow. Two hundred and four are still standing here." His voice caught on the number, just slightly, just enough. Old Marta and the twins' grandfather had gone in the night, quietly, choosing to stay inside their memories rather than carry them out. Nobody had argued. There were kinds of love that looked like staying, and they had all learned that on the salt fields.

"And us?"

"You're last," Juniper said. "I asked to be. Odile's already through—she went with the children, holding their hands so they wouldn't be frightened of the thorns. She didn't look back once." A pause. "She's stronger than any of us."

"She drew this country from memory while sitting in a jail cell," Wren said. "Looking back is the only thing she's ever done. She just does it facing forward."

That earned her half a smile, tired and real. Then Juniper's face did the thing it did when he was about to say something careful, something scribe-shaped, something weighed to the grain.

"Wren. When we go through—"

"Don't."

"—the world on the other side hasn't changed. The treaty still stands. The Prophecy Office will call this morning a weather event, at most. Hale burned his commission but Hale isn't the office, and Corvane—"

"Corvane is ash or arrested or running, and I don't care which." She stepped closer. Behind him the bell rang again, fainter, as if the belltower itself were forgetting how bells worked. "Juniper. I spent my whole life learning how things end. Smugglers don't get happy endings, we get *uncaught* ones. So don't stand there drafting the terms of our surrender before we've even crossed. Let me have ten steps of not knowing. That's all I'm asking. Ten steps where I don't owe anyone anything and nobody owes me."

He looked at her for a long moment. Dawn was coming up behind them, through the dissolving hills, and it lit him gold at the edges, and she thought—not for the first time—that if she'd had a mapmaker's gift she would have drawn him exactly like this and labeled it *here, this is the place.*

"Ten steps," he agreed. "Then we start the ledger."

"No. Then we start the *morning*. Ledgers can wait until after breakfast."

* * *

They went through together, because the Door had opinions about alone.

It had taken them most of the previous day to understand that. The Thorn Door opened for consent—it always had, that was its whole terrible design—but it opened *wider* for company. One person passing through alone slipped through a gap barely wide enough for breath. Two who crossed hand in hand found the branches parting like a curtain. And when the first families of Grayhollow had gone through, arms linked, children lifted onto shoulders, the whole gate had groaned open like a ship coming about, and for one moment Wren had seen the other side: a grey field under a grey sky, the ordinary world, waiting with its arms folded.

Now it was their turn. Two hundred and four had passed; the passage had narrowed again, politely, patiently, back down to the width of a held hand.

Wren took Juniper's hand. His palm was ink-stained even now—even after everything, the ink had gotten into the creases of him and would never fully leave, and she found she hoped it wouldn't.

"On three?" he said.

"No counting. Counting's how Corvane thinks."

So they didn't count. They just stepped, together, into the thorns.

It did not hurt. She had expected it to hurt—had braced for it the way you brace for a needle, for the toll bridge's cold hands, for every price this journey had charged—but the thorns parted around them like water around a keel, and for one suspended heartbeat Wren stood *inside* the Door, in the place between, and felt—

—felt Vesper.

Not the country. The *idea* of the country. Every road Odile had ever drawn, every song the elder had taught them in the wrong language, every name spoken twice to hold it steady against the borderlands' slow tide. All of it gathered in the dark of the gate like a congregation, and it looked at her—or she imagined it did—and it was not angry. That was the thing that undid her, standing there in the thorn-dark with her hand crushed in a scribe's ink-stained grip. It was not angry at being erased. It was *grateful* to be carried.

*Take the morning,* something said, in a voice like the bell, like bread, like green. *Take the morning and make room.*

Then they were through, and the light was grey, and the air tasted of nothing in particular, and behind them the Thorn Door was closing.

Wren turned in time to see it.

She would see it for the rest of her life, she suspected, in the space just before sleep: the blackthorn drawing shut branch by branch, patient as a book closing, and through the narrowing gaps the last of Vesper going up in gold—streets she had never walked, bells she had never heard, the whole impossible resurrection of one night folding itself away like laundry, like letters, like a flag. And in the final sliver of gap, just before the thorns met, she saw the field of Grayhollow exiles on the far side—no. No, they had come through. They were here, around her, two hundred strong in the grey grass, turning to watch their country end.

What she saw in the last sliver was the land itself. Whole. Green. Brief as a struck match.

Then the thorns touched, and there was a sound like a word being finished, and where the Door had been there was a hedge. An old, ordinary, overgrown hedge at the edge of a fallow field, in a country whose name Wren knew and suddenly hated, and it was already starting to rain.

* * *

Nobody moved for a long time.

The rain came down soft and indifferent, beading on wool, dripping off chins. The exiles stood in the wet grass looking at the hedge, and the hedge stood looking at nothing, and somewhere beyond the field's far fence a road ran east toward towns with names in the conqueror's language, toward offices that filed nations under *resolved*, toward a world that had made its arrangements and did not intend to be disturbed by two hundred people carrying a ghost.

It was one of the twins—a girl, seven years old, rain-soaked and furious—who broke the silence.

"That's it?" she demanded, of the universe generally. "We walk all that way and it's *rain*?"

A ripple went through the grown-ups. Someone laughed, wetly. Someone else started crying, which in Wren's experience were frequently the same sound wearing different coats. And then old Sefa—Sefa, who had carried the elder's songbook wrapped in oilcloth against his heart the entire march—began, quietly, to sing.

The song was in Vesperan. Of course it was. It was the one about the ferryman who rows the river of names, and the verse was the one Wren knew best because Juniper had stumbled over it for three days straight and she had corrected him nightly until he got it right:

*Row me over, row me home,*
*the water keeps what stone has lost.*
*I gave my name unto the foam*
*and counted nothing as the cost.*

Two hundred voices took it up, raggedly at first, then together, and they sang it standing in the rain in a foreign field at the edge of a closed door, and Wren understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that this was what victory sounded like when it actually arrived. Not trumpets. Not dawn breaking over restored streets. Just people, singing the wrong language in the right place, refusing to be quiet.

Juniper's hand found hers again. She hadn't noticed him letting go.

"Ten steps," he said.

"What?"

"You asked for ten steps of not knowing." He nodded past the hedge, past the field, to the road, where the rain was silvering the ruts and the world lay open and unwritten in every direction. "You've had them. We're eleven steps out, by my count, and I've been counting anyway, because someone has to and you refuse."

Despite everything—despite the ache behind her ribs where a childhood memory used to live, despite the grey sky and the dead hedge and the enormous weight of the morning—Wren laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, like a bird flushed from cover.

"All right, scribe. Eleven steps. So what now? What does a free man do at step eleven?"

And Juniper Quintana—debt-bound no longer, prophecy-amended, alive by a forged amendment in his own hand and a cartographer's paid-in-full grief—looked out at the rain and the road and the vast unarranged distance, and Wren watched him arrive at the same terrifying answer she had been circling all morning.

"I don't know," he said, wonderingly. "I genuinely don't know. I've owed someone something since I was nine years old. There was always a next entry, a next payment, a next date in a book with my name in it." He turned to her, and the rain ran down his face, and he was smiling like a man standing at the edge of a high place. "There's no entry. Wren, there's no entry for today. We're off the page."

"Off the page," she repeated.

"Completely. Unwritten."

And she thought of a compass that pointed at places that didn't exist, sold to her by a fool in a riverside market a lifetime ago; she thought of a smuggler's first rule, which was *never carry what you can't afford to lose*, and her second rule, learned too late and kept forever, which was *carry it anyway*.

"Good," she said. "I've never trusted pages. They're always telling you what happened instead of what's about to."

She pulled her coat collar up, turned to face the road, and raised her voice over the rain and the fading song.

"Grayhollow!" she called. "Eyes forward! First town's a day's walk east, and I don't know what's waiting there, and neither does anyone else, and that—" her voice caught, and she let it, and pushed through, "—that is the best news any of us has had in years. Walk!"

They walked. Two hundred and four people out of a country that no longer existed, through the rain, toward a world that would have to make room whether it liked it or not. Odile fell into step beside Wren without her atlas, sketching the road ahead on scrap paper out of pure incurable habit, and Juniper walked at the center of the column with the silent book against his chest, and the hedge grew small behind them, and the rain eased, and the road went on.

It was nearly noon when the boy came running up the column from the rear, breathless, waving a piece of paper.

He'd found it nailed to the field's boundary post, he said. Fresh. The ink still shining.

Wren took it from him, and read it, and felt the morning tilt beneath her feet.

It was written in a neat, official, Prophecy Office hand—and beneath the official hand, in cramped familiar script she knew better than her own heartbeat, someone had added a single line.

The notice declared the Thorn Door sealed, the Vesper matter closed, all persons emerging therefrom to be reported and detained for processing.

The added line said: *They're already counting. Come find me before they finish. — H.*
