## 2. The Night Without Stars

The evening prayer in the temple of Vesper began, as it always did, with the lighting of the tallow lamps and the recitation of debts.

Wendell stood in the third row of the congregation, hands folded, lips moving through words they had known since before they could read. *We owe the valley our breath. We owe the valley our labor. We owe the valley our silence.* The high priest's voice rolled down from the pulpit like slow water over smooth stones, and beneath it the congregation murmured its responses, a hundred soft voices braided into one obedient sound.

Wendell had spent the whole day copying ledgers. Their fingers still smelled of ink; there was a smudge of it along the edge of their left palm that no amount of scrubbing would lift, as though the debt records had worked their way under the skin. In the scriptorium they had transcribed forty-one names that afternoon—debts of grain, of candle-tallow, of days owed at the harvest wall—and each name had been written in the same careful hand, because Wendell Lockridge had been taught that a scribe's hand must be as impersonal as winter. No flourishes. No personality. The ledger did not care who you were. It only cared what you owed.

That was the comfort of Vesper, if you let yourself feel it as comfort. Nothing here was left to chance. The wards along the valley's rim glowed faint violet on foggy nights, a ring of quiet light that said: *nothing comes in, nothing goes out, and so nothing can hurt you.* The temple fed you. The ledger named you. The walls of your life were drawn so close you could touch them all at once, like a child sleeping in a box too small to turn around in.

Wendell had stopped turning around years ago.

They glanced sideways, as they always did during the third recitation, toward the bell tower's shadowed gallery above the nave. Maren was there. The bell-keeper never came down for prayer—the temple said her post required her, though everyone knew the truth was that the temple preferred her where the congregation could not see her too closely—but she always watched, a thin silhouette against the tower's arched window, and when Wendell looked up she lifted one hand in a small, irreverent wave.

Wendell almost smiled. Almost.

Then the stars went out.

It happened between one verse and the next. Through the great oculus in the temple roof—the Eye, the founders had called it, the window through which Vesper watched the heavens—the night sky was suddenly, impossibly blank. Not clouded. Clouds had texture, a gray-on-gray shifting, a sense of something passing. This was absence. As if someone had reached up and unpicked the stars from the dark like beads from a garment.

The murmuring faltered. Died. A hundred faces tilted upward at once.

High Priest Corvane's voice continued alone for three full verses, rich and untroubled, before he too looked up and stopped mid-word. In the sudden silence Wendell could hear the lamps hissing, and someone near the back beginning to weep in small, embarrassed gasps.

"Peace," Corvane said at last, and his voice was calm the way deep water is calm. "The heavens test us. A faithful valley does not flinch at a dark window." He raised his arms, sleeves falling back from wrists banded with silver. "We will finish the recitation. We will go home. We will bar our doors, as is prudent on any strange night, and in the morning the stars will have returned, and we will speak of this no more."

And the congregation, hungry for permission to be unafraid, obeyed. The verses resumed, shakily at first, then steadier. Wendell sang the words about owing and being kept, but their eyes stayed fixed on the black Eye overhead, and their mind kept returning to one detail, small and wrong: the last time they had seen the sky, an hour before dusk, the stars had been there. Whatever had happened had happened *between* then and now. Inside the valley's own night.

Nothing comes in, Wendell thought. So what went out?

* * *

Maren found them in the scriptorium an hour later, by lamplight, pretending to work.

"You should be home," Maren said. She leaned in the doorway with her bell-mallet still tucked in her belt, her gray hair loose, her eyes reflecting the lamp flame. "Corvane ordered the doors barred."

"So did you come to arrest me?"

"I came because you'd never actually go home." She crossed the room and dropped into the chair opposite, long legs sprawled, entirely without reverence, which was the most Maren thing about her. Outside the scriptorium's narrow window, the valley lay under a darkness deeper than any Wendell had ever seen. No moon either, they realized. The whole sky scraped clean.

"The wards," Wendell said quietly. "Have you looked at them?"

"I rang the warning bell an hour ago. Nobody asked why." Maren turned her head and spat, delicately, into the ink bucket. "I walked up to the north rim after. Close as I dared." Her jaw worked. "Wendell, the light's gone out of them. All the way around. Thirty years I've lived under that ring, and tonight it's just—stone. Cold stone."

Wendell's stomach tightened. "Corvane says the heavens are testing us."

"Corvane says whatever keeps people kneeling." Maren studied them across the lamp. "You noticed something. You've got your noticing face on. The one you think nobody can read."

Wendell hesitated. Then, because it was Maren, they said it aloud. "The stars didn't fail from outside. If something had broken through the wards—if outsiders had done something—we'd have seen it happen at the rim first. The light would have torn. Instead the sky just... emptied. From everywhere at once. Like the dark came from inside the valley and spread outward."

Maren was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its usual dryness. "From inside," she repeated. "You know what my grandmother used to say? That the wards weren't a fence. That they were a lid." She laughed, but there was no humor in it. "Old women's stories. The kind the temple burned out of the archives two hundred years ago."

"Lids keep things in," Wendell said. Neither of them moved. The lamp flame bent in a draft that should not have existed, because every door in the temple was barred, and the cold that touched it was the first true cold Wendell had ever felt in their life.

Then the world broke.

There was no better word for it. A sound came rolling down from the rim of the valley—not thunder, not an avalanche, but something higher and thinner, a vast crystalline shriek, like the whole sky being dropped onto stone. It went through the floor, through the teeth, through the chest. Every lamp in the scriptorium guttered. Somewhere below, the great temple bell swung on its own and tolled once, a single wounded note.

And through the narrow window, Wendell saw the horizon bloom violet.

The wards—flaring, all around the valley's rim at once, brighter than Wendell had ever seen them, bright enough to throw shadows across the fields—held for one heartbeat, two, three, a ring of desperate light against the dark.

Then they shattered.

Not faded. *Shattered.* The violet ring burst outward like a struck pane of glass, and for an instant Wendell could have sworn they saw the fragments—shards of light tumbling down the mountainsides, winking out as they fell. The shriek climbed to a pitch beyond hearing and cut off, and the silence afterward was worse than the sound, because it was the silence of something that had made noise for three hundred years and would never make noise again.

Then the cold arrived.

It poured into the valley like water into a cracked bowl. Wendell felt it through the stone walls, through wool and skin, a cold with intent behind it, a cold that had been waiting. Frost bloomed across the inside of the scriptorium window in feathered patterns, growing even as Wendell watched, and beyond the glass the first snow was already falling—dry, fine, glittering strangely in the last violet afterglow, snow that fell straight down without wind, as if the air itself were afraid to move.

"People," Maren breathed. "The harvest villages. They'll be running for the temple."

"They'll freeze on the roads." Wendell was already moving, snatching cloaks, their body acting on some instinct older than obedience. "Ring the bell again—all of them, the alarm pattern, get everyone moving toward the temple fires—"

"And say what? Corvane will call it panic-mongering."

"Then let him call it that while he stands somewhere warm!" The words came out of Wendell sharper than anything they had ever said in this building, in this life, and Maren blinked at them, and then grinned—a fierce, frightened grin—and ran for the stairs.

* * *

By midnight the temple nave held half the valley.

Fires roared in braziers meant for candles. Farmers huddled with their children wrapped in quilts and tablecloths; the weaver Odile had carried her loom-shuttle and nothing else and could not explain why; old Tam Voss sat by the fire telling anyone who would listen that he had seen the wards flare once before, sixty years ago, just a flicker, and the priests had told him he'd dreamed it. Snow drifted in through the doors each time they opened, piling in white ridges on the threshold stones, and no one could remember snow. There had never been snow in Vesper. The wards kept the seasons gentle; that was simply what the wards *did*, the way the temple fed the poor and the ledger named the indebted. The world outside was a story told to children. The world outside had just come inside.

Corvane stood on the pulpit steps in his full vestments, silver-banded, unmoved by the cold in a way that made the crowd press toward him. His voice carried over the fear like a blade laid flat across water.

"This is the work of outsiders," he said. "Heretics beyond the rim who have envied our peace, who have gnawed at the wards for generations. Tonight they succeeded. Tonight they have stolen your stars and broken your shield." He let the murmur of anger build before cutting it off with one raised hand. "But hear me: Vesper has survived worse than jealous neighbors. We will endure this winter as we have endured everything—by holding together, by keeping faith, by *paying what we owe.*"

Wendell, standing at the edge of the crowd with Maren, felt those last words land strangely, like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was there.

"Outsiders," Maren muttered. "Convenient. Can't interrogate a sky, but you can hate a stranger."

But Wendell wasn't listening anymore. They were looking at the frost on the great doors—at the shattered ring's afterimage still ghosting their vision—and thinking about the direction of the breaking. Everyone had heard it roll down from the rim, and everyone assumed it had come from beyond the rim, from the heretics Corvane named. But Wendell had been standing in the scriptorium, facing north, and they had seen the wards die in sequence. South first. Then east and west, a heartbeat later. North last, like an echo arriving late.

Like a crack starting at the center of a pane and racing outward to the edges.

The wards hadn't been broken from outside. Something had pushed from within—from the heart of the valley itself—and the rim had simply given way.

Wendell turned to tell Maren, and stopped, because Maren had gone very pale and very still, staring not at Corvane but at the floor of the nave, at the flagstones beneath the altar dais, where—faint, rhythmic, impossible—a seam of violet light was glowing through the stone. Pulsing. Slowly. Like a heartbeat.

"Wendell," Maren whispered. "What's under the temple?"
