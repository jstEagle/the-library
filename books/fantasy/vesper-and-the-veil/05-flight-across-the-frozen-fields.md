## 5. Flight Across the Frozen Fields

**Wendell**

The snow came down like ash from a fire no one had lit.

Wendell ran with Maren's hand locked around their wrist, boots punching through the crusted drifts of the barley fields, and every breath was a knife swallowed sideways. Behind them, the temple bells were ringing—not the measured hours, not the call to prayer, but the broken, frantic clanging of someone hauling the ropes with both fists and no rhythm at all. Alarm. Heresy. *Run.*

They had run before, in dreams. Everyone in Vesper had. But in the dreams there was always a door at the edge of the valley, and here there was only the border ridge, black and distant, and between them and it lay four miles of fields going white under a sky that had forgotten how to be a sky.

"Left," Maren gasped. "The drainage cut—follow it, they'll ride the high road."

Wendell swerved. The cut was a shallow trench where summer meltwater used to run toward the millrace; now it was a rib of ice, and they slipped along it half-falling, half-flying, the world reduced to the slap of frozen stalks against their cheeks and the bell-sound fading behind them into something almost merciful.

Almost. Because under the bells there was another sound now, one Wendell had been trying not to name since the wards shattered. A whispering. Like wind through a keyhole, except it came from everywhere, and it did not move when the wind moved.

"Maren." Their voice came out in shreds. "The things you told me. In the archive. The things the wards kept *in*—"

"Don't."

"But I can hear them."

Maren stopped so abruptly that Wendell stumbled into her back. She turned, and in the flat grey light her face looked carved out of tallow, older than her thirty years, older than the valley itself. Snow had gathered in the hollows of her eyes.

"I know," she said quietly. "I've heard them since the glass broke. That's why we keep moving. They find stillness interesting." She squeezed Wendell's wrist once, hard, and let go. "Come on. The watchtower's close. Old Ellery's tower, on the mill road. It has walls. Walls matter now."

*Walls matter now.* Wendell wanted to laugh and could not find the air for it. All their life, walls had been the whole of the world—the valley rim, the wardline, the temple's whitewashed corridors, the ledger's ruled margins. They had never once thought of walls as something you could be *outside* of.

The snow thickened. Somewhere to the north, a sound rolled across the fields that was not thunder, because thunder does not pause, does not listen. Wendell did not look back. Looking back felt like an invitation.

* * *

**Maren**

Maren counted their footsteps because counting was a prayer she still believed in, and because if she stopped counting she would start listening to the whispering, and if she started listening she would start answering.

Forty years in the bell tower had taught her what most of Vesper preferred not to know: that silence is never empty. She had spent decades listening to the space between the bells, and in that space she had found the archive, and in the archive she had found the truth that now chased them across the freezing fields alongside Corvane's riders.

*The valley is a mouth,* the oldest document said, in ink gone brown as dried blood. *The wards are its teeth.*

She had read that line a hundred times over the years and always flinched from it. Now she understood the flinch for what it was: the body knowing before the mind agreed.

"There." Wendell pointed with a mittened hand, and Maren followed the gesture to the watchtower rising out of the whiteness like a broken tooth. Old Ellery's tower—built, the histories claimed, by the first generation, though the histories claimed everything about the founders and explained nothing. It had stood empty since before Maren's grandmother. Its roof had fallen inward; its single window gaped like a socket.

But it had stone walls three feet thick, and a floor below ground, and right now Maren would have traded her remaining years for less than that.

They reached it as the light began to fail—which was wrong, because the light should have failed hours ago, the days shortening unnaturally since the stars went out, winter arriving like an army rather than a season. The doorway had no door, only a curtain of frost-hair hanging from the lintel. Maren pushed through it and felt the temperature drop and then steady, the stone holding the deep cold of the earth instead of the killing cold of the sky.

Inside: rubble, a rotted ladder, the black square of the cellar stair. And on the wall beside the stair, half-hidden under lichen, carvings. Maren brushed the growth away with her thumb and her stomach dropped through the floor.

Spirals. The same spirals from the founding contract, the ones the archive called *the Veil's signature*. Cut deep into the stone by hands that must have been shaking.

"Wendell," she said. "Look at this."

But Wendell had gone still by the window, head tilted, listening—and Maren saw their expression change into something she had never seen there in all their years of friendship. Not fear. Recognition.

* * *

**Wendell**

The voice came like water finding a crack.

*Is someone there?*

Wendell pressed both hands over their ears and it changed nothing, because the voice was not coming through the air. It was coming up through them, through the blood the founding contract had written their family name into, through whatever thread had been tied three hundred years ago and had never quite frayed.

*Please. Is someone— I heard the wards break. Three hundred years I have listened for that sound.*

"Who's there?" Wendell whispered aloud, and hated how small it sounded.

Beside them, Maren was reading the spiral carvings with her fingers, murmuring translations under her breath, and Wendell understood with a lurch that Maren could not hear it. Whatever spoke, it spoke only to them. To the blood.

*I am Aldric.* The voice was thin as winter light, worn smooth as a river stone, and underneath the strangeness of it—underneath the impossible fact of a voice inside their skull—there was something so plainly, achingly human that Wendell's throat closed. *I was king of this valley when it was not yet a valley. I am the thing your temple prays against. And I am so tired. I have been so tired for so long.*

"You're the Veil," Wendell breathed. "You're what the wards hold."

A pause. When the voice came again it was smaller, and it shook.

*No. The Veil wears me. There is a difference, though I understand why no one left alive would believe it. Your founders took a dying king and a hungry god and sewed them together with a scribe's contract, and they told themselves it was mercy. It was arithmetic. One life to buy ten thousand.* Another pause, longer. *Your name. What is your name? The contract had a name on it. The scribe. Lockridge. Are you—*

Wendell's knees gave. They sat down hard on the frozen rubble, snow melting against their palms, and Maren turned sharply. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"It knows my name," Wendell said. "It—it knew my grandmother's name. It's been alone in the dark for three hundred years and the first thing it asked about was the scribe."

Below the fear, below the absurdity, something else was opening in Wendell's chest, warm and terrible: pity. Pity for the monster in the cage. Or the man in the monster. They could no longer find the seam between them, and suspected no one ever had.

*They will come for you,* Aldric said, and now the weakness in his voice curdled into urgency. *The priest. He has read the same contract you have. He knows what your blood can do. Child, listen to me—he does not want to free me and he does not want to keep me. He wants to use you. Run from him. Whatever he offers you, whatever absolution, whatever peace—run.*

Hoofbeats. Faint, but real, carrying over the frozen fields from the direction of the mill road.

Maren's head snapped up. "Cellar," she said. "Now."

* * *

**Wendell**

The cellar was a stone throat, cold enough to make their breath fall as glitter. They wedged themselves beneath the collapsed staircase, Maren's shoulder against theirs, and above them the hoofbeats grew, circled, and stopped.

Voices. Torches bleeding orange through the frost-curtained window.

"They came this way," someone said. "Tracks end at the tower."

"Then they're in it." A third voice, dry and unhurried, and Wendell's blood turned to well-water. Corvane. The high priest had come himself. "Brother Anselm, watch the road. The rest of you—no torches inside. We do not want to burn what we need."

*Maren.* Wendell found her hand in the dark and gripped it. Her fingers were ice and they gripped back anyway, fierce as a vow.

Footsteps on rubble, directly overhead. Dust sifted down through the broken ladder-hole, catching the torchlight like falling stars—like the stars that no longer existed anywhere in the sky.

"Wendell Lockridge," Corvane called out, conversational, almost gentle, the voice he used at funerals. "I know you can hear me. You were a good copyist. Careful. Obedient. I never once had to correct your hand." A pause, and Wendell could picture him exactly: hands folded, head bowed, the very portrait of sorrow. "Whatever the archivist told you, whatever lies live in those forbidden pages—I forgive you. Both of you. Come home, and all debts are struck from the book. Every entry. Clean pages. Is that not what you have wanted your whole life?"

*Don't,* whispered Aldric, faint now, stretched thin. *He will take it unwilling. Unwilling blood makes the binding stronger, and it makes it crueler, and you will be the nail, child, you will be hammered in forever—*

Wendell stared into the dark and felt the strange double weight of it: the priest above offering them erasure, the king within begging them to remain themselves. Between those two hung the whole shape of their life, and for the first time they saw it clearly—not a shelter, but a ledger, and every kind word in it an entry marked *owed*.

Maren's lips moved against their ear, barely a breath: "We don't answer. We wait for dark. Then the drainage cut, then the ridge."

Above them, Corvane waited too. Patient. Certain. The way a man is certain who has never once been told no.

And in the marrow of Wendell's bones, soft as snowfall, the dying king began to speak again—not begging now, but teaching, syllable by syllable, words old enough to predate the temple, old enough to unmake it:

*If it comes to the worst, child, remember: the contract cuts both ways. Blood freely given opens the lock. Now listen, and I will teach you the words.*
