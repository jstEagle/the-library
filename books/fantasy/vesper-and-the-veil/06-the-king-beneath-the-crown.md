## 6. The King Beneath the Crown

He had been a man once. He held to that.

Aldric Vael, third of his name, crowned at nineteen in a hall that smelled of beeswax and pine smoke, with his mother's ring too loose on his finger because grief had worn her hands to bone before it took her entirely. He had been a man once. He repeated it the way a drowning sailor repeats his own name—not for information but for anchor. Because down here, in the place without light or season or distance, names were the only things that kept their shape.

The rest of him did not.

* * *

Time inside the Veil was not a river. It was a tide pool, evaporating and refilling with rain that never fell. Aldric measured it in fragments—memories surfacing like drowned leaves, growing waterlogged, sinking again.

He remembered the last morning of his humanity with terrible clarity. The plague had come up the river roads, and his physicians had burned their own instruments rather than touch the sick, and his kingdom—the kingdom he loved the way you love a limb, without deciding to, without being able to stop—had begun to whisper the old word. *Sacrifice.* Not the clean kind. The kind the founders of Vesper had practiced before they were founders, when they were only desperate people on a cold hillside with a dying king and an entity that fed on endings.

They had told him it would be quick. They had told him he would be honored. His own brother had held the chalice, weeping, and Aldric had drunk because a king who refuses to save his people is no king at all, and because he was already dying, and because love is the most efficient lie ever devised.

Then the words came. The binding words. Spoken by a scribe whose name the contract recorded in careful ink—a scribe whose descendants, Aldric knew now, still lived in the valley that grew over his grave like moss over a fallen tree.

And the Veil had come into him like winter coming into a house through every crack at once.

* * *

Three hundred years. Perhaps more. The number had stopped meaning anything around the second century, when he realized he could no longer remember his wife's face, only the fact that she had one, only the fact that he had loved it.

That was how the Veil fed. Not on blood, not on breath. On *shape*. It wore him the way a river wears a stone, patiently, without malice, until what remained was smooth and featureless and fit perfectly into whatever hollow it wished to fill. And what it wished to fill, always, was the valley. The wards sang against it day and night—a sound he felt as pressure behind his eyes, a hymn with no mercy in it—and the Veil pressed against them the way a tongue presses a broken tooth. Testing. Waiting. Learning the shape of its cage by wearing the shape of the man who was its cage.

Because that was the cruelty at the center of everything: they had not locked the Veil away from the world. They had locked it into *him*. The wards did not hold a monster in a pit. They held a king in his own skin, and the skin was failing.

* * *

Sometimes, in the long dark, he dreamed himself back into the human world. These dreams were the only mercy the Veil allowed, perhaps because they made him softer, easier to wear.

In the best of them he was in the temple of Vesper before it was a temple—when it was only a meeting hall with a crooked door—and Maren's grandmother's grandmother was arguing with the first high priest about whether the wards should be inscribed with the truth or with a merciful lie. Aldric had stood in that room, newly bound, barely able to speak through the thing coiled in his chest, and heard the priest say: *They will not follow rules built on horror. Give them debt instead. Debt is a horror that feels like order.*

And the scribe—young, ink-stained, exhausted—had written it all down. Every clause. Every condition. Including the one at the bottom, in smaller script, almost apologetic:

*The binding may be undone only by blood freely given, not taken; by a hand that opens rather than a hand that closes; by consent, which is the one coin the Veil cannot counterfeit.*

The founders had left the door unlocked. They simply never told anyone where it was, or that it existed, because a seal everyone knows can be opened is no seal at all. So they buried the truth under ledgers, under tithes, under three centuries of small debts, and the key passed down through the scribe's bloodline like a heirloom nobody remembered owning.

Aldric had felt it wake. Recently. A warmth moving through the ward-song, faint as a candle carried across a frozen field. Someone with the old blood had touched the founding pages. Someone was reading.

*Please,* he thought toward that warmth, not knowing if thought could travel. *Please read to the end.*

* * *

Other times the dreams were not mercies at all. Other times the Veil let him see through his own eyes while it walked him, and he watched his hands—his mother's ring long since absorbed, his knuckles gone smooth as river stones—do things he would burn kingdoms to undo.

It had learned to use his voice. That was worst of all. In the early years it had spoken only in silence, but voices are shapes too, and it wore his the way it wore everything: badly at first, then better, then perfectly. Now, when the wards thinned, it pressed his voice against them like a mouth against a window.

*I am your king,* it said to the valley, in his voice, with his grief, using memories it had stripped from him like bark from a birch. *I am cold. I am sorry. Let me in.*

And the people of Vesper, who had been taught for three hundred years that their king was a story, a warning, a bedtime terror—those people heard a dead man begging at their walls, and some of them, Aldric knew, some of them wanted to open the door.

He screamed against it. He screamed the way he had screamed the first century, when screaming still changed anything. It did nothing. Grief, he had learned, is just another shape, and the Veil wears grief more beautifully than anything alive ever could.

* * *

But lately—lately something had changed.

The wards had cracked. He had felt it happen: a sound like breaking glass translated into pressure, into cold pouring inward, into the Veil rising off him like steam off a wound. For one wild moment Aldric had thought *freedom*, and then understood, with a despair older than the valley itself, that freedom for the Veil was not freedom for him. If it reached the valley unfed, unbound, unworn, it would consume everything that remembered him—including, eventually, the memory of Aldric Vael himself. He would not even be a ghost. He would be a hunger with no history.

So he did the only thing left to a man who has been erased: he hid pieces of himself where the Veil did not think to look.

It watched his mind for escape plans, his heart for rebellion, his voice for lies. But it did not watch his grief. Grief was its food, its instrument, its favorite costume. It never imagined grief could also be a message.

Into his grief, then, he folded the truth, the way his mother had once folded notes into the linings of his coats: *the binding can be undone; willing blood, freely given; a hand that opens; consent, the one coin it cannot counterfeit.* He pushed the fragments outward along the crack in the wards, toward the warmth he had felt, toward the old blood, toward whoever was listening on the other side of three hundred years.

*If you are the scribe's child,* he thought, *then you are the lock and the key both, and I am sorry—I have been sorry for three centuries—for what that will cost you.*

*Do not let them take it from you. That is the trap within the trap. Blood taken renews the binding. Blood given ends it. They will try to take.*

*Choose. Please. Whatever else this valley has stolen from you—choose.*

* * *

On the far side of the dark, muffled by wards half-broken and a winter falling like a verdict, Aldric felt the warmth answer.

Not words. Not yet. Just a presence, small and stubborn and terrified, sheltering in a ruined watchtower at the valley's edge—a girl-shaped warmth with ledger-ink on her fingers and a bell-keeper beside her, and around them both, closing in through the snow, the patient hunger of the thing that wore his face.

The Veil stirred beneath him, gathering itself, sliding into his posture, his gait, his voice. It was going to the tower. It was going to speak to them in a dead king's borrowed sorrow, and it would be so convincing, because every word of its grief would be true.

Aldric sank his remaining self into the fragments, into the folded grief, and sent one last message down the crack in the world:

*When you hear my voice at your door—it will not be me. But the words I gave you are me. Trust the words. Not the voice.*

*And whatever it begs for—*

Beneath him, the Veil smiled with his mouth, and began to walk through the snow toward the watchtower where Wendell Lockridge slept.
