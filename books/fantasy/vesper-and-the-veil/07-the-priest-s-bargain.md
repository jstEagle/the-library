## 7. The Priest's Bargain

Corvane had not slept in three days, and the temple had begun to speak to him.

He knew it was exhaustion. He knew it was the cold seeping through stone that had never before known winter, frost blooming in the joints of pillars like white lichen, the braziers burning through their stores of cedar at a rate the storekeepers reported with trembling hands. He knew all of this and yet when he walked the colonnade at night, alone, his breath hanging before him like a small ghost, he heard the walls murmuring. Not words. Something older than words. A hunger with a grammar.

The Veil was awake beneath them. He could feel it the way a man feels a rotten tooth—not pain exactly, but attention.

He stopped in the Hall of Ledgers, where the great books lay open on their chains, and set his palm flat against the founding contract's glass case. The vellum inside was brown as autumn. Three centuries of dust and reverence, and beneath it, in faded iron-gall ink, the signature of a scribe named Lockridge.

*Blood of the hand that wrote may renew what was written.*

Corvane had read that line forty times since Maren's treachery came to light—since the girl had broken into the sealed archive and the boy Wendell had fled into the frozen fields with her. Forty times, and each reading the line seemed less like scripture and more like an instruction left by a careful man for a desperate one.

They would come back. Corvane was almost certain of it. Fugitives lasted poorly in a valley turning to winter, and the boy—Wendell had spent nine years copying ledgers in this temple, ink-stained and obedient, flinching at raised voices. Such creatures did not run far. They ran to the edge of their courage and then they looked back at the warm place behind them.

Corvane intended to be standing in the warm place when he did.

* * *

They came on the fourth night, half-frozen, escorted by the two hunters Corvane had sent to find them—not dragged, he had been specific about that, but *found*, offered shelter, offered fire. It was important that the boy walk in on his own feet. Everything depended on what the boy believed he was choosing.

Corvane received them in the small chapel behind the sanctuary, the one with the hearth, because he wanted Wendell to feel warmth on his face while they talked. The bell-keeper Maren stood in the doorway with frost melting in her grey hair, glaring at him with the flat hatred of someone who had read the archives and understood what he was. Let her glare. She was a footnote. The boy was the page.

"Sit," Corvane said gently, and poured the wine himself, warming it with the poker first, watching Wendell's chapped hands close around the cup as if around a rescued bird. "You've been in the cold four nights. I'm sorry for that. I sent men to bring you home, not to harm you."

"You sent men to drag me to an altar," Wendell said. His voice was hoarse but steady. Corvane noted the steadiness and adjusted his approach accordingly. This was not the flinching creature from the scriptorium. The fields had changed him. Or the voice in his head had—the king's voice, if the old accounts were true. That was a complication.

"I sent men to find you before something else did." Corvane sat across from him, folding his hands, letting the silence do some of his work. Outside, wind moved through the dead orchards with a sound like sand through an hourglass. "You've seen them by now. The things in the snow. You know the wards are down. Do you know why?"

"Maren told me."

"Maren told you a story." Corvane let weariness soften his face, and it was not entirely feigned; he was tired down to the marrow. "A story with a monster in it and a villain, and she cast me in the villain's part, which is flattering, actually, because villains in stories are always the only ones who understand the stakes. Yes, Wendell. There is something beneath us. Yes, the founders bound it. And yes—" he leaned forward, "—your ancestor wrote the binding. Your blood is the key. All of that is true. Now let me tell you the part she left out."

Wendell said nothing. Maren shifted in the doorway. Corvane continued.

"The binding is failing. Not because of anything we did—because three hundred years is simply what a thing like that lasts. When it fails completely, the Veil comes through whole, wearing whatever is left of its king, and it will drink this valley to the stones. Every soul. Every field. Every memory of every name in those ledgers out there. That is the future Maren offers you by running. That is the future of honesty, if you like. Honest, and dead."

"And your alternative?" Wendell asked quietly.

"A new binding. Renewed while the old one still has strength enough to anchor it. Performed at the high altar, with the proper rites, using blood of the founding line." Corvane held the boy's gaze. He did not look away and he did not soften it further; there was a kind of mercy in plainness, at this stage. "Your blood, Wendell."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, in the dark of the sanctuary, a beam settled with a groan, and all three of them pretended not to hear it.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will have you held, and the rite performed anyway, and I will spend the rest of my life praying forgiveness for it." Corvane said it without apology, because apology would have cheapened it. "But I am not asking you to refuse or consent. I am asking you to listen to what I offer."

He rose and went to the writing desk in the corner, where the document had waited for two days under a cloth. He brought it back and laid it before Wendell: a single page, sealed with the temple's sun-and-veil sigil in wax the color of dried blood.

"Absolution," Corvane said. "Total. Every debt carried by every soul in this valley—struck from the ledgers. Nine years of your own servitude, erased. Your mother's debts, your grandmother's, back to the founding. No one in Vesper will owe anyone anything ever again. The Ledger of Small Debts will be burned in the same fire that seals the binding, and I will stand before the community and confess that the debt system was a corruption, that no god ever demanded it, that it was men who built it and men who profited. You will not be a sacrifice, Wendell. You will be a redeemer. They will sing your name for as long as there is anyone left to sing."

Maren made a sound in the doorway—half laugh, half sob. "And the valley stays walled. Forever. Behind new wards, starving slowly, generation after generation buried in snow, because you can't bear to tell them the truth—that there's a whole world outside and it's been waiting for them."

"The world outside," Corvane said, "is currently eating people in the orchards. I have seen the bodies, Maren. Have you?" He turned back to Wendell, and here he let his voice drop, let it go quiet and human, because this was the truest thing he would say tonight. "I am an old man. I will die behind these walls either way, seal or no seal. I gain nothing from this bargain except a valley that survives me. I am asking you to save eight hundred lives at the cost of one—and the one is yours, freely named, freely honored. Name me the man who wouldn't make that trade, and I'll call him wise and watch him be mourned."

Wendell looked down at the page. For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind and, very faintly, from somewhere below the flagstones, a rhythm like breathing that was not any of theirs.

Corvane watched the boy's face the way he had once watched ledgers: for the small tremors that betrayed the true sum underneath. Fear, yes. Grief. And beneath both—he saw it, and his heart lifted despite everything—a terrible, exhausted longing. The longing of a debtor shown a clean page.

"You'd really burn the ledgers," Wendell said. "All of them. Everyone walks free."

"On my office and my life."

"And the valley lives. The children live."

"The children live," Corvane said. "That is the entire point of everything I have done, everything I am, and everything I will burn for afterward. Yes."

Wendell closed his eyes. When he opened them, something in them had gone still and settled, like water after a stone.

"All right," he said. "I'll do it. Freely. On one condition."

"Name it."

"Maren goes free too. Full absolution. She walks out of this temple tonight and no one touches her."

Corvane inclined his head, gracious, magnanimous, already composing the announcement. "Done."

Maren stared at Wendell with an expression Corvane could not read and did not try to. She opened her mouth—closed it—then said, very softly, so that only the fire and Corvane heard: "Wendell. Whatever you think you're doing. Don't."

"It's all right," Wendell said, and smiled at her, and it was the old smile from the scriptorium, the shy one, the one that used to mean *I saved you the last of the lamp oil*. "It's going to be all right."

Corvane felt the relief move through him like warm wine. He gave orders—quarters for the boy, a bath, food, the rite at dawn—and when they had been taken away, he stood alone in the chapel with the signed absolution glowing faintly in the firelight, and permitted himself, for the first time in three days, to breathe.

It was only later, much later, kneeling at the altar rail in the freezing sanctuary while below him the hungry thing turned over in its sleep, that he remembered the detail. Small. Insignificant. The way Wendell had asked for Maren's freedom *first*, before his own name was even dry on the page—as though the boy were settling accounts.

As though he were putting his affairs in order.

Corvane rose, uneasy, and went to check the reliquary where the founding contract lay under glass.

The case was locked. The vellum inside was intact. But the iron-gall ink of the oldest line—the line about willing blood—had faded to nothing, and in its place, in fresh brown-black strokes no scribe had written, new words were drying on the three-hundred-year-old skin:

*Given, not taken. The door opens from within.*

Below the sanctuary, something began, very softly, to laugh.
