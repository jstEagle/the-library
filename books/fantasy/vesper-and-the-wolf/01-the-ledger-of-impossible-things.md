## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The vial arrived at the ninth bell, wrapped in oilcloth and carried by a courier who would not meet Silas Fairweather's eyes.

That was normal. Most couriers wouldn't. The Vesper Guild's counting house sat at the end of Candlemaker's Row, behind a door of black oak with no sign and no knocker, and the people who came to it brought things that made their hands shake: a ring containing someone else's luck, a letter written in a dead man's hand, a jar of laughter harvested from a funeral where nobody had liked the deceased. They came because the Guild paid fairly, asked nothing, and told less.

Silas set the vial on her scale, unwrapped it, and looked at what was inside without touching it.

Grief. Bottled grief, if the seller's claim held true—and the seller's claims always held true, that was the one courtesy the trade extended to itself. The liquid inside was not quite liquid. It moved the way smoke moves underwater, slow and reluctant, a grey so deep it looked like the memory of a color rather than a color itself. When she tilted the vial two degrees, the grief pooled against the glass and stayed there, clinging, as though it had learned somewhere that letting go was not something it knew how to do.

She weighed it first. Grief ran heavy—always had, in her experience of ledgers if not of life. Then she held the vial up to the lamp and counted the striations in the smoke, each band a separate mourning laid down like sediment. Seven bands. Someone had grieved seven distinct times into this vessel, or one person had grieved once with terrible thoroughness.

"Provenance?" she said, without looking up.

The courier shifted his weight. "Seller declines."

"Condition?"

"Unopened. Sealed by—" He hesitated, which was also normal. "By a priest, they said. A real one. Old rite."

Silas wrote *vial, grief, sevenfold, sealed* in the ledger in her small exact hand, and beneath it she wrote the price, which was high enough to make the courier's breath catch and low enough that the Guild would triple it before the week was out. She did not ask where the grief had come from. That was the rule, hers before it was the Guild's, and she enforced it on herself more strictly than on anyone: *never ask where goods come from.* Asking was how appraisers started wondering about the people attached to the goods, and wondering was how appraisers stopped being precise, and precision was the only thing Silas Fairweather had ever been praised for, back when anyone had praised her for anything.

She corked the vial in wax, shelved it between a phial of borrowed courage and a box of names that had never been spoken aloud, and turned to the day's real work: balancing the ledgers.

There were eleven of them. One for each category of impossible thing the Guild traded, plus a twelfth, locked, that only she and the Guildmaster could open. Silas worked through them in order, quill moving in strokes so uniform you could have measured them with a ruler. Debts owed. Debts paid. Interest accrued on a decade of sleep sold to an insomniac count. A standing order for bottled springwater from a river that had dried up four hundred years ago—the water remembered being a river, and wealthy clients drank it to dream of valleys.

Her left forearm ached faintly, the way it did when the weather turned or when she worked too long without thinking about anything else. She rolled her sleeve down over the scars and did not look at them. She never looked at them. Three pale lines crossing the inside of her wrist and forearm, old as her earliest memory, smooth as candlewax. The physician her family—her *former* family; there was a thought she kept in the same locked drawer as the twelfth ledger—had hired when she was a child had called them surgical. Neat. Deliberate. Made by a blade held by someone who knew exactly what they were doing.

Silas had decided long ago that some questions were just the rule against asking, pointed inward.

She was halfway through the fifth ledger when the client arrived.

He came quietly, which distinguished him from most clients, who came loudly in their fear. A merchant by his coat, good wool, city-cut, though the coat had been brushed hard to remove travel dust and hadn't entirely forgiven him for the journey. He carried a reliquary the size of a prayer book, silver-cased, and he set it on her counter with both hands, gently, the way you set down something sleeping.

"I'm told the Guild buys relics," he said.

"The Guild buys everything." Silas opened the reliquary. Inside, on faded velvet, lay a sliver of bone the length of her smallest finger, browned with age, drilled through with a hole for suspension. Old work. Very old. Older than the case pretending to hold it.

"Whose bone?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters for the price." It didn't, really—she'd price it by age and resonance either way—but the question sometimes shook loose what the seller actually knew, and what sellers actually knew was often more interesting than what they claimed.

The merchant wet his lips. "They say it's from the Godswar. A saint of the old days. My grandfather bought it off a pilgrim road and my father swore it was nothing and I've sworn it was nothing, but lately—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Lately things have been *happening* around it. Small things. Candles burning steady in drafts. My daughter's fever breaking the same hour I prayed over it—I know how that sounds, I'm not a praying man, I've never—"

"You're not selling me a miracle," Silas said. "You're selling me a bone."

"Yes," he said. "Yes. Of course."

But his hands were still on the reliquary, and when she reached to close the lid, he didn't move them. And then, in the way that exhausted men sometimes forget themselves entirely, he bowed his head over the relic and spoke—not to her, not to the room, but downward, into the silver case, in a voice worn smooth from disuse:

"*If you're in there—if any of you are in there—my daughter is sick again. The fever came back. I'll give anything. Do you hear me? Anything.*"

The lamp flame stood perfectly still.

Not steadily. *Still.* In a counting house with three doors and a draft that lived in the walls and a courier who hadn't yet found the courage to leave, in a city where every flame bent toward its own death eventually, the lamp burned straight up like a drawn blade.

Silas felt the ache in her forearm turn to heat.

She looked down at the ledger open before her, at the entry she had been writing—*reliquary, bone fragment, provenance declined*—and watched the ink finish drying. Except it wasn't drying. The letters were darkening, drinking the lamplight, going from brown-black to a black that had depth to it, that had *appetite*, until the words on the page looked less like writing than like holes cut in the paper with something showing through from underneath.

And then, below her finished line, in ink that no pen had touched, new letters began to surface. Slowly. Like bodies rising in water.

*HEARD.*

The merchant lifted his head. His face had changed—washed clean of exhaustion, lit from somewhere behind the eyes with a gratitude so vast it looked, on him, almost like terror. "It answered," he whispered. "Oh gods. Oh, *old* gods. It answered."

"Get out," Silas said.

He went, clutching the reliquary, running, and the courier had already gone, and the counting house was silent except for the lamp, still burning straight as a sword.

Silas looked at her own hand holding the quill. Steady. Her hand was always steady; it was the one thing about herself she had never had cause to doubt. But under the rolled sleeve, the three scars were burning now, all three lines at once, hot as fresh iron, and twenty years of silence had ended without asking her permission.

On the page, the ink was still moving. More letters rising now, crowding, eager, filling the margin beneath her neat entry in a script older than any alphabet the Guild traded in—a script she found, to her horror, that her eyes could read.

*THIRTEEN TURNS. THE LEDGER OPENS. SHE WHO WROTE THE FIRST ENTRY WILL WRITE THE LAST.*

Silas Fairweather sat alone in the counting house of the Vesper Guild, in the city she had protected for twenty years without knowing why, and read what the ledger wrote back.

Then she got up, took the twelfth ledger from its lock, and broke her own rule for the first time in her career.

She asked where the goods came from.
