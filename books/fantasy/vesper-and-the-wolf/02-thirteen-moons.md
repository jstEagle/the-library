## 2. Thirteen Moons

The Guildmaster kept a clock that didn't work, and he wound it every morning anyway.

Silas had asked him once why. He'd said, *Because the guild trades in impossible things, and I like to keep one habit that's merely stupid.* She'd laughed then. This morning she watched him wind it and felt nothing but the cold arithmetic of the ledger still inked into her mind—*one prayer answered, one entry unmade, one debt incurred by no hand we know.*

"Sit," the Guildmaster said.

She sat. His office sat above the counting house, all dark wood and darker glass, and through the floorboards she could hear the clerks below arguing about the vial of grief—the one from yesterday, quarantined in a lead box because its contents had begun to *weep*. Condensation on the inside of the glass. A sound, if you held it to your ear, like someone trying very hard not to cry.

"You slept," the Guildmaster observed.

"Barely."

"And the ledger?"

"The black page hasn't faded. It hasn't spread either. I checked at dawn." She set her hands flat on her knees to keep them still. "The entry for the grief-vial is gone. Not crossed out. Gone. The paper where it was written is smooth as a child's cheek."

The Guildmaster wound the dead clock another quarter-turn. "And the client?"

"Herrin Vole. Sold us a relic he swore came from his grandmother's estate. A prayer-token, pre-war make. He prayed over it while I appraised it. Out loud. Old words." She recited them without meaning to; they'd lodged in her head like a splinter. "*Vaul who hungers, Vaul who keeps, hear the one who sows and reaps.*"

Something moved behind the Guildmaster's eyes—quick, controlled, gone. Silas had spent twenty years reading faces across an appraisal table. She knew what a flinch looked like even when it was buried alive.

"You know that litany," she said.

"I know *clients*, Fairweather, and clients panic. That's your problem now." He set the clock key down with a small, deliberate click. "Word of yesterday's—event—is already moving through the lower city. If our people believe prayers are being answered again, they'll stop trading in bottled grief and start pouring their own out onto any shrine they can find. Do you understand what that does to our inventory? To our *prices*? Panic is bad for business. Hope is worse."

"Hope," Silas repeated.

"Hope makes people generous. Generosity is a rounding error." He leaned forward. "Find out what answered Herrin Vole. Find out whether it's going to answer anyone else. Quietly. No clerks, no ledgers, no guild seal. You were a swordspeaker once—" her scars pulled tight under her sleeves, as if the old wounds had heard him say it— "which means you know how to walk into a room and have people tell you things they shouldn't. Use it."

"And if something *is* answering?"

The Guildmaster smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him. "Then appraise it."

* * *

The exiled astronomer lived at the top of the Saltspire, in the tower the universities had condemned forty years ago and never gotten around to demolishing. Silas climbed two hundred and eleven steps, counted automatically, and knocked on a door painted with a fading diagram of the heavens—or possibly of an onion. With astronomers it was hard to say.

Maren Oduya opened the door before she touched it. She was small and brown and sharp as a compass needle, with ink stains up both forearms like failed tattoos.

"Swordspeaker," she said.

"Retired."

"There's no such thing. Come in before the stairs hear you."

The tower room was a chaos of brass and paper. Charts covered every wall, layered so deep that the oldest ones—pre-war star tables, worth more than the building—served as wallpaper. Maren cleared a chair by lifting an orrery off it and setting it on the floor, where it continued turning with a soft mechanical patience.

"You got my letter," Silas said.

"I got your letter three years ago asking me to send word if anything in the sky went wrong." Maren dropped into her own chair and laced her fingers over one knee. "Three years, nothing. Then four nights ago, everything. Sit. Drink. Don't touch the astrolabe, it's mid-calculation and it bites."

Silas sat. Didn't drink. "Tell me."

Maren pulled a chart from the wall—a fresh one, the ink barely dry—and spread it across the cluttered table between them. The moon's path was drawn in silver, month by month, thirteen arcs curving around a central point like a snail shell made of light.

"Every culture on this coast counts twelve moons to the year," Maren said. "Twelve full turnings, and then the calendar resets, and everyone pretends the moon has finished its business. But the moon doesn't read calendars, Fairweather. The true cycle—the *physical* cycle, the time it takes the moon to return to the exact same point against the exact same stars—is thirteen turnings and a fraction. The calendars round down. They've always rounded down."

"So the thirteenth turning happens anyway. Every year. What's different now?"

"What's different is drift." Maren tapped the chart. "The fraction accumulates. For centuries it's been too small to matter—hours, then days, swallowed by the reset. But fractions are patient. This year, for the first time in recorded history, the moon will complete its thirteenth turn *before* the calendar lets go of the twelfth. Both cycles closing together. A closed loop." She looked up. "Do you know when that last happened?"

Silas's mouth was dry. "I can do the arithmetic."

"Then do it."

"Three hundred years ago." The number arrived in her voice flat and cold, like a coin laid on a counter. "The Godswar."

"The Godswar," Maren agreed. "Which ended, according to every history book that survived it, at the moment of the last closed loop. The histories disagree on how it ended. They agree only that it ended *suddenly*. One day the gods were walking the earth burning cities, and the next day the sky was empty and stayed empty for three centuries." She leaned back. "I've spent my whole exile studying why the calendars lie. I thought it was sloth. It turns out it might be scar tissue."

Silas stared at the silver spiral of the moon's path, and something turned over behind her eyes—not a memory, because she had no memory of this, but the *shape* of one, the way a body remembers a wound after the flesh has forgotten the blade.

A battlefield. Not seen—*known*, the way you know the layout of your own house in the dark. Ash falling like grey snow. And a date, written in a script she shouldn't be able to read, standing in her mind with terrible clarity: the day the moon would finish its thirteenth turn.

Nineteen days from now.

"Maren," she said slowly. "If I told you I knew the exact date of the closed loop—not calculated it, *knew* it—what would you say?"

The astronomer studied her for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor. Inside, the orrery turned its patient circles on the floor.

"I'd say," Maren said quietly, "that the histories are wrong about one more thing. They say all the swordspeakers died in the war." Her eyes dropped, precise as an instrument, to Silas's sleeves—to the scars beneath them, the oath-marks that every child learned to recognize in schoolbook pictures. "They're wrong about that the way calendars are wrong about the moon. Rounded down. Swept aside. But the fraction remains."

Silas stood. Her legs felt like borrowed equipment.

"Nineteen days," she said. "Something answered a prayer in my counting house four nights ago. Herrin Vole prayed to Vaul, and something *kept* the appointment. And I have a date in my head that I have no right to know, and handwriting in a language I've never learned waiting somewhere in this city with my name on it."

"That's not a question, swordspeaker."

"No. It's an inventory." She was already moving toward the door, toward the stairs, toward the city spreading out below the tower like a ledger waiting to be balanced. "I need to see what I wrote."

Behind her, Maren called out—one last figure added to the account:

"The loop closes at moonrise! Whatever ended the war, it happened at *moonrise*!"

Silas took the two hundred and eleven steps down without counting a single one.
