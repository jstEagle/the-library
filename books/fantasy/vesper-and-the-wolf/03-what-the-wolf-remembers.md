## 3. What the Wolf Remembers

The fever broke on a Tuesday.

Wren remembered it that way because Tuesdays were market days, and she'd been lifting apples off a cart when the sound came through the tenement window above her—her sister's voice, ragged and thin for three years, suddenly clear as a bell.

"Maren?" Wren had dropped the apples. She'd climbed the drainpipe with her hands bleeding, burst through the window of the room they shared, and found Maren sitting up in bed, color in her cheeks, her eyes bright and wet and *hers*.

"I prayed," Maren said. "I didn't think anyone was listening anymore. But I prayed anyway."

"To who?"

Maren's smile went strange then, soft and far away. "To the one who remembers us."

---

Three weeks later, Wren knelt on the cold floor of a shuttered tannery in the lower city, and the god Vaul looked at her through the eyes of a dying old man.

She'd been told to expect fire, or light, or at least something grander than this: a stooped figure by a brazier, hands like crumpled paper, breath whistling in a ruined chest. The old man—Brother Anselm, he said his name was, though he laughed when he said it, like the name was a coat he'd borrowed—turned his head slowly toward her.

"You're the sister," he said. "The healer's sister."

Wren kept her hand near her knife. Old men in abandoned tanneries were usually exactly what they appeared to be, which was to say dangerous in the boring way. "I came to thank you. For whatever you did."

"Whatever I did." He chuckled, and the chuckle became a cough, and the cough bent him nearly double. When he straightened, his eyes had changed. Not glowing—that would have been easier, somehow. Just *older*. Like looking down a well and realizing the bottom was much farther than you'd thought. "Child, do you know what your sister had?"

"The wasting sickness. Everyone knows what she had."

"Everyone knows its name. No one knows why it takes." He gestured, and Wren felt something move through the room—not wind, not warmth, but attention. The whole building seemed to lean toward him. "Three hundred years ago, we knew. We knew everything. And then the swordspeakers came with their oaths and their blades, and the gods were driven out, and all that knowledge went with them. Your sister was dying of a wound in the world, little thief. A wound left where divinity used to be."

"And you healed it."

"Healed is such a small word." Brother Anselm rose, unsteady, and crossed the floor to her. Up close he smelled of tallow and old blood. "I *filled* it. Do you understand what that costs? Do you understand what I gave?"

Wren thought of Maren eating a full meal for the first time in a year. Of Maren laughing. Of the night Wren had sat by the bed doing arithmetic in her head—the cost of a physician, the cost of medicine from the Vesper Guild, the number of purses she'd have to cut—and come up short every time. She'd sat there doing sums while her sister slept, and she'd hated herself, and hated the city, and hated the neat little counting house up on Lantern Hill where people traded in bottled grief while girls like Maren died in rooms that smelled of piss.

"No," Wren said honestly. "I don't know what it cost you."

Something flickered across the old man's face—surprise, maybe, or pleasure. "No," he agreed. "You don't. That's rare, in this age. Most people would ask what it cost *them*." He reached out and touched her forehead with one finger, light as ash falling. "You'll do."

"For what?"

"For errands." His smile was kind and terrible. "I'm waking up, child. After three hundred years under the stone, I'm waking up, and my body—" he spread his ruined hands— "is not what it was. I need feet. Hands. Eyes in the streets. Someone the world doesn't watch, because the world has forgotten to believe she matters." He tilted his head. "You matter to me, Wren Ashgrave. Is that enough payment? To matter to something again?"

It shouldn't have been. Wren had spent nine years on the streets learning that being useful to powerful things got you used up. She could hear her own voice in her head, the cynical narrator of her own life, listing all the reasons to walk out of the tannery and never come back.

But Maren was alive. Maren was downstairs right now, in the tannery yard, helping the other faithful carry water because her legs worked, because her lungs worked, because a god had reached into the world's wound and pulled her out of it.

"Yeah," Wren heard herself say. "That's enough."

* * *

The work was simple at first. Carry messages between the faithful—a fishwife in Saltrow, a lamplighter off Cordwain Street, a debt-collector with soft hands and a soft voice who turned out to be able to mend broken bones with a touch. Word spread through the lower city the way water spreads through cloth, silent and unstoppable. The sick came first, carried on doors torn off hinges. Then the desperate. Then the merely greedy.

And the miracles kept coming. Not just healing—Vaul's power leaked into everything now. Bread that didn't mold. Wells that ran sweet. A widow whose dead husband's debts simply vanished from every ledger in the city, ink fading like breath off glass.

But there was a price, and Wren saw it before most did, because she watched the faces.

Old Tam, the lamplighter, healed of the palsy in his hands—he stopped greeting his daughter in the mornings. Not coldly. He just... didn't seem to know he should. The fishwife cured of the lung-rot asked after her husband at dinner, and her children went quiet, because their father had been dead six years, and she'd forgotten him entirely, the way you forget a dream by noon.

"Forgetfulness is the toll," Brother Anselm told the gathering one night, when the questions got too loud. He stood at the front of the tannery, and lately he stood straighter, and lately his voice carried without effort. "Memory is the currency of the divine. It always was. The guild trades in memories too—do you imagine they give refunds? At least I am honest about the price. You forget one thing. One person, one moment, one grief. And in exchange, you live."

"One *person*," someone said from the back. It was Tam. "My daughter came to me crying and I didn't know her name."

The room held its breath. Wren's hand drifted to her knife.

Brother Anselm looked at Tam for a long moment, and his expression was sorrow itself, perfect sorrow, sculpted sorrow. "Would you give her back, then? Would you return your working hands and take back the shaking ones? Tell me, friend. Look at your steady fingers and tell me you'd trade them for a face you can no longer picture."

Tam couldn't answer. Wren watched him try, watched his mouth open and close, watched him look at his own hands—hands that could thread a lamp wick again, that could hold a cup without spilling—and she watched the war inside him end the way it ended in all of them.

He knelt. "Forgive me," he whispered.

"There is nothing to forgive." Vaul raised him up and embraced him, and the room sighed, and the doubt died there, smothered gently, like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

Wren told herself the chill that walked up her spine was just the draft from the broken windows.

* * *

The relic job came on the night of the new moon.

"It's called a reliquary of the Unspoken Name," Brother Anselm said, tracing the shape of it in spilled salt on the floorboards. "A box, iron, small enough to hold in two hands. The Vesper Guild keeps it in their vault beneath the counting house on Lantern Hill. Inside is a name that was never spoken—a true name, unclaimed since the beginning. With it, I can finish waking. Without it..." He smiled his kind, terrible smile. "Well. Three hundred years is long, but even patience starves eventually."

Breaking into the Vesper Guild was suicide. Everyone in the lower city knew it. The guild's wards weren't magic, mostly—they were worse. They were *accounting*. Every item in that vault was weighed, measured, entered in a ledger in a hand so precise it looked printed, and if so much as a hairpin went missing, someone up on Lantern Hill would know its weight to the grain.

Which was why Wren went in through the chimney.

She dropped into the counting house at midnight, soot to the elbows, and found the place exactly as wrong as she'd expected. Ledgers everywhere, stacked in towers. Ink pots sealed with wax. And a smell underneath it all—old paper, yes, but also something metallic, like the air before lightning.

The vault door was in the floor behind the master desk, and it was locked with a mechanism, not a spell, which almost made her laugh. Thieves' heaven. She was crouched over the lock, picks in her teeth, when the candle on the desk guttered though there was no draft.

"You're standing on a ledger worth more than this district," said a voice above her. "I'd appreciate it if you wiped your boots."

Wren spun, knife out.

A woman stood at the top of the stairs from the street—tall, gray-eyed, dressed in plain dark wool with the austerity of someone who owned expensive things and refused to enjoy them. Her sleeves were pushed up. Her forearms, Wren noticed with a thief's eye for detail, were mapped with old scars, pale lines running in patterns too regular to be accidents. Sword scars. Who trained with swords anymore?

"You're the ledger-keeper," Wren said. "Fairweather. Silas Fairweather."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes—quick, unreadable. "You know my name. I don't know yours. That seems unfair, given that you're robbing me."

"Not robbing. Reclaiming." Wren rose slowly, keeping the knife low. "That box belongs to someone else. Someone it was taken from a long time ago."

"Everything in this guild was taken from someone a long time ago. That's rather the business model." Fairweather descended the stairs without hurry, and Wren realized with a lurch that the woman wasn't looking at her knife at all. She was looking at Wren's hands. At the soot. At the salt-white residue on her sleeve where she'd gripped the chimney stones. Reading her, the way she probably read everything—line by line, entry by entry. "You've been in the tannery on Marrow Lane. Tannin. And you're wearing a charm of knotted red thread, which means the old god's faithful, which means—" She stopped.

"Means what?"

Fairweather's jaw tightened. "Means you should tell whoever sent you that the reliquary stays here. Tell them the guild's ledgers are balanced, and I intend to keep them that way."

"They're not balanced." The words came out of Wren hot and fast, and she heard the fervor in her own voice and couldn't stop it. "They can't be. You people have been selling pieces of the world for centuries and calling it trade. Grief in bottles. Years in jars. You put a price on everything sacred and you never once asked who you were stealing from. Well, *he's awake now*, and he's coming to collect, and when he does, every ledger in this building is going to burn—"

"Ink," Fairweather said quietly.

"What?"

"Your mouth is full of ink. I can smell it." The ledger-keeper took another step down, and up close her eyes were the exact gray of old paper, and there was something in them that made Wren's knife arm tremble—not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like looking at a blade and knowing it had been drawn before. "I don't know what he promised you, girl. But I've seen what his gifts cost. I've seen it written down. In my own hand."

"Then you've seen lies."

"I've seen *numbers*." For just a moment, something cracked in Fairweather's composure—grief, or guilt, or both—and Wren understood, with a thief's instinct for the weak seam, that she'd found it. The flaw in the vault door. "Numbers don't lie. People do. Gods most of all."

Above them, through the ceiling, through the roof, the thirteenth moon hung invisible and black over the city, one turn from full.

Wren smiled, and it felt good, and it felt awful, and she meant every word. "Keep your numbers, ledger-keeper. The wolf is coming to count sheep."

She threw the smoke-pellet she'd bought off a hedge-witch in Saltrow, and in the choking gray dark she went out the way she'd come in, empty-handed but certain—certain as faith, certain as fever—of only one thing:

Next time, she wouldn't leave without the box.
