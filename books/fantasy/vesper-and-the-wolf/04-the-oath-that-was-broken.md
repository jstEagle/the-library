## 4. The Oath That Was Broken

The ink led me through the city like a wound through flesh.

I had spent two days following it, and in that time I had learned three things about the black trail. First: it did not fade. Ordinary ink dried to brown, cracked, flaked. This stayed wet. I found a smear on a doorpost in the Tanner's Reach and touched it with my thumb, and it came away on my skin like fresh blood from a cut, except cold, cold as well-water, and it would not wash off until I scraped my thumb raw against the whetstone in my coat pocket.

Second: it moved downhill. Always downhill. Water seeks the sea; this sought something older than the sea.

Third: it was getting thicker.

The first night it had been a thread, a hairline of black threading through the gutters where the rain hadn't reached. By the second morning it was a finger's width. By dusk, walking down the Stair Streets where the poor stacked their houses like driftwood, I could have followed it blindfolded by touch alone, wading through it, if I'd been fool enough to try.

I was not fool enough. But I was curious enough, and curiosity has always been the most expensive thing I own.

"You're going down there," said Maren, the chandler's widow who sold me candles at the corner of the Stair Streets, and who had decided over eleven years of weekly purchases that this entitled her to an opinion about my life. She nodded at the alley mouth where the ink ran into the dark between two leaning houses. "People go down there. They don't come back up talking."

"What do they come back up doing?"

"Praying." She spat, expertly, into the gutter—missing the ink by a hand's width, which I noted and did not mention. "Old words. Words nobody taught them. My sister's boy came back up off that street last month singing in his sleep, and his mother swears he never learned the song. Silas." She gripped my sleeve. Her hands smelled of tallow and ash. "Whatever's down there, it isn't paying you enough."

"Nothing pays me enough," I said. "That's how I know the work is honest."

She let me go, but she watched me all the way to the alley mouth, and I felt her eyes between my shoulder blades the whole way down, the way you feel weather coming.

* * *

The shrine sat at the bottom of the Stair Streets where the city gave up trying to be a city and became instead a throat—a sunken court, older than every building above it, older probably than the streets themselves, a bowl of worn stone that the newer city had simply grown around and forgotten. The Guild's maps marked it as *subsidence, structural, non-navigable*. The Guild's maps were wrong about a great many things, and I was beginning to suspect they were wrong about this one on purpose.

The ink pooled here. It lay across the flagstones in a broad black sheet, mirror-still, reflecting not the grey sky above but something else—something that flickered at the edge of vision like a face seen in a crowd. I kept my eyes on my boots and crossed it in six steps, counting them, because counting is what I do when the world stops making sense. Numbers hold. Numbers don't answer prayers.

The shrine itself was a niche carved into the far wall, maybe four feet high, framed by pillars worn smooth as river stones. Once there had been a statue in it. Now there was only a shape of absence, a god-shaped hole in the stone, and the walls around it—

The walls around it were covered in writing.

Not carved. Written, in the same black ink, in lines and lines of it, climbing toward the ceiling and running down to the floor, dense and furious and beautiful, the handwriting of someone who had too much to say and no time left to say it. And I stood there in the cold and read it, or tried to, because the letters were wrong. The alphabet was nothing I knew—not Low Speech, not Old Imperial, none of the trade-scripts the Guild kept in its reference vaults. The characters hooked and coiled like barbed wire, like thorns, like the tracks of something dragged.

Except.

I knew the hand.

You spend twenty years keeping ledgers. You learn what a hand looks like—the way a person crosses their sevens, the way their capitals lean, the small stubborn habits that survive even when the writer doesn't know they're writing. And the hand on those walls leaned hard to the right, and its sevens were crossed with a single slashing stroke, and its line slanted uphill when the writer was angry, and it was angry now, it had been furious, the last lines gouged so deep into the stone that the chalk beneath showed white through the ink.

It was my handwriting.

I don't mean it resembled my handwriting. I mean I stood in front of that wall and looked at the way the letter *v* ended in a small downward hook, a habit I have never been able to break, that three different Guild scribes have teased me about across three different decades—and there it was, hundreds of times, in a language I could not read, in a place I had never been, in ink that predated the plaster on the houses above us by centuries if it predated anything at all.

"No," I said aloud. My voice sounded very small in the sunken court. "No, that's not—"

My oath-scar woke up.

I need you to understand what that means, because for twenty years it has been nothing. A pale seam across my left palm, running heel to finger, the mark every swordspeaker took at binding—I'd told clients it was an old kitchen injury, and they'd believed me, because nobody looks twice at a clerk's hands. It had never hurt. Not once. Not in the war's last year, not in the burning, not in the twenty years since, when I had trained myself to look at it the way you look at a scar from a childhood fall: evidence of something survived, meaning nothing.

It burned now. It burned like the whetstone had taken my thumb again, then worse, then worse than that, a white heat uncoiling up my forearm and into my chest, and I dropped to my knees on the flagstones and pressed the palm flat against the stone floor because that was the only thing my body knew to do, some reflex older than my memory, older than the Guild, older than the city.

And the floor remembered.

* * *

A battlefield. That was the first thing. Not the fighting—the aftermath, the terrible quiet after, the field under a sky the color of old pewter, and the ground moving. Not with wind. With breathing. The dead lying in drifts like leaves, and among them things that were not men, vast shapes cooling like iron pulled from a forge, and the smell—I can still smell it, ozone and blood and something sweeter underneath, something like incense rotting.

And a voice. Close. Right in front of me, though I could see nothing but the pewter sky and the breathing ground.

*Please.*

A god's voice. I knew it was a god's voice the way you know your own name—it came from everywhere and nowhere and it was tired, so tired, tired the way mountains must be tired, and it was begging.

*Please. Look at me. Look at what we've become. We were meant to be loved, not fed—we cannot stop, we do not know how to stop—you made us out of wanting and now the wanting eats the world—*

My hand rose into the vision. My hand, younger, the scar on it fresh and red instead of pale, holding a sword that drank the light around it. And the voice saw the sword and did not flinch away, and that was the worst part, that was the part I will carry now until whatever memory takes the rest of me:

*Yes,* said the god. *Yes. Thank you. Thank you—*

The blade fell.

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor of the shrine with my cheek against cold stone and my heart slamming and the taste of ozone in my mouth, and the burn in my palm had faded to an ache, a deep muscular ache like a fever breaking. Above me the walls went on whispering their thorn-hooked letters. I lay there a long time. Long enough for the light in the court to shift from grey to darker grey.

Then I got up, because that is the other thing I do when the world stops making sense. I got up, and I walked to the wall, and I put my burned palm against the deepest-cut line of writing, the last thing written, the angriest.

And I understood it.

Not translated it—understood it, the way you understand a scream, the way the meaning arrives whole before the words do. One sentence, in a language I should not know, in my own hand, twenty years old at least:

*I did what was asked of me, and I will not remember doing it, and the world will be better for both.*

Below it, smaller, added later, the ink thinner, the hand shakier:

*If you are reading this, I was wrong about the second part.*

I took my hand off the wall. The ink clung to my palm for a moment, reluctant, then released.

I am a precise woman. I balance accounts. I do not believe in omens, and I do not believe in answers that arrive without invoices attached. But I stood in that drowned little court with a god's death behind my eyes and my own handwriting accusing me from the walls, and I understood that somewhere in the Guild's vaults—in the ledgers I keep, in the entries I have balanced and filed and never once questioned—was the record of what I had done and what I had been paid to forget.

Someone had bought my memory. Someone had sold it. And the account was coming due.

Behind me, at the top of the Stair Streets, a bell began to ring—the Vesper Guild's evening bell, which I had heard ten thousand times and never once counted. I counted it now. Thirteen strokes.

The bell had never rung thirteen strokes.

I started climbing.
