## 5. Borrowed Years

The lower city learned Vaul's name the way it learned everything: in whispers, then in shouts, then in the price of bread.

Wren heard it first from a lamplighter who'd been coughing blood since winter. Three days after he prayed at the drowned shrine under Tanner's Row, his lungs were clean and his voice was bright and he couldn't remember his mother's face. He said this cheerfully, which was the worst part. He said it like a man reporting good weather.

*Vaul takes only what you can spare,* the whisper went. *Vaul heals what healers can't.*

By the end of the week there were queues at the shrine. By the end of the month there were two more shrines, and the queues had families in them, and children, and old men carried on doors.

And Sera was well.

That was the miracle Wren kept returning to, turning it over like a coin she suspected was clipped. Her sister had been dying for two years—slowly, politely, the wasting sickness eating her from the inside while she smiled and made jokes about her own funeral arrangements. Wren had stolen everything worth stealing to pay for physicians who shook their heads with increasing confidence. Then Wren had knelt in the dark under Tanner's Row, out of options and out of pride, and prayed to a god three centuries dead, and something in the dark had *listened.*

Sera woke the next morning hungry. She ate a whole loaf and complained it was stale. She walked to the market and back without stopping to rest. She was *well*, and Wren wept into her shoulder like a child, and Sera patted her back with the polite confusion of someone comforting a stranger.

"You're very kind," Sera had said. "But do I know you?"

Wren had laughed, because grief does that sometimes, laughs when it should scream. "It's me. It's Wren."

Something flickered across her sister's face—a fish seen deep underwater, gone before you could name it. "Wren," Sera repeated, tasting the name. "That's a nice name."

* * *

The forgetting spread through the healed like dye through water. The lamplighter's mother. The cooper's wife of thirty years. A boy cured of the pox who no longer knew his own brother's laugh. Always one person, one love, plucked clean out of them—and always they were cheerful about it, serene, as if the hole in their hearts had been sanded smooth. That was the part that made Wren's skin crawl. Grief left ragged edges. This left none at all.

She went back to the shrine to demand answers, and found Vaul waiting, because of course he was. He wore the shape of a lean man in a gray coat these days, ordinary as a clerk, with eyes the color of deep water where no light reaches. When he looked at you, you felt *known*, all the way down, every theft and kindness catalogued and forgiven.

"Wren." He said her name like a blessing. "You look tired."

"My sister doesn't remember me." Her voice cracked on it. "You took that. You took *me* out of her."

"I took nothing." He spread his hands, gentle, reasonable. "Healing is not free, child. Nothing true is. I ask each soul to give up one memory—one thread from the garment. Would you rather I take their lives? Your physicians took your coin and gave you nothing. I take a thread and give back the whole cloth."

"She was my *sister.*"

"And she is alive." His voice softened, and that softness was worse than any rebuke. "She wakes in the morning and the sun is a gift to her. She eats and is fed. You wanted her well. She is well. Is her gratitude owed to the memory of you, or to the life you won for her?"

Wren opened her mouth and found nothing in it. That was his talent, she was learning. He didn't argue. He rearranged the world until your argument had nowhere to stand.

"Come," he said. "You deserve to understand. Not the half-truths the others get. *You* deserve the truth, Wren, because you came to me first, before the crowds, when I was still small and hungry. Let me show you why I must grow."

He touched her forehead, and the world fell away.

* * *

She stood on a battlefield under a red moon, and the air smelled of scorched gold and burning hair.

Gods were dying here. She understood that immediately, the way you understand fire is hot—not by thought but by the screaming of everything in you at once. They were vast, even wounded, even broken: shapes of storm and root and tide collapsing into the mud, and around them, small as wasps and just as lethal, moved the swordspeakers.

White-cloaked figures with scars crawling up their arms like ivy, speaking words that weren't words, and where the words landed, gods came apart. Not killed the way men are killed. *Unmade.* Unremembered. She watched a god of harvests—the name wouldn't come to her, the vision itself had been scoured of it—fold inward like a burning letter, and where he had been there was only a stain and a silence, and the swordspeaker who did it turned away already seeking the next.

"They called it justice," Vaul's voice said, everywhere, inside her skull. "They called it an ending. Look closer, Wren. Look at what your heroes truly were."

One figure moved through the slaughter ahead of the others, and the dying gods flinched from her. Tall. Gray-eyed. A blade that drank light. Where she passed, the ground remembered nothing afterward—not the battle, not the dead, not itself. She raised her sword over a god pinned beneath fallen stone, a god who was *begging*, arms open, mouth shaping mercy, mercy—

The blade fell.

The god came apart like smoke in wind.

And the woman lowered her sword, wiped it clean, and walked on, and her face—

Her face was the face from the guild counting house. The ledger-keeper with the precise hands and the careful eyes. Fairweather. Silas Fairweather.

Wren came back to herself on her knees in the shrine's dark, sick and shaking, Vaul's hand resting on her head like a father's benediction.

"Now you know," he murmured. "They will tell you the war ended. They will tell you peace came. Ask yourself what peace costs, and who paid it, and who grew fat on the payment. Your guild trades in memories, child. Do you know where the trade began? In the spoils. They butchered my family and sold the pieces, and they have grown rich on the carcass for three hundred years, and now that we return—now that we offer healing freely—they send their knives."

"The ledger-woman," Wren whispered. "Fairweather. She—"

"Is the last of them. The last oath-bound swordspeaker, though she has hidden her scars and buried her oath and plays at arithmetic while the world forgets what she did." His grip tightened, tender and terrible. "She unmade gods who were begging for mercy. And mark this, Wren, and mark it well: she will do it again. That is what oaths are for. She has begun already—asking questions, following trails, sharpening herself. When the moon completes its turn, she will try to finish what her order started."

The shrine dripped. Somewhere above, the lower city went about its evening, the healed and the halved walking home to suppers they could enjoy because they couldn't count what was missing.

"I believed you," Wren said. Her voice sounded far away. "I stole for you. I carried your tokens. I brought my sister to you."

"And I saved her." No anger. Only sorrow, vast and patient as weather. "I have never lied to you, Wren. Not once. Everything I showed you tonight is true. That is more than the guild will ever give you."

It was true. That was the trap of him—he never lied, he only chose which truths to drown you in. She thought of Sera's polite smile. *That's a nice name.* She thought of the lamplighter whistling past his mother's door. She thought of the gray-eyed woman wiping her blade clean while a god begged.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked, and hated how easily it came, how much it sounded like devotion.

Vaul smiled, and in the dark of the shrine his eyes were the only light, deep and cold and full of drowned things.

"There is a temple beneath this city where the truth sleeps," he said. "Our truth, written in living stone. She will go there soon, following her little black trail, digging for proof. Let her find it. And when she does—" his hand closed gently on Wren's shoulder, warm as fever— "be there. What the murals show her, she will twist. What she cannot twist, she will destroy. She is the last blade of a genocidal order, Wren, and blades exist for one purpose."

He leaned close, and his breath smelled of deep water.

"Stop her. However you must."

* * *

That night Wren sat on the sill outside Sera's window until the lamps died, watching her sister sleep—whole, rosy, breathing easy, dreaming dreams that had no room in them for a sister named Wren.

*I did this,* she thought. *I traded you away.*

No. Vaul had traded her away. Vaul, who had healed what no physician could touch. Vaul, who had shown her the truth no guild-ledger would ever record. Vaul, who asked so little—a thread here, a thread there—and gave back lives.

The moon hung low and swollen over the rooftops, one night short of some turn or other the astronomers muttered about. It looked almost close enough to touch. It looked almost like an eye.

Wren pulled her knife, checked its edge out of habit, and began, quietly, to plan how a thief follows a swordspeaker into the dark.
