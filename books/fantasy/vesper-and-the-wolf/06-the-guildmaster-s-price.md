## 6. The Guildmaster's Price

The Guildmaster kept her office above the counting house, behind a door of black oak that had no handle on the outside. You knocked and it opened, or you knocked and it didn't, and in eleven years Silas had never once seen it fail to open for the person the Guildmaster wanted.

Tonight it opened before Silas finished knocking.

"Sit," said Maren Vesper, and Silas sat.

The office was smaller than clients imagined. No vaults, no trophies, no shelves of impossible things. Just a desk scarred by two centuries of elbows, a lamp turned low, and a window that looked down into the counting house floor, where clerks moved between the cages like patient fish. Maren liked to watch. That was the whole architecture of the woman: she watched, she weighed, she wrote things down.

Silas stood anyway. She'd learned long ago that Maren offered chairs as a way of measuring how tired you were.

"You've been following ink," Maren said.

"I've been doing my job."

"Your job is ledgers." Maren's hands folded on the desk. She was small, silver-haired, with the kind of face that made strangers confess their debts. "The ink is not your job. The shrine in Tanner's Row is not your job. The astronomer with the exile's brand who told you about thirteen moons—definitely not your job."

Silas kept her breathing even. "You've had me followed."

"I've had everyone followed since the first prayer came back answered. I'm the Guildmaster, Silas. Panic is a commodity like anything else. I don't buy it, so I contain it." She tilted her head. "You went to the shrine. You saw the writing."

"I saw my own handwriting in a language dead three hundred years."

"And what did you feel?"

"That's not a question you get to ask me."

Maren smiled, and it was the worst thing Silas had seen all week, because it was gentle. "Sit down, swordspeaker."

The word landed like a dropped coin. Silas sat.

* * *

Maren poured tea from a pot that had no business being warm—it was guild stock, a borrowed hour of heat, cheap at the price—and pushed a cup across the desk. Silas didn't touch it.

"Do you know how the Vesper Guild started?" Maren asked.

"Bottled grief. Someone found a way to decant it, someone else found buyers."

"Someone found a way to *decant* it." Maren laughed softly. "No. Grief doesn't bottle itself, Silas. Nothing bottles itself. Every impossible thing we trade has an origin, and you've spent eleven years enforcing our one rule: never ask where goods come from. Do you know why that rule exists?"

"To keep appraisers from asking questions that get them killed."

"To keep them from finding the answer." Maren leaned back. "The answer is that grief can be drawn out of a person the way water from a well—but only if something on the other side of the well is pulling. Something thirsty. We called them the deep clients. The ledgers call them Account Holders. You'd know them by older names."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

"The old gods," Silas said.

"The old gods." Maren said it the way she said quarterly figures. "They never died, whatever the histories say. They starved. A god runs on worship, and after the war nobody prayed anymore—too frightened, too sensible. So they slept, and the world forgot them properly, and for three centuries the guild has run a quiet little arrangement. We harvest memory. Memory is the richest food there is—thicker than prayer, more honest. And in exchange, the sleeping ones let us skim their power. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names that were never spoken. All of it poured up from below, all of it priced by me."

Silas's hands were flat on her knees. Her oath-scar ached under her sleeve, a low banked burn she'd almost convinced herself was imagination. "You've been feeding them."

"I've been *managing* them. There's a difference, and the difference is the city still stands." Maren's eyes were steady. "For three hundred years the arrangement held because they slept and we rationed. Then three weeks ago, someone prayed over a stolen relic in my counting house and something answered, and now they're waking faster than we can feed them. Waking gods don't want rations. They want tribute. And tribute means taking memory straight out of living heads—the way your friend the thief's sister lost her healer's face, if you've heard that story. Everyone downstairs has heard that story."

"The healed girl in the lower city."

"Forgetting someone you love. That's the interest rate now." Maren spread her hands. "So. Here is where you come in."

* * *

She opened a drawer and took out a ledger—not a guild ledger. It was bound in grey leather gone soft with age, and when she set it on the desk Silas felt the scar on her forearm ignite like a struck match.

She knew that book. Not its contents. Its *weight*. Her body knew it the way a drowning body knows which way is up.

"This was found in the drowned temple," Maren said, "eighty years ago, by a diver working for my grandmother. It's written in Godstongue. Three generations of Vespers have failed to read it. Two weeks ago, it opened for me." She turned it around. The pages were full of Silas's handwriting—the same slanted, precise script she used every day, but wilder, pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places.

"It's an account," Maren said. "Yours. Opened the day the war ended. And it has never been closed."

"I don't remember any of this."

"No," Maren agreed. "That's rather the point. Twenty years ago you came to us with no history and a scar that predates your earliest memory, and I hired you because you could count faster than anyone I've ever met and because you flinched at nothing. I told myself the missing years were none of my business. But the ledger knows, Silas. And the Account Holders know. And now that they're waking, they want their account settled."

"What do they want?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and Silas understood that everything so far—the tea, the watching window, the gentle voice—had been the approach to this one sentence, delivered as carefully as a blade laid on silk.

"They want the last swordspeaker neutral. The war ended badly for them, and they believe—I've read enough of this to piece it together—that it ended *wrongly*. That something was taken from them at the end. Something only you can return, because you're the one who took it. In exchange for your past—all of it, every year they hold—they will stand down. Sleep again. Let the thirteenth turn pass unmarked. The city keeps its memories, the guild keeps its trade, and you—" Maren's voice softened cruelly, "—you finally learn who you are."

* * *

Silas stood. The chair scraped back and the sound seemed very loud.

"You're offering to sell me my own life."

"I'm offering to buy your neutrality. The past is merely the currency." Maren hadn't risen. "Think about what you're refusing. You have nothing, Silas. No mother, no name that isn't borrowed, no history except eleven years of arithmetic. They're offering you twenty years back. Your whole self. Don't tell me you haven't wondered."

Every night. Of course every night. She had built herself out of precision precisely because there was nothing underneath to build from. And here it was, priced, on a desk, in reach.

"And if I say yes?"

"Then tomorrow morning you walk into the temple district, you do whatever the account requires—you'll know when you see it, the ledger will show you—and the gods go back to sleep. Everyone forgets the miracles ever started. The thief's sister remembers her brother. Life resumes." Maren finally stood, and even across the desk she barely reached Silas's shoulder. "And if you say no, then they wake fully, and they take what they're owed without bargaining, and the thirteenth turn comes due on a city that never agreed to the debt."

"There's a third option."

"There's always a third option. It's usually worse."

"Tell me what actually ended the war." Silas leaned forward. "Not what the account says. What happened. Because I've seen a battlefield, Maren. I've seen a god begging. If I did that—if I killed one of them—then your Account Holders aren't creditors. They're survivors of something *I* did, and I want to know why I did it before I pay anyone anything."

Something crossed Maren's face then, quick as a clerk's error caught and corrected. Pity, maybe. Or fear.

"You think I know?" Maren said quietly. "Child, my family built its fortune on the aftermath. On grief bottled by the thousand while the smoke was still rising. On years harvested from soldiers who'd stopped wanting theirs. On names sold back to widows at four times the purchase price. We grew rich because the war emptied people, and empty people sell anything." She closed the grey ledger and rested her hand on it. "I don't know what you did. I know only that everything I have—this house, this city's whole economy of forgetting—grows out of the hole you left behind. Whatever truth is in this book, it's the one thing I've never been able to price."

"Then keep it," Silas said. "Keep your bargain. Keep your fortune."

She walked to the door. Behind her, Maren said, "They won't stop with offers. You understand that. The next messenger won't knock."

Silas paused with her hand on the black oak.

"Good," she said. "I'm done waiting for answers to arrive politely."

* * *

The counting house floor was dark, the clerks gone home, the cages locked. Silas crossed it alone, boots loud on the boards, and stopped at her own desk in the corner where the ledgers lived.

Her inkwell was full that morning. Now it was empty, and she had written nothing since noon.

In the bottom of the glass, the last of the ink had gathered itself into a single line of characters, upright and deliberate, in the language she shouldn't know. She copied them onto a slip of paper before they faded, though some part of her already understood them, the way her body understood the weight of the grey book:

*THE ACCOUNT COMES TO YOU.*

Underneath, in the same hand, a second line, fresher, the ink still wet:

*BRING THE LEDGER OR WE WILL COLLECT IN THE LOWER CITY FIRST.*

The lower city. Where prayers were being answered nightly now. Where a thief named Wren carried messages for a hungry god, and a healed girl was forgetting her brother one day at a time.

Silas took the grey ledger from beneath her coat—she had lifted it from Maren's desk on her way out; Maren had almost certainly let her—and weighed it in her hands.

Below the counting house, far below the cellars, past the sealed doors the guild never spoke of, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count.

She blew out the lamp and went down to meet it.
