## 7. Where Prayers Go to Die

The stairs down were older than the city above them.

Silas counted them out of habit—forty-one steps from the guild's sealed cellar door to the waterline—and told herself the counting was precision, not fear. The lantern in her left hand threw a coin of light against wet stone. The air tasted of salt and old iron and something sweeter underneath, like incense burned down to its ghost.

The drowned temple had been on the guild's asset rolls for two hundred years. *Subterranean structure, unrecoverable, value nil.* She'd written the entry herself in her first year as keeper of the ledgers, copying it faithfully from a ledger three generations dead. Nobody had ever gone down. Nobody had ever needed to.

But the black ink had needed to.

It had started three nights ago—a line of the ledger's ink lifting off the page like something waking, crawling down the margin, off the desk, across the floorboards, and into the cellar wall. Silas had followed it the way she followed everything: with an inventory, a plan, and no questions asked until the asking was unavoidable. The ink had pooled at the base of the forty-first step and gone still, waiting, like a dog that knows the way better than its master.

She descended into the water.

It came to her knees, then her thighs, black and warm as spilled tea, and the lantern light couldn't reach its bottom. Her oath-scar—the old sword-cut that ran from her right wrist to her elbow, the mark every swordspeaker wore like a signature—had begun to burn the moment she touched the first stair. It burned now like a live coal wrapped in her skin.

"Twenty years," she said aloud, and her voice came back wrong off the stone, doubled, as if the temple were repeating her for practice. "Twenty years of quiet, and now this."

The corridor opened into the nave.

Silas raised the lantern and forgot how to breathe.

The temple was not drowned. The temple was *flooded*—there was a difference, she understood suddenly, the way one understands the difference between a house that has fallen down and a house that is holding its breath. Water filled the great hall to waist height, and beneath its surface, and up every wall, murals moved. Not flickered. *Moved.* Painted figures walked their painted processions, slow as seasons, and where the water touched them they turned their heads to look at her.

She knew the style. Every child in Vesper knew the style—it was on the coins, the guild seals, the courthouse friezes. Godswar art. The oldest art in the world, all of it supposedly destroyed when the war ended.

All of it supposedly destroyed by *her*.

"No," Silas said quietly. "I would remember burning a thing like this."

Wouldn't she?

She waded toward the nearest wall. The mural showed a city—Vesper, or its ancestor—kneeling before a line of towering figures crowned in flame and antler and moonlight. The gods. And pouring from the kneeling city, drawn upward in silver threads, came—

Memories. She recognized the shape of them because she handled them daily: the guild's whole trade rendered enormous and terrible. A mother's face going gray as it left her. A soldier's wedding day unraveling into thread. Whole lifetimes siphoned up into divine mouths.

The next panel. The same city, emptier. The faces blank as eggs.

The next panel after that, the paint so fresh it still gleamed: a battlefield under thirteen moons, and a single small figure walking alone between the god-corpses, sword bare, blood to the elbows.

The figure had her scarred arm.

Silas's lantern hand shook. The water shivered around her waist. On the wall, the painted figure turned—not the mural's slow seasonal turning but a real turn, sudden, human—and looked at her with her own face, twenty years younger, mouth open around words the paint could not hold.

Behind her, at the top of the stairs, someone struck a flint.

* * *

Wren had been given three instructions and a knife that drank light.

*Follow the swordspeaker down. Wait until she finds what she's looking for. Then put the knife in her heart, and Vaul will give your sister back her memory of you.*

Simple work. Wren had done harder things for less. She'd picked the lock on the guild's cellar door without breathing, trailed the woman through forty-one steps of black water, and kept the knife dry inside her coat while her heart tried to climb out through her throat.

Because here was the thing nobody told you about working for a god: you could feel him watching. All the way down. Like sun on the back of your neck in winter.

Now she stood at the edge of the flooded nave, half-hidden behind a fallen pillar, knife loose in her grip, and watched the swordspeaker stare at the walls.

Silas Fairweather didn't look like a butcher. Wren had imagined a monster—everyone in the lower city had, since Vaul's sermons began seeping through the drain-grates like warm smoke. *The swordspeakers slaughtered us in our sleep. The swordspeakers cut the sky open. The last one still walks your streets wearing a clerk's face.* Wren had fed her sister soup through the worst of the fever and hated this stranger with a hatred so clean it felt like health.

But the woman in the water just looked *sick*. She stood in front of the moving murals like someone reading a letter about their own funeral.

And the murals—Wren couldn't stop watching them either. She'd never seen Godswar art. Nobody had. The priests said the swordspeakers burned it all, another crime on the pile. But here it was, alive, and it was showing—

Showing a city giving everything away. Showing people lining up to pour their memories into the gods' mouths, smiling as they did it, smiling with less and less behind their eyes each time. Showing a mother who gave her child's name and then walked past the child in the street without stopping. Showing a whole generation hollowed out like gourds, and the gods growing fat and bright and *hungrier*, and the tribute rising each year because the gods' hunger rose, and there was no enough. There was never any enough.

"That's not how he told it," Wren said.

Her own voice betrayed her. The swordspeaker spun, knife-hand dropping, lantern rising, and for a long moment they stared at each other across the black water—thief and clerk, killer and target, both of them lit gold at the edges by moving paint.

"You're the thief," Silas said. "From the reliquary job."

"And you're the butcher." Wren stepped out from the pillar, knife forward, because the script demanded it. "Vaul sent me."

"I know." The woman didn't reach for a weapon. That was worse. "He's watching through you right now, isn't he? I can feel it. Like standing in a doorway with a draft behind you."

"He says you ended the world once," Wren said. "He says you'll try again."

"The walls say otherwise." Silas turned back to the mural, slowly, keeping her hands visible, and pointed with the lantern. "Look at it. Really look. He's been showing you battlefields and corpses, hasn't he? Swordspeakers as slaughterers. Look at what actually happened."

"I don't have to look at anything—"

"*Look.*"

And maybe it was the temple, maybe it was the water, maybe it was the painted figure with Silas's face still holding its silent scream—but Wren looked.

The mural moved for her. She hadn't known it could do that. The procession rippled, panels sliding like cards, and the story poured itself into her: the tribute years, the emptying, the gods' hunger doubling each thirteenth turn of the moon. Then the swordspeakers—not as monsters but as *clerks*, accountants of the impossible, trying to balance a ledger that only ever flowed one way. Trying to negotiate. Trying to cap the debt. And the gods refusing, because a god fed on worship the way a fire feeds on a house, and worship freely given was never enough; it had to be *taken*, had to be memory, had to be everything a person was.

Then the last panel. One swordspeaker walking into the gods' own hall under thirteen moons. One blade. And the greatest of the gods—the wolf-crowned one, the healer, the one whose voice Wren heard in her sleep now, warm as broth—on his knees.

Begging.

The painted god's mouth moved, and though the temple gave no sound, Wren heard it anyway, the way you hear your own name across a crowded room: *Please. Please. I cannot stop. Whatever I say, whatever I promise—I cannot stop. End me before I end them all.*

The blade fell.

The hunger died with him. The remaining gods, starved and unanchored, sank into sleep. Three centuries of silence. Three centuries of a city allowed to keep its own mind.

"No," Wren whispered. The knife point dipped. "No, he—he healed my sister. He *healed* her. Why would he—"

"Because he wants out," Silas said gently, terribly. "And because the price of his miracles is the same price it always was. Ask your sister who you are, Wren. Ask her what she traded."

"She doesn't—" Wren's voice cracked. "She was dying. She doesn't remember me because of the *fever*, the fever took—"

The mural answered her. It slid another panel forward, fresh paint gleaming: a girl in the lower city, fevered, and a silver thread descending from the moon, and the thread taking hold not of the sickness but of the girl's memories—one bright thread among thousands being drawn up and away, payment rendered, and somewhere far below a thief standing in a doorway feeling a draft on her neck.

Wren sat down in the water. She didn't decide to. Her legs simply stopped being interested in standing.

"He took me from her," she said. "He healed her and he took *me* out of her, and then he told me it was the swordspeakers' fault, and I believed him because believing him felt like—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It felt like having a purpose. It felt warm."

"I know," Silas said. "That's what worship is. That's why they need it taken rather than given. Given love can be withdrawn."

The knife was still in Wren's hand. She looked at it, at the light-drinking blade, at the god-shaped warmth pressing at the back of her skull saying *finish the work, little messenger, finish the work and be rewarded*—and she threw it. It skipped once off the black water and sank, and the warmth behind her skull became cold, became *furious*, and the temperature of the whole temple dropped as if the water had remembered it was supposed to be the sea.

Above them, faintly, through forty-one steps of stone, the bells of Vesper began to ring out of order.

Silas went very still. "That's not the hour bell."

"No," Wren said. She knew the lower city's bells the way she knew lock mechanisms. "That's the panic pattern. That's *all* of them."

The murals had stopped moving. Every painted figure in the drowned temple had turned to face the stairs—to face *up*—and the water around their waists had begun, very slowly, to rise.

Silas lifted the lantern and looked at the newest panel, the one that hadn't existed when she entered: thirteen moons over the guild's counting house, and the doors thrown open, and silver threads pouring out into the streets by the thousand.

"He's not waiting for the turn anymore," Silas said. "He's calling in every debt the guild ever brokered. Right now. Tonight."

The water climbed another inch.

"Can you stop him?" Wren asked.

Silas Fairweather looked at the painted image of her own younger self, sword raised over a begging god, and her scarred arm burned so brightly beneath the skin that the lantern seemed dim beside it.

"I've done it once," she said. "I don't remember how."

They waded for the stairs together.
