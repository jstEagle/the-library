## 8. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon had been wrong for three nights.

Wren noticed it first from the roof of the tannery, where she went to think because thinking was easier with the city's stink below her and nothing above but sky. The moon should have been a thin silver paring by now, sliding toward dark. Instead it hung fat and full and faintly green at the rim, like a coin left too long in vinegar.

*Thirteen turns,* Vaul had told her, his voice coming up through the cobblestones, through the soles of her feet, warm as bathwater. *Thirteen turns since my brothers and sisters were caged. When the wheel closes, I will be whole.*

She'd believed him. She still believed him. That was the problem—belief sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, and every day it burned a little hotter, and every day she found new reasons not to look at what it was burning.

Her sister's name was Lark. Wren repeated it to herself on the rooftop like a prayer to no one. *Lark. Lark. Your sister is Lark.* Because two weeks ago, Lark had looked up from her broth and said, in a voice gone soft and pleasant as milk, "You're very kind to visit me. Do I know you?"

The fever was gone. The rot in her lungs was gone. The doctors had called it a miracle, and it was, and miracles had a price tag, and nobody had told Wren that the price would be *her*.

Vaul said forgetting was mercy. Vaul said memory was the heaviest thing a soul carried, and he was only lightening the load. Vaul said a great many beautiful things in a voice like warm water, and Wren had drunk them all down.

Tonight the wheel closed.

* * *

The lower city had stopped sleeping.

Wren ran the rooftops toward the river and saw it everywhere: doors standing open at midnight, people drifting into the streets with their faces tipped up toward the swollen green moon. An old man stood in a doorway weeping without sound, clutching a child's shoe. A woman walked her own route to work at an hour when no work existed, hands moving through the motions of a life she might already be losing the shape of.

And under everything—a hum. Not sound exactly. More like the feeling of a word on the tip of ten thousand tongues.

*He's beginning,* Wren thought, and her stomach dropped through the rooftops, through the city, through the world. *He's taking it back. All of it.*

Because that was the part Vaul hadn't led with. The memories the guild had traded for generations—bottled grief, borrowed years, names never spoken—all of it had passed through god-hands at some point, or been paid into god-shrines, or been squeezed out of human skulls by prayers that answered too well. It belonged to him, he said. He was only collecting what was owed.

He'd shown her the Godswar to make her understand. He'd filled her head with it: swordspeakers wading through divine blood, gods screaming down into silence, the world saved by slaughter. And at the center of it, a woman with grey eyes and a scarred throat, her blade coming down—

*Silas Fairweather,* Vaul had named her. *The butcher of heaven. Kill her, little thief, and the wheel turns freely.*

Wren had wanted to kill her. She remembered wanting it the way you remember being drunk—furious and certain and not quite yourself. Then she'd gone down into the drowned temple after the woman, knife in hand, and seen the murals move, and learned what the gods had demanded before Silas Fairweather ever lifted her blade: *memory*. Whole lifetimes of it. Cities emptied out from the inside, smiling and obedient and hollow, feeding their years and loves and griefs to altars until there was nothing left worth saving.

The god who healed Lark was the god who had eaten cities.

Wren ran faster.

* * *

She found Lark in the shrine on Candlemaker's Row.

Of course she did. Half the street had drifted there, drawn like moths, standing shoulder to shoulder in the little vaulted room where the walls sweated golden light. Vaul's presence filled it the way water fills a lung. At the center, where the altar had been, something was *coalescing*—a shape of light and shadow that was almost a body, almost a face, patient as a mountain deciding to stand up.

And Lark stood closest of anyone, right at the altar's edge, arms open, face shining.

"Lark." Wren shoved through the crowd. "Lark, listen to me—"

Her sister turned. And for one horrible heartbeat Wren saw the polite blankness again, the stranger wearing her sister's face—and then something moved behind Lark's eyes, some deep sediment of self the miracle hadn't reached, and her expression cracked open with recognition and terror at once.

"Wren." Her voice came out ragged. "Wren, oh gods, Wren, I can't—I can't hold on to—" She grabbed Wren's wrists hard enough to bruise. "It's taking more. It said healing was enough before but it's taking *more*, everyone's more, can't you feel it? Mama's face is going. I woke up tonight and I couldn't—there was a lullaby she used to sing and it's just *gone*, there's a hole where it was—"

"Lark—"

"You have to stop him."

The words landed like a slap. Wren actually stepped back. "Stop—he *saved your life*—"

"He saved my life with pieces of other people!" Lark's voice climbed, cracking against the vaulted ceiling. Several of the worshippers turned, faces serene, unbothered, and that serenity frightened Wren more than any scream could have. "Don't you see? Don't you see what this room is? Look at them, Wren. Look at their *eyes*."

Wren looked. The crowd swayed gently together, breathing in time, and their eyes were full and wet and empty as rain barrels. A boy of maybe twelve smiled at nothing. An old woman mouthed a name over and over—*Tomas, Tomas*—like she was trying to keep hold of it with her lips alone.

"He's unraveling them," Lark whispered. "All the minds that ever traded with the guild. He's pulling the threads. And when the moon finishes turning—" She looked up, through the shrine's high window, at the green-rimmed disc hanging over the city. "There isn't much moon left, Wren."

"I know." Wren's mouth was dry. "I know. But if I stop him, the healing goes too. Everything he gave comes undone. You'll be sick again, Lark. You'll *die*."

"Then I'll die as me." Lark seized her sister's face in both hands, and her eyes were fierce and wet and utterly, unmistakably herself—the girl who'd taught Wren to pick locks, who'd laughed until she wheezed, who'd held Wren through the worst winter of their lives. "Better six months of me than sixty years of this hollow smiling thing. Better dead than *eaten*. Please. You're the only one who can get close to him. He trusts you. He *uses* you, but he trusts you, and that's the same door."

The light behind them pulsed. The coalescing shape at the altar had shoulders now. Hands. A suggestion of a crown made of candle flames.

"Wren," said the warm-water voice, filling the room, gentle as ever. "Come forward, little thief. The wheel is closing. Be beside me when it does."

And Wren stood between them—her god on one side, her sister on the other—with the coal of belief burning in her chest and finally, finally letting her feel what it had been burning all along.

It had never been faith. It had been fuel.

* * *

"You showed me the war," Wren said aloud. Her voice sounded small in the golden room. "You showed me the swordspeakers killing gods. You didn't show me why."

The shape paused. Just for a moment. A candle-flame crown flickering.

"The murals lie," Vaul said. "Dead gods paint themselves kindly."

"They weren't lies. I *saw* them move. I saw what you took before the war. Memory as tribute. Cities fed to altars until there was nothing left but the smiling." Wren's hands were shaking. She made herself keep talking. "Silas Fairweather didn't butcher heaven. She cut the leash. And you've spent three centuries underground learning to tell the story the other way around."

The warmth in the room changed temperature. Not colder—*emptier*. Warmth draining out like bathwater through a plug.

"Little thief," Vaul said, and the endearment had teeth in it now. "I healed your sister. I gave her back to you from the grave's edge. And this is my thanks—to be lectured by a gutter-rat with knife-scars and no future?"

"She doesn't remember you gave her anything," Wren said. "That's how you take your payment, isn't it? You heal the body and eat the love. Well, I'm done being grateful for scraps off a god's plate."

The last sliver of ordinary moon slid toward the green rim overhead. In the window's curve, the two lights touched.

The hum beneath the city rose into a note. Every person in the shrine sighed at once, a long contented exhale, and the old woman mouthing *Tomas* went silent mid-syllable, her lips still moving around a name that was no longer there.

"No," Lark breathed. "No, no—it's starting, Wren, it's *starting*—"

Beyond the shrine walls, the city began to make a sound Wren had never heard before: thousands of people crying out at once, not in pain but in *loss*, the collective gasp of minds feeling fingers close around their dearest things. Somewhere a bell tower rang wild and arrhythmic. Somewhere a horse screamed.

Vaul's body finished assembling itself in a bloom of gold. He was beautiful. That was the worst part. Tall and kind-faced and radiant, exactly the god from the stories, exactly the savior from the fever-dreams, and he looked down at Wren with genuine sorrow.

"It didn't have to be this way," he said. "I would have given you a place at my side. I would have let you keep her."

"You'd have let me keep a Lark-shaped hole," Wren said. "I've met her. She smiles too much."

She drew her knife—not at the god, she wasn't insane, steel meant nothing to him—but she put herself between Vaul and her sister anyway, feet planted, chin up, a five-foot-nothing thief squaring off against a rising god with a stolen relic's worth of courage and nothing else.

Vaul regarded her almost fondly. "And what will you do, little messenger? Run to the butcher? She's already gone down to the drowned temple looking for answers. By the time she climbs back out, the city will be mine and her secrets with it."

"Then I'd better go meet her," Wren said.

She caught Lark's hand and squeezed it once—hard, fierce, a promise shaped like a grip—and shoved backward through the crowd of swaying, emptying faithful, out the shrine door, into the screaming streets, toward the river and the dark water and the temple underneath.

Behind her, the thirteenth turn completed, and the moon turned green as a wound, and the god began to collect.
