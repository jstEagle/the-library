## 9. Swordspeak

The counting house had become a cathedral in the way that rot becomes a garden—slowly, and then all at once.

Silas came up the guild stairs into a hall transformed. The long counters where she'd spent twenty years balancing ledgers were gone, pried up and stacked like firewood. In their place, the floor had been scored with channels, and the channels ran with something that was not ink but wanted to be: black, glistening, moving against the slope of the room toward the center, where Vaul stood.

He was larger now. That was the first thing her body understood, before her mind caught up—the pressure of him, the way the air bent around his shoulders like light through bad glass. He had been a voice in a shrine, a warmth in a dying girl's chest. Now he filled the counting house the way winter fills a valley.

Around him knelt the faithful. Hundreds of them, packed between the empty shelves where impossible things had once been catalogued. Silas recognized faces from the lower city—the cooper's widow, the lamplighters' boys, a woman who sold eels off a cart near Tanner's Row. Their eyes were open but their gazes were elsewhere, fixed on some middle distance where a god was showing them their own best memories on an endless loop.

And above them, suspended in cages of gold wire where bottled grief had hung last week, were memories.

She could see them. That was the horror of it—not metaphor, not ledger-logic, but sight. Thousands of small lights, each one a moment someone had traded or lost or prayed away. A child's first steps. A wedding morning. A face at a deathbed. They drifted upward out of the kneeling crowd like sparks off a bonfire, and Vaul drank them with his whole skin.

*The thirteenth turn,* she thought. *He's collecting.*

Wren stood at his right hand.

The thief had changed too, and not the way gods change. She was thinner, hollowed, her hands wrapped in bandages stained brown. When her eyes found Silas across the hall, there was a flicker in them—quick, drowned, gone—that might have been the girl from the drowned temple, the one who had wept over murals of slaughtered gods and then wept harder over the truth beneath them.

Then it closed over, and Wren said, "She came."

"I told you she would." Vaul's voice was better than it used to be. It had learned music. "The last swordspeaker comes to balance her books. Welcome, Silas Fairweather. Your account is overdue."

Silas walked forward. Not fast, not slow. The way she walked to any appraisal: measuring, unhurried, certain that everything in the room could be priced.

"You've taken the city's memories," she said.

"I've *returned* them." He spread his hands, and the little lights swirled toward him adoringly. "They were never theirs, child. Memory is tribute. It always was. The old law, older than your guild, older than your oath: mortals pay in remembering, and gods keep the accounts. I am merely resuming collections after an unfortunate interruption."

"Three hundred years of interruption."

"A bookkeeping error." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been designed by something that once loved humans, and that was the worst thing about it. "You'll want to argue. They always do, at the end. So let me show you what you were."

The pressure came down on her like a hand.

But Silas had spent three days in the drowned temple reading the living murals, and she had come up those stairs knowing this would happen. She had done her arithmetic in advance. When the vision tried to force its way into her—a battlefield, a blade, a god's blood going black as ledger ink—she held it at arm's length and examined it the way she examined any goods brought across her counter.

*Provenance,* she thought. *Where did this memory actually come from?*

It was his. Every image of the war she carried that wasn't hers—and now she knew which ones weren't, could feel the seam of them like a badly stitched hem—had passed through Vaul first. He had edited the war. He had shown the world swordspeakers as butchers and himself as the last innocent, and he had buried the one scene that mattered.

So instead of drowning, Silas opened her mouth and spoke.

Not a prayer. Not a plea. An oath.

---

She hadn't spoken it aloud in twenty years. She hadn't spoken it aloud even when she took it, if the temple murals told true—oaths of the swordspeakers were given in silence, pressed into the scar tissue of the forearm, a vow made flesh so it could never be unsaid. Her scar had burned for three days straight since the drowned temple. Now it burned like a brand held to a wick, and she let it.

"*I am the edge that answers,*" she said.

The word went through the hall like cold water through oil.

The kneeling faithful shuddered. One woman gasped as though surfacing from deep water. The drifting lights—the stolen memories—stopped mid-drift, trembling in their suspension like insects caught in amber.

Vaul turned fully toward her for the first time, and his beauty cracked along fault lines she could suddenly see.

"*What are you doing.*"

"My job," Silas said. "*I am the silence before the strike. I am the word that ends words.*"

Every syllable landed physically. She felt it in her teeth, in the scar, in the floorboards under her boots. This was what the guild had never known about its last swordspeaker, what she herself had only remembered in fragments: swordspeakers didn't fight with blades. Blades were for afterward. Swordspeakers fought with speech—oaths so bound to truth that reality itself honored them. A swordspeaker could not lie. That was the price. That was the power. Everything she said while speaking the oath *became*, because it was already true.

"*I am the accounting that cannot be falsified.*"

"No." Vaul's voice lost its music. Underneath it was something rawer, older, frightened. "You are a clerk. You broke your oath. You are nothing but a broken—"

"*I know what Vaul asked of me.*"

The hall went silent. Even the memories stopped humming.

Silas stepped closer, and the black channels in the floor seemed to lean away from her feet.

"At the end of the Godswar," she said, and her voice did not shake because the oath would not permit it, "the gods were losing. Not to armies. To us—to the oaths, to the unbinding. And Vaul—" she looked up at him, at the god who had healed a dying girl and started eating a city, "—Vaul was the first to break. Not corrupted by the war. Corrupted *before* it. He had been drinking memory for centuries by then, quietly, politely, the way the guild drinks coin. The other gods were going to unmake him. Strip him down to nothing and reforge him honest. And he couldn't bear it. So he came to the battlefield, to the last swordspeaker standing, and he knelt."

"Stop."

"He *knelt*, Wren," Silas said, and found the girl's eyes. "He knelt in the mud and the blood of his own siblings and he begged. Not for mercy. For the blade. He said, *I cannot stop being what I have become, so be my mercy.* Those were his words. I remember them now because they're mine. Because they were true."

"Liar." But Vaul's voice cracked on it, and every kneeling mortal heard the crack. "She lies! She killed us! Look at her hands, look at what she—"

"*He begged me,*" Silas said, louder, and the oath carried it into every skull in the room, "*and I gave him what he asked, and I have carried the weight of it for twenty years without knowing why my nights were empty, and you have worn his face in my dreams and called it my sin.*"

The memories hanging in the air began to fall.

Not drift—fall, like rain reversing, pouring back down into the crowd below. The cooper's widow clutched her head and laughed and sobbed at once. The lamplighters' boys woke weeping, calling names. All across the hall, people were getting their dead back, their loves, their losses, the full terrible weight of themselves, and Vaul screamed—

—and the scream was the sound of a ledger burning.

He swelled. That was the last resort of a drowning thing: size. He poured himself outward, filling the hall, pressing at the walls, at the windows, at the seams of the world, and Silas felt the city beyond begin to bend toward him like iron filings toward a lodestone. Somewhere out there, thousands of minds were still tethered to him, half-drained, and he meant to pull them all in at once and become too vast to unmake.

Her scar was white-hot. Her mouth tasted of copper and old ink.

There was one oath left. She had known it since the temple. It was the one she'd sworn on the battlefield twenty years ago, the one that ended the war, the one whose price she had paid without ever seeing the bill.

A swordspeaker's final oath required payment. Truth given freely, in quantity. Last time, she had paid without understanding—and the guild's ledgers had recorded the transaction in a hand she didn't recognize, and she had woken with holes in her life shaped like people.

This time she understood exactly what it cost.

*Can you do it twice?*

The question wasn't Vaul's. It was hers, and it had been hers for three days, and here at the bottom of it she found the answer was simple, the way all terrible arithmetic is simple.

She looked at Wren.

The girl was staring at her god with an expression Silas recognized from the ledgers—the look of someone auditing a lifetime of entries and finding fraud on every page. Wren's bandaged hands were shaking. Her sister was out there somewhere in the waking city, remembering her again, remembering everything, and Wren was watching the thing she'd traded her soul for come apart at the seams.

"Wren," Silas said quietly. "When this is done, someone will need to keep the accounts. Someone who knows both sides of the ledger now."

Wren looked at her. Something passed between them that had no name in any language the guild traded in.

Then Silas turned to face the god, and drew breath, and began the final oath.

"*I am the last of the line that answered,*" she said, and the walls rang. "*And I pay the ancient price, freely given, in the only coin the gods may not refuse—*"

Vaul lunged. Not at her body—at her voice, at the air carrying it, a wall of screaming dark.

"—*the truth of what I love.*"

The oath took hold of her memories the way a tide takes hold of a swimmer.

She felt them go one by one, and she named them as they left, because the oath demanded truth and she would give it even now, especially now: *the Guildmaster's dry laugh, gone; the smell of the counting house at dawn, gone; the exiled astronomer's maps spread across her table, gone; the temple murals and the girl who cried in front of them, gone; Wren—*

Wren's face. Wren's voice saying *she came*. Wren's hand pressing a stolen relic back into Silas's palm in the dark under the city.

Gone.

Gone.

Gone.

Silas stood in the center of a hall filling with returning light, holding a single sentence in a language that was leaving her even as she spoke it, and finished:

"*—and with this truth I bind you, Vaul, who begged for the blade and received my mercy twice.*"

The god came apart like ink in water.

* * *

Afterward there was quiet, and dust, and dawn coming gray through windows that had somehow survived.

Silas Fairweather stood alone in the ruined counting house among kneeling strangers who were slowly, groggily rising, clutching their own heads, their own lives, their whole returned weight of memory. She felt—light. Clean. Empty in the way of a balanced book.

A girl with bandaged hands approached her slowly across the debris. There were tear tracks cutting through the grime on her face, and she was looking at Silas with an expression of such concentrated grief that Silas, out of professional habit, reached for the only comfort she had left to offer.

"Are you here to do business?" she asked kindly. "We reopen at noon."

The girl made a sound like something breaking and put her hand over her mouth.

Behind her, on the far wall, the old guild ledger lay open where it had fallen. Its pages were turning themselves, gently, one by one, in a wind that wasn't there—past twenty years of entries, past the war, past everything—stopping at last on a blank page.

At the top of the page, in ink that was still wet, new writing appeared:

*Received: one god, sealed. Received: one oath, fulfilled.*

*Pending: one debt, unpaid.*

*The moon turns again in thirteen years.*
