## 10. The Empty Ledger

The counting house was quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Silas stood at the great altar-desk where the ledgers had been chained for three hundred years, and the pages before her were no longer ink. They were mouths. Every line of every entry—the bottled grief, the borrowed years, the names that had never been spoken—had peeled itself off the paper and gone up, streaming through the broken skylight into the thirteenth moon's light, into the hands of a god who was learning, moment by moment, how to be whole again.

Vaul filled the room without touching it. He was taller than the rafters and no larger than a man, depending on which way she refused to look at him. His voice came from everywhere, warm as a hearth.

"You cannot fight me, swordspeaker. Your blade is memory, and I am made of everything this city ever forgot."

"I know," Silas said. "That's why I'm not going to fight you."

She set her sword on the desk. The oath-scar along her forearm had stopped burning an hour ago; now it simply ached, like an old wound before rain, like a truth before it is spoken.

"You want payment," she said. "You've always wanted payment. That's what none of your faithful understand—not even Wren. You don't give miracles. You *trade*. And you've been trading with this guild for years, taking memories as offerings, hoarding them against this night." She turned a page. Blank now. All of them blank. "So let's settle accounts."

The god went very still. Gods, she remembered—no, gods, she *inferred*, because that memory was not hers anymore—gods are greedy, and greed makes them patient, and patience makes them stupid about the shape of a bargain.

"Name it," Vaul said.

"The memories you've taken. All of them. Every healed hand, every mended bone, every grief you swallowed in the lower city. Give them back to the people they belong to. Whole. Now."

"And in exchange?"

Silas looked at the blank ledger, and past it, at the door where Wren stood with her sister's hand in hers, both of them gray-faced, unraveling, the girl who had once known her own name now struggling to remember the woman holding her.

"In exchange," Silas said, "I'll pay the one debt you've never been able to collect."

"There is nothing left of you I want."

"There is." Her voice did not shake. She had spent twenty years building a woman out of precision and routine precisely so that her voice would never shake again. "Every memory I have of someone I love. Not traded. Not stolen. *Given.* Freely offered, which means freely binding. Even you can't refuse a gift honestly given—it would unmake the whole rotten economy of worship you've rebuilt."

Vaul laughed, and the windows wept frost. "You'd be empty. A shell with a ledger."

"I've been empty before. I got better at it."

She thought of Wren's crooked grin over a stolen relic. Of her sister's laugh, thin and bright as spun glass. Of the Guildmaster's grudging nod, which was as close to love as that old spider ever came. Of the astronomer's maps spread across a rooftop, two cups of bad tea between them. Small things. A life assembled from small things, because the large ones had been cut out of her long ago by her own hand and her own blade.

She could survive losing small things. She had proof.

"Do you remember," Vaul said softly, cruelly, "what you told me on the battlefield? Before the end?"

"No," Silas said. "And after tonight, I never will have to."

She drew the oath-scar across her palm with the edge of the ledger's chain—a swordspeaker's signature, blood for binding—and spoke the words she had not spoken since the war ended and the world forgot why it had survived.

*"I am the account. I am the balance. What I hold, I pay."*

The moonlight bent toward her like water finding a drain.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the worst part.

Pain would have meant the memories were fighting to stay. But they came gently, the way a client surrenders a relic across the counter—reluctant, then resigned, then gone. She felt Wren go first: the alley, the theft, the knife-point argument that had become an alliance, the night in the drowned temple when Wren had seen the murals and wept and chosen her side. Gone. Then Wren's sister, whom Silas had only met twice but had already begun, treacherously, to think of as *ours*. Gone. The Guildmaster's rare approval. The astronomer's terrible kindness. The taste of shared tea.

She held onto each one as long as she could, the way you hold a door against a flood, knowing the arithmetic will win.

The last thing to leave was not a memory at all. It was the knowledge that she was choosing this, and that somewhere in the city a thief with quick hands was going to wake up remembering everything, including her, and that this was the point—that someone should remember, even if it could never be her.

Then the flood took the door, and the walls, and the name of the person she had done it all for.

* * *

Silas woke on the floor of the counting house with dust in her hair and sunlight—ordinary, thirteenth-turn-spent, merely lunar sunlight—falling through the mended skylight.

The ledgers were full again. Every entry restored, every debt recorded, every impossible thing accounted for. The ink was black and obedient and dead. On the final page, in her own handwriting, though she had no memory of writing it, stood a single line:

**Paid in full. Account closed.**

Her palm bore a thin white scar she didn't recognize. Her forearm bore older ones she didn't either. She was, she understood dimly, a woman who had scars the way other people had letters from home—evidence of a life she couldn't quite recall living.

She felt fine. That was the horror of it, and also, somehow, the mercy. There was no wound where the memories had been, because a wound requires the memory of what was cut away. She was simply precise, and orderly, and alone in a large room, and none of it hurt at all.

Outside, the city was screaming—but screaming *joyfully*, the way crowds do when a plague lifts. Three hundred years of stolen recollections were pouring back into their owners: mothers remembering daughters, neighbors remembering feuds and reconciliations alike, the whole vast tangled ledger of a city's heart rebalancing itself in a single dawn.

Somewhere below, a god was shrinking back into shrines and stories, sealed behind a payment he could never refund.

Silas straightened her collar, righted her chair, and opened a fresh ledger.

* * *

Wren found her there three days later.

She came in the front door like she owned it, because apparently she always had—there was a scuff on the threshold her boots had worn, though Silas had no memory of the boots or the wearing. Wren stopped halfway across the counting room, and her face did something complicated: hope and grief wrestling in the same small space, neither winning.

"It's you," Wren said. "You're really—" She stopped. Started again. "Do you know me?"

Silas looked up from the ledger. The young woman in the doorway was a stranger: sharp-eyed, road-worn, standing like someone accustomed to leaving rooms quickly. Pretty, in a weathered way. Familiar the way a scar is familiar—present, meaningful, illegible.

"I don't," Silas said, and heard how flat it landed, and tried to soften it. "Should I?"

Wren laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and cracked at the edges. "No. No, you shouldn't. That was the deal, wasn't it? That was the whole—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Gods. You paid *everything*. For people who'll never know. For a city that thinks the moon just hiccupped."

"I'm sorry," Silas said, because the stranger was crying and it seemed required. "Have we met? Were you a client? If you're owed something, I can check the—"

"Don't." Wren's voice came out hard. Then soft again, immediately, like a blade sheathed mid-swing. "Don't check the ledger. Please. Just—" She breathed. Composed herself with visible effort, the way people do when they've decided to survive something. "My sister's better. She remembers me. Everyone does, everyone in the lower city, whole again. Whatever you did, it worked."

"I'm glad," Silas said, and found, to her surprise, that she meant it in some place deeper than memory—a cellar below the flooded floors, where nothing had been able to reach.

Wren looked at her a long moment. Cataloguing, maybe. Memorizing for both of them.

"They say there was a swordspeaker, once," Wren said. "Who ended the Godswar. Who killed a god because he begged her to. And they say when the gods woke again, she did it twice." She smiled, and it broke her face open. "They don't know her name. Nobody does. Isn't that funny? The most important person in the world, and nobody knows her name."

"It sounds lonely," Silas said.

"Yeah," Wren said. "It sounds exactly like that."

She crossed the room—quick hands, quick feet, a thief to the last—and pressed something into Silas's palm. A small tin of tea. Bad tea, by the smell of it, cheap and over-steeped and perfect.

"For the ledger-keeper," Wren said. "From a friend."

"I don't think we're friends," Silas said gently. "We just met."

"I know," Wren said, already turning toward the door, already walking out of the story. "But we were. Somebody should get to keep that, even if it's only me."

The door closed. Sunlight moved across the floor. Silas sat with the tin in her hands and a strange ache in her chest that had no name attached to it, and after a while she set it on the shelf beside her desk, where it stayed, and where she never asked herself why.

* * *

Spring came to Vesper City the way debts come due: gradually, then all at once.

The guild reopened its counters. Clients returned with their impossible things—bottled grief, borrowed years, names never spoken—and Silas appraised them with her usual precision, her rule unchanged: never ask where the goods come from. The Guildmaster watched her sometimes, across the counting room, with an expression she couldn't read and didn't try to. Once, he began to say her name and stopped, and looked at her the way men look at graves, and said only, "The third-quarter entries need balancing."

"Yes, Guildmaster," she said.

At night she climbed to the roof sometimes—she didn't know why; it seemed to be a habit she'd kept without keeping it—and watched the twelve moons make their ordinary rounds. The thirteenth was dark now, or absent, or sleeping; the astronomers argued about it in their towers. Below her, the city glowed and muttered and dreamed, whole, ungrateful, alive.

On her desk, the current ledger lay open to a fresh page. At the top, in her own neat hand, she had written the date and nothing else. The rest of the page waited, blank and patient, the way all her pages waited now—for entries, for debts, for whatever impossible thing tomorrow would carry through her door.

She dipped her pen.

She paused, for just a moment, over a feeling she had no word for: the sense of a page missing from a book she'd never read, of a name on the tip of a tongue that had forgotten tongues.

Then she wrote the first line, and the pen moved, and the ledger filled, and that was enough.

It had to be enough. It was what she'd paid for.

Below, in the lamplit streets, a thief with quick hands was telling anyone who'd listen about a swordspeaker whose name nobody knew—and saving the true version, the whole version, the tea-and-ledger version, for one listener only: a girl who was getting better every day, and who would grow up hearing about a woman named Silas Fairweather, and wonder, all her life, why her sister cried whenever she said the name.

The page turned. The ink dried. The city slept.

And in the counting house above the drowned temple, the last oath-bound swordspeaker worked late into the night, precise and orderly and free, keeping the books of a world that no longer remembered what it owed her—

—which was, in the end, exactly how she had written it.
