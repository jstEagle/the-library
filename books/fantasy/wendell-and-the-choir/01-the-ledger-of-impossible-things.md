## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The bone was a woman's finger bone, and she had been a weaver, and she had died in the winter of her forty-third year with wool still caught under her nails.

Jude Eastwick knew this because she told him — not in words, exactly, but in the way the dead spoke through their relics: as pressure, as temperature, as fragments of sensation that rose up his wrist like water climbing a thread. He held the finger bone against the whetstone's edge and listened to her while he worked, the way another man might hum along to a song he half remembered.

*Cold,* she said. *The loom stopped. The candle went sideways. Someone was calling my name from very far away, or perhaps I was calling theirs.*

"Almost done," Jude murmured to her. It was a habit he'd never examined too closely — talking to the dead as though they could hear him back. They couldn't. Whatever remained in the bone was an echo pressed into calcium, a groove worn by the weight of a life. It had no ears. But it seemed rude, somehow, to carve a person's last testimony without acknowledging them.

He turned the bone under the lamp. Outside the workshop window, Vellum Street was going dark in the honest way that streets do — lamplighters working their rounds, a cart groaning over cobbles, the smell of tallow and wet stone settling in for the night. The Guild's tower stood at the end of the street, black against the bruised sky, its windows lit from within by a light that never flickered. Jude had stopped wondering about that light years ago. The Vellum Guild traded in impossible things; it did not owe anyone explanations.

The relic he was carving would be sold as a *listening token* — a small luxury for Guild clients who wanted to hear, once, briefly, the voice of someone they had lost. The woman's family had sold her remains to the Guild's procurers three years after her burial, which was legal, which was contracted, which was entered in the ledgers under *Acquisitions, Posthumous, Voluntary*, and which Jude tried very hard not to think about as anything other than a line item.

He set down the finished token. It was beautiful work, if he said so himself, and no one else ever would: the bone shaped into a small hollowed shell, polished until it held the lamplight like a drop of milk. When the buyer held it, they would feel the weaver's cold, her stopped loom, her candle leaning sideways. For eleven seconds — the Guild had measured it — they would know what it was to be her at the end.

Then the echo would gutter out, spent. Relics were single-use. The dead gave what they had, once, and then they were only bone.

Jude wrapped the token in cloth and wrote its entry in the day-book:

*One listening token, human source (F., 43, weaver). Commissioned by Aldous Ren, mercer. Fee: 14 crowns, 6 shillings. Carver's share: 2 crowns.*

His handwriting was very good. That was one of the things the Guild valued about him — not the gift, though the gift was why they'd bought his apprenticeship when he was nine years old and orphaned and already talking to a sparrow's wing bone he'd found in the gutter. No, what Corvane valued was that Jude Eastwick balanced his books to the farthing, carved to the hair's width, and never asked questions that weren't on the invoice.

*Careful,* his master Grell used to say, back when Grell was alive and still had hands steady enough to carve. *In this trade, precision is piety. A carver who is sloppy with measurements will be sloppy with souls.*

Grell had been sloppy with neither, in the end. He had simply grown old, and the Guild had grown tired of paying for old men, and one morning his bench had been cleared and a younger apprentice installed and no one had told Jude anything except the new figure in the wage column. Jude had carved a relic from Grell's knuckle — quietly, without authorization, keeping the token hidden in the false bottom of his toolbox for eleven years now, unspent. He could not bring himself to use it. As long as it stayed whole, some part of Grell remained unspent too.

That was the kind of arithmetic Jude did best: the kind that didn't balance, that he kept off the books entirely.

* * *

The workshop was also his home, which was common enough among Guild tradesmen and lonely enough that Jude had arranged it carefully to disguise the fact. A bed behind a curtain, disguised as storage. A single chair at the table, positioned so that visitors — rare, transactional, brief — would not notice there was nowhere else to sit. A kettle, two cups, one of them chipped and used daily and the other kept clean for clients who never came twice.

He made tea and opened the great ledger.

This was the part of the night he actually loved, though he'd have denied it under oath. The Ledger of Impossible Things — the Guild's master record, bound in vellum that Jude did not let himself speculate about — lay open before him, and his task was simple: make the impossible add up. Every relic acquired, every relic sold, every fee, every tithe to the Guild's mysterious upper floors, every carver's share. The dead did not keep accounts. Someone had to.

*Acquisition: femur, human source (M., 61, soldier). Provenance: battlefield of Cael Sarrow, recovered by licensed procurer. Cost: 3 crowns.*
*Disposition: ground and blended into memory-ink, lot 44. Yield: 2 ounces. Sold to House Vess at 40 crowns the ounce.*

Memory-ink. The Guild's most profitable line. Write with it, and whatever you wrote would carry a faint residue of the dead soldier's knowing — tactics, terrain, the smell of gun-smoke. Generals loved it. Jude had once transcribed an inventory in ordinary ink directly after using memory-ink, and for three lines his hand had written things he didn't know: *the ford is shallowest at the willows; count your men twice at dusk.* He had burned the page and washed his hands until they bled a little, and then he had entered the ink sale in the ledger, because that was the job.

Tonight the entries came easily. The weaver's token. Two reliquary clasps. A commission for a mourning ring, source unnamed, which meant the client had supplied their own bone and Jude preferred not to know whose. Rain began against the window, soft and businesslike. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the city muttered itself toward midnight.

Jude worked down the columns, carrying figures, ruling lines, initialing each page in his small precise hand. And then he reached the entry dated nine days past, and stopped.

*Acquisition: courier's satchel contents, assorted. Source: [name struck through]. Provenance: [blank]. Received by: G. Corvane, personal. Value: pending assessment.*

Pending assessment. Nine days pending. In eleven years, Jude had never seen an entry sit pending longer than a day. The Guild assessed everything immediately — that was practically its religion. Everything weighed, everything priced, everything entered. Corvane himself had drilled it into Jude at his first audit: *"An unentered thing is a debt, Master Eastwick, and debts are how empires rot."*

Jude read the entry again. The struck-through name bothered him more than the blanks. Strikethroughs happened when a source's identity was suppressed — usually meaning the source was someone the Guild had acquired while still breathing, which was illegal, which was why the suppression existed. Jude had seen maybe four such entries in his career and had learned to look away from them, the way you look away from a fight you can't win.

But nine days. And *courier*. Couriers carried things between Guild houses — letters, contracts, occasionally relics in transit. If a courier's satchel had been seized rather than delivered, somewhere a Guild house was missing its property, and somewhere else a ledger wouldn't balance, and the whole vast machine of the Vellum Guild ran on nothing but balanced ledgers.

Jude dipped his pen. Hesitated.

There was a column for *assessed value*, waiting to be filled. His job was to fill it. His job was always to fill it — to take the world's grief and horror and wonder and assign each item its number until the numbers closed like a mouth.

He wrote: *Value: pending assessment — query to Guildmaster's office.*

It was the closest thing to a question he had ever put in the ledger. His heart did something strange and small as he blotted it, some mixture of thrill and nausea, like the first step onto ice.

The dead woman in the finished token, wrapped in its cloth on the shelf, seemed to press a little colder against the wood. *The candle went sideways,* she had said. *Someone was calling my name.*

"Tomorrow," Jude told her, and blew out the lamp.

* * *

He woke — or half woke — at some hour with no name, to the sound of the bells.

Not the hour-bells. Not the alarm that rang when a procurer went rogue, which had happened twice in his time and sounded like any other alarm, just longer. This was different. This was low, and slow, and wrong — a bell tolled from the Guild tower in a rhythm Jude had never heard, three strokes and a silence, three strokes and a silence, like something knocking politely at a door it intended to come through anyway.

He sat up in the dark. The rain had stopped. The rhythm continued, patient, enormous, filling the city the way water fills a cellar.

Three strokes. Silence. Three strokes.

Jude had balanced the Guild's books for eleven years. He knew its language the way priests know liturgy — every fee schedule, every tithe code, every notation. And he knew, with the certainty of a man who has spent his life among numbers, that this bell was not announcing a rogue procurer or a fire or a death.

It was announcing a discrepancy.

Somewhere in the city tonight, something had gone missing from the Guild's possession — something important enough that the tower itself was counting the loss aloud to the dark. And nine days ago, an entry had been left pending, a name struck through, provenance blank, received by Corvane personally.

Jude sat in his narrow bed with his knees drawn up, listening to the bell knock and knock, and understood with quiet, rising dread that the ice he'd stepped on that evening was much thinner than he'd thought — and that beneath it, something in the deep water had already felt the ripple.

He got up, lit the lamp, and took down the great ledger to check whether the entry had changed.

It had. Where *pending assessment* had been, in ink that had certainly not been there at midnight, in a hand almost exactly like his own, a single word now stood:

*Received.*

And below it, faintly, as if the writer's pen had trembled: *Do not open the satchel.*
