## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The courier came through the door the way a wave comes over a seawall — all at once, and too late to stop.

Jude heard him first, of course. He always heard them first. The workshop's evening quiet had been threaded with something new for the better part of an hour: not the familiar murmurs of the dead in his carvings, but a living sound, ragged and human, footsteps that dragged rather than stepped. He had assumed it was a drunk navigating the Bonehouse district's crooked lanes, or one of the Guild's junior clerks come to pester him about an entry. He did not look up from his work.

He was carving a knucklebone that night — a woman's, small and worn smooth by forty years of laundry work before her death. The dead woman's voice came through it faintly as his burin touched the surface, the way it always did: *the blue dress, tell my daughter about the blue dress, she'll want the blue dress.* Ordinary grief. Ordinary work. Jude Eastwick had spent eleven years translating such whispers into the little carved tokens the Vellum Guild sold to mourners who could afford certainty, and he had learned to keep both halves of himself separate — the craftsman who balanced ledgers, and the man who listened.

Then the door burst open, and the two halves collided.

The man who fell across his threshold was perhaps thirty, dressed in the grey-and-silver livery of the Crown Post, though the livery had once been finer than this. Mud to the knee. One boot missing. And blood — so much blood that Jude's first coherent thought was not *a dying man* but *that much blood will ruin the floorboards,* which was the kind of thought a bone-carver learns to have and then hates himself for.

"Shut it," the courier rasped. "Door. Shut it."

Jude shut it. He would wonder later why he obeyed — some instinct older than thought, the same instinct that made him listen to bones. The latch clicked home, and the workshop became very small around them: the lamp on the bench, the racks of finished relics humming their low chorus of the departed, the stranger bleeding onto stone.

"They're coming." The man tried to rise, failed, and braced himself against Jude's workbench instead, leaving a red handprint on the maple that Jude would never quite scrub out. "They were behind me at the Ferrow bridge. Two of them. Grey cloaks. They stopped at the water — they don't like crossing running water, I gained a street — but they'll circle. They always circle."

"Who's coming? Who are you?"

The courier looked at him then, and Jude saw that his eyes were wrong — not injured-wrong, but *spent*-wrong, the way a candle looks in its last inch. "You're the carver," he said. "Eastwick. The one who hears them."

"I carve relics. That isn't—"

"It is." A wet laugh cost the man dearly; he folded forward over the bench. "I've carried this thing three hundred miles because every other name on my list was bought or buried. You're the last honest pair of hands in Vellamark. Maybe the only ones."

He pulled something from inside his coat. It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and it was heavy — heavier than paper had any right to be, dense as a ledger of lead. He pressed it into Jude's hands with both of his own, and his hands were freezing, and they shook, and he held on even after Jude had taken the weight, so that for a moment they stood there together, four hands around a dead man's parcel.

"You have to read it," the courier said. "Not tonight. Tonight you hide it. But you have to *read* it, carver. Before they find you. Before they find anyone."

"What is it?"

"A map."

"I've seen maps. The Guild keeps—"

"Not like this." The courier's grip tightened. His voice dropped to something barely above the whisper of his own blood hitting the floor. "It's drawn on vellum. Look at the vellum. *Look* at what it's made of."

Jude looked down at the parcel in his hands, and the dead woman in the knucklebone went suddenly silent, as if she too were holding her breath.

* * *

He unwrapped it because the courier made him — because the man said, "Now. While I can still see your face when you understand," and there was something in the request that was less like urgency and more like a sacrament. Some things must be witnessed by the living or they do not count.

The oilcloth fell away. Inside lay a roll of vellum the color of old cream, mottled faintly along one edge with a pattern like grain — no. Not like grain. Like pores. Jude had worked with skin-leather all his life; he knew calfskin and kidskin and the fine translucent membrane the Guild called *saint-parchment*, and he knew, with a craftsman's sick certainty, that this was none of them. This was human. Large human. The kind of hide you get from a very large person, prepared by someone patient and unhurried.

And it was tattooed. No — *inscribed.* Ink sat in the surface in lines fine as hair, and where the ink ran along the mottled edge, it followed the grain of the skin itself, curving where the original body had curved.

"It's a chart of a skeleton," the courier whispered. "Every bone. Drawn on the man himself. Or part of him." A breath. "A king's part of him."

Jude unrolled it carefully across the bench, weighting the corners with two knucklebones and a paperweight shaped like a sleeping hound. Lamplight moved across the vellum, and the map resolved.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it — it was *beautiful*, rendered with the precision of an anatomist and the devotion of an iconographer. A full skeleton, anterior view, each bone labeled in a script Jude half-recognized: old court-hand, the kind used for charters and coronation oaths. Along the spine, each vertebra bore a small circled mark, and beside each mark, a name. Twelve vertebrae marked. Twelve names. The rest of the skeleton was annotated in a different hand, denser, more recent — measurements, dates, marginal notes in a code of small musical symbols that meant nothing to Jude and everything, he sensed, to someone else.

At the crown of the drawn skull, where a saint's icon might bear a halo, the mapmaker had inked a ring. A crown. And inside the ring, written so small Jude had to lean close enough to fog the vellum with his breath:

*it sings.*

"The king's own skin," the courier said. "Or close kin to it — the Guild records say the chart was made from the founder-king's reliquary remains. Every royal bone accounted for. Every royal bone *claimed.* Do you understand what I'm telling you? Someone has mapped the king the way you'd map a mine. Because that's what he is now. A mine. Something is being taken out of him, and this—" he tapped the twelve names with a bloody finger, "—this tells you where it's kept."

Jude's mouth had gone dry. "Taken out of him. Taken *what* out of him?"

The courier smiled, and it was the worst thing Jude had seen that night, worse than the blood, because it was gentle. "Memory. The crown doesn't sit on his head, carver. It sits *in* him. There are twelve voices bound into it — singers, once, living people — and they hold the king's remembered life the way a hive holds honey. He rules because they remember ruling for him. He smiles because they remember smiling. And when the honey runs low—" the smile flickered, "—they need more. Or they need a new voice. I don't know which. I was told to bring the map to someone who could hear the dead, and let the dead explain the rest."

"Who told you?"

"A dying man. Who was told by a dying woman. We pass it down the line like a fever." The courier sagged, sliding down the face of the workbench until he was sitting on Jude's floor, his legs out straight, the map still pinned under one hand as if he feared it might be taken back. "There's a whole chain of us, carver. Couriers. Archivists. One choir-mistress, years ago. All of us carrying pieces of the truth toward someone who could use it. Most of us didn't make it. I'm not going to make it." He said it without drama, the way a man reports weather. "But you will. You have to. Because you can hear them, and they have been trying to say this for twenty years."

Jude crouched down across from him. Up close, the courier smelled of iron and horse and something under both, sweetish and wrong, that Jude's craftsman's mind identified as *sloughing tissue* before his human mind could flinch away. "Let me fetch a surgeon. There's one two streets—"

"No surgeons. Surgeons ask questions, and questions reach the Guild by morning." The man caught Jude's wrist. His grip was astonishingly strong for a dying man — the grip of someone who had trained his whole body for exactly this final task. "Listen to me. My name is Perrin Voss. Write it somewhere. I carried this from Corvane's own archive — from *inside* the Vellum Guild, carver. Your Guild. The people you balance ledgers for. They know what the crown is. They've always known. They don't want it destroyed." His eyes bored into Jude's. "They want it *managed.*"

The word landed like a stone in still water. *Managed.*

"That's not possible," Jude heard himself say. "The Guild trades in relics. Grief-work. Tokens for mourners. I've kept their books for eleven years. Impossible things, yes — but small ones. Honest ones."

"Read your own ledgers more closely sometime. Ask yourself why the Guild pays for royal death-masks. Ask yourself where the 'choir maintenance' stipend goes — line forty-one, third column, every year, since before you were hired. You balance numbers, Eastwick. You never once asked what they bought." Perrin Voss coughed, and the cough was deep and bad, and when he wiped his mouth his hand came away dark. "I liked my work. Twenty-two years in the Crown Post. I delivered love letters and tax notices and I never asked anything of anybody. Then a colleague died and left me a sealed letter, and I opened it, and now here I am, ruining a stranger's floor." Another wet laugh. "Sorry about the floor."

"Don't talk. Save your—"

"There's no saving it. There's only the spending." He pulled Jude closer, and his whisper was fierce now, urgent, a man sprinting the last length of a very long race. "When I die — not if, when — they'll feel it. The Guild has bells. Old bells, under the city, that nobody rings anymore except for this. When I stop, you'll hear them, and you'll know they know, and you'll have until dawn, maybe less. Take the map into the bone-cellars under your shop — I saw the door, everyone in this district knows the Bonehouse has cellars — and stay there until you find the singer. Sera Ilvane. She was court soprano. She refused to sing at the renewal ceremony and they exiled her for it, and everyone said she was mad, and she wasn't mad, she was the only one who *looked.* Find her. Tell her Perrin Voss finally got it through."

"Got what through? Voss—"

"The choir." His voice was failing now, fraying at the edges like rope. "Tell her I said — the choir. It's real. She was right. She was right all along, and I'm sorry it took a dead man's skin to prove it—"

His hand slipped from Jude's wrist.

For a moment he simply sat there, propped against the workbench, looking almost peaceful, a tired courier resting between deliveries. Then his chin dropped, and the lamplight caught his open eyes, and the workshop was quiet in a way rooms are never quiet when a living person fills them.

Jude knelt there, holding a dead man's map, listening to the silence where a voice had been.

And then the dead spoke.

Not the knucklebone woman. Not any of his relics. The room itself seemed to exhale — a pressure, a presence, dozens of faint voices overlapping at the threshold of hearing, and Jude realized with a lurch that they were coming from *Perrin Voss*. From the body. From whatever clung to a man in his final minute and lingered after, the way warmth lingers in a chair. Jude had never heard a fresh ghost before. Eleven years among the dead, and he had never once heard the newly dead, and now he understood why the old carvers called it a gift with a lock on it: the lock had just come off, and what came through was Voss, repeating, fading, urgent:

*the choir — the choir — tell her the choir —*

Then nothing. Just a body, and blood cooling on stone, and a map made of a king.

Jude sat back on his heels. His hands, he noticed distantly, were steady. They were always steady; it was the rest of him that shook. Somewhere in his chest a ledger-entry was forming itself against his will — *one courier, deceased; one map, received; one debt, outstanding* — and he hated how the habit of accounting survived even this.

He should fetch the watch. He should fetch anyone. Instead he found himself doing what he always did with the dead: giving them their due. He closed Perrin Voss's eyes. He took the grey cloak from the hook by the door and laid it over him, because no one should be found cold-eyed under open sky, even a stranger, even a liar, even a man who had just turned eleven years of Jude's careful life into wreckage.

That was when the bells began.

* * *

He had heard the Guild bells his whole life in Vellamark. Everyone had. They rang the hours, they rang market-days, they rang the slow mourning-peal when a Guild member died — nine strokes, a pause, nine again. Jude knew their voices the way he knew his own heartbeat.

This was not those bells.

It began as a single tone far off across the city, deep enough to feel in the teeth rather than hear — a note with no melody in it, sustained, deliberate. Then a second bell joined it, a semitone higher, dissonant, wrong, the two notes grinding against each other like millstones. Then a third. A fourth. Within a minute the whole city's bell-towers were sounding, and they were not ringing in any pattern Jude had ever learned, because it was not a pattern. It was a *chord.* A vast, grinding, discordant chord rolling over the rooftops of Vellamark, over the Bonehouse district, through the walls of his little workshop, and every relic on every rack began to scream.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. The dead in his carvings did not shriek — they *harmonized*, dozens of faint voices rising together into a single sustained note that matched the bells above, and Jude stood in the middle of his workshop with his hands over his ears and understood, with total clarity, that the dead were afraid.

*They know,* the chord said, in a language below language. *They know, they know, they know.*

Perrin Voss had told him true. The Guild felt the courier's death, and the Guild was calling something up from underneath the city, and whatever it was calling, it had never been rung in Jude's lifetime. Eleven years of ledgers, and no entry for this.

He moved. Fear makes some men freeze and some men fast, and Jude Eastwick had spent his whole life being the second kind. The map went back into its oilcloth, the oilcloth into the false bottom of his ledger-chest, beneath eleven years of immaculate accounts — let them audit *that*. The lamp he left burning; a dark workshop would say *fled*, and a lit one said *nothing happened here, move along.* He dragged Perrin Voss's body behind the curtain of the back alcove, out of sightline of the door, and hated himself for the tidiness of it, and did it anyway.

Then he stood in the center of his workshop, in the light of one lamp, surrounded by the humming fear of the dead, and made himself think.

*Dawn,* Voss had said. He had until dawn, maybe less. The bells said *less.* The enforcers would come quietly first — that was the Guild's way, silk before steel, a polite knock and a question about missing couriers — and Jude would have to lie to their faces, and he had never lied to anyone in his life, because liars kept bad books and bad books got audited.

*Find Sera Ilvane.* A disgraced singer. An exile. A madwoman, if you believed the gossip — and Jude, standing in his workshop while the city tolled a chord of alarm, realized that he had believed the gossip, because believing the gossip was easier, because the gossip required nothing of him.

The bells ground on. Under them, faint as breath, the dead in his relics repeated the only instruction they had:

*tell her — tell her — the choir —*

Jude Eastwick picked up his coat, took one last look at the orderly, honest, carefully balanced life arranged around him like a set of books awaiting audit, and reached for the handle of the cellar door.

Three sharp knocks fell upon the front door of his workshop.

Through the wood, calm as a clerk announcing business hours, a voice he recognized from Guild functions said: "Master Eastwick. Guildmaster Corvane would like a word."
