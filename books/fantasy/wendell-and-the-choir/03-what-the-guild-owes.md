## 3. What the Guild Owes

Jude had spent his whole life learning that doors were honest things. A door announced itself — knock, hinge, threshold — and what came through it came on terms you could read like a ledger line: debit, credit, balance. He stood now with his hand still resting on the cellar latch, the courier's map rolled tight against his ribs beneath his coat, and understood for the first time that a door could lie too.

"Master Eastwick." The voice again, patient as interest accruing. "The hour is inconvenient, I know. The Guildmaster is aware."

He knew that voice. Pellam Ostray, Second Clerk of Acquisitions, a man built like a folded letter, who had once spent an entire Guild banquet explaining to Jude the proper way to appraise a saint's fingerbone without ever once looking at Jude's face. Men like Ostray did not come to bone-carvers' workshops at midnight unless something had already gone badly wrong somewhere else.

Jude let go of the cellar handle. The map pressed its long thin shape against his side like a second spine.

"One moment," he called, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. He crossed the workshop in six steps, and in those six steps he did three things. He banked the carving lamp low so the workbench fell into shadow. He slid the courier's bloodied satchel under the floorboard where he kept the Guild's reserve of grave-wax. And he looked, one last time, at the ledger lying open on his desk — the entry that would not balance, *one courier, one delivery, no return* — and closed it.

Then he opened the door.

Ostray stood in the fog with a lantern held at hip height, its light arranged to illuminate nothing above his own chin. Behind him, at the edge of the lamplight, two shapes waited by a black carriage. They were not moving. Enforcers never moved when they could simply *be*, which was worse.

"Master Eastwick." Ostray's mouth performed a smile. "You're awake. Good. The Guildmaster dislikes repeating himself."

"It's past midnight," Jude said.

"Yes," Ostray agreed pleasantly. "It is."

* * *

The carriage smelled of cedar and cold iron. Jude sat opposite Ostray, knees nearly touching, and watched the fog slide past the windows in grey ropes. Neither enforcer had spoken. One of them had taken his coat at the door — politely, with both hands, the way you'd take a coat from a man you expected to hang later — and Jude had felt the map shift against his ribs and had kept his breathing level through sheer clerical discipline.

"You'll wonder why the Guildmaster wants you," Ostray said, conversationally, watching the window. "I'll tell you what I tell everyone. The Guildmaster wants what the ledgers say he's owed. Nothing more. Nothing personal." He turned his head. His eyes were the color of dishwater. "Though in your case, Master Eastwick, there may be a small complication."

"The courier," Jude said.

He hadn't meant to say it. The word left him before he could weigh it, and he watched it land — saw the tiny stillness in Ostray, the fractional pause of a man confirming a suspicion — and felt the first cold drop of the new thing he was going to have to learn tonight.

"So you've heard." Ostray sighed, folding his hands. "A courier failed to report. Assigned to your district, which makes you, administratively speaking, the last known point of contact. The Guildmaster will want to know everything you know. Everything." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "And you will want to tell him. Believe me. It goes so much more smoothly when people tell him."

The carriage turned. Through the window Jude caught a slice of the river, black and patient, and the far shore where the Vellum Guild house stood with its lamps burning in every window at every hour, because the Guild believed darkness was where mistakes were made.

His heart was doing something unprofessional. It was not fear, or not only fear. Underneath the fear ran a thin bright wire of something he had no honest name for — the same feeling, he realized with horror, that he got when a relic balanced perfectly, when the numbers agreed, when a dead man's last transaction finally reconciled. *Interest.* The Guildmaster wanted something from him, and some small ugly part of Jude was flattered.

He pressed the feeling down like a fly in a book.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane received him in the Map Room, because of course he did.

Jude had been inside the Guild house perhaps forty times in eleven years, always for audits, always escorted, always in the clerical corridors where the walls were hung with framed receipts for impossible things: *one year of someone else's luck; the echo of a bell rung before it was cast; forgiveness, certified, non-transferable.* He had never been invited up. The Map Room occupied the top floor of the tower, and its ceiling was painted with the sky as it stood on the night the Guild was founded, and its floor was covered — wall to wall, edge to edge — with maps.

Not maps of places. Jude understood that within four steps. Maps of *debts*. Cities rendered not in streets but in obligations, each district shaded by what it owed and to whom, rivers drawn as flows of payment, mountains as accumulated interest. In the corner nearest the great desk, one map glowed faintly under a lamp, and Jude made himself not look at it, and then made himself look, because a bone-carver who couldn't bear to look at bones was no use to anyone.

It was a map of a human skeleton. Full-length, anatomically exact, annotated in a fine red hand along every vertebra and rib. And it was not the map in his coat. It was older, cruder — a copy of a copy — but the notation matched, mark for mark, and the sight of it went through Jude like a fishhook.

"Master Eastwick." Corvane rose from behind the desk.

He was smaller than Jude remembered, and better dressed than anyone had a right to be at this hour, in dove-grey velvet with a chain of office that was not gold but carved bone, bleached white, each link a different knuckle. His face was pleasant the way a well-kept account book is pleasant: balanced, legible, utterly without warmth. He extended both hands and took Jude's right hand between them, and his grip was dry and cool and exactly firm enough.

"Thank you for coming at such an hour. Sit. Wine?"

"No, thank you."

"Of course. You're working." Corvane sat, gestured Jude into the chair across the desk, and laced his fingers. For a moment he simply regarded Jude with the mild attention of a man reading a column of figures, and Jude sat in the silence and listened — because listening was the one thing he was genuinely good at — and heard, beneath the tick of the tower clock, beneath Corvane's even breathing, a sound he had never heard in all his years of hearing the dead.

Nothing. Corvane made no sound at all. Not the hum of a living body, not the whisper of a haunted one. Just silence, vast and deliberate, like a room swept clean.

Every hair on Jude's arms stood up.

"I'll be brief," Corvane said, "because your time is valuable and mine is priceless. Last night a courier named Ferren Voss failed to make his report. You know the name."

It was not a question, but Jude answered it anyway, and here — here was the moment, though he didn't fully understand it until later — here was the moment he lied for the first time in his life.

"I know it from the ledgers," Jude said. "One delivery, my district. No return logged."

"Correct." Corvane smiled. "And did he reach you?"

The map lay against Jude's ribs, warm now from his own body, like something alive. He thought about the courier's hands, the blood between the fingers, the way the man had gripped Jude's wrist with the strength of the almost-dead and said *the choir* as if the words cost him his last coin. He thought about the ledger entry that refused to balance, and how he had spent eleven years believing that everything, given enough care, would balance eventually.

"He reached the street," Jude said. "I heard knocking. By the time I opened the door there was no one there. Fog was thick. If a man was dying twenty feet from my workshop, Master Corvane, then he died alone, and I'm sorry for it, but I never saw him."

Silence.

Corvane continued to smile. The smile did not change by a single muscle, and that was the most frightening thing Jude had ever seen — a face that could hold its expression while behind it something turned over, weighed, considered. The sick thrill in Jude's chest curdled even as it sang. *I did it,* part of him marveled. *I lied to the Guildmaster of the Vellum Guild, to his face, and he hasn't struck me down.* And another part of him, colder, was already cataloguing the sensation for what it was: the exact taste of the first theft. How easy. How *smooth*. How the second one would be easier still.

"Fog," Corvane said musingly. "Yes. The river keeps its secrets." He unfolded his hands and placed them flat on the desk, and Jude noticed for the first time the object sitting between them: a courier's satchel, empty, its strap cut clean through.

"We found this," Corvane said, "two streets from your workshop. Cut, not torn. Whatever Voss carried, he gave it to someone, or hid it, or lost it to a knife. The Guild has invested a great deal in that delivery, Master Eastwick. More than you could imagine. More than I am permitted to describe." He tilted his head. "So I find myself wondering what a careful man like you — a man who balances accounts for a living, who notices everything, who hears things, they say, that other men can't — might have noticed. On his street. In his fog."

*He's testing the lie,* Jude thought. *Not breaking it. Testing whether it holds weight.*

"I noticed the bells," Jude said. "Afterward. The alarm toll. I've worked for the Guild eleven years and I've never heard that pattern. That's all I noticed, and it frightened me, and I sat up with my lamp until it stopped." Every word true. That was the trick of it, he was discovering — build the lie out of truths, mortar them tight, and even a man who traded in impossibilities would find nothing to pry loose.

"The bells frightened you," Corvane repeated softly.

"They frightened everyone in the district."

"Mm." Corvane leaned back. And then, with the air of a man setting a second, finer trap inside the first, he said: "Tell me, Master Eastwick. What do you suppose the Guild *is*?"

Jude blinked. "An exchange. For impossible things."

"Everyone says that. Everyone is wrong, or rather, everyone is shallow." Corvane rose and walked to the glowing map of the skeleton, and stood before it with his hands clasped behind him, and the lamplight made his shadow stretch up the tower wall like something hanged. "Coin is only the smallest of our instruments. We trade in what cannot be minted. Years. Names. Voices. Loyalties. Grief, in bulk quantities." He glanced back over his shoulder. "People, Master Eastwick. People are the oldest currency there is, and the only one that appreciates. A man's debt can be bought. A man's fear can be bought. Given sufficient patience, a man himself can be bought, and unlike coin, he breeds more of himself."

The tower clock ticked. Somewhere below, in the clerical corridors, a clerk laughed at something, ordinary and human, and the sound made Jude's skin crawl.

"Why are you telling me this?" Jude asked.

"Because I value you." Corvane returned to the desk and sat, and the pleasantness of his face reassembled itself like water closing over a stone. "Eleven years, no discrepancies, no complaints, no ambitions. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone who comes to the Guild wants something. You only want the numbers to agree. It's a kind of innocence. I find it—" he searched, "—restful."

He slid a paper across the desk. A writ, sealed in green wax. Jude read it and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair.

*A commission of inquiry. Authority to enter any premises, examine any record, question any person, in the matter of the missing courier Ferren Voss. Signed: Corvane, Guildmaster.*

"This names you," Corvane said, "as my instrument in the search. You know your district. You know its dead — better than anyone, I'm told. If Voss passed anything to anyone in the fog, you will find it, and you will bring it to me, and the Guild will remember its friends." He smiled again, and this time the smile reached his eyes, which was infinitely worse. "And if it should turn out that Voss reached your door after all, and that something was left in your keeping—"

"Nothing was left in my keeping."

"—then the arithmetic," Corvane continued, as if Jude had not spoken, "becomes different. Theft from the Guild is not punished, Master Eastwick. It is *collected*. With interest. We are very patient creditors." He stood, extending his hand once more, dry and cool. "Find my courier's cargo. Sleep well. Oh — and Master Eastwick?"

Jude paused at the door.

"The bells will ring again tonight," Corvane said pleasantly. "Do listen for them. They mean we're getting closer."

* * *

They put him out on the street a mile from his workshop, as if distance were courtesy. The fog had thickened. Jude walked with the writ folded in his pocket like a knife against his thigh and the map against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and he made himself think, because thinking was the only tool he had ever trusted.

Corvane suspected him. Of course Corvane suspected him — the commission wasn't trust, it was a leash dressed as a gift. The moment Jude found nothing, he'd be useless; the moment he found something, he'd be guilty. There was no move on that board except the one Corvane hadn't imagined yet, because Corvane believed everyone wanted something, and Jude had just learned, in the worst hour of his life, that Corvane was nearly right. The sick thrill was still in him. He could still feel the shape of the lie in his mouth, smooth as polished bone, and he hated how well it had fit.

But Corvane had made one mistake, standing before that glowing map with his back turned. He had assumed Jude couldn't read the notation.

Jude could. He'd read it by lamplight in the first minute, before he made himself stop looking. Red ink, vertebra by vertebra, twelve marks descending the spine — binding-marks, choral notation, the kind used to keep voices in line. And beside the twelfth vertebra, in the old copy on Corvane's floor as surely as on the vellum against his ribs, the space for a name had been left blank.

Someone was being saved a seat.

Ahead, through the fog, the bells began again — three strokes, pause, three — sweeping the city like a hand searching under furniture. Jude turned away from his own street, away from his ruined, honest, audited life, toward the terraces where the court musicians kept their lodgings, toward the one singer in Vellum City who had refused, publicly and catastrophically, to sing for the king.

If anyone knew what those twelve marks meant, she did.

Behind him, faint under the bells, the dead in the relics he still carried whispered their broken instruction — *tell her, tell her* — and for the first time since the courier died, Jude thought they might mean it literally.
