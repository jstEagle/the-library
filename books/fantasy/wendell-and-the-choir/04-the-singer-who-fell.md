## 4. The Singer Who Fell

The map would not stop whispering.

Jude had laid it flat on his workbench beneath three river stones and a candlestick of lead, and still it murmured — not in words, exactly, but in the way a room murmurs after an argument has ended, the air itself still holding the shape of voices. He had worked with vellum all his life. Calfskin, lambskin, once the skin of a drowned sailor the Guild had purchased from a river-widow at a fair price. Every skin remembered something. But this — this was different. This skin remembered being *worn*.

He had not slept. The Guild's alarm bells had stopped tolling near dawn, which was somehow worse than the tolling itself; silence meant they had stopped searching loudly and started searching quietly.

So he did what he always did when he could not balance a ledger: he went to find someone who could read the entry.

There were perhaps six people in Vellamark who might recognize notation drawn on royal bone-vellum, and five of them were dead or employed by the Guild. The sixth was Sera Ilvane, who lived above a dyer's shop in Tanners' Row, where the air smelled permanently of vinegar and mordant, and where no one respectable went twice.

Jude went at dusk, carrying the map rolled inside a length of drainpipe, and told himself it was a professional consultation. He was very good at telling himself things. It was how he had survived twenty years of hearing the dead without once screaming in the street.

* * *

Sera Ilvane opened her door before he finished knocking.

She was smaller than the portraits had made her — the Guild's illustrated broadsheets had shown a towering figure in exile's grey, chin lifted, defiant as a statue of some inconvenient saint. In person she was a compact woman of perhaps forty, with ink-stained fingers and the particular posture of someone whose spine had been trained for twenty years to hold a note and now held only resentment. Her hair was going silver at the temples in two sharp wings, like frost climbing a window.

"You're the bone-carver," she said. Not a question. "Eastwick. The one who keeps the ledgers."

"I'm—"

"Everyone in this quarter knows your face. You came through last spring buying knucklebones off the rag-pickers." She stepped back from the doorway, which was either an invitation or an ambush, and Jude, having no better options, took it.

Her rooms were narrow and fiercely clean. Sheet music covered one wall, pinned in overlapping layers like feathers on a bird. A single chair faced a single window that looked out on nothing but the dye-house chimney. On the sill sat a chipped cup holding three dried flowers that might have been roses once, in another country, in another life.

"Close the door," she said. "And whatever you're carrying in that pipe, put it on the table. I could hear it from the stairs."

"The pipe is—"

"It hums," she said. "Everything you own hums. Sit down, Master Eastwick. You look like a man who hasn't slept, and I don't give consultations to standing men."

He unrolled the map.

He watched her face while she read it, because he had learned long ago that faces told truer stories than mouths. First there was nothing — the trained blankness of a performer. Then her eyes moved along the spine of the figure drawn there, the king's skeleton rendered in careful ink, each vertebra ringed with small dense characters, and the blankness cracked.

"Where did you get this?" Her voice had dropped half an octave. "Who died for it?"

"A courier. He came to my workshop last night with his side opened up. He said—" Jude heard himself say it again, and his mouth went dry around the words. "He said *the choir*. Then he died."

Sera Ilvane did not move for a long moment. The dye-house chimney coughed smoke past the window. Somewhere below, a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

Then she reached out and touched the map with two fingers, lightly, the way one touches a sleeping animal, and began to laugh. It was not a pleasant sound. It was the laugh of a woman who has waited years for a knock at the door and finds, when it finally comes, that the visitor is worse than the waiting.

"Do you know what they exiled me for?" she asked. "Officially?"

"Refusing to sing at the coronation renewal."

"Unofficially, I stood up in front of the assembled court and said the king was wearing people." She tapped the map, once, hard. "Not *possessed*. Wearing them. Like a coat. Like a choir robe. And every singer in that hall knew it — felt it, the way you feel a wrong note in your teeth — and every one of them kept singing, because the alternative was what happened to me." She looked up. "You want to know the worst part? They didn't even punish me properly. They just erased me. No trial, no scandal sheets. One day I was first soprano of the Court Choral; the next, my name wasn't in any program, any ledger, any memory. My own mother wrote to me asking who she was. So yes, Master Eastwick. I believe your dying courier. I have been *waiting* for him for eleven years."

Jude sat very still. The dead whispered constantly in his life, but the living almost never said anything worth hearing, and here was a woman saying all of it at once.

"Can you read these marks?" he asked.

"I can read them better than whoever drew them." She pulled the map closer, tilting it toward the lamp. "This isn't cartography. Look — the artist has drawn the skeleton true, but the annotations aren't anatomical. They're musical. Pitch names, duration marks, breath signs. Each vertebra carries a name and a part." Her finger traced down the column of the spine. "Cervical: four voices. Thoracic: eight. Lumbar—" She stopped. Her finger had stopped too, resting on a mark near the base of the drawing, and Jude saw her go pale in the lamplight in a way that had nothing to do with fear of him.

"What is it?"

"These are binding-marks." She said it flatly, the way one reports weather. "Old ones. Pre-Founding, maybe older. We studied them in conservatory — one week, one dusty lecture, 'historical abuses of the sung art,' and then we were told to forget them because no one alive could perform them." She laughed again, that terrible dry laugh. "No one alive. That's rather the point, isn't it? A binding-mark doesn't hold a living voice. It holds a voice that has nowhere else to go. You sing the name into the mark, and the dead singer sits down inside it and stays."

"Sits down," Jude repeated. His hands, on his knees, had gone cold. Twenty years of carving relics, twenty years of listening to the dead speak through bone, and it had never once occurred to him that someone might build a *chair* out of the principle.

"Twelve marks," Sera said. She counted them aloud, touching each, and her voice grew quieter with every touch until the last word was nearly breath. "Twelve names. Twelve singers bound into twelve bones of the king's own body. And the crown at the top of it all, conducting." She looked at him across the table, and her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath everything, lit with a hope so bitter it had curdled into something close to madness. "Eleven years ago I stood in that throne room and I *heard* it. Twelve-part harmony coming out of one throat. Perfect unison between the king and a hundred servants who should have had a hundred separate voices. And I thought — I'm mad. Grief does this. Overwork does this. Everyone told me so, kindly, patiently, until I believed them." She pressed her palm flat against the map. "Someone finally believes me. Do you understand what that's worth? Do you know what I would have paid, eleven years ago, for one person to say *I hear it too*?"

"I hear the dead," Jude said quietly. "It's my trade. Whoever died holding a piece of bone, I can hear them through it when I carve. The Guild uses me to authenticate relics." He hesitated, then gave her the truth, because she had given him hers. "Since the courier died, this map has been talking to me. Not words. More like... rehearsal. Like a choir warming up somewhere underneath the world."

Sera closed her eyes. When she opened them, the fury had settled into something colder and more useful.

"Then we're past believing," she said. "We're at doing. Which brings me to my price, Master Eastwick, because I assume you didn't crawl into Tanners' Row out of charity for my reputation."

"I don't have money."

"I don't want money." She stood, crossed to the wall of sheet music, and pulled down a yellowed page from near the top — a court program, ten years old, its printed text struck through with a single black line where her name had been. "I want to be there when it ends. Whatever *it* is. I spent six years singing beside two of these names." Her finger hovered over the map without quite landing, as though the ink might burn. "Marenne Cotrell. Aldous Fenn. Fellow students. Both died suddenly, both buried quickly, both — I realize now — never confirmed dead by anyone who'd actually seen a body. I sang their parts at their memorial services. I remember their voices better than my own." She turned to face him fully. "If they're sitting in those chairs, Master Eastwick, then someone owes them an ending. And if the Guild comes for you — and they will, they've already rung their bells for you — then you'll need someone who knows how the palace hears. How it listens. Where the acoustics carry and where they die."

"You're offering to help me break into the palace."

"I'm offering to help you do whatever has to be done," Sera Ilvane said, "to make sure no one ever sings in that building again without choosing to. Pack light. We leave before the morning bells." She paused at the window, silhouetted against the chimney-smoke, and added, almost as an afterthought:

"And Eastwick? There's a name missing from this map. Count again. Eleven marks filled, one blank. Choirs are greedy things. They always want one more voice."

Jude counted. She was right. At the base of the spine, where the twelfth mark should have been, the vellum lay smooth and empty — and as he looked at it, the humming under the world shifted key, warm and welcoming, like a chair being pulled out for a guest.

Outside, far away toward the Guildhall, a single bell rang once and stopped.

Not the alarm. Something quieter. Something patient.

"They've found the courier's body," Jude said.

"Then we're already late," said the singer who fell, reaching for her cloak.
