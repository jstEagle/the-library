## 5. Thief of Grief

Doss had three rules, and he'd broken all of them by noon.

Rule one: never work with strangers. Rule two: never work for free. Rule three — the important one, the one that kept him breathing — never stop moving.

He was currently sitting still in the back room of a bone-carver's ruined workshop, eating cold sausage off a knife-point while a disgraced soprano argued with a man who smelled of bone dust and bad decisions, and he was doing it *for free*, and every one of his rules lay around him like dropped coins.

"You're not listening," Sera Ilvane said. She had a voice like a struck bell even when she was angry, which was unfair, and she was using it now on Jude Eastwick, who had the particular stubbornness of a man who balanced ledgers for a living and could not abide an unbalanced anything. "The gates at Vellum Gate are warded against intent. You can't forge papers past them anymore — Corvane's people check the ink itself. What we need is someone who can walk through a crowd without being *seen*."

"And you think I'm that someone," Doss said, "because?"

"Because I've heard of you." She turned, and her eyes were the color of old wine and twice as sharp. "The Mourner. The man who cries at funerals he wasn't invited to."

"That's slander," Doss said. "I cry at funerals I was *paid* to attend. Mostly."

It was half true, which was the best kind of true. Doss made his living in mourning crowds, and there were always mourning crowds in this city, because the city was very good at killing people quietly and then being very sorry about it. He slipped into them the way water slips into cracks. A ward built to catch intent — to catch *want*, the hot little flame of theft or violence — found nothing in him to grip. Grief didn't register as intent. Grief registered as weather.

That was the trick, and the trick was also the trap, and nobody knew about the trap except Doss, and he intended to keep it that way until the day it ate him.

"Here's what I don't understand," he said, pointing the knife at Eastwick. "You've got a map drawn on the skin of a dead king. You've got a singer who got herself exiled from the palace. And you want to walk into the capital and do — what, exactly? Steal the crown off the king's head? Because I'll tell you now, friend, crowns are the one thing I've never lifted. Bad weight distribution. Everyone notices a missing crown."

"We're not stealing the crown," Eastwick said quietly. "We're returning something to it."

Doss looked at him. Eastwick had the kind of face that made you want to check your pockets — not because he looked dishonest, but because he looked like a man who would notice if you were, and Doss had spent twenty years perfecting the art of not being noticed. There was something else under it, too. Something that moved behind his eyes like fish under ice.

"The dead talk to you," Doss said. It wasn't a question. The whole city whispered about the bone-carver who heard whispers. "That true?"

"They talk," Eastwick said. "I listen. Sometimes they say useful things."

"Do they ever say anything about me?"

"Why would they say anything about you?"

"No reason." Doss grinned, and the grin fit his face the way a mask fits a face, snug and practiced. "Just checking my reputation extends past the grave. That's the dream, isn't it? To be remembered."

Sera snorted. Eastwick didn't smile at all, which told Doss more than a smile would have. Men who worked with the dead didn't joke about being remembered. They knew what remembering cost.

* * *

They left at dusk, because wards relaxed at shift-change and so did guards, and because Doss insisted, and they'd learned in two days that Doss insisting was like weather insisting. You dressed for it or you stayed home.

The city between the bonehouse district and Vellum Gate was holding a funeral.

There was always a funeral somewhere, but this one was large — a guildmaster's daughter, by the banners, drowned in the canal though everyone politely agreed she'd fallen. The mourning crowd spilled down the Processional like spilled ink, hundreds of them in grey, candles guttering in the wind off the river, and the sound they made was the sound Doss knew better than his own heartbeat: that low collective hum of grief, not weeping exactly, but the thing underneath weeping, the drone of a hundred hearts all beating slightly out of time with themselves.

"There," Doss said. "We walk in the middle of it. Stay close. Don't look at anyone's face."

"Why not?" Eastwick asked.

"Because faces are how you get caught. Look at feet. Feet don't lie and feet don't remember you." Doss rolled his shoulders, cracked his neck, and did the thing he did before every job, the thing no one had ever seen him do because he did it inside, behind his eyes, where it couldn't be witnessed.

He opened the door.

Not a real door. A door in himself, one he'd built years ago out of necessity and nailed shut from the outside, and every time he opened it he swore it would be the last time, and every time he opened it anyway, because a man has to eat, and because — though he'd die before admitting it — because opening it felt like drinking after a long thirst, and that was the part that frightened him most.

The grief came in.

It came in the way it always came: all at once, everywhere, a cold flood up through the soles of his feet. The guildmaster's daughter's mother, who was somewhere ahead in the crowd, grieving with a specificity that hit Doss like a fist — *the way she used to sing in the bath, the way she used to sing in the bath, who sings in the bath now* — and beside her a brother whose grief was angrier, sharper, edged with guilt because he'd been meant to meet her that night and had gone drinking instead. Behind them, an old servant who had loved the girl silently for thirty years and would never say so now. Behind him, a candle-seller grieving not the girl but his own son, dead eight years, using this funeral as a place to grieve where no one would ask why.

Doss took all of it. He took it the way sand takes rain.

This was the trick, the whole trick, the reason the Mourner could walk through a warded gate with a stolen ring in each boot: the ward reached for his intent and found nothing, because there was nothing of *him* anywhere near the surface. He was wearing other people's sorrow like a cloak, layer over layer over layer, and underneath the cloak there was just — nothing. A hollow. A man-shaped absence walking through the crowd, unnoticed not because he was hidden but because there was so little of him left to notice.

It worked beautifully. It had always worked beautifully.

And underneath the cloak, behind the door he'd opened, his own grief sat waiting. It sat the way a debt sits. The way winter sits behind autumn, patient, certain, already counting the days.

*Don't think about it,* Doss told himself, moving through the crowd, Eastwick and Sera tucked in behind him like ducklings. *Keep moving. Moving is the whole game.*

His own grief was nine years old and wore his sister's face.

He hadn't opened that particular box since — well. Since. There had been a night, once, in a rented room in Saltrow, when the jobs had stopped for a week and the money had run out and Doss had sat still for three days straight, and on the third day the door he'd nailed shut had come off its hinges, and he'd spent four hours on the floor of that room learning what drowning felt like from the inside. After that he'd made rule three. Never stop moving. A man in motion is a man the past can't corner.

"Your feet are dragging," Sera murmured at his shoulder.

"My feet are fine."

"You've gone grey. Your hands are shaking."

"It's called craft," Doss said. "You wouldn't understand. Singers just open their mouths and fall backward into the music. Thieves have to *work*."

She gave him a look that said she saw straight through him, which was impossible, because no one saw through him, that was the entire architecture of his life. But she let it go, and the crowd carried them forward, and the gate rose ahead out of the dusk — Vellum Gate, forty feet of black stone, its arch threaded with ward-chains that hummed a note only dogs and thieves could hear.

* * *

The gate-wardens were Guild men tonight. Doss clocked it immediately: the grey-and-bone livery worn over mail, the way they weren't checking papers so much as *faces*, the way their eyes moved with that flat mechanical patience he'd last seen on men hunting a murderer. They were looking for someone. Two someones, probably — a bone-carver and a fallen soprano — and possibly a third someone whose description they hadn't gotten yet, because Doss had made a career of descriptions sliding off him like rain off wax.

"Change of plan," he breathed. "We're not going through the gate."

"The map says—"

"The map doesn't know what I know. There's a second way. Drainage. Old cistern route under the gatehouse, predates the wards. The Guild sealed it forty years ago and then forgot about it because the Guild forgets everything that doesn't make money." He was already drifting sideways, peeling them out of the funeral crowd's current, toward a shrine alcove where mourners paused to light candles. "Problem is, the cistern mouth opens in the paupers' yard behind the Chapel of Rest. And the paupers' yard is—"

He stopped.

He hadn't meant to stop. His body simply decided, the way bodies do, and for one horrible moment Doss stood motionless in the candlelight of the shrine and felt the cloak slip.

It happened like this: a woman at the shrine was lighting a candle with hands that shook, and her grief was so raw and so familiar that it reached through the flood of borrowed sorrow and touched the box. *My boy,* she was thinking, not praying, just thinking, over and over, *my boy, my boy, my boy,* and the words went through Doss like a blade through wet paper, because he had thought those exact words, in that exact rhythm, kneeling in exactly such a yard nine years ago, in another city, in another life, over a grave so small the mason had charged him half price.

The cloak thinned. The flood receded. And underneath it, rising like a body surfacing in dark water, came—

"Doss." Eastwick's hand closed on his arm. Warm. Solid. Alive. "Hey. Thief. Stay with us."

"I'm here." His voice came out wrong. Scraped. He dragged the grin back up onto his face and pinned it there. "Just calculating angles. It's a very mathematical profession, thieving. Lots of geometry."

Eastwick looked at him with those ice-water eyes, and Doss had the sudden, appalling certainty that the man could hear something — not his thoughts, nothing so clean as that, but the shape of the silence in him. The hollow under the cloak. The box with the sister in it.

Whatever Eastwick heard, he had the grace not to name it. "The yard," he said instead. "You were saying."

"The yard's full of ghosts," Doss said, grateful for the pivot, hating how grateful. "Real ones, maybe — I don't know, that's your department — but certainly the memory kind. Paupers' yards are where the city puts the people it doesn't want to remember. Unclaimed dead. Plague pits. The Guild's old debts." He flexed his fingers, shook the cold out of them, and found to his relief that the cloak was settling back into place, the borrowed griefs flowing in again to fill the space his own had briefly occupied. "Most thieves won't work near one. Says it's bad luck. Really it's that the quiet gets into you, and quiet is where the past does its hunting."

"And you?" Sera asked. "Does the quiet get into you?"

"I'm mostly quiet-proof," Doss said. "Comes of being hollow."

He said it as a joke. It landed as a confession. Nobody laughed, including him.

* * *

The cistern route was everything he'd promised and less: low, dripping, black as a closed eye, running beneath the gatehouse foundations in a brick throat older than the Guild, older than the walls, maybe older than the crown itself. Doss went first with a shuttered lantern, and behind him came the bone-carver and the singer, and the three of them moved through the dark of the city's basement like secrets through a locked house.

Halfway along, the passage widened into the old cistern chamber, and Doss raised the lantern, and the light caught the walls.

Bones. Not arranged, not honored — *packed*. Generations of unclaimed dead stacked in the lime-white niches of the cistern walls, thousands of them, tens of thousands, a sediment of the forgotten. The air was thick and sweetish and utterly silent.

Doss's skin crawled. Not from the bones. From the silence.

Because the silence was full. He could feel it pressing against the cloak, against the door, against the box — a vast attention with nothing specific in it, the pressure of ten thousand people who had never been listened to, all of them, in some way he couldn't name and didn't want to, *aware*.

"This is the worst idea I've ever had," he said, "and I once stole a bishop's teeth."

"Keep moving," Eastwick said softly.

And then, as they crossed the cistern floor, Doss felt it happen — felt the pressure change, the way air changes before a storm. The silence leaned toward Eastwick. Toward the bone-carver specifically, the way flowers lean toward a window. Doss couldn't hear anything, not properly; whatever conversation the dead were having, it wasn't in words he was built to receive. But he could see it land on Eastwick's face, could see the man tilt his head, listening, and nod slowly, as if receiving directions.

"What," Doss said. "What are they saying."

Eastwick was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had that careful quality of a man choosing which truth to hand over. "They're saying there's a road ahead of you that you've been avoiding for a long time."

"I asked what they were saying, not for a fortune."

"They're saying—" Eastwick stopped. Started again. "They're saying the box you keep it in is getting thin."

The lantern shook in Doss's hand. Just once. Just enough.

He laughed — a real laugh, ugly and short, torn out of him rather than performed. "You know what your problem is, bone-carver? You listen to everybody. Dead kings, dead paupers, dead whoever. You never once think maybe a man keeps a box shut because the thing inside it is *his*, and he's earned the right to it, and hauling it out in a drainage tunnel in front of an audience is the last thing in the world he will ever, ever do."

"I know," Eastwick said gently. "I carve relics for a living. I know exactly what people keep shut, and why, and what it costs them to keep it that way." He turned back toward the far passage, where a ladder of rusted iron climbed toward a grate and, above it, the ordinary noise of the living city. "I'm not asking you to open it. I'm telling you what the dead told me, because they asked me to. They said: tell the thief the door doesn't hold forever. Tell him it's lighter carried than dragged."

Doss stood in the dark with ten thousand forgotten dead and two strangers and a lantern, and for a moment — one long, swaying moment — he wanted to sit down. Right there, in the lime and the dark, and stop moving, and let it come, let the whole nine years of it break over him like the flood he wore as a cloak, and be done, be wrung out, be empty in the honest way instead of the hollow way.

Then Sera sneezed at the dust, and the spell broke, and Doss discovered he was already climbing the ladder, because his body had made rule three for him, the way it always did.

Never stop moving.

The grate gave with a squeal. Night air poured in — horse dung and woodsmoke and frying onions, the glorious stink of the living city — and Doss hauled himself up into an alley behind the chandlers' row, inside the walls, past every ward Corvane's silver could buy, and pulled the others through after him.

"Well," he said, brushing lime off his knees, reassembling the grin piece by piece like a man rebuilding a wall. "That's the easy part done. Now we just have to sneak into a convent full of nuns and steal a princess."

"A princess," Sera repeated flatly.

"The king's sister," Eastwick said. "If she still lives. If she remembers."

Doss looked from one to the other, and then out at the rooftops of the capital, gold-lit and indifferent, and felt — beneath the cloak, beneath the grin, faint as a pulse — something that wasn't dread for once. Something almost like appetite.

"Nuns, a princess, and a choir of ghosts," he said. "Friends, I have a feeling this job is going to break every rule I own."

Somewhere across the city, deep in the Guildhall, a bell began to toll — not the alarm from the bonehouse district, but a new pattern, slower, heavier, a sound like a door closing — and Doss, who knew every bell in the city by its grief, realized he'd never heard this one before either.

Neither, judging by their faces, had the others.

"Move first," Doss said. "Ask questions never. Convent's east. Try to keep up."
