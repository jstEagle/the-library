## 6. The King's Forgotten Sister

The bells of Saint Vessia rang at dawn, and Maren counted them the way she counted everything now — out of habit, out of spite, because counting was proof that her mind still worked even if no one else believed it did.

Seven bells. Matins. Then the smaller bell for the kitchen, then the creak of Sister Odile's boots on the flagstones outside her cell, always three steps slower on the left than the right. Twenty years in this convent and she could have drawn the whole building from memory with her eyes shut: every stone, every draft, every lie.

She rose before the light finished arriving and knelt at her window to watch the walls of Corivenne catch the sun — that pale gold line along the ramparts, five miles east across the wet fields. From here the palace was only a suggestion, a crown of towers above the morning haze. Her brother lived inside it. Or something wearing her brother's face lived inside it. She had stopped being able to tell anyone which, including herself.

*You imagined it,* they had told her. First gently, by the physicians. Then firmly, by the priests. Then not at all, which was worse, because silence meant they had stopped bothering to correct her and had simply begun to manage her.

She pulled on her habit. The wool smelled of tallow and old rain. She had been beautiful once — there were portraits, though all of them had been burned or painted over, she'd learned that much from a visiting merchant who'd drunk too much communion wine — and she had been *recorded* once, her name inked into genealogies, announced at feasts. Princess Maren Aldousin, second child of King Edvard the Patient. Then Edvard died, and Aldric was crowned, and within a season Maren Aldousin had become Sister Maren of Saint Vessia, and within two more there was no record anywhere in the kingdom that a princess had ever existed at all.

Erasure, she had come to understand, was not a knife. It was an accounting error, repeated patiently until the error became the truth.

She went down to the chapel for prayers she no longer believed in, kneeling among women who had chosen their obscurity and therefore wore it lightly, while hers had been fastened onto her like a collar. She mouthed the responses. Above the altar, the painted Vessia gazed down with her serene, painted mercy, and Maren thought: *you never once answered me either.*

Because that was the thing. That was the thing she had carried for twenty years like a stone sewn into her chest.

Three nights before the coronation — the coronation as it had originally been planned, midsummer, the feast of Saint Aldous the Founder, when half the kingdom would have stood witness in the great square — Maren had been home. Home meaning the palace, meaning the wing reserved for family, meaning the rooms she'd occupied since birth. She had woken past midnight to weeping.

Not ordinary weeping. She knew ordinary weeping; she'd heard her father do it behind his study door after her mother died, the careful, ashamed kind. This was different. This was a sound dragged out of a person against his will, the sound of someone being *emptied*, and it came through the wall from her brother's apartments, and between the sobs — this was the part no one believed, this was the part that had unmade her life — between the sobs, Aldric had been singing.

Her brother could not carry a tune. He had joked about it his whole life, bellowing tavern songs flat as a dropped plate. But what came through the wall that night was harmony itself, layered voices, a dozen throats in perfect accord, singing words she didn't know in a cadence that made her teeth ache — and threaded through it, thin and drowning, her brother's own voice crying *no, please, I'm still here, Mare, I'm still—*

And then the coronation was moved. A royal proclamation, three weeks before midsummer: the ceremony postponed to the eve of autumn, "for reasons of state." No feast in the square. No public holiday. A closed rite in the palace chapel, witnessed by perhaps forty people, all of them chosen.

Maren had gone to the Lord Chamberlain. She had said, *something is wrong with my brother, I heard him, I heard them.* She remembered the exact look he gave her — not anger, not disbelief. Pity, worn like a mask over calculation. Within the month she was packed off to Saint Vessia "for her health," and the physicians used words like *grief* and *hysteria* and *the strain of mourning*, and her letters to friends went unanswered because her friends had been instructed, she later understood, to consider her dead.

Twenty years. She had spent twenty years being told she imagined everything, and the cruelest part — the part she admitted to no one, not even God, especially not God — was that some mornings she almost managed to believe them. Some mornings the memory softened and she thought, *perhaps it was a dream, perhaps grief for Father bent something in me, perhaps—*

And then, on the first cold morning of autumn, three travelers came up the convent road through the mist, and one of them carried a roll of vellum that made her twenty years of doubt burn away like frost off a slate roof.

* * *

Sister Odile brought the news at terce, breathless in a way that was nearly scandalous. "Visitors," she whispered. "At the gate. A carver, a woman who says she was a singer at court, and a young man who—" she hesitated, "—who apologized to me for existing. They're asking for you. By your *title*, Maren. Not your name. Your title."

Maren set down the candle she was trimming very slowly, so that her hands would not shake where Odile could see them.

"Show them to the parlor," she said. "And Odile — whatever you hear in that room, it stays there."

"I hear nothing in any room," Odile said, with the dignity of a woman who had survived four abbesses, and swept out.

Maren took the long way to the parlor. She needed the corridor's length to compose her face, because beneath the habit, beneath the twenty years, she was aware of a wildness rising in her like water coming to a boil, and wildness was the one thing she could not afford. If she came into that room screaming *I told you, I told you all*, she would be the madwoman again, and these strangers would leave, and the stone would go back into her chest for another twenty years.

So she walked slowly. She arranged herself. And when she opened the parlor door, she saw them — the bone-carver with ink-stained fingers and the look of a man perpetually apologizing; the singer, tall and hollow-eyed, standing as though a stage might appear under her feet; the thief perched on the windowsill like a cat that had decided the room belonged to him — and she said, calmly:

"You have until the noon bell. Speak."

It was the carver who spoke first. Jude Eastwick, he gave his name, and he held out the roll of vellum with both hands, the way one offers bread or a blade.

"Your Highness," he said. "We think your brother is dead. We think something else is wearing him."

* * *

They unrolled the map on the parlor table, weighting the corners with candlesticks, and Maren looked down at it and felt the floor tilt.

Vellum. She knew vellum — every nobleman's daughter knew calfskin from lambskin from the fine grey stuff made, rarely, terribly, by special license, from human skin. This was the last kind. She could tell by the way it took the ink, the faint grain like the ghost of pores. Someone had flayed a king to make this map, and the map charted — bones. A skeleton rendered in loving anatomical detail, spine to skull, each vertebra ringed with notation she couldn't read but recognized the *shape* of, because she had seen its like once before, scratched into the plaster wall of her brother's antechamber the week before the coronation was moved, and had been told by servants that it was nothing, a mason's mark, and had been told by everyone else that she was seeing things.

"That notation," she said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "I've seen it. In the palace. On the wall outside my brother's chambers."

Sera Ilvane — the singer — went very still. "Binding-marks," she said. "Choral binding. Each vertebra carries a name. Twelve names." She touched the rings of script with one fingertip, not quite touching. "Twelve singers, bound into the crown. Their voices sustain... whatever wears him."

"Then show me the date," Maren said. "If this thing was made for the coronation, there will be a date. There will be a record of when the binding was sung."

Jude turned the vellum toward the window light. Along the lower margin ran a line of small figures — numerals mixed with notation. He read them aloud, frowning, translating the carver's shorthand he clearly half-knew himself, and Sera leaned in beside him, and together they worked it out while Maren stood with her fists clenched inside her sleeves.

"The eve of autumn," Sera said at last. She looked up. "The changed date. The night with no witnesses."

"Yes," Maren said.

"But the original coronation—" Jude began.

"Midsummer," Maren said. "The feast of Saint Aldous the Founder. Proclaimed a year in advance. Every lord in the realm had accepted. Every road into the capital was booked solid with carriages. And then, three weeks before — a postponement. For reasons of state." She laughed, and it was not a pleasant sound; it startled even her, how much bitterness could live in one syllable. "Reasons of state. Do you know what I have called that night, for twenty years? I called it *the night they moved the wedding*. Because that's what it was. Something married itself to my brother, and it could not bear witnesses, because witnesses remember, and memory is the one thing it cannot abide."

The thief — Doss — had stopped lounging. He sat forward on the sill, and there was something in his face that was not mockery anymore. "You heard it," he said. "Before it happened. You heard the binding."

"I heard my brother," Maren said, "singing in twelve voices and begging for help between them. And I told the Lord Chamberlain, and the Lord Chamberlain told the physicians, and the physicians told my brother's new advisors, and within a month I was here." She looked around the parlor — the whitewashed walls, the plain cross, the herb garden visible through the window where she had spent two decades growing things that could not run away. "They erased me. Not killed — erased. There is no record in Corivenne that King Aldric ever had a sister. I checked. For years, I checked. Merchants, archivists, a herald I paid in smuggled candles. Nothing. It is as if I was scraped off the page."

"And you thought you imagined it," Sera said quietly. Not a question.

"Every day," Maren said. "Every single day, some part of me negotiated with the idea that I was mad, because madness would at least be *mine*. Madness would mean the world was still rational and only I was broken. What I could not bear—" and here her voice finally cracked, and she let it, because these three had earned the crack, "—what I could not bear was the alternative. That I was sane, and alone, and right."

The room was silent. Outside, the convent bells began to mark the hour, and Maren realized with distant surprise that it was not yet noon. They had done all this in less than an hour. Twenty years, undone in an hour.

Jude Eastwick cleared his throat. "Your Highness. Why did they let you live?"

"Because a dead princess leaves a grave, and graves get visited, and mourners talk," Maren said. "I understood that early. A mad sister in a convent is invisible. A dead one is a story. They chose invisibility." She untied the cord at her waist and let the habit fall open slightly, showing the plain wool dress beneath, the body underneath the body. "I have been furniture for twenty years, Master Eastwick. Furniture hears everything."

"What have you heard?" Sera asked.

Maren walked to the window. The palace towers stood in the haze, gold-tipped, patient.

"Twice a year, a carriage comes from the city. Never announced. It brings letters for the abbess — sealed, private, and the abbess burns them after reading, I've watched her do it from the orchard. The same seal each time. Not the royal seal. A guild seal." She turned back. "A wax stamp shaped like a quill crossing a ledger-line. Do you know it?"

Jude had gone the color of tallow. "The Vellum Guild," he said. "That's my guild. My *employers*."

"Then you may be the only man alive who can read those letters' language without opening them," Maren said. "Because I'll tell you what else I've heard. Every year, the abbess receives a payment. And every year, on the anniversary of the autumn coronation, she orders the chapel locked for the whole night and posts a rule that no sister may leave her cell. And every year — every year for twenty years — I sit by my window, and I listen toward the east, and twice, *twice*, I have heard it. Carried on the wind, five miles, which should be impossible. Singing. Twelve voices in perfect accord." Her hands had stopped shaking. That was the strange gift of rage fully crystallized: it stilled everything it touched. "It has been practicing, Master Eastwick. All this time, it has been practicing being my brother. And it is getting better."

Doss whistled softly. "Five miles. Through wind. That's not singing, that's a *claim*. That's a thing marking territory."

"It's a thing that thinks no one is listening," Maren said. "It forgot about me. Everyone forgets about me. It is the single advantage of my entire life, and I am done wasting it." She crossed to the table and put her hand flat on the map, on the flayed skin of some dead king, on the route of vertebrae that led, she now understood, from the base of the spine to the crown of the skull — a pilgrim's road written in bone, ending at the head of a living man.

"I know the palace," she said. "Not the way a servant knows it, or a guard, or a thief. I know it the way family knows it — the walls that are hollow, the doors that stick, the stair behind the chapel that Father sealed when I was nine because Aldric and I kept hiding in it. I can get you into the crypts. I can get you to my brother. And when we find whatever sits on his throne wearing his voice—" she looked up, and let them see all of it, the twenty years of it, "—I want to be in the room."

Sera spoke first, soft and fierce. "You're not what I expected, Highness."

"No one expects anything of me," Maren said. "It's marvelous. You can do anything at all."

Jude was looking at the map again, at the twelfth ring of notation near the crown, and his brow had furrowed in a way that made Sera lean toward him sharply, as if she already feared what he'd seen. He started to say something — "This name here, the last one, the script is different, it's almost as if—" — and stopped, and folded the vellum closed, and did not finish.

But Maren had spent twenty years learning to read the faces of people who were keeping things from her. She had invented the skill in this very convent, reading the abbess, reading the physicians, reading the pitying visitors. And she saw, in the glance that passed between the carver and the singer, a door closing on a room she had not yet been shown.

Later, she promised herself. Later, when we are moving, when they need me too much to refuse me — I will open that door.

For now, she rang for Sister Odile and asked for bread, cheese, and the good wine the bishop sent at Christmas, and began, for the first time in twenty years, to plan a journey east.

The noon bell rang. Somewhere beyond the fields, faint as a memory, the wind off Corivenne carried — or perhaps did not carry, perhaps it was only wind, perhaps it was only her — a thread of harmony, patient and practiced, singing her brother's stolen voice.

Maren stood at the window and listened until it faded, and smiled, and it was not a madwoman's smile at all.

"They moved the wedding," she said to the empty room. "I'm going to attend the funeral."
