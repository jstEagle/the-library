## 7. Enforcers at the Bonehouse

The dead woke him before the bells did.

Jude had fallen asleep at his workbench again, cheek on the ledger, a half-carved knucklebone still pinched between his fingers — an old woman's finger-bone, bought fair from her granddaughter, and through it the woman herself murmured her endless inventory of pickled things: *sixteen jars of plums, fourteen of eel, the good vinegar in the blue crock, don't let them take the blue crock.* She had been reciting for three days now, patient as tide. He'd learned to sleep inside other people's voices the way sailors sleep inside storms.

So when the murmur stopped mid-jar, he woke.

That had never happened. The dead did not stop. The dead did not *hurry*. But the old woman's voice went thin and urgent, like smoke pulled backward into a chimney: *Boy. Boy. They're coming up Candlemaker's Row. Six of them. Iron shoes. They have your name written on vellum.*

He sat up so fast his neck cracked. The workshop was dark except for the banked coals of the carving lamp and the grey line of pre-dawn under the door. Outside, the city was quiet in the way cities are quiet just before they decide not to be.

*Which way will they come?* he asked the bone, because you could ask the dead anything if you held their remains and kept your voice steady.

*Front,* she said. *But one is circling to the yard. They mean to leave nothing to bury.*

Jude Eastwick had kept ledgers for eleven years, and in eleven years he had never once run from anything. Running was what debtors did. Running was what liars did. He was a man of accounts: every bone entered, every voice heard, every whisper transcribed and balanced against the Guild's great books. His life was a column of figures that always summed.

He took the map from its hiding place inside the hollow leg of his workbench — vellum made, Sera said, from a king's own skin, charted with the royal skeleton itself — rolled it tight, and pushed it deep into the breast of his coat, against his heart, where it lay like a second heartbeat that wasn't his.

Then, because he was still Jude Eastwick even with iron shoes coming up Candlemaker's Row, he did something no sensible man would do. He opened the ledger to the last clean page and wrote, in his small exact hand:

*Inventory removed for safekeeping: one map, provenance royal, value unreckonable. Debtor: the Vellum Guild, who taught me that everything has a price, and never told me I would be the price.*

He blotted it. He left the book open on the bench where anyone could read it. Let Corvane choke on an honest entry.

*Back stairs,* the old woman whispered. *Quickly, boy. The blue crock — never mind the crock. Go.*

* * *

They came through the front door as he came out the back, and the two events met in the middle of his life with a sound like the world's largest book being torn in half.

Jude was three steps down into the bone-cellars when the first enforcer's boot hit the workshop floor above. He felt it through the stone — a thud, then another, then the particular grinding crash of his workbench going over, the ledgers sliding, the little drawers of sorted relics spilling their careful order across the floor. Eleven years of taxonomy destroyed in eleven seconds. He knew each drawer by touch. Third from the left, the teeth of children who died of fever. Second row, the wrist-bones of hanged men, which whispered rope-ropes-ropes in their sleep. The shelf where he kept the only relic he'd ever carved for himself: his mother's wedding ring, bone-white, carved from her own finger after the fever took her, because she'd asked him to and because hearing her hum lullabies had been worth every hour of the carving.

He heard it crack under a boot heel from thirty feet of solid rock away.

Jude put his hand flat against the cellar wall and made a sound he didn't recognize as coming from himself — low, animal, brief. Then he stopped, because the wall answered.

The bone-cellars ran beneath the whole of Candlemaker's Row, older than the Guild, older than the Row itself; some said older than the city walls. Every carver who had ever worked above ground had braced these tunnels with the leavings of their trade — offcuts, failures, bones too damaged or too haunted to sell. A hundred years of discarded voices lined the walls, mortared into the stone like fossils, and Jude had walked these corridors a thousand times hearing nothing but the usual dim sea-sound of the mostly-forgotten dead.

Now they were shouting.

Not with mouths. With attention. It came up through his palms and the soles of his feet, a pressure, a direction, the way water finds the crack in a dam: *left, boy, left, the iron men are marking the doors above; down, down, they know about the cistern stair; not that way, that way floods.* And beneath the directions, threaded through them like gold wire through rough cloth, names — his name, *Eastwick, Eastwick, the carver, the listener, keep him, he hears us, no one else has ever heard us* —

"You're guiding me," he said aloud, stupidly, to a tunnel full of dead strangers' cast-off bones. "Why?"

The answer came from everywhere and nowhere, from a femur set into the wall beside his shoulder, worn smooth by someone's long-ago chisel: *Because you listened when we were merchandise. Because the ones who hunt you sell us by the pound. Run, carver. We will hold the dark open for you.*

He ran. Behind him, muffled through stone, the workshop burned. He smelled it before he understood it — tallow-smoke and scorched bone, a smell he would carry in his nose for the rest of his life. Enforcers did not search, apparently. Enforcers erased. Somewhere above him, eleven years of his life was becoming ash and cracked marrow, and the Guild's accountants would enter it all in neat columns: *one workshop, razed; stock, destroyed; carver Eastwick, delinquent, reward offered.*

His eyes stung. He told himself it was the smoke.

* * *

The cellars narrowed, branched, descended. Twice he heard iron boots overhead, tracking him by the sound of his breathing, and twice the dead misdirected the hunters — he felt them do it, felt the walls themselves seem to lean, felt some trick of echo and old stone send his footsteps bouncing west while he went east. Once an enforcer actually descended into the tunnels, torch-first, and the darkness ahead of Jude simply *closed*, the dead pressing their silence into the air so thickly that the man's torch guttered and his nerve broke and he climbed back up swearing about rats.

"Thank you," Jude whispered, hoarse.

From the walls, faintly, amused: *Mind the step.*

It should have been comforting. It was, in its way — more comfort than he'd known in years. But there was grief in it too, grief with edges. All this time. All this time the dead had been waiting in the walls of his own trade, listening to him balance their prices in ledgers, and he had never once thought to ask them for anything but information. He had treated them exactly as the Guild treated them. Sources. Stock. Entries in a book.

"I'm sorry," he said to the tunnel, and meant it, and the tunnel — gently, terribly — forgave him, which was worse.

The cistern stair let out behind the tanneries, where the air was thick enough to chew and no enforcer with a working nose would linger. Dawn was coming up the colour of dirty pewter. Jude stood in the reeking alley with the map against his chest and the smell of his burning life in his hair, and he made himself think like a ledger instead of a man, because a man would stand here weeping and a ledger would find the next entry.

Assets: one stolen map of the king's bones. One gift the Guild suddenly wanted badly enough to burn buildings for. Liabilities: no workshop, no stock, no home, a name on a hunting-warrant. Outstanding obligations: a dying courier's last word — *the choir* — and a promise he hadn't known he was making when he caught the man as he fell.

Where did people go when the Guild hunted them? Not the inns — the Guild owned the inns. Not the temples — the Guild tithed the temples. You went to the people the Guild had already broken, because broken people kept the only honest books left in the city.

Sera Ilvane was lodging above a dyer's shop in Weft Street, exiled soprano, sleeping in a room that smelled of indigo and defeat. Doss was wherever Doss was, which was nowhere, which was everywhere, which was the space in a mourning crowd that everyone's eyes slid past. And beyond the walls, past the warded gates he could no longer pass openly, in a convent he'd never visited, waited a princess whose name appeared in no record he had ever audited — which, Jude now understood, was itself a kind of entry. Erasure was a transaction too. Someone had paid to make Maren disappear, and debts like that always came due.

*Weft Street first,* whispered the bones in the cellar-wall behind him, reluctant to let him go. *And carver — when you reach the palace, remember us. Remember what they built their crown from.*

"I'll write it down," Jude said. "All of it. In a ledger no one can buy."

* * *

He found Sera already awake, already packed, standing at her narrow window with her back straight as a pikestaff, watching smoke rise over Candlemaker's Row.

"They burned it," she said, without turning. "I saw the glow an hour ago. I've been standing here deciding whether to run alone or wait to see if you were alive." Her voice did something complicated on the last words. "I'm glad I waited. Sit down. You look like something the tide rejected."

"The Guild knows about the map." He told it plainly, sitting on her cot among her two remaining dresses: the courier, the chase, Corvane's silk-and-knives summons, the lie he'd told — his first lie, and how it had tasted of copper and freedom. "They're not hunting a thief, Sera. They're hunting a replacement. I don't know yet why me. But the twelfth chair of whatever this choir is — I think they intend to fill it."

Sera had gone very still, the stillness of a woman hearing an old nightmare knock politely at the door. "Eleven singers," she said. "I told you what I saw from inside the palace. Eleven voices in perfect accord, and the king's mouth moving with them. If there's a twelfth chair empty—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "They auditioned us, Jude. Before my exile. The court choir, the 'renewal performance.' They listened to us sing for hours, and the king never blinked, and afterward two of the girls who sang best — Brissa and Annet — they died. That's what we were told. Died. Within a week of each other." Her hands had begun to shake. "I refused to sing at the renewal and they called it treason of the throat. But I think — God help me, I think I wasn't refused exile. I was *spared*. Discarded as insufficient."

The room was quiet. Below them the dyer's vats breathed their chemical breath, and somewhere a cart rattled toward the gates.

"Then we get the others," Jude said. "Doss can take us through the gates inside a funeral crowd — grief is the one thing the warders don't check. And the princess—"

"Maren." Sera almost smiled, bitter as alum. "The sister nobody remembers. Yes. If anyone can confirm when the coronation changed and why, it's the woman they wrote out of the world."

A soft knock at the door — three quick, irreverent taps, nothing like a Guildman's fist. Sera opened it on Doss, thin and grinning and smelling faintly of other people's sorrow, a satchel over one shoulder and soot on his cheekbones.

"Heard you were having a fire," he said, strolling in uninvited, helping himself to the room's single stool. "Terrible thing, fires. Also terrible thing, the six large gentlemen asking after a bone-carver at every gatehouse since midnight. Lucky for you I collect gossip the way other men collect debts." He looked at Jude, and the grin dropped away all at once, like a mask on a string. "You're burnt. Not your skin. Underneath. I can walk through grief, friend, and yours is coming off you in sheets." He dug in his satchel, produced a heel of bread and a flask, and shoved them across. "Eat. Grieve later. We've a convent to burgle and a princess to annoy, and if we're not past the north gate by noon bell, the next chapter of your life gets very short and very loud."

Jude looked around the dye-stained room at the three of them — a disgraced singer, a thief made of borrowed sorrow, and himself, a carver with no workshop and a king's skeleton folded against his heart. None of them belonged anywhere. All of them had been entries in someone else's ledger: *exiled, erased, expendable.*

Well. He knew how to start a new book.

"We'll need horses," he said, "or a miracle."

"Horses," said Doss, standing and stretching until his spine popped. "Definitely horses. Miracles cost extra."

And far below Weft Street, deeper than any of them guessed, in the dark under the burning ruin of the Bonehouse, the dead settled along their mortared walls and began — patiently, hungrily, hopefully — to count the hours until the carver came back and gave them all a voice.
