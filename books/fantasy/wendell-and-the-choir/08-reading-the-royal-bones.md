## 8. Reading the Royal Bones

Sera Ilvane had spent eleven years trying not to think in notation. It was a discipline like any other — you built it the way you built a high C, breath by breath, refusal by refusal — and for eleven years it had held. She had scrubbed floors at the convent of Saint Vessia with her mind deliberately empty of staves. She had let the chapel bells ring without counting their intervals. She had even, in the worst winter, stopped humming.

Then Jude Eastwick unrolled the map across the trestle table in Maren's cell, weighted its corners with candlesticks and a loaf of bread, and every wall she had built came down at once.

The vellum was wrong. She knew that before she knew anything else. Vellum has a grain, a faint nap that catches candlelight unevenly, like skin — because it *is* skin, usually sheep or calf, and anyone who has spent years handling choir folios learns to read its surface the way a blind woman reads a face. This vellum was too fine. Too pale. It caught the light the way the inside of a wrist does, and when Sera laid her palm flat against it, it was warm.

Not lamp-warm. Body-warm.

"Jude," she said. "What did the courier tell you this was made from?"

"Vellum." He was standing at the window, one hand on the shutter, watching the road below the convent walls. He hadn't slept; the hollows under his eyes had gone the color of old ivory. "He didn't have time for details."

"It's human," she said. "And not just human. Old. Cured slowly, stretched over years — this isn't something you make in a week. Someone kept this skin the way a vintner keeps a cask." She heard her own voice go flat and clinical, the voice of a woman reading a program, and hated how steady it was. "Someone wore this person first. You don't cure a hide this well unless you've worn it close for a long time."

Maren went still by the hearth. Doss, who had been picking at the bread, put it down.

"The king's own bones," Jude said quietly. "That's what he called it. A map of the king's own bones."

"Then we should hope the king never learns what his map is wearing." Sera bent over it.

The drawing itself was elegant — of course it was, everything the Guild touched was elegant, evil dressed in good tailoring. A skeleton, drawn life-size in fine brown ink, each bone rendered with an anatomist's precision: the skull crowned, the spine descending in a long graceful curve down the center of the sheet, ribs fanning out like the frame of a fan vault, the long bones of the arms and legs extending toward the margins where they simply *ended*, cropped by the edge of the skin they were drawn on. Around the skeleton ran columns of small marks, dense as insect tracks.

She had expected words. Names, dates, instructions. What she found instead was music.

"Oh," she said. And then, because some part of her was still a girl of nineteen standing in the palace rehearsal hall with her heart going like a hammer: "Oh, no."

"What?" Maren crossed to her. "What is it?"

"It's a score." Sera traced the columns without touching them. "These aren't annotations. They're voice parts. Look —" she moved her finger up the left margin, "— this line sits high, here, see the interval jumps? That's a soprano line. Descant tessitura, written for someone comfortable above the staff. This one on the right never leaves the lower fifth — bass. These are *parts*, Princess. Twelve of them. One for each —"

She counted. She counted twice, because the first time she made it eleven and her mind refused the number, and the second time she found the twelfth column tucked along the spine itself, following the vertebrae down like a runner of embroidery.

Twelve.

"Each bone has a name," Jude said from the window. His voice had changed. "I noticed last night. I thought they were owners' marks. Guild inventory."

"They're not inventory." Her mouth had gone dry. "Look at the third cervical vertebra. There — the small mark beneath the joint."

He came to the table. So did Maren. Even Doss drifted over, though he pretended to be interested in the candle.

The mark was small, no bigger than a thumbnail: a vertical stroke crossed by three horizontals, with a hook at the base and two dots flanking the head of the stroke. Anyone else would have seen a sigil, a maker's stamp, a decorative flourish. Sera saw a note. Not a pitch — a *name*. The binding-marks were a notation she had seen exactly once in her life, scratched into the margin of a forbidden folio by a teacher who burned it afterward and told her she had imagined it.

It was a system for writing names as music. Every name could be sung. Every name, once sung correctly, could be *held*.

"These are binding-marks," she said. "Old ones. Pre-Guild, maybe — or Guild-founding, which may be the same thing. Each mark encodes a name phonetically. Pitch for the vowels, duration for the consonants. If you sing the name as written — precisely as written, in tune, in time — you don't just say it. You fix it. You pin the person to the sound." She looked up. "You bind them."

"To what?" Doss asked. For once there was no humor in him at all.

Sera looked back down at the map, at the twelve columns running alongside twelve regions of the skeleton, and felt the answer arrive in her body before her mind, cold moving down through her chest like well water.

"To the bone. Each singer is bound to a bone. Look at the distribution — the skull carries the descant, of course, the crown of the head, the highest resonance chamber in the body. The sternum takes the second soprano. The ribs take the altos — twelve ribs, but only four marked, the rest left clear for — for resonance, God help me, the ribcage is a *sounding box* —" Her voice cracked and she pressed on. "The pelvis takes the tenors. The femurs the baritones. The spine runs the whole score, top to bottom, all twelve parts in sequence. It's not a map of a skeleton. It's an *instrument*. A body built to be played."

Silence. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere below, a cow complained to the grey morning.

"That's why the king sings in unison with his servants," Maren said at last. Her voice was very controlled, the control of a woman who had spent twenty years being told she was mad and had finally stopped believing it. "I told you what I saw. A hundred voices, one voice. I thought it was training. Court discipline."

"It's leakage." Sera's hands were shaking now; she folded them to stop them and they shook anyway. "A bound voice doesn't stay politely inside its bone. It resonates. It wants company — harmony is a hunger, ask any singer. The choir sings through the king, and everything near him starts to come into tune. The servants. The court. Maybe the whole city, given enough years. Hollowmere —" she glanced at Doss, who looked away, "— Hollowmere was a rehearsal. This is the performance."

"And the names," Jude said. He had gone very quiet, the way he went quiet in the workshop, she imagined, when a relic began to speak. "Read them."

"I don't know if I —"

"*Read them.*"

Because he needed to know, she understood. Because he had carried this map for days, had lied to Corvane for it, had lost his workshop and his ledgers and his whole careful transactional life for it, and he deserved to know what he was carrying. She took a breath the way she took breaths before entrances — low, disciplined, filling the back of the ribs — and sounded out the first mark.

The descant, at the crown. Three syllables, rising. "O-ren... vel... la. Orenvella." The name sat strangely on her tongue, half familiar, like a word from a dream. "Second soprano, sternum: Cassian... der... voth. Cassiandervoth." Down through the altos at the ribs: "Meira. Liss. Odile-of-the-Weir. Anselm." The tenors: "Perrin Duval. Tomas-who-was-called-Finch." Baritones: "Harrow. Bellamy Cray." Bass, at the seat bone, deepest of all, a name that seemed to hum in the floorboards when she said it: "Greville."

Twelve names. She said all twelve, and the cell felt smaller after, the way a room feels after a bell has been rung in it.

And then she reached the eleventh name, the second-to-last baritone, the mark at the left femur, and stopped breathing.

Three syllables. Low, then a leap, then a fall. She knew that leap. She had heard that exact melodic shape a thousand times, because it was the way his name *wanted* to be said — because he had joked about it, once, laughing, in the novices' gallery: *even my name is a falling phrase, Sera, my mother doomed me to melancholy before I could walk.*

"Tobias," she whispered. "Tobias Renk."

Nobody spoke. The fire ticked.

"He was in my cohort." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Palace choir school. He stood behind me and to the left — second row, always second row, he sang baritone and he was *good*, better than good, he had this — this dark bottom to his tone that the masters said was wasted on a boy his age. We used to sneak honey-cakes from the coronation feast rehearsals. He wanted to compose. He wrote a motet for four voices that the choirmaster called 'technically accomplished and spiritually unsuitable.'" She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Spiritually unsuitable. Tobias. He was the most spiritual person I ever —"

She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she took them away the map was still there, patient, warm, waiting.

"He died," she said. "The year before my exile. Fever, they said. They buried him fast — no lying-in-state, no requiem, which was strange, because he was palace choir, he'd earned a requiem. I remember being angry about that. I remember thinking, *he gave them six years of his voice and they won't even give him one mass.*" She looked up at Jude. "There was no fever. There was a chair. There was someone with a stylus and this notation, and they sang his name until it stuck."

"The other one," Jude said gently. "You said two."

"Odile." The name came out of her like a splinter. "Odile-of-the-Weir — she took the court name when she joined, we all did, but she was Odile Marsh before, from the river districts, alto, sat beside me for three years. She had a crooked tooth and she covered her mouth when she laughed and she was the only person in that whole gilded hive who asked me questions about myself instead of telling me things about themselves." Sera's throat closed. She forced through it. "She 'drowned.' Two years before Tobias. They pulled her from the palace cistern. Except Odile swam like an otter, grew up on the Weir, she used to say water was the only place the masters couldn't follow her. And now here she is." Her finger hovered over the fourth rib-mark, not quite touching. "Here she is, pinned to a rib in the king's chest, singing forever."

Doss said, softly, "They didn't die. They were *harvested*."

"Yes."

"For the crown."

"For the crown." Sera straightened. Something was happening in her chest, something hot and old, a coal that had been banked for eleven years under ash and routine and deliberate forgetting, and it was catching now, and she let it. "Do you understand what this means? Everyone thinks the king is enchanted. Possessed. Some demon in a crown, poor man, tragic man. But demons are simple. Demons you can exorcise. This —" she swept her hand across the map, "— this is *people*. Twelve people, stolen one at a time, across — how many years? The binding-marks are layered; look, the ink over Tobias's mark is darker, newer. They've been added *sequentially*. One voice every few years, maybe. A slow rotation. A choir that replaces its singers as they wear out."

"Wear out how?" Maren asked.

"I don't know. Burn out. Get devoured. But it means the twelfth chair —" and here her voice did break, properly, and she hated herself for it and kept talking anyway, "— the twelfth position doesn't stay empty out of sentiment. Somewhere in the capital there is a room with twelve chairs, and one of them is waiting, and the Guild knows it, and the Guild is hunting us because —"

She stopped.

Because the thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do, fully dressed and smiling.

"Because of *you*," she said to Jude. "Oh, God. Oh, Jude. Your gift. You hear the dead through what they touched. You're not a threat to the choir. You're a *candidate*. Who better to bind than a man who already listens? Who already gives his attention to the dead for hours at a day? Half the work is done — they wouldn't need to teach you to surrender your voice, you've been pouring it into bones since you were a child. The twelfth mark isn't blank because they haven't chosen." She gripped the edge of the table. "It's blank because they've been waiting for *you*."

Jude didn't move. He stood in the grey light from the window with the map's warmth radiating against his hands, and his face did something she had never seen a face do — it went through fear and arrived somewhere past it, at a kind of terrible confirmation, the look of a man whose ledger has finally balanced in the worst possible way.

"The courier," he said. "He didn't give me the map to save the king."

"He gave you the map because you're the only bait that works," Sera said. "Or the only weapon. I don't know which yet. Maybe both. Maybe the dead who guided him knew exactly what they were doing." She rounded the table, took his shoulder, turned him to face her. He was younger than she'd first thought, and tired, and so alone it hurt to look at him. "Listen to me. Whatever happens — whatever Corvane offers, whatever the choir whispers, however much they promise you'll get to *keep listening* — you do not sit in any chair. You do not sing any name they give you. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I can't keep," Jude said. "Ask my ledgers."

"Then make one you can."

For a moment he said nothing. Then: "I promise I'll decide for myself. That's the only honest promise there is."

It wasn't what she wanted. It was, she suspected, the most he had. Outside, the convent bell rang prime, and the sound fell over the fields like a net, and Sera thought: *they rang bells like that the morning they told us Odile drowned. Bells are just binding-marks for buildings.*

"We need to move," Maren said, already reaching for her cloak. "If the Guild wants Jude for the twelfth chair, then every hour we travel together, we're delivering him."

"No," Sera said.

They all looked at her.

"We're not delivering him anywhere. Because I know this notation now — really know it, not just read it. Binding-marks can be *un*-written. Any note that can be sounded can be countered; that's the first law of harmony. Sing the name wrong — a quarter-tone off, a duration stretched — and the binding frays. Sing twelve names wrong, in twelve voices, at once, and —" She let the sentence hang, unfinished, enormous.

"You want to sing them apart," Doss said. "Unravel the whole score."

"I want to give Tobias a requiem," Sera said. "Eleven years late. And Odile. And every name on this cursed skin." She rolled the map closed, carefully, the way you handle a wound. "But first we need to reach the crypt where the instrument is kept. And first before that —"

The knock came at the convent door. Three strikes, evenly spaced, patient as a metronome.

Everyone froze. From the courtyard below, Sister Betha's voice, puzzled and polite: "Yes? May I help you, sir?"

And a voice Sera knew — silk over wire, a voice she had heard across a burning workshop — answered, warm as a tuning fork:

"Good sister, I've come about a debt. Tell Master Eastwick that Guildmaster Corvane does not enjoy being lied to twice."
