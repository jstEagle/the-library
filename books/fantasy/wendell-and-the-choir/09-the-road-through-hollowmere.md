## 9. The Road Through Hollowmere

Doss had a rule about villages: never sleep in one. Villages were where grief went to settle, the way silt settled in riverbends. Cities you could lose yourself in — grief got diluted there, spread thin across ten thousand lives until it was just weather. But a village held everything it had ever lost, generation upon generation of it, packed into a few muddy lanes and a churchyard, and if you stood still long enough in a place like that, it would find you.

So he kept moving. That was the whole trick of Doss Fenwick: motion. He'd been running since he was eleven years old, since the night his mother died and he discovered that if he walked through a crowd of mourners wearing their sorrow like a borrowed coat, nobody looked at him twice. Grief made people invisible. He'd built a life on that discovery, a career out of it, and if you asked him — which nobody did — he'd tell you it was a fine arrangement. He took other people's sadness so they could set it down for an hour, and in exchange he stayed unseen, uncaught, unburied.

The only flaw in the arrangement was that he never put any of it down.

He carried it all. Forty years of other people's worst days, folded and stacked inside him like contraband. He knew how to keep it packed. He knew the weight of every bundle by feel, the way a smuggler knows his cargo. The widow's grief from Marrowgate, light as ash. The father's from Cullis Row, dense as wet stone. And underneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth and chained shut, the one bundle that was his own — the one he hadn't opened since he was eleven, because he was fairly sure that when he finally did, there wouldn't be enough left of him to close it again.

Keep moving. That was the discipline.

Which was why Hollowmere annoyed him before they even reached it.

* * *

They came down off the ridge road in late afternoon, four travelers with dust on their boots and the capital still two days east. Jude walked in front with the map rolled tight against his chest like a man carrying a live coal. Sera had her arms crossed against a wind that wasn't cold. Maren rode the mule with the rigid spine of someone who'd spent twenty years being told she didn't exist.

Doss brought up the rear, because the rear was where he always walked — close enough to see everyone's faces, far enough that no one watched his.

"Something's wrong with that village," Maren said.

"You say that about every village," Sera said.

"And I'm right more often than not."

Doss squinted at Hollowmere below them. It sat in a bowl of green fields, tidy as a toy: slate roofs, a millpond catching the last sun, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless air. Smoke rising straight. That was the first thing. Windless days happened, but combined with what he saw next, he started counting.

There were people in the fields. A dozen or more, bent over rows of late cabbages, working. And every single one of them turned to watch the road at exactly the same moment.

Not one after another. Not a ripple of heads turning as the news passed along the row. All at once, like a flock of birds changing direction.

Doss had seen crowds do a lot of strange things. He'd slipped through funerals where the mourners swayed together, through riots where rage moved through bodies like current through water. Crowds were animals; they synchronized. But this was different. This wasn't feeling spreading. This was one feeling, worn by many faces, and it arrived on all of them simultaneously.

"They've seen us," he said quietly. "All of them. At once."

Jude stopped walking. The bone-carver had a way of going very still when something frightened him, hands tucked into his sleeves like a man keeping them warm, though it wasn't cold either. "Maybe they're just friendly."

"Nobody's friendly all at once," Doss said.

They stood on the road a moment longer, four figures deciding whether to walk into a picture that had already noticed them. Then Maren clicked her tongue and the mule moved forward, because Maren had spent twenty years being erased and had developed a violent allergy to turning back.

The village smiled at them the whole way in.

That was the second thing. Every face they passed wore the same smile — not similar, not the general good cheer of country folk seeing strangers, but *identical*. Same width. Same lift at the corners. Same crinkle around the eyes, same exact degree of teeth showing. Doss tested it the way he tested everything, counting. He counted forty-one smiles between the village gate and the well at the center of town. Forty-one smiles, one smile.

He'd stolen joy before. It was rarer work than stealing grief — joy was heavier, stranger stuff, and most people guarded it — but he knew its weight. Joy had a texture like warm bread. What hung over Hollowmere felt like warm bread too, fresh from an oven, except that under the smell of it was something else. Something faintly wrong, the way meat can be wrong. Sweetness laid over rot.

And beneath the sweetness, almost too deep to notice: nothing. Where the grief should have been — and every village had grief, rivers of it, decades of it soaked into the churchyard walls — Hollowmere was dry. Doss walked its lanes and felt no sorrow at all, and that absence scared him worse than the smiles, because he had never once in his life walked into a human settlement and found it empty of mourning.

It was like arriving at a house and finding the cellar bricked over.

"We need supplies," Jude said. "Feed for the mule. We shouldn't stay past dark."

"Past dark," Doss agreed, and meant *not one minute longer.*

* * *

The innkeeper's wife served them stew with that smile fixed on her face, and Doss watched her hands while she worked, because hands told the truth mouths were taught to hide. Her hands were careful. Competent. And trembling — just slightly, just at the edges, the tremor of someone holding a heavy lid down on something boiling.

Her name was Bess, according to the embroidered cloth she set before them. When Maren asked how long she'd lived in Hollowmere, Bess said, "All my life, and glad of it," in a voice as smooth as poured cream, and her hands shook harder.

When Sera complimented the stew, Bess beamed — the smile widening by precisely the degree the others' smiles widened — and said, "Everything here is good. Everything here has always been good." And for half a heartbeat, half a syllable, something flickered behind her eyes. Something looking out through her the way a prisoner looks out through a high window.

Doss felt it then. Felt it the way he felt everything, through the skin he'd never learned to close.

Joy. A flood of it, pouring off the woman, off the room, off the whole village — joy without source, joy like water from a broken cistern, everywhere and belonging to no one. His gift drank it in before he could stop it. That was the trouble with his talent: it wasn't a door he opened. It was a door that was always open, and things walked in.

He gripped the edge of the table. Kept his breathing slow. Packed it away, bundle by bundle, in the vaults inside him where he kept everything he'd ever taken.

But this bundle didn't pack right.

Grief folded small. Grief was used to being carried; it curled up in the dark and waited. Joy didn't curl up. Joy *expanded.* It wanted air, wanted singing, wanted to be given back, and Doss had spent his whole life giving back only sorrow — sorrow he understood, sorrow with edges he could grip. This stolen happiness filled him up like floodwater filling a boat, warm and golden and utterly wrong, because none of it was his and all of it was trying to make him *smile.*

He caught himself smiling. Sitting there in the inn, stew untouched, he realized his mouth had curved into the exact smile — the identical one, the forty-second instance — and he couldn't remember starting it.

He dug his thumbnail into his palm until it hurt.

"Doss?" Jude was watching him. "You've gone pale."

"Fine," Doss said, and his voice came out bright and pleasant, and that frightened him so badly he stood up, chair legs shrieking, and said in a growl he wrestled back down into his own register, "I need air."

Outside, the evening had gone violet, and the villagers were lighting lamps, and every lamp-lighter turned to watch him pass with the smile, the smile, the smile, and each one of them radiated joy at him like open furnaces, and his open doors drank and drank and drank.

* * *

He made it to the millpond before the flood hit.

He remembered it in pieces afterward. Kneeling on the stones. The water black and still, reflecting a sky with no stars in it yet. The joy in him swelling past every wall he'd ever built, forty years of discipline dissolving like sugar in rain, and underneath the joy — surfacing now, because joy was buoyant and lifted everything — the bundles. All of them. The widow of Marrowgate weeping in his chest. The stone-heavy father from Cullis Row. Decades of borrowed mourning rising in him like drowned bodies coming up when a lake warms.

And beneath those, straining at its chains, the oilcloth bundle. His own.

*Mother.* Eleven years old. The smell of tallow and sickness. Her hand gone cold in his before he understood what cold meant, and the terrible discovery that followed — that he could take the grief of strangers, that invisibility was a trade, that he had sold his one true mourning for a lifetime of other people's — and the thing he'd never said aloud, not once, not to anyone: that he couldn't remember her face anymore. That he'd carried everyone's dead so long he'd let his own slip through his fingers.

The joy made it worse. That was the cruelty of it. Joy was buoyant; it floated everything to the surface. He knelt on the millstones drowning in other people's happiness and his own oldest grief, and somewhere above him voices were calling his name — Sera's voice, sharp with fear; Jude's low and steady — and he couldn't answer, because his mouth wanted to do something else. His mouth wanted to *smile.*

The pond lay before him, black and welcoming, and some calm, flooded, smiling part of him thought: *the water would hold it all. The water would hold everything, and then it would be quiet.*

A hand seized his collar and hauled him backward onto the stones.

Maren. Of all of them, Maren — the princess who'd been erased, who knew what it was to have your name taken. She knelt over him, gripping his shoulders, and she didn't say anything kind. She said, "Look at me. Not the water. *Me.*"

And Doss looked at her, and Maren's face was furious and tear-streaked and absolutely, ferociously *unsynchronized* — a face doing exactly what it wanted, feeling exactly what it felt, and it was the ugliest, most beautiful face he'd ever seen, because it was the only one in Hollowmere that wasn't smiling.

He breathed. Once. Twice. The flood didn't recede — floods don't — but it stopped rising, and he learned to float in it, the way he'd learned to carry everything else.

"I've got it," he rasped. "I've got it. Help me up."

Sera and Jude took an arm each. As they hauled him to his feet, Doss looked back at the pond, and in its black mirror he saw the villagers gathered at a distance, a silent semicircle of lamplight and identical smiles, watching him the way children watch a beetle.

Watching. Waiting. Taking notes.

"They're not happy," he said, and his voice was his own again, rough and low. "Understand me. There is no joy in this village. There's joy *in storage.* They're not people anymore — they're jars. Somebody's been bottling these folk, and what's left inside is looking out the windows."

Jude's hand went to the map against his chest. "The choir."

"The choir wears a king," Doss said. "This is what it does to practice on. To learn on. We're standing in the draft of something that hollows people out and calls it peace." He looked at the semicircle of smiling faces, and for the first time in forty years, Doss Fenwick wanted to stop moving. Wanted to plant his feet and fight, because some things deserved to be run *from*, and some things needed to be run *at.*

"We leave tonight," Maren said.

"No," Doss said. His hands had stopped shaking. "We leave tonight, aye. But first — somebody's keeping all that stolen joy somewhere. Joy doesn't vanish. It's stored, weighed, *accounted for.*" He bared his teeth at the smiling semicircle. "And I want to know who's holding the ledger."

Behind them, at the edge of the lamplight, the innkeeper's wife stood alone. Her smile was perfect. Her hands were shaking.

And when she spoke, her voice was not one voice but many, harmonized into a single gentle chord:

"You should rest, travelers. The road east is long."

*The road east.* Toward the capital. Toward the palace. Toward the crypt.

Toward whatever was waiting for them, already rehearsing their names.
