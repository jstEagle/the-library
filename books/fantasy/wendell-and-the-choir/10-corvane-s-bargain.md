## 10. Corvane's Bargain

The rain found them on the third day out of Hollowmere, a thin cold drizzle that turned the road to porridge and made the hedgerows drip like eaves. They walked in a loose line — Doss ahead, hood up, reading the road the way he read crowds; Sera behind him with the map rolled tight inside her coat; Maren at the rear, walking like a woman who had spent twenty years indoors and intended to make up for it with interest. Jude carried the ledgers. He had not been able to leave them. Three volumes of the Vellum Guild's accounts, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, heavy as a sleeping child.

He had stopped hearing the dead since Hollowmere. Not entirely — but quieter, as if the village's stolen joy had packed their mouths with wool. The relic bones in his satchel were silent when he touched them at night. It should have been a mercy. It felt like deafness.

They made camp under a stone bridge where a stream ran brown and quick, and it was there, while Sera coaxed a fire from damp wood and Doss dozed sitting up the way he always did, that Jude saw the lantern coming up the road.

One lantern. No escort visible. No horses.

"Company," Doss said without opening his eyes.

"I see him," Jude said.

"You see *a* man. I see a man walking like he owns the rain." Doss stood, unhurried, and drifted toward the bridge's shadowed arch. "That's Guildmaster Corvane."

Maren's hand went to the knife at her belt. Sera rose from the fire, and for a moment the flames lit her face in a way that made her look, briefly, like the soprano she had once been — spine straight, chin lifted, ready to be looked at.

Corvane came down the bank alone. He wore no livery, only good grey wool and a travelling cloak beaded with rain, and he carried the lantern as if it were an affectation rather than a necessity. Up close he was smaller than Jude remembered from the Guildhall — or perhaps it was only that here, away from his bells and his clerks and his bone-white tower, he was merely a man of fifty-odd years with clever hands and eyes the colour of ledger ink.

"Jude Eastwick." Corvane smiled. It was a good smile. It had been used on many people. "You've made me walk a long way in the wet."

"You shouldn't have."

"No." He set the lantern on a flat stone and lowered himself onto it after, perching like a heron, cloak pooling. "But then, neither should you have done any of what you've done. And yet here we both are." His gaze moved around the camp with the ease of a man appraising stock: Sera, rigid by the fire. Maren, blade half-drawn. The dark arch where Doss had gone invisible. "The exile. The erased princess. And somewhere, my thief. A remarkable collection. You always did have an eye for inventory, Eastwick."

"What do you want?"

"To talk. Nothing more — for now." Corvane spread his hands, palms up, an accountant's gesture, everything shown, nothing hidden. "I'll be plain, because I know you value plainness. The Guild knows where you are. Has known since Hollowmere. We could have taken you there — or on this road, tonight, with forty enforcers instead of one old man and a lantern." He tilted his head. "I asked them to wait. That should tell you something about how much I want this conversation."

"It tells me you want something badly enough to stand in the rain for it."

"Yes." The smile thinned into something more honest. "I do."

* * *

Jude had balanced Corvane's personal ledgers for six years. He knew the man's handwriting better than his own — the small precise numerals, the way he underlined figures twice when they displeased him. He knew that Corvane drank vinegar-water in the mornings and kept a relic carved from his mother's finger-bone in a drawer he never opened. He knew, though he had never said so aloud, that the Guildmaster heard nothing through any bone ever put in front of him. The dead did not speak to Corvane. They spoke *about* him, sometimes, in the voices of people he'd ruined, and what they said was never kind.

So Jude was not afraid of him, exactly. But standing in the firelight while the rain ticked on the bridge stones, he felt the particular dread of a clerk who has found the error and must now tell the master.

"The courier," Corvane said. "Wren Aldous. Good man. Fast. Honest, which in our trade is a professional liability. He stole the map from the palace archive — or was given it; we've never established which, and now he's dead, so the question is moot." He warmed his hands over the lantern's glass. "You buried him. You took the map. And then, instead of bringing it home as your contract required, you went to ground, gathered a little company of the dispossessed, and started walking toward the capital. My capital. My Guild's city. Toward the thing under the palace."

"You know all this."

"I know everything the ledgers touch, and lately everything touches the ledgers." Corvane looked up, and the lantern light caught his face from below, hollowing his cheeks, making him briefly skull-like. "Do you know what the choir is, Eastwick? Truly? Not what the dead whisper through your pretty carvings — what it *is*, in terms of assets?"

Jude said nothing.

"It is the single most valuable holding this kingdom has ever produced. Twelve voices bound into one instrument. An instrument that wears a crown. Do you understand what that means?" Corvane's voice stayed soft, conversational, the tone of a man discussing grain tariffs. "It means continuity. Kings die — kings are *supposed* to die, that's the whole architecture of the succession — but the choir doesn't. The choir remembers every treaty, every debt, every secret worth keeping, going back two hundred years. While the choir sings, the realm has a memory that outlives its rulers. No war of succession. No regency. No collapse. Just policy, sustained, patient, forever."

"And bought with twelve lives."

"Bought with twelve *gifts*." Corvane spread his hands again. "Singers. People whose voices would otherwise have been spent on operas and weddings and died forgotten in parish registers. The Guild gave them permanence. There are worse fates. I could name you fifty merchants in Vellum Row who'd trade places tomorrow."

"One of those singers was a girl named Liss Halloway." Sera's voice cut across the fire, low and shaking. "She was nineteen. She wanted to sing at the Amberhall. She didn't want to be a chair."

Corvane turned to her with genuine courtesy. "Ah — Ilvane. Yes. You were in the conservatory cohort. Two of yours, I believe." He said it gently, almost kindly, which was worse than cruelty. "I'm sorry for it. Truly. But I won't apologize for arithmetic."

"That's the trouble with you," Sera said. "You think grief is arithmetic."

"I think," Corvane said, turning back to Jude, "*everything* is arithmetic. That's why I keep you."

And there it was. The offer, still unspoken, hanging in the wet air between them like smoke.

* * *

Here is what Jude Eastwick had never told anyone: he had imagined being rich.

Not often. Not greedily. But on winter nights in the workshop above the bone-cellars, when the candles guttered and the dead murmured through the day's carvings and the rent was due to a landlord who never asked why his tenant smelled faintly of grave-dust — on those nights he had imagined a room with thick walls, where the whispers couldn't reach. A table that wasn't scarred. A life where his gift was a profession instead of a haunting. Where someone paid him enough that he could say no to the jobs that turned his stomach — the love-charms carved from murdered wives' knuckles, the luck-pieces ground from plague teeth, all the small indecencies the Guild dressed up as services rendered.

Power offered to the powerless does not arrive looking like power. It arrives looking like *rest*.

"Here is my offer," Corvane said, and now he spoke the way he wrote — each word placed like a figure in a column. "Come back. Bring the map. Bring the princess, if she'll come — she's worth more to us breathing than dead, and I give you my word she will not be harmed. In exchange: partnership. Not employment. Partnership. Your name beside mine on the Guild charter. A seat in the hall where the impossible things are priced. And the twelfth chair stays empty."

Jude heard himself say, "Empty."

"For now. For a generation, perhaps. The choir can run on eleven — thinner, weaker, but functional. What I propose is this: we stop feeding it kings. We feed it *volunteers*. Old singers at the end of long careers, who choose the chair the way a scholar chooses a crypt. Paid. Honoured. Their families endowed. We turn the abomination into an institution, Eastwick. We make it lawful. We make it *ours*." He leaned forward, and the lantern threw his shadow huge across the bridge stones. "And you — you would be its keeper. Its tuner. The only man alive who can hear whether the harmony is true. Think of it. Every voice in that crown, and you would hear them all, every day, and they would *know* you. Two hundred years of royal memory, and you would be the one it trusted."

The rain filled the silence.

Jude looked at the fire. Sera was watching him with an expression he couldn't bear to read. Maren had not moved, but her knuckles were white on the knife.

And the temptation was real. He would not insult himself by pretending otherwise. Partnership. Safety. Thick walls. And more than safety — *purpose*. All his life he had been a vessel, a listening thing, a boy who heard the dead and got paid for it and went home alone. Corvane was offering him a place at the centre of the largest sound in the world. The choir would need him. The dead in the crown would need him. He would never be lonely again — he would be *surrounded*, attended, held in the attention of twelve bound souls and two hundred years of kings.

He thought: *I could fix it from inside.* That was the sweetest part, the part that almost worked. He could sit in the hall, hear the harmony strain, refuse to let another Liss Halloway go into the dark. He could starve the choir gently, decade by decade, until it faded like an old song. Reform without ruin. Justice without blood. The accountant's dream: correct the books without burning the library.

He thought it for four full breaths. He let himself think it. That was the honest thing, and he clung to honesty the way a drowning man clings to anything.

Then he looked at Sera.

She had sat back down by the fire. Her hands were in her lap, and she was not looking at him — she was looking at the flames, and her mouth was set in the shape it had worn the night she told him about the coronation, the night she described standing in the gallery while a hundred servants answered the king in perfect unison, one voice wearing a hundred faces. She had refused to sing for that thing. She had lost everything — her name in the conservatory rolls, her rooms, her career, her country's stage — because she would not lend her throat to it. And here was Jude, four days her friend, weighing an offer to become its warden.

*Liss Halloway was nineteen,* she had said. *She didn't want to be a chair.*

There was no version of the institution Corvane described that changed what the chair was. Volunteers, endowments, honours — all of it was varnish on a box where a living person was nailed shut forever and called an asset. Jude had spent his whole life listening to the dead tell him how they died. He knew what bound things sounded like. Even the willing ones. *Especially* the willing ones, after the first hundred years, when willingness wore through and there was nothing left inside the harmony but the note itself, repeating, repeating, repeating.

And beneath that — simpler, colder — was Wren Aldous, dying in his workshop with the map pressed into his hands, spending his last breath not on a prayer but on a name: *the choir.* The courier had trusted him. Not the Guild. Him. Jude Eastwick, bone-carver, keeper of other people's accounts. Wren had looked at the one man in Vellum Row who might actually *hear* what was wrong and had bet his death on him.

You don't balance a ledger by moving the theft to a different page.

"No," Jude said.

Corvane didn't move. "Hear the terms—"

"I heard the terms. I balance numbers for a living, Guildmaster. I know a debt dressed as a gift when I'm handed one." He set the oilcloth-wrapped ledgers down between them, deliberately, on the flat stone next to the lantern. "You want these. You want the map. And you want me in a chair of your own design — it just isn't made of bone. Partnership. Keeper. Tuner. Every word you said means *warden of the cage.*"

"The alternative," Corvane said quietly, "is that the choir keeps eating kings until there's nothing left of any of them, and then it comes looking for new material. Commoners, Eastwick. You saw Hollowmere. That was a trial. A *pilot*. When the royal line runs dry — and it is running dry; the king has almost nothing left, you know that better than anyone — the technique goes to market. Whole villages. Whole towns. Singing smiles in every window. Is that what you want? Because that is what happens if you destroy the only institution capable of controlling it."

"You're describing yourself," Jude said. "You're the control. You're the market. Hollowmere happened on the Guild's ledger — I've read the entry. Line thirty-one, year before last. 'Field trial, harmonic containment, rural site.' Initialled C.C. Don't tell me what happens if the choir runs wild. Tell me what happens when *you* run it."

For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's eyes that was not patience.

"You lie well," he said. "For a first attempt."

"I had a good teacher. Six years of reading your handwriting."

The lantern flickered. And Jude understood, with the flat clarity that sometimes came to him in the moment before disaster — the same clarity the dead had, he supposed — that the conversation was over. That it had been over since he said *no*, and everything since was Corvane arranging the scene for whatever came next.

"Last chance," Corvane said. "The map. The ledgers. Hand them over, and I walk back up the road alone, and none of this ever touches you. I'll even leave the princess out of it. That's generosity, Eastwick. Take it."

Jude picked up the ledgers and hugged them to his chest. "No."

Corvane sighed, and it was almost fond. "I was afraid you'd say that. Well. I did try to save us both the mess."

He reached down and snuffed the lantern with two fingers, and the night went black as the inside of a closed book.

* * *

What followed lasted less time than it takes to describe.

The first thing was a whistle — Doss, from the dark under the bridge, three notes, high-low-high, the signal they'd agreed on outside Hollowmere. The second thing was Corvane's voice, raised not in anger but in command, speaking a single word — *"Now"* — and the answer to it: the tolling of a bell. Not a Guild bell. A handbell, small and clear, somewhere up the slope beyond the hedge, and Jude's skin crawled because he knew that bell, had catalogued it, *relic-class: alarm, bronze, provenance unknown,* and wherever it rang, enforcers converged.

They came out of the hedgerow. Four of them, then six, grey-cloaked, faces masked with vellum — actual vellum, stretched and painted blank, so that each enforcer wore a face with no features except what the light gave it. Jude had processed their equipment requisitions. He had never seen them worn. It was worse than he'd priced.

"Sera!" Maren's voice, and then the crash of the princess meeting the first enforcer with her shoulder rather than her knife, because a knife against vellum masks and Guild-forged mail was a joke and she was not a woman who joked anymore.

"Run!" Doss erupted from under the bridge like something born there, and he did not fight — he *moved*, through the enforcers the way he moved through mourning crowds, and where he passed they faltered, staggered, one of them making a sound like a man remembering a funeral. Whatever Doss carried, it landed on them like a wet cloak, and for three full seconds the line broke.

Three seconds was enough.

"Eastwick!" Corvane's voice, calm even now, even in the dark. "You're costing me money!"

Jude ran. He ran along the stream with the ledgers crushed against his ribs and the map's weight against his sternum, and behind him the camp dissolved into shouting and the ring of steel on stone and Sera's voice — her real voice, the one they'd bound her for — rising over the chaos in a long, pure, furious note that made two enforcers drop their staves and cover their ears. She was singing them backward. Of course she was. It was the only weapon she'd ever owned, and she'd sworn off it, and she was using it anyway, for them.

*Maren.* He skidded, turned. Through the dark he saw her — a pale blur by the fire, backed against the bridge wall, an enforcer reaching for her — and he saw Doss come out of nowhere and take the man's knees, and he saw Maren go over the wall into the stream, deliberately, choosing the water over the hands.

After that, the night scattered them like a dropped deck.

Jude ran until the stream became a ravine and the ravine became a wood, and then he stopped running because his lungs ordered him to, and he stood in the dripping black under a beech tree with the ledgers against his chest and listened. Rain. Wind. Far behind, the handbell, still ringing, growing fainter. No footsteps. No voices.

No Sera. No Maren. No Doss.

He waited until dawn came up grey and useless, and then he climbed the ravine and went back along the stream to the bridge, because he was the kind of man who checked the accounts even when he dreaded the total.

The camp was wrecked. Fire kicked apart. Lantern gone — Corvane had taken it, or left it; either way it was not there. On the flat stone where the lantern had sat, weighted with a pebble against the wind, was a single sheet of paper, written in that small precise hand he knew better than his own:

*"The offer stands until dawn tomorrow. After that, the price changes. — C.C."*

And beneath it, in a different hand, hurried, pressed hard enough to tear:

*"We're alive. Split up. The mill at Ferrow's Cross. Trust no one who finds you — find US. — S"*

Jude stood in the ruins of the fire and laughed, once, a cracked sound with no humour in it, because of course. Of course she'd left a note. She was a singer; she understood that a message meant nothing unless it reached its audience. And of course Corvane had let the note stay. Of course he had read it, copied it, and left it exactly where a guilty, hopeful, lonely man would find it — because a rendezvous written down was a trap with better handwriting.

Or it wasn't. Or Sera had left it in the ninety seconds before capture, and it was true, and they were waiting.

Two entries in the same ledger. One hand careful, one hand desperate. Both claiming to be the truth.

Jude folded the note and put it inside his coat, against the map, and stood a moment longer in the wet ashes, doing the only thing he knew how to do with an account that wouldn't balance: he carried it forward.

Then he turned north, toward Ferrow's Cross, and began to walk — knowing that whatever waited at the mill, Corvane's men would be counting on him to lead them straight to it.
