## 11. Maren's Confession

The ruin had been a chapel once, before the river changed its mind and left it standing in a field of silt and thorn. Maren chose it because she could read buildings the way other people read faces — this one said *forgotten*, and forgotten was what they needed.

She watched them come in across the dead water meadow as the light went copper: Jude first, walking like a man carrying something breakable against his chest — which he was; then Doss, who moved through the tall grass without bending a single stalk, the way he moved through everything; then Sera, hood up, hands jammed in her pockets, humming under her breath. Not a song. A scale. Maren had learned in three days of travel that Sera hummed scales when she was frightened, climbing them like rungs on a ladder out of a well.

They were her people now. That was the word for it, though she hadn't earned the right to use it yet. *Her people.* Twenty years in a convent taught you many words and gave you almost nothing to attach them to.

"You're staring," Doss said, dropping onto a fallen lintel beside her. He always knew when someone was looking at him. She suspected it was a professional hazard.

"I'm counting," Maren said. "You're four minutes late."

"Rabbit." He held up a string of them, three brown bodies swinging. "Doss provides. It's in my contract."

"You don't have a contract."

"That's why I provide voluntarily. Voluntary provision is how you avoid contracts." He began gutting the first rabbit with a small knife that appeared from somewhere inside his coat, and his voice dropped, losing its performance. "Jude's bad tonight. You see it?"

Maren looked across the ruined nave to where Jude sat with his back against a pillar, the map unrolled on his knees even though there was no light left to read it by. His fingers moved over the vellum the way a blind man reads. "I see it."

"He keeps saying the note doesn't matter. The one Sera left him. He keeps saying it like a man rehearsing." Doss's knife paused. "I've spent my whole life around people pretending not to grieve. I know the posture."

"And yours?" Maren asked, because she was old enough now to be direct, and because deflection was a currency she'd stopped accepting.

"Mine's fine," Doss said, in the tone of a man locking a door. "Mine's in a box. Boxes are useful. Ask me about the rabbits instead."

* * *

The fire was small and shielded by the chapel's broken apse, smoke crawling low along the stone. They ate in the companionable silence of people too tired for talk, and Maren found herself watching each of them in turn and doing arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Ferrow's Cross.

Sera Ilvane: exiled for refusing to sing, carrying the guilt of two dead friends whose names she'd read on a map made of a king's skin. Jude Eastwick: workshop burned, life burned, hunted for a gift he'd never asked for, holding a ledger entry that wouldn't balance. Doss: grief in a box, boxes are useful.

And herself. Princess Maren, erased from every record in the kingdom, twenty years a nun under a name that wasn't hers, following a rumor of a brother who might already be gone.

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called its two-note question over and over, getting no answer.

"I have to tell you something," Maren said.

The words came out louder than she intended. All three of them looked up. Jude closed the map. Sera stopped humming mid-scale, which Maren had never seen her do, and understood suddenly how much courage the small sound cost her, and how much it meant that she'd given it up.

"It isn't strategy," Maren went on. "It isn't useful. I've been trying to make it useful all week — dressing it up as testimony, as evidence about the coronation date, as anything but what it is. But if we're going to walk into that palace, you should know what kind of person is leading you there. What kind of person I am."

"Maren—" Jude started.

"Let me say it while I still can." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees, the way Mother Superior had taught her to press down fear in chapel, and hated that the habit still lived in her body after all these years. "You've all lost things. Real things. Homes, names, friends. And I've been sitting among you like a woman owed something, because I lost a crown I never wanted and a brother I barely got to keep. But the truth is worse than that. The truth is that I didn't lose him. I *left* him."

The nightjar called again. Nobody moved.

"The year he was crowned, I was nineteen. We weren't close, not the way storybook siblings are close — he was eight years older and being groomed for a throne while I was being groomed for a marriage treaty that never happened. But we had a game. When we were children. He'd stand on one side of the wall between the east gallery and the nursery wing, and I'd stand on the other, and we'd knock on the stones. A code. Two knocks meant *are you there*. Three meant *yes*. One long scrape of a fingernail meant *come find me*." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she recited scripture. "He invented it because I was afraid of the dark corridors and he wanted me to know he was near even when I couldn't see him."

Sera had drawn her knees up to her chest. Doss had stopped pretending to clean his knife.

"A week after the coronation — the real coronation, the one that was supposed to happen at noon with ten thousand witnesses, before they moved it to midnight with none — I was walking the east gallery. And through the wall, I heard knocking. Two knocks. Then again. Then again, faster." Maren's throat closed. She pushed through it. "And then the scrape. One long scrape. *Come find me.*

"And I stood there, and I listened, and I told myself it was the pipes. The palace was full of pipes and settling stone and wind in the flues. I told myself I was tired, that I'd been drinking wine at the coronation feast, that a princess who hears voices through walls is a princess who gets sent away, and I had just watched them move a coronation to midnight, and I knew — some animal part of me *knew* — that whatever was happening in that palace, the safest thing I could be was nobody. Unnoticed. Unrecorded." She laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "So I did what I'd been trained to do my entire life. I curtsied to no one, walked back to my rooms, and requested a transfer to the convent at Saint Ellery's. For my health. For quiet. And twenty years later, when the records were purged and my name scraped off the rolls, I told myself I'd been erased. That it was done *to* me."

She looked at the fire.

"But I heard him. Through the wall. My brother knocked our childhood code and asked me to come find him, and I walked away, and I let myself believe I was mad so I wouldn't have to believe I was a coward. And every year since, on the anniversary, I've lit a candle and prayed for his soul as if he were dead, because praying for a dead brother is holy and admitting you abandoned a living one is not."

The silence afterward was enormous. The fire popped. Out in the dark the nightjar had finally stopped asking.

It was Doss who spoke first, quietly, without any of his usual armor. "What was the code for *thank you*?"

Maren blinked. "There wasn't one. Why would—"

"There should have been one," Doss said. "For a wall you can talk through. Seems like an oversight." He turned his knife over in his fingers. "I had a sister too. Didn't tell you that. Nobody asks a thief about family, they ask him about locks." He wasn't looking at anyone. "When the fever took our street, I carried her to the temple door and they wouldn't open it — plague rules — and I sat outside holding her until she stopped needing holding. And afterward I didn't cry. Not once. Everyone said what a strong boy, how well he bore it. And the truth was I wasn't bearing anything. I just decided that if I never felt it, it couldn't take me too." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "Thirty years. It's very patient, grief. It just sits outside your door and knocks. Two knocks. Are you there."

Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the opposite. "I refused to sing," she said, to the fire, as if continuing a conversation they'd all been having for years. "Everyone thinks it was principle. It wasn't. Or — it was, but underneath the principle was cowardice too. I heard the rehearsal from inside the choir loft. The king's voice. Perfect, every time, never a breath out of place, never tired, never human. And the others sang toward it like moths, and I stood there knowing something was wrong and I told myself *you're a singer, singers imagine things*, and then I simply... declined. Gave up my position, my name, my music. Ran. And I told myself for six years that I'd taken a stand." Her jaw tightened. "I took a seat. On a boat. Going the other direction."

Jude was last. He sat with the map across his knees, and when he finally spoke his voice was the careful ledger-keeper's voice, the one that measured things exactly.

"When the courier died in my shop, I hid the map before I sent for a physiker," he said. "I had time. He was dying in front of me and I had time, and I spent it hiding evidence, because the Guild's bells had started tolling and I was afraid. He died alone on my floor calling for a choir, and I stood in the back room with my ear against the door listening to whether the enforcers were coming." He folded his hands. "I balance accounts. I've always told myself that means I'm honest. But an honest man would have stayed with him. I just didn't want the debt on my page."

"So that's us," Maren said. Her eyes were burning and she let them. "A coward, a runner, a boy who locked his grief out, and a man who hid behind a door. That's the company that's going to save the king."

"No," Sera said.

They all looked at her. In the firelight her face had changed — the bitterness burned off it, or maybe just refined into something hotter and cleaner.

"That's the company that knows what it costs," Sera said. "Do you understand? Corvane will send men who've never confessed anything in their lives. The choir itself is built out of people who never admitted what they were — twelve voices bound because someone decided mercy was easier than truth. And we're four liars sitting around a fire telling the truth for the first time, and it's the only weapon we have that they can't take away." She reached out and put her hand flat on the ground between them, palm up — an offer, or a dare. "Maren. You heard him. You heard him and you spent twenty years paying for it in a cell you built yourself. That's not walking away. That's a penance with your name on it, and tomorrow we're going to finish it."

Slowly, Doss put his hand down next to hers. Then Jude, hesitating, his fingers ink-stained even now. Maren looked at their hands — a thief's, a carver's, a singer's — and thought of a wall in the east gallery, and two knocks, and laid her own hand down among them.

"Two knocks," she said, and her voice broke and held. "*Are you there.*"

"Three knocks," Doss answered. "*Yes.*"

Nobody laughed. It wasn't funny. It was the truest liturgy any of them had ever spoken, and they sat with their hands stacked in the firelight like conspirators, which is what they were, and like family, which is what they had become somewhere on the road without noticing the exact moment of it.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Maren kept the first watch, as she always insisted on doing. She sat in the mouth of the broken apse with her grandmother's knife across her knees and watched the moon come up over the silt flats.

She heard footsteps behind her — Jude, wrapped in his coat, the map's roll poking from his pocket like a bone.

"You couldn't sleep either."

"I slept," he said, settling beside her. "I dreamed in numbers. Corvane's face kept resolving into columns." He was quiet a moment. "What you said tonight. About the wall. You know it changes nothing about what happens next."

"I know."

"But it changes everything about who walks in."

Maren nodded, not trusting her voice.

They sat together in the cold, and the moon climbed, and Maren found herself thinking about walls — how her whole life had been a series of them, convent walls and palace walls and the wall of her own disbelief, and how every single one of them had been passable, if only she'd been willing to knock.

Then Jude went rigid beside her.

"Maren." His whisper was barely air. "Listen."

She listened. At first: wind, the nightjar returned, the small sounds of sleeping people. And then, beneath it, far off across the dark meadow — rhythmic, patient, unmistakable.

Hooves. More than one horse. Moving at a walk, deliberately slow, the way men ride when they want to arrive unhurried and be certain of their welcome.

"They followed us from the ashes," Jude breathed. "Or they never lost us at all. Either way—" He turned to wake the others, and Maren rose, knife in hand, and looked out at the darkness where the riders would soon resolve into shapes.

Twenty years of waiting was over. Whatever came out of that field at dawn, Princess Maren would be standing in the open to meet it — no walls, no orders, no more pretending she hadn't heard the knock.

Three knocks, little brother, she thought. Hold on. Yes.
