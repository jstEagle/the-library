## 12. The Twelfth Name

Sera Ilvane had spent eleven years learning to read music the way other people read faces — quickly, hungrily, always looking for what was being hidden. It was a habit she'd never lost, even after the court took her voice's reputation and broke it against the stones of exile. So when Jude spread the map across the table of their safehouse in Hollowmere's abandoned tithe-barn, she did not look at it the way Maren did, with dread, or the way Doss did, with superstitious distaste. She looked at it the way she had once looked at a rival soprano's score: searching for the trick.

The map was drawn on vellum the color of old cream, and it was wrong in a way that took her breath every time she saw it. Not paper. Skin. The skin of a king, tanned and stretched and inked with the architecture of his own skeleton. The spine ran down the center of the sheet like a river on a pilgrim's map, each vertebra rendered in fine brown line-work, annotated in a hand so precise it might have been engraved.

She had been studying it for three days. Three days of tracing notation that was not quite notation, marks that were not quite words, while the others slept or argued or, in Doss's case, paced the barn's perimeter counting escape routes he would never admit to counting.

On the third night, by the light of a single oil lamp, Sera found the twelfth vertebra.

And found nothing there.

* * *

"Jude." Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew she was frightened. Fear made her precise; it had always been so. Onstage, terror was the thing that kept her pitch true. "Jude, wake up."

He was sleeping in the loft above the stalls, curled around his satchel of carving tools like a man guarding a wound. He woke the way she'd learned he always woke — instantly, completely, no groggy border between sleep and attention. His eyes found hers in the dark and waited.

"The map," she said. "Come look at this. Now."

He came down the ladder without a word, barefoot on the cold boards, and knelt beside the table where the lamp still burned. Maren stirred in her corner but didn't wake; Doss, who claimed to sleep lighter than any cat, mercifully did too.

"Here." Sera put her finger on the twelfth vertebra, low on the sheet, just above the rendering of the royal pelvis. "Eleven names. You've seen them. Every one marked with a binding-name in this script — the same script, all eleven, like a hand copying out a litany."

"I've seen them," Jude said quietly. "I can't read them. That's been your work."

"Yes. And I've read them. Eleven singers, bound into the crown, their voices braided into whatever wears the king." She heard her own voice thinning and hated it, steadied it. "But the twelfth is different. Look."

He leaned close. She watched him trace the empty space with his eyes, and she watched the moment he understood — because his face did what it always did when the dead spoke to him through some carved relic: went very still, very careful, the stillness of a man standing at the edge of ice.

"There's no name," he said.

"There's no name," she agreed. "But there *is* a mark. A binding-mark, full and complete, the whole architecture of the spell — except where the name should go, the vellum is blank. Not worn. Not scraped. *Blank.* Do you understand? You don't leave a blank in a binding. The name isn't missing. The name hasn't been chosen yet."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from under the door. Somewhere outside, an owl called once and thought better of it.

"They're saving a seat," Jude said.

"They're saving a seat." Sera sat back on her heels. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking, and she pressed them flat against her skirts like a girl in a deportment lesson. "A choir of twelve. Eleven voices sustaining it. But choirs decay, Jude. Voices tire. Bodies fail. Whatever this thing is, however old, it has been running for decades on eleven chairs and a king who has almost nothing left to give. It needs a twelfth voice. A fresh one. And it didn't take just anyone's name and scratch it out — it left the space *open*. Deliberate. Waiting."

"For whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She looked at him then, really looked, and felt the answer arrive in her chest like a dropped stone hitting water. "Ask yourself who the Guild has been hunting. Not us. Not Maren, though she's the blood heir. Not me, though I'm the one who fled their choir-school. They burned your workshop, Jude. They razed it to the foundations over a piece of vellum they could have copied. They want the map destroyed, yes. But more than that—"

"Don't," Jude said.

It was barely a word. It came out of him like something knocked loose, and Sera realized she had never heard him interrupt anyone before. In the weeks she had known him, the bone-carver had been the most patient person she had ever met — a man who balanced ledgers of impossible things, who listened to the dead with the courtesy one extends to guests. He did not interrupt. He did not raise his voice.

"—they want *you*," she finished anyway, because she had spent eleven years being polite about the truth and it had cost her everything. "Your gift. You hear the dead through what they touched. You could sit in that chair and be heard *by* them. You're not just a replacement voice, you're a perfect one. Corvane doesn't want the map back. He wants the carver."

Jude stood up. It was not a graceful motion; it was the motion of a man whose body had decided before his mind did. He walked to the barn's small window and stood looking out at the moonlit fields of Hollowmere, at the village beyond where every window was dark and every door had smiled at them identically that morning.

"No," he said.

"Jude—"

"You don't know that. You're guessing. A blank space on a—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. When he turned back, his composure had reassembled itself, but she could see the seams now, and she was furious at herself for putting them there. "Even if you're right. Even if. What would you have me do?"

"Know it," Sera said. "That's all. Know what you're carrying. Because you've been treating this like a delivery, Jude — like the courier's last errand. Get the map to the crypt, finish the job, balance the ledger. But you're not the courier. You're the cargo."

The silence after that was long enough for the owl to try again outside, and fail again.

* * *

Maren woke to the sound of low voices and came down the ladder with the unhurried dignity of a woman who had spent twenty years being erased and had decided, somewhere in those years, that she would never again hurry for anyone's comfort. She listened to Sera explain it twice — once patiently, once again when Doss appeared, drawn by the lamplight, rubbing his eyes and demanding to know why everyone was awake and whether it was his fault.

"A blank chair," Maren said at last. She had gone gray. Sera watched her hand drift to her brother's name on the map — the first vertebra, the atlas, the bone that holds up the head — and stop just short of touching it. "They crowned him on a night with no witnesses. They hollowed him out over years. And the whole time, they were keeping a place ready. Like a hostess keeping a chair for a guest who hasn't arrived."

"Not a guest," Doss said. He had gone uncharacteristically quiet, which for Doss meant his humor had packed its bags and left. He crouched by the map but wouldn't touch it; Sera had noticed early on that he handled grief the way other men handled fire, at one remove, with tools. "A sacrifice. My mother used to tell stories about the old harvest rites. Before the Church got squeamish. You don't fill the field's mouth with just anybody. You give it the best of what you have. The sweetest stalk. The finest beast." He looked up at Jude, and there was something raw in his face that he immediately tried to bury under a shrug and failed. "You hear the dead, friend. You're the finest beast they've found."

"Thank you, Doss," Jude said dryly.

"I live to serve."

"This is why the enforcers didn't kill us at the bonehouse," Sera said, pressing forward before the deflection could take root. She was on her feet now, pacing, because sitting still had become impossible. "Think about it. Guild enforcers raze a workshop over a stolen ledger-page and somehow the carver walks out alive through the cellars. They intercepted us on the road and Corvane offered *partnership* — offered it to Jude, specifically, richly, with silk in his voice. You don't recruit the evidence. You recruit the asset." She stopped, turned. "They've been herding us. Every mile toward the capital, every step closer to the crypt — we think we're running from them, but we're running the way cattle run. Down the lane they've built for us."

"That's grim," said Doss.

"It's *true*—"

"It's grim and it's true, both things can share a sentence." He ran a hand through his hair. "So what do we do? Turn around? Go home?" He gestured vaguely at the ruined barn, the smiling village, the world. "To what?"

"To nothing," Maren said. "There is no home to go to. There is only my brother, wearing a crown that eats him, and a thing inside it that has been patient for longer than any of us have been alive." She looked at Jude, and Sera saw the princess surface fully in her face for the first time — not the convent ghost, not the forgotten sister, but the last of a royal line, iron under the softness. "So we go forward. But we go forward knowing what they want, and we make certain they cannot get it."

"How?" Jude asked.

"By not letting you out of our sight," Sera said. "By not letting you play the hero alone in any corridor, crypt, or chapel between here and the crown-chamber. By—" She faltered, hearing herself, hearing what she was actually saying beneath the tactics. She had lost people before. Fellow students, taken by 'fevers' two winters before her exile, whose names she had now found inked into a king's spine. She had sworn, walking out of the palace with her reputation in ruins, that she would never again stand in a choir and watch a voice be taken while she sang along. "By getting there first. By choosing the ground. If they need you in that chair, Jude, then the chair is ours to guard, not theirs to fill."

Jude looked at the four of them — at Maren's iron, at Doss's buried flinch, at her own shaking hands, which she had finally stopped hiding — and something in his expression shifted. Not resolve exactly. Something quieter and more frightening. Recognition.

"You sound like someone who's done this before," he said. "Watched it happen."

Sera held his gaze. "Two of the names on that map are girls I trained with. Lisseny Hargrave. Odile Vass. They died of fevers, the school said. Quick fevers, sad fevers, closed-coffin fevers." Her voice did not break, which cost her more than breaking would have. "I sang the requiem for Lisseny. I stood in the gallery and sang, and I remember thinking her voice sounded strange in the acoustic that day, thinner, as if the vault itself was singing a half-step off. As if something else was wearing her sound." She looked down at the map, at the third vertebra, at the neat brown letters spelling LISS. "I could have known. I could have asked questions. Instead I sang the descant beautifully and went to supper."

"Sera." Jude's voice was gentle. "You were nineteen."

"I was a coward with good pitch." She straightened. "Not anymore. So hear me, all of you, and especially you, bone-carver: nobody sits in that chair. Not you, not anyone. We go to the crypt, we do what must be done for the king, and we come out with twelve chairs and twelve empty seats, and if the choir wants a twelfth voice it can learn to whistle."

Doss barked a laugh, sudden and startled. Even Maren's mouth twitched.

But Jude was looking at the map again, at the blank space on the twelfth vertebra, and Sera saw his lips move slightly, reading the empty mark the way he read everything — listening. And she remembered, with a cold drop through her stomach, what he had told them about his gift. That he heard whoever died holding the bone. That the vellum was skin. That skin, technically, was bone's softer cousin, and the dead did not always distinguish.

"Jude," she said slowly. "What are you hearing?"

He didn't answer at first. The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the village, a dog began to bark — the first dog in Hollowmere, Sera realized, that had made any sound at all since they arrived — and then stopped mid-cry, as if corrected.

"The courier," Jude said at last. His whisper had gone strange. "When he gave me the map, he said a name. I thought it was delirium. I thought it was a prayer, or a curse, or just a dying man's noise." He raised his eyes to hers, and they were the eyes of a man reading a ledger entry that refuses to balance. "He said: *they'll want the listener.* I thought he meant himself. I thought he meant the dead in general." His hand rose, trembling, and hovered over the blank twelfth vertebra without touching it. "He meant the chair."

The dog outside began barking again — closer now, frantic, and this time it did not stop.

Doss was moving before any of them, crossing to the window in three silent strides, flattening against the wall beside it. Maren snuffed the lamp with a breath. In the sudden dark, Sera heard the village dog cut off a second time, abruptly, mid-note — silenced the way a singer is silenced, cleanly, professionally — and then, rolling up the moonlit road from the direction of the capital, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she had dreaded every night since the bonehouse burned:

Guild bells. Tolling the alarm. And they were tolling *closer*.
