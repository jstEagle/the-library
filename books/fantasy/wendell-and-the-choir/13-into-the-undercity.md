## 13. Into the Undercity

Doss had stolen a lot of things in his life — purses, names, a bishop's ring off his finger while the man wept at his own trial — but he'd never stolen a road before. That was what this felt like: lifting something enormous and heavy from under the city's skirts and hoping nobody noticed it was gone.

The entrance was behind a tannery in Saltrow, down a stair that smelled like a hundred years of bad decisions. Maren went first with the lantern, because she insisted, because she was a princess who had apparently decided that twenty years in a convent entitled her to lead every expedition into a hole in the ground. Sera followed, hood up, hands jammed in her sleeves like she was holding herself together at the seams. Jude came last, which Doss appreciated. He liked knowing where the bone-carver was. The man carried death around with him like a dog on a leash, and dogs made Doss nervous when he couldn't see them.

"Rules," Doss said, before they went under. "Rule one: don't touch anything that looks like it's waiting to be touched. Rule two: if I say walk, you walk. If I say run, you run, and you don't ask why until you're above ground again. Rule three—"

"Is there a rule three?" Maren asked.

"There's always a rule three." He looked at each of them in turn. "Nobody grieves down here. Not out loud. Not where it can pool."

Sera frowned. "Pool?"

"You'll see." He turned and took the stairs.

* * *

The undercity wasn't one place. It was a sediment — layers of the capital that had been buried rather than fixed, streets drowned by their own foundations, cellars beneath cellars, aqueducts that had carried water until they carried something else. Doss knew them the way other thieves knew rooftops. He'd spent six years down here after the fire, moving through the dark because the dark didn't look at him twice, and because grief — real grief, the old thick kind — collected in low places like fog, and he could drink it.

That was the trick of him, the thing that made him useful and made him wrong. Grief walked past him. Crowds in mourning parted around him without knowing why; watchmen forgot they'd seen him; doors that should have been barred stood open, because sorrow made people careless and Doss wore sorrow like a borrowed coat. It wasn't invisibility. It was worse. It was being *recognized* as something sad, and therefore not worth stopping.

He'd been doing it so long he sometimes forgot which griefs were his.

The first tunnel was easy. Old brick, dry air, the bones of a street that had been paved over two kings ago. Maren's lantern threw their shadows long and thin, four figures stitched together at the edges.

"This is where the processions used to go," she said quietly. "Before my brother's coronation. They'd bring the dead up through here to lie in state."

"Everything comes through here eventually," Doss said. "Water. Wine. Bodies. The city doesn't throw anything away. It just puts it downstairs."

They walked. And then the tunnel bent, and the air changed, and Doss felt it hit him like walking into a wall of warm water.

Grief. Old grief. *Old* old — not the fresh sharp kind that came off funerals, but grief that had been sitting in the dark for decades, curing, thickening, going dense as tallow. The purge tunnels. He'd known they were on the route; he'd hoped to skirt them. No such luck. The map Sera carried — the map of the king's own bones, drawn on vellum no one talked about too loudly — sent them straight through the deep galleries where, sixty years back, a frightened regent had cleared the city of everyone who sang the wrong hymns. Three thousand people, the histories said. The histories were written by the regent's friends. Doss figured it was more.

The walls started to sweat shadows.

Not ghosts, exactly — not yet. Just impressions, the way a mattress remembers a sleeper. A woman's shape pressed into the brick, arms crossed over her chest. A child's small silhouette at knee height, repeated, and repeated, and repeated, dozens of them along one stretch of wall, all facing the same direction, all waiting for someone who hadn't come back.

Maren stopped walking. "Saints' mercy."

"Don't stop," Doss said. "Stopping is how it gets in."

"What gets in?"

"The—" He gestured vaguely at everything. "It's not a thing. It's weather. You stand still in weather, you get wet." He kept his voice light because that was his job, the humor, the shrug, the grin that said *nothing touches me*. "Keep moving. Eyes forward. If you feel something pressing on your chest, that's normal. Breathe through it."

Sera was breathing through it visibly, her shoulders climbing toward her ears. Jude — Jude had stopped.

Of course he had.

* * *

Doss felt it before he saw it: a change in the pressure, the way the tunnel's weight shifted, the way water parts around a stone. The dead of the purges — the actual dead now, not just their stains — were coming up out of the walls. He watched them do it. Watched the silhouettes peel free of the brick and stand in the passage, translucent as breath on glass, dozens of them, then hundreds, filling the gallery ahead from floor to vault, a congregation with nowhere left to kneel.

Every hair on Doss's arms stood up. His whole body screamed *run*, and rule two was his own rule, and he broke it, because he wanted to see what the bone-carver would do.

Jude stepped forward. Past Maren. Past the lantern-light. Into the middle of the crowd of the dead like a man wading into a river he'd swum since childhood.

And they *bowed*.

Not dramatically. Nothing so theatrical. They simply inclined toward him, all of them, the way wheat leans after wind, the way a room turns its attention to the person who has just said the true thing. Doss heard — or thought he heard, or felt in his teeth — a sound like a great many voices exhaling at once. Recognition. Relief. The sound of people who have been waiting a long time finally seeing someone who can hear them.

One of them came close. An old woman, her shadow-shape wrapped in a shawl that had rotted off her bones decades ago. She raised a hand — not reaching, just showing, palm out, the way you show an empty hand to prove you mean no harm — and her mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the distance, Jude nodded as if she'd spoken plainly.

"I know," Doss heard him say. His voice was rough and quiet. "I know. I'll carry it. All of it. I promise."

The woman's shape loosened. Dissolved upward like smoke deciding to be sky again. And all down the gallery, the others followed — peeling away from the walls, rising, thinning, going wherever the dead go when someone finally takes their message. In less time than it takes to sing a psalm, the tunnel was empty. Just brick and lantern-light and four living people standing in a silence so complete it rang.

"Well," Doss said, because somebody had to. "That's convenient."

Jude turned around. He looked wrung out, pale as his own carvings, but steady. "They let us pass."

"Yeah, I noticed." Doss hopped up onto a broken kerb and walked the length of it like a tightrope, because his legs needed something stupid to do. "Sixty years these poor bastards have been down here, and they part like curtains for the bone-carver. Must be nice."

"Doss." Sera's voice had an edge.

"I'm just saying. Some of us have been carrying other people's misery since we were nine years old, and nobody ever bowed to *me*." He grinned. The grin held. The grin always held. "Must be the hat."

"You don't wear a hat," Maren said.

"That's why it works."

* * *

They camped in a side chamber that had once been a wine cellar, because Doss said the grief pooled thinnest there — joy-soaked ground, generations of celebrations soaked into the stone, and even now the air tasted faintly of spilled sweetness under the mold. Maren slept first. Then Sera, curled against the wall with the map-roll clutched to her chest like a child with a doll.

Doss sat up, keeping the watch he'd assigned himself, sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen.

Jude couldn't sleep either. Doss watched him across the dying lantern: sitting cross-legged, turning a small carved bone over in his fingers — a knuckle-bone, some relic or other — listening to whatever whispered out of it. His face in the low light was open in a way faces almost never were. Attentive. Tended to.

Doss looked away. Sharpened. Looked back.

"Ask me," he said, before he could stop himself.

Jude glanced up. "Ask you what?"

"Whatever you're wondering. Everybody wonders. You've all been wondering since Saltrow." He kept his eyes on the knife. "How does a thief end up able to walk through a funeral like a draft through a window. Go on. Everyone else is asleep. Nobody has to know you asked."

Jude set the bone down carefully, the way he handled everything, like the world was made of eggshell. "Do you want me to ask?"

"No," Doss said honestly. "But you're going to anyway, because you're polite, and I'm going to answer, because I'm tired, and then we'll both pretend it didn't happen. That's how these things work. I've read about them."

Jude waited. He was good at waiting. It was, Doss thought, probably his most annoying quality.

"The fire," Doss said. "When I was nine. Copperlane ward. You won't have heard of it — the Guild bought the histories same as they buy everything. My mother ran a pie shop. My father drank. My little sister—" The knife stopped moving. Doss looked at the blade instead of at Jude. "There were eleven of us kids sleeping in the loft above the shop when it caught. Eleven. I got out. I remember getting out. I remember the cold on the roof opposite, and I remember watching, and I remember that the thing I felt wasn't sadness. It was — nothing. A door closing. And the next morning I found out that if I stood near grieving people, I could take what they were feeling. Drink it down. Wear it. And the nothing would go away for a while."

He turned the knife over. "So that's what I did. For twenty years. Every funeral, every plague-house, every widow on every corner — I've eaten more grief than any ten priests. And it works, right up until it doesn't, and then I have to keep moving, because the day I stop—" He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something. "The day I stop, everything I ever swallowed stands up inside me and asks for its turn. So I move. That's the whole secret of Doss. Motion."

The silence stretched. Doss braced for pity — he knew all its shapes, he'd pickpocketed enough of it.

What he got instead was Jude saying, quietly: "Your sister. What was her name?"

Doss blinked. "What?"

"When you carry everyone's grief," Jude said, "does anyone ever ask about hers? Her name. What she liked. Whether she laughed."

Doss opened his mouth. Closed it. Twenty years of swallowed sorrows sat in his chest like ballast, and not one soul — not one priest, not one lover, not one fellow thief — had ever asked him for the *name*.

"Nell," he said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle like cheap plate. "Her name was Nell. She was seven. She laughed at everything. Terrible laugh. Like a goose being robbed."

Jude nodded slowly, and reached into his coat, and brought out a sliver of pale bone — raw, uncarved, blank. "I can't bring her back," he said. "I can't make the dead speak who won't. But if you ever want to give me something of hers — anything she held — I'll carve it. And if there's anything left of her anywhere, anything at all, you'll be the first person in twenty years to hear her voice."

Doss stared at the blank bone. Something enormous moved in his chest, something with Nell's terrible laugh at the center of it, and he understood suddenly, with sick clarity, why he'd never stopped moving.

Because if he stopped — really stopped — he'd have to face the possibility that she was gone, truly gone, and that no amount of borrowed mourning would fill the hole shaped exactly like a seven-year-old girl.

"If you're lying to me," Doss managed, "I'll steal everything you own. Twice."

"I'd expect nothing less."

* * *

They broke camp before the false dawn and pushed deeper. The route bent downward, following the map's strange notation — Sera reading the binding-marks aloud in a whisper, Maren holding the light, Jude navigating by the dead the way sailors navigate by stars. And the dead kept coming, gallery after gallery, plague-pits and purge-tunnels and the drowned foundations of hanged men's courts, and every time they rose out of the dark, they rose for *him*. They parted for him. They pressed close and mouthed their messages and he received them, grave and gentle, a ferryman working the last crossing.

Doss walked at the rear and told himself the ache in his chest was just the tunnel's weather.

Near midday — if "day" meant anything down here — the passage opened into a vast cistern, dry for a century, its floor a mosaic of cracked tiles. And across that floor, between them and the far stair that climbed toward the palace foundations, stood the dead of the great purge. Thousands. Rank upon rank in the gloom, silent, patient, packed shoulder to shoulder like a congregation that had been waiting sixty years for the sermon to start.

Maren's lantern shook. Sera made a small sound. Even Jude went still.

Then the ranks began to move — not against them, but aside, folding open down the center like a book being opened to its marked page, a corridor of living darkness leading straight to the stair.

"They know where we're going," Jude said softly. "They know what's wearing the crown." He looked back at the three of them, and in the lantern-light his eyes were wet and certain at once. "They've been waiting for someone to climb up and end it."

Doss looked at that corridor — thousands of the dead, opening a road for the bone-carver, for the singer, for the princess, while he stood at the edge of the light like a man outside a window looking in at a family dinner — and he felt the jealousy rise in him, black and familiar, and he felt something else rise underneath it, older and heavier, the thing he'd been running from for twenty years.

Because the dead had never answered him. Not once. But Nell —

Nell might answer Jude.

"Right," Doss said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Up we go, then. Palace by dawn." He pulled his collar up, checked his knives, and grinned his thief's grin at the assembled dead. "Hope none of you mind a bit of burglary on the way."

And as they started across the mosaic floor, through the parted sea of the patient dead, Doss felt — faint, impossible, coming from somewhere high above, from the direction of the crown — a single thread of song.

A girl's voice. Seven years old. Laughing.

He stopped walking. The whole column of the dead seemed to stop with him.

"Doss?" Jude called back, wary. "What is it?"

Doss swallowed hard against the thing in his throat. "Nothing," he said. "Keep moving. Rule two."

But his legs had gone to water, because the singing hadn't stopped — and it was coming from below.
