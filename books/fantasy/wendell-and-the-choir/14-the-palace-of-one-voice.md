## 14. The Palace of One Voice

Maren had not worn silk in twenty years.

The dress Sera had stolen for her — *borrowed*, Sera insisted, with the particular emphasis of a woman who had once sung for royalty and considered theft beneath her only in principle — was deep green, the color Maren's mother had favored, and it fit as though the seamstress had known her. That frightened her more than the guards at the gate did. Clothes that fit were clothes someone remembered. She had spent two decades being forgotten so thoroughly that even her own reflection had begun to feel like a rumor, and now here was a gown that fit her shoulders exactly, and a maid's coif over her cropped hair, and a forged invitation in Doss's quick fingers, and all of it conspiring to make her real again.

*You are Sister Anselm of the Convent of Quiet Waters,* Doss had told her, pressing the card into her hand an hour before, his voice pitched low and easy in the alley behind the tannery where they'd changed. *Convalescent. You've been ill. Ill people get to look pale and say nothing.* He'd grinned at her then, that crooked grin that never quite reached whatever he kept locked behind his eyes. *Best disguise there is. Nobody interrogates grief. They just step around it.*

She had wanted to tell him she knew all about being stepped around. She hadn't. Some confessions were still too heavy even after you'd set them down once.

Now she moved through the palace gates on the arm of a Guild clerk whose name she would never learn — Doss, gray-wigged and bespectacled, wearing another man's livery of office like a second skin — and the stones under her slippers sang.

That was the first wrongness, and it nearly stopped her heart in the courtyard. Not sang the way cathedral floors sang, with echo and cold. They sang *in tune*. A single held note, low and patient, rising through the flagstones into the soles of her feet, and every servant who crossed the courtyard walked in time with it without seeming to know they did. The scullery boys. The grooms. A woman carrying a tray of wine cups, her steps falling precisely on the beat like a dancer who had never been taught to dance but dreamed it nightly.

*Maren,* Jude had said to her, three nights ago around a fire in the ruins of Hollowmere's chapel, his voice still rough from the smoke of his burned workshop, *whatever you see in there — however bad it looks — your brother may still be in it. Underneath. Don't decide he's gone until we've seen the crown itself.*

She had promised. She held the promise in her mouth now like a pebble, tasting it, reminding herself of its weight as the doors opened onto light and music and the smell of roasted pheasant and beeswax and, underneath it all, faint as a stain, something sweetish and wrong. Like fruit beginning to turn.

The banquet hall swallowed them.

* * *

It was beautiful. That was the cruelest part.

Maren had grown up in this hall. She had run races down its length when it was empty, her hem in her fists, her brother Aldric pounding after her and always, always letting her win by half a stride because he was kind before he was anything else. She knew the banners of the nine provinces, the gallery where the musicians played, the high table on its dais beneath the window of colored glass where their father had sat with a face carved from state and their mother beside him with a face carved from patience.

Everything was where it should be. Everything was *more* than it should be. The candles numbered in the hundreds and burned without guttering. The flowers were out of season and flawless. The courtiers wore jewels like frost on branches, and the music — the music was perfect. Too perfect. She counted bars without meaning to, an old habit from convent psalms, and realized the ensemble had not varied its dynamics in eleven minutes. No crescendo. No breath. A heartbeat performed by machines that had studied hearts from a distance.

And the servants.

They moved between the tables like water finding its level, and they were smiling. All of them. The same smile. Maren watched a footman refill a lord's cup and saw the smile sit on his face the way paint sits on wood — applied, even, uncreased by any feeling underneath. Then a serving girl stumbled slightly on the dais steps, caught herself, laughed — and the laugh came out in the same pitch, the same duration, the same *shape* as the footman's smile, as the chamberlain's bow, as the herald's cry. A hundred faces, one expression, worn in common like a cloak passed hand to hand.

Doss's hand tightened almost imperceptibly on her arm. Through the gray wig, his whisper barely stirred the air: *Feel that?*

She felt it. Everyone carried grief differently; Doss had told her once that he read sorrow the way sailors read weather, by the pressure of it, the direction of its wind. And what he was reading now, she could see it in his face — was pressure without weather. Grief with nowhere to go. A hundred servants each holding a small dead thing inside them, some private mourning or fear or longing, pressed down flat and silent, and above it all that identical smile floating like oil on drowned water.

*They're not happy,* Doss breathed. *They're* ***harmonized.***

Then trumpets, and the hall rose, and Maren's legs nearly failed her, because the doors at the far end opened and her brother came through them.

* * *

Aldric.

He was forty-six years old and looked sixty. He was crowned — of course he was crowned; he was never uncrowned anymore, she understood that now, the crown had become less an object than an organ — and the gold of it caught the candlelight and threw little burning coins across the ceiling. His face was her father's face aged badly, her mother's jaw gone soft, and his eyes —

His eyes were the worst thing in the room, and the room contained a hundred smiling servants and a choir of bound souls she could not yet see.

Because his eyes were *kind*. That was the obscenity of it. Whatever wore him had studied kindness the way the musicians had studied music, and it performed kindness beautifully: the warm tilt of the head, the slow generous sweep of his gaze across his courtiers, the small smile — the same smile, Maren realized, the *same exact smile* — that the scullery boy had worn in the courtyard. The king smiled at his people with the borrowed face of a serving girl's joy, and no one flinched, because no one remembered anything different, because remembering had been eaten.

He took the throne. He lifted one hand, and the hall fell silent so completely and so instantly that the silence itself made noise, a little popping in Maren's ears like the hush after lightning.

"My friends," the king said.

And the chamberlain, standing thirty feet away, said, "My friends." Not echoing — *simultaneous*. The words left both mouths at once, one voice pitched high and one low, and beneath them Maren heard — felt, in her back teeth, in the floor, in the wine trembling microscopically in her untouched cup — a third voice, a fourth, a twelfth, a hundredfold, the whole hall breathing the greeting as one throat.

"— welcome," said the king, and the steward said *welcome*, and the captain of the guard said *welcome*, and somewhere in the galleries the musicians' bows paused together on the string like a held breath.

"— to this feast," said the king, and the queen's empty chair seemed to say it too, and the candles, and the stones.

Maren stood in a crowd of nobles with her head bowed as a convalescent should, and tears slid silently down her face, and no one looked at her twice, because everyone in this hall had learned not to notice weeping, or perhaps had been gently harmonized out of the noticing.

It wasn't the unison that broke her. She had prepared for the unison. Jude had described it, Sera had explained the binding-marks, Doss had warned her about the leak of harmony into the household. She had rehearsed this moment in her mind a dozen times, and in none of those rehearsals had she broken.

What broke her was smaller. Halfway through the speech — a speech about harvests and loyalty and the bright future of the realm, delivered in a voice that was her brother's voice worn smooth as river glass — the king paused to drink. And in the pause, in the half-second before the cup touched his lips, his face *slipped*.

Just for an instant. Just the way a mask slips when the wearer blinks. And underneath — underneath there was a flicker of confusion, of animal lostness, of a man waking in a moving carriage and not knowing the country outside the window. His gaze found hers across the hall. Found it, snagged on it, held.

And his lips moved. No sound. Only a shape, formed clumsily, the way a child forms letters:

*Ma-ren.*

Twenty years since anyone had spoken her name aloud. Twenty years since the records had burned her out of the family rolls and the priests had assured her, gently, pityingly, that she had imagined her own childhood. And now her brother — what was left of her brother, the dredged silt of him, the last inch of water in a well the choir had drunk to mud — had found her across a hundred faces and a hundred identical smiles, and shaped her name with lips that no longer fully belonged to him.

The cup rose. The mask resettled. The king drank, and smiled, and continued his speech about the bright future of the realm, and Maren stood in the green dress that fit her perfectly and grieved as she had never grieved at their parents' graves, because those had been bodies, and this was worse. This was a soul being kept alive the way a fire is kept alive — fed, tended, consumed.

*I'm here,* she shaped back, soundless, knowing he couldn't hold the thread long enough to see it. *I'm here, Aldric. I'm going to get you out.*

* * *

They withdrew from the hall during the third toast, Doss guiding her with the unerring instinct of a man who navigated crowds the way fish navigate currents. In the shadowed colonnade beyond the banquet doors, Maren braced a hand against the cold stone and let herself shake.

"He knew me." Her voice came out shredded. "For one second, he — Doss, he *knew me*."

"I saw." Doss had shed the gray wig; his own hair stuck up wildly, and his face was gray as ash. He looked, for the first time since she'd known him, genuinely afraid — not of enforcers or locks or the Guild's long reach, but of something he couldn't pickpocket. "That's not how it works, though. That's not how possession works. I've walked through haunted houses, Maren, I've slept in graves when the road ran out. Dead things don't leave windows open. Whatever's got him—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Whatever's got him is keeping that window open *on purpose*. Or it's leaking. Either way—"

"Either way he's drowning slowly instead of quickly."

"Either way we're out of time." He glanced back toward the doors, where the muffled harmony of the feast pulsed like a heartbeat through the walls. "Jude thought we'd have until dawn tomorrow. Watching that—" He shook his head. "I don't think we have until dawn tomorrow. I think the well's nearly dry. I think the choir is hungry *now*, and hungry things stop being patient."

"The crypt," Maren said. The word steadied her, gave her something with edges. "Sera said the map shows the entrance through the old ossuary, below the east wing. If we can reach it tonight—"

"Maren." Doss caught her sleeve, and his grip was gentle and desperate at once. "There's something else. When the king looked at you — when he said your name — the whole hall *leaned*. Every servant turned their heads two degrees toward you at the same moment. All hundred of them. Like iron filings to a magnet." His eyes were very dark and very steady. "I don't think you were noticed by the king. I think you were noticed by the *choir*. And I think it just learned that the one person in the world who remembers Aldric as a man is standing in its dining room."

Above them, faint through floors and stone, the banquet music swelled — and beneath the music, unmistakable now that she knew to listen, twelve voices sang a note that no instrument could make, patient and sweet and terribly attentive, like something turning its head.

Somewhere below the east wing, in the dark beneath the bones of kings, a door that had been sealed for twenty years began, very softly, to unlock itself.
