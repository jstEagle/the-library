## 15. Captured

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above the old rendering yards, chosen by Doss because — as he put it — no one with a working nose would come looking for them there. He was right about the smell. What he was wrong about was everything else.

Jude woke in the grey hour before dawn to the sound of Sera humming.

She sat on the windowsill with her knees drawn up, watching the fog roll off the rendering vats below, and she was humming something small and looping that he didn't recognize until it snagged on something inside him and turned over. Then he did. It was the phrase from the map — the notation wound around the twelfth vertebra, the one whose name had been scraped blank.

"Stop," he said, before he could decide whether to say it.

Sera's humming cut off. She looked at him across the dark room, where Doss slept sitting up against a beam out of habit and Maren lay curled like a woman guarding something under her ribs. "It won't leave me alone," she said quietly. "The melody. It's been circling since I read the map. Like a fly at a wound."

"It's bait."

"I know it's bait." Her voice frayed at the edges. "Knowing doesn't make it stop wanting to land."

Jude crossed the floor and sat beneath the window, his back against the wall, because standing felt too much like pacing and pacing felt too much like panic. Three days since Corvane's bargain. Three days since Jude had looked a man in his silk-wrapped face and refused him, and watched the refusal settle into Corvane's eyes not as anger but as arithmetic. *One refuses,* Corvane had seemed to think. *Therefore one must be spent elsewhere.*

They should have moved last night. Doss had argued for it — *we're smoke, we drift, we don't sleep two nights in the same rafters* — but Maren's knees had swollen from the road, and Sera had been gray-lipped with exhaustion, and Jude had made the call. He had weighed comfort against caution the way he weighed ledger entries, and he had gotten the sum wrong.

He was still turning that error over when the bells began.

Not the Guild's alarm — he knew that tolling now, would know it in his grave, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause, like a heart learning to fail. This was different. This was every bell in the district at once, ragged and overlapping, and beneath it came a sound Jude had heard only once before: the low, wet groan of the rendering-yard gates being forced.

"They didn't follow our trail," Doss said, already awake, already moving, his voice stripped of all its humor. "They were led. Someone sold us."

Maren sat up. "Who?"

But Jude already knew, and the knowing went through him like a bone-saw. There was only one person who knew where they'd run after the fight scattered them. Only one person they'd trusted enough to send word to through a cutout, a beggar, a whisper down a stairwell. *Come to the tannery. Regroup.* And only one person whose interest it served for four fugitives to be gathered in one room, ripe for harvest.

Corvane hadn't offered him partnership. He'd offered him a longer leash.

"Back stair," Doss said. "Now. Maren first — she's slowest, no offense, Your Highness—"

"None taken, thief."

"—Jude behind her, I'll—"

The door came off its hinges before anyone reached it.

* * *

There were six of them in the doorway, Guild enforcers in their bone-white masks, and behind them more coming up the outside stairs, boots ringing on iron. They moved the way the Guild taught everything to move: without waste, without mercy, without any motion that couldn't be entered in a ledger afterward.

Doss hit the first one with a rendering hook and it was like watching a man fight a tide. He was fast — faster than anything human had a right to be — and grief poured off him in waves that made the enforcers stagger, made them clutch their masks, made one drop to his knees weeping for a mother he might never have had. But there were too many, and they had trained against fear, and fear was most of what Doss carried.

"Go!" Doss shouted, and it wasn't a battle-cry, it was an accounting — *this is what I'm worth, this is what I buy you, take the trade.* "GO!"

Sera grabbed Jude's sleeve. Maren was already moving, limping hard toward the trapdoor in the floor that opened onto the curing rooms below. Jude went with them because going was the only thing left that wasn't dying uselessly, and even so it took everything he had not to turn around. He heard Doss go down — heard the particular sound of a body hitting tannery boards, a sound he'd heard once when his father fell dead across his own workbench — and he heard Doss laugh as they took him, a wild cracked laugh, and then he heard nothing, because Sera slammed the trapdoor and the dark closed over them like water.

The curing rooms were a maze of hanging hides, pale and stretched and terrible, and Jude thought absurdly that they looked like the map — like vellum waiting to be written on. Behind them the floor shuddered with boots. Ahead, a hatch, a drain-channel, the old smuggler's cut Doss had scouted on their first night here and described with a thief's loving precision.

They almost made it.

An enforcer dropped through the ceiling hatch ahead of them — they'd taken the loft from above and below both, of course they had, the Guild never spent less than necessary — and Sera did the thing Jude would remember for the rest of his life, however long or short that proved to be. She stepped in front of him. A court soprano, a woman exiled for a single act of refusal, a body built for candlelight and applause, and she planted herself between Jude and the masked man like a door swinging shut.

"The twelfth chair is mine, then," she said, and her voice filled the whole curing room, that voice, the voice that had made bishops weep, aimed now like a weapon. "Tell Corvane he'll have to carry me singing."

They carried her singing. Jude fought — he genuinely fought, with a boning knife from the curing bench and a fury he hadn't known lived in him — and someone's masked elbow found his temple, and the world went white and sideways, and through the white he heard Maren screaming his name and felt her hand close on his collar, dragging, dragging, and then cold air and falling and the black mouth of the drain-channel swallowing them both.

* * *

He came back to himself underground, in the dark, with Maren's hands pressed over his ears.

"Don't listen," she was saying. "Don't. Whatever you hear up there — don't listen."

Which was, of course, the one instruction Jude Eastwick could not follow.

Listening was what he *was*. He had spent his life listening to the dead through the bones they'd touched, and the crypts beneath the tannery district were old, older than the Guild, older than the palace, riddled with ossuary galleries from the plague years when the city had run out of ground and started stacking its dead like cordwood. The walls around them were mortared with femurs. And the dead in those walls were waking, because the bells above were still ringing, and because somewhere far above, in a chamber he could picture with horrible clarity, Sera Ilvane was being strapped into a chair of carved bone while a man in silk explained the terms of her new employment.

He heard her before Maren could stop him.

Not with his ears. With the gift — the cursed, precious, ruinous gift — reaching up through the bone in the walls the way roots reach through soil. The dead kings' crypts ran deep beneath the capital; the Guild's bones ran everywhere; and bone, Jude had learned at his father's bench, remembers everything it touches.

What he heard was rehearsal.

Sera's voice, thin and strained, singing the binding-phrase — the same looping melody she'd hummed at the window — and beneath it, around it, eleven other voices holding the harmony open for her like arms held wide. Patient. Welcoming. *Come in, come in, we've kept your place.* And a twelfth voice that wasn't a voice yet, just a shape in the music, a hollow where a name used to be, and the choir pouring itself toward that hollow the way water pours toward a drain.

"They're rehearsing her," he said aloud, and his own voice sounded wrong in the dark, flattened, echoing. "They're teaching her the part. She's fighting it — she's flat on purpose, she's dragging the tempo, God, she's brilliant — but they have time. They have until dawn."

Maren's grip tightened on his arm. "Then we have until dawn."

"Dawn." Jude laughed, and it came out as something closer to a sob. "Do you understand what happens at dawn? The binding has to be renewed at first light — it's in the map, in the marginalia Sera translated. Eleven voices, renewed daily, and the twelfth chair empty for three days now. The choir is starving, Maren. That's why Corvane moved tonight. That's why he sold us. Not to punish me. To *feed* them." He pressed his palms into his eye sockets until sparks bloomed. "And I led her here. I sent word. I chose the safehouse. Every step of this, I walked her to the chair myself."

"No." Maren's voice cracked across the dark like a whip, and in it he heard twenty years of convent silence breaking all at once. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to make yourself the author of everything. Corvane betrayed us. The choir hunted us. You *refused* him — I watched you refuse him when refusing cost you everything you had. If you want to kneel in the dirt and call yourself guilty, wait until we've actually lost. Right now you're just tired, and tired men always think the ledger's closed."

For a moment Jude could only stare at the shape of her in the darkness — the erased princess, the forgotten sister, kneeling in plague-bone dust with her swollen knees and her brother's crown eating her family alive somewhere overhead — and he thought: *this is what the kingdom threw away.*

"You sound like her," he managed. "Sera. When she told me the map's notation was choral marks. Same fire."

"She'd be insufferable about the comparison." Maren hauled him upright by the forearm, and her strength surprised him, the strength of a woman who had spent two decades hauling water and being told she imagined things. "The crypts. You said the dead part for you. The undercity runs beneath the palace — Doss said the purge-tunnels connect to the royal ossuary. Can you find the way?"

"I can find *a* way." Jude flexed his fingers, feeling for the gift the way a man feels for a tooth that's gone loose. "The dead know me. Whether they'll help me—" He thought of the choir's voices, welcoming, patient, hungry. "Some of them will. Some of them are the ones doing the hunting."

"Then we ask nicely and run faster." Maren was already moving, one hand trailing the femur-mortared wall like a rail. "Dawn, Jude. We have until dawn."

* * *

The ossuary galleries went down in spirals, each level older than the last, and the dead got louder as they descended — not louder in volume but in *proximity*, the way a crowd gets louder as you push into it. Jude walked with one palm trailing the bone walls and let the voices break over him.

*A cartographer, 312 years dead, muttering survey measurements.* *A child, nameless, singing a skipping rhyme with no ending.* *A plague-doctor reciting a list of symptoms like a prayer.* They rose around him and fell away, curious, and some of them — the old ones, the deep ones, the ones laid down before the Guild existed — went quiet as he passed, the way a forest goes quiet around a predator. Or around a king.

"There," Maren whispered.

Ahead, where the gallery narrowed, the wall of bones had been broken open — recently, crudely, from the other side. Through the gap breathed air that smelled of stone and incense and, faintly, unbearably, of the palace chapel where Maren had been crowned alongside a brother who was already, even then, beginning to disappear.

Jude stopped with his hand on the broken bones, and the dead in them spoke to him all at once, a dozen whispering mouths:

*She runs ahead of you. The singer. Not her body — her voice. They're pulling it down the tunnels to the crown-chamber, rehearsing as they go, and she is fighting every note, and she is losing, and she knows exactly how long she can hold out, because she counted, because she's a professional, because she wanted you to know.*

"How long?" Jude asked the wall.

The bones answered together, in the voice of the cartographer, precise as a survey:

*Until dawn. And dawn is one hour away.*

Jude stood in the dark with the dead murmuring around him and felt time collapse to a point — one hour, sixty minutes, the width of a hair — and inside that hair, everything: Sera in the twelfth chair, Doss in a Guild cell or worse, Maren's brother wearing his sister's grief like a borrowed coat, and the choir, the patient, starving choir, opening its arms.

"Maren," he said. "Run."

They ran.

Behind them, far above, faint as memory, Sera's voice rose through the stone — fighting, bending, dragging every note out of true — and for one impossible moment the whole ossuary rang with her defiance, every dead throat in the walls joining her unasked, a chorus of the discarded singing against the dawn.

Then the melody caught her. Bent her flat note true. Folded her voice into the harmony like a page into a ledger.

And the twelfth chair, somewhere below, was no longer empty.
