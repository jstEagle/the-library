## 16. The Crypt Beneath the Crown

The stairs went down farther than any palace had a right to be deep.

Jude counted the steps because counting was what he did when his hands wanted to shake — two hundred and eleven steps before the torchlight Maren carried stopped reaching the walls on either side of them, and the dark became a country they were traveling through rather than a room they stood in. Two hundred and twelve. Two hundred and thirteen. Behind them, muffled by stone and distance and the whole sleeping weight of the palace above, Sera's voice had gone quiet.

He would not think about what that meant. Not yet. Thinking about it was a door, and behind the door was a chair, and in the chair was —

*Not yet.*

"Jude." Maren's whisper came from somewhere at his shoulder. She had taken the stairs like a woman descending into her own grave — steady, unflinching, as if twenty years of being erased had taught her that the only way through erasure was to walk straight down its throat. "How far does this go?"

"The map says it goes to the king." He kept his voice low. His voice had been low for three days now, ever since the bonehouse burned; some part of him seemed to believe that if he spoke quietly enough, the Guild's bells could not find him. "And the map is drawn on the king. So I suppose it goes exactly as far as he does."

Ahead of them, faint as breath on winter glass, the vellum glowed.

It had been glowing since the twelfth chair filled. Jude had felt it change against his chest like a second heartbeat — the skin-map of the royal skeleton, inked in binding-marks and vertebra-names, waking up as though the thing it charted had turned over in its sleep and noticed them coming. Now it shed a pale amber light that showed them the stair, and only the stair, and nothing of what waited beyond it.

Maren looked at the light with an expression Jude was beginning to know well: grief and fury braided so tightly he could not have said which strand was which. "My brother," she said. "That light is my brother."

"It's the choir wearing your brother."

"I know what it is." Her knuckles whitened around the torch. "I'm telling you what it *was*."

The stair ended.

They came out into a gallery so vast that Maren's torchlight died a few feet past the threshold, swallowed by a darkness that seemed not empty but *attentive*. The floor under their boots was not stone. Jude knelt and pressed his palm flat against it and understood, with the cold certainty of his trade, what he was kneeling on.

Bone. Polished, fused, ancient bone — femurs and ribs and skulls laid in courses like brick, worn smooth by centuries of feet. The entire floor of the crypt beneath the crown was made of the dead.

"They built it from the kings," Maren breathed. "All of them."

"Not all." Jude unfolded the map and held it up. In its glow he could see the gallery stretching away into the dark, and along both walls — *there*, where the torch found nothing but the map insisted otherwise — shapes. Niches. Thrones of pale stone, ranked in tiers like a theater, descending toward something at the far end that even the map's light would not touch. "Only the ones who mattered. Look."

The map had changed again. He was almost used to it now — the way the ink crawled when no one watched, redrawing itself in notation that Sera read like scripture and he read like a ledger. But this was new. Down the length of the gallery, in a hand older than the binding-marks, older than the choral script, words were surfacing on the vellum one line at a time, the way frost climbs a window.

*You are standing in the House of Continuance.*

Maren read it aloud in a whisper. Then she looked at Jude. "Did you write that?"

"No." His mouth had gone dry. "But I know who did."

Because he had heard that hand before. Every carver who worked bone heard it eventually — the deep, patient pressure beneath the whispers of the recently dead, the voice that belonged to no single skull because it belonged to all of them. The dead spoke to him in fragments, in last thoughts and unfinished sentences. This was different. This was a sentence being finished, deliberately, across forty feet of vellum made from a king's skin.

*We were not always what you have seen.*

The words kept coming. Jude walked, and the map wrote, and Maren held the torch high over both of them as though light could act as witness.

*Our founder — Aldric, third of that name — buried his father in this place with every honor, and then discovered that honors are for the living, and the dead do not receive them. He sat by the sarcophagus for nine nights. On the ninth night he asked the question that built everything you see.*

*Why should a good king stop being good just because he stops breathing?*

Jude stopped walking.

The gallery stretched ahead of them, tier upon tier of empty thrones, and the words kept surfacing on the skin of a dead man in his hands, and for a moment — one long, cold moment — Jude Eastwick, keeper of impossible ledgers, hearer of the dead, did not see a horror at all.

He saw a grieving son.

* * *

"He asked the dead to stay," Jude said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like a coin rubbed featureless. "That's all it was, at first. That's all it *is*. A son who couldn't let go."

Maren had gone still beside him. In the torchlight her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish. "Aldric the Third," she said slowly. "The Merciful. They taught us — they taught *me*, before — that he ended the famine. That he opened the granaries. That he wept at executions and pardoned half of them." Her jaw tightened. "They never taught us what he built underneath the palace."

"Because what he built wasn't wicked." Jude heard himself say it and flinched, but the map was already writing his thought faster than shame could catch it:

*No. It was mercy. Hear us. We ask only to be heard rightly, once, before you do what you have come to do.*

"You know why we've come?" Maren's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Then you know what you took from me. From *him*. Twenty years — twenty years of my brother smiling at banquets with someone else behind his eyes, while I was locked in a convent and told I was mad for hearing him cry through the wall—"

*We know,* the vellum wrote, and the letters trembled, and Jude realized with a lurch that the dead kings were afraid. *We know what it became. We are asking you to understand what it was, because the difference is the only weapon you carry that the choir cannot take from you.*

The map went dark for a heartbeat. Then, deeper in the gallery, thrones began to glow — faint outlines, one after another, like lamps lit down a long hall. And the whispering started.

Jude had heard the dead his whole life. Through relics, through bone, through the carved and the buried and the burned. He knew every register of their voices: the pleading, the accusing, the confused, the peaceful. What rose in the ossuary now was none of these. It was a chorus of old men speaking in unison — not the choir's perfect, hungry unison, but the rougher harmony of many voices agreeing, the way a family agrees, the way a council agrees — and every word landed in Jude's bones like a hammer on a tuning fork.

*Sit,* they said. *Hear the ledger. We will keep honest accounts.*

Ledger-keeping. They had chosen the one phrase in the world that could make him kneel.

He sat down on the lowest step of the nearest throne, and Maren, after a moment, sat beside him, torch upright between them like a candle at a vigil. Above them, tier upon tier, the ghost-lights of the dead kings brightened. And the oldest voice of all — Aldric's, Jude was somehow certain, the founder's, the first grief — began to speak.

* * *

*I ruled for thirty-one years,* the voice said, and the map transcribed it in flowing script that Jude read by feel more than sight, his fingers resting on the vellum like a man reading braille. *In those years I ended a famine, signed four treaties, buried a wife, and executed a man I later learned was innocent. Do you know what I thought about on my deathbed? Not the treaties. The innocent man. I lay there drowning in everything I had gotten wrong, and I thought: all of this judgment, all of this hard-won knowing, and it dies with me. My son will begin again at zero. He will make new mistakes, worse ones perhaps, and his son after him, and the kingdom will stagger from coronation to coronation like a drunkard from wall to wall, learning nothing, keeping nothing.*

*So I asked the bone-singers of my court — there were twelve of them, then, and they sang at funerals, only at funerals — whether a soul might be persuaded to remain. Not to rule. To* advise. *To sit beside the living king, invisible, and say: I tried that. It fails thus. Here is where the innocent man is buried; go and weep at his grave, and do better than I did.*

Jude's throat ached. Because he had spent his life among the dead, and he knew — he *knew* — that this was true. The dead were not monsters. They were people who had run out of time, stuffed full of things they'd never gotten to say. Of course someone had tried to build a door instead of a wall. Of course the first attempt had been made out of love.

"What happened?" he asked quietly. "Where did it turn?"

The silence that followed had texture. It was the silence of men looking at each other across a table, deciding how much truth the stranger could bear.

*The twelfth singer happened,* the voices said together. *Her name was Liss. She loved the founder-king — not as a subject loves, but wholly — and when he died she could not stop singing. The binding was meant to hold one soul gently, the way a cup holds water. Liss poured herself into it entirely. And a cup cannot contain a river. The binding stretched. It learned to hold more than one. It learned that many voices, bound in harmony, make a single voice too large and too patient for any living ear to refuse.*

*The second king heard his father's advice and obeyed it, and prospered, and asked for more. The third asked for his father's memories, not merely his counsel — why learn slowly what can be borrowed? The fourth did not ask. By the seventh reign, the choir no longer advised kings. It chose them. It wore them. And it discovered that memory is food, and royal memory is a feast, and it has been eating ever since — eating the very wisdom it was built to preserve, until nothing remains of Aldric's mercy but the appetite.*

The ghost-lights flickered. For a moment Jude saw them clearly — not lights at all but figures, translucent, seated in their thrones with their heads bowed, and every one of them wore the expression he had seen a hundred times in the faces of the newly dead: the look of someone who has finally understood, too late, what they set in motion.

"We can starve it," Jude said. "Sera found it in the notation. Unbind the harmony, and it comes apart — it has to, it's nothing *but* harmony—" He stopped. Because the dead kings had begun, very softly, to shake their heads.

*You can unbind it,* they said. *And the king dies. Understand what your friend's counterpoint will do. The choir is the only thing holding that ruined body upright. It ate him decades ago, child. There is almost no brother left inside the crown for your princess to save. When the music stops, what remains will stop with it.*

Maren made a sound Jude had never heard a human being make — small, involuntary, like something breaking in a locked room. "No," she whispered. "No, I *heard* him. Through the wall. He was crying. He was *in there*, he was asking for help—"

*He was,* the voices said, gentle as snowfall. *For a long time, he was. The choir keeps a little of what it eats alive, the way a farmer keeps seed-corn. Enough to suffer. Enough to hope. It makes the memory sweeter.* A pause, and then, with terrible kindness: *Princess. Your brother has been seed-corn for six years. What you heard through the wall was real. What remains now is the rind of it. You may free him, or you may lose him — but you cannot do both, and either way, he is already gone further than love can follow.*

Maren stood up. She stood the way Jude imagined her brother must once have stood at state occasions — spine straight, face composed, grief held behind glass. She picked up the torch.

"Then we go down," she said, "and I hold whatever is left of him while it ends. And then I burn this house of continuance to its foundations, and I put my name in the record of it, since apparently I'm the only one left who remembers anyone's name."

She started down the gallery toward the dark.

Jude lingered one moment longer. The dead kings' lights dimmed around him, and the oldest voice spoke once more, alone, in a register he recognized now as a father's:

*Carver. One warning, and then we spend the last of ourselves lighting your way. The choir knows you've come. It has known since the twelfth chair filled. It is not afraid of your counterpoint, because it has heard rebellion sung before, in its own ranks, and folded it in. It is not afraid of the thief or the singer or the blood of kings.*

*It is afraid of only one thing in the world. It is afraid of being remembered wrongly. So when you stand before it — and you will stand before it within the hour — it will show you the founder's mercy. It will show you Liss's love. It will make you feel, bone-deep, that destroying it is cruelty. That is not deception. That is its history, worn like a face.*

*Judge it by what it does, not what it was. Even we, who were it, beg you for that.*

The lights went out, tier by tier, like a household going to sleep. And in the sudden dark, the map flared bright as a signal fire, and far ahead — down through the last descent, past doors Jude could not yet see — something vast and patient and terribly loving began, softly, to sing.

Jude got up and followed Maren into the dark, toward the sound of the thing that had begun as a boy who missed his father.

The twelfth throne, when they reached it, was warm.

And waiting in it, hands folded, wearing Sera's stolen voice like a borrowed coat, the choir said, in perfect harmony, in the accent of a king three hundred years dead:

*"Welcome home, carver. We've kept your chair."*
