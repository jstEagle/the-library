## 17. Twelve Chairs

The map had lied to her, Sera decided, in the way that all maps lie: by omission. It charted the descent through the royal ossuary with loving precision — *femur arch, third rib gallery, the hall of kneeling kings* — but nowhere did it mark the sound.

Because there was sound. There had been sound for the last hundred steps, rising through the bone under their feet like water through chalk, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard, and she wanted to die.

That was the first warning. She'd been a singer long enough to know when beauty was being used as a hook.

"Stop humming," Doss said beside her. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at his own hands, which were trembling. "I can feel it wanting my mouth."

"I'm not humming." But she checked her lips and found them parted, found a thread of tone caught between her teeth like a hair. She bit down on it. The note kept singing anyway, somewhere behind her sternum, patient as tide. "It's in the walls. It's in the *stone*."

"It's in everything down here." Jude's voice came from ahead, already sanded down to a rasp — he'd been whispering since the ossuary gates, since the dead kings had pressed their warnings into him like coins into a beggar's palm. He held the map open against the wall with one hand; the vellum made of a king's skin seemed to drink the torchlight, and the inked skeleton on it pulsed faintly at the skull, marking where they were. Almost there. "Maren. Your brother — is he—"

"Alive." Maren's voice was flat as a blade laid on a table. She hadn't spoken more than single words since they'd entered the crypts, and each one cost her something visible. "The choir keeps him alive. That's the whole horror of it. It needs the body breathing or the binding frays." Her jaw worked. "He used to sing. Did you know that? Badly. In the bath, at harvest feasts. Off-key and delighted about it." She started walking again before anyone could answer. "Let's go hear what they've done with him."

The passage narrowed, then opened.

Sera felt the opening before she saw it — a change in pressure, the way a concert hall announces itself to a performer before the doors swing wide. A hush with architecture in it. Then the corridor turned, and the crown-chamber stood revealed, and every wrong note she had ever sung in her life rose up in her throat begging to be spent.

It was a rotunda carved from the living rock beneath the palace, its dome ribbed like the inside of a skull. Twelve thrones of bone ringed a central dais, arranged in a perfect circle — no, not a circle, she corrected herself, a *choir formation*, crescent within crescent, the way a master would seat voices by weight and color. Each throne had been grown rather than built, vertebrae fused and flared into armrests and high backs, and eleven of them were occupied.

The singers sat translucent as breath on glass. They wore the shapes they'd died in — court dress two reigns out of fashion, a novice's robe, one figure still in traveling mud — and none of them moved except to breathe, if you could call it breathing, that slow tidal shimmer. Their faces were serene. That was the worst part. Serenity like that had to be maintained. Serenity like that was *work*.

And above them, at the chamber's crown where the dome's ribs converged, hung the actual crown — iron and gold, black with age, turning slowly though there was no wind. And beneath it, enthroned on a twelfth throne grander than the rest, sat the king.

Her king. Maren's brother. The man whose coronation she had refused to sing.

He looked asleep. He looked *peaceful*. His chest rose and fell with the same tidal rhythm as the singers, and his face wore the same terrible serenity, and when he opened his eyes — he opened them without any change in expression, lids lifting like curtains on an empty stage — Sera understood that nothing behind them was home.

"There you are," the king said, and the voice was a chord. Not one voice. A dozen, maybe more, braided so tightly they passed for one until you listened the way she listened, and then you could hear the seams: a bass underneath, two altos, a bright soprano threaded through like wire. "We wondered which of you would come down the long stairs. The Guild said the carver. But the carver is only interesting because of who he carries." The chord-voice warmed, and the warmth was the most practiced thing in the room. "Sera Ilvane. We heard your audition years ago. You were twelve. You sang the Lamentation of Ashes and you wept in the seventh bar, and we thought: *there*. There is a voice that grieves properly. Grief is the grain we carve along."

"Get away from her," Jude rasped.

"We are not speaking to you yet, bone-carver." The king's head turned — smoothly, too smoothly, the motion of something that had learned human movement from watching rather than being. "Though we will speak to you soon. We have been keeping a chair warm."

Sera followed the gesture and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

The twelfth throne. Set into the crescent at the place of honor — the position directly opposite the king, the anchor voice, the foundation. It was empty. It was empty and it was *waiting*, and the bone of it was paler than the others, newer, and as she looked at it the tidal breathing of the eleven singers hitched — once, all together, like a congregation seeing the bride.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the terms yet," the choir said pleasantly.

* * *

They were given terms because the choir, whatever else it was, had manners. That was the thing Sera kept circling back to as the king's mouth laid out its offer in that braided voice: it had *manners*. It said please. It apologized for the restraints that rose from the floor — bands of pale bone, grown smooth as river stone — and closed around her wrists, her ankles, her throat, light as jewelry. It offered Jude and Maren seats. It thanked Doss for coming, though Doss had been bound separately, further off, and was being watched by two of the translucent singers who had risen from their thrones and drifted to stand over him like ushers.

"The terms are these," the king said. "Sing with us. Take the twelfth chair willingly, and the transition will be gentle. You will keep everything you are — your name, your memories, your anger, especially your anger; anger makes such lovely harmonics. You will sing the kingdom stable for a hundred years. No famines while you hold the harvest verse. No wars while you hold the marching one. You have seen the court, girl. You have seen what a king is when he is only a man — greedy, frightened, small. We are what a kingdom looks like when it decides to be good *permanently*."

"I've seen what you do to villages," Maren said.

"Hollowmere was a tuning exercise," the choir said, with the mild regret of a conductor apologizing for a flat rehearsal. "No one suffered. They simply stopped disagreeing. Isn't that what every ruler has ever wanted? We merely achieved it."

"And my brother?" Maren's voice cracked down the middle. "What's left of him?"

The chord-voice paused. For just a moment, one thread of it — a low, tired baritone — surfaced alone, and the king's face flickered, and for one heartbeat Sera saw a man drowning behind his own eyes.

Then the harmony closed over him. "The vessel retains what is useful," the choir said. "As will you."

Sera flexed her fingers against the bone bands. They didn't bite. They didn't need to. The whole chamber was the restraint — the acoustics alone could pull a trained voice out of her body like a splinter. She'd felt it from a hundred steps away. From ten feet, seated in the choir's own geometry, she could feel her larynx warming, her diaphragm settling into supported breath without her permission, her body *preparing* to sing the way a dog prepares to point.

They weren't going to force her. That was the trap inside the trap. A forced voice was a closed door; the choir needed her *open*. It needed her to choose, the way the other eleven had chosen — she looked at their serene faces and saw it now, saw the choice fossilized in every one of them, the moment of consent preserved like amber. They had all believed they were saying yes to mercy.

"Sera," Jude whispered. He was three feet away, bound to a chair of grown bone, and his ruined voice could barely carry even in this perfect acoustic. But he didn't need volume. He looked at her the way he'd looked at the ledger entry that wouldn't balance, and she suddenly understood that this man heard the dead in everything he touched, and right now he was hearing *her*, alive, and refusing to look away. "You don't owe them a note."

"Don't I?" she said lightly. Too lightly. "Eleven chairs, one empty, dawn coming. Someone has to fill the vacancy. That's just arithmetic."

"It's never just arithmetic," Jude said. "I keep ledgers. Nothing is ever just arithmetic. There's always a name attached."

The choir let them talk. That frightened her more than anything — the patience of it, the confidence. It had done this eleven times. It knew exactly how long a resistant voice took to ripen.

So she did the only thing left to her, the thing they train into you at the conservatory before they teach you anything beautiful: she began, very quietly, to sabotage.

* * *

It started with breath.

A singer's first sin is audible breath, and Sera committed it gloriously — great ragged gasps, timed wrong, placed loud, so that when the choir finally drew her into the harmonic field and her body began to resonate whether she willed it or not, her breath tore holes in the tone. The chord above the dais shivered. One of the eleven singers' brows creased — the first expression any of them had shown.

"Your technique is suffering," the choir observed. "Fear does that. It will pass."

"Mm," Sera said, and hummed a semitone flat.

She felt it land like a thrown stone in a pond. The choir's harmony was a machine of intervals, and intervals are mathematics made audible, and she had just fed it a wrong number. The braid of voices tightened around the flaw, corrected, absorbed it — but the correction cost them a visible effort, a ripple running around the crescent of thrones like wind through wheat.

"Do that again," Doss called from across the chamber, delighted, "and I'll marry you."

"Doss," Maren warned.

"He's working his wrists loose," Jude breathed, barely a sound. "Left side. Don't look."

She didn't look. She sang instead — or rather, she let the choir think it was taking her voice, opening her throat just enough, feeding the great machine a stream of almost-right notes. A third that leaned sharp. A suspension resolved a beat early. An appoggiatura where no appoggiatura belonged. Each error was small enough to seem like fear, and each one made the choir spend a little more of itself on repair, and she watched the repair happen — watched the translucent singers lean fractionally toward her, their serenity straining at the seams, eleven faces doing the silent furious work of covering for a soloist who would not blend.

It was the oldest war in music. Conductor against chorister. Blend against self. And she had been exiled from the court for refusing to blend with a lie, and she would be damned in whatever crypt this was before she blended with this one.

"You are doing this deliberately," the choir said at last. The warmth had gone out of the chord-voice. What remained was colder and older and, frankly, more honest. "The wrong notes."

"Am I?" Sera widened her eyes. "Grief does that. You said yourself — grief is the grain you carve along. Well. Here's my grief." She leaned back in the throne of bone as far as the bands allowed, and smiled at the thing wearing her king. "You should have picked a soprano who never trained. You should have picked someone innocent. Because I have spent twenty years being told my voice belongs to the crown, and I have thought about this exact room every night since they walked me out of the palace gates, and I promise you—" she took the sweetest breath of her life, "—I have rehearsed."

She sang.

Not the choir's song. A descending line, ugly on purpose, a tritone dropped into the middle of their perfect fifth like a knife into a wedding cake, and she threw it into the resonant dome and let the chamber itself betray its masters — the acoustics that had been built to amplify harmony now amplified *her dissonance*, multiplied it, returned it to the eleven singers doubled and redoubled until the whole crescent shuddered.

One of the translucent figures screamed. Silently — the mouth opened, the face broke, and no sound came out, and somehow that was worse than sound. The serenity tore like wet paper. Underneath it was a young woman, terrified, twenty years dead, *awake*.

"Oh," Sera breathed, staring. "Oh, you can wake them up."

"Bind her throat," the choir said, and for the first time it sounded like many things arguing.

The bone bands at her neck began to tighten—

—and stopped. Because across the chamber, Doss stood up.

* * *

He had worked one wrist free and then simply stood, and the two translucent singers who had been minding him reached for him and passed through him like smoke through a doorway, and Sera remembered what he was — the thief who walked through grief, who carried other people's sorrows like a folded cloak — and she saw him unfold it.

Doss opened his arms, and everything he had ever taken from strangers poured out of him.

Not light. Not fire. *Weight.* The chamber's air thickened; the torch flames bowed; the eleven singers recoiled in their thrones as decades of borrowed mourning crashed over them — the grief of a thousand funerals, of warded gates and purged tunnels and a village that smiled identical smiles, all of it carried by one thin man who had never once set it down, and now set it down everywhere at once.

The choir's harmony buckled. Notes slid. The king on the grand throne gripped its armrests, and the low tired baritone surfaced in his throat, alone, drowning, and Sera heard it say — in a voice that was one man and no chorus at all:

"*Maren?*"

And the bone band at Sera's throat went slack, because the choir had stopped paying attention to her.

Every eye in the dark — living, dead, and otherwise — turned to the dais, where Princess Maren was already moving, skirts gathered in both fists, running for her brother across the floor of the crown-chamber as the great iron crown above began, slowly, to turn faster.

And the twelfth throne, empty and waiting, began to glow.
