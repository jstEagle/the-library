## 18. The Choir Unmasked

The relic was warm in Jude's pocket.

That alone should have frightened him. Bone did not hold heat — not like this, not like a living thing pressed close to a fire. But he had carved the founder-king's knucklebone three days ago in a cellar that smelled of tallow and old fear, and from the first stroke of his knife it had been warm, as though the man whose finger it had once belonged to were still flexing it, still impatient, still waiting two hundred years for someone to ask him a direct question.

Jude had asked the dead questions all his life. They answered in fragments, in whispers through the grain of whatever they'd died holding. But this thing in the crown-chamber above them — this choir wearing his king like a coat cut from his skin — it had never once been asked anything by someone who could hear the answer.

*They will lie,* the dead kings murmured through the ossuary walls around him, their voices layered like sediment. *It lies the way water lies. Smoothly. By shape.*

"Then I'll make it speak plainly anyway," Jude whispered back.

Maren crouched beside him at the mouth of the stair, her brother's signet ring clenched in her fist hard enough to blanch her knuckles. Above them, through twelve feet of royal stone, Sera's voice drifted down — thin, strained, singing the wrong notes with the precision of a woman buying seconds with each one. A pause. A discord. Another pause.

"She can't stall forever," Maren said. "Whatever's up there, it's patient. Patience is the one thing it has more of than we do."

"Then we stop being patient." Jude drew out the knucklebone. In the crypt-dark it gave off no light, but it seemed to gather what little there was, the way a cup gathers wine. "I need you to get me close."

"Close." Maren laughed without humor. "You want to walk into a room with eleven bound spirits and a hollowed king and get *close*."

"I want to hold this bone where its owner used to sit," Jude said. "The founder-king built the choir. Whatever mercy started this, it started in his hands. And dead men answer best when you bring them home."

He heard her swallow in the dark. Then she nodded once, and they climbed.

* * *

The crown-chamber had not changed since he'd last seen it, except that everything in it had grown worse the way fruit grows worse — slowly, sweetly, rotting from the center outward. Twelve thrones of pale bone arranged in a crescent beneath the vaulted dark. Eleven occupied by singers who were no longer quite singers: translucent figures stitched from voice and habit, their mouths moving in perpetual half-song even when silent. And at the crescent's heart, on a throne older and grander than the rest, the king.

King Aldous sat upright, hands on the arms of his throne, eyes open and fixed on nothing. His chest rose and fell with perfect regularity. His face wore an expression of mild, pleasant attention — the expression of a man listening to music he could not turn off. He looked, Jude thought, like a house with all its furniture still arranged and no one living in it.

Sera stood before the twelfth chair, empty, its bone surface gleaming with fresh polish. Her shoulders were rigid. Her voice came out steady, note after deliberate wrong note, and Jude could see the cost of it in the tremor of her hands, the sweat at her temple. The eleven singers had turned toward her, heads tilted, mouths working — tasting her errors, savoring them, waiting.

Doss was nowhere visible, which meant he was exactly where he'd said he'd be: folded into the shadow behind the chamber's eastern pillar, carrying his cloak of borrowed grief, invisible to anything that fed on harmony. Jude caught the faintest flicker of his eyes and looked away before the choir could follow his gaze.

Maren stepped out first. That was the plan — the sister, the erased one, walking into the light where the thing wearing her brother's face would have to reckon with her.

"Aldous," she said.

The king's head turned. The movement was smooth, graceful, entirely wrong — the head turned *all the way*, past any angle a living neck permitted, and then corrected itself with a small mechanical courtesy, as if embarrassed.

"Maren." The voice was Aldous's voice, warm and deep, and underneath it, faintly, eleven other voices breathing in time. "You've come such a long way to be disappointed again."

"You know my name."

"I know everything the records forgot." The choir smiled with the king's mouth. It was a kind smile. That was the worst part; it was genuinely, architecturally kind. "We kept it for you. All these years. Your name, safe, in the dark, where nothing could take it from you."

"You took everything else."

"We took nothing that wasn't offered." The king rose from his throne, unhurried, robes settling around him like poured wax. "Your brother offered himself at his coronation renewal, freely, gladly, because he loved his kingdom and feared his death. We are only what love builds when it refuses to let go. Ask your carver there. He of all people understands — every relic is a refusal to let go, isn't it? Every bone he carves is a small resurrection. We are simply honest about the practice."

Jude stepped out of the stair-shadow.

The choir's attention came off Maren and settled on him like a physical weight, like snow sliding from a roof. Eleven translucent faces turned. The king's eyes found him, and in them Jude saw something he hadn't seen from across the chamber: hunger held with enormous discipline. A dog that had learned table manners.

"The bone-carver," the choir said pleasantly. "Our twelfth chair. You've come early. How considerate. We do hate to chase."

"I didn't come to sit." Jude walked forward, slow, keeping Maren at his shoulder. His hand closed around the founder-king's knucklebone in his pocket. "I came to ask questions. You've never been asked, have you? Two hundred years of singing, and everyone who might have asked you something true was either dead or bound."

"Questions are how it begins," the choir said, and for just a moment the pleasantness thinned and something vast moved underneath it, the way a whale's shadow moves under calm water. "Questions are how your Guild found us. Questions are how kingdoms fall."

"Then answer one," Jude said, "and I'll stop asking."

He brought the bone out of his pocket.

* * *

The effect was immediate and terrible.

The eleven singers stopped mid-breath. Their half-song cut off so completely that the silence rang. And the king — the thing in the king — went still in a new way, a hunted way, its eyes fixing on the small yellowed knucklebone in Jude's palm as though he'd drawn a blade.

"Where," the choir said, and the harmony cracked down the middle, two voices pulling apart, "did you get that."

"From a man who kept his promises," Jude said. "Which will mean nothing to you. Hold it yourself, if you doubt me. Oh — you can't. You haven't touched anything real in two hundred years, have you? You wear hands. You don't have any."

The king took a step forward. The eleven singers rose from their thrones in unison, a single motion like grass bending in wind, and Sera's voice broke off entirely as she backed against the twelfth chair.

"Put it down," the choir said. All the warmth was gone now. What remained was old and cold and very tired. "Carver. Put it down, and we will discuss your friends' lives like reasonable people."

"No," Jude said. "You'll listen. Because he's listening too." He lifted the bone higher. "Founder. First of your line. The man who wrote the first verse. I'm holding your hand. Answer me."

For a heartbeat, nothing. The chamber held its breath.

Then the knucklebone flared — not with light, but with *voice*, a sound that bypassed the ear entirely and struck the bones of everyone present, a sound like a door opening in a house sealed for centuries. And out of the king's throat, unbidden, dragged up through whatever mechanism the choir used to speak, came another voice: dry, cracked, human, furious.

"*What have you done with my work.*"

The choir convulsed. The king's body staggered as though struck. The eleven singers clutched at their own throats, mouths opening on silent screams, and through all of them the founder-king's voice poured, using their instruments against their will, because a dead king's word carried weight in a room built on dead kings' words.

"*I made you a lullaby,*" the voice thundered. "*A mercy. A way for a good king's wisdom to outlast his failing body — a season, perhaps two, while an heir grew ready. Not this. Not centuries. ANSWER THE CARVER.*"

And Jude asked.

His voice shook, but he made it carry. "What do you eat?"

The silence stretched. The choir fought itself — he could see it fighting, the king's face cycling through expressions like a man flipping through masks, mildness, rage, sorrow, blankness — and then the truth came out the way infected matter comes out, under pressure, all at once.

"Memory," it said. "Royal memory. We must eat to remain coherent. A choir is many voices agreeing, and agreement requires shared ground, and the only ground a king offers is his own past. We take his childhood. His griefs. His loves. We take them and we harmonize them and we sing them back until they are ours and he—" the voice faltered, and for one raw moment it sounded almost like confession, almost like shame, "—he forgets why he ever wanted anything."

"How much does Aldous have left?" Jude asked.

The choir did not answer.

"How much," Jude repeated, and raised the bone, and the founder's presence surged through the chamber like cold iron, "*answer him*—"

"Less than a year's worth," the choir said. Flatly. The way a merchant names a price he cannot negotiate. "Fragments. A face here, a name there. He remembers how to sign documents. He remembers the shape of statecraft. He does not remember his mother's voice. He does not remember that he had a sister." A pause, and then, with terrible precision: "Until tonight. Tonight he remembers you, Maren, because we let the memory surface to wound you. We spend his memories like coin. We are nearly spent."

Maren made a sound beside him — small, wounded, furious — and Jude gripped her arm and held her where she was, because the moment was not done.

"And if you finish eating him," Jude said, "you need a new host. A new treasury. That's the twelfth chair. That's why the Guild hunts me. My gift — hearing the dead through what they held — I'm not a singer. I'm a *storehouse*. Bind me to that chair and you don't just gain a voice. You gain every voice I've ever heard. Every dead man who ever whispered through my knives. An archive."

"Yes," the choir said simply.

Not denial. Not deflection. *Yes*, with the calm of a thing that had never needed to justify itself, because no one had ever been able to make it.

Jude felt the cold clarity settle over him then, the thing he would carry out of this room for the rest of his life. He had come down into the dark believing he was hunting a possession — a sickness in the king that could be cured, a spirit that could be cast out, a wrong that could be righted and leave everyone whole. He had imagined the exorcism. The waking. Aldous standing up out of his own body, blinking, freed, embraced by his sister.

There would be no waking. There was almost no one left to wake.

This was not a possession. This was a parasite, and the host was nearly consumed, and parasites were not cured. They were starved. They were starved, or they were fed, and every hour he stood here negotiating was an hour the thing spent picking clean the last bones of a man who had once, twenty years ago, secretly taught his erased sister to whistle in the palace orchards because she'd been sad.

"You're not a mercy anymore," Jude said quietly. "Maybe you were, once. Maybe he really did build you out of love." He turned the founder's knucklebone over in his fingers, and felt the dead king's rage and grief burning in it like a coal. "But a mercy that eats its beneficiary isn't mercy. It's just appetite with good manners. And I'm not going to cure you. I'm going to starve you."

The choir looked at him for a long moment.

Then it smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was not kind anymore, and behind it Jude heard eleven voices draw breath together, and the founder-king's voice in the bone cried out a warning he couldn't parse fast enough—

—and every candle in the chamber guttered at once, and from far below, far away, through stone and earth and the bones of the city itself, bells began to toll.

Guild bells. The alarm he'd heard only once before, the night a dying courier bled out on his workshop floor.

Dawn was coming. And Corvane was coming with it.
