## 19. Dawn Binding

The doors of the crown-chamber had no right to open that fast.

Jude heard them before he saw them — the groan of ancient hinges, then the drumbeat of boots on ossuary stone, dozens of boots, moving in the terrible unison he had learned to dread. The enforcers came down the stair in ranks of four, lantern-light climbing their faces until they all looked like the same face, and behind them, unhurried as a man arriving at his own dinner party, came Guildmaster Corvane.

His coat was midnight silk. His hands were folded at his waist. He looked at Jude sitting on the steps beside Maren and smiled the way a ledger smiles when it finally balances.

"There you are," Corvane said. "Right where you were always going to be."

Jude got to his feet. His legs felt borrowed. Around him the chamber breathed — twelve thrones of pale bone arranged in a crescent beneath the dais, eleven of them occupied by singers who were not quite there, translucent as breath on glass, their mouths open in a silence that was somehow louder than sound. On the central throne sat the thing wearing the king: a broad-shouldered shape in royal white, its crown a woven circlet of finger-bones that seemed to drink the lantern-light rather than reflect it.

And in the twelfth chair — empty, waiting, angled toward the others like an empty seat at a family table — Sera knelt, bound at wrists and ankles with vellum strips that crawled faintly, like skin trying to remember being alive.

"Sera—"

"Don't," she said. Her voice was raw from hours of wrong notes, of singing flat and sharp and sideways while the choir leaned into her skull like listeners at a recital. "Don't waste it on my name. Save your voice, bone-carver. You're going to need every note."

Corvane descended the last step. "You've seen the chamber now. You understand what it is." He gestured, and the translucent singers turned their heads — all eleven at once, smooth as weathervanes — to look at Jude. "Continuity. That's all this is. Kings die; kingdoms rot in the gap between one man's death and the next man's learning. We solved that. We solved death, Eastwick, or the useful part of it. All I ask is that you help me solve it better."

"You ask me to sit in that chair."

"I ask you to take your place." Corvane's voice went soft, and softness from him was worse than shouting. "A gift like yours, wasted on ledgers. You could hear every king who ever was. You'd never be lonely again. You'd never be *powerless* again." He spread his hands. "Think of what you were three weeks ago. A clerk. Now look where refusing has brought you."

Jude looked. He looked at Sera kneeling in the twelfth chair with blood at the corner of her mouth. At Doss, pressed against the far wall, gray-faced, his hands shaking with everything they carried. At Maren, standing between her brother's puppet-body and the drawn steel of six enforcers, a princess with no name in any book, holding a candlestick she'd torn from the wall because it was the only weapon in reach.

"Where refusing has brought me," Jude said, "is here. With them." He met Corvane's eyes. "Bind me if you can. But you'll have to make me sing, and I promise you, Guildmaster — you don't want to hear what I sing."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — the first honest thing he'd shown all night. Doubt. Then he snapped his fingers, and the enforcers moved.

* * *

Maren had spent twenty years practicing stillness. Stillness at the convent table while sisters spoke of her as though she were furniture. Stillness in the abbess's office while a Guild clerk explained, kindly, that Princess Maren had never existed and would she please sign this paper saying so. Twenty years of swallowing fire and calling it patience.

She burned through all of it in four steps.

The first enforcer didn't expect a woman in a nun's habit to swing a brass candlestick at his jaw. The second expected it and caught the second swing on his forearm — but catching it cost him the moment he needed to stop her from shoving past him entirely, and suddenly Maren was through the line, running up the shallow steps toward the dais, toward the throne, toward the brother she hadn't spoken to in twenty years and who had maybe a handful of himself left inside that white-clad shell.

"Alric!" She used the name no one had used since their mother died. "Alric, it's Mare. It's Mare, you stubborn ox, you *look at me*—"

The king's head turned.

It turned slowly, with a grinding patience that made her stomach drop, and for one heartbeat she saw him — really saw him, her brother at nine years old with mud on his boots, laughing at her over a stolen honeycomb — looking out from behind eyes gone wide and vacant as wells. His lips moved. No sound came. But the shape they made was old, was small, was a child's word learned before any tutor ever corrected him:

*Mare.*

Then the crown tightened. She watched it happen — the finger-bones flexing against his temples like a hand closing — and the wells filled back up with something else, something patient and hungry and very, very old, and the king of Vellmark said, in a voice like twelve voices poured into one throat:

"The sister is not in the score."

"No," Maren said, and raised the candlestick. "I'm the errata."

An enforcer's gauntleted hand closed on her shoulder and hauled her backward off the step. She fought — she fought with twenty years of erased names and signed confessions and prayers to a God who apparently kept ledgers too — but they were strong and trained and there were so many of them, and they dragged her down onto the cold stone with her cheek against the floor, close enough to see the dust of ground kings packed into the mortar seams.

Close enough to hear, threaded under the boot-stamps and the chains, a whisper rising out of the mortar itself.

*Hold, little sister,* said the floor of the crypt, in her father's voice. *Hold. Help is coming. The quiet boy brought us with him.*

* * *

Doss had been afraid his whole life of exactly this moment, though he'd never known its shape until now.

He had spent nineteen years moving. Always moving. Because grief was patient and grief was heavy and grief, given one still hour, one quiet room, one closed door, would catch him the way deep water catches a stone. So he'd run rooftops and slipped crowds and stolen bread from funerals because mourners never count their purses, and everywhere he went he picked things up. Not coins. Not rings. Grief. The widow's first winter. The father's empty chair at supper. The child's unanswered question at the graveside. He wore them like a cloak, a thousand sorrows sewn into his shoulders, and he told jokes because jokes were the needle that kept the cloak pinned shut.

Now the enforcers had Sera by the arms and Maren under their boots and Jude walking toward the twelfth chair with two men gripping each elbow, and Doss stood alone against the wall with nowhere left to run, and the cloak's pins were pulling loose.

"Hey," he said. His voice cracked down the middle. "Hey! Which one of you fine gentlemen buried a daughter?"

They didn't stop. Of course they didn't stop. But one of them flinched, just slightly, at the word *daughter*, and Doss felt it land — felt the grief answer him from inside the man's chest like a struck bell — and understood, all at once, with the clarity of a man falling, what he actually was.

Not a thief. A resonator. Every sorrow he'd ever carried knew its home, and homes *called* to them.

"Fine," Doss whispered. "Fine. Take it all back. Take everything."

He stopped holding.

It was like opening a door at the bottom of the sea. Nineteen years of carried grief came off his shoulders in a single tide — the widow's winter, the empty chair, the child's question, the soldier's letter unsent, the mother's lullaby half-remembered, hundreds of sorrows, thousands, a whole city's worth collected crowd by crowd and funeral by funeral — and he did not aim them. He simply let them go home.

The nearest enforcer dropped his sword and sat down on the ossuary floor and began to weep for a daughter dead eleven years, weeping as he had not permitted himself to weep at the actual graveside, because there had been arrangements to make and men watching. The second enforcer tore off his helmet and called out a name — a name Doss had taken from a grieving wife outside the Saltgate two winters past — and the third and fourth collided with each other trying to get away from sounds only they could hear. Grief moved through the enforcer ranks like fire through dry grass, finding every locked door and opening it, and men who had come to bind a singer to a throne of bones knelt in the dust of dead kings and remembered why they had once been boys who cried.

Doss stayed on his feet. Barely. He felt hollowed, scoured, light in a way that terrified him more than the weight ever had — because underneath the cloak, at last exposed, was the thing he'd been running from since he was eight years old: a small cold room, a small cold bed, and a door his mother had closed gently from the outside and never opened again.

"Oh," he said, to no one. "Oh, *there* you are."

He did not fall. He wanted to. Instead he looked up at the dais, at Sera in the twelfth chair, and shouted in a voice scraped raw and new: "SERA! The chair's bound with vellum — burn the words off it! It's all writing, it's *only writing!*"

* * *

Sera had been stalling for six hours, and her repertoire of wrong notes was nearly exhausted.

The choir did not punish error. That was the horror of it. When she sang flat, they sang around her, folding her mistake into their harmony like a river absorbing a thrown stone, and each time they did, she felt the vellum bindings drink a little more of her — her range first, then her memory of the conservatory, then, horribly, her memory of *why she'd refused to sing at the coronation*, which was the one thing she had sworn to keep. She was losing herself measure by measure, and the worst part was how gentle it felt. Like drowning in warm water. Like being appreciated.

So when Doss's voice cracked across the chamber — *it's only writing* — she seized the thought like a rope thrown to a swimmer.

Writing. Yes. She'd read the binding-marks herself, weeks ago, on the map of the king's bones: notation, nothing more mystical than notation, a score written in skin. And a score could be misread. A score could be *altered*. She was, whatever else they'd taken from her, still the finest sight-reader the conservatory had produced in a generation.

The vellum strip binding her right wrist lay against her cheek where they'd wrenched her arm up. She turned her head and bit down.

Ink flooded her tongue — bitter, metallic, wrong — and the strip screamed. Not metaphorically; the vellum shrieked in a register below hearing, and the binding-marks written on it blurred as her saliva broke the sizing of the ink, and her right hand came free.

An enforcer lunged for her — then staggered, sobbing, as Doss's tide reached him. Sera rolled off the chair, tore the ankle-bindings against the chair's own carved edge, and stood up in the center of the twelve thrones with blood in her mouth and one thought burning brighter than fear:

If the choir needed a twelfth voice to stay coherent, then the twelfth voice did not have to *cooperate*.

"Jude!" she screamed. "The counterpoint! Whatever you're going to do — do it NOW!"

Across the chamber, Corvane had stopped smiling.

* * *

Jude had heard the dead his whole life, but he had never once heard them *together*.

That changed when the enforcers fell and the chamber went strange and quiet, and every relic he had ever carved — every whisper-bone, every keepsake, every impossible thing that had passed through the Vellum Guild's ledgers under his careful clerk's hands — woke at once along the walls of the crypt and began, softly, to hum.

They had followed him. Of course they had. The dead whose last held objects he had shaped into instruments of listening had been listening back for years, and when he fled the burning bonehouse they had traveled with him, relic by relic, pocket by pocket, all the way here, to the root of the thing that ate kings.

Corvane grabbed his collar. Up close the Guildmaster's composure had hairline cracks running through it, and through the cracks Jude could see the truth: Corvane was afraid. Had always been afraid. Power was just fear wearing good tailoring.

"Sit in the chair," Corvane said, "or I will have you bound into it, and I will keep your family alive as long as you sing well—"

"They're not my family," Jude said. "They're better. They're the kind you choose." And he drove his thumb into the older man's wrist — the pressure point every carver knows, the place where the small bones meet — and stepped out of Corvane's grip and walked, unbound and unbowed, to the twelfth chair.

It was warm. That was the worst discovery of the night: the chair was warm, and it fit him, and some traitorous part of him sighed with relief at the fit. The choir turned toward him, eleven faces of translucent longing, and the thing in the crown said, almost tenderly:

"*Yes.* We have been so tired. Sing with us, little listener, and rest."

Jude sat down. He looked at Sera, bleeding and grinning like a woman who had just won an argument with a book. He looked at Doss, hollow-eyed and standing guard over nothing but his own grief. He looked at Maren, rising from the stones with her father's voice still fading beneath her, and at the king on the throne, and in the king's vacant eyes he saw — far down, at the very bottom of the well — a boy with mud on his boots, waiting.

"Dawn's coming," Jude said. His voice was steady. Inside, he was already leaving. "Let's give it something to rise on."

He opened his mouth, and the dead kings of Vellmark opened theirs with him, and the first note of the twenty-first verse went up through the crown-chamber like a crack going up through ice—

—and Corvane, screaming, threw himself at the chamber's great lever, the one that would seal the doors and drown the room in binding-smoke, and the whole crypt shuddered as dawn's first light found the high vents and the true song began.
