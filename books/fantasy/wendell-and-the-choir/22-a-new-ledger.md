## 22. A New Ledger

The workshop smelled of beeswax and river-stone dust, and on the first morning of its existence it smelled also of burnt porridge, because Doss had insisted on making breakfast and Doss had never once in his life made anything that wasn't a disaster or a theft.

"Technically," he said, scraping the blackened pot with a knife that had no business being used for scraping, "I didn't steal the oats. I *liberated* them from a granary that was overcharging widows."

"You stole them," Sera said. She sat on the workbench with her boots swinging, a habit Jude had given up correcting, her voice still careful in the mornings — she warmed it like an instrument now, scales under her breath before she spoke too much. The twelfth chair had left marks on her that didn't show. Some mornings her pitch went flat without warning, as if something had been taken out of her throat and not entirely replaced.

"Stolen oats make terrible porridge," Doss said. "Liberated oats, apparently, make charcoal."

Jude stood at his bench by the window and let them argue. It was a good sound. He had spent years in a workshop where the only sounds were chisel and whisper — the dead murmuring through whatever bone he held, telling him their names, their debts, the small unfinished business they clutched like coins. Now the whispers were gone. What remained was porridge burning and Sera laughing — actually laughing, short and rusty, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years — and sunlight coming through glass that Maren had paid to have replaced three times because Doss kept testing whether the new wards would let him walk through.

They did not. He took it personally every time.

* * *

The sign above the door read **EASTWICK & COMPANY — CARVING, RESTORATION, HONEST ACCOUNTS**, and the last two words had been Maren's addition, painted herself, on a ladder, refusing help, in letters slightly too large.

"People will think it's a joke," Jude had told her.

"People thought you were a joke for thirty years," she said. "Look how that turned out for everyone."

She came most evenings now, when the council let her escape. She arrived the way weather arrives — everyone in the room adjusting themselves around her presence without quite noticing they'd done it. She'd bring bread from the palace kitchens and news from the rebuilding: the Guild's ledgers burned to nothing, Corvane vanished somewhere east of Hollowmere with whatever remained of the enforcers who hadn't melted back into the population like salt into water; the villages along the royal road waking up one by one from their identical smiles, weeping all at once and differently, which everyone agreed was the healthiest thing any of them had ever done.

Hollowmere itself was still smiling. That was the part Maren couldn't fix, and she hated it. The choir's technique had been practiced there, refined there, and when the harmony broke, whatever had been done to those people stayed done. Healers came and went. Sera went twice, sang for hours in the village square, and reported that some of them had begun to frown again — small progress, she called it, and then cried about it in the cellar where she thought no one could hear.

Jude heard. Not the way he used to hear — not the dead, never the dead anymore — but the living were loud enough in their own way, and he had learned, in these months, that listening was not a gift he'd lost. Only a particular frequency of it.

He carved anyway. His hands needed it the way other men's hands needed drink or prayer. Small things mostly: buttons, combs, a chess set for the guard captain who had once looked the other way when four suspicious people slipped into the palace during a state banquet and whom Maren had since promoted on the grounds of convenient blindness. He carved from shed antler and river bone and the honest ivory of animals that had died ordinary deaths, and none of it whispered, and he had stopped flinching at the silence.

Mostly stopped.

* * *

On a grey Tuesday in the month the city called Emberfall, a woman came in off the street holding a bundle wrapped in a shawl, and Jude knew before she spoke what it would be, because they always came in holding bundles.

"My mother," she said. "She died in spring. This was her — well." She unwrapped it on his bench: a finger bone, yellowed, worn smooth at the joint. "The old carvers said you could — that you used to be able to—"

"I can't hear them anymore," Jude said. It was the truth and he said it plainly, because plainness was the whole point of the new ledger. "Whatever I had, I spent it. In the crypt, under the palace. There was a price and I paid it and I don't regret it, but I won't sell you a lie either."

The woman's face did the thing faces did — the collapse, the reassembly. "Then why keep the shop? Why the sign?"

"Because bones are still worth carving. Because a comb made well outlasts grief, and grief needs things that outlast it." He turned the finger bone gently in the light. It was a fine bone. Whoever this mother had been, she had worked with her hands. "And because I'll tell you this for free: your mother held things carefully. See the wear here, at the joint? She gripped hard. Whatever she loved, she loved with her whole hand."

The woman looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed, wetly, and wiped her eyes with her wrist. "She did. She strangled every cat in the neighborhood with affection." She left the bone with him anyway — *make something*, she said, *anything, and I'll pay what it's worth* — and walked out lighter than she'd come in.

Doss appeared from the back room the moment the bell over the door settled. "You know," he said, leaning on the doorframe, "you could charge for that. The reading. People would pay."

"It's not a reading. It's observation. Any carpenter could tell you the same thing about wear patterns."

"Any carpenter wouldn't have said *she loved with her whole hand*. That's the difference. That's the thing you pretend isn't the thing." Doss stole an apple from the bowl on the counter — Jude watched him do it and said nothing, because the apples were bought with money Doss had stolen fair and square and the whole economy of the shop rested on not examining it too closely. "Maren wants to give you a title, you know. Court Bone-Carver. Official position. Seal and everything."

"I know."

"She's going to keep asking until you say yes."

"I know that too."

"And you're going to keep saying no because—" Doss bit the apple, chewed, gestured with it at the window, the street, the world. "Because titles are just ledgers with better handwriting. Something like that. You gave the speech at dinner last week."

"Something like that," Jude agreed.

But it was more than that, and Doss knew it was more than that, and neither of them said it, because the found family they had assembled ran on a fuel of unsaid things, generously supplied. The truth was that Jude Eastwick had spent his whole life keeping accounts for powers that traded in people, and he would not be entered into anyone's ledger again — not the crown's, not the Guild's, not even a grateful one. He kept his own books now. They balanced. That was enough.

* * *

Sera sang on Emberfall evenings, sometimes, when the mood took her — not performances, nothing so formal, just her voice moving through the workshop while he carved and Doss pretended to inventory stock he was actively diminishing. Her voice had changed since the crypt. Everyone said it was richer. Jude suspected what had actually happened was that something had been carved out of her and what grew back grew back deeper, the way a tree grows around a wound.

One evening she stopped mid-phrase and looked at him across the lamplit bench.

"You felt it just now," she said. "Don't lie. Your hands went still."

"They go still all the time. I'm thinking about joinery."

"You felt *listened to*." She said it the way she said everything now, directly, having spent enough of her life singing words written by other people. "That's the third time this week. I watch you when I sing. When I hit certain notes — low ones, the ones near where the binding-marks sat — you stop carving and you look like a man standing in a doorway."

Jude set down his chisel. Considered lying. Found, as always, that the habit of honesty had grown over the scar tissue of his old discretion like bark over a cut.

"When I was in the chair," he said slowly, "singing against them — the dead kings lent me their voices. All of them. Every king back to the founder. For a few minutes I was the loudest man who ever lived, and then the crown cracked and the voices went wherever voices go, and mine went hoarse, and I woke up on the crypt floor unable to hear so much as a whisper from a relic held to my ear." He picked up the chisel again, turned it. "I made my peace with that. I want you to know that. I'm not haunting my own workshop waiting for ghosts."

"I know."

"But." The word hung there. He let it.

"But," Sera echoed, softly, and began to hum again — the low notes, the ones near the marks — and Jude bent back over his work, and there it was again: not a voice, not a whisper, nothing so definite as hearing. More like the feeling of standing in an empty church and knowing, without evidence, that the emptiness was polite. That something had stepped back to give him room. That the silence was attending.

He carved. The bone curled away under the blade in clean, pale ribbons. And if the quiet seemed to lean in — if the lamplight seemed to hold its breath at certain frequencies of Sera's song — well. He had spent his life among impossible things, and he had learned the most important lesson the Vellum Guild had never put in its ledgers:

that some accounts are never closed, only carried forward.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first snow falling fat and silent on the day they opened the ledger properly — a real book, bound in calfskin, ruled in Maren's own hand, with columns for debts owed and debts forgiven and a third column nobody had planned for that Doss labeled **MIRACLES (PENDING)** and nobody crossed out.

The first entry was in Jude's writing: *Received, one workshop. Paid for, everything.*

The second was Sera's: *Owed to twelve names, sung. Marked paid in full.*

The third was Maren's, and it took her a long time to write, and she wrote it small: *Owed to my brother: a kingdom that remembers him rightly. Balance outstanding.*

And the fourth entry was Doss's, scrawled crookedly, and it said only: *Found: more than I lost.* And beneath it, after a pause visible in the pressure of the pen: *Still looking for the rest. Will advise.*

Jude closed the book and set it on the shelf above his bench, between the jars of polish and the little carved owl he'd made in his first week — an owl whose eyes, he noticed now, seemed to have found their way, without any decision on his part, toward the corner of the workshop where the shadows pooled deepest after dark.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the honest city, on the bells and the bakeries and the long slow work of becoming. Inside, the lamp burned. Sera hummed. Doss counted apples that refused to stay counted.

And in the deepening quiet of the corner where the shadows pooled, Jude could have sworn — though he would deny it under oath, and had, once, to Maren, who had smiled and let it go — that something shifted its weight, patient and unhurried, and waited, with the courtesy of the dead, to be heard.

He reached for a fresh piece of bone, and began.
