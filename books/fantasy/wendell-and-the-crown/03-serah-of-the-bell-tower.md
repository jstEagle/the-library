## 3. Serah of the Bell Tower

The bells had been wrong for three days before anyone else noticed.

Serah Voss noticed because she had spent eleven years learning their moods the way other people learned the moods of cats or lovers — by proximity, by patience, by being struck across the knuckles often enough to develop a feel for it. The great bell, Mercy, was supposed to speak in E. She knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat. But for three days, when she pulled the rope at dawn, Mercy had answered in something a quarter-tone flat, and underneath the note there was a second sound, faint as a whisper through a wall, that no bell in the tower should have been able to make.

She had checked the clapper. She had checked the headstock. She had climbed into the belfry with a lantern and a wrench and a very unprofessional vocabulary and found nothing amiss at all, which was somehow worse than finding something amiss. Bells went out of tune. That was weather, that was time, that was the ordinary slow argument between bronze and gravity. Bells did not go out of tune *in one direction only*, as though they were leaning toward something.

And bells certainly did not whisper.

On the third evening she stood alone in the ringing chamber with her hand resting on Mercy's rope, not pulling it, just holding it, the way you might hold the hand of someone who was talking in their sleep.

"Say it again," she said quietly. "Whatever it is you're saying. Say it slower."

The tower was silent. The city below was doing its evening business — cart wheels, gull cries, someone two streets over burning onions, which meant the Widow Anselm's boarding house was serving stew and therefore Serah could expect an invitation if she played her cards right. Ordinary sounds. Good sounds. She held on to them like stones in a river.

But under all of it, under the gulls and the carts and the onions, she heard it again: *down, down, down.* Not in words, exactly. More like the memory of words, the way you remember warmth after leaving a fire. A voice in the bronze, patient and enormous and sad, saying the same thing it had been saying since the night she'd woken on the mountain road with dirt under her fingernails and four months missing from her life.

*Down.*

Serah let go of the rope and pressed both palms against her ears, which any first-year ringer would tell you does nothing against a sound coming from inside your own skull.

"I'm not going back," she said out loud, to the bell, to the voice, to whatever was listening. "Do you hear me? I got out. Whatever bargain was struck, whoever struck it, I'm not party to it anymore."

Mercy hummed, very softly, the way a struck glass hums. It sounded almost sorry.

That was the worst part. It sounded *sorry*.

* * *

She had left Ashfall in the spring with a ringer's satchel, a letter of introduction from the Guild of Impossible Things — Rafael had pulled strings, red-faced and stammering about how it wasn't strings, it was simply procedure, and he'd tripped over the guild seal twice presenting it — and a commission: the mountain parishes wanted their carillon inspected, and the Guild, ever short-handed, ever strange, had agreed to send its most junior qualified assessor.

Rafael had walked her to the east gate carrying her bag even though she'd told him twice she was stronger than she looked. He'd stood there in his ledger-keeper's gray with ink on his fingers and his collar crooked, and he'd said, "Four months. That's — I've calculated it. One hundred and twenty-two days. Give or take. I did give or take. There's a margin of error."

"You calculated my trip," Serah said.

"I calculate things," Rafael said, with dignity. "It's what I'm for."

"And the margin of error?"

"Three days," he admitted. "I didn't want to seem presumptuous."

She had kissed him then, on the cheek, because she was a coward about some things and brave about others and had never worked out which category that fell into. And he had gone pink to the ears and said, "One hundred and nineteen days, then," and she had laughed all the way up the mountain road.

That had been the last easy day for a long while.

The mountain parishes were lovely. The work was honest. And on the nineteenth day, in the village of Cold Bracken, where the church bell was older than the parish and hung in a tower built around it rather than the other way round, Serah had swung the little bell for vespers and heard it answer in a voice that was not its own.

Not broken. Not cracked. *Answering.* As though somewhere far beneath the floor of the world, something vast had heard the small bronze voice calling down and down through stone and root and dark water, and had called back.

She had told herself it was echo. Echoes in mountains are strange; everyone says so. She had finished the inspection, written her report in a steady hand, and moved on to the next parish, where the same thing happened, except this time the bell had also rung once, by itself, at midnight, when no one was near it.

By the fortieth day she was sleeping with wax in her ears and waking anyway, dreaming of roots. Not trees' roots — older, thicker, paler, roots like the ribs of something buried alive, spreading down through the dark under the world, and at the center of them a weight, a presence, a *patient enormous sad thing*, turning over slowly in its sleep. In the dreams she always stood very close to it. In the dreams she was never afraid, and that frightened her more than anything, because fear is the body's way of telling you where the edges are, and in those dreams she had no edges at all.

On the sixty-third day she stopped writing reports. On the seventy-first she stopped sleeping in inns. On the eighty-eighth she woke on a mountainside she did not remember climbing, with dirt under her nails, a bruise on her hip shaped like a handprint, and a conviction sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone:

*They know where I am. They know what I heard. And something in me has already said yes to them, and I don't remember when.*

So she ran. She was not proud of it. She told herself it was strategy — go home, warn the Guild, get ahead of it — but on the bad nights, huddled under hedgerows with her satchel for a pillow, she admitted the truth: she ran the way anything runs from a trap it can feel closing. Not bravely. Just fast.

It took her fifty-one days to reach Ashfall. She knew because she counted, the way she knew Rafael would have counted, and the thought of him counting made her walk faster on the last day, until she was nearly running again, but running toward instead of away, which is a different animal entirely.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a narrow building wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold, according to its sign, CORRECT HOOKS, and Serah had never once managed to learn what a correct hook was or how it differed from the incorrect kind. The guild's own sign read ESTABLISHED BY ROYAL CHARTER, MOSTLY, which she had always found reassuring in its honesty.

She arrived at dusk, dusty, smelling of hedgerow, and stood in the street looking up at the lit window on the third floor where a lamp burned late every night, because a certain junior ledger-keeper believed that ledgers deserved vigilance the way bells deserved ringing.

Her heart did something complicated. She let it.

The door was unlocked — it usually was; the guild had a theory about locks that Serah had never gotten a straight answer on — and she climbed the stairs past the smell of tallow and old paper and knocked on the third-floor door before she lost her nerve.

Footsteps. A pause. A small, strangled sound that she recognized, with a rush of tenderness, as Rafael Brightwater attempting to compose himself and failing.

He opened the door holding a ledger like a shield and wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed a greeting for one hundred and twenty-two days and now cannot remember a single word of it.

"You're early," he said finally. "Or late. Depending on the margin of error."

"Hello, Rafael."

"You're — " He looked at her. Really looked, the way he looked at entries in a book, line by line, checking for discrepancies. And Serah watched the discrepancy register: the dust, the thinness, the shadows under her eyes, the way she flinched when a cart rattled past outside. His face did something careful. "You're fifty-one days," he said. "I counted too. I didn't want to say so."

"No," she said, "you wouldn't."

"Come in. Sit down. There's tea, it's terrible, Odell buys it from a man who claims it came off a ship that sank, which raises questions we've agreed not to ask." He was talking too fast and knew it and couldn't stop, which was how Rafael loved people: at speed, with footnotes. "How were the parishes? Was the work — did you find — "

"Rafael." She set down her satchel. "Before anything else. Before tea, before the margin of error, before you ask me one more gentle question in that voice you use when you're worried but trying not to show the arithmetic of it." She took a breath. "Something is wrong with the bells. All of them. All the way up the mountain range. And it isn't the bells."

He sat down slowly, the ledger still in his hands, and she saw the moment the ledger-keeper in him took over from the boy — saw him set aside the reunion, carefully, the way you set aside a letter you'll open later, and turn to the emergency. She loved him for it and resented it in equal measure, and understood that these were the same feeling wearing different coats.

"Tell me everything," he said. "In order. Dates if you have them."

"I have dates," Serah said. "I'm a ringer. We count everything. It's what I'm for."

* * *

She told him all of it, and he wrote it down, because of course he did, in a fresh notebook he kept for exactly this kind of thing, and watching him write her nightmare down in his neat clerk's hand — *roots, pale, rib-like; subject reports absence of edges* — was one of the stranger experiences of her life, somewhere between being cared for and being catalogued.

When she reached the part about waking on the mountainside with the bruise, his pen stopped.

"A handprint," he said.

"Small hands," she said. "Smaller than mine. Child-sized, maybe, or — " she gestured vaguely, " — or something that has hands the way a candle has a flame. An approximation. Something wearing the idea of a hand."

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, in a completely different voice, flat and careful, "Serah. Three days ago an entry changed in one of our oldest books. A prayer marked unanswered for two hundred years now reads answered. Master Vane says it's a transcription error. I've checked the transcription. It isn't."

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way the ringing chamber tilted when Mercy swung true.

"What prayer?" she asked.

"It doesn't say. Old entries don't. They're marked by symbol — a circle for granted, a slash for refused, a blank for unanswered. This one was blank for two centuries. Now it's a circle." He turned the notebook toward her, and there it was, copied in his precise hand: a date, a reference number, and two symbols, blank and circle, side by side like a before-and-after.

Serah stared at it and felt the dream rise in her again, the pale roots, the patient weight, and this time she put edges on herself deliberately, dug her nails into her palm, because she needed her edges now.

"The court beneath the mountain," she said.

Rafael looked up sharply. "What do you know about the court beneath the mountain?"

"Nothing. Everything. Nothing useful." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "There was a woman in Cold Bracken. Old, half-blind, sold charms at the market that mostly worked, which in my experience means she was honest about being dishonest. When I asked her about the bell — I shouldn't have asked, I know that now, you don't ask about bells, you ring them — she went white and gripped my wrist and said, 'Girl, if you can hear it answering, you're already in its book.' And then she told me a story her grandmother told her, about the old days, before the Guild, when the deep places had courts and the courts had kings and every prayer was a contract and every contract was collected." She met his eyes. "She said the kings slept. She said somebody had written them to sleep. And she said—" Serah's voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "She said the last thing they hear, on their way down, is bells. Because bells carry. Down through stone. Down through root. And something down there has spent two hundred years listening to us ring, Rafael, and three days ago — "

"— three days ago it started answering," Rafael finished.

Neither of them spoke. Outside, the city went on being a city: the chandler closing up, the hook-seller arguing with a customer about whether a hook could be correct, the Widow Anselm's stew smoke drifting sweetly past the window. Ordinary sounds. Serah held on to them.

Then Rafael stood, crossed to the shelf where the guild's oldest books lived behind a curtain of dust cloth, and stood there with his hand on the cloth without pulling it back.

"There's one book," he said, "that predates the charter. Predates the guild's whole filing system. We call it the First Ledger. Nobody's entered anything in it in living memory. It's not even — officially it doesn't exist. Odell keeps it locked and pretends it's a misprint in the inventory." His jaw worked. "Two hundred years of prayers marked unanswered. If the blanks are turning to circles—"

"Burn it," Serah said.

He turned. "What?"

"Burn it. Tonight. Don't file a report, don't convene the council, don't check the transcription a fourth time — burn it, and scatter the ash in running water if you can manage it, and if anyone asks, tell them I knocked it into the stove, I'll take the blame, I'm new back, nobody expects better of me." She was on her feet now too, and her voice shook, and she let it. "Rafael, I dreamed of that place for eighty-eight nights. I felt it *notice* me. Whatever is written in that book, it isn't history anymore. It's a list, and something is reading down it, and every name it reaches—"

"It's a guild record," Rafael said, but gently, the way you correct a friend. "It's paper and iron gall ink. Burning records doesn't unmake debts, Serah, it just unmakes the evidence. Believe me, I've thought about it. Every clerk thinks about it. The debt survives the document. That's practically the first law of the trade."

"Then what does unmake a debt?"

And Rafael Brightwater, junior ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, opened his mouth to answer — she watched him do it, watched the confidence assemble itself — and then closed it again, because he was honest, that was the whole trouble with him, honest down to the marrow, and the honest answer was *nothing that I know of*, and they both heard it even though he didn't say it.

"I'll look into it," he said at last. "Properly. Tomorrow. Tonight I'll — " he glanced at the curtained shelf, and she saw real fear move through him, quick as a fish under ice, " — tonight I'll sit with it. Read it. Know what we're dealing with before we feed it to a stove. You don't burn a book you haven't read. That's not caution, that's — it's how you end up burning the wrong book."

"Rafael—"

"One night," he said. "Give me one night with it. Then we decide together. Both of us. I've done the sums, Serah. One hundred and twenty-two days apart, and I'm not doing the next sum alone."

And there it was, the whole of him, in one sentence: the clerk and the boy, the footnotes and the heart, offering her partnership as naturally as other men offered flowers. Serah felt her anger go out of her like air from a bellows, and what remained underneath was tiredness and love and terror in roughly equal parts.

"One night," she said. "And you sleep. Actually sleep. If that thing notices you the way it noticed me, it will notice a tired man faster than a rested one. Trust me on the mechanics of being noticed."

"I trust you on everything," Rafael said, and went pink immediately, as though the sentence had escaped without authorization. "That is — statistically speaking. Based on available data."

"Available data," Serah repeated, and laughed despite everything, really laughed, and saw him relax half an inch, and thought: *this. Whatever comes down out of the mountain, hold on to this.*

* * *

They shared the terrible sunken-ship tea. He showed her the guild's latest acquisitions — a bottle of grief traded in by a fisherman who'd decided he'd carried it long enough, a jar of borrowed luck due back Thursday — and she told him about the parishes, the good parts, the ones she'd been saving up on the road like coins: the choir in Cold Bracken that sang flat on purpose because the founder had hated harmony; the innkeeper who paid his tab in stories. He listened the way he did everything, completely, taking no notes at all, which was how she knew it mattered to him.

Later, wrapped in a blanket on the guild's battered couch while he sat at his desk with a single lamp, she drifted at the edge of sleep and heard pages turning, slow and careful, and underneath the page-turning, faint as a whisper through a wall, she heard it again.

*Down, down, down.*

Not from the bells this time. From the book.

She opened her eyes. Across the room, Rafael had gone very still, one hand flat on the open First Ledger, his face in the lamplight pale and puzzled, like a man listening to a sound he can't locate.

"Rafael," she said quietly. "What does it say?"

He didn't answer for a long moment. When he did, his voice was strange and small.

"It's an entry," he said. "Dated tomorrow."
