## 5. The First Collection

There is a particular smell to old paper that young men never notice and old men cannot forget. It is the smell of patience. Of things set down carefully, in the correct order, by hands that believed the order would hold.

I had been smelling it for fifty-one years, and on the morning the Collectors came, I noticed for the first time that it had changed. Underneath the patience there was something else now — something like smoke, or like the memory of smoke. The ledgers were afraid. I want you to understand how absurd that sentence sounds even to me, a man who has spent his life insisting that paper is paper and ink is ink and the Guild of Impossible Things is, whatever its charter claims, merely an office with poor lighting and worse tea.

But the ledgers were afraid. And so, as it turned out, was I.

* * *

Let me tell you about my morning, because mornings are what I have instead of children, and I am fond of them in the way one is fond of anything one has raised oneself.

I rose at half past five, as always. I boiled water for tea that Rafael would later describe, with the tactless precision of youth, as "essentially warm disappointment." I put on the grey coat with the ink-stained cuffs that Serah once mended for me three years ago, before she left, and which I have not let anyone touch since, because her stitching is better than mine and because — well. Because of other reasons I did not examine closely at the time and examined rather too closely now.

Then I went down to the Hall of Records to do what I had done every morning since Odell Vane was a title and not yet a man: I checked the oldest ledger. The First Book. The one nobody was supposed to open anymore, least of all me.

And there it was again. Another entry, rewritten overnight.

*Prayer of Maren Ostler, widow, for the safety of her sons at sea. Status: unanswered.* That was what it had said yesterday. I knew because I had copied it out myself, twice, in my own hand, to be sure I wasn't mad.

This morning it read: *Status: answered. Both sons returned. Payment rendered: forty years of Maren's sleep, collected in full.*

Forty years of a dead woman's sleep. Collected. As though sleep were coin in a purse someone had finally come to empty.

I stood there in the cold hall with my tea going lukewarm in my hand and felt something I had not felt since I was a boy in the flood year: the specific vertigo of standing on ground that has decided, quietly, without consulting you, to become water.

"Master Vane?"

Rafael. Of course it was Rafael. The boy kept hours like a lark and a conscience like a church bell, and both of them rang too early for my comfort. He came down the aisle between the shelves with his arms full of the day's intake — bottled grief, two jars; borrowed luck, returned late with interest; somebody's grandmother's recipe for forgetting, which we were legally obliged to store and morally obliged to lose — and he stopped when he saw my face.

"You've seen it," he said.

"I've seen it."

"It's not just the one anymore." He set the jars down on the reading table with more care than he usually spent on anything, including himself. "The third shelf. All of it. Every prayer marked unanswered from the founding onward — they're changing. I counted forty-one changed entries before dawn. Master Vane, prayers don't *answer themselves*. Something is answering them. Something is—"

"Collecting," I said.

He looked at me. He has his mother's eyes, whoever his mother was; he has never told me and I have never asked, because there are courtesies even between people who lie to each other daily. "You know what's happening," he said. "Don't you. You've known since the first entry changed."

Now, here is where I should tell you that I lied to him. I am, after all, good at it. Fifty-one years of keeping a guild's secrets will make a liar of anyone with a spine soft enough to stay. And I opened my mouth to do it — to say something soothing and hollow about transcription errors and clerical enthusiasm — and what came out instead was:

"Sit down, Rafael."

Because I was tired. That's the whole confession, if you want it. I was seventy-three years old and tired, and the truth had gotten heavy, and for once I wanted to set it down somewhere that wasn't my own chest.

* * *

I told him most of it. Not all. Never all — I am not a saint, I am a bookkeeper — but most.

I told him that the Guild of Impossible Things was founded on a loan. That the power to trade in grief and luck and memory and prayer — the power that made us rich and strange and necessary — was never ours. It was lent. By the old gods, the deep ones, the ones beneath the mountain and under the lake and behind the wind, back when the world was younger and gods still answered letters. They lent it on terms. And the terms included a clause that every founder signed without reading properly, because founders are just bookkeepers who got ambitious, and ambition makes terrible readers.

*Repayable upon waking.*

"They were asleep," I said. "For six hundred years. We built a guild in the shadow of a sleeping creditor and told ourselves the shadow was ours."

Rafael was very quiet. Then: "Odell. What happens when a debt this size comes due?"

I looked at the First Book, lying open on the table, its pages shivering faintly in air that was not moving.

"We find out," I said.

* * *

They came at dusk. Of course they did. If you are going to haunt a place, you may as well have a sense of theater about it.

I heard them before I saw them — a sound like pages turning, coming from the street, from everywhere at once. Rafael was in the records room arguing with Councilor Hesk about whether to alert the city wardens (Hesk wanted to negotiate; the council always wants to negotiate; negotiation is what cowards call the thing they do while hoping the problem eats someone else first). I was alone in my office with the First Book, doing the thing I had been avoiding for three days: reading the founding contract all the way through to the signatures.

Mine was the last one. Renewed eleven years ago, when the guild's charter came up for re-sealing. I had signed it in my neat, careful, patient hand, the hand of a man who believed the order would hold.

Under my signature, in ink that had not been there eleven years ago, in ink that looked freshly wet, someone had written:

*Received and noted. Balance outstanding.*

The door did not open. Doors are such a formality, for some visitors.

They simply were there — three figures standing in my office among my shelves and my sad little stove and my portrait of the first Master, whom I had always suspected of being a fraud and now knew to be one. They were made of ash and ledger-paper. I mean that literally, and I have earned the right to be literal. Their bodies were grey ash packed tight as clay, and their clothing — their robes, their collars, their long clerk's hoods — was paper, page after page of it, covered in fine script, and the script was moving. Updating. Every debt in the world, worn as a uniform.

The tallest one held a book. Not our book. A black one, bound in something that had been alive once and had opinions about it.

"Odell Vane," it said. Its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down on wet paper. "Keeper of record. Signatory of renewal."

"I am," I said. My voice held. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, since no one else will: my voice held.

"The account of the Guild of Impossible Things is called. Balance due upon waking. You are signatory of the most recent instrument."

"I'm aware of the terms," I said. "I should like to discuss a restructuring."

Rafael appeared in the doorway then. I saw him over the ash-creature's shoulder, and I saw his face do the thing young faces do — the quick slide from confusion to understanding to horror, horror arriving last because hope always walks ahead of it and takes its time dying.

"Restructuring," the Collector repeated. Paper rustled. Somewhere inside it, a page turned. "The debtor requests restructuring."

"If the guild's assets were liquidated—" I began, because I am a bookkeeper to my marrow, and faced with the end of everything I reached for the only tool I have ever trusted, which is arithmetic.

"The guild's assets," said the Collector, "include one soul, pledged as surety against renewal. Down payment accepted."

"No," said Rafael, from the doorway. "No, wait — the contract was the guild's debt, the guild can pay, there are assets, there's the vault, there's—"

"The vault holds borrowed goods," said the Collector, turning its hood toward him with the unhurried courtesy of a clerk who has all eternity and knows it. "Borrowed goods return to lenders. Only the pledge is ours."

And it opened the black book.

I will try to describe what happened next, though description fails the way a net fails around water. The Collector did not touch me. It wrote. It dipped a pen that was a splinter of bone into ink that was not ink, and it wrote my name in the black book, and as the pen moved, I felt myself being *entered*. Filed. There is a sensation — I imagine — of being folded. Not painfully. That was the worst part. It didn't hurt at all. It was like being put away neatly by someone who loved tidiness more than they loved you.

"Odell," Rafael said, and he was moving, actually lunging across the room, my foolish brave boy, and the second Collector simply extended one ash-grey arm and he struck it and rebounded as though it were the side of the world. He hit the floor. He got up. He hit it again. It did not even turn its head. You cannot fight a filing system by hitting it, though I confess the sight of him trying warmed me in a way I intend to take with me wherever souls go.

"Rafael," I said. "Stop. Listen to me. Stop."

He stopped. He was crying without any of the dignity-destroying noise of crying, which is somehow worse to watch, and he was looking at me the way I had once looked at my own master, sixty years ago, in this same room, on the night I learned what the guild really was.

"Listen," I said. "The First Book. The original contract. Everything comes from it — every renewal, every copy, every debt. Understand? Nothing stands without the root."

"Odell—"

"*Listen.*" My voice cracked on it, and I found I didn't mind. "Find the root. And Rafael — the girl. Serah. She knew. Before any of this started, she knew. Ask her why."

The pen finished its last stroke.

The world went quiet in a way I had never experienced, because I had never before been present for the removal of myself from it. The office remained — the stove, the portrait, the tea gone cold in its cup, the boy on his knees among the spilled bottled grief, one jar broken, sorrow spreading across the floorboards in a thin silver puddle that made everyone in the room think, briefly, of their mothers.

And I remained too. But differently. I stood beside my own body — my body, which stayed in the chair, breathing, blinking, its hands resting on the desk like a man pausing between columns — and I watched the Collectors fold inward, page by page, ash by ash, until they were nothing but a drift of grey and a single loose leaf settling to the floor.

On the leaf, in fresh ink, in a hand disturbingly like my own:

*Down payment received. Balance collection to follow. Do not attempt flight. Debtors who flee accrue interest.*

* * *

Here is what it is like, being what I am now. I do not recommend it, though I note the recommendation is somewhat beside the point.

I could not leave the room. I tried. I walked to the doorway and the doorway declined to have me. I am filed, and filed things stay where they are put until the account settles. So I stood in my own office, insubstantial as morning fog, and I watched Rafael Brightwater gather himself off the floor, and I watched him do the hardest thing I ever saw a person do.

He went to my body. He took its hand — my hand — and pressed it, and said my name twice, and when the body blinked and breathed and gave him nothing back, not a flicker, not a spark, he closed his eyes and mastered himself the way I taught him to master a trembling hand before signing something important. Breath. Stillness. Then work.

He worked through the whole night. I watched. It is the only thing I can do now, and I discover I am helpless to stop doing it, which I suspect is the point. Punishment, properly designed, is just attention with nowhere to put itself.

He searched the archives. He pulled the sealed contracts, the ones under triple lock, and I watched him break the locks with a poker from the stove because he had forgotten the keys and could not wait. He spread the founding documents across the floor of the Hall of Records in the lamplight, kneeling among six hundred years of paper like a man kneeling among bones, and he read, and read, and read.

Toward dawn he found the clause. I saw him find it — saw him go absolutely still, the way water goes still just before it freezes. His lips moved over the words. *Repayable upon waking. Surety: the signatory's soul. Full settlement: upon surrender of the original instrument.*

The original instrument. The First Book. Or — and here his eyes lifted slowly from the page to the far wall, to the old relief carved above the archive door that every keeper for six centuries had taken for decoration — the thing the First Book was copied from.

The root.

"He'll go down," I said, to no one, to the room, to the dark. My voice made no sound at all. Filed things don't get to warn anybody. "That fool, that brave, impossible fool. He'll go down after it."

Rafael stood up in the grey light, holding the founding contract against his chest like a shield or a wound, and he looked — I will remember this as long as remembering lasts — he looked exactly like a man deciding.

Below the guild, below the city, below the roots of the mountain, something ancient stirred in its sleep of chains, and turned its attention, gently and terribly, toward the sound of a bell that had begun to ring — though there was no bell tower within a hundred miles, and no ringer living who could have rung it.

Except one.

Serah was coming home.
