## 6. Down Is a Direction

The council chamber of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled of beeswax, old paper, and the particular staleness of men who had made their careers out of not deciding anything.

Rafael stood at the long table's foot with Odell Vane's empty body in his thoughts and the master ledger's absence like a stone in his chest. Around him sat the seven senior members of the guild — or rather, six sat and one hovered, because Elder Pruneworth had never trusted chairs since an incident involving an enchanted stool in the Year of the Wet Ledger, which nobody ever explained to junior staff.

"The matter before us," said Elder Casseline Hobb, rapping her gavel on a soundboard that muffled it into a polite *thup*, "is the question of Master Vane's condition. And the related question of what, precisely, we intend to do about the court beneath the mountain."

"We intend," said Elder Marsh, a round man whose opinions were as soft as his waistcoat, "to do nothing rash."

"Nothing rash," agreed Elder Fennick.

"Nothing at all, ideally," said Pruneworth, from the air near his chair.

Rafael counted to ten. It did not help. He counted to twenty. That helped only marginally, and mostly because it gave him time to notice that Elder Hobb was watching him over her spectacles with something that might have been sympathy, or might have been the look of a woman calculating how much trouble one junior ledger-keeper could possibly be.

The answer, Rafael suspected, was a great deal.

"Master Vane is not *ill*," he said. "He is not resting. His soul has been taken as payment on a debt this guild signed four hundred years ago, and every hour we sit here polishing our quills, he breathes a little more emptily. I have read the ledgers. I have read all of them." This was true; insomnia and lowly ambition made a thorough reader of a man. "The entries are still changing. Three more prayers flipped from unanswered to answered overnight. The gods are awake, Elders. They are collecting. And they started with us."

A murmur went around the table like wind through dry reeds.

"Young Brightwater," said Marsh soothingly, "your devotion to your master does you credit. But the court beneath the mountain is a matter for diplomacy. We shall send envoys. A formal petition. Perhaps a gift basket."

"A gift *basket*," Rafael repeated.

"Bottled honey," said Marsh. "The good kind. Gods are known to appreciate sweetness."

"With respect, Elder, the god beneath the mountain just took a man's soul as a down payment. I don't think it wants honey. I think it wants what it is owed, and I think it will keep taking until someone finds the courage to say *enough*."

"And what would you have us do?" asked Fennick, spreading his hands. "March down there? With what army? The Guild of Impossible Things hasn't fought anything since the Affair of the Sentient Umbrellas, and we lost that."

"We lost that because the umbrellas were *right*," muttered Pruneworth.

Hobb raised a hand, and the room quieted. She looked at Rafael for a long moment — long enough that he felt like an entry being audited.

"What do you propose?" she asked.

"I propose we go down there and take Master Vane back."

"Steal from a god."

"Reclaim," said Rafael. "There's a difference. It's in the ledgers. Everything is in the ledgers — that's the whole point of them. If the debt is real, then so are its terms, and if the terms are real, then somewhere in those books is a way through them. Debts can be disputed. Debts can be offset. Debts—" he heard himself, and hated how much he sounded like Odell, "—debts can be *renegotiated*. But only if we hold the book."

Silence. Then Marsh cleared his throat and delivered the verdict with the gentle finality of a man tucking in a child:

"The council votes to negotiate. Envoys, petition, gift basket. Motion carried?"

Six hands rose. Pruneworth abstained, on principle, having once been burned by a motion.

"Motion carried," said Hobb. Her eyes found Rafael's again, and held them. "And Brightwater — you will remain in the city. You are grieving, you are angry, and you are precisely the sort of young man who does something brave and stupid and irreversible. Am I understood?"

*Understood* was not the word Rafael would have chosen. *Underestimated*, perhaps. Or *correct*.

"Perfectly, Elder," he said.

* * *

He stole the master ledger at half past two in the morning, which was, in his professional opinion, the only honest hour of the day.

It was almost insultingly easy. The guild's security consisted of a lock designed by a man who believed all thieves were deterred by riddles (they were not; they were deterred by locks), a guard named Tullins who slept like the dead and snored like the dying, and the universal assumption that no junior ledger-keeper would dare. Four hundred years of accumulated power, and it had never once occurred to anyone that the most dangerous person in the building might be the one who shelved the files.

Rafael took the ledger from its case in the Vault of First Accounts, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tucked it under his arm like a loaf of bread. Then, because he was Rafael Brightwater and not a proper thief, he left a note.

*I have taken the master ledger to settle Master Vane's account in person. I am sorry. I will bring it back if I can. — R.B.*

Then, after a moment's thought, he added: *P.S. The gift basket idea is genuinely terrible.*

He was three streets away when he admitted to himself that he had no plan beyond *down*. Down was a direction. Down, according to the oldest maps in the archive — maps that predated the guild, predated the city, predated several geological features — was where the court lived, beneath the roots of the Grayfell Mountains, nine days' travel north. Nine days alone, carrying a book that could forge debts as easily as record them, walking into the hall of a thing that ate souls the way he ate breakfast rolls.

He was twenty-three years old. He had never been further than the salt marshes. He owned one coat, and it had a burn hole shaped like a small country.

"Well," he said to the empty street, "down is a direction."

"That," said a voice behind him, "is the worst battle cry I have ever heard."

Serah stepped out of the shadow of the chandler's awning, hood up, travel cloak already dusty, a pack on her shoulders that looked packed by someone who knew exactly what packing meant. In the lamplight her face was thinner than he remembered, and there were new lines around her eyes — but her eyes themselves were the same, dark and level and entirely unimpressed by him, which was, historically, the thing he loved best about them.

"Serah." His heart performed a maneuver that would have gotten a lesser organ dismissed from service. "You're— I thought you'd gone. You *did* go. Months ago. Without—"

"Yes," she said. "I know. Walk with me. Standing in doorways is how people get arrested."

They walked. The city slept around them, and above the rooftops the stars seemed, to Rafael's newly educated eye, slightly too attentive.

"I heard about Odell," Serah said quietly. "The whole city heard. The bells rang wrong this morning — off-key, all of them, like they were mourning. They do that now. They've started doing things." She glanced at him. "You're going after him. To the mountain."

"How did you—"

"Because you're carrying a very large book badly disguised as bread, and because you're you." She stopped at the corner where the North Road began, and turned to face him. "You can't come with me, Serah. It's dangerous. It's — I don't even know what it is, exactly, except old and hungry and awake. I'm doing this so nobody else has to."

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No, you're not doing it so nobody else has to. You're doing it because you're angry and guilty and you think suffering alone is the same thing as being noble. It isn't. It's just suffering, with worse company." She adjusted her pack strap. "And you're not going alone, because you'd die in the first tollhouse. You'd try to pay a bridge troll with a compliment."

"That happened *once*," Rafael said, "and the troll found it charming."

"The troll told everyone. You're a cautionary tale in three villages." She looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw in his face made her shoulders drop a fraction — the surrender of someone who had been holding a secret up like a roof beam and could no longer bear the weight. "Rafael. There's something you need to know before we go another step. And you need to know it now, in the dark, where you can't see my face properly, because I can't say it twice."

His stomach performed the maneuver this time.

"When I left," Serah said, "I wasn't visiting my aunt in Marrowdale. My aunt in Marrowdale is a fiction I invented because you ask kind questions and I am a coward." She drew a breath. "I served the court beneath the mountain. For six years. As an acolyte. A priestess-in-training, before I ran."

The street was very quiet. Somewhere a cat knocked over a bottle, considered the consequences, and fled.

"Served it," Rafael said carefully. "How? What does — what does serving it *mean*?"

"It means I rang bells for it. It means I learned its names, the true ones, the ones that make your teeth ache. It means I watched petitioners trade ten years of their lives for a son's fever to break, and I watched the fever break, and I watched the son grow up and forget his mother's face because the years came out of her *memories*, one by one, and everyone smiled because the bargain was kept." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "It means I owe it six years of service I never finished paying, and I escaped by faking my death, and the only reason it hasn't collected yet is that dead women make poor collateral. Until they walk back through its gates. Which is what we'd be doing."

Rafael stood very still. The ledger under his arm felt suddenly heavier, as though it had heard everything and was taking notes.

"You could have told me," he said finally. "Months ago. Years."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you look at me like I'm a person who rings bells," she said, "and I was afraid that if you knew what I was, you'd start looking at me like an entry in a book." She met his eyes. "So. Now you know. Your mentor's soul is held by the god I used to pray to, and the woman you — the woman whose company you tolerate is its runaway priestess, and every step toward that mountain makes both of those facts more dangerous. You should turn me away. Any sensible person would."

Rafael thought about it. He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how — turning the thing over, looking for the flaw, the catch, the fine print. Six years. Bells and names and bargains. A faked death. A lie between them like a splinter working toward the bone.

And underneath all of it, steady as a bell note: *she came back*. She had run from a god, and when she heard he was walking toward one, she had put on her boots and come back.

"Here's my problem," he said at last. "I'm a ledger-keeper. It's my whole soul, such as it is. And a ledger-keeper knows that a debt honestly declared is worth more than a clean page. You owed me the truth. You've paid it. Late, with penalties, but paid." He shifted the ledger to his other arm. "So we go together. But Serah — no more entries hidden from the record. Whatever else you're carrying, I get to read it before we reach the gates. Agreed?"

Something moved across her face — relief, and fear, and something warmer than either.

"Agreed," she said. "And Rafael? One more thing you should know before we take a single step north."

"Which is?"

She nodded at the ledger under his arm, and her voice dropped low, the way people speak in churches and near sleeping animals.

"The last time I saw the Deep King's hall, there was a book on its altar bigger than that one, older than yours, chained shut with seven locks. And every petitioner who passed it whispered the same warning, in the King's own voice, like a prayer running backward:" 

She looked up the dark road, toward the mountains he could not yet see.

"*Whoever holds the ledger holds all debts. And all debts hold him.*"

Rafael tightened his grip on the book, told himself firmly that it was only leather and paper, and stepped onto the North Road beside her.

Down, it turned out, was also forward.
