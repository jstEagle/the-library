## 7. The Road of Small Mercies

*Rafael*

The road out of the city smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet, which Rafael would later understand was the first warning.

He had never traveled this far from the Guildhall in his life. Junior ledger-keepers were not encouraged to wander; wandering led to questions, and questions led to the archives, and the archives were where he had found the founding contract that had ruined his comfortable, resentful little existence. Now here he was on the King's Road with a stolen master ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart, a pack containing two shirts, a tin of sardines, and a penknife, and Serah walking beside him with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves as if she were cold, though it was high summer.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you walk along composing an argument you'll never say out loud. Your eyebrows do all the talking."

"My eyebrows are very articulate," Rafael said. "It's how I got promoted to junior ledger-keeper. The senior keeper said I had the most expressive brows in the counting room."

Serah laughed — a short, startled sound, as if she'd forgotten laughing was available to her — and then pressed her lips together again, and Rafael's chest did something complicated and painful. Three months she'd been gone. Three months of letters returned unopened, of the bell tower silent, of everyone saying *she went to family in the provinces* in the tone people used when they meant *don't ask.* And now she walked beside him toward the mountain whose roots she dreamed about, and every laugh she gave him felt like a coin slipped under a door.

The first village was called Marrow's End, which Rafael thought was a terrible name for a place until he saw it, at which point he thought it was honest advertising.

It sat in a fold of hills where the road dipped toward a river, and even from a distance he could see the water was wrong. Too wide. Too still. It lay over the fields like spilled ink, and the village itself stood knee-deep in it, houses listing, doors swollen shut, chickens perched on fence posts with the resigned expressions of chickens who had been perching for some time.

"They prayed for rain," Serah said quietly.

"How do you know?"

"Because I can hear it." She touched her ear, the way she always did now, as if listening to something just behind the world. "There's a bell in the village square. Bronze. It's been ringing since the rain started. Nobody's ringing it. It rings because they asked for rain, and the asking is still... open. Unfinished. Like a note held too long."

They waded in to their shins. A woman stood in her doorway wringing out a blanket with the mechanical patience of someone who had stopped expecting anything to improve. When she saw them she called out, "If you've come from the relief office, tell them we need boats, not bread!"

"We're not from any office," Rafael said. "What happened?"

"What happened?" The woman gave a laugh with no floor under it. "Drought, three years running. Wells down to mud. So old Tam Wicker — may he rest, though I doubt he does — he got the whole village together and we prayed. Properly prayed, mind, not the usual mumble. We promised anything. And then—" she gestured at the drowned world around her with the dripping blanket— "it rained."

"For how long?"

"Eleven days." She said it flatly. "It's still raining up in the hills. The river keeps coming. And when we pray for it to stop, nothing answers, because—" she smiled, and it was the worst smile Rafael had ever seen— "we already spent our asking. One prayer each. That was the bargain. Nobody told us we only got one."

Rafael's hand drifted to the ledger beneath his coat without his permission. He could feel it there, warm as a sleeping animal. Later, alone, he opened it and found the village's entry: *Marrow's End — petition granted — rain, abundant — consideration received: one prayer's worth of faith, per soul, in perpetuity.* In perpetuity. They had sold not their rain but their future prayers, every one, forever, and the god had taken payment in advance and delivered eleven days of weather.

"That's not a bargain," he said aloud to no one. "That's a loan shark."

"Careful," Serah murmured. "Words travel downhill here."

* * *

*Serah*

Serah had forgotten how loud the world was when you could hear its bells.

In the court beneath the mountain there had been no bells, only the deep slow pulse of the mountain's own blood, and she had told herself that silence was peace. But out here every village had its bronze voice, and every bronze voice was speaking, and most of what they said made her want to sit down in the road and weep.

At Marrow's End she had helped move furniture to upper floors and said comforting things she didn't believe, and Rafael had done something with the ledger — she hadn't asked what; she wasn't sure she wanted to know — that got the woman's grandmother's goat back from a neighbor who'd claimed it during the flood. Small mercies. The road's name was proving accurate in ways she resented.

Two days on, they came to a village called Ashen Well, and here the bell didn't ring at all, and somehow that was worse.

The village was beautiful. That was the first wrongness — whitewashed cottages, roses climbing trellises, a green so neat it looked combed. People worked in gardens and nodded pleasantly as travelers passed. An old man waved from a chair outside the smithy. Everything was lovely and calm as a painting of a village rather than a village.

Then Serah noticed that nobody remembered her name after she'd given it.

She introduced herself to the same woman four times over the course of an hour. Each time the woman beamed and said, "Sarah! Lovely! I'm Maren!" and each time it was new to her. Children played a game with rules that changed mid-game because no one could hold the rules in mind long enough to object. A man walked into his own house, stood in the doorway frowning, and walked out again.

"There was sickness," Maren told them cheerfully, for perhaps the third time. "Fever took half the village, oh, years ago now. Or last month. We all prayed to be healed. And we were! Every single one. Best health in the province. Doctor says we're the soundest folk he's ever seen." She paused, brow furrowing gently, like a pond disturbed by one drop. "...Who are you, dear?"

"Healing that takes memory," Serah said to Rafael that night, in the loft of the inn where the innkeeper had already forgotten they'd paid. She kept her voice low, though it hardly mattered. "I remember the terms now. I heard them recited in the court. *Health of body, purchased with the past.* The god doesn't take memory cruelly. It takes it as fuel. Memories burn hotter than wood."

Rafael sat on the edge of the cot with the ledger across his knees, not reading it, just holding it, the way you'd hold a snake you hadn't yet decided to keep. "Odell used to say the guild's oldest books recorded bargains both ways. Fair trade, freely made. He was proud of that. 'We never cheat anyone,' he said. 'We only witness.'" His jaw tightened. "These aren't trades. These are traps dressed as gifts."

"Yes," Serah said. And then, because she owed him more than yes: "And it gets worse the closer we get. These villages dealt with minor gods. Threshold-spirits, river-queens, things with small appetites. The Deep King is different. The Deep King doesn't take memories or prayers." She looked at her hands. "It takes service. Years of it. Whole lives filed away."

She felt him looking at her and made herself meet his eyes.

"Someday soon," she said, "you're going to ask me what I owe. And I'm going to have to answer."

"I'm not going to ask," Rafael said. "You'll tell me when you're ready."

"That," Serah said, "is exactly why I'm terrified of you."

He laughed, and she let herself lean against his shoulder, briefly, like a bird testing a branch. Outside, the forgotten village slept the sleep of the innocent, dreaming nothing at all.

* * *

*Rafael*

On the fifth day they reached Hollowbridge, and Rafael learned what it looked like when a prayer was answered *correctly* — and discovered that correct was somehow the cruelest version of all.

Hollowbridge had prayed for a miracle of a different kind. Rafael pieced it together from a shrine notice board, where a proclamation in gold leaf announced: *Thanks be to the Kindly Court! Our beloved Alderman Corwin, dead these six months, restored to us whole!* Below it, in smaller print, someone had nailed a list of rules: *Do not speak to him of his death. Do not speak to him of his wife (deceased). Do not ask him to leave the shrine grounds.*

They found him in the shrine garden, tending roses with careful, deliberate movements, like a man performing a task he'd read described in a book. He was a big gray-bearded fellow with a face built for laughter that no longer lived there. When Rafael greeted him, Corwin smiled precisely and said, "Good morning. Lovely day. Lovely day."

"Are you well?" Rafael asked.

"I am alive," Corwin said, with the exact intonation of someone reciting a fact from a ledger. "Alive is well. Lovely day."

Serah made a small sound and turned away. Rafael lingered. He couldn't help himself; he was a ledger-keeper, and somewhere in him a column wanted balancing. "Alderman — do you remember dying?"

For a moment — half a heartbeat — something moved behind the man's eyes. Something vast and drowning. Then the smooth surface closed over it. "Lovely day," Corwin said. "Lovely day. Lovely day."

Rafael walked out of the garden and didn't stop until they were over the bridge the village was named for, and then he gripped the stone parapet and breathed until the shaking stopped.

"They brought him back wrong," he said. "Or — no. That's the lie I want to tell myself. They brought him back *exactly right*, according to the contract. Restored to us whole. Nothing in the contract says *restored to us as ourselves.* The words did what words do. The god did what gods do. Everyone kept their side of the bargain and the result is—" he gestured helplessly back at the shrine— "that. A man tending roses forever, saying lovely day."

"And the villagers can't even grieve him properly," Serah said, "because grieving him might count as complaining, and complaining might void the miracle, and then they lose him twice." She shook her head slowly. "That's how the court works, Rafael. That's the whole architecture of it. Not cruelty for its own sake — cruelty as *precision*. Every wish granted so literally that the wishing becomes a trap. The worshippers built those contracts themselves, clause by clause, over centuries, each one trying to protect themselves from the last one. The god is chained by them. And the chains reach up out of the ground and hook into everyone who ever knelt."

Rafael looked at her. "You talk like someone who's read the contracts."

"I talked like someone who *wrote copies of the contracts*," Serah said, "for eleven years. And then I'll tell you the rest, when we're through the gates and it's too late for you to turn back." Her mouth twisted. "No. That's a lie. I'll tell you tonight. I promise. Tonight."

But tonight, as it happened, never quite arrived, because that evening the sky over the eastern hills turned the color of a bruise, and the bells began.

* * *

*Serah*

Serah heard them before Rafael did — she always did now — a chorus rising from every direction at once, villages she couldn't even see, bronze voices all crying the same note of joy and terror.

"They're answering everything," she whispered. "All at once. Everywhere. Do you feel it? The whole country is praying, Rafael. Half of them praying *to*, and half praying *please make it stop*, and the gods can't tell the difference anymore, because to a contract there's no difference between worship and begging."

Rain came in the night, but not like Marrow's End — this rain fell upward, or seemed to, mist rising off the fields in silver ropes while the stars burned dry overhead. In the morning they passed a farmstead where the wheat stood taller than a man, heavy-headed, golden, magnificent — and every stalk was empty husk. Abundance of shape, nothing inside. A prayer for plenty answered by a god who understood plenty as appearance.

They passed a crossroads shrine where a young woman sat rocking a baby that never cried, never slept, never grew hungry — a blessing, the grandmother insisted, wringing her hands, a blessing, the child would never suffer, never suffer, never suffer — and Serah thought: *and never live, either,* and had to walk away fast before she said it aloud.

By afternoon Rafael had stopped talking much. He walked with the ledger clutched against his ribs and his face set in the expression Serah privately called his *counting face*, except that now he was counting wounds instead of coins.

"It's not malice," he said finally, at dusk, as they crested a hill and saw, far ahead, the mountain — a black triangle biting into the sky, with a thread of torchlight winding up its flank like a lit fuse. "That's what I keep coming back to. A monster would at least be *simple*. You could hate a monster. But this is bookkeeping. Literal-minded, clause-bound, perfectly fair bookkeeping, performed on human lives by powers that don't understand what fairness costs. Odell would have loved it. That's the worst part. There's a version of me that finds it elegant."

"I know," Serah said. "I found it elegant too, once. For about a year. Then I filed a child's contract and elegance stopped being a word I could use."

He glanced at her sharply. "A child's—"

"Tonight," she said. "I promised. Tonight, at the fire, I'll tell you everything — what I was, what I signed, what I ran from, and why Veyra will send collectors after me the moment my name touches the mountain's air. All of it. Because tomorrow we reach the tollhouse at the border of the court, and after that there are no more roads, only debts." She looked at the distant torch-thread climbing the black flank of the world. "And Rafael — one more thing. At the tollhouse, whatever it asks of you, whatever it offers you — and it *will* offer — remember the wheat. Remember the alderman. Remember that every gift down there is a sentence written in your own hand and signed in your own blood."

"I remember," Rafael said. And then, with the small crooked smile that had undone her at nineteen and was undoing her still: "Besides. I'm a junior ledger-keeper. Reading the fine print is the entire job."

The mountain waited. Behind them, faint and fading, a village bell rang *lovely day, lovely day* into the dark, and Serah walked on toward the roots of the world carrying the one debt she was most afraid to name.
