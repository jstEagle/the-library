## 10. The Gates of the Under-Court

The mountain had a name once — Grayhollow, or Greyhollow, depending on which cartographer you asked and how much he'd been paid — but nobody called it that anymore. Now it was just the Mountain, the way a knife in your ribs is just *the pain*, and everyone within three days' walk knew what you meant.

We saw it for two days before we reached it: a black shoulder rising out of the earth like something surfacing from deep water, wearing clouds the way old men wear hats they've forgotten they have on.

"It's bigger than I imagined," Serah said.

"Everything about this is," I said.

She gave me a look. "That's not comforting, Rafael."

"I wasn't going for comforting. I was going for honest. It's a new policy. I'm told it suits me."

It did not suit me. I'd spent my whole career at the Guild of Impossible Things learning to say whatever made a client stop crying, and honesty sat on me like a coat cut for someone else. But the road had been changing me, the way roads do. Three weeks of walking through villages where prayers were being answered like invoices collected — rain that came when called and drowned the barley; a fever broken in a child while her grandmother forgot her own name mid-lullaby — will do that. You stop dressing things up. There wasn't much left worth dressing up in.

* * *

We heard the queue before we saw it.

Not voices, exactly. Not weeping, though there was some. It was more a texture in the air, a low hum of want — thousands of people wanting things at once, the way a hive hums with work. Then the road crested a hill, and there it was.

A line. A line of people, winding down through a valley and up the mountain's flank and out of sight into the dark of the root-gates, so long that it stopped being a line and became a river, a slow brown river of coats and bundles and bent backs, flowing at perhaps a hundred paces an hour toward the gates of the Under-Court.

"Oh," said Serah quietly.

There were tents along it. Cook-fires. Children chasing each other between the legs of petitioners who had clearly been waiting long enough to become landmarks. An old woman had set up a stall selling boiled chestnuts to the queue, and doing brisk trade, because if there is one universal truth in this world it is that wherever human beings are made to wait, someone will sell them something hot.

"How long?" I asked a man near the front of our stretch of the river. He had a farmer's hands and a scholar's eyes, an odd combination, and he looked at us the way you look at newcomers who haven't yet learned the local grief.

"To reach the gates? Eleven months, if you're lucky. Fourteen if you're not." He shrugged. "I've been here since thaw."

"And then?"

"Then you get your audience. If you can pay the toll." He held up his hand, and I saw that his little finger was gone — not lost, not bandaged, simply *gone*, the stump smooth and old as though it had never been there at all. "Paid that one last week. A year of memory for every joint, they say, if you want to skip ahead. The finger bought me nothing but a better place in line." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was genuinely contented. "Worth it, mind. My daughter's sick. Whatever they ask, it'll be worth it."

Serah turned away. I watched her jaw tighten, watched her hands close around the strap of her pack until her knuckles went white, and I understood that she was looking at the queue and seeing herself in it — seeing the girl she had been, years ago, standing somewhere in a line like this one, waiting to sell something she could never buy back.

"We should keep moving," she said.

* * *

The root-gates themselves deserved better than the word *gates*. They were doors cut into the living rock at the base of the Mountain, tall as cathedral spires and twice as dark, carved over every inch with script that hurt to look at directly — not because it was ugly, but because it was *true*, and truth at that scale gives a person a headache. Before them stood the tollhouse: a squat stone building wedged between the doors like a barnacle, lit by lamps that burned without oil, tended by clerks whose faces I couldn't hold in memory no matter how hard I looked. Every time I glanced away and back, I had the sense I was meeting them for the first time.

Above the tollhouse, in letters of hammered bronze, hung the terms:

**ALL WHO WOULD AUDIENCE MUST PAY. THE COURT ACCEPTS YEARS, MEMORIES, NAMES, LOVE, LUCK, OR GRIEF. THE COURT DOES NOT ACCEPT COIN. COIN IS FOR PEOPLE. WE ARE NOT PEOPLE.**

I found that oddly funny, in the way you find things funny when you're frightened — the pettiness of it, the bookkeeping pride. Even gods, apparently, wanted their signage clear.

"The ledger," Serah murmured. "Rafael. Show me what it says now."

I drew it out of my coat — Master Odell Vane's great ledger, stolen fair and square from under the noses of a guild council too cowardly to fight and too proud to run, and heavy as a sleeping cat. I'd taken to keeping it against my chest, where I told myself it was safest and knew, in the small hours, that it was warmest. That should have worried me more than it did.

I opened it to the page for the Under-Court and read aloud, softly, so only she could hear:

"*All petitioners must pay passage. No exceptions. Debts of fortune may be tendered in substitution.*"

"Debts of fortune," Serah repeated.

"That's guild language. Old guild language. It means—" I stopped, because I already knew what it meant, and knowing it felt like stepping off a ledge. "It means misfortune owed to you. Bad luck that's come to you unjustly. Sickness you didn't earn. Grief that isn't yours by right. The ledger records those too — that's half of what the Guild ever did, tracking who the world had wronged. And if the ledger says such debts may be tendered—"

"Then you could pay your way in," Serah said slowly, "with somebody else's bad luck."

"With somebody else's *unjust* bad luck," I corrected, because apparently even at the gates of hell I was still a ledger-keeper, and precision was the one virtue I had left. "Which makes it almost respectable."

"Almost respectable," Serah said flatly. "Rafael. Look around you."

I looked around me. At the river of people. At the man with nine fingers. At the children playing tag among the tents, who would grow up in this queue the way fish grow up in water, and who would one day stand at those doors and be asked what they had, and answer *me*, and go in.

"I am looking," I said.

* * *

Here is the thing I have tried, in the years since, to explain honestly, and mostly failed: it didn't feel like corruption. It felt like efficiency.

We had walked three weeks. Serah's feet were bleeding through her second pair of boots. Behind us, somewhere down that eleven-month river, were people who would die in that queue — not metaphorically; actually die, of winter, of the coughing sickness that moved through tent-cities like a tax collector — without ever reaching the doors. And in my hands was a book that said, in ink older than the guild, that debts of fortune could be tendered.

And there was, I told myself, so much of it lying around. The world was *full* of unearned suffering. It pooled in places like rainwater. Surely — surely — taking a little of it, here, to get us through those doors faster, to get to Odell's soul sooner, was not theft. It was drainage. It was mercy with good posture.

You can justify anything with bookkeeping language. That is the first lesson of my profession and, I now understand, its final warning.

"There'll be a cost," Serah said. We stood apart from the queue, behind the chestnut seller's stall, arguing in the low urgent tones of people who have discovered that love does not prevent arguments, only makes them worse. "There's always a cost. You taught me that."

"I taught you that debts have costs. This isn't a debt. It's a transfer."

"A transfer *of what*, Rafael? Whose misfortune? How do you choose? Do you look down that line and pick someone?" Her voice cracked, and she lowered it. "Because I've seen what happens when power picks. I lived in that court. I know how it chooses, and it always has a reason, and the reason is always that it was easier."

"I wouldn't pick anyone," I said. "I'd let the ledger pick. It knows what's owed better than I do. It's impartial."

"Ledgers aren't impartial. Ledgers are written by people." She took my hand then, and her grip was fierce. "Listen to me. I fled that place. I faked my own death to get out of it. And the thing that finally broke me wasn't the god — the god never touched me, not once. It was the *clerks*. The little kindnesses. The clerk who moved me up the list because I reminded him of his daughter. The clerk who forgave a widow's toll because it was nearly festival-day. Every one of them thought they were being merciful. And every mercy was a thread, and the threads wove the net, and the net held all of us." She pressed my hand between both of hers. "Don't be a kind clerk, Rafael. Be something else."

For a moment — I want to record this honestly, because the rest of the account is not flattering — for a moment I meant to listen.

* * *

What undid me was a boy.

He couldn't have been more than ten, sitting alone by a cook-fire near the front of our stretch of the line, mending a shoe with string and terrible technique. When I passed, he looked up with the practiced blankness of a child who has learned that adults in queues are either rivals or resources, and I saw the sickness in him — the gray undertone to his skin, the careful way he breathed, rationing air like coin.

His mother, a tent-flap told me, had gone through the gates six days ago. She'd paid a year of memory for a place near the front. She hadn't come out. Nobody comes out. But the boy waited anyway, mending his shoe, saving his strength, certain that she'd send for him.

And I stood there holding a book that could move him up a line he shouldn't be in at all, and I thought: *one entry. One small entry. Spend a debt of fortune — some rich man's gout, some merchant's undeserved bankruptcy — and put this child at the front of the queue, and his mother's sacrifice won't have been wasted.*

Do you see it? Do you see how reasonable? That's the trick of it, and I fell for it with my eyes open, which is somehow worse than falling blind.

I opened the ledger. I found the boy — the book knew him instantly, the way it knows everything, his name (Tomas), his debt of fortune (a fever contracted through no fault of his own, severity: mortal, unless). And beside it, glowing faintly in the margin like a banknote waiting to be cashed, the option presented itself, patient as a spider.

*Tenderable.*

I looked at the entry for a long time. Then I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the tollhouse: if the court accepted the boy's misfortune as payment, the boy would owe nothing — but the court would *hold* it. His fever, his unfairness, his dying-young luck, filed away in the deep archive alongside everything else the court had ever collected. Given to the god beneath the mountain like a coin dropped in a well.

And the well, Serah had told me, spends what it's given.

"No," I whispered to myself.

But my hand was already moving. Because here is the second trick, the deeper one: I told myself I wasn't spending *his* misfortune. I was spending the *debt* — the abstract, the accounting entry, the injustice itself, which surely belonged to no one, which was just a wrongness floating loose in the world. I wrote the transfer in a fine steady hand. I dated it. I initialed it, R.B., junior ledger-keeper, Guild of Impossible Things, in the manner of a man signing a receipt rather than a confession.

The ledger drank the entry. Somewhere in the world, I understood, a wrongness had changed hands, and I had not even looked to see whose.

The boy's status changed on the page. *Position in queue: first.*

"Next," said a voice at my elbow, and I nearly jumped out of my skin.

One of the faceless clerks stood beside me. It had appeared without crossing any distance I'd seen, and it was looking at the open ledger with what I can only describe as professional appreciation.

"You're carrying guild paper," it said. Its voice was dry and pleasant, like pages turning. "We haven't honored a guild instrument in four hundred years. The guild owes us rather a lot, as I recall." A pause. "But the book itself is valid. The book was always valid. Come inside, keeper. Both of you. The King dislikes waiting, and you've saved him some."

It turned and walked toward the doors, and the doors began, very slowly, to open.

Serah stared at me. "What did you write?"

"One entry," I said. My voice sounded far away. "Just one. For the boy."

"And the rest of the line? Rafael — the whole queue just moved. Look."

I looked. The river of people was flowing — actually flowing, stumbling forward, a mile of petitioners surging toward the gates as the ledger's single entry rippled outward through the court's systems like a stone dropped in a frozen lake cracking the ice. Because of course. Of course. Nothing in that court existed separately; every position in the line was a link in a chain of debts, and I had pulled one link, and the whole chain had come alive in my hand.

They were cheering. That was the worst part. They were cheering and weeping and pressing forward, thanking the Mountain, thanking the King, blessing the unknown saint who had opened the way — and every one of them was now one step closer to a door that took payment in years and memories and names, and I had opened it, and they were grateful.

"They'll pay," Serah said quietly. "Every one of them will pay, and they'll think it was a gift."

"I know."

"Do you?" She searched my face, and whatever she found there made her step back half a pace. "Oh, Rafael. You liked it."

I wanted to deny it. Standing there, with the great doors groaning open onto darkness and lamplight and the smell of old paper and older stone, with a thousand voices rising in gratitude for a mercy that was a trap — I wanted, badly, to deny it.

Instead I closed the ledger, and held it against my chest where it was warmest, and said, "Let's go in. Odell doesn't have much time."

And we crossed the threshold together, into the Under-Court, where something vast and weary and chained was waiting to make me an offer I was already afraid I'd spend a century regretting refusing.
