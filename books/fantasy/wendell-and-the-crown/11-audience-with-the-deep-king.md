## 11. Audience with the Deep King

The throne room of the under-court was not what Rafael had expected, which was itself a surprise, because Rafael had expected very little and been disappointed even by that.

He had imagined — based on the tollhouse keeper's warnings, the petitioners' whispers, and one particularly vivid nightmare involving teeth — a cavern of black stone and screaming. What he found instead was a hall like a cathedral that had been built by someone who loved cathedrals but could not afford one: pillars of living rock ribbed with veins of pale gold, a ceiling lost in darkness where something dripped with the patience of centuries, and a floor worn smooth by ten thousand thousand feet. The air smelled of rain on hot stone. Somewhere far off, water sang.

And at the end of it all, the god.

Rafael had prepared himself for vastness. He had not prepared for *weariness*.

The Deep King did not sit upon its throne so much as rest against it, the way an old man rests against a doorframe when no one is watching. It was large in the way a mountain is large — not a body but a presence, a shape of shadow and slow light that filled the dais and spilled down the steps like spilled ink deciding to be dignified about it. Chains ran from its shoulders, its wrists, the crown of what might have been its head, and disappeared into the walls. There were hundreds of them. Thousands. Each link inscribed with writing too small to read, though Rafael's fingers itched to try, because he was a ledger-keeper down to the marrow and small writing was his native language.

Each chain, he realized slowly, was a contract.

"Ledger-keeper," said the Deep King. Its voice was the sound a mountain makes settling in winter — deep, unhurried, and faintly amused. "You are smaller than I expected."

"People say that about me a lot," Rafael said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which he chose to regard as a professional triumph. "Usually right before they give me more work."

A sound moved through the hall. It took Rafael a moment to identify it as laughter, because it came up through the floor rather than out of the god, rattling his boot-soles and the loose coin in his pocket. Even the chains seemed to shift, just slightly, the way a sleeping dog twitches at a familiar word.

"You brought my book," the Deep King said.

"I borrowed your book," Rafael corrected, and then, because honesty had gotten him this far and lying would probably get him flattened, "stole. I stole your book. From my own guild. Which is technically also your book, according to a founding contract nobody told me about, which I have *feelings* about."

"Good feelings or bad?"

"Mostly bad. Some confused. One or two that I'd describe as 'the specific anger of a man who has done the accounts faithfully for eleven years only to learn the whole shop was mortgaged before he was born.'"

The great head inclined — a movement like a hillside agreeing to be a valley. "That is an accurate summary of my own feelings regarding the arrangement," the Deep King said. "We have that in common, you and I. Come closer, thief. Let me look at you."

* * *

Walking toward the throne was the longest forty feet of Rafael's life. The chains grew clearer as he approached, and clarity made them worse. Each link bore a name, a date, a promise. *Marta of the Fenlands, in exchange for her son's fever breaking, one hour of prayer daily until death.* *The Guild of Impossible Things, in exchange for the power of true record, repayment upon waking.* *Odell Vane, renewal signed in the guild's name, collateral: his soul.*

There were children's names. Rafael made himself keep walking.

Up close, the god resolved into something almost comprehensible — a face like weathered cliffstone, eyes the color of deep lakes where the light gives up halfway down. Its expression was the worst part. Not rage. Not hunger. *Tiredness*, deep as the mountain itself, the tiredness of someone who has been awake far longer than anyone should be awake and has run out of things to be angry about.

"Do you know," the Deep King said, "what these are?" It lifted one wrist, and the chains chimed softly, a sound like a thousand distant bells — Serah's bells, Rafael thought absurdly, and his chest tightened.

"Contracts," Rafael said. "Bargains. Prayers that got... notarized."

"*Prayers*," the god repeated, and the word came out with an edge that made the nearest torches gutter. "Once, little keeper, prayers were free. A woman in a drought held up her hands and asked for rain, and I gave it because her asking was beautiful and because giving was what I *was*. No price. No signature. No chain." The eyes closed briefly. "Then someone clever decided that gifts should be recorded. That favors should accrue interest. That a god who answered freely was a god being *wasted*." A pause. "Your guild was founded by such clever people. They came to me when I slept and they wrote, and wrote, and wrote, and every prayer they brokered became a link, and every link became law, and now—" it spread its arms, and the chains rose with them, a forest of iron, "—now I am the most thoroughly documented creature in existence. I cannot sneeze without breaching clause fourteen of an agreement signed by a shepherd four hundred years dead."

Rafael looked at the chains. Then at the ledger under his arm. Then at the chains again.

"That's why the entries changed," he said quietly. "In our oldest books. 'Unanswered' becoming 'answered.' You woke up, and you started collecting."

"I started *enforcing*," the Deep King said. "Believe me, I take no joy in it. But a contract is a contract is a contract, and I am bound to honor them as much as they bind me. That is the elegant cruelty of the thing your clever founders built. When I collect a debt, I do it *because* I must. The drowning rain, the healing that eats memory — those were not cruelties I invented. They were terms. Written by men. Signed by the desperate." Its gaze settled on Rafael with the weight of a landslide considering whether to happen. "Your Master Vane signed a renewal knowing the price. Did he tell you?"

"He told me," Rafael said. "After I found it. He cried, actually. It was awful. He's an awful old man and I love him and I want him back."

Something flickered across the stony face — surprise, perhaps, or its geological equivalent.

"Honesty," the Deep King murmured. "From a ledger-keeper. How novel. Do you know how many souls have stood where you stand and lied to me? Flattered me? Offered me bargains?" A dry rumble. "They always offer me bargains. As if I did not have enough."

* * *

It offered him tea.

This was so profoundly not what Rafael expected that he accepted before his brain caught up with his mouth, and thus found himself seated on a stone bench that rippled up out of the floor like a polite boil, holding a cup of something dark and bitter that tasted the way caves smell, while a god the size of a church drank from a basin the size of a pond.

"Serah of the Bell Tower waits outside my gates," the Deep King said.

Rafael's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "She's not part of this."

"She rang for me once." The god's voice went softer, if a mountain can be said to go soft; the whole hall seemed to lean in. "Years ago. Before she fled. She rang the bronze above my roots and the sound reached me down here, and for three minutes — three minutes, keeper — I remembered what it was to be prayed to out of love rather than obligation." The lake-eyes fixed on him. "I have thought about those three minutes for six years. That is the length of my entertainment. You may weep for me now, if you like."

"I'm not going to weep for you," Rafael said, and was surprised to find his voice rough. "I'm going to feel horrible about you privately, at a later date, when it won't be rude."

The laughter came again through the floor, longer this time. "Ohh, I see why the book chose you."

"The book didn't choose me. I stole the book."

"Mm. And yet." The god set down its basin. The amusement drained away, leaving bedrock. "Let us speak plainly, Rafael Brightwater, junior ledger-keeper, senior soul-thief. You came for Odell Vane's soul. I have it." One vast hand gestured, and from the shadows beside the throne a shelf slid forward, and on the shelf sat jars — hundreds of them, thousands, each glowing faintly with the light of a contained life. Rafael's stomach turned over. Near the front, labeled in Odell's own fussy handwriting (*Vane, O. — renewal, 40 yrs — PAID IN FULL pending*), sat a jar with a small, indignant swirl of light inside, like a firefly that had strong opinions about being jarred.

"There he is," Rafael said. His throat was tight. "He looks annoyed."

"He is annoyed. He has been filing complaints since he arrived. Verbally. At me." A pause. "I have chosen not to hear them, as I am within my rights."

"What do you want for him?"

The Deep King was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, counting seconds the way Serah counted them, and Rafael thought of her waiting beyond the gates, and made himself breathe.

"A century," the god said at last. "One hundred years of service. You will sit where your founders sat — no. Better. You will sit *beneath* where they sat, at a desk I will have carved, and you will keep my book honestly. Every bargain entered true. Every debt weighed fair. Every collection reviewed for cruelty, and mitigated where the terms allow. Your founders built a machine that grinds the desperate, keeper. I am chained to it. I cannot stop it. But a man with a steady hand and a sick conscience—" the lake-eyes gleamed, "—a man like that could make the grinding gentler. Could save the Martas of the world their worst clauses. Could find the loopholes the desperate never knew to ask for."

"And Odell?"

"Returned to his body the moment you sign. Breathing, complaining, alive. You have my word — and unlike your guildmasters, keeper, my word is the only currency I have left, so I spend it carefully."

Rafael looked at the jar. At the small indignant light that was the man who had taught him double-entry, who had snuck him honeycakes during inventory, who had signed away his soul to protect a guild full of fools. A century. Rafael was twenty-nine. A hundred years beneath the mountain, and he would come out — if he came out — an old, old man, while Serah...

While Serah.

The temptation did not arrive roaring. That was the terrible thing about it, Rafael thought later. It arrived the way damp arrives in a cellar: quietly, reasonably, pointing out that the alternative was a good man's soul in a jar forever, that the work was *good* work, honest work, the best work Rafael could imagine — fair ledgers for the powerless, mercy threaded through machinery of iron. Who else would do it? Someone worse. Someone like Veyra, whose smile Rafael had already learned to distrust. The world needed a decent person at that desk.

The world needed *him* at that desk.

And underneath that reasonable voice, faint as a bell heard through miles of stone, another voice said: *you liked cutting the line at the gates. You liked spending another man's misfortune. You liked knowing things. This is how it starts — not with a villain's laugh, but with a good reason.*

"You're doing it now," the Deep King said quietly.

Rafael blinked. "Doing what?"

"Weighing it. I can hear it. The arithmetic." The god leaned forward, and the chains groaned, and for one moment the weariness in its face cracked open into something older and sadder. "Let me tell you what your founders never understood, keeper. I do not want your century. I have had ten thousand centuries of service, and every one of them arrived in chains. Do you know what I want?" It raised one shackled hand, and the links sang their sad bell-song. "I want what she gave me. Three minutes of bronze, rung for no reason except that ringing was beautiful. I want to be *asked*, freely, and to be able to answer, freely, and for neither of us to owe the other a single thing afterward." The hand fell. "But I cannot want it aloud, you see. Wanting is not in my contracts. So instead I offer you a hundred years, because offering bargains is all I am permitted to do — and I watch every petitioner walk out of this hall either enslaved or empty-handed, and I wait for the one who does something none of my contracts anticipate."

The hall was silent. The dripping water counted.

"What would that be?" Rafael asked. "The thing none of your contracts anticipate?"

The Deep King smiled, and it was like watching dawn come up over a canyon — vast, slow, and impossible to look away from.

"I genuinely don't know," it said. "That is the entire point."

* * *

Rafael stood. His legs were unsteady. The ledger under his arm felt suddenly less like a stolen book and more like a live coal.

"I need time," he said.

"You have until moonrise," said the Deep King. "Above ground, that is. Down here we keep no moons, so I will know it's happened when the bells ring — she still rings them, your Serah, did you know? Every evening, out past my gates, on a hill where the sound carries down. She says she's practicing. She has been practicing for six years." The god settled back against its throne, closing its eyes, and the chains dimmed with it, and Rafael understood with a lurch that the god was going to sleep, right there, mid-audience, because it was tired, so tired, and no one had ever given it permission to rest.

At the edge of sleep, the great voice rumbled one last time, soft as shifting gravel:

"One hundred years, keeper. Or find the thing my contracts don't anticipate. Choose quickly. Choose *well*. And Rafael Brightwater—"

The lake-eyes opened a sliver, gleaming.

"—do not decide alone. That is the first mistake they all make. The clever ones especially."

Rafael walked back up the long hall with the ledger burning under his arm and the arithmetic still turning in his chest — a soul against a century, a friend against a future, duty against the memory of Serah's face when she'd said *come back to me* — and he did not notice, because he was a ledger-keeper and not a spy, the small paper figure standing in the shadow of the last pillar, whose smile was all business, and who had heard every word.
