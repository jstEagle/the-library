## 12. The Century Bargain

Serah had learned, in her years below the mountain, that the deep court never raised its voice. That was the first thing they taught you — or rather, the first thing you learned without anyone teaching it, the way you learn that fire is hot. The court did not shout. It simply waited, and its waiting was louder than any bell she had ever rung.

So when the Deep King made its offer, it did not thunder. It merely let the words settle into the vast dark of the throne hall like silt into still water, and Serah felt them land in her chest one by one, each heavier than the last.

*A century of service. Odell Vane's soul returned to his body, whole and breathing. In exchange: Rafael Brightwater, keeper of the ledger.*

She watched Rafael's face.

That was her mistake, maybe. Or her salvation. She wasn't sure yet which. But she watched him, and she saw the thing she had been afraid to see since the moment he'd pulled the master ledger from his coat in the tollhouse and discovered what it could do — she saw the light come on behind his eyes. Not greed. Rafael Brightwater was incapable of greed; she'd once watched him give away his last coin to a beggar who turned out to be better off than he was. No, what she saw was worse than greed. It was *purpose*. It was a man looking at an ocean of suffering and being handed a bucket, and thinking: *I could carry this. I could carry all of it.*

"Rafael," she said quietly.

He didn't hear her. He was looking up at the god — at the vast, weary shape of it, half-drowned in chains, each link stamped with a seal she recognized because she had once pressed her thumb to a thousand of them — and the god was looking back with eyes like wells, and between them passed something Serah understood far too well. Pity. The terrible, flattering pity of the powerful for the burdened.

"I would need to understand the work," Rafael was saying. His voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was trying to sound older than he was. "The terms. What exactly a keeper does."

"Rafael." Louder now.

"And what happens to the souls already held here. The ones in the archive." He swallowed. "There are children's souls down there. I saw them."

*You saw them,* Serah thought, and grief went through her like a blade through wet cloth. *And now you will never stop seeing them. That is how it starts. That is exactly how it started with me.*

The Deep King's chains shifted. Somewhere in the dark above the throne, a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat.

"The keeper writes what must be written," the god said. "Balances what must be balanced. The work is old, and it is heavy, and no one has done it kindly in a very long time." A pause, long enough for hope to grow teeth. "It could be done kindly."

"There," Serah said. "Do you hear it? Do you hear how it says that?"

Rafael turned to her at last, and his face was open and honest and utterly lost, and she wanted to shake him and kiss him in equal measure, and she settled — barely — for grabbing his sleeve.

"We need to talk. Now. Before you say anything else."

"Serah—"

"*Now*, Rafael."

* * *

The Deep King permitted them a chamber. Of course it did. The court beneath the mountain was generous with chambers the way a fisherman is generous with hooks — every courtesy came baited. They were given a small room off the throne hall, walled in black stone veined with something that glowed faintly gold, like the mountain's own blood had learned to shine. There were two chairs. There was a table with nothing on it. There was a door that was very pointedly not locked, because the court had never needed locks.

Serah closed the door anyway. It felt important.

"You're considering it," she said.

"I'm *listening* to it. There's a difference."

"Is there?" She heard her own voice go sharp and hated it, and kept going anyway. "Because from where I'm standing, it sounds like the same difference as between *drowning* and *considering the water*."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. Welcome to the under-court. Fairness died here centuries ago and they filed its soul in drawer nine thousand and forty."

He almost smiled. She saw him almost smile, and it nearly undid her, because that was the other weapon the court used — it made you love things, and then it priced them.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit *down*, Rafael."

He sat. Good. Sitting men argued slower.

"Tell me what you're thinking," she said, and made her voice gentle, because she had learned gentleness down here too, learned it the hard way, learned it as a survival skill rather than a virtue. "All of it. The ugly parts especially. Especially the parts you think I'll hate."

He looked at his hands. Ledger-keeper's hands, ink-stained at the nails even after everything, even down here where ink was blood and paper was skin.

"All right," he said. "Here's the ugly part. When it offered — for one moment — one single moment — I thought: *finally.* Finally someone's asking me to do something that matters. Ten years I've been the junior keeper. Ten years of cataloguing bottled grief and checking other people's arithmetic while Odell got the respect and the council got the power and I got the corner desk that wobbles. And now—" He spread his hands. "Now the oldest debt in the world needs settling, and the only person in the room who actually understands ledgers is *me*, Serah. Not the council. Not Veyra. Me. Somebody has to keep those accounts until they can be closed properly, and if I don't, who does? Another Veyra? Another hundred years of children filed like invoices?"

"So you'd take the crown to close the accounts."

"I'd take the crown to *fix* them."

"And then?"

"Then—" He stopped.

"Then," Serah said, "you'd be the keeper. For a century. And I would be—" Her voice caught, and she was furious at it, and pushed through. "What, Rafael? What am I in this plan? Because I've done the arithmetic you're not doing. A century is longer than both our lives put together. You'd be down here writing balances while I'm up there growing old, and then dead, and then — what, do I get filed too? Drawer nine thousand and forty-one? Will you visit my soul-jar on feast days? Read my name off the spine?"

"It wouldn't be like that."

"How would it be, then? Tell me. Use small words. I'm only a woman who spent six years as this thing's priestess, so I may need the simple version."

He flinched. Good, some unkind part of her said. Let it sting.

"You left," he said quietly. "You got out. If I'm the keeper, I can make sure nobody else gets taken. Nobody else's grandmother signs something they don't understand. Nobody else's mentor—" His voice cracked on *mentor*, and she saw him think of Odell, breathing but empty in a bed three kingdoms away, and she hated how much she understood him, because understanding was the trap's finest hinge.

"Rafael." She knelt in front of his chair, so their eyes were level, because this next part needed to be said eye to eye or not at all. "I served it. Six years. Do you know what my duties were? Do you know what a priestess of the deep court actually *does*?"

"Prays. Tends the shrine. Rings the—"

"I read names." Her mouth had gone dry. "Every day, I sat in the little alcove beside the throne, and petitioners came, and I wrote their bargains into the book, and I watched their faces when they understood what they'd signed. And do you know what I learned? The ones who meant well were the worst. The desperate ones knew they were selling something. But the *kind* ones — the ones who wanted to fix everything, heal everyone, carry the whole ocean — they always believed they'd be the exception. The first keeper in history who wouldn't be changed by the holding." She took his hands. They were cold. "I watched a woman named Maren take the keeping for thirty years because she wanted to shorten the queue at the gates. Thirty years, Rafael. By the end she was spending lives like coppers because she'd stopped seeing people and started seeing *entries*. She wasn't evil. She was tired, and the book did the rest."

"This isn't Maren's book. This is mine, and I know the danger, and knowing is half—"

"Knowing is *nothing*." She hadn't meant to shout. The black walls took the sound and gave back a faint golden shimmer, and she lowered her voice, fast, before the mountain could listen too closely. "Knowing is nothing. The book doesn't corrupt you the way poison does. It corrupts you the way a chair does. It just fits. Every day it fits a little better, and one day you can't remember why you ever wanted to stand."

Silence. The gold veins pulsed slowly, like the mountain dreaming.

"Say I refuse," Rafael said at last. "Then what? Odell stays a husk. The archive stays full. The gates stay crowded with people trading years for audience. We walk out with our love intact and our hands clean and nothing else changes, ever, and I have to live with that." His jaw set. "I've lived with small compromises my whole life, Serah. My whole life has been small compromises in a wobbling chair. This is the first time anyone's offered me a big one."

"A big compromise is just a small one wearing a crown."

"That's very quotable and it doesn't answer me."

"Fine. Then here's my answer." She stood, because kneeling was making her feel like a petitioner, and she had sworn on her fake grave never to kneel in this place again. "Refuse. Refuse, and then we do it the hard way. We find Odell's soul-jar ourselves. We steal it. We break it. And then we find whatever book binds *this* thing—" she gestured upward, toward the chained god, toward the weight of ten thousand centuries of signed prayers, "—and we look at it properly. Not as keepers. As thieves. Thieves at least know they're thieves."

"Steal from a god."

"People steal from gods constantly. It's called prayer. Everyone wants something for nothing." She tried for humor and heard it come out cracked. "We'd just be more honest about it than most."

He looked up at her, and for a moment — one shining, stupid, wonderful moment — she thought she'd reached him. His face softened. His hands opened.

And then the door opened too.

* * *

A steward stood there — not Veyra, one of the lesser ones, a gray person made mostly of posture — and announced in a voice like dry paper that the Deep King wished to remind the candidate that the offer included certain provisions, and that the provisions were generous, and that time in the court moved differently than time above, and that Master Vane's body, though stable, was not indefinitely so.

"Generous provisions," Serah repeated, when the steward had bowed itself away. "Did you hear that? It's sweetening the pot. Gods don't sweeten pots, Rafael. Gods *are* the pot."

"It mentioned provisions before. I assumed—"

"You assumed. Yes. You've been assuming since we walked in. Assuming the offer is honest, assuming the terms are fixed, assuming you'd be the exception." She paced the little room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a habit from her priestess days when pacing was the only rebellion available. "Let me tell you about the provisions. Year one, you'll write fairly. Year five, you'll start rounding. Year twenty, you'll tell yourself the rounding is mercy — this soul stays a decade longer because its widow is frail, that prayer goes unanswered because answering it would cost too much. Year fifty, someone begs you for their child's soul and you check the ledger first and the ledger says the account is settled and you believe the ledger, because by then believing the ledger is easier than believing your own heart. And year ninety-nine, when I am dust and the guild is a story and everyone who ever loved you is a memory you keep in a jar you are *not allowed to open*—" She stopped pacing. Stood over him. "Year ninety-nine, Rafael, you will be the loneliest creature under this mountain, and the god will look at you with those well-eyes and say *thank you for your service*, and it will even mean it, and it will not free you, because the keeping is the chain, and the chain needs a neck."

His eyes were bright. Angry-bright. "You think I haven't thought about the cost?"

"I think you've thought about the cost the way a man thinks about drowning while standing on a dock. It's very different in the water."

"And what would you have me do instead? Walk away? Leave Odell empty? Leave the children in their drawers?" He stood up too, and now they were both standing, toe to toe, and the gold veins were pulsing faster, and somewhere far above them the mountain groaned like an old ship. "You talk about the hard way. Stealing and breaking. Beautiful. And if we fail? If Veyra catches us — and Veyra *will* catch us, that creature runs this court like a personal business — then we lose everything. Odell. The souls. Each other. At least the bargain saves *one* thing."

"At the price of everything else!"

"One thing! It saves one thing and it's the thing that matters most! Those souls are *real*, Serah, they're people, and you want me to gamble them on a burglary because a century feels too long to you—"

"Because a century IS too long! Because I KNOW what a century down here does, I paid six years and it took me two more just to remember my own favorite color!" Her voice broke clean in half. "You want to know the worst part? The worst part is that you'd be *good* at it. That's the horror of it, Rafael. You'd be kind for years. You'd be fair for decades. You'd be the best keeper this court has ever had, and it would change *nothing*, because the court doesn't need a good keeper, it needs A keeper, any keeper, a warm body to hold the pen, and the pen holds YOU. I watched it happen to Maren. I watched it happen to the one before Maren. I ran rather than let it happen to me, I faked my own DEATH, I let my mother mourn me, and I would do it all again, and you stand there weighing it like a column of figures—"

"Because someone HAS to weigh it! Someone has to do the arithmetic nobody wants to do! That's all a ledger is, Serah — it's caring with numbers! And yes, maybe I'd fail, maybe the book would eat me alive, but at least I'd have TRIED, at least I'd have—"

"At least you'd have WHAT? A century of self-importance and a jar for a wife?"

The words landed like a dropped anvil. She saw them land. She saw him go white, and she would have given anything — anything in any ledger in any court — to unsay them.

"That's not—" he started.

"No," she said. "No, it isn't. I didn't mean—"

But the door was opening again, and the gray steward was bowing, and the moment was closing like water over a stone.

"The Deep King," the steward intoned, "graciously extends the deliberation period. Also, the High Priestess Veyra requests the presence of the lady Serah, regarding certain irregularities noted in the archive."

Serah's blood went cold. "Irregularities."

"Records touched. Drawers disturbed. The Veyra is thorough." The steward's papery face did something that might, in a living creature, have been a smile. "The Veyra is also waiting."

Rafael stepped toward her, hand outstretched, all the quarrel forgotten, all the anger burned down to fear. "Serah—"

"Go," she said, and was proud of how steady it came out. "Think about what I said. All of it. Even the cruel parts. *Especially* the cruel parts." She paused at the door, and looked back at him — at his ink-stained hands and his stubborn, decent, doomed face — and softened despite everything, despite the mountain listening, despite the fear crawling up her spine. "For what it's worth: I didn't mean the jar. I'd haunt you far too thoroughly for a jar to hold."

And then she followed the steward into the dark, toward Veyra, toward the archive, toward whatever irregularities bore her fingerprints — while behind her, in the little golden-veined room, the man she loved sat alone with a god's offer and a century's silence, beginning, she feared, to do the arithmetic.
