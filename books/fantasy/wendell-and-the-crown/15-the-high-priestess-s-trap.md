## 15. The High Priestess's Trap

The trouble with lying to a god's court, Rafael decided on his third morning in the under-mountain, was that everything down here already knew how to read a ledger.

He had learned this the hard way at breakfast. The court provided meals — gray bread, gray broth, a fruit that tasted faintly of copper and apology — and each plate arrived with a small slip of paper beneath it, itemizing what the meal had cost someone, somewhere. This morning's slip read: *One loaf. Paid from the account of a miller's daughter who once wished for rain.* Rafael had pushed the bread around his bowl and eaten it anyway, because hunger is honest even when everything else isn't.

Three days since the Archive of Souls. Three days since he had stood among those thousands of jars, each labeled in a hand too neat for the horror of its contents, and felt something in him stop being negotiable. He knew where Odell's soul sat now — Shelf 114, Row 9, Jar 3,317, filed under V for *Vane, Odell (outstanding)*. He had memorized it the way you memorize the face of someone you've wronged.

What he did not know was how to get it out.

The archive was guarded by things that were mostly paperwork. Not figures of ash and ledger-paper like the collectors who had taken Odell — those had been the court's muscle, blunt instruments. These were subtler: doors that required signatures, corridors that rearranged themselves according to entries in books Rafael couldn't see, a stairwell that asked him, in a voice like a dry quill, to state his business and then wrote his answer down somewhere before letting him pass. He had tried three routes to Shelf 114. Each time, by the time he was two turns away, the way ahead had been quietly closed — a door bricked over, a corridor "under audit," a bridge of black stone folded up like a fan.

Someone was reading his intentions faster than he could form them.

Which was why, when the summons came, he was almost relieved.

* * *

It arrived as a letter, which in the under-court meant it arrived as a small gray man made of folded paper who unfolded himself on Rafael's cot, cleared a papery throat, and said, "Her Stewardship requests the pleasure of your company," in a voice like a receipt being torn.

"Does she," Rafael said. "And if I decline?"

The paper man consulted his own sleeve. "Then she requests the displeasure of your company, which is less pleasant for everyone involved, particularly you."

"Well. When you put it that way."

He followed the little herald through corridors he was beginning to know too well — past petitioners sleeping in alcoves, trading dreams for bread; past a fountain that didn't flow with water but with slow, silver words, prayers that had pooled somewhere and never drained; past a wall of small bells that rang, very softly, whenever anyone in the court lied. The bells rang constantly. Rafael had stopped noticing after the first day. He noticed them now, and made himself stop again, because a man walking toward a trap should not look like a man who has just remembered where all the tripwires are.

Veyra received him in a room that was, Rafael thought, the most honest space in the entire under-mountain: an office. Everything else here was cathedral or cavern or archive, built to make you feel small before something eternal. But this room had a desk. It had inkpots and a blotter worn smooth by decades of elbows. It had a window — an actual window, though what it looked out on was a wall of the mountain's root, veined with pale light — and it had a chair across from the desk that was comfortable in the specific way of chairs meant to make you stay longer than you wanted.

Veyra herself was smaller than Rafael had imagined. He had pictured the court's mortal steward as something towering, a woman carved from contract-paper and cold iron. Instead she was a compact, gray-eyed woman of perhaps fifty, dressed plainly, with ink stains on her fingers that Rafael recognized with a lurch of kinship — the stains of someone who had spent her whole life keeping accounts. She looked tired in the way that only people doing exactly what they chose to do look tired.

"Rafael Brightwater," she said. "Junior ledger-keeper. Guild of Impossible Things." She smiled without warmth, but not without humor. "You have no idea how many years I've waited for one of you to come down here. We used to get guild auditors every decade or so. Then nothing, for sixty years. I'd begun to think you'd all forgotten us. Which, given your profession, would have been fitting."

"We didn't forget," Rafael said. "We just preferred not to think about you."

"Oh, I know. Thinking about us requires admitting we exist, and admitting we exist requires admitting you owe us." She gestured to the chair. "Sit. Would you like tea? It's paid for out of my own account, so you can drink it with a clean conscience. Those are rare down here. Clean consciences. I keep a shelf of them."

Against his better judgment — against several of his better judgments, lined up and objecting — Rafael sat. "You know why I'm here."

"I know you've been to the archive three times." She poured tea from a pot that steamed with the smell of something green and growing, impossibly, this far underground. "I know you've looked at Shelf 114 more often than any visitor in the last century. I know you've been asking the archivist-clerks about release procedures for soul-jars, and I know they've been telling you the same thing they tell everyone: that jars may only be opened by the Keeper, upon full satisfaction of the underlying debt." She set the cup in front of him. "I also know that yesterday morning you asked a very interesting question. You asked whether a debt could be satisfied by a substitute asset."

Rafael kept his face still. Inside, he was doing rapid, unflattering arithmetic. He had asked that question of a minor clerk, a gray little creature of receipts, in what he'd thought was a quiet corner. Either the clerk had reported him within the hour, or —

"You're wondering how I know," Veyra said, reading him with the ease of long practice. "The answer is disappointingly simple. Nothing in this court is private. Every conversation held within these walls is, technically, a transaction — words exchanged for words — and transactions generate records. I am the keeper of records." She spread her hands. "I don't spy on anyone, Master Brightwater. I simply never throw anything away."

"That must be a heavy way to live."

"It's a profitable way to live. That's better." She drank her own tea and studied him over the rim. "So. Let's not waste each other's time, because time down here is billable. You came to steal Odell Vane's soul. You cannot steal it. The archive will not permit it, the guards will not permit it, and — this is the part I want you to hear clearly — *the debt itself* will not permit it. A soul-jar isn't a lockbox, Master Brightwater. It's a balance. As long as Vane's account shows outstanding, that jar stays shut, and no amount of cleverness changes arithmetic."

"I've heard arithmetic is negotiable down here."

"Arithmetic is *everything* down here. That's rather the point." She leaned forward. "But debts can be settled. Transferred. Forgiven, by the proper authority. And I am — short of the King itself — the proper authority."

The bells on the distant wall rang softly. Rafael wondered idly whether they rang for her.

"And what," he said carefully, "would the proper authority want in exchange?"

"Ah. Now we're negotiating." Veyra looked genuinely pleased, the way a fisherman looks pleased when the line finally goes taut. "Before I name my price, let me tell you what I know about your companion, so you understand that I'm not guessing."

The tea went cold in Rafael's mouth, though it hadn't touched his lips.

"A bell-ringer," Veyra continued, pleasantly. "Serah. Who served in this court for six years as an acolyte of the bronze, until she died — officially — of a fever in the upper world, and was buried, officially, under a stone with someone else's name, and has been living, unofficially, as a free woman ever since. Do you know what we call that, in the ledger? We call it *fraud against the court*. We call it an outstanding balance of six years' service, plus penalties. We call it," she said, and her voice lost none of its pleasantness and somehow gained weight instead, "*collectible*."

"She's paid enough," Rafael said. "She gave this place six years of her life."

"She gave six years of service under contract, then stole herself back. Those are different things. You of all people should appreciate the distinction — you keep ledgers." Veyra set down her cup. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so attend. Bring me Serah. Deliver her to the court, willingly, to complete her term of service — and I will mark Odell Vane's account *satisfied*, open his jar with my own hand, and give you passage out with his soul under your arm. No tricks. No fine print. I'll write the terms myself, in front of you, and you may keep the page."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I find her anyway. I have been finding people in this mountain for thirty years, Master Brightwater. She is hiding in a place where hiding is impossible, among creatures whose entire nature is the pursuit of what is owed. The only question your refusal answers is whether she comes to me dragged or walking — and whether your old master rots on Shelf 114 while you watch." She folded her hands. "I'm told he still breathes, up there. Empty. Like a house with the furniture gone. How long does a body last like that, do you think? A year? Two? Long enough for you to live with it?"

Rafael looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. Under the table, his heart was hammering out something frantic, but his hands had apparently decided, on their own, to lie.

Because that was the thing Veyra didn't know — the thing he had to make sure she went on not knowing. Serah wasn't hiding. Serah was waiting for him at the edge of the petitioner camps with a plan of her own, a plan that involved the toll-god's old road and a forged writ he'd spent two nights scratching out, a plan that would put them both at Shelf 114 tomorrow night when the archive's clerks changed shift and the audit-bells went quiet. If he refused Veyra outright, she would tighten her search tonight instead of next week. If he accepted, she would believe she had all the pieces in her hands — and a steward holding every card stops looking for the ones missing from the deck.

It was the most cold-blooded thought Rafael Brightwater had ever had, and it fit in his head with the horrible comfort of a key in a lock.

"All right," he said.

Veyra raised an eyebrow. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. I came down here for Odell. I owe him — owed him — more than I can say, and I've already cost him everything by being slow and careful and righteous." He heard his own voice go flat and bitter, and was grateful for it; grief makes excellent camouflage. "I won't lose him to save someone who lied to me from the moment she walked back into my life."

Something flickered behind Veyra's gray eyes — not belief, exactly, but the recognition of a shape that looked like belief. "She doesn't know you're here?"

"She thinks I came to negotiate. She doesn't know about the offer." True, true, and true; the far wall of bells stayed silent, and Rafael felt a small, shameful thrill at hearing the quiet.

"No," Veyra mused. "People who feel betrayed are much more reliable than people who feel loyal. Loyalty wavers. Betrayal hardens." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her — real paper, pale, not the gray flesh-stuff of the court's lesser documents — and began to write in a quick, beautiful hand. "Terms. One: the acolyte Serah, delivered to the Court of Accounts within six days, walking and willing. Two: upon delivery, the account of Odell Vane is marked satisfied in full, all penalties waived. Three: safe passage out of the under-court for yourself and the released soul, unmolested by collectors, clerks, or customs." She signed it — *Veyra, Steward of the Deep Court, Keeper of All Outstanding Things* — and slid it across the desk with a pen.

"Sign," she said. "A bargain unwritten is just a wish, and wishes are worthless down here. Ask anyone."

Rafael took the pen. His signature, he reflected, was about to become the most expensive four letters of his life — and possibly the cheapest, depending on how the next six days went. He wrote *Rafael Brightwater* in the careful hand Odell had drilled into him a decade ago, and watched Veyra take the page, blow it dry, and file it away in a drawer that clicked shut with the sound of a small door locking on a small piece of his soul.

"Six days," she said. "After that, the offer expires, the search begins in earnest, and I'm afraid your master's jar gathers interest. Interest on souls compounds, Master Brightwater. It's very ugly."

"I understand."

"One more thing." She rose, and for a moment, standing, she seemed taller than she had — or perhaps it was only the light off the root-veined window going dim. "You're wondering whether I mean to keep my word. Everyone wonders. So let me tell you the truth, since truth costs me nothing: I always keep my word. It's the only currency I have that never devalues. Whatever else happens — whatever you do, whatever she does — the terms of that page will be honored to the letter." She smiled, and this time there was warmth in it, which was somehow worse. "To the letter, Master Brightwater. I'd advise you to read yours very closely before you act on it."

* * *

He walked back through the corridors slowly, letting his shoulders slump, letting his face do the work of a man who had sold something precious and knew it. Petitioners watched him pass with the flat sympathy of the long-suffering. Somewhere a bell rang; somewhere else, three more answered it, and he couldn't tell if they were ringing for lies or for truths, and he was too tired to care.

In the shadow of an aqueduct that carried not water but names — he could hear them trickling, syllable by syllable, into some reservoir below — he stopped, checked twice that nothing papery was following him, and took out the scrap of birchbark he'd been carrying for three days, the one with the map of the archive's blind corridors scratched in charcoal.

Six days. Veyra had given him six days, believing she had bought his obedience and, with it, the location of a woman who trusted him.

He had five nights to prove her wrong — to reach Shelf 114, Row 9, Jar 3,317, crack a balance that could not be cracked, and get out before the Keeper of All Outstanding Things discovered that her newest debtor had just written the largest entry of all into her perfect books.

He was halfway through folding the birchbark away when he saw it: tucked into the aqueduct's stone seam, a fold of gray paper, unfolded and refolded many times, left deliberately where a passing man would look.

He picked it up with numb fingers. In handwriting he knew better than his own — handwriting he had watched bend over candlelit ledgers for years, handwriting that had taught him his letters — were three words:

*They know. Run.*

Odell's hand. Odell, whose body breathed empty in a bed above, whose soul sat in a jar on Shelf 114.

Odell, who could not possibly have written anything at all.
