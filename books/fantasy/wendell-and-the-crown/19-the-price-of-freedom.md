## 19. The Price of Freedom

The burning of the ledger did not end so much as it *continued* — a slow unraveling that went on for hours after the last page curled to ash, as though the book had been only the visible edge of something far larger, and everything behind it was now coming loose.

Rafael stood in the great hall of the under-court and watched freedom happen.

It was not a quiet thing. When the chains shattered — and they shattered all at once, a sound like a thousand bells dropped down a well — the god's court began to come apart with them. Walls of black stone softened into shadow and then into nothing. The archive of souls burst open like a granary in a storm, and the souls poured out, thousands upon thousands of them, rising through the collapsing ceiling like starlings startled from a roost. Rafael had expected weeping, perhaps. Wailing. What he got instead was laughter — thin, rusty, disused laughter, the sound of people remembering they had voices.

"They're going home," Serah said beside him. Her hand found his. It was cold; the priestess-bonds had drained her badly, and there were marks on her wrists that would take more than ointment to fade. But her grip was strong. "Or wherever home is, now."

"Does anyone know where that is?"

"No," she said. "I think that's rather the point."

Above them — or what passed for above, in a place where *down* had been a direction for so long that up had grown embarrassed about itself — the Deep King rose. Rafael had seen the god bound: vast and weary, folded into its chains like an old man into a too-small chair. Unbound was another thing entirely. Unbound, the god filled the sky of the dying court the way weather fills a valley, and Rafael's knees informed him, without consultation, that they would very much like to bend.

He kept standing. It seemed important.

The god looked down at him — or he felt himself looked at, which amounted to the same thing — and then it turned away, and began to leave. Not walking. *Departing*, the way a season departs. And as it went, the other gods went with it, the small ones and the strange ones, the god of thresholds from the tollhouse and the rain-gods and the memory-healers and things Rafael had no names for, streaming out through the mountain's roots like light through a cracked door.

And Rafael understood, watching them go, what they were leaving behind.

They were leaving the prayers.

* * *

He thought about it properly on the climb back up, because there was nothing else to do for six days of climbing except think, and his legs were too tired to interrupt him.

Every prayer ever marked *answered* in the guild's books had been answered by contract. A bargain struck, a debt incurred, a soul or a year or a memory pledged against rain, or healing, or luck. The gods had been bound to answer — that was the whole rotten architecture of it, the thing Veyra had built her stewardship on, the thing Odell had renewed with his signature and paid for with his self. Bound to answer, whether they wished to or not. Whether the answer drowned a village or emptied a mind.

Now they were free. And free things could say no.

"Some prayers will go unanswered forever," Rafael said aloud, on the fourth day, to the tunnel at large. His voice echoed in a way that suggested the mountain was already forgetting how to be a court and reverting to being merely a hole in the ground.

Serah walked half a step ahead, one hand trailing the wall. "Yes."

"You say that very calmly."

"I rang bells for eleven years, Rafe." She didn't turn around. "Do you know how many people came to the tower asking me to pray for them? Hundreds. And I prayed, and sometimes it worked and sometimes it didn't, and everyone always wanted to know why. As if there were a reason. As if the universe were keeping accounts." She laughed, short and unamused. "There *were* accounts. That was the whole problem. Now there aren't. Some prayers won't be answered because nobody owes anybody anything anymore."

"And you think that's better?"

She stopped then, and turned, and in the grey light of the tunnel her face was tired and fierce and beautiful in the way that truths are beautiful, which is to say uncomfortably. "I think," she said, "that a gift isn't a gift if it can be collected. I think a god that answers because it's chained is just a very large clerk. And I think—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—I think I spent years being part of a machine that ate grandmothers' grandchildren, and I would rather live in a world where my prayers might fail than one where they always succeed at someone else's cost."

Rafael considered this. He was a ledger-keeper by training and temperament, and some traitorous corner of his mind kept trying to draft columns — *benefits, costs, net position* — and he kept having to strike them out.

"All right," he said finally. "But when someone prays for rain next summer and doesn't get it, and their crops fail, and they come to the guild — to whatever's left of the guild — wanting to know why, I'd like you to be the one who explains it."

"That's cowardice dressed as delegation."

"Yes," Rafael agreed. "I'm told it's a specialty of junior ledger-keepers."

That earned him half a smile, which on the sixth day of climbing, in the dark, with ash still in her hair, he counted as a fortune beyond entry in any book.

* * *

The guild, when they emerged blinking into daylight above the mountain town of Hollowmere, was in exactly the state Rafael had predicted: besieged.

Not by collectors — the collectors had gone to paper and smoke along with Veyra, and good riddance, though Rafael found he could not quite enjoy it; Veyra had been a monster, but she had been a monster made by the system, shaped by it, filed neatly into it, and there was a version of events in which Rafael Brightwater became her, given thirty years and a desk. No, the guild was besieged by petitioners. Hundreds of them. They had heard — word traveled strangely fast when the world's rules changed; Rafael suspected even rumor had been freed from some old contract — and they had come demanding answers.

Why had the rain stopped answering? Why had the healing-shrine gone cold? Why had old Marta's luck, which had held steady for forty years like a well-tended fire, suddenly guttered out?

Master Quillon and the council stood on the guild steps making noises about transition periods and interim arrangements, and the crowd made noises back, and none of the noises were kind.

Rafael shouldered through it all without stopping. He had one purpose, and it was waiting for him in the vault beneath the guildhall, where the guild kept the things it dared not look at.

Odell Vane's body had been breathing for three weeks. That was the horror of the collectors' work — they took the soul and left the machinery running, and so Odell sat in a chair in the vault, upright, eyes open, chest rising and falling with terrible patience, while his soul sat in a jar two hundred miles underground filed between a farmer who'd traded his daughter's laughter for a good harvest and a child who'd been collateral on a debt she was born owing.

Rafael carried the jar up out of the dark himself. He had refused help twice, once politely and once less so.

The soul-jar was smaller than he remembered. In the under-court's archive it had seemed obscene — a whole man, a whole infuriating, brilliant, cowardly, beloved man, reduced to an inventory item, shelf 4,417, lot 88. Here, in the daylight of the vault, cradled against Rafael's chest, it seemed impossibly small. Like carrying a candle. Like carrying an egg.

"He'll wake diminished," Serah had warned him, back in the court, when he'd found the shelf. "Souls that spend time in jars... they lose things. Edges. The parts that were sharp."

"He's eighty-one," Rafael had said. "How sharp can he get?"

But he hadn't believed it, quite. He had carried the jar up six days of tunnel believing that he would hand it back and Odell would be Odell — would adjust his spectacles, make some dry remark about Rafael's handwriting, and resume being the fixed point around which Rafael's whole life had orbited.

* * *

They opened the jar in Odell's own study, because it seemed right. Because if a man must wake from being an invoice, let him wake among his own books.

Serah unstopped the jar. There was no flash, no drama. The soul left it the way warmth leaves a cup of tea — invisibly, and toward the person waiting for it.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then Odell blinked.

Then Odell sneezed, which was so profoundly ordinary that Rafael laughed, and the laugh came out wet and embarrassing, and he decided not to care.

"Rafael." The old man's voice was hoarse from disuse. He looked around the study as though confirming each object individually — the globe with the missing northern kingdoms, the teacup full of pens, the portrait of some guildmaster dead four centuries whom Odell claimed to have known personally (a claim Rafael had never been able to disprove and had stopped trying to). "You're standing in my study holding an empty jar. Either I've died and this is a very poorly managed afterlife, or—"

"You're alive," Rafael said. "You're alive, Master Vane. It's over. The debts are voided. All of them."

Something moved across Odell's face — relief, Rafael expected, or gratitude. What came instead was slower and sadder. The old man raised his hands and looked at them, turning them over, and Rafael saw it then, the thing Serah had promised: the edges gone. Not the mind — the mind was there, sharp as ever behind the spectacles. But something *smaller*. Something that had once filled the room.

"I remember the court," Odell said quietly. "I remember the jar. I remember..." He frowned, groping. "I used to know a song. My mother's. Four lines, about the river in spring. I've known it my whole life." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and clear. "It's gone, Rafael. I can feel the shape of where it was."

"The gods took pieces of themselves when they left," Serah said gently. "Or maybe the jar did. Nobody knows yet. Maybe it comes back."

"And maybe it doesn't." Odell set his hands flat on the desk, the gesture of a man bracing for bad figures. "Tell me the rest. All of it. Don't arrange it kindly."

So Rafael told him. The ledger burned. The contracts voided. The guild's power — loaned, all of it, every impossible trade the guild had ever brokered — gone with the gods who had lent it. Bottled grief unstoppered and returned to its owners. Borrowed luck called home. The guild dissolving, the council already drafting the dissolution papers with the enthusiasm of men who had never once in their lives drafted anything honest and were finding it exhilarating.

"And the prayers?" Odell asked, when he finished. "The ones that will never be answered now? The farmers, the sick, the frightened? They'll come here looking for the guild that promised—"

"The guild never promised," Rafael said. "The guild *collected*. That's what we did, Master Vane. We just wrote it in such fine script that nobody read the terms."

Odell was silent a long moment. Then he did the last thing Rafael expected, which was to smile — a small, crooked, genuinely amused smile, the first true one Rafael had seen from him since before the collectors came.

"There it is," the old man said. "There's the spine. Three months ago you'd have apologized to me for the weather." He pushed himself up from the desk, unsteady, and Rafael moved to help him and was waved off with a flick of fingers that still knew exactly how much help they wanted, which was none. "Diminished," Odell murmured, testing the word. "Mortal. Missing a song." He looked at Rafael over the spectacles. "Forgiven?"

"You don't need my forgiveness."

"No. But I'd like it anyway. It's cheaper than absolution and better company." He extended his hand, and Rafael took it, and the grip was thinner than it had been, an old branch instead of an oak limb, but it was *there*, and it was his mentor's, and Rafael held on perhaps a moment longer than dignity strictly allowed.

"For what it's worth," Odell said, "you did the one thing I never could. I signed the renewal because I couldn't bear to watch the guild shrink. You burned the whole thing rather than wear its crown." He snorted. "I trained you to keep accounts, boy. Apparently I also taught you which ones to close."

* * *

They stayed three days in Hollowmere, because Odell needed tending and because Rafael, despite everything, could not simply walk past a petition queue without stopping.

He stopped at every one. He had no power to offer — that was rather the entire point — but he had the truth, and it turned out the truth was worth something, though less than anyone liked. He explained to the farmer whose rain-prayer would now go wherever unanswered prayers go that no one owed him rain, and no one ever really had; that the rain he'd gotten for forty years had been bought with someone else's misfortune, filed somewhere, accruing interest. The farmer cursed him, spat near his boots, and then, at the gate, turned back and asked, in a smaller voice, what a man was supposed to do instead.

"Ask anyway," Rafael said. "Ask your neighbors. Ask the sky. Just don't expect a receipt."

On the third evening, Serah found him on the guildhouse roof, where he'd gone to be alone with a thought that wouldn't finish itself.

"Odell's packing," she said, sitting down beside him. "He says he's going to the coast. Says he wants to hear the sea before he forgets how songs go entirely. He also says—" she cleared her throat, and did a passable imitation of the old man's dry rasp, "—'tell the boy that the blank ledger he's hoarding is the only honest book the guild ever produced, and that he'd better write something in it eventually, because a blank page is just pride wearing modesty's coat.'"

Rafael huffed. "He's diminished, all right. Diminished straight into wisdom."

"It happens," Serah said. "Rarely. Usually the other way."

Below them, the town lamps were coming on one by one, and somewhere a child was ringing the small bell in the market square for no reason at all, which children do, and which no god had commanded, and which answered nothing and no one, and was perfect.

"One more thing," Serah said. She was quiet a moment. "The bell tower. Mine. I rang it today, before I came up. First time since—" She shook her head. "It sounded different. Smaller. Or maybe I'm bigger. But Rafe — I prayed while I rang. Just a little one. Nothing important. And I don't know if anyone heard." She looked at him, and the lamplight caught the marks on her wrists, fading now, almost gone. "And it was the first prayer I've ever said that wasn't a transaction. It felt like learning to walk again."

"What did you pray for?"

She smiled and leaned her head against his shoulder. "Nothing owed," she said. "Nothing owed to us. I'm practicing."

Rafael put his arm around her and looked out at the darkening world — freer, poorer, stranger, its old bargains all torn up, its gods scattered like seeds on wind — and in his satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, the blank ledger waited with its first page still empty.

Except that tomorrow, he supposed, it wouldn't be.

Because at dawn they were due in Bellhollow, where the guild's final chapter waited to be written — and where, three days prior, a letter had arrived bearing no seal, no signature, and a single line in handwriting Rafael knew better than his own:

*Come see what the bells are saying now.*
