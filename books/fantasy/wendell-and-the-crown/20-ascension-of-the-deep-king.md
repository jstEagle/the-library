## 20. Ascension of the Deep King

I had forgotten what sky looked like.

That is a strange sentence for a god to write, and stranger still to think, but truth is truth even when it is embarrassing. For nine hundred years my ceiling had been stone — first the mountain's roots, then the vaulted dark of the under-court, then the low green glow of the archive where the souls were kept like jars of preserves. I remembered sky the way a man remembers his mother's face: with love, and with the creeping suspicion that memory has improved upon the original.

Then Rafael Brightwater tore a ledger in half, and the roof of my world came off.

Not all at once. First the chains — those went with a sound I can only describe as a bell being unmade, every link singing its own small funeral as it fell away from me. Then the contracts, centuries of them, peeling from my skin like wet paper. Then the court itself, which had grown around me the way a pearl grows around a wound, layer upon layer of petition-halls and tollhouses and queue-lines that stretched for miles through the dark — all of it dissolving now into dust and light and the smell, impossibly, of rain.

And then, above me, where the mountain's heart had been, there was a hole.

Through it I could see stars.

I will confess something here, in whatever record survives this account, if any record survives at all: I sat down. A god, older than the mountain that had caged me, sat down on the rubble of his own prison and wept at the sight of stars. If you find this undignified, I invite you to spend nine hundred years answering prayers out of obligation rather than love, and then see how dignified *you* feel when it ends.

The souls went first. Of course they did. They poured upward through the hole like a flock of birds released from a barn — thousands of them, tens of thousands, Odell Vane among them somewhere, and the children too, the little ones whose soul-jars I had never been able to open no matter how I strained against my chains. They streamed past me as they rose. Some paused. One — a girl who had died owed to the court before she was old enough to have done anything worth owing for — reached out and touched my cheek with a hand made entirely of light and forgiveness, and said, "You were down here too, weren't you?"

"Yes," I said.

"That's sad," she said, with the blunt mercy of children, and then she rose away and did not look back, because the dead are wiser than the living about when to stop looking back.

I watched until the last of them was gone. Then I stood, and shook the mountain's dust from my shoulders — an operation that took some time, as I was, at that moment, roughly the size of the mountain — and began to rise.

* * *

Dissolving the under-court was not difficult. It had only ever been held together by the debts, the way a spiderweb is held together by tension; cut the tension and the whole thing sighs into nothing. The tollhouse of thresholds went first, its keeper-god — a minor thing named Harrow, all hinges and squinting eyes — stepping free of his post with the expression of a clerk who has just been told the office is closing forever. He bowed to me. I waved him off. There would be time enough for bowing later, or better yet, never.

The petitioners' camps dissolved more slowly. Mortals do not scatter as easily as gods; they have belongings, and grievances, and opinions. Hundreds of them stood blinking in the collapsing halls, holding their numbered tickets for audiences that would now never come, and I felt a pang about that — genuine regret, mind you, not the performative sorrow of a king dismissing his court. These people had walked miles. Some had traded years for their place in line. Years! Given freely to a queue!

So I did what I could, which is what any honest power does. As the halls came apart around them, I let the ledger's final burning reach into each of their pockets and purses and hearts, and I returned what had been paid. The woman who had traded ten years of her life got them back — though I noticed, with interest, that she spent the first returned year simply sitting down and laughing, which struck me as an excellent use of it. The man who had given up his memory of his brother's face found it waiting behind his eyes where it had always been, and he wept so hard he frightened the people near him, and then they wept too, and soon there was a great deal of weeping and laughing happening simultaneously throughout the ruins of my kingdom, which is, I have since learned, how most good news arrives among mortals.

There were some things I could not return. Debts already collected and spent — those were gone, eaten by the old system the way a fire eats wood, leaving nothing but the shape of the burning. To those petitioners I could offer only an apology, delivered not in words but in something better: the sudden, inexplicable certainty, planted gently in each of their hearts, that they had been wronged by something larger than themselves, and that the wrong was over, and that it had not been their fault. It is astonishing, I have found, how much a mortal can carry if they know it was not their fault.

By the time I reached the surface, the mountain was just a mountain again. Good. It had never wanted to be anything else.

* * *

They were waiting at the root-gates — the two of them, the ledger-keeper and the runaway priestess, standing in the trampled grass where the queue-lines used to begin. Rafael Brightwater had ash on his face and a look in his eyes that I recognized, because I had seen it in mirrors of dark water for nine centuries: the look of someone who has done an enormous thing and has not yet begun to feel the weight of it. That feeling comes later. It always comes later. I intended to say something about that.

Serah stood beside him with her hand on his arm, and I noted — gods notice everything; it is our curse and occasionally our entertainment — that her hand was not gripping but resting. That is the difference between holding on and holding, and it is the entire secret of love, though mortals insist on taking centuries to discover it.

I made myself small as I approached. This is a courtesy gods extend to mortals; appearing in one's full aspect tends to cause fainting, religious movements, and in one memorable case in my youth, an entire village deciding to invent taxation. So I folded myself down to the size of a tall man, wore the shape of an old traveler in a grey cloak, and walked the last hundred yards on feet that pressed the grass only as much as any feet should.

Rafael saw through it immediately, of course. Ledger-keepers always do. You cannot fool a man who reads what things truly owe.

"You're smaller," he said.

"I'm polite," I said.

Serah laughed — a short, startled laugh, the kind that escapes before fear can catch it — and then pressed her hand over her mouth, because she had served in my court and knew that laughing at the Deep King was traditionally punished by a century of scrubbing the archive floors. "Old habits," she managed.

"Serah of the Bell Tower," I said, and watched her flinch. "Yes, I know your name. I knew it before you fled. I want you to hear this plainly, because I suspect you have been carrying it like a stone: you owe me nothing. You never did. Your debt of service was the foulest entry in the foulest book ever kept, and I am glad — *glad* — that you ran from it. I only wish I could have run with you."

She stared at me. Somewhere behind her eyes, a weight shifted.

"You knew," she said. "All those years. You knew what Veyra was doing with the service-debts."

"I knew everything that happened in my court," I said, "and could prevent none of it. That is what a contract does, child. It does not merely bind your hands. It ties them behind your back and then makes you watch." I let that sit in the air between us, because some truths need room. "Veyra is ash now. I did not unmake her. She was mostly paper by the end — she had sold so many pieces of herself to keep her position that when the book burned, there was very little left to burn. But I did not save her either, and I will not pretend I mourned."

Rafael shifted. "The souls," he said. "Are they—"

"Free. All of them. Rising, most of them, toward whatever waits above — and before you ask, no, I don't know what waits above. Gods don't get told that sort of thing. We're staff, not management." I smiled, and saw Rafael decide, visibly, whether it was safe to smile back. He decided yes. Wise boy. "Your master Odell is among them. Diminished — the collectors took the sharp edges of him, the ambition, the part that signed what he signed. What wakes will be simpler than the man you knew. Simpler, and I think, kinder. Whether that is a mercy or a theft, I leave to you to decide. Both answers are true."

Rafael looked down at his hands. "He'll remember what he did?"

"He'll remember everything except the ability to excuse it. Yes."

"Good," Rafael said quietly. And then, with the gentle stubbornness I had already come to expect from him: "We'll take care of him."

"I know," I said. "It's in the ledger."

"There is no ledger," Serah said. "You burned it. We *watched* it burn."

"I know," I said again. "It's still in the ledger. That's rather the point of what you did, Brightwater. You didn't destroy the accounting of the world. You destroyed the *collectors*. The accounts themselves — kindness given, kindness owed, kindness returned — those go on. They're the oldest ledger there is, and nobody keeps it, and everybody writes in it whether they mean to or not." I tapped his chest once, lightly, with one finger. "You've been writing in it all along. You're simply doing it without ink now."

* * *

We walked a while, the three of us, out along the mountainside where the petitioner-camps had been. Freed folk were drifting down the road toward the valleys, dazed and light, stopping every few steps to touch things — trees, stones, each other's faces — as if confirming the world still permitted touching. A child was teaching her grandmother to whistle. The grandmother had traded her whistling to the court eleven years ago for her husband's safe return from a war, and had gotten neither the whistling nor, in the end, the husband, and now she stood on the mountainside making small broken sounds that grew steadily less broken, while the child giggled and corrected her technique.

I stopped to listen. Rafael and Serah stopped with me, because when a god stops, mortals tend to stop too, though I appreciated that Rafael at least pretended he'd just wanted to rest.

"Why did you answer prayers?" he asked suddenly. "Before. Before the contracts. You said — in the throne hall, you said you used to do it out of love. Why?"

What a question. Nine hundred years of philosophers and priests had asked me variations of it, usually while prostrate, which rather ruined the conversation. This boy asked it standing in the grass, with ash on his face, as if asking a farmer why he plants.

"Because I could," I said. "That's the whole of it. Because the rain needed somewhere to fall and the widow needed rain, and I happened to be standing in between. Because a prayer is a hand reaching up in the dark, and I was tall enough to reach back. There's no grand theology in it, Brightwater. The grand theology came later, when men decided that reaching must be purchased, and built tollhouses on the dark." I shrugged, an act requiring some care even at reduced size. "Love is not mysterious. It is only generous, and generosity frightens people, so they build systems around it to slow it down."

"And now?" Serah asked. Her voice was careful. "Now that you're free. Will you still answer? Or—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. The villages we had all three passed through on the way down — the drowning rain, the healing that ate memories — hung unspoken in the mountain air.

I turned to face her fully, and let the traveller-shape thin, just slightly, so she could see what spoke to her. Not the full aspect. Never that, not to these two. But enough.

"Now," I said, "I will answer sometimes. When I am passing. When it costs me nothing but the trouble of stopping. When the hand reaching up happens to be near my own." I held up a finger before hope could bloom too brightly in either of their faces. "Understand what freedom means, both of you, because you bought it for everyone and you deserve to know what you bought. Freedom cuts both ways. It always has. The gods are free of the bargains — and the bargains were cruel, and I do not regret their ending, not for one heartbeat. But the bargains were also *promises*. Every answered prayer in those books was a promise kept. Now there are no promises. A farmer may pray for rain and receive it, or receive sun, or receive nothing at all, and no ledger anywhere will tell him why. The world has just become enormously less reliable."

"It was reliable before?" Rafael said. "People drowned in answered prayers."

"They drowned in *managed* prayers," I said. "Managed by clerks. At least clerks are consistent." I sighed, and the wind on the mountainside sighed with me, which is the sort of thing that happens around gods even when we wish it wouldn't. "No. You're right, and I'm being an old fool mourning my own cage's furniture. The world before was a machine that ground children into invoices. The world now is weather. Weather kills people too. But weather also — " I gestured at the mountainside, at the whistling grandmother, at the light coming gold and unhurried through the pines — "weather does *this*. Unpromised. Unearned. Nobody's fault and nobody's gift. That is what you've made, Brightwater. A world where good things happen for no reason. It will break hearts. It already breaks mine, a little, every hour. And it is infinitely better than what stood here yesterday."

Serah had gone quiet in the way of someone assembling a question from fragile parts. When it came, it came softly.

"What will you do? Now. Where does a freed god go?"

"Ah," I said. "Now *that* is a question worthy of a priestess. Even a retired one." I looked up, past the pines, past the peak, to where the evening's first star was considering whether to come out. "Up. That's the direction, isn't it? Down was always my direction — down into the earth, down into the dark, down into other people's debts. I have been down for nine hundred years. I find I have a curiosity about up." I smiled at them both. "There are others like me, you know. Old ones, scattered, half-asleep under hills and rivers and the roots of very patient trees. Some of them are waking now that the books are burned. I intend to go find them. We are going to have a long conversation about the last thousand years, and it is going to be either a council or a catastrophe, and frankly at my age one hardly distinguishes."

* * *

But before I went — before the mountain and the road and the two of them fell away below me — I stopped, and turned back, and did the thing I had actually descended to the surface to do.

I thanked him.

Not with treasure. I had no treasure; the court was dust. Not with power; he had refused the crown, and I respected the refusal more than any gift I might have forced upon him. Not even with words, at first — words are cheap, and gods' words cheapest of all, being weighted with awe whether we intend it or no.

Instead I knelt.

A god, kneeling, in the trampled grass before a junior ledger-keeper with ash on his face and his beloved's hand in his. I knelt the way the widow kneels at the graveside, the way the soldier kneels when the war ends and no one is watching. And Rafael Brightwater — bless him, bless his stubborn bookkeeping heart — immediately tried to pull me back up.

"Don't — please don't — that's not — people don't kneel to *me*, that's completely backwards—"

"People," I said, remaining exactly where I was, "kneel to power. I am not kneeling to power. I am kneeling to a man who was offered everything and took only what was his." I looked up at him from the grass, and let him see, for one unguarded moment, the whole of what I was — vast and weary and old beyond his counting, and lighter than I had been in nine centuries. "Thank you, Rafael Brightwater. Not on behalf of the gods. On behalf of everyone who ever prayed into the dark and deserved better than an invoice. Thank you."

He had gone red to the ears. Serah was crying and pretending not to, badly.

"One warning," I said, rising at last, brushing grass from my knees with great dignity, "and then I'll be gone, and you can both collapse dramatically, which you've clearly earned. Freedom cuts both ways — I said that, and I meant it, and here is the edge of the blade you'll feel first. You voided every debt. Every one. Including the debts *owed to you*. The guild is finished; its power is gone; the world no longer owes Rafael Brightwater anything at all. No pension. No gratitude from the powerful. Half the people whose chains you broke will blame you within a year, because freed prisoners often miss their cages and resent the locksmith. You will be poor, and obscure, and occasionally cursed by strangers." I grinned, and my grin, even at traveller-size, showed rather too many teeth. "It is the best life anyone has ever been given. Guard it jealously."

"I will," he said. And then, because he was who he was: "Will I see you again?"

"Undoubtedly," I said. "Though perhaps not as you'd expect. Gods are poor at staying gone. We turn up in odd places — in weather, in luck, in the pause before someone decides to be kind. Look for me there." I stepped back, and let the traveller-shape fall away like a coat, and rose, and felt the sky take my weight gladly after nine hundred years of stone.

Below me, two small figures stood in the gold light, hand in hand, looking up.

I rose until the mountain was a shadow and the shadows were a map and the map was a memory, and I turned my face toward the horizon where the other sleepers waited, and I flew.

Behind me — faint, almost lost in the wind, carried up from a valley where a bell tower stood over a town that would never know how close it had come to ending — a bell rang once.

Just once. Unasked. Unowed.

I smiled, and flew faster, and did not look back again — because looking back is for the dead, and for the first time in nine hundred years, I was neither.
