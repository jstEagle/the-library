## 22. An Entry in a Blank Book

The bell over the door of Brightwater's Honest Goods had a crack in it.

Rafael had chosen it deliberately. The bell-ringer who sold it to him — a cheerful woman named Doss with flour on her apron and no idea what she was selling — swore it was worthless for anything but scrap. "Sounds like a coin dropped down a well," she'd said, and knocked half the price off, which was the only kind of bargain Rafael allowed himself these days: the honest kind, where both parties walked away slightly pleased and nobody's soul changed hands.

He hung it anyway. It rang like a coin dropped down a well, and he loved it.

"Rafael!" called Marta from the back room, where she weighed out tea leaves with the gravity of a judge sentencing a criminal. "There's a boy at the counter asking whether we sell luck."

"We don't," Rafael called back, coming through the doorway with a crate of candles on his hip. "We sell soap."

The boy — maybe ten, gap-toothed, sunburned from the fields — looked up at him with the particular hopefulness of children who have heard their grandmothers talk about the old days. "Not even a little?"

"Not even a little." Rafael set the crate down and leaned on the counter, which was scarred and honest and had never once been used to record a debt. "But I'll tell you what I do know about luck, if you've got a minute."

The boy had a minute. Children always did, in those years after; time had loosened its grip on everyone, like a fist slowly unclenching.

"Luck isn't a thing you buy," Rafael said. "It's a thing that happens when you're standing in the right place doing the right work when it comes by. So plant your seeds early, mend your fence before the storm, and be kind to people who can do nothing for you. That's three-quarters of luck right there."

"What's the other quarter?"

"No one knows. That's what makes it luck." He pressed a honey-candy into the boy's palm — not free, exactly; the boy paid with a button he'd found, and Rafael declared it fair trade, because it made them both laugh, and laughter was legal tender in his shop. "Come back when you need soap."

The bell clanked its broken well-coin sound as the boy left, and Rafael stood a moment in the doorway watching him run down the lane, and felt the old ache — the one that lived just under his ribs, shaped like a ledger — settle back into its usual quiet.

* * *

The shop had been Serah's idea, though she denied it.

They had come to Millbrook two years after the mountain emptied, following a winter of wandering that neither of them talked about much. There had been a stretch of road near Harrow Fen where every house stood dark, where prayers had gone unanswered so long that the people had simply stopped asking — and Rafael had wept there, quietly, into his collar, while Serah held his arm and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. Freedom cut both ways. The Deep King had warned him of it with the god's vast, tired voice, and Rafael had believed the warning, and believing it had not made the dark houses easier to walk past.

"You could rebuild it," Serah had said that night, by their small fire. "The guild. People would follow you. Half the world still calls you the Ledger-Breaker."

"The other half calls me the man who let the rain drown Vellin."

"The other half is wrong."

"Both halves are wrong," Rafael said. "That's rather the point. There shouldn't be halves at all. There should just be people, owing each other nothing but kindness, keeping their own accounts." He had turned his hands over, palms up, as if checking them for ink. They were clean. They had stayed clean. "I'm done keeping accounts, Serah. All of them. Even good ones."

She had studied him across the fire for a long moment — her face older now, marked at the temple where the priestess-brand had been, a pale star of scar tissue that ached in cold weather. Then she said, "So open a shop."

"A shop."

"A shop. Sell things. Candles, thread, soap, tea. Things that are what they say they are. No trades, no bargains, no impossible anything." She had smiled then, the crooked smile that had undone him since he was nineteen years old and too shy to speak to a bell-ringer. "Call it something dull. Nothing frightens people more than a dull name on a shop that sells only truth."

And so: Brightwater's Honest Goods. It was, Rafael thought most mornings as he swept the step, the finest joke he had ever told, and almost nobody got it.

* * *

Tuesdays were slow, which suited him. On Tuesdays he did the accounts.

Not ledgers — he kept no ledgers, would keep none, would burn any ledger brought into his shop within the hour (there had been three attempts; word had gotten around). But a man selling goods needed to know what he had and what it cost, and so on Tuesdays Rafael sat in the back room with a slate and chalk, and counted, and wiped the slate clean each week, beginning again from nothing.

Serah came in from the bell tower — the town had asked her to ring the evening bell, once they learned who she was, or rather once they decided it didn't matter who she had been — and found him squinting at figures.

"You're smiling at your slate," she said. "That's suspicious behavior in an accountant."

"I'm not an accountant. I'm a shopkeeper. Accountants deal in abstractions. I deal in soap."

"Mm." She hung her coat on the peg — the peg wobbled; everything in the shop was slightly imperfect, and Rafael refused to fix most of it, on the theory that perfection was how cults started — and poured herself the last of the morning tea without asking, which was her way of saying she lived here, which she did, though they had never married.

"Why haven't we married?" he asked her once, years ago, on this same question's behalf.

"Because marriage is a contract," she'd said, "and I have sworn off contracts, and so have you, and I will not begin our life together with the very thing we spent our youth destroying." Then she'd kissed him and added, "Also because you haven't asked properly, and if you ever do, ask me in a way that owes me nothing and I owe you nothing, and then yes."

He hadn't managed it yet. He was working on it. Some entries took years to write correctly.

"Odell wrote today," she said now, settling onto the stool across from him. "Third letter this season. He's teaching letters to fishermen's children in Saltmere. He says they're vicious little students and he deserves every minute of it."

"How is his memory?"

"Better some days. He remembered my name in this one." She said it lightly, but her hand wasn't quite steady on the cup, and Rafael covered it with his own. Odell Vane, who had signed away his soul to save a guild he loved, who had woken from his century of collateral diminished and mortal and forgiven — forgiven by Rafael, mostly, though some mornings forgiveness required re-signing, like a document in fading ink. The old man had chosen Saltmere because it was far from any mountain and close to the sea, and because, he wrote, "the sea keeps no records."

"He signs his letters 'no debts,'" Rafael said. "Every one. It's become his signature."

"It's a good signature."

"It's the best one I know."

* * *

In the afternoon, a traveler came in.

Rafael knew at once that she was a traveler, and more than a traveler, though she wore a plain gray cloak and carried a walking staff of no particular distinction. It was the way the light sat on her. Light behaved strangely around the freed ones — pooled, lingered, as if reluctant to leave. In the years since the burning, Rafael had crossed paths with perhaps a dozen of the old gods walking loose in the world, and each encounter had been different, and none had been what he expected. One had opened a bakery in Corvane. One had become a midwife. One had simply walked into the sea and was seen sometimes by fishermen, far out, standing on the water, watching the horizon with what they described as enormous patience.

This one browsed the shelves. She picked up a candle, smelled it, set it down. Examined the soap. Read the label on the tea — HONEST TEA, GROWN BY PEOPLE PAID FAIRLY, WHICH SHOULD NOT BE REMARKABLE BUT IS — and made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"You wrote the labels yourself," she said. Not a question.

"I did."

"They're very good. Slightly bitter. Accurate." She turned, and beneath the gray hood her eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone. Rafael did not bow. He had stopped bowing to gods the year he tore up the book, and none of them had taken offense yet; he suspected they found it restful. "You don't recognize me."

"I recognize all of you," Rafael said carefully. "That's the trouble. You're all wearing shapes you didn't used to wear. But you have the sound of the deep places about you. And you're taller than you're pretending to be."

The god smiled — a wide, weary, wonderful smile — and let the gray cloak fall away for just a moment, and the back room filled with the smell of cold stone and underground rivers, and Rafael's knees informed him they would very much like to bend, and he declined, politely.

"Your Majesty," he said. "You're a long way from the sea route. I heard you went north."

"I go where I like now. That is rather the entire point." The Deep King — though the title fit poorly now, like a coat outgrown; Rafael supposed the god was simply King of Himself, which was more than any of them had ever been — drifted along the shelves, trailing faint echoes. "I visited your guildhall, before it came down. Or what remained of it. A very small person was sweeping the steps where the doors used to be. She told me the man who broke the book had gone off to sell soap."

"She told you true."

"So I see." The god picked up the cracked bell from the counter, turned it in fingers that could have closed around mountains. "This is mine, you know. Or it was. Bronze remembers. This was cast from ore dug from my roots, in the years when my people still sang instead of signed." It rang it, once, softly — and the sound that came out was not the broken well-coin clank but something vast and low and mournful and glad, a note that passed through the floorboards and down through the earth and kept going, and somewhere far below, Rafael would have sworn, something answered.

Then the god set it down, and it was a cracked bell again, worth half price, sounding like a coin dropped down a well.

"Why visit?" Rafael asked. "Truly. If you've come to thank me again—"

"I thanked you. I meant it. I won't repeat myself; gratitude repeated becomes obligation, and I have developed opinions on obligation." The god's eyes glinted. "No. I came because I am assembling something, and I wanted to ask you first, face to face, maker to maker."

"Assembling?"

"A new court. Of a kind. Though 'court' is the wrong word — courts are where petitions are heard, and I am finished hearing petitions. More a... gathering. The freed ones drift, Rafael. We are unbound, and unbinding is glorious, and also we are lonely, and the world has forgotten how to speak to us except in fear or flattery. So some of us mean to meet. Once a season, perhaps. In no fixed place. To remember one another. To sing, possibly. To sit in a room where no one owes anyone anything, and see what grows there." The god paused. "You would not be summoned. No one will ever be summoned again. But you would be welcome. You and the bell-ringer both. She served my prison faithfully against her will, and I have never properly apologized, and I find I want to. Apologizing is new to me. I'm told it gets easier."

Rafael stood very still behind his counter, among his honest candles and his honest soap, and felt the old shape under his ribs — the ledger-shaped ache — turn over in its sleep and subside.

"I'll come," he said. "Once. To see it. I can't promise more than that."

"That," said the Deep King, "is the only kind of promise worth having." At the door, the god paused, gray cloak settling back into ordinariness, and added, almost casually: "The blank book you kept. The one from the guild archive. Do you still have it?"

"I do."

"Has it stayed blank?"

"Every page."

"Good," said the god, and the bell over the door gave its broken little clank, and the shop was empty, and the air smelled faintly of stone rivers for the rest of the day.

* * *

That night, long after Serah had gone up to bed, Rafael sat at the kitchen table with the blank book.

It was a modest object. Plain boards, good stitching, paper that had waited forty years in the guild archive for an entry that never came. He had carried it out of the burning hall himself, the only thing he saved, and he had never been entirely sure why — except that a book that had never recorded a debt seemed like a thing worth keeping, the way one keeps a seed from a tree that fell.

He opened it to the first page. The lamplight lay warm on the empty lines.

For years he had left it blank, and told himself it was principle. Tonight he admitted the truth: it was fear. Whatever he wrote in this book would matter. Books like this one always mattered. Ink was a door, and doors swung both ways, and he had spent his whole life learning what came through them.

But the god had asked. And the god had said *good*, in that voice like a landslide being gentle, when told the pages were empty — as if emptiness itself were the achievement, as if a blank page were not an absence but a held breath, waiting.

Rafael dipped his pen. Ordinary ink, bought honestly from a stationer in Corvane. He held it over the first line for a long time, listening to the house breathe, to Serah's slow sleep upstairs, to the wind moving through the valley where nothing was owed by anyone to anyone, where prayers went unanswered and children planted seeds early and mended fences before storms, and it was enough, it had always been enough, it was everything.

He wrote:

*Nothing owed. Nothing owed to us.*

He looked at it a long while. Seven words. The shortest entry of his career, and the truest, and the only one he had ever written that bound no one to anything.

Then, somewhere — far off, or very deep, or possibly inside the cracked bronze bell hanging in the dark shop next door — faintly, unmistakably, a bell answered.

Rafael smiled, capped the ink, and blew out the lamp.
