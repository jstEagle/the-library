## 1. Ink Sold by the Dram

The charms sold best at dusk, when the lamplighters came through and the honest work of the day was done and people had time to remember what they were afraid of.

Ivo Delacroix had learned that in her first year behind the stall, back when she still flinched at the word *charm* spoken aloud, as if a Warden might materialize out of the temple's whitewashed columns and drag her into the street for it. Six years later she said it the way she said *bread* or *rent*: flatly, commercially, without belief. "Blessed ink," she'd tell a nervous young wife. "One dram, mixed into your husband's tea, and he'll find his way home no matter how deep he drinks." It was water from the cistern with a pinch of ash and a drop of lamp oil, and it worked exactly as well as anything else in Vell, which is to say it worked because people needed it to.

Magic was outlawed in Vell. The selling of magic was outlawed harder. But the Temple of the Ninefold Flame held the only license in the city to traffic in the appearance of it, and Master Corvane liked to say that hope was a commodity like any other, and that the Temple was simply its most efficient distributor. He said it while wearing rings worth more than Ivo's entire debt, which was considerable.

"Two drams today," said the lamplighter, Maret, sliding his copper across the scarred wood of the counter. His hands smelled of pitch and iron. "One for my knees. One for my girl's cough."

"The knees take the amber vial," Ivo said, reaching for them without looking. She knew her stock by touch the way other scribes knew their letters. "The cough takes the blue. Don't mix them up, or he'll come home with strong legs and a worse throat."

Maret laughed, short and tired. "You ever think about selling something that actually works?"

Ivo looked up at him. He was a big man going soft around the middle, forty if he was a day, with soot ground so far into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing would ever get it out. He didn't mean anything by the question. Nobody in Vell meant anything by their questions anymore; meaning had been taxed out of the language decades ago, along with everything else the Wardens couldn't put a number on.

"Every day," she said, and gave him his change.

He went off down the colonnade, lighting lamps that would burn until the Ashen Wardens' curfew bell snuffed them, and Ivo turned back to the real work — the work that actually paid down what she owed, dram by dram, page by page.

Behind the stall, through a door locked with three keys (two of which hung on Corvane's belt), lay the Scriptorium of the Ninefold Flame. And within the Scriptorium, on a lectern older than the city's current name, lay the Ledger of Hours: a book of prophecies written a century past by an order of monks who had believed, correctly, that one day writing itself would become contraband.

Ivo copied it. That was her debt-work. Not the charms — the charms were pocket money for the Temple, a few coppers of grace sold to people who couldn't afford the real thing, which was nothing, because there was no real thing anymore. Her actual labor, the labor Corvane had bought when her mother died owing the Temple four hundred marks, was transcription. She sat six hours a night under a lamp with a green shade and copied the Ledger of Hours onto fresh vellum, in her own small precise hand, for a master copy that Corvane kept in his private rooms and never showed anyone.

She was not permitted to read what she copied.

This was, Ivo had long ago decided, the single cruelest thing about Vell, and Vell was not a city that rationed its cruelty. Anyone could forbid you to speak. The Wardens forbade speech as a matter of course, and prayer, and certain colors of cloth, and the keeping of animals with unusual eyes. But to make a woman spend six hours a night tracing the shape of every word — to force the sentences through her hand, her wrist, her fingers, until they lived in her muscles if nowhere else — and then to forbid her the meaning of them? That required imagination. That required a special appetite.

She had broken the rule exactly once, in her second year, and paid for it with eleven days in the dark cell beneath the Scriptorium and a fine of thirty marks added to her total. She had learned her lesson. Mostly. In the way that a person standing at the edge of a cliff has learned about falling.

Because here was the thing about copying prophecy: your eye did it whether you wanted it to or not. The words passed through her vision on their way to her pen, and some of them stuck. Fragments. Shards of the century-old text lodged in her memory like splinters:

*...and the thirteenth moon shall rise over Vell, and the Ember shall choose...*

*...the keepers burned, the keepers buried, the keepers forgotten by design...*

*...on the last night of the ninth month, a man will step out of the margin...*

That last one she had copied four nights ago, and it had lodged deepest, the way a fishhook lodges deeper the more you pull. *A man will step out of the margin.* She'd written it in her careful scribe's hand and felt something turn over in her chest — not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The feeling of hearing your own name called in a crowd where nobody knows you.

Tonight, the ninth month ended.

* * *

The stall closed at the second bell. Ivo counted the till twice, recorded the sum in the ledger Corvane would inspect in the morning, and carried the charm bottles inside to their shelves, each stoppered vessel catching the green lamplight like a row of dull jewels. Then she climbed the stairs to the Scriptorium, let herself in with her single key, and sat down at the lectern where the Ledger of Hours waited, open to tonight's pages, weighted at the corners with river stones.

The book was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Whoever had made it a hundred years ago had bound it in leather gone soft as skin, had ruled its margins in gold, had illuminated the chapter-heads with flames rendered so finely they seemed to flicker when she wasn't looking directly at them. A book this loved was supposed to be read aloud, passed hand to hand, argued over in kitchens. Instead it sat in a locked room in an outlaw city, and the only person who touched it was a woman forbidden to understand it.

Ivo dipped her pen. She set her ruler. She began.

The passage was one she hadn't copied before — the Ledger's later leaves, the prophecies the monks had written nearest the end, when whatever they had seen had begun to frighten even them. The handwriting deteriorated across these pages, the letters leaning as though pushed by wind. She matched the deterioration with her own hand, because fidelity was the whole of her craft, and found it strangely affecting: a century-dead monk's panic preserved in her steady strokes.

*They will call it a weapon,* she wrote. *It is not a weapon. It is a witness. We are sending it forward because a witness must survive the trial.*

Her pen paused above the next line.

Not because the text was difficult. Because the date was difficult. The monks had dated their entries — day, month, year, in the old reckoning — and Ivo, who had spent six years converting old reckonings to new ones for Corvane's accounts, converted this one automatically, the way she breathed.

Then she stopped breathing.

The entry was dated one hundred years ago to the day. To the hour. The monks had written, a century in the past, a timestamp that fell — she checked the temple clock through the doorway, heart tapping against her ribs — within the next quarter-hour.

*On this night,* the monk had written, *at the turning of the watch, the margin opens. What we send will arrive. Pray the city has grown kinder in a hundred years. Pray it, though we know it hasn't.*

Ivo sat very still. The green lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the temple bells gathered themselves for the change of the watch.

She should close the book. She should fetch Corvane, or better, say nothing at all, finish her pages, go home to her rented room above the tannery, and sleep the dreamless exhausted sleep of the indebted. In the morning she would have 1,842 days left on her contract, and she would count them the way she always counted them, one at a time, like coins hoarded against a freedom that never quite arrived.

Instead she read the line again. *What we send will arrive.*

The first bell of the watch rang out over Vell, bronze and enormous, and the sound seemed to pass through the Scriptorium like a hand passing through smoke.

And in the margin of the page — in the gold-ruled border where no monk had written anything, where there was room for nothing but ornament — the blank vellum began, very softly, to glow.

Ivo did not move. Later she would wonder at that: that her body, trained by six years of obedience, chose this moment to disobey every instinct it had. She simply watched as the glow gathered, and warmed, and took the shape of something like a door, and the smell of soot filled the room, sharp and recent, like a fire just put out.

The last thing she thought, before the margin opened, was the most scribe-like thought she'd ever had:

*Someone is going to have to record this.*
