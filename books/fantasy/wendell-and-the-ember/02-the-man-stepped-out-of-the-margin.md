## 2. The Man Stepped Out of the Margin

I told myself I would not go back to the archive.

I told myself that all through the evening meal — barley porridge, temple-issued, eaten standing at the scullery window while the lamplighters worked their way down the Processional. I told myself while I washed my one good pen and set it to dry. I told myself while I lay on my cot in the scribes' dormitory, staring at the ceiling beams, listening to Sister Anthe cough in the next bed over.

The line was a coincidence. That was the whole of it. A century-old book, a thousand dates copied by a thousand bored hands, and somewhere in the drift of ink and error, one of them happened to land on tonight. Probability did that. Probability was the only magic left in Vell, and it wasn't even magic.

But at half past the eleventh hour I was still awake, and at quarter to midnight I was dressed, and by eleven minutes to the hour named in the prophecy I was easing open the door of the deep archive with the copyist's key that Master Corvane had never asked me to return.

The stairwell smelled of cold stone and older paper. My lamp made a small yellow room around me and nothing beyond it. I counted the steps the way I always counted them — forty-one down, forty-one up, the arithmetic of my whole narrow life — and told myself I would only look. One glance at the page. Confirmation that the ink had smudged, or that I'd misread the calendar conversion, or that some dead scribe had been sloppy with his numerals. Then back up the stairs, back to bed, back to counting days toward a freedom that arrived at the rate of four coppers a week.

The archive received me the way it received everything: without comment.

It was colder down there than usual. Or I was noticing it for the first time because everything felt sharper tonight — the rasp of my own breathing, the tick of the lamp's metal handle cooling, the particular hush of a room where no one has spoken aloud in years. The shelves went back into the dark in ranks, holding the city's permitted past: tax rolls, land grants, the sanitized chronicles of the Reformation, when the Wardens burned the colleges and outlawed the working of magic and Vell learned to call its amputation *peace*.

The book of prophecies lived in the third rank, third shelf, behind the chain. I unhooked the lamp, set it on the reading ledge, and drew the volume out with both hands, the way you'd handle something sleeping.

*The ninth night of the Ember Moon,* the old hand said, in faded iron-gall brown. *At the turning of the twelfth hour, the bearer comes out of the margin.*

There it was again. Tonight's date, converted from the old reckoning, sitting on the page like a coin someone had left for me specifically. And beneath it, in the same script but pressed harder, as if the writer had leaned into the words:

*Do not let the Wardens take what walks.*

My mouth had gone dry. I remember that distinctly — the taste of dust and copper, the way my tongue stuck to the roof of my mouth. I remember thinking, quite calmly, *this is how it feels just before your life stops being yours.* Because I had felt it before. The day the debt assessors came to my mother's door, when I was small enough that the whole memory fit inside a single image: her hands flat on the table, the ledger man's seal coming down like a verdict.

Then the lamps in the wall sconces lit themselves.

Not all at once. One, then the next, then the next, down the long ranks of shelving, a run of small flames chasing away from me into the dark like something startled. And the air changed pressure — my ears popped, the way they do when a door opens somewhere far off — and the temperature dropped so fast my breath came out white.

The book got heavy in my hands.

That is the only way I can describe it. A moment before it had been paper and board, two pounds of vellum at most. Then it weighed like a child. Like a body. I nearly dropped it, and as I fumbled my grip, the page under my thumb began to glow along the lines of the letters — not burning, not charring, just *waking*, the ink going soft and gold as if lit from underneath.

And the margin — the blank space beside the prophecy, the empty margin every scribe leaves for corrections —

The margin opened.

I don't have better words than that, though I've spent since then trying to find them. It didn't tear. It didn't ripple. It simply stopped being blank, the way a closed eye stops being shut. And out of it, hand first, then shoulder, then the rest of him, came a man covered in soot.

He fell more than stepped — caught himself on the reading ledge, knocked my lamp spinning, and went down on one knee on the archive floor, dragging breath into his lungs in great tearing pulls, as if he'd been underwater. He was youngish, thirty perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and a face smudged gray to the cheekbones, and in his right fist he clutched something against his chest the way you'd hold a wound closed.

Something that glowed.

Not firelight-glow. Heartbeat-glow. It brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, steady as a pulse, a coal the size of a plum throwing off light the color of the last coals in a dying hearth — orange shading into a red so deep it was almost secret. I could feel it from six feet away. Not heat, exactly. *Attention.* The sensation of being looked at by something patient.

"Close it," the man rasped. His voice sounded scraped. "Whoever you are — close the margin."

"I don't know how," I said. Which was true, and also the most honest thing I'd said in years.

"It closes when—" He coughed, doubled over. "When the hour passes. How long? What hour is it?"

"The twelfth. Just turned."

"Then we have minutes." He got his feet under him and stood, swaying, and I saw that his coat was scorched down one side, the fabric gone stiff and black, and that he was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse until I realized what it was: recognition. Not of my face. Of my *kind*. "You're a scribe."

"Debt-bound," I said, because apparently my mouth had decided honesty was the order of the night. "Temple copyist. Third rank."

"Third rank." Something moved through his face — relief or grief, hard to say. "Then you can read the old calendars. Good. Good." He said *good* twice, as if convincing himself. "Listen to me. My name is Wendell. I am a keeper of the Ember, and I have come a hundred years to keep it from the Ashen Wardens, and in about four minutes this room is going to fill with them, because the margins sing when they open and the Wardens hear singing in their sleep."

Every sentence landed separately, like stones dropped one at a time into still water. I should have laughed. I should have called for the night proctor, should have done any of the ten sensible things available to a woman whose entire survival strategy for nine years had been *be unremarkable*.

Instead I heard myself say: "The prophecy says not to let the Wardens take what walks."

Wendell stared at me. The coal in his fist pulsed — bright, dim — and in its light I saw his eyes go wet and fierce. "You've read it."

"I copy it. I'm not allowed to read it." I heard the bitterness in my own voice and didn't try to sand it down. "Nine years of copying prophecies I'll be whipped for opening. So yes. I've read it."

Somewhere above us, faint through forty-one steps of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The *alarm* — the flat, urgent triple-strike the temple rang only for fire or breach, and I knew, with the certainty of a woman who has spent her life watching powerful men's faces, that it was neither.

"They're early," Wendell said. "They're always early now. They've had a century to get good at this." He looked around the archive wildly, then at me, and held out the coal an inch, as if offering it, as if testing what I'd do. "Take it. If they take me, take it and run and—"

"No," I said.

It came out faster than thought. Flat, absolute, surprising us both. And here is the thing I have turned over a hundred times since: I didn't refuse out of courage. I refused because some part of me, some part older than my debt and deeper than my fear, recognized that coal. The way you recognize a song you haven't heard since childhood — not the words, just the shape of the melody in your chest.

The Ember pulsed. And for half a heartbeat, I swear it pulsed *in time with my heart*, and Wendell saw it too. I watched him see it. Watched his face do the same slow collapse mine must have been doing.

"You felt that," he whispered.

Boots on the stairs. Many boots, descending in rhythm, and beneath them the dry whisper of ash-cloaks against stone.

* * *

Here is what nine years of debt servitude teaches you: how to lie quickly, convincingly, and in the direction of whoever holds your ledger.

The first Warden came around the shelf-rank with his lamp raised and his censer swinging, filling the aisle with the smell of burnt sage and cold iron. Under the hood, his face was young and tired and utterly certain. Behind him came two more, and behind them, the sound of more boots on the stairs, still coming.

"Acolyte," the young one said. "Report."

I was already moving — I want that recorded somewhere, that my body knew what to do before my mind finished being afraid. I stepped between Wendell and the Wardens, snatched the fallen prophecies off the ledge, and thrust the open volume at them like evidence.

"Breach in the binding room," I said. "A century-old codex with unstable preservation charms. It ignited its own margins — spontaneous combustion of the annotation layer, it happens with iron-gall ink if the humidity breaks. I was doing a late correction pass when it flared. This man—" I jerked my chin at Wendell, soot-blackened, scorched-coated, glowing-fisted Wendell, and heard myself invent with a fluency that would have horrified my teachers, "—is Brother Cassian, from the bindery. He came to help me suppress it. We've contained it. Mostly."

The young Warden looked at the book. At the margin, which had, thank every indifferent god, gone dark and ordinary the moment the hour passed. At Wendell, who had — blessedly, miraculously — understood the assignment, and folded himself into a bow of exactly the depth a bindery brother owed a Warden, head down, fists hidden.

"Spontaneous combustion," the Warden repeated.

"Ink is volatile," I said. "You burn things. You know."

One of the other Wardens made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The young one's eyes came back to me, and there was a long moment — three heartbeats, maybe four, the coal in Wendell's hidden fist pulsing against the silence like a second clock — where I understood that my entire remaining life was balanced on whether a tired man in a hood decided a debt-scribe was worth doubting.

"Your name," he said.

"Ivo Delacroix. Ledger number 4471, bound to Master Corvane's account."

"Delacroix." Something flickered behind his eyes at that — not recognition, exactly. Filing. "Corvane's scribe. The one who copies the sealed shelves."

"I copy whatever Master Corvane assigns."

"Yes," the Warden said softly. "I imagine you do." He closed the book in my hands with two fingers, gently, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes. "Contain your fire, acolyte. And Delacroix — if anything else walks out of a margin tonight, you'll report it. To us. Before you report it to Corvane."

They withdrew. The boots receded up the stairs, taking their censer-smoke with them, and the archive settled back into its cold and its silence, and I stood there holding a forbidden book against my chest with both arms like a shield, shaking so hard my teeth clicked.

Wendell straightened out of his bow slowly. In the dark, the coal's glow had gone down to almost nothing — banked, watchful, a heartbeat resting.

"That," he said quietly, "was either the bravest or the stupidest thing I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a woman duel a river."

"Don't talk," I managed. "If you talk, I'll start screaming, and I won't stop."

He nodded solemnly, which I suspect was the correct response. Then he held up the coal between us, letting its ember-light touch both our faces, and said the words that ended the life I had been counting days through:

"You lied well, scribe. Now lie better. Because the Warden knew your name, and Master Corvane's ledgers have ears everywhere — and if he learns what you're hiding, he won't send Wardens." Wendell's eyes were steady on mine, soot-gray and ancient. "He'll send your debt."

Above us, muffled through stone, the temple bells began ringing the alarm a second time.
