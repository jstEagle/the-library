## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The Ember pulsed against my ribs like a second heart, and I ran.

I had been a keeper of the Margin for eleven years before they sent me through. Eleven years of copying prophecy in a scriptorium that smelled of tallow and cold stone, of learning which lines were load-bearing — which words, if altered by so much as a stroke, would collapse a century of foretelling. I knew the Ember the way a priest knows his god: intimately, at a distance, with fear. I had never been meant to carry it. The margin had chosen me the way floodwater chooses a door — not because I was strong, but because I was nearest.

Now Vell's lower quarters swallowed us whole.

The city above was bad enough — the ash-choked lanes where the temple's incense smoke settled like gray snow on every awning. But down here, below the aqueduct arches, the ash *lived*. It drifted in slow spirals through the gaps between buildings, fine as flour, and it got into everything: my mouth, my eyes, the seams of the borrowed coat Ivo had thrown over my shoulders. It tasted of burned paper. Of course it did. This whole city was built on burned paper — on the pyres where the Ashen Wardens had fed a hundred years of books, charms, and names.

"Left," Ivo said, breathless. "Not the main stair — they'll have eyes on it."

I followed her because she was the only thing in this century I understood. She moved through the lower quarters the way water moves through cracks: without thinking, without asking permission. A nod to a woman selling roasted chestnuts. A coin pressed into a child's palm with a word too low for me to catch. Doors opened for her that would not have opened for me.

*She is debt-bound,* the margin had told me. *A scribe. She will be there when you come through. Trust her or die.*

Prophecy is rarely generous with its details.

We cut through a dye-yard where bolts of cloth hung like flayed skins, blue and rust bleeding into the fog, and I felt the Ember shift against my chest — warm, insistent, *awake* in a way it had never been in the scriptorium. In my century it had slept like a banked coal. Here it beat.

"It knows this place," I said.

"Don't talk." Ivo glanced back at me, and her face did something complicated — fear and fury and a third thing she was trying very hard not to name. "Wardens don't chase. They corner. We need to be somewhere they can't—"

The bell began.

Not the temple bell. The other one — the one I'd only read about, the Warden bell, cast from melted shrine-metal, tuned to a note that made the teeth ache. It rolled down through the quarters in slow, deliberate strokes, and everywhere around us the city changed texture. Shutters closed. Voices dropped. Somewhere a dog began to howl and was silenced.

"They've named you," Ivo said quietly. "That's the summons bell. When it rings, everyone in Vell is legally obligated to report anything that glows."

"And do they?"

She looked at me like I'd asked whether fish obeyed the tide. "They're paid to."

* * *

We holed up in a cistern house beneath a tenement on Coriander Street — a low brick vault where rainwater once collected and now collected only ash, drifts of it ankle-deep, graying everything. Ivo barred the door with a length of pipe and then stood with her back against it, breathing hard, watching me with the expression of someone auditing a ledger that refuses to balance.

"Start talking," she said. "You stepped out of a book. You're going to explain that."

So I told her. Sitting in ash, in a dead cistern under a living city, I told her about the Margin — the order of prophecy-keepers who tended the great codices in the century before the ban, who copied and guarded and, when the war came, did the one thing left to do. The Wardens were coming for the archives. Not to read them. To burn them. And the Ember — the compiled memory of everyone the war would erase — could not be allowed to burn with them.

"We couldn't destroy it," I said. "It isn't a weapon. Destroying it would be—" I searched for the word and hated that there wasn't a better one. "*Murder.* Every memory inside it, gone. So we did the only thing the margins allowed. There was a passage. A door. Dated tonight, a hundred years forward. Someone had to carry the Ember through and keep it from the Wardens until—" 

"Until what?"

"Until the thirteenth moon." I turned my palms up. "I'm a keeper, not an interpreter. The passage ends. That's what passages do."

Ivo laughed — one sharp, ugly syllable with no humor in it. "Of course it does. Prophecies are written by people who want to be believed, not people who want to be *useful*." She dragged a hand through her hair, leaving gray streaks in the black. "And the Wardens? They exist in your time too?"

"The Wardens *started* in my time. They were soldiers first. Then archivists of the burning. Then—" I gestured vaguely upward, at the city, the century, all of it. "Whatever they are now. They were founded to make sure the war never came back. Their method is simple: no memory, no magic, no war. Burn the record, burn the practice, burn the *name*, and peace holds."

"That's not peace," Ivo said. "That's amnesia with a badge."

"I know." And I did know, had known for years, in the way you know a thing you are powerless to change — dully, daily, like a stone in the shoe. "But knowing hasn't stopped them for a hundred years."

The Ember pulsed. I put my hand over my chest without meaning to.

Ivo saw. Her eyes narrowed. "It does that a lot?"

"Only since we came through. In the scriptorium it slept. Here it's—" I hesitated. "Here it's *listening*."

* * *

It happened when she reached past me for the water skin.

Her fingers brushed the coat — brushed *me* — and the Ember flared. Not painfully. Not even brightly. But I felt it happen, the way you feel a door open in another room: a shift in pressure, a current finding its channel. The Ember reached across the touch and poured.

I saw what it showed her, because the Ember has no privacy. It shows the giver as much as the given.

I saw a street.

Narrow, cobbled, crooked with age. Laundry strung between upper windows like signal flags. A chandler's shop with a painted sign — a fat white candle with a smiling face, the paint peeling at the corners. A girl of six or seven sitting on the steps of that shop, braiding grass stems with ferocious concentration, while a woman leaned in the doorway behind her — dark-haired, laughing at something out of frame, one hand resting on the girl's head like a blessing.

The street had a name. The Ember gave it freely, generously, the way it gives everything:

*Lantern Row.*

I heard Ivo make a sound. Small. Strangled. The sound of a person catching their own weight falling.

"Lantern Row," she whispered. "That's— that's not possible. Lantern Row burned. Before I was born. It's in the histories, it—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself into something harder. "There's a tax office on Lantern Row now. I've walked past it a thousand times. There's no chandler's shop. There's no—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. We both stood in the ash-drifted dark of the cistern, holding the same image between us like something hot: a woman's hand on a small girl's head. A mother. A street that officially died decades ago, alive in a shard of coal, wearing Ivo's own face at seven years old.

"How," Ivo said. Not a question. A demand aimed at the universe. "How does your Ember have my childhood? My childhood is *gone*. Everyone says so — the Wardens took it when I was small, after the fever, they told me, standard confiscation, unlicensed magic in the household, they told me, they *documented* it—"

"Did they?" I asked gently. "Show you the documentation?"

Silence. Long silence. The ash sifted down through the vault's broken ceiling in threads of gray light.

"No," she admitted finally. "They told Master Corvane. Corvane told me. I signed papers I wasn't allowed to read. I was eight." Her jaw worked. "I've spent nineteen years paying a debt whose terms I can't remember incurring. For a crime I can't remember committing. In a house I can't remember living in."

The Ember pulsed again, softer now. Almost tender. And I understood — with the sick, vertiginous clarity of a man standing on a floor that has just revealed itself to be a trapdoor — that the Ember had not shown her a stranger's memory.

It had shown her *her own*.

"Ivo," I said carefully. "Keepers don't just guard the Ember. Some of them come from Ember-keeper lines. Families. The memory passes down, and the Ember... recognizes its own." I swallowed. "If your mother was on Lantern Row, in that doorway, in *my* century—"

"Then my mother was a keeper." Her voice had gone flat and strange, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant in a language they almost understand. "Then the fever was a story. Then the confiscation was a story. Then my debt—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, worse: "Then my whole life is an edit. Someone sat down with the ledger of me and *struck lines*."

Outside, distantly, the Warden bell rang again. Closer. Patient. The sound of an institution that has burned a hundred years of Lantern Rows and never once run out of fuel.

Ivo looked down at her hands — scribe's hands, ink-stained, docile — and when she raised her eyes to mine there was something new in them. Not the weary longing I'd seen in the archive. Not even fear.

Arithmetic.

"Corvane owns my debt," she said slowly. "Corvane decides what I read, what I copy, what I'm allowed to remember wanting. And tonight a man walks out of a book carrying the one object in the world full of everything I've lost." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing I'd seen in either century. "Tell me, Wendell. What are the odds that's coincidence?"

I had no answer. The Ember beat against my ribs, faster now, like a heart preparing to break — or to be handed over.

And far above us, boots on stone. Many boots. Descending.
