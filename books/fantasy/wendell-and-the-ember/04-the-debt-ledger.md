## 4. The Debt Ledger

Master Corvane kept his office above the counting floor, where the temple's wealth was weighed and recorded in ledgers so old their spines had gone the color of dried blood. Ivo climbed the stairs at dawn, summoned by a boy who hadn't given her time to wash the ink from her fingers, and she spent every one of the ninety-three steps rehearsing innocence.

She needn't have bothered. Corvane already knew.

He sat behind a desk of black oak that had belonged to three masters before him, and he did not look up when she entered. He was writing. That was the thing about Corvane that unsettled people who didn't know him — they expected cruelty to be loud. His cruelty was penmanship. Neat, unhurried strokes, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Sit," he said.

Ivo sat. The chair across from him was lower than his, an old trick she'd catalogued years ago along with everything else about the man: the way he wore gray instead of black because black was what Wardens wore and he liked the distinction noted; the way he never ate in front of anyone; the way he said *we* when he meant *I*.

"You sold eleven charms yesterday," he said, still writing. "Four were watered past tolerance. You've grown careless."

"The ash water's been short this week. The dilution—"

"I know exactly what the dilution has been." He set down his pen with a small, deliberate click. "I know everything that happens under my roof, Ivo. That is not a boast. It is a description of my function."

She folded her hands in her lap so he couldn't see them tremble. Outside the tall window, Vell was waking — smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, straight as plumb lines, the city holding itself upright out of habit.

"Do you know why your debt has never been forgiven?" Corvane asked.

"Because it keeps growing. The interest—"

"Because you are useful." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were the pale gray of ledger paper. "A debt-bound scribe with access to the archive, no family, no loyalties, no questions. Do you imagine that's an accident? That you simply wandered into my temple fifteen years ago, a half-starved child with a head full of nothing?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the counting clerks began their morning murmur, numbers being read aloud like prayers.

"I don't understand," Ivo said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "You don't. That too is by design." He opened a drawer and drew out a single sheet of paper, laid it flat, and turned it to face her. It was a page from the prophecy folio — the restricted section, the century-old book she copied but was never permitted to read. And there, boxed in fresh red ink, was the line she had found last night. Tonight's date. Written a hundred years before tonight existed.

"You accessed this without sanction," Corvane said. "At the eleventh hour. And within the same hour, a man appeared in the archive who does not exist in any census, any registry, any record of births or deaths going back two generations. A man carrying contraband of the highest order." He leaned back. "I am told he was soot-marked. That he clutched a coal that glowed."

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. She thought of Wendell hidden in the disused cistern beneath the tannery row, of the lie she'd told the Wardens — *he came through the south door, he ran toward the river* — and how easily it had come, how little it had cost her, and how much it would cost now.

"I saw a man," she said. "He ran past me. I was frightened. I told the Wardens what I saw."

"You did." Corvane smiled, and it was almost warm, which was worse than cold. "And the Wardens believed you, because the Wardens are soldiers and you are a scribe, and no one suspects ink of lying." He tapped the prophecy page once. "But I am not a soldier, Ivo. I am a reader. And readers know that the most dangerous lies are the ones written in a steady hand."

* * *

He rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind his back, and for a moment he let her see something that looked almost like weariness.

"Do you know what the Ember is?" he asked.

"A story," Ivo said. "A coal from the old war. Children's tales."

"It is a record." He said it flatly, the way he corrected a clerk's arithmetic. "Everything the war burned, everything the ban erased — it holds all of it. Names. Streets. People the city decided never existed." He turned. "The Wardens want it destroyed because destruction is the only erasure they trust. Others want it crowned, raised up, made into a new flame to relight the old fires. Both sides are wrong, in my judgment. But both sides would pay enormously for it."

"And you?"

"I am a man of the temple. I do not destroy or crown. I *trade*." He came back to the desk and stood over her, and this close she could smell the lamp oil on him, the dust of old paper. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once. Bring me the Ember before the thirteenth moon rises, and your contract burns. Every dram of debt, every year of servitude — gone. You walk out of this temple a free woman with coin in her pocket and no one's name on your back."

Ivo's heart was doing something shameful. It was leaping.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of diluted charms and copied pages she wasn't allowed to read, of counting days like a miser counts coins, of waking with the ledger number tattooed inside her eyelids. Freedom had been a sum she'd stopped expecting to reach, like a horizon. And here it was, set on a desk in front of her, priced at one coal.

"Yes," she heard herself say.

The word came out smooth. Steady-handed. And she hated it — hated how fast it came, how naturally temptation fit her hand, like a pen she'd been holding all her life without knowing. Some part of her had been waiting for exactly this offer since she was a child, some part that understood, even then, that her freedom would only ever be sold to her by the man who owned her, and that she would pay whatever he asked.

Corvane studied her face for a long moment, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy him.

"Wise," he said. "You have until the thirteenth moon. Nine days. Fail, and I will give the Wardens a full account of your night in the archive — your access, your lie, your fugitive. They do not burn debts, Ivo. They burn debtors." He picked up his pen again, dismissal in the motion. "One more thing. The man. When you find him — and you will find him, because he has nowhere else to go and you are the only person in Vell he knows — be careful. The prophecies say things about people like him. About what they're *for*."

"What do they say?"

"That they aren't people at all," Corvane said mildly. "That they're sentences. Written by someone with an agenda, walking around in a body. Sentences can be edited." He glanced up. "Or crossed out."

* * *

She walked down through the counting floor with her spine straight and her stomach sour, past the clerks murmuring their numbers, past the great iron-bound ledgers chained to their desks like prisoners. At the door, a Warden in ash-gray armor stood post, and Ivo met the slit of his visor and felt nothing — no fear, no guilt. Just arithmetic. She had lied to save a stranger, and now she had lied to save herself, and the two lies sat side by side in her chest without touching, the way entries sit side by side in a ledger, each in its own line, each canceling or compounding according to rules she hadn't written.

*Sentences can be edited.*

She thought of Wendell's face in the archive light — the confusion, the soot, the way he'd gripped that glowing coal like a man holding his own heart outside his body. He had stepped out of a book. Which meant someone had written him in. Which meant someone could write him out.

By the time she reached the tannery row, the shame had settled into something harder and more useful. Not the shame of the lie — she could live with that. The shame of the *yes*. Of how quickly her mouth had made a promise her mind hadn't finished weighing. Corvane hadn't bought her obedience. He'd only ever rented it. And now, for nine days, he believed he owned the ending of her story.

Let him believe it. She was a scribe. She knew better than anyone that a text could say one thing in its fair copy and quite another in the hand that drafted it.

The cistern hatch was where she'd left it, hidden under rotting sacking. She lifted it and whispered down into the dark:

"Wendell. We need to talk about what you are."

Below, in the black, the Ember pulsed once — like a heartbeat answering its name.
