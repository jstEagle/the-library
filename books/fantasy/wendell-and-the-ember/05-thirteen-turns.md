## 5. Thirteen Turns

The cellar smelled of tallow and old salt, the way smugglers' cellars always did — as if the sea had been down here once and never quite left. Wendell had been in a hundred hiding places across a century of running, and they all had the same bones: low ceilings, bad light, a door that locked from the wrong side.

He lit three candles anyway. If the Wardens found them, darkness wouldn't save anyone, and he wanted to see Ivo's face when she understood what he was about to tell her. He'd learned that much in two days of knowing her: she could survive almost any truth, but not one delivered in shadow.

She sat on an overturned crate with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, watching him the way scribes watched documents — like something that might be forged.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "The actual beginning. Not the version where you're mysterious."

"There is no other version."

"Try."

So he sat down across from her, on a barrel stinking of fish brine, and folded his soot-marked hands on his knees.

"The Ember was made at the end of the war," he said. "Not during it. After. When both sides realized they'd burned more than each other. They'd burned *records*. Whole bloodlines, whole languages, whole ways of doing magic — gone, because the people who carried them were gone. So the last keepers gathered everything that remained. Every memory the war had erased. They pressed it all into a single shard of living fire and gave it a name, because things with names are harder to destroy."

"The Ember," Ivo said flatly.

"The Ember." He turned his palms up. "It isn't a weapon. It's a witness. But a witness of that size — a century of stolen lives — can be used as one. Whoever holds it holds every erased person's power, their knowledge, their grief. Crown it, put it on a throne or an altar, and you've restarted the war with better ammunition. Destroy it, and you kill those people a second time. Permanently this time."

"And your job was to carry it forward."

"My order kept it. The Keepers of the Turning Page. We were prophecy-keepers — we didn't read the future, we *preserved* it. Wrote down what was coming so that when it came, someone would recognize it. A century ago, my masters saw the Wardens coming for us. The ban on magic was spreading city by city, and Vell's Ashen Wardens were the most thorough. So they wrote one final prophecy and sent me through with the Ember, to arrive on a specific night, in a specific place, at a specific hour."

"Tonight," Ivo said. "Your date."

"Tonight. My date." He smiled without humor. "I memorized the passage before I stepped through. I can recite it in my sleep. I have recited it in my sleep, for years, waiting for the night it would stop being words."

"Say it."

He closed his eyes. The candle flames leaned toward him, just slightly, the way flames lean toward anything warm.

"*When the thirteenth moon rises full over Vell,*" he said, "*the Ember must be given its ending. Broken upon the ash altar, and the silence endures another age. Or set upon the crown of the city, and the fire wakes, and the war returns wearing new faces. There is no third road written. What is unwritten belongs to no one.*"

Silence. Somewhere above them, cart wheels rattled over cobblestones, and Ivo flinched at the sound before catching herself.

"When?" she asked. "The thirteenth moon. How long?"

Wendell looked at the ceiling, counting days the way he counted everything now — backward, toward an ending.

"Nineteen nights," he said. "Nineteen nights, and then everyone in Vell who wants that stone will come for it. The Wardens know the date too. They've always known. Why do you think they watch the archive? Why do you think Master Corvane keeps a debt-bound scribe copying prophecies she's forbidden to read?"

*Because he knew I'd find it,* Ivo thought, but she didn't say it aloud. Wendell watched her not saying it, and let her keep it.

"Nineteen nights," she repeated. "And there's no third option."

"Not written."

"You said that like it matters."

"It's the only thing that matters." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. My entire life — my entire *century* — has been a script. I was chosen because I was obedient. I walked through fire into a foreign time carrying a stone I'm not permitted to use, to deliver it to an ending someone else wrote a hundred years ago. I am a courier, Ivo. Couriers don't get third options. But you—" 

He stopped.

He hadn't meant to say the next part yet. He'd planned to teach her the prophecy first, establish the stakes, build slowly toward the thing he'd noticed on the first night and had been turning over ever since like a coin he couldn't stop flipping. But she was looking at him with those tired, hungry eyes, and the cellar was small, and the candles were warm, and the truth came out ahead of schedule.

"But me what?" she said.

"Give me your hand."

"I don't—"

"Give me your hand, and don't touch the stone. Just hold it near. An inch away. I want to show you something."

The Ember sat between them on the crate, wrapped in a scrap of grey cloth that had once been part of Wendell's traveling cloak. It pulsed under the fabric — slow, steady, patient — like something sleeping that had learned not to trust sleepers.

Ivo set down her untouched tea. She pulled off her right glove — she still wore the scribe's gloves out of habit, ink-stained at the fingertips — and held her bare hand over the bundle.

Nothing happened.

Wendell frowned. "Closer."

She lowered her hand until her fingers brushed the cloth.

The Ember woke.

Not gradually — instantly, the way a struck bell doesn't ease into ringing. Light bled through the grey fabric, gold and deep red, and a sound came with it: a hum, low and resonant, rising through the crate, through the floor, through the soles of Ivo's feet and up into her teeth. The candles flared. The shadows ran for the walls.

Ivo snatched her hand back. The hum cut off mid-note. The light died. The Ember settled back into its slow pulse, calm as a heart between beats.

"What was that," she said. It wasn't a question.

"That," Wendell said quietly, "is what I've been trying not to think about for two days."

He reached out and laid his own palm flat against the cloth. Nothing. No flare, no hum. The Ember ignored him the way it had ignored him for a hundred years — tolerated him, carried him, but never *answered* him. He was its courier. He had accepted that.

"Now you," he said, and nodded at the bundle.

"Don't," Ivo said.

"Just touch it. Through the cloth. One second."

She touched it. The bell rang again — that same shattering chord of light and sound, brighter this time, as if the Ember had recognized her and was glad. Her face went white in the glow. She pulled away and stood up so fast the crate scraped the floor, and she stood with her back against the cellar wall, breathing hard, staring at the little grey bundle like it had bitten her.

"It knows me," she said. "Why does it *know* me?"

"I don't know."

"You're supposed to know. You're the keeper. You're the one who memorized the prophecy."

"The prophecy says nothing about you." He said it gently, and watched it land badly. "You weren't in the text, Ivo. I've read that passage ten thousand times. There's a keeper who sent me, a city that received me, an hour, a moon, an ending. There's no scribe in a temple stall. There's no woman who lies to Wardens to save a stranger. You are not in the script."

"Then why does it sing for me?"

"Because somewhere in that stone," Wendell said, "there may be a page about you that no one wrote down. Or—" and here he made himself say the rest, because she deserved the whole weight of it, "—or there's a page about you that someone *removed*. The Ember holds memories the war erased. Confiscated ones. Burned ones. If it recognizes you, Ivo, then some part of you was taken from you. Or taken *for* you. Or taken *because of* you."

Ivo slid slowly back down the wall until she was sitting on the cold stone floor, knees drawn up, arms around them. In the candlelight she looked suddenly very young, and very old, the way people do when a suspicion they've been carrying for years finally puts on a face.

"My childhood," she said. "I told you. Before the debt. There's nothing there. Everyone said it was the fever. Everyone said the fever took it."

"Fever doesn't take memories in clean lines," Wendell said. "Fever takes randomly. Raggedly. What you describe — a smooth blank from one year to the next — that's not illness. That's *editing*."

"By whom?"

"Who profits from a debt-bound scribe who can't remember where she came from?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Above them the city went on — wheels, voices, a drunk singing three streets over, all the ordinary machinery of Vell grinding through its evening. Nineteen nights from now, Wendell thought, none of it would sound ordinary anymore.

"Corvane," Ivo said at last. Just the name. She said it the way you'd set down something heavy.

"He owns your debt."

"He owns my debt. He assigns my hours. He decides which prophecies I copy and which pages stay shut. He's known about you since the night you arrived — he told me so himself, offered me my freedom if I handed you over." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And I said yes. Did I tell you that? I looked at freedom after nine years of counting days, and I said yes."

"You didn't do it."

"No. I didn't do it." She stared at her bare right hand, the one that had made the Ember sing. "But I would have. That's the part that frightens me. He built a cage I fit into perfectly, and when he opened the door, I nearly walked through it."

Wendell got up, crossed the cellar, and sat down on the floor beside her — not touching, just near, shoulder to shoulder against the same cold wall. It seemed important, suddenly, that neither of them be alone with what they were thinking.

"Here's what I know," he said. "In nineteen nights, the thirteenth moon rises. The Ember gets broken or crowned, and either way the war comes back. Those are the only roads my masters wrote. But my masters wrote them a century ago, in a world that no longer exists, and they never met you." He turned his head to look at her. "*What is unwritten belongs to no one.* That's the last line. I used to think it meant the future was free. Now I think it means the future is *unassigned*. Nobody's holding it. Which means somebody could pick it up."

"Me," Ivo said.

"Or nobody. Or both of us. I genuinely don't know. I've been a courier my whole life; I only recently learned couriers are allowed to open the package." He huffed something like a laugh. "But I know the stone sings for you and not for me, and I know your missing years line up with the exact kind of memory the Ember carries, and I know Corvane has spent nine years keeping you blind, busy, and indebted in a temple full of prophecies you weren't allowed to read. None of those things are accidents. Someone arranged them. And whoever arranged them knew about tonight long before either of us did."

Ivo was silent. Then, very softly, in the voice of a woman testing ice:

"Wendell. If my mother was a keeper — if that's what's in the stone — then the Ember isn't humming at me like a stranger. It's humming at me like family."

The candles guttered, though there was no draft.

Wendell didn't answer, because there was nothing honest to say. Instead he watched her look down at her own hand — at the ink stains, the calluses from nine years of pens, the fingers that had touched a shard of compiled grief and been *recognized* — and he saw the dread in her face curdle into something else. Something hotter. Something with a direction.

"Nineteen nights," she said. "Then I want to see what's in it. All of it. Whatever Corvane took from me, whatever your masters buried — I want it back."

"That might be the road that starts the war."

"Then it's a good thing," Ivo Delacroix said, standing, pulling her glove back on over a hand that still faintly glowed at the knuckles, "that I've had nineteen years of practice following roads other people wrote for me. I'm overdue to write one myself."

Above them, muffled by floorboards and earth, the bells of Vell rang the ninth hour. And beneath the grey cloth, patient as a held breath, the Ember began — very faintly, very softly — to hum again, though no one was touching it at all.

* * *

They heard the footsteps an hour later.

Not the heavy cadence of Wardens — Wendell knew that tread the way he knew his own heartbeat — but something worse: unhurried, familiar, the walk of a man descending stairs he owned. Ivo was asleep against the wall when the third step from the bottom creaked, and her eyes snapped open, and Wendell saw her go rigid with recognition before her mind caught up to her body.

Master Corvane stood at the foot of the stairs, lantern raised, smiling the smile of a creditor surveying collateral.

"How curious," he said pleasantly. "I spend nine years teaching a girl obedience, and the moment I turn my back, she learns to hide things in cellars." His gaze moved to the grey bundle on the crate, then back to Ivo's face, and his smile widened by a single, terrible degree. "Well. Shall we discuss terms, scribe? Or shall we discuss your mother?"
