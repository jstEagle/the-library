## 6. The Memory in the Coal

The cellar smelled of tallow and old rain, and Ivo had not slept.

She sat on a crate with the smuggler's lamp turned low, and the Ember lay in her cupped hands like something asleep. It was smaller than she'd expected, for all its weight in the world — a coal no bigger than a plum, veined with light that pulsed slow and patient. A heartbeat that wasn't hers. Except it had been humming against her palm since the cellar door closed, and Wendell had watched it happen with an expression she couldn't read and didn't like.

"It doesn't do that for me," he'd said. "It hasn't done that for anyone in a hundred years."

She should have thrown it into the cistern then. She thought about it now, honestly considered it: the water was right there, black and still, and the Ember would hiss out like any other fire. That was the sensible thing. That was what a debt-bound scribe did with dangerous things — she catalogued them, she shelved them, she kept her hands clean and her ledger balanced.

But her whole life, Ivo had noticed, ran on other people's ledgers.

"You don't have to," Wendell said from the doorway. He'd wedged a chair under the handle and sat with his back to it, soot still ground into the creases of his knuckles, as if he'd been pulled through a chimney rather than a century. "Touching it on purpose. The memories come whether you want them or not, but you can wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For morning. For courage. For someone to tell you it's safe." He shrugged. "No one will tell you it's safe. I can tell you it's true, which is worth more."

Ivo looked down at the coal. Its pulse quickened under her gaze, the way a dog's tail moves when it knows its name is coming.

Her mother's face was a smudge. That was the thing she never told anyone, the thing she'd carried since she was small enough to be ashamed of it: she could not remember her mother's face. She remembered hands — flour to the wrists, a burn scar shaped like a comma on the left thumb. She remembered a voice saying *count backwards from ten, little bird*. But the face above those hands was fog, and when she reached for it, the fog pushed back, smooth and total, like a page scraped clean for reuse.

Palimpsest. She'd learned the word at nine years old, copying in Master Corvane's archive, and had gone cold at her own desk because she understood suddenly that she was one.

"I'm going to touch it," she said.

Wendell nodded once, and said nothing else, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in years.

* * *

She pressed both palms flat against the Ember.

The world went out like a pinched wick.

Then — light, but not lamplight. This light came from everywhere, warm as a kitchen at dawn, and Ivo was standing in a kitchen. Her feet knew the floorboards before her eyes did; they creaked in the third board from the stove, always had, always would. Copper pots hung from hooks. Rain on the window. And at the table, grinding something in a mortar — dried emberroot, she'd know that bitter smell anywhere, it was the smell of every bad winter of her childhood — sat a woman with dark hair going grey at the temples.

Ivo knew her instantly. Not the way you know a stranger described to you. The way you know your own name.

"Mama," she said, and her voice came out six years old.

The woman looked up and smiled, and there was Ivo's own crooked eyetooth in her mouth, and Ivo felt something in her chest crack along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"You're late," her mother said, mild as anything. "I've been keeping your seat warm for twenty years."

"I don't—" Ivo's throat closed. "I don't remember you. They took—"

"I know what they took." Her mother set down the mortar. Her left thumb wore the comma-shaped scar. "Sit down, little bird. You only get so long in here, and I have been rehearsing this conversation since before you were born."

Ivo sat. The chair received her like it had been waiting too.

"You were an Ember-keeper," Wendell had said, once, carefully, in the cellar — but hearing it and seeing it were different countries. Now she saw it. The kitchen wasn't just a kitchen. Along the back wall, where a poorer house might keep its pantry, her mother kept a shelf of coals — dozens of them, each in its own clay cradle, each pulsing with its own slow heartbeat. An archive made of fire. Each one a life.

"We didn't hoard them," her mother said, following her gaze. "We *kept* them. There's a difference, though the Wardens never learned it. Every coal is somebody. Somebody the war erased, or the peace after it erased, which was crueler because it smiled while it did it. When Vell outlawed magic, they didn't just ban the working of it. They banned the remembering of it. You can't make people forget a war unless you take away everyone who was in it."

"And you were one of the somebodies."

"I was the last keeper in the lower quarters. Your father was a scribe — that's where you get the ink under your nails, that's blood, not choice." Her mother laughed, and the sound went through Ivo like a hook through cloth. "When the Wardens came, I had time to do one thing. One. So I didn't hide the coals. I hid *you*."

"Me?"

"The Ember isn't a weapon, whatever Corvane's kind tell themselves. It's a witness. It holds everything we ever were. But a witness needs somewhere to stand, little bird. Someone who could grow up inside the city walls, inside their ledgers, inside their blindness, and never know what she was carrying." Her mother reached across the table and took Ivo's hand, and her grip was exactly as strong as Ivo had spent twenty years failing to remember. "So I gave you to the fire. All of it. Your first steps, my face, the street, the shelf of coals, the word *keeper*. I folded it all into the Ember and sealed it, and I let them take the rest. Let them think they'd won. A scraped page, Ivo. A palimpsest. Under every text, the old writing waits."

"They sold me," Ivo said. The fury arrived all at once, fully grown, no childhood left to soften it. "They didn't take me in. Corvane bought my debt when I was seven. He's been charging me interest on my own life for twenty years. I copy prophecies I'm not allowed to read in a temple built on the ashes of—"

"Yes."

"—and it wasn't misfortune. It wasn't bad luck. It was a *cage*. He knew. They all knew. A keeper's daughter, memoryless, bound by debt so deep she'd never look up from the desk long enough to wonder why the Ember hums when she walks past—"

"Yes," her mother said again, gently, and let her rage fill the kitchen like smoke. She didn't flinch from it. She'd been dead a long time; she could afford patience. "They designed it well. Debt is better than chains. Chains you can break. Debt, you carry willingly, and you count the days toward a freedom they never intended to grant. Tell me — how many days left?"

"Two hundred and eleven," Ivo said automatically, and hated herself for knowing.

"And if you walked out tonight?"

"They hunt runaways across three provinces. And—" She stopped. Because here was the thing, the rotten core of it, the thing she'd been circling for days without naming: two hundred and eleven days had been bearable. Two hundred and eleven days had been a *plan*. Take the number, divide the wage, endure. She had organized her entire soul around a release date, and now she understood the release date was a fiction, a lure, the cheese in the trap. Corvane had offered to erase her contract for the Ember. Erase. As if he could simply strike the line through her name whenever he pleased, and had chosen, year after year, not to.

"He was never going to free me," she whispered.

"No."

"The debt was the point. The debt was how you keep a blind woman blind — give her a horizon she can't reach, so she never looks behind her."

Her mother squeezed her hand. Outside the kitchen window, the rain had stopped, and the light beyond it was changing, thinning, going grey at the edges like ash cooling.

"I don't have much time," Ivo said. Panic rose in her. "Mama, I don't remember any of this — when I wake up, will I—"

"You'll keep it. That's the difference between you and the others he's scraped. You're touching it awake, willing, and you're the keeper's daughter. What you take from the fire stays lit." Her mother stood, and pulled her up by both hands, and for one moment Ivo stood in the kitchen as a child and a woman at once, held by the arms that had held her first. "Listen to me. The Ember must be destroyed or crowned — that's what the prophecy says, and prophecies are written by people with small imaginations. Destroyed, the erased stay erased forever, and Vell gets its clean white history. Crowned, it becomes a weapon, and the war starts again with new names. But there was always a third thing, little bird. The thing keepers whisper about. The Ember can be *carried*. Taken into a living body, kept by a keeper, so the memory survives even if the coal does not."

"That sounds like dying."

"It sounds like becoming." Her mother smiled, crooked eyetooth and all. "You were always going to be a writer. Make sure they can't confiscate the draft."

The kitchen was dissolving now, the copper pots going soft at their edges, the shelf of coals winking out one by one like windows going dark down a long street. Ivo clawed for her mother's hands and found them already turning to warmth.

"Wait — the street, our street, what happened to—"

"Gone. But not forgotten." The voice was everywhere now, in the pulse of the coal, in her own pulse, indistinguishable. "That's the whole point of you."

* * *

She came back to herself on the crate in the smuggler's cellar, gasping, tears cutting cold tracks through the grime on her face. The lamp had burned down to a stub. The Ember lay quiet in her lap, its pulse slowed, satisfied, like a heart after telling the truth.

Wendell was crouched in front of her, having the decency to look terrified on her behalf.

"How long?" she rasped.

"Half the night. You burned hot enough that I could see the light through your fingers." He hesitated. "What did it show you?"

Ivo wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Grief was there, enormous, a sea she'd be crossing for years. But under the grief, banked and steady, was something hotter. Fury with a shape to it now. Fury with a name attached, and a title, and a ledger, and a tower office smelling of sealing wax where a man had counted interest on a stolen child for twenty years while calling himself her salvation.

Master Corvane knew what she was. Had always known. Had built the cage around the very thing he feared, and charged her rent for the privilege.

"I need paper," she said.

Wendell blinked. "Paper."

"Ink. A nib. Whatever you can find in this hole." She was already standing, already feeling for the stub of pencil she kept in her sleeve the way other women kept knives. Twenty years of copying other people's words, other people's prophecies, other people's edits of the world. Her hands wanted to shake and she wouldn't let them. "Because tomorrow morning, Corvane expects me at my desk. And I'm going to walk in there and deliver exactly what he asked for."

Wendell's face went carefully blank. "The Ember?"

"No." Ivo smiled, and felt her mother's crooked eyetooth bare itself in the answer. "A bill of sale. His. Itemized. Everything he stole — with interest compounded over twenty years. I've decided to stop being the debt, Wendell."

She looked down at the coal glowing in her palm, patient as a promise.

"I've decided to start being the collector."
