## 7. A Bargain in Ash

The cellar smelled of tallow and old salt, and Wendell had learned, in the six days since the world ended, to sleep with one ear open.

He heard it now. Not a sound — an absence. The smuggler's quarter had a rhythm at night: drunks singing off-key two streets over, the groan of the tide gates, rats in the walls conducting their small economies. All of it stopped at once, like a held breath.

"Ivo." He said her name quietly, the way you'd set down something fragile.

She was already awake. She'd been sleeping less than he had, curled on a pallet near the coal shelf where the Ember sat in its iron dish, humming its low note that only she could hear. Her eyes found his in the dark and he watched her understand it too — the silence, the wrongness of it.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Too many to be burglars."

They moved without speaking, because they'd had six days to learn each other's silences and this was what six days bought you: a woman who knew to douse the lamp while you were still reaching for it, a man who knew to slide the Ember into its lead-lined satchel before his boots touched floor. Wendell slung the strap across his chest and felt the weight settle against his sternum like a second heart. In a way it was. It held more hearts than he could count.

The cellar door exploded inward.

Not kicked — *unmade*, the hinges simply ceasing, the wood falling apart along lines no saw had cut. Ashen Wardens came through the gap in their grey lacquered coats, and behind them, unhurried, came Master Corvane himself, holding a lantern that threw his shadow enormous against the stone.

Wendell did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat. Six Wardens, narrow stairway, one exit. Ivo beside him with a scribe's hands and no training. The Ember against his chest, which meant the Ember was already lost unless—

Unless someone carried it out through the coal chute.

There was exactly one person who could fit.

"No," Ivo said, because she'd been reading his face for six days too.

"The chute," he said. "It opens behind the tannery. Go left, not right — right is the watch post."

"Wendell—"

"*Go.*" And because he was a prophecy-keeper and lying was a sin he'd never mastered, he added the truth: "I'll be behind you. I promise nothing else."

That was the last lie either of them told that night, and it wasn't even a lie yet. It just became one.

She went. He heard the scrape of the chute, the tumble of her body into the dark, and then the Wardens were on him and there was no more time for anything but the work of losing well.

* * *

They took him to the Wardenhouse, which was built of the same grey stone as everything Corvane touched, and put him in a cell with no window, because windows were a kind of mercy and mercy was not in the budget. They took the soot-marked coat, the prophecy-keeper's cords from his wrist, the little knife he'd carried across a century. They left him the Ember's absence, which was heavier than any of it.

He sat on the bench and waited, because waiting was also part of keeping. Somewhere above him, dawn happened without his permission.

Corvane came at midmorning. Wendell knew it was midmorning by the strip of light under the door, and knew it was Corvane by the footsteps — unhurried, soft-soled, the walk of a man who had never once needed to run.

"You're older than the records say," Corvane observed, settling onto the stool across the bars as if visiting a friend. "The archive lists a prophecy-keeper named Wendell, sent forward with a relic. It lists him as dead. Paperwork error, I assume."

"I'm alive," Wendell said. "That's the error everyone keeps making."

"Mm." Corvane folded his hands. He had the face of a patient man, which is the most dangerous face there is. "Here is what will happen. You will tell me where the girl went. You will tell me what the Ember does. And then, if you're useful, you'll be permitted to continue existing, which is more than your order managed."

"And if I'm not useful?"

"Then you'll be ash in the incinerator chute by evening, and I'll have lost nothing but an afternoon's conversation." Corvane smiled faintly. "You've noticed, I hope, that I don't need to threaten you. Threats are for people who want something. I only need time, and I own all of yours."

Wendell leaned back against the cold wall. A century ago he'd have prayed. His order was dust now, its prayers outlawed, and besides — he'd read enough prophecy to know how prayers got answered around here.

"She won't come for me," he said.

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But she'll come for the ledger."

Wendell went very still.

"Ah," Corvane said, watching him. "You know about the ledger. Then perhaps you know what's written in it. Perhaps she told you — or perhaps you saw it yourself, in that coal of hers. A debt entered the day after her mother burned. A debt with no origin, no goods received, no service rendered. Just a number, and a signature." He unfolded his hands. "Mine."

The cell was very quiet.

"I signed her into bondage when she was seven years old," Corvane continued, in the tone of a man discussing drainage. "Her memories went into the confiscation vaults the same week. Do you know why? Because her mother hid something in her — or rather, her mother *was* something, and the child was the safest place to keep it. An Ember-keeper's line doesn't end when you burn the keeper. It ends when you burn every memory of her. And we couldn't burn those, because burning them would have—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—*inconvenienced* certain parties. So we shelved them. And we shelved the girl among them, blind, indentured, copying prophecies she wasn't allowed to read. For twenty years she has filed the evidence of her own erasure under my supervision."

Wendell thought of Ivo's face the night she'd pulled her hand back from the coal, shaking. *My whole life was someone else's edit.* He had thought grief was the worst thing the Ember could show a person. He'd been wrong. Grief was only the first draft.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

"Because you'll tell her." Corvane rose, smoothing his coat. "Or rather, I'll tell her, in front of you, and you'll watch her face do whatever it does. Tonight, at the exchange. She's already agreed to bring the Ember."

"She wouldn't—"

"She would," Corvane said, "for you. That's the trouble with people. They keep having things they'd trade anything for." At the door he paused, and looked back with something almost like kindness, which was the cruelest thing Wendell had seen in a hundred years. "For what it's worth, prophecy-keeper — I don't want the war back either. I want the Ember because it's the last loose page. Burn it, shelve it, crown it — any ending is better than an open book. You of all people should understand."

The door closed. Wendell sat in the dark and understood, finally, what it meant to be kept like a document: filed, referenced, produced when convenient.

He began, very quietly, to work on the hinge pins with the edge of a loose bench slat. He had until tonight. Prophecy had never given him more than that.

* * *

They brought him up at moonrise, bound, and stood him in the ash-yard behind the Wardenhouse, where the thirteenth moon was already climbing — fat, white, three nights from full, close enough now that you could feel it pulling at the tide of things.

And there was Ivo.

She stood at the far end of the yard with the satchel over her shoulder, flanked by two Wardens who weren't touching her, which meant she'd walked in on her own feet. Her face was pale and perfectly composed, the face of a scribe delivering a manuscript, and Wendell's stomach dropped through the ash beneath his boots, because he knew that composure. It was the composure of someone who had decided something and hadn't told anyone what.

Corvane stepped between them, lantern raised, and made a ceremony of it.

"Ivo Delacroix. Your debt stands at nine thousand marks, accrued interest included. Bring me the Ember before the moon reaches full, and the ledger closes. Freedom, clean paper, a life. Refuse, and the debt passes to your heirs — of whom you have none, so it passes to your bones, which I will keep in a drawer." He gestured, and a clerk scuttled forward with the contract itself, unrolled, the wax seal fat and red as a wound. "You may verify the terms."

"I don't need to verify them," Ivo said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. "I copied them myself. Twice. Watered ink, third draft, temple stall four." She looked at Corvane, and Wendell saw something move behind her eyes that had not been there six days ago — a banked light, patient and enormous. "You wrote me into your ledger when I was seven. You shelved my mother's face in a vault. You let me spend twenty years filing my own disappearance under *miscellaneous*."

Corvane's smile didn't waver, but his weight shifted, a fraction, toward the Wardens. "Terms are terms, scribe."

"Oh, I agree." Ivo lifted the satchel strap over her head. Held it out. "Here's the Ember, Master Corvane. As contracted."

The yard went silent. Even the Wardens stopped breathing.

Corvane reached for it — and Wendell, watching, saw the exact moment the trap closed, though it took him another heartbeat to see whose trap it was. Because Ivo's hand didn't open. The strap slid free of her shoulder and hung in the air between them, held by nothing, glowing through the canvas like a lantern through skin, and Corvane's fingers closed on empty cloth that suddenly wasn't there.

The satchel dropped to the ash. Empty.

The Ember rose out of it slowly, turning, shedding sparks like a molting star, and settled against Ivo's chest — not touching her, hovering a finger's width from her heart, humming a note that made the Wardens' blades ring softly in their sheaths.

"It only answers to me," Ivo said. "You knew that. It's why you couldn't touch it yourself. Why you needed a debt-bound girl with keeper's blood to fetch it, carry it, deliver it." She turned her head and looked at Wendell, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "You knew what I was before I did. You built my whole cage around it."

Corvane's voice came out flat now, all the patience spent. "Take her."

Six Wardens drew. Wendell strained against his bonds. The thirteenth moon cleared the tower line, huge and white, and Ivo Delacroix stood in its light with a living fire floating at her heart, and she smiled — small, terrible, entirely her own.

"Three nights," she said, to Corvane, to the Wardens, to the moon itself. "You have three nights to decide what you're willing to burn. Because I've read the prophecy, Master Corvane. I copy it every year." The Ember flared, and every shadow in the yard bent toward her like grass in wind. "And none of them end the way you planned."

Above the Wardenhouse, the moon climbed, and somewhere in the deep archive below the city, a vault full of confiscated memories began — very faintly, for the first time in twenty years — to glow.
