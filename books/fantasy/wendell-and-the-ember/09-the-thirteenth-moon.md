## 9. The Thirteenth Moon

The roof of the deep archive was built for no one. That was the point of it — a flat slab of black slate above the sealed stacks, ringed by gargoyles worn faceless, accessible only through a hatch Ivo had unlocked with a scribe's key and a scribe's crime. From up here Vell looked almost gentle: ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke into the dark, the ash-lamps of the lower quarters strung like dim orange beads, the temple spires cutting black wounds in the sky.

And above all of it, rising over the eastern wall like something that had been waiting a very long time for its cue, hung the thirteenth moon.

It was wrong, of course. Every calendar in Vell had thirteen months and twelve moons, and every clerk who noticed the discrepancy had been quietly reassigned or quietly disappeared. The thirteenth moon was the moon the Wardens had edited out of the sky's paperwork, and now here it was anyway, fat and white and faintly gold at the rim, as if the Ember's glow had bled upward into it.

Ivo stood at the roof's center with the Ember cupped in both hands, and it beat against her palms like a second heart.

She had climbed up here to decide. That was the lie she'd told herself on the stairs, anyway. Destroy it or crown it — those were the only two endings the prophecy allowed, Wendell had said, and he'd said it with the flat certainty of a man reciting his own obituary. Burn the witness, and everything it remembered died with it: her mother's face, the street that no longer existed, the names of everyone the old war had erased. Crown it — set it on some new brow, let it speak — and the war came back, whole and hungry, because a witness that loud always starts as testimony and ends as a call to arms.

Two doors. Both of them opened onto fire.

*There's always a third door,* her mother's voice said in her memory — not a memory she'd owned a week ago, but hers now, recovered, hoarded, *there's always the one they didn't draw on the map.*

Below her, the hatch banged open.

Corvane came up onto the roof the way he did everything: unhurried, immaculate, his grey robes spotless despite six flights of dust, his ledger — her ledger, her debt, forty pages of her life priced out in ink — tucked under one arm like a hymnal. Behind him came six Ashen Wardens in their bone-pale cloaks, and behind them, filling the parapet, more: a dozen, two dozen, cinders drifting off their shoulders, their masked faces turned up toward the illegal moon.

"Ivo." Corvane's voice carried in the cold air, mild as ever. "You've been busy."

"You've been predictable," she said. Her voice shook. She let it. Let him hear how much it cost her; let him think that was fear.

He stopped ten paces away, close enough that she could see the lamplight of the city reflected in his eyes, and nothing else. No reflection of the moon. She wondered if that was what happened to a man when he sold enough of himself to the ban.

"The Ember," he said. "Give it to me, and your contract burns tonight. Forty years of debt, gone. You walk out of Vell a free woman before dawn. You could be in Merrow by spring. A little house. A garden. Everything you counted your days toward, in that little tally you thought I didn't know about."

He did know about the tally. Of course he knew. He'd known about everything — the stall, the watered charms, the prophecies she copied without reading, the cage he'd built around her life and called an accounting.

"You erased my childhood," Ivo said. "Then you charged me rent on the empty room."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — not guilt. Irritation. The irritation of a craftsman whose mechanism has developed opinions. "Your mother left you a dangerous inheritance. I contained it. Containment has costs. You paid them. That isn't cruelty, Ivo, that's bookkeeping."

"Then here's my entry in the ledger." She raised the Ember. It flared against the thirteenth moon, coal-red bleeding into gold. "Paid in full."

"Take it from her," Corvane said, without heat, the way he ordered ink.

The Wardens moved.

And the roof moved first.

It came from the west parapet — a shape in soot-stained clothes hauling itself over the stone on arms that should have been too exhausted to work, dropping onto the slate between Ivo and the advancing line. Wendell. He was limping, his cell-grey face split at the eyebrow, his wrists still ringed with the red marks of Warden irons, and he was grinning like a man arriving late to his own funeral.

"How," Ivo said.

"There was a loose hinge," Wendell said. "In the cell. And in the prophecy. Turns out neither of us was written to stay where they put us." He planted himself at her shoulder, swaying, breath ragged. "Sorry. I know how this scene goes. In the version I was sent to live, I die here buying you three seconds."

"Not this version," Ivo said.

"No," Wendell agreed, watching the Wardens come. "I'm rather fond of this version."

The first Warden reached them — tall, ash-cloaked, cinder falling from its sleeves — and swung a rod of black iron that hummed with nulling-spells. Wendell caught it barehanded and screamed, and held on anyway, and Ivo felt the Ember answer him, felt heat roll up her arms like a tide coming in.

*Ivo,* the Ember said, inside her skull, inside her chest, in the voice that was a thousand voices braided into one. *Choose.*

"Burn it," Corvane called from behind his line. "Or give it up. Those are your options, girl. They were always your options."

And there it was — the trap she'd been circling all along, the two doors drawn on the map by the same hand that had drawn her cage. Destroy the witness. Crown the witness. Silence or war.

Her mother's voice again, patient as embers: *the one they didn't draw.*

Ivo looked at the Ember. Really looked. Not a weapon. Not a crown. A witness — the compiled memory of everyone the war had erased, every confiscated childhood, every name struck from every ledger, all of it burning in a shard of coal because no one had been willing to carry it. Because carrying it would mean becoming it.

Becoming it.

"Oh," she said aloud. "Oh, you absolute cowards. You couldn't destroy it and you couldn't let it speak, so you made *me* — made people like me — so someone else would have to hold it forever."

Corvane's composure cracked, just once, just enough. "Ivo. Whatever you're considering—"

"It's not destruction," she said. "It's not coronation. It's *custody.*"

She brought the Ember to her lips.

Wendell saw it first. "Ivo—"

"It remembers me," she said. "My mother. My street. Everyone they burned out of the record. It's been trying to come home since the day I was born." She looked at him, and found, absurdly, that she was smiling. "Tell it I said thank you. For the detour."

She swallowed the Ember whole.

* * *

Fire is a language. That was the first thing she learned, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, in the white-out instant when the coal went down her throat and the world went silent and Wendell's shout stretched into a note that never ended.

The fire spoke in memories.

They came all at once and they came slowly, which should have been impossible and wasn't, because time, she discovered, was just another thing the Wardens had edited. Her mother's kitchen, steam on the window. The street with the crooked lamp — her street, the one that no longer existed, suddenly existing again, brick by brick, voice by voice. A boy named Tam who had died holding a bridge. A woman who had sung to prisoners. An entire district that had stood where the temple gardens now stood, erased so thoroughly that even its ghosts had been assigned debts.

Every name. Every face. Every person the war had eaten and the peace had buried and the ban had forbidden anyone to mourn.

They poured into her, and it should have destroyed her — she understood that now, understood why the prophecy offered only burn-or-crown, because any ordinary vessel would have burst. But she was not an ordinary vessel. She was a debt-bound scribe who had spent her whole life copying other people's words without being permitted to keep a single one of her own, and keeping things was the one skill Vell had accidentally taught her completely.

She took the memories the way she'd taken dictation. Fast. Careful. Line by line.

The light came out of her then — not outward, not a blast, but *through*, a gold-white radiance rolling up under her skin, streaming from her eyes, her mouth, the seams of her, until she stood on the archive roof as a figure made of recorded fire, and every Warden on that parapet saw, in the blaze of her, the faces of the kin they'd been forbidden to remember. Mothers. Brothers. The ones who'd been taken for questioning and never logged as returned.

The rods dropped from their hands. One by one. Like snow deciding, finally, to stop falling upward.

When the light settled — sinking into her veins, banking down to a warm coal-glow beneath her skin, a heartbeat that was hers and also everyone's — Ivo Delacroix was still standing. Changed. Unburned. Full.

The thirteenth moon hung overhead, and for the first time in a century, it looked less like an intruder and more like a witness acknowledging another one.

Corvane stared at her across twenty feet of slate and silence. His ledger had slipped from under his arm. Pages of her debt lay scattered across the rooftop, turning slowly in the wind, forty years of arithmetic fluttering like dying birds.

"That," he said finally, and his voice had lost its mildness entirely, "is not possible."

"You should know," Ivo said. Her voice came out layered now, faintly doubled, hers and the chorus underneath. "You wrote most of my impossibilities yourself."

She reached down and picked up one page of the ledger. Just one. The first one. *Debt incurred: age seven. Item: memory extraction, full set. Balance outstanding.*

She pinched the corner between two fingers, and the paper caught fire — clean gold flame, obedient, hers — and burned to nothing without scorching her skin.

Around the parapet, the Wardens were kneeling. Not to her, she realized. To the faces they'd seen in her light. Grief does that, when it's been locked up long enough; it comes out looking like worship.

Wendell stood beside her, soot-streaked and bleeding and free — truly free, she could see it, the prophecy's script burned off him like a scab — and he was looking at her the way scholars look at texts they'd been told were apocryphal.

"So," he said. "What happens now?"

Ivo looked out over Vell — the chimneys, the ash-lamps, the spires, all of it lit faintly gold now in her sight, every hidden thing glowing to her like writing on a page only she could read. Somewhere below, temple bells had begun to ring, confused, counting a thirteenth hour that had never been on their schedule.

"Now," she said, tucking the last unburned page of her debt contract into her coat, "we go return some property. Starting with mine."

She stepped toward the hatch — and stopped, because far below, at the base of the tower, the great sealed doors of the deep archive were opening, and what came out of them was not Wardens.

It was a procession. Hooded, silent, carrying candles that burned the same gold as her veins — and at its head, a figure Ivo recognized from a memory that had been stolen from her before she could walk.

A figure wearing her mother's face.
