## 10. What Ivo Keeps

The fire went down my throat like a swallow of summer.

That was the first thing I understood about it — not pain, not the annihilation I had braced for on the archive roof with the thirteenth moon burning white above me and Corvane's Wardens closing like a noose. Warmth. The Ember had been a coal in Wendell's hands and a weapon in theirs, but in mine it was simply coming home. It slid past my ribs and unfolded there, and every erased year of my life lit up inside me at once.

I remember falling to my knees. I remember Wendell's hand hovering an inch from my shoulder, not daring to land, because he had read the prophecy a hundred times and the prophecy said nothing about this. The prophecy offered two roads — destroy or crown — and I had walked off the map between them, and the whole city held its breath to see if the ground would forgive me.

The light settled into my veins the way ink settles into paper. Not sitting *on* me. Becoming part of me.

And then I could see everything.

Not visions, exactly. Not the violent reel the Ember had shown me when I touched it in the smuggler's cellar, my mother's face swimming up out of the dark, her keeper's sigil, the night the Wardens came. This was quieter. This was *resident*. Somewhere behind my eyes was a room that had never existed before, and its shelves were full, and every shelf held someone Vell had decided to forget.

I stood up. My knees held. The moonlight came down through the archive roof's broken tiles and it did not pass through me — it bent toward me, faintly, the way water bends toward a drain, and I thought: *oh.* I was going to have to learn to control that.

Corvane stood ten feet away with his ledger under one arm, because of course he had brought the ledger. Master of the Scriptorium, owner of my debt, editor of my life. He had worn the same expression for eleven years — patient, proprietary, the look of a man watching an investment ripen. He wore it now, even as the light bled out from between my teeth when I spoke.

"Ivo." His voice was gentle the way a trap is gentle before it closes. "Whatever you've done, it can be undone. Give it to me. You were always meant to be its vessel — that's why we kept you close, why we fed you, why we—"

"Why you erased me," I said. "Yes. I know now. All of it."

Something flickered across his face. Not guilt. Recalculation. Eleven years of owning me, and he still thought the arithmetic would hold.

"Your contract," he said, "is ironclad. Signed by your own hand, witnessed by the temple, enforceable by—"

"By fire," I said. "Everything in Vell is enforceable by fire. That was always the problem."

I crossed to him. The Wardens tensed — six of them ringing the roof, ash-grey cloaks, blades drawn, the same blades that had chased Wendell and me through the lower quarters for nine days. Their captain, a woman named Serah whose face I had memorized during our flight and whose name I only learned *now*, in the flood, because her grandmother had been an Ember-keeper too, erased from every record except the one I had just swallowed — their captain took a step forward, and I raised one hand, and I touched Corvane's ledger.

Just the ledger. Just the contract bound inside it, the one with my signature, my debt, my eleven years priced out in ink and drams.

It caught like dry linen.

No drama. No roar. A small, clean flame that ran along the lines of text and ate them in order, line by line, clause by clause, until the page where my name had been was nothing but a lace of ash drifting off the roof into the thirteenth moon's light. Corvane snatched his hand back. The fire did not touch him. It had never been interested in him. It burned exactly what I asked it to burn and nothing more, which is more than I can say for any Warden or temple master in the history of this city.

"That's destruction of temple property," Corvane said. His voice had gone thin. "That's—"

"Paper," I said. "It was always just paper. You made us believe it was iron."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a hundred thousand erased lives: they are not quiet.

They are not screaming, either. I had expected screaming. What I got instead was something closer to a city at evening — a murmur, a vast low hum of ordinary things. A woman singing to a child in a dialect that hasn't been spoken in Vell for eighty years. A man counting coins. Someone laughing at a joke whose punchline I will keep forever, because the person who told it exists nowhere else now but behind my eyes. The Ember was never a weapon. I'd known that since the deep archive, since I read the original prophecies by scribe's-light three nights ago. It was a witness. It was everyone the war and the ban and the tidy erasures had taken, compiled into one coal and sent forward through time in the hands of a boy who loved it enough to carry it through a century.

And now it lived in me, and so did they.

Serah lowered her blade first.

I didn't ask her to. I hadn't planned anything — I want that on the record, whatever record survives, because Corvane will tell it differently. I was standing on the archive roof with ash on my lips and a moon overhead and no plan at all, and Serah looked at me, and I looked back, and I let her see.

That's all it was. I opened the door in the back of my mind — the room with the shelves — and I let her look in.

She saw her grandmother. I know she did, because she made a sound I have only heard once before, the night I found my mother's face in the Ember: a small, cracked note, the sound a person makes when a wound they'd forgotten they had suddenly remembers itself. Her blade rang against the rooftop tiles. She pulled off her gauntlet with her bare hand, and pressed her palm flat over her heart, the old salute, the keeper's salute, the one that had been outlawed so thoroughly that most Wardens probably didn't even know what it meant anymore.

But she knew. And the five Wardens behind her saw their captain do it, and one by one — slowly, the way a spell unwinds — they looked at me, and I let them look, and one by one their blades came down.

A woman saw her brother, conscripted and erased. An old man saw the street he grew up on, the one that burned in the last year of the war, the one that officially never existed. A young Warden, barely older than Wendell, saw nothing he recognized and wept anyway, because grief doesn't need a name to be real.

Corvane stood alone in the middle of all that lowered steel, holding a ledger with one scorched page, and for the first time in the eleven years I had known him, Master Corvane had nothing to say.

"You can't enforce the ban," I told him. Not cruelly. I want to be clear about that too — I wasn't cruel. I was tired, and full, and free, and those three things together make a person very difficult to frighten. "You can't arrest a memory. You can't confiscate what's inside a living woman. I am the archive now, Corvane. Every name you burned, I keep. Every street you unmade, I walk in here—" I tapped my temple "—every day. You outlawed magic because magic remembers what you did. Well. Now remembering has legs."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, the thing that proved he understood power better than anyone in Vell ever has: he closed his ledger, and he turned, and he walked down the archive stairs without another word. Not defeated. *Deferred.* I knew that even then. Men like Corvane don't end; they wait. But that was a problem for a tomorrow I finally owned.

* * *

Wendell found me at the roof's edge after the Wardens had gone, all except Serah, who stationed herself at the stair without being asked and has not really left since.

"So," he said. He sat down on the parapet next to me, boots dangling over a hundred feet of Vell, soot still in the creases of his knuckles from a journey across a century. "The prophecy ends here. 'At the thirteenth moon, the Ember shall be destroyed or crowned, and the road chosen shall remake the world.' That's the last line. There's nothing after it. I checked. I checked for a hundred years."

"Terrifying, isn't it," I said. "An empty page."

"It's the best thing I've ever seen." He turned the coal-black mark on his palm over, the keeper's sigil that had faded to almost nothing the moment I swallowed the Ember. Freed from the script. He could go anywhere now, do anything, be anyone other than the boy the prophecy wrote. "I could go back. The door I came through — it doesn't close for another three days. I could return to my own time. Report. Tell them the Ember reached safety."

"Is that what you want?"

He was quiet for a while. Below us, Vell sprawled in its usual grey — the ash-choked lower quarters, the temple spires, the market where I had sold watered-down charms by the dram for eleven years. Somewhere in it, people were waking to rumors. The Wardens standing down. The master of the Scriptorium walking home alone. A scribe on a roof glowing faintly at the seams.

"No," Wendell said. "I spent a hundred years being a delivery method. I'd like to try being a person. Preferably somewhere with decent bread."

"There's a stall on Cordwainer Lane," I said. "Terrible bread. We'll start there."

* * *

We came down from the roof at dawn.

I won't pretend Vell transformed overnight. Cities don't. The ban still stands in law; Corvane still has friends; there are Wardens in the provinces who haven't yet seen what Serah saw, and some of them will come looking, and I will show them, and some will understand and some won't. That work will take years. Maybe my whole life. Good. I have a life again — one that's mine, unedited, unsold, unbought. It seems right that it should have work in it.

But word travels fast in a city built on secrets, and by the time we reached the bottom of the archive stairs, people were already waiting in the courtyard. Not crowds. Not yet. A dozen, maybe two dozen — faces I half-knew from the market, faces I fully knew now from the shelves behind my eyes. A lamplighter whose father I remembered. Two sisters whose family name had been struck from every register in Vell. They didn't kneel. They didn't cheer. One old woman just looked at me and said, "You have her laugh. Marta. You have Marta's laugh," and then she cried, and I cried, because I did. I have Marta's laugh. I have all of them. That's the bargain I made on the roof, and I'd make it again.

Wendell stayed close, and when the crowd pressed in he put himself slightly in front of me, out of habit, out of a hundred years of guarding a coal — and then he caught himself and stepped aside, grinning, because I was not a coal anymore. I was considerably harder to carry.

"What now?" he asked, as we walked out through the temple gates into the morning, past the stall where I used to sell charms by the dram, past the notice board where my debt had been posted for eleven years, out onto the open road where the fog was lifting off the hills.

I reached into the fire behind my eyes — gently, the way you open a book you love — and let one memory rise to the surface, warm in my palm like a coal: a street that no longer exists, cobbled, sunlit, full of voices Vell tried to bury. I held it up to the light.

"Now," I said, "we start writing it all back down."

And somewhere behind my ribs, a hundred thousand voices murmured their agreement, and the road went on ahead of us, long and bright and utterly unwritten.
