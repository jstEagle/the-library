## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The rain in Vell did not fall so much as seep, the way guilt seeps — through seams, through shutters, through the spaces between a person's ribs where they thought they'd sealed themselves shut. Hazel Coldharbour stood beneath the crooked awning of her stall in Tallowmarket Row and watched it silver the cobbles, and she thought, not for the first time, that the city had been built by people who expected to be rained on forever and had made their peace with it.

She had not made her peace with anything. She had simply gotten very good at pretending.

"Charms," she said, and her voice came out low and rough from disuse, like a door that hadn't been opened all winter. "Luck-charms. Love-charms. Charms against toothache and tax collectors."

No one looked at her. That was the point. In Vell, magic was outlawed, and so the selling of charms had become an art of invisibility: you did not cry your wares, you murmured them; you did not display them, you kept them wrapped in oilcloth beneath a tray of perfectly legal buttons; you did not meet anyone's eyes, because eyes were how the Wardens found you, eyes and the particular smell of working magic, which was faintly of green things and struck flint and which Hazel had spent seven years learning to suppress until even she sometimes wondered whether there was anything left under her skin but a woman who sold buttons.

The buttons were real. She'd learned button-craft from a woman named Maren who ran the haberdashery two doors down and who believed Hazel was exactly what she appeared to be: a plain, quiet widow's daughter from upriver, unlucky in love, tidy in her accounts. Maren brought her tea on cold mornings. Hazel drank it and felt the warmth of the lie all the way down.

A figure detached itself from the grey press of pedestrians and stopped at her stall. Young man, clerk's ink-stains on his fingers, a ledger clutched under his arm the way other men clutched Bibles or weapons. His collar was soaked through and he was shivering, though Hazel suspected the shivering had started before the rain.

"You're the button woman," he said.

"I'm the button woman," Hazel agreed.

"Maren said—" He glanced over his shoulder. The market crowd flowed around them, umbrellas tilted, faces down. "Maren said you might have. Other stock."

Hazel's hands went still on the tray. This was the moment, every time, the moment where the whole careful architecture of her life balanced on a knife's edge. She could send him away. *I only sell buttons.* She could report him — some did, out of fear or spite or the small civic madness that anti-magic laws bred in people, the way damp bred rot. Or she could do what she always did, which was look at him properly for three seconds and decide whether he was worth the risk of herself.

He had the look of a man who had already decided to be desperate. Those were the safest customers, in Hazel's long experience. Desperation kept its mouth shut.

"What kind of luck?" she asked quietly.

"All of it." A wet laugh escaped him, half sob. "I'm due before the Assessor's office Thursday. There's a discrepancy in the harbor ledgers — not mine, my superior's, but his brother-in-law is a Warden sergeant, and if I sign the attestation I'm clean, and if I don't—" He swallowed. "My mother lives in Saltrow. If I'm arrested she has no one."

Saltrow. Hazel knew Saltrow. She had sold charms in Saltrow her first winter in the city, back when her hands still shook every time she worked the smallest cantrip, back when she still said *Mother Yew would know what to do* to herself like a prayer she couldn't stop repeating.

She reached beneath the tray.

The charm was a small thing, as her charms always were — a knuckle of river-smoothed wood, hazel wood, bored through and threaded on a leather cord, with something inside it that was not quite a seed and not quite a word. She had made it three nights ago by candlelight, whispering into it the way her mentor had taught her to whisper: *not commanding, asking. Magic in Vell must be polite or it will be noticed.*

"Three silvers," she said. "And you never met me."

"I never met you." He fumbled the coins onto the tray among the buttons, and his hand brushed hers, and he flinched — not at her, she realized, but at something he'd felt. People sometimes felt it. A warmth, a hum, like touching a windowpane on a summer day when frost was somehow on the other side. "What—"

"Don't ask questions." She pressed the charm into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "Wear it under your shirt. Don't tell anyone, ever, not even your mother, especially not your mother. And Thursday, when you go before the Assessor, speak the truth slowly. The charm doesn't make liars of other people. It just makes the truth land soft."

He stared at her. Rain dripped off the awning between them, stitching a curtain of silver thread.

"You sound like you've done this before," he said.

"Every day," Hazel said, "for seven years," and then hated herself for saying it, because it was too much, too revealing, and she watched the clerk's face try to fit that sentence together — *seven years* meant she'd been doing this since she was barely more than a child, meant there was a before, meant—

The bells began.

* * *

There were four kinds of bells in Vell, and Hazel knew them the way sailors know weather. The hour bells, honest and dull. The temple bells, sweet and lying. The alarm bells, which no one living had heard. And the Wardens' sweep-bells, which rang in threes, low and getting lower, like a heartbeat slowing down.

Threes. Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

The clerk heard it too. His face went the color of the rain. "Is that—"

"Go." She was already sweeping the coins into her apron pocket, already folding the oilcloth over the tray, her hands moving with the terrible calm that seven years of practice builds, the calm that is just fear wearing a workman's apron. "Walk. Don't run. Running is a confession. Walk toward the fishmongers' arcade and take the second left, and whatever happens, keep walking."

"But the charm—"

"Is nothing. It's a piece of wood. You bought a souvenir." She pushed the wrapped tray at him. "Carry that. Sellers get searched. Buyers get waved along. Go."

He went. She watched him merge into the crowd, hunched under his ledger, a man carrying buttons and a piece of hazel wood and his mother's whole future, and she allowed herself one breath of something that was almost hope before she turned to the business of dismantling her own life in under ninety seconds.

The false bottom of the stall came up. Into it went the charm-box, the whispering-shells she used to test for listening spells, the bundle of dried rowan, the little iron nail bent into a circle that Mother Yew had given her when she was nine years old and which she had carried, God help her, through everything, through the flight and the border and the seven gray years, unable to throw it away and unable to use it, a relic of a woman she had betrayed.

The awning came down. The stall folded flat, becoming a handcart, becoming furniture, becoming nothing. Hazel had designed it herself, in her first year, with a carpenter who asked no questions because she paid in charms against his lumbago. By the time the first grey cloaks turned into Tallowmarket Row, there was only a woman with a handcart of buttons standing patiently in the rain like everyone else, waiting to see what the city wanted from her today.

The Wardens moved through the market the way water moves through cloth — everywhere at once, touching everything, leaving everything colder. There were six of them. Hazel counted without appearing to count. They walked in pairs, and each pair had a sniffer: not a dog, though the dogs hated them, but a person, usually young, usually hollow-eyed, who had been trained — or drained — into sensitivity. Sniffers could taste working magic the way Hazel could taste a lie. They stood at intervals and breathed the market in, slow and horrible, like men smelling a feast.

One of them stopped ten feet from her cart.

Hazel became a button merchant. It was the deepest craft she knew now, deeper than any spell: the slight stoop, the chapped red hands (she rubbed them with nettle-juice each morning to keep them honestly ugly), the flat, patient boredom of a woman whose whole horizon was a tray of horn and bone. She let her mind go shallow and gray. *Buttons,* she thought. *Rain. Tea later, if Maren comes. Buttons. Buttons.* She had learned this trick from watching oysters, of all things — an oyster, threatened, closes and becomes a stone, and the stone is not a disguise, the stone is real, the stone is the oyster choosing to be less than it is.

The sniffer drifted closer. Close enough that she could see the fine tremor in his jaw, the way his nostrils whitened as he drew air across his tongue. Twenty. Fifteen feet. Hazel's heart maintained, by long discipline, the pace of a woman waiting for a bus.

Then the clerk — her clerk, her stupid brave shaking clerk — walked past the far end of the row with the button-tray under his arm, and the sniffer's head snapped toward him like a compass needle finding north.

No. No, no —

But the charm was quiescent. She'd made it quiescent; it wouldn't wake until Thursday, until his lips shaped truth in front of the Assessor. It should have read as dead wood, as nothing, as buttons —

The sniffer took two steps after him and stopped. Frowned. Shook his head like a man clearing a wrong note from his ear, and turned back to the market, and Hazel understood with a cold flood of relief that the boy's own fear had drowned the charm's faint signature — terror was loud, terror was a foghorn, and the sniffer had heard only a guilty conscience, which in Vell was as common as rain and proof of nothing.

The sweep passed. The bells faded. The market exhaled, and umbrellas bobbed, and somewhere a fishwife resumed cursing her neighbor with renewed vigor, and Hazel stood with her handcart in the rain and discovered that her legs were shaking now that it was safe for them to shake.

That was the thing about self-sufficiency, she thought, as she wheeled the cart toward home through streets running silver and black. Nobody told you that it was mostly adrenaline. Nobody told you that being enough for yourself meant being the only one holding the rope, every hour of every day, forever.

* * *

Her room above the chandler's shop on Rope Lane had one window, one bed, one stove, and forty-one hidden caches of magical supplies, of which the Wardens, in seven years of sweeps, had found none. She lit the stove, hung her coat to drip, and sat on the bed with the bent iron nail in her palm while the kettle came reluctantly to a boil.

Seven years ago tonight — near enough; the anniversary slid around the calendar like something alive — she had run. Not walked, not slipped away. Run, through the hedge behind Mother Yew's cottage, through the wet dark of the downs, with the old woman's screams behind her and Warden lanterns behind the screams, and she had not stopped running until she saw the walls of Vell, and then she had stopped, and looked back at the empty hills, and understood that she had traded one life for another and that both of them were now forfeit.

They had come for Mother Yew because of Hazel. Because Hazel had broken the law — the deep law, the mortal law, not the garden-law — and used forbidden craft to pull a drowning child back from the edge of death, there on the riverbank in front of witnesses, light streaming from her hands like something shameless, something joyful. She had been sixteen and the child had been blue and there had been no time to think, and afterward there had been nothing but time, and in all that time she had never once gone back, never once sent word, never once done anything but let an old woman hang for her sin — or worse than hang; the Wardens had gentler methods for witches, slower ones — while Hazel sold luck to clerks and called it survival.

*Mother Yew would know what to do.*

She caught herself thinking it again, the old prayer, and set the iron nail down on the sill as if it had burned her. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the city, and the gaslamps came on one by one along Rope Lane, each one a small stubborn argument against the dark.

Maren knocked at six, as she always did on Thursdays, with tea and gossip and a plate of seed cake. Hazel opened the door and arranged her face into the shape of a woman whose day had contained nothing worse than damp.

"You look peaky," Maren declared, pushing past her with the authority of fifty years and five foot nothing. "Sitting in that drafty market. You'll catch your death."

"I've caught worse," Hazel said, and poured the tea, and listened to gossip about the haberdasher's guild and the mayor's scandalous third wedding, and laughed in the right places, and was, for an hour, almost entirely a button merchant.

It was Maren, on her way out, pausing with her hand on the latch, who detonated the small bomb.

"Oh — nearly forgot. My niece Tabitha's girl, Pemmy, you remember, works at the mapmaker's on Quill Street? Well." Maren's eyes had gone bright with the particular joy of the matchmaker who has already won. "There's a young man there. Mapmaker's assistant. Wendell Ash. Quiet as church, but Pemmy says he's got lovely manners and a position, and he's agreed to meet you. Tomorrow evening. The Gilded Perch, by the canal, seven o'clock."

"Maren—"

"He's agreed, Hazel. Do you know how hard it is these days to find a young man who'll agree? Half of them are Wardens' informants and the other half are worse." She patted Hazel's cheek with a hand like a warm sparrow. "Seven o'clock. Wear the blue. You're not getting any younger, love, and neither am I, and I'd like to see you settled before I die."

The door closed behind her. Hazel stood in her small room with its forty-one secrets and its single window, and the rain wrote its endless gray script on the glass, and she thought about the word *settled*, and about how the last time she had let anyone close — truly close, close enough to name her, close enough to matter — she had destroyed them.

Tomorrow evening. A teahouse by the canal. A stranger named Wendell Ash.

She told herself she would not go. She told herself it firmly, the way she told herself every year that this would be the year she stopped keeping the iron nail on the windowsill.

Then she took the blue dress out of the chest at the foot of the bed, shook out its creases, and hung it by the stove to dry — telling herself, all the while, that she was only airing it.
