## 2. A Table for Two

The rain had been falling on Vell for three days without conviction — a thin, grey drizzle that never quite committed to being weather, only to being misery. It came down the chimney of the tenement on Corvess Lane in a fine smoke-smelling mist, pooled in the dip of the windowsill where the putty had failed years ago, and turned the cobbles outside into a river of dull pewter. Hazel Coldharbour knew every way rain could ruin a person. She had catalogued them the way other women catalogued suitors.

She was restringing a bundle of dried rowan berries at her window when Mrs. Pell knocked — which was to say, when Mrs. Pell opened the door without knocking and stood in it like a verdict.

"You're not dressed," said her landlady.

Hazel did not look up from her work. "It's half past four."

"And you'll want the whole hour to argue with me, so we may as well begin." Mrs. Pell was a broad woman built along the lines of a canal barge, with a voice that carried across water and a face that suggested she had personally audited every disappointment in the district and found most of them justified. She planted herself in the middle of the small room, hands on hips, and surveyed Hazel's domain: the drying herbs hung from the ceiling beams, the jars of tinctures labeled in a careful merchant's hand, the little table where Hazel stitched charms into ribbon and buttonholes and sold them, legally, as *keepsakes*.

"I've told you," Hazel said. "I'm not going."

"You haven't told me anything. You've told me *no*, which is different, and I don't accept it." Mrs. Pell advanced and sat down on the bed, which groaned. "My nephew Tam works the counting-house off Saltwharf Lane. Decent boy. Steady. He's got a friend — quiet sort, does maps for the Survey Office. Wendell Ash. And before you make that face—"

"What face?"

"That face. The one like you've bitten a lemon that bit you back." Mrs. Pell leaned forward, and for a moment the bluster dropped away, and what was underneath was worse: kindness. "Hazel. Love. You're twenty-six years old and you spend your evenings talking to dead plants. You flinch when people touch your shoulder. You haven't been to the Lantern Night festival once since you took my rooms, and I know for a fact you were in — I heard you moving about up here while the whole street was dancing."

"I had stock to finish."

"You had a wall to hide behind, is what you had." Mrs. Pell's voice gentled further, which was somehow more relentless than shouting. "I'm not asking you to marry him. I'm asking you to sit across a table from a living man who isn't buying anything from you, and drink tea, and let someone look at your face for an hour. That's all. One hour. If it's dreadful, you come home and I'll never say another word about it, and I'll even mean it, mostly."

Hazel set down the rowan berries. Her fingers had gone still in that particular way they did when old things stirred — seven years of careful, deliberate solitude shifting its weight like a sleeper turning over.

There were reasons she lived the way she lived. Good ones. Reasons with teeth. Every person she let close was a person who might one day notice the small wrongnesses about her: how she never quite looked at mirrors, how her charms worked a little too well, how she went grey-faced and silent whenever a Warden's whistle sounded in the street. Solitude wasn't loneliness. It was armor. She had spent seven years telling herself that, and she had gotten very good at believing things she told herself; it was practically a professional skill.

But Mrs. Pell was looking at her with those audit-everything eyes, and the rain was coming down without conviction, and somewhere under the armor there was a girl who remembered what it felt like to laugh at a table with someone who knew her name.

"One hour," Hazel said.

Mrs. Pell beamed like a lighthouse. "You'll wear the blue."

"I'll wear whatever—"

"The blue, Hazel. The one you keep folded at the bottom of the chest like it owes you money. Trust me. I've raised four children and buried two husbands, and I have never once been wrong about a dress."

* * *

The teahouse was called the Merrow's Rest, and it clung to the edge of the Gilt Canal the way old buildings in Vell clung to everything — out of habit and spite. Its lower floor was stone, dark with a century of damp; its upper story leaned out over the water on timber legs, and the windows of the first-floor sitting room gave onto the canal itself, so that the lamplight inside doubled in the black water outside and the whole room seemed to float between two reflections.

Hazel arrived early, because arriving early meant choosing the seat with her back to the wall and a clear view of the door, and because she wanted time to compose herself. She had worn the blue. She hated that Mrs. Pell had been right about the dress. It was a deep indigo thing with faded embroidery at the cuffs, seven years old and cut for a girl slightly softer than the woman she'd become, and when she'd caught herself in the cracked glass above the washstand she had looked — briefly, dangerously — like someone who might be worth looking at.

She ordered tea she didn't want and watched the door through the steam.

At five minutes past the hour, he came in.

She knew him immediately, though she couldn't have said how. Not by description — Mrs. Pell's intelligence had been vague on the matter of his appearance, offering only *quiet sort* and *does maps*. But something in the way he entered marked him: he paused just inside the doorway, dripping rain, and instead of scanning the room for his companion he looked first at the room itself — up at the beams, along the water-stained plaster, out the window at the canal — with an expression of such unguarded, scholarly delight that Hazel felt something in her chest loosen before she'd decided to let it.

He was tall in a way that suggested he hadn't entirely noticed being tall, with the slight stoop of a man accustomed to ducking under low archive shelves. Brown hair, rain-flattened. A long face that would never be called handsome by anyone in a hurry but which held, around the eyes, a kind of patient attention — the look of someone who had spent his life noticing things and keeping them. His coat was good wool gone soft at the elbows. Ink stained the cuff of his right sleeve, a constellation of small dark stars.

He saw her then, and his whole face went briefly, catastrophically blank, and then flushed, and then he crossed the room with his hand already extended and withdrew it halfway, and finally settled on a sort of bow that knocked his elbow against a passing waitress's tray.

"Miss Coldharbour? I'm — Wendell. Ash. Wendell Ash. Obviously. You knew that, or — I mean, Mrs. Pell would have—" He stopped. Took a breath. Started again, with the air of a man carefully setting down something fragile. "Hello. Thank you for coming. Please sit down before I knock anything else over."

And despite herself — despite seven years of practiced wariness, despite the little cold voice that reminded her this was a mistake, that closeness was a debt and debts were dangerous — Hazel laughed.

It startled her more than it startled him. It came out rusty and short, more breath than sound, but it was real, and his answering smile was so plainly, uncomplicatedly pleased — as if her laughter were a rare bird he'd been hoping to spot — that the cold voice faltered mid-sentence and forgot what it was saying.

They sat. The waitress brought tea, proper leaf tea in a brown pot, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence should have been awful and wasn't. Rain ticked against the window. Below, the canal moved its slow cargo of lamplight.

"So," Hazel said, because someone had to. "Mrs. Pell tells me you make maps."

"I do." He said it the way some men said *I'm in love* — with a helpless warmth he seemed unaware of. "For the Survey Office. Though 'make' is generous. Mostly I correct other people's errors, and mostly their errors are interesting, which is the problem. I'm supposed to fix them in an afternoon and instead I spend three days wondering how a man could survey the same street twice and get two different distances both times." He caught himself. "That's — I'm sorry. That's a terrible answer. Nobody wants to hear about surveying errors on a—"

"On a blind date arranged by a landlady who buried two husbands and fears nothing," Hazel said. "No, go on. How *did* he get two different distances?"

Wendell blinked at her. Then he lit up like a lamp turned wick-high, and told her.

She learned, over the next hour, that the street in question was Tanner's Row, and that its length varied depending on the direction it was walked, and that the discrepancy was small enough to dismiss and consistent enough to haunt him. She learned that he kept a notebook of such things — *anomalies*, he called them, in a self-deprecating tone that dared her to mock him — and that the notebook was nearly full. She learned that he drank his tea too strong and ate the seed-cakes in order of size, smallest first, "so the last one is the best," and that when she pointed out this was objectively insane he defended the position with the gentle immovability of a man arguing geometry.

And she learned — slowly, in pieces, the way you learn the shape of a room by candlelight — that he noticed things he shouldn't.

It started small. When she reached for the pot, he turned the handle toward her without looking, having tracked her habits for exactly one pour. When she mentioned the rain, he said, "It'll stop by nine. The gulls came inland this morning; they always know," and she thought, absurdly, *that's hedge-craft, that's weather-sense, that's what Mother Yew used to teach the first-years* — and crushed the thought like a spark in dry grass. When the waitress refilled their cups, Wendell thanked her by name, having read it off her apron, and asked after the limp she favored, and the girl went away pink to the ears, and Hazel watched him do it and understood that this was simply how he moved through the world: attending. Paying out attention like coin, to everyone, expecting nothing back.

Then, near the end of the second pot, he said, quietly, almost to himself:

"You count the exits."

Hazel went still.

"Not — I don't mean anything strange by it." His ears had gone red again. He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, not meeting her eyes. "It's just. When we sat down, you chose the chair facing the door. And when the two men came in earlier, wet from the wharf, loud — you didn't look at them, but you *did*, if that makes sense. In the window. Their reflection. And when the Warden's bell rang on the far bank you stopped breathing for about four seconds." He glanced up, apologetic and utterly without judgment. "I map places for a living. I suppose I can't help mapping people too. I'm sorry if it's rude. Most people find it rude."

Most people found it terrifying, Hazel thought. Most people, if they watched her that closely, would eventually see the other things — the way her eyes went to iron and away again, the charm-stitch scars on her fingertips fine as spider silk, the seven-year absence where a history should be.

But he wasn't afraid. He wasn't even curious, really, in the grasping way curiosity usually ran. He had laid his observation on the table between them like a stone he'd found and thought she might like, and now he waited to see if she'd take it or throw it back, and either way he wouldn't think less of her.

"My father was a smuggler," Hazel heard herself say.

It was a lie. It was also, in the strange economy of lies, a kind of gift — the first rung of a ladder she hadn't climbed in seven years. Her father had been a cooper. There had been no smugglers. But the lie explained the exits, and the exits needed explaining, and he nodded slowly and accepted it, and she realized with a small internal shock that she had chosen a lie that *protected him* rather than one that protected her. When had she last done that?

"He taught you to watch doors," Wendell said.

"He taught me to watch everything." That part was true enough to hurt.

The rain stopped, as he had promised it would, somewhere in the middle of the third pot. The clouds tore open in the west and the last of the daylight came through gold and sudden, and the canal below turned from pewter to hammered brass, and the whole floating room filled with light like a lantern with the shutter slid back. Wendell turned to the window to watch it, and the gold caught the ink-stars on his cuff and the rain still beaded in his hair, and Hazel looked at him — properly looked, letting herself — and felt the armor shift again, plate by plate.

Seven years. Seven years of selling luck to strangers and hoarding her own like a miser, of flinching from whistles and mirrors and kindness alike, of telling herself that alone was the same as safe. And here was a man who counted seed-cakes smallest-first and apologized to waitresses and mapped people because he couldn't help it, sitting across from her in a borrowed hour, asking nothing, seeing everything, and finding — apparently — nothing in her that made him want to leave.

It was hope. That was the danger of it. Hope was the one charm she'd never learned to stitch, the one her mentor had said couldn't be made, only risked.

"Miss Coldharbour?" He had turned back, and there was something shy and resolute in his face, the expression of a man stepping off a known map. "This is going to sound mad. And you're entirely free to say no, and Mrs. Pell will never hear it from me, and if you say no I'll simply—"

"Wendell."

"Yes?"

"Breathe. Then ask."

He breathed. Then he reached into his coat and drew out a slim book — old, by the look of it, bound in leather gone the color of tea leaves, its corners worn round — and laid it on the table between the cups with both hands, the way one sets down something that might wake.

"Before I ask anything," he said, "there's something I have to tell you. About why I agreed to tonight. And it involves this." He looked at the book, then at her, and his eyes were steady and frightened and honest, and outside the window the last light drained off the canal like a tide going out.

"It involves both of us," he said. "By name."

The teahouse lamps guttered, all together, though no door had opened.
