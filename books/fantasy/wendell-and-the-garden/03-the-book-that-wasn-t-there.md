## 3. The Book That Wasn't There

Wendell Ash had spent the whole walk to the teahouse rehearsing a version of himself that did not exist: a man who was easy, who laughed at the right moments, whose hands stayed still on the table instead of folding and unfolding like paper birds. He had practiced smiling in the dark glass of a stationer's window and abandoned the practice immediately. The reflection had looked like someone being polite to a stranger.

The rain had followed him from the archive district all the way down to the canals, thinning as it went, until it was less rain than a fine grey mist that beaded on his coat and made the lamplight along the water look soft-edged, as though Vell itself were slightly out of focus tonight. The canal moved slow and green-black beneath the bridges. Somewhere upstream a barge rang its bell twice, unhurried, and the sound traveled across the water the way sounds do in rain — closer than it should have been.

He was early. He was always early. Being early meant he could choose the seat with his back to the wall, which meant no one could look over his shoulder, which mattered more than it should have, because of the book.

He thought about the book the way a man thinks about a stone in his shoe. Not constantly. But every few steps, there it was.

* * *

The Vell Municipal Archive kept its catalog in forty-two ledgers, each one cross-referenced by hand in three different inks — black for holdings, red for restricted, blue for lost or destroyed. Wendell had worked among those ledgers for six years, first as a copyist's assistant and now as a junior cartographer-archivist, which was a grand title for a man who mostly corrected the spelling of street names on survey maps and fetched boxes from the deep stacks. He loved the work with an intensity he found slightly embarrassing. There was a rightness to it. Every document had a place. Every place had a number. Nothing in the archive was ever simply *gone*; things were only misplaced, waiting for someone patient enough to find them.

Which was why the book troubled him so badly. Because the book was not misplaced. The book was not anywhere at all.

He had found it on a Tuesday in the third week of autumn, in the reading room's reference alcove, wedged upright between a folio of canal easements and a water-damaged history of the Warden corps. It was a slim volume bound in grey-green leather gone soft as chamois, unlettered on the spine, and when he pulled it free the space behind it did not gape the way a missing book leaves a gap. It closed up. He remembered noticing that — remembering it clearly, the way one remembers the details of dreams precisely because they are impossible — the neighboring volumes easing together like water finding its level, so that when he glanced back a moment later there was no room on the shelf where the book had been, and no book in his hands either, except that there was, warm and real, smelling faintly of crushed leaves and candle smoke.

He checked the ledgers. All forty-two. Black ink, red ink, blue ink. No entry. No accession number, no acquisition record, no card in the card drawers, not even the ghost of one — no pale rectangle where a card had been removed, no pencil shadow of a title rubbed away. The book had never been catalogued, which in the archive's quiet theology meant the book had never existed.

And yet he had read it. Twice. He could not now recall most of what it contained — that was another wrongness, the pages sliding out of memory like wet leaves through fingers — but he remembered the part that mattered, because he had copied it out by hand onto a slip of archive paper and then, in a fit of sense, burned the slip in the reading-room stove, and then, in a far worse fit of sleeplessness, written it out again from memory:

*When the ash-born meets the runaway of the cold harbour, under a leaking roof and a shared light, the hedge shall open, and the garden shall call its own home.*

He had told himself it was a joke. Someone's idea of humor — a cartographer's prank, a test for new staff, a piece of Victorian nonsense slipped into circulation by some bored clerk a hundred years ago. Archives attracted that sort of thing; every collection had its forged map of Vell-with-a-second-moon, its letter from a king who never reigned. Except that the paper was old in a way forgery rarely managed, the ink browned at the edges of each stroke, and the binding leather had been dyed with something that shifted color when he tilted it toward the lamp — grey to green to a green so deep it was nearly black, like looking down into the canal at night.

And except for the names. *Ash-born.* His surname was Ash. He had been born, as far as anyone knew, in Ashlane, two streets over from the ash-yard where the tanners burned their scraps. And *the runaway of the cold harbour* —

Well. He hadn't known what that meant. Not until his landlady's landlady, Mrs. Petch, had pressed a folded note into his hands outside the baker's on Thursday and said, in the tone of a woman settling a debt, "Her name's Hazel Coldharbour. Charm-seller, top of Gullwing Stairs. Don't tell her I said the Coldharbour part, she doesn't use it. Seven o'clock, the Perch and Penny, don't be late, and Wendell—" here Mrs. Petch had gripped his sleeve with surprising strength, "—be *kind*. She's had a hard road."

Coldharbour. Runaway. Under a leaking roof and a shared light — the Perch and Penny's roof leaked; everyone in Vell knew it; the owner had been promising repairs for a decade and the rain came through over table four in a bright thread of drips that they caught in a copper pot.

He had almost not come. He had stood in his rooms above the map shop for an hour with his good coat half on, telling himself that coincidence was the mortar of the universe, that cities were full of ashes and harbours and runaways, that prophecy was the name frightened people gave to pattern-seeking. And then he had put the coat on anyway. Because underneath the fear, quieter than the fear, was something he had never admitted to anyone: he had spent his whole life feeling like a word in a language he couldn't speak. Like a page cut from a book and pasted into another one, roughly, at the wrong size. If there was even a chance the grey-green book knew why—

The door of the Perch and Penny opened ahead of him, spilling warmth and lamplight and the smell of burnt sugar, and Wendell stopped rehearsing, took a breath that tasted of rain and coal smoke, and went in.

* * *

She was already at table four.

He recognized her before Mrs. Petch's description finished assembling itself in his mind — the charm-seller from the stairs, though he'd only ever seen her at a distance, hunched under her awning against the weather. Up close she was thinner than he'd expected, with rain-frizzed dark hair escaping a braid and eyes that did a quick, professional sweep of him as he approached: coat, boots, hands, face. Cataloguing him. He knew that look; he gave it to documents. He wondered what category he'd been filed under, and suspected it wasn't flattering.

"You're Wendell," she said. Not a question.

"I'm early," he said, which answered nothing, and felt his ears go hot. "I mean — yes. Wendell Ash. Sorry. Hello."

Something flickered across her face at the *Ash* — quick, there and gone, like a fish turning under the canal's skin — but she only pulled out the chair opposite her. "You're dripping," she said. "Sit before you flood the floor."

The table between them held a dented teapot already sweating steam, two cups, and the copper drip-pot positioned under the leak, where water gathered and fell with a soft, regular *plink* that would become, within the hour, the rhythm everything else arranged itself around. He hung his coat, sat, and discovered that the seat with its back to the wall had been taken — she'd chosen it herself, he realized, and something about that steadied him oddly. Two people who both needed to see the door. At least they'd understand each other about that.

They talked about nothing for a while, and the nothing was strangely comfortable. She asked what he did and he explained maps — the corrections, the surveys, the way the city changed faster than the paper could hold it, new bridges, filled-in canals, streets renamed after aldermen nobody remembered. She listened with her chin on her hand and said, "So you spend your life writing down where things are," and he said yes, and she smiled for the first time, small and crooked, and said, "That's either the most honest job in Vell or the most futile," and he laughed — actually laughed, surprised by the sound coming out of him — and said, "Most days I'd say futile, but I'm paid in futility, so."

The tea was over-steeped and bitter. The drip-pot filled and was emptied and filled again. Outside, the mist thickened until the canal lamps became smudges of gold, and the little teahouse seemed to contract around their table, warm and close, the world beyond the fogged windows reduced to rumor.

It would have been easy, he thought later, to let the whole evening stay there — in the safe country of small talk, two lonely people enjoying an accidental kindness. But the book was in his shoe, and it ground with every minute, and when she laughed at something he said about aldermen — really laughed, briefly, before catching herself as if laughter were a coin she couldn't afford to spend — he heard himself say:

"Can I tell you something strange? You're allowed to get up and leave afterward. I want that understood in advance."

Her smile cooled by degrees, wary again, but she nodded. "Go on."

Wendell turned his cup in its saucer once, twice, buying time in rotations. Then he reached into his satchel — he had brought it, God help him, he had brought it to a blind date like a man bringing evidence to his own trial — and set the grey-green book on the table between the teapot and the drip-pot.

"This isn't from my shop," he said. "I mean, it is, it was, it's — I work in the city archive. Maps, records, that sort of thing. Three weeks ago I found this on a shelf in the reading room. Only it isn't on any inventory. Not any of them. Forty-two ledgers, and this book is in none of them. No record it was ever acquired. No record it exists." He heard his own voice going too fast and forced himself slower. "In my line of work, that's not unusual, exactly. Things go astray. But things leave *traces*. A gap on the shelf. A card. A note in a margin somewhere saying *missing, presumed destroyed.* This has nothing. When I take it off the shelf, the other books close over the space like — like water. Like it was never there. And when I put it back—"

"Put it back where?" Hazel said quietly. Her eyes hadn't left the book. Her hands had gone very still on the tabletop, stiller than stillness, the way a person goes still when they've heard a sound in another room.

"That's the thing." He swallowed. "There isn't a where anymore. The shelf closes. And then a week later it's back, in a different alcove, wedged somewhere new, and the space closes behind it again. It moves. Or—" he laughed, without much humor, "—or I'm losing my mind, which honestly is the leading theory. My second theory is that some clerk a century ago had a very elaborate sense of humor."

"And the third theory?"

He turned the book around so it faced her. The cover was blank. It was always blank; he had never seen a title on it, and yet he had never once doubted which book it was.

"The third theory is that it's real," he said. "And that it's about us. About tonight."

The plink of the drip-pot sounded very loud. Somewhere behind the counter a kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Hazel didn't move. In the lamplight her eyes had gone the flat reflective grey of the canal, and Wendell realized with a lurch that he had made a mistake — that whatever he'd imagined this confession would buy him, her silence was costing more than he'd budgeted for, and she was measuring the distance to the door.

"The book says—" he began.

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice underfoot. "Don't say it."

"*When the ash-born meets the runaway of the cold harbour,*" he said anyway, because apparently he was the kind of fool who stepped on the ice anyway, "*under a leaking roof and a shared light, the hedge shall open—*"

"—and the garden shall call its own home," she finished.

The words dropped between them like a stone into a well. They listened together for the splash, and it went on falling, and falling, and did not land.

"You know it," he whispered. "You've read it. How could you have — it's not in any catalog, it doesn't—"

"My mother had a copy." Her voice had gone somewhere far away and very young. "Or a piece of one. Pages. She used to read it like scripture, or like — I thought it was nonsense. I thought it was the kind of nonsense that gets people burned." Her eyes came up and fixed on him, and he saw that the wariness had inverted somehow; she was no longer deciding whether to flee from him but whether to flee *with* him, and both options terrified her equally. "Say your name again. Your whole name."

"Wendell Ash."

"Ash-born," she breathed. "Born where?"

"Ashlane. Off the ash-yard, technically, my mother worked the—"

"Stop." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Stop. This is — Wendell, listen to me. Whatever this is, whoever gave you that book, whatever game—"

"No game." He leaned forward, and his voice came out lower and steadier than he felt, steadier than he had ever felt about anything. "Hazel. I checked the ledgers. All of them. I checked the accession books back to the founding. I checked the restricted index, the destruction lists, the private collections register. I am *good* at finding things — it's the only thing I'm good at, it's the whole of me, and I am telling you this book does not exist, and it is sitting on this table, and it named you seven years before I ever heard your name, and it named me before I was born, probably, and I have spent three weeks telling myself I imagined it and I cannot — I *cannot* make myself believe that anymore, and the worst part, the truly unbearable part—"

His voice broke. He hadn't planned for that. He stared down at his hands, folded on the table like paper birds, and heard himself finish in almost a whisper:

"—the worst part is how much I *want* it to be true. Because if it's true, then there's a reason I've always felt like a page pasted into the wrong book. A reason I can't remember being a child. A reason my mother went white whenever I asked about before Ashlane. If it's true, then I'm not broken. I'm just — from somewhere else."

The kettle behind the counter reached its whistle and was taken off the heat. The drip-pot filled. Plink.

When he finally dared look up, Hazel Coldharbour was crying — silently, furiously, the way people cry who learned young that tears cost money — and she was looking at him with an expression he had no category for, no drawer to file it in, an expression like grief and recognition having a child.

"I ran away from someone," she said. "Seven years ago. From a teacher. From — it doesn't matter. What matters is that the last thing she ever said to me was a prophecy. About a boy who casts no shadow." She laughed, wet and awful. "I thought she was cursing me. I thought it was a warning. I built my whole life on the assumption that if I stayed small enough, sold charms small enough, wanted anything small enough, whatever she saw coming wouldn't find me."

"Hazel—"

"And then Mrs. Petch corners me outside the bakery and gives me your name, and I thought, fine, one evening, one cup of tea, I can survive one evening of pretending to be a normal woman. And you sit down and you're *kind*, which is worse than rude, rudeness I could have handled, and now you put that book on the table and say the words my nightmares are made of, and you *believe* them." Her voice rose, fraying. "Do you understand what you've done? For seven years nobody has believed me about anything, and I liked it that way, belief is dangerous, belief is how they find you — and you sit there with your mapmaker's hands and your terrible sincere face and you *believe*, and now it's real, now it's happening, now the roof is literally leaking on us like something out of a—"

She stopped.

Wendell had stopped breathing. Because she had said it — *something out of a* — and the sentence had died in the air between them, unfinished, unnecessary, because they both knew how it ended. *Under a leaking roof and a shared light.*

The lamp above their table guttered. Not dimmed — guttered, the flame bending sideways as though a draft had come through a door that was not open, and the shadows of the teahouse swung like weathervanes in a storm. The drip-pot overflowed, unnoticed. On the table between them, the grey-green book lay with its blank cover facing the ceiling, and in the shifting light the leather moved through its colors — grey, green, that green so deep it was nearly black — and then, slowly, like frost forming on a window, like something waking and stretching after a hundred years of sleep, letters surfaced on the cover. Old letters. Browned at the edges of every stroke.

Two lines. Two names, curling into being as they watched, the ink crawling like living things:

*Wendell Ash.*
*Hazel Coldharbour.*

And beneath them, in smaller script, still writing itself, letter by deliberate letter, as the lamp bent low and the fogged windows went dark as pond ice:

*Tonight.*
