## 6. Rules of the Withered Ground

The fox-headed herald walked ahead of them through the petrified orchard, and his shadow moved wrong.

Hazel noticed it first because she had spent seven years training herself to notice things — a Wardens' cloak at the end of an alley, a customer's hand drifting toward her charm-pouch, the particular stillness of a street that meant someone was watching. Noticing was how she'd stayed alive in Vell. So she noticed that Briarlock's shadow fell to the east while the pale, sourceless light of this place came from everywhere and nowhere, and that it lagged half a step behind him, like a dog that had learned not to trust its master.

She decided not to mention it. There were already too many things to mention.

The orchard stretched in every direction: rows of trees that might once have been apple or pear or something no mortal orchardist had ever named, now frozen mid-decay into grey stone. Fruit hung from the branches like fists of slate. When the wind moved — and the wind did move, though Hazel couldn't feel it on her skin, only see it dragging through the higher branches with a sound like sand poured over sand — the stone fruit knocked together with a hollow clacking that echoed far longer than it should have.

"Is everything here dead?" Wendell asked. His voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when he was genuinely unsettled, which Hazel was beginning to recognize as different from the polite quiet he used when he was merely embarrassed. In the two hours since they'd been pulled through a hole in the world, she had learned perhaps four things about Wendell Ash, and one of them was that he asked questions even when he was afraid. Especially when he was afraid.

"Not dead," Briarlock said, without turning. His voice was a dry rustle, like paper being folded. "Sleeping badly. There is a difference, though I grant you it narrows."

He was small — barely up to Hazel's ribs — and walked upright with a herald's staff taller than he was, topped by a lantern that burned with a flame the color of old bruises. His coat was once-fine livery gone threadbare at every seam, embroidered with sigils Hazel almost recognized and then couldn't look at directly, the way you can't look at your own face in a dark window. His head was entirely a fox's: sharp-eared, amber-eyed, the muzzle moving expressively when he spoke as if the human body beneath it were an afterthought.

"Where is *here*, exactly?" Hazel asked. "Before we go any further. I want it said plainly."

Briarlock stopped. He turned with the unhurried precision of a creature who had waited a very long time for this conversation and intended to conduct it properly.

"The Sundered Garden," he said. "The borderland. The hedge between the mortal realm and the Otherworld. Once it was a garden in truth — tended, ordered, alive. A place where the two worlds touched without tearing." He gestured with the staff, and the bruise-colored flame swung across the stone orchard, lighting row after row after row of it, all the way to a horizon that hazed into grey. "Now it is what you see. The hedge has gone to rot from both sides."

"And we're standing in the rot," Hazel said.

"You are standing in the rot," Briarlock agreed. "You have been standing in it since the moment you drew breath, child, but until tonight you stood on the mortal side of it, where the rot is better hidden." His amber eyes fixed on her, and there was nothing animal in them at all. "Vell is built on the hedge's near edge. Did you never wonder why magic curdles there? Why the Wardens' wards work so well, and why the city forgets things other cities remember?"

Hazel's mouth went dry. She thought of the luck-charm she'd sold that morning — sold, before all this, in another life, hours ago — and how she always told buyers to keep it away from running water. Running water carried things away in Vell faster than it should. She'd known that the way you know a scar: intimately, without asking where it came from.

"Tell us," Wendell said softly. "All of it. Please. I'm a mapmaker. I can't bear an incomplete map."

Something flickered across the fox's muzzle that might have been approval.

* * *

They walked as he talked, because Briarlock refused to talk standing still — "the ground listens better when you give it somewhere to go" — and so they moved down the endless rows of stone trees while the herald laid out a century of ruin in his dry, folding-paper voice.

"There were three oaths," he said. "When the worlds were young enough to be foolish, they made war on each other — the way neighbors do, over fences and wells and whose cattle trampled whose field. The war would have ended both. So three keepers came to the Heartwood Gate, at the garden's center, and each swore an oath, and the three oaths together wove the covenant: the covenant that keeps the mortal realm and the Otherworld from devouring one another. The Sundered Garden was made to house the covenant. It was the loom. The oaths were the threads."

"What kind of oaths?" Wendell asked.

"Each keeper gave something that could not be taken. The first gave their whole self — name, memory, will — and became the Gardener, the one who tends the covenant's roots forever. The second gave their freedom, binding themselves to the gate as its living lock. The third—" Briarlock paused, and for the first time his staff dipped, the bruise-flame guttering low. "The third gave their death. They swore to die when the covenant died, and no sooner, so that the covenant would always know if it was failing. Three keepers. Three threads. A weave that cannot be mended by fewer than three."

The wind dragged through the high branches again, that sound like sand over sand, and somewhere far off the stone fruit clacked against itself in long rolling waves, like applause from an audience of the dead.

"And now?" Hazel asked, though she already knew. You always knew, with stories like this. Her mentor had taught her that much before Hazel had taught herself everything else the hard way. *Every bargain has three parts, girl: what's given, what's kept, and what's owed. Learn to tell them apart or the telling-apart will be done to you.*

"The first went mad," Briarlock said. "Tending alone will do that. The second vanished — a hundred years ago, on a night like tonight, and the gate has been locked ever since. And the third—" the fox's ears flattened, "—died. Slowly. Over decades. The covenant does not fail all at once, children. It frays. Like rope. Like cloth. Like a hedge."

He stopped walking. They had come to the edge of the orchard, where the rows ended at a ridge of grey earth, and beyond it Hazel saw the river.

It was the color of dishwater and moved like syrup, slow and heavy, carrying nothing visible — no leaves, no fish, no reflection of the sourceless sky. It simply flowed, obediently, in a direction that Hazel's eyes kept sliding off of. Along its bank grew reeds that were also stone, and among the reeds stood shapes that Hazel looked at once and then carefully did not look at again, because they were roughly man-shaped and roughly kneeling, and she had seen enough statues in her life to know that some of them had been people who chose poorly.

"The grey river runs between the garden and the Otherworld," Briarlock said. "It is the covenant's moat, and it is going out, the way a tide goes out. When it runs dry, there is no border. When there is no border, the Otherworld pours into yours, and yours into the Other, and neither can hold the other's weight. Cities unravel. Seasons forget their order. People forget each other." He turned to face them fully, and the lantern-light made his amber eyes glow like coals in a dying fire. "The covenant must be renewed at the Heartwood Gate before the next dark moon. Eleven nights from now. By three oath-keepers, giving freely what the oaths require."

"Three," Hazel repeated. "We're two."

"Yes," said Briarlock.

"And you've been waiting a hundred years for us," Wendell said. It wasn't quite a question. "For the prophecy. For the ash-born and the runaway of the cold harbour."

"I have waited a hundred years for anyone," Briarlock said. "That the prophecy named you specifically is either great fortune or great cruelty, and I have not yet decided which." He began walking again, along the ridge above the grey river, and they followed because there was nowhere else to follow. "Understand what you have inherited. This is not a quest. Quests are for heroes, and heroes are a story mortals tell themselves so that sacrifice sounds noble. This is a debt. An inheritance of debt. Neither of you asked for it, and neither of you can refuse it, because refusing it means watching everything you love come apart at the seams — slowly, at first, and then all at once."

Hazel hugged her arms around herself. The air here had no temperature, exactly — it neither warmed nor chilled her — but she felt cold anyway, a cold that started behind her sternum and worked outward. Seven years. Seven years of careful, small, survivable life. Selling charms under three false names. Keeping her head down. Keeping her power folded up small and hidden like contraband. And all of it, the whole time, she had been standing on the rotten edge of a dying world, and hadn't known, and couldn't have done anything about it anyway—

Except that wasn't true, was it. The prophecy book had found *her*. Had named her. *The runaway of the cold harbour.* Someone, somewhere, a century ago, had written her into this story, and she had run straight into its open arms without even seeing the trap, because she'd been lonely enough to say yes to a blind date.

"We should turn back," she said. "Through the tear. However we got in, we get out the same way, and we let the realms—"

"You cannot," Briarlock said, not unkindly. "The tear closed behind you. Tears do not stay open; they are wounds, and wounds close. And even if it had not — even if you stood on the mortal side of the hedge right now — the fraying would find you. It is already finding Vell. You felt it, surely. Things going missing that shouldn't. Names slipping. Streets that rearrange themselves overnight." He looked back at her, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "You are hedge-born, child. Both of you, in your ways. The fraying touches hedge-born first. It always has."

Wendell made a small sound beside her. Hazel glanced at him. He was staring at the river with an expression she couldn't read — not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. As if the grey water were reminding him of something.

"Briarlock," Wendell said slowly. "The second oath-keeper. The one who vanished. Vanished *how*? Do you know?"

"I know what the garden knows," the fox said. "And the garden remembers a woman of power, bound to the gate, who tore herself loose of her binding and fled into the deep places — mad with grief, or clever with purpose, or both. The Hollowed have taken many into their keeping since. Some captives live a long time down there." His muzzle twitched. "Longer than they should."

Hazel's heart did something complicated and painful. She thought of a cottage that smelled of yew bark and woodsmoke. She thought of a door slamming behind her, seven years ago, and running, and never looking back, because looking back was the one thing she'd promised herself she'd never do.

She didn't say any of this. But Briarlock's ears swiveled toward her anyway, the way a fox's ears swivel toward a mouse under snow, and she understood that the herald missed nothing, and that in this place she would have to learn to guard her thoughts as well as her tongue.

* * *

They made camp that night — if night it was; the sourceless light dimmed to a deeper grey, and Briarlock called it night, so night it was — in the shell of a stone barn at the orchard's edge. The walls were petrified timber grown over with mineral lace, and the roof had fallen in long ago, leaving a jagged crown of rafters against the darkening sky. Briarlock set his lantern in the center of the floor and the bruise-flame threw their shadows huge and wavering up the walls: two tall ones, one small one, and the small one's shadow arriving late to everything.

Hazel sat with her back against the wall and tried to inventory what she had left. One satchel: three luck-charms, a warding knot, a packet of salt, a stub of candle dressed with her own blood, a needle, black thread, and eleven copper coins from a city that might not exist in a month. One knife. One coat. One companion, currently sitting across the dead fire-ring, turning his own hands over and over as if checking them for damage.

One name. That was the thing that kept snagging. *Names have power* was the first law Mother Yew had ever taught her, before anything else, before kindness even — and here was a herald talking about oaths that required names given freely, and Hazel's whole body had gone tight and defensive around hers like a fist around a coin.

"Wendell," she said.

He looked up. In the bruise-light his face was all planes and shadows, and there was something about it she couldn't stop noticing — something that didn't fit, some quality of the light on him that was different from the light on her, though she couldn't have said how. Flowers, she remembered. Briarlock had said flowers bloomed where he slept. She'd filed that away with the lagging shadow and the wrongness of the light, in the growing drawer of things about Wendell Ash that didn't add up.

"When you gave your name at the well," she said. "At the Well of Names. What did it cost you? Exactly."

Wendell's hands went still.

"It's gone," he said quietly. "Vell. The archive. The teahouse — I know we were in a teahouse, I know it mattered, I can feel the shape of the mattering like a hole in a tooth. But the evening itself—" He shook his head slowly. "I remember arriving *here*. I remember the tear opening. Everything before the tear is... smoothed over. Like a page someone erased and wrote on top of. I can see the ghost of the writing underneath, but not the words."

Hazel's chest ached. Because she remembered the teahouse — the rain on the canal, the terrible tea, the way he'd laughed at her joke about the landlady, the exact moment the conversation had stopped being an ordeal and started being easy — and he didn't, and that asymmetry felt like a theft committed against something that had barely gotten the chance to exist.

"That was the toll," she said. "Your memories of home. Of the beginning."

"So it seems." He managed something that was almost a smile. "I'd like to think I'd have paid it anyway. If I'd known. I'd like to believe I'm the sort of man who pays his debts."

"You don't have to perform bravery for me," Hazel said, sharper than she meant to. "Not after less than a day."

"No," he agreed, and to her surprise he didn't flinch. "But I'd rather owe the truth than the performance. That's the same lesson, isn't it? From your herald's lecture. What's given, what's kept, what's owed."

She stared at him. "You were listening very closely."

"I listen closely to everything," Wendell said. "It's my one skill. Maps are just listening with a pen." He hesitated, then added, softer: "I listened to you, too. At the teahouse. Before. Whatever's been taken, I know it was good. I can tell from how you look at me — like you're guarding something worth having."

Hazel looked away, at the lantern, at the bruise-flame, at anything else. Above them, through the broken roof, the first stars were coming out — stars in patterns she didn't know, constellations arranged like a language she'd almost learned as a child and forgotten on purpose.

Across the camp, Briarlock sat with his staff across his knees, and his amber eyes were open, and they were watching Wendell with an expression Hazel had last seen on the faces of the faithful looking at relics. Reverence. And underneath the reverence, unmistakably, fear.

*He knows something,* she thought. *About Wendell. About what he is.*

She filed it in the drawer. The drawer was getting full.

"Briarlock," she said aloud. "The third oath-keeper. The one who went mad. The Gardener." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "If we renew the covenant — if we somehow find a third keeper and give what the oaths require — what happens to the Gardener? To the mad one?"

The fox was silent for a long moment. The flame in his lantern burned down and down, the color of a healing bruise going yellow at the edges.

"The Gardener prunes," he said at last. "Anything that grows too close to the truth of the covenant, the Gardener cuts away. It is not malice. It is worse than malice — it is *tending*. The Gardener believes, with the whole of a ruined mind, that order requires sacrifice, and that the sacrifice must always be someone else's." His ears flattened. "Avoid the Gardener, children. Avoid the deep groves. Avoid anything in this garden that looks *too* well cared for."

Hazel felt the cold behind her sternum spread outward, finger by finger.

Because she was thinking of the orchard they'd walked through all day. Row after row after row of stone trees, planted in perfect lines, pruned to perfect shapes, maintained in their death with a care that no wind or weather could explain.

Someone had been tending them.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around what passed for midnight, Wendell drifted off against the barn wall, and Hazel watched — she couldn't help watching — as the mineral lace on the stones nearest him shivered, and split, and put out one small green shoot, trembling in the sourceless light like a held breath.

Briarlock saw it too. The fox's shadow arrived late to his own flinch.

"Eleven nights," Hazel whispered into the dark, to no one, to the garden, to whatever was listening. "Two keepers. One gate. And a third we have to find in a place where everything that isn't stone wants to eat us."

The green shoot by Wendell's hand unfurled a single leaf.

And far off across the grey fields, faint as a memory, Hazel heard — or thought she heard — the sound of shears closing on a stem.

Somewhere out in the dark, something was gardening.
