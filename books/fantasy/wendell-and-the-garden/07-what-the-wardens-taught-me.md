## 7. What the Wardens Taught Me

The Sundered Garden did not have weather. It had moods.

Hazel felt this before she understood it — a pressure behind the eyes, a grey weight settling over the petrified orchards as they walked, as though the whole borderland were remembering something it would rather forget. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, sourceless and flat, the color of old pewter. It cast no shadows worth having. Briarlock trotted ahead of them along a lane of stone apple trees, his fox ears swiveling at sounds Hazel couldn't hear, his brush of a tail flicking like a metronome counting down to something.

Behind her, Wendell walked with his hands in his pockets and his head tipped back, reading the dead branches overhead like lines of a map only he could see. He had been quiet since the Well of Names. Quiet in a way that made her chest ache, though she couldn't yet have said why — she didn't know what he'd paid, not fully, not yet. She only knew that when he looked at her now there was a politeness in it, a carefulness, as if she were a place he'd read about but never visited.

*You gave your name for both of us,* she thought, and hated herself a little for letting him.

She hugged her satchel closer and let the walking take her somewhere else entirely. Walking always did that. Walking was how she'd survived seven years in Vell — one foot, then the other, then the other, until the thing behind you became the thing behind the thing behind you, until memory wore smooth as a river stone.

But the garden did not let things wear smooth. The garden remembered everything. She could feel it pressing against the soles of her feet through her boots, patient as roots, asking.

And so, somewhere between the third petrified orchard and a bridge of grey stone that crossed a river the color of dishwater, the past caught up with her, and this time she let it.

* * *

It had rained the night Tam Isley fell into the cistern.

Hazel could still feel that rain if she let herself — the particular cold of it, the way it beaded on wool and refused to soak through, the smell of wet coal smoke rising off the rooftops of Hollowmere, the village where Mother Yew kept her cottage at the crook of two lanes. She had been seventeen. She had been an apprentice for six years by then, and she had been *good*, which was the problem. Good apprentices learn their lessons too well. They learn that magic has edges, and that the edges are there for reasons older than anyone living, and they learn to keep well back from them.

*Never work another's blood without their leave.* That was the lesson. The first law Mother Yew had ever taught her, scrawled in chalk on the slate by the hearth when Hazel was eleven: *the body is its own country; you do not cross its borders uninvited.* Blood-magic, healing worked on the unwilling or unknowing, charm laid into flesh — forbidden, all of it. Not because it failed. Because it *worked*. Because once you crossed into someone's blood you could never fully come back out, and neither could they.

Tam Isley was eight years old, the cooper's youngest, all elbows and missing teeth, and he had fallen through the rotted grate of the old cistern behind the tannery during a game of chase, and the water down there was black and deep and cold as a held grudge.

By the time they pulled him out he had been under too long. Everyone knew what too long meant. His lips were the blue of evening. His father knelt in the mud holding him and making a sound Hazel had never heard a grown man make, a sound like a hinge rusting shut.

The village surgeon had come and gone. The surgeon had said the word everyone says at such times, the small terrible word, *gone*, and had packed his bag with the brisk gentleness of men who do this often, and had left.

Mother Yew stood at the edge of the crowd with rain running off the brim of her hat, and her face was closed, and her hands were folded, and Hazel knew — knew with the sick certainty of six years of lessons — exactly why those hands were folded. Because the law was the law. Because the body is its own country. Because to pour life into a boy who could not ask for it was to cross a border no hedge-witch crosses, and the penalty for crossing was not written in any book Hazel had ever seen, because the penalty was the kind of thing that didn't need writing. The kind of thing the Wardens handled.

The Wardens. Even in Hollowmere, even at the ragged green edge of Vell's reach, everyone knew the Wardens. Grey cloaks, iron badges stamped with a circle broken by a line — the sign of the Severance, the great cutting-away, the century-old decree that magic belonged to no one and therefore to the state, and the state wanted none of it. The Wardens did not investigate hedge-witches. They *harvested* them. There were stories. There were always stories, told low, in kitchens, with the door shut: the witch of Marrow Lane who went in for questioning and came out of the Warden cells three days later smiling at walls; the cunning-man who burned his own cottage rather than be taken; the children born with the talent who learned, before they learned their letters, to hide it the way other children learned to swallow.

Hazel looked at Tam Isley's blue mouth and his father's ruined face and the rain coming down like the sky was trying to wash something away and failing, and every lesson she had ever learned stood in a row inside her like fence posts, neat and white and load-bearing.

And she stepped over the fence.

She didn't decide to. That was the thing she could never explain afterward, the thing that kept her awake in rented rooms in Vell for years: there was no decision. There was a gap between one heartbeat and the next, and in the gap she was already kneeling in the mud with the boy's cold wrist in her hands, and her own blood was already answering, and the forbidden words were already moving through her mouth in a voice that did not sound quite like hers — older, lower, a voice with roots in it.

Blood calls to blood. That was the second thing Mother Yew had taught her, back when teaching was still chalk on slate and honey on bread. Hazel had no blood-kin to Tam Isley. But she had spent six years learning that all the old laws were one law wearing different coats, and the coat this night was simple: *life will go where life is wanted, and it does not check invitations.*

She opened herself the way Mother Yew had shown her to open for the mildest charm — the little luck-knots, the fever-easers, the bread-that-doesn't-stale — and then she kept opening, past the mild, past the permitted, down to the place where her own pulse became a door. She poured. It hurt. It hurt the way cold water hurts, the way truth hurts, a bright tearing coldness running out of her and into him, and she felt the exact shape of what she was spending, though she would not understand until much later that what she was spending was not strength but *self* — that magic drawn that deep takes payment in the coin of the one who draws it.

Tam Isley coughed.

The sound went through the crowd like a crack through ice. Someone screamed. Someone laughed, high and unhinged with relief. The father made the hinge-sound again, but opening this time, and clutched his son, and the boy breathed, ragged and furious and alive, alive, alive.

And Hazel knelt back in the mud with her hands empty and her heart hammering and the rain coming down, and she looked up, and across the circle of villagers she found Mother Yew's eyes.

Her mentor was not looking at the miracle. Her mentor was looking at the *mud*. At the churned ground around Hazel's knees, where the rain had begun to fill the prints of her kneeling, and where — Hazel saw it a half-second later, saw it with the lurch of a floor dropping away — the signs were already showing. A ring of darkened earth. The grass at the circle's rim, greening impossibly, out of season, out of reason. The signature of working, written in a hand any Warden could read from thirty paces.

*Run*, Mother Yew's face said. No — her face said nothing. Her face was a wall. But her hands, those folded hands, moved — one finger, just one, a small motion toward the dark of the lane behind the tannery, there and gone, quick as a blink.

Hazel did not run. That was the shame underneath the shame, the one she never told anyone, the one that sat at the bottom of everything like a stone at the bottom of a well. In the moment, kneeling in the rain with a saved child gasping ten feet away, she had felt — triumphant. Lit up. She had done the impossible thing and the world had not ended, and some hot young part of her had wanted Mother Yew to *see*, to look at her and know that the fences were only fences, that the girl she'd raised was greater than the rules—

So she stayed. Just a moment too long. Long enough for the crowd to turn. Long enough for old Marta Cooper to point a shaking finger and say the word: *witchcraft.*

Long enough for the Wardens' horn to sound at the far end of the lane.

* * *

"—and here we dig."

Briarlock's voice dropped her back into her body like a stone into still water. He had stopped at the lip of a sunken road, a trench of grey earth cutting through the orchard, and he was regarding Hazel with one amber eye and an expression she was beginning to suspect was permanent: courteous, ancient, faintly amused, and utterly unreadable.

"You went away," he observed.

"I was here," Hazel said.

"Your feet were here." He tapped the ground with a claw. "The rest of you was in a place that smells of rain and coal. This ground remembers nothing so loud." He turned and led them down into the sunken way. "Walk while you grieve. It is the custom of this country. Grief standing still pools. Grief walking drains."

Wendell glanced at her sideways, and whatever he meant to say, he seemed to think better of it, and looked ahead instead, and Hazel was grateful, because she did not want to say any of it aloud. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Maybe that was the whole architecture of her — a person built around a sealed room, furnished with charm-craft and caution and a stall in Copperlane Market, and the room at the center locked, and the key thrown into the grey river years ago.

Except the garden didn't allow locked rooms. She could feel it trying the door even now, patient as water.

They walked. And because walking was how she survived, she let it take her back again. She owed the memory that much. She owed Tam Isley that much, wherever he was now — grown, surely, if the mortal world still ran true enough for growing; a man of twenty-five with a scar on his palm where the cistern grate had cut him, who might remember the rain and the blue and the cough, or might have folded it away like everyone folds such things away.

* * *

She had run, in the end. Of course she had run. When the horn sounded, the triumph curdled in a single breath into animal terror, and she ran — down the lane behind the tannery, over the wall into the churchyard, through the lych-gate, out along the mill path with the rain hiding her and her heart slamming like a fist on a door.

She got perhaps half a mile.

They found her in the osier bed by the millrace, three of them, grey cloaks dark with rain, iron badges dull as old teeth. She remembered almost nothing of the taking itself — terror burns memory the way lime burns flesh, leaving shapes and not substance. What remained were fragments. A gloved hand closing on her arm, gentle as a manacle. A voice saying *you're not in trouble, girl, you're evidence.* The interior of a covered cart, breathing straw and old blood. And through a slit in the canvas, one glimpse, one only, of Hollowmere falling away behind them — and standing in the lane where the lane bent, small in the rain, motionless, Mother Yew, watching her apprentice being carried off in her stead.

In her stead. That was the phrase the village used later, the phrase that reached Hazel even inside the Warden compound, passed cell to cell like contraband: *the old woman confessed. Said it was her working. Said the girl was only near, only helping carry herbs. Said the ring in the mud was hers.*

They believed her. Why wouldn't they? A hedge-witch of forty years' standing, known to every household within a day's walk, confessing clean? The Wardens took Mother Yew away in the same style of cart Hazel had ridden in, and the village watched, and Tam Isley's father stood at his gate holding his living son, and said nothing, and looked at nothing, and Hazel understood even then that his silence was not ingratitude. It was the silence of a man who knows that gratitude, spoken aloud, is a debt the powerful collect with interest.

And Hazel?

Hazel was released. Three days in a cell, questions asked in voices like oiled wood, and then a grey-cloaked woman had come and looked at her a long time and said, *confession has been entered; you are a witness, not a worker; go home, girl, and be nobody* — and Hazel had walked out of the compound into the rain, and stood in the empty lane, and understood that she was free.

Free. The word tasted like ash and honey at once. Free because someone else had eaten her crime. Free because the person who had taught her every law she'd ever broken had stepped forward and broken the greatest one herself, in ink, in front of iron, and taken the harvest that was planted for Hazel.

Any decent person would have gone to her. Would have demanded to recant, to confess over her, to share the cart at least. Hazel knew this. Hazel had been raised better than this; that was the whole point of Mother Yew.

Hazel ran instead.

Not to Hollowmere. Not to the Warden road. Away — south, then east, following the canals, sleeping in hay barns and under bridges, trading charms for bread in villages where no one knew her face, until the villages gave out and the city rose up out of the marsh mist with its thousand chimneys and its hundred wards and its million ways to be no one. Vell. Seven years now. Seven years of luck-knots sold cheap in Copperlane, of fevers eased quietly in stairwells, of keeping her gift folded up small as a moth in a matchbox, of flinching at grey cloaks, of learning the sweep-schedules of the Wardens the way farmers learn weather.

Seven years of telling herself the story that kept her upright: *I had no choice. If I'd confessed they'd have taken us both. One witch lost or two — I chose one. Anyone would have.*

It was even true, in the way most comforting things are true — technically, partially, from certain angles, in bad light. What it left out was the moment in the rain when she had stayed to be seen. What it left out was the year she could have gone back and hadn't, and the year after that, and the year after that. What it left out was that shame, left long enough unattended, stops feeling like shame and starts feeling like *personality*. Caution. Independence. A woman who needs no one, who trusts no one, who sleeps with her satchel strapped to her wrist and her name written nowhere.

A runaway of the cold harbour. The book had called her that, and she had laughed, and the laugh had died in her throat, because the book had seen the room at the center of her that no living person had ever entered.

* * *

The sunken road climbed, and the grey river appeared below them on the right, sliding slow and silent between banks of pale gravel, carrying — Hazel squinted — leaves. Real leaves, brown and curled, drifting downstream from some living place far upstream, the only green in motion in the whole vast withered country. Briarlock watched the leaves pass with an expression she might almost have called hunger.

"The river remembers too," he said, apropos of nothing, or of everything. "Everything the garden loses goes into the river. Everything the river gains comes out of the garden. You are walking along the margin of a memory, hedge-witch. Best decide what you intend to do with yours."

"My what?"

"Your memory. Your crime. Your rain." He flicked an ear. "You think I cannot smell it on you? Guilt has a scent. So does fear, so does love, so does a name freely given." His amber eye slid briefly, meaningfully, to Wendell, who was trailing his fingers along a stone fencepost with the absent tenderness of a man greeting old friends he couldn't identify. Something in Briarlock's face softened and shuttered at once. "This country runs on payment given freely. You of all people should know the law. You have been paying installments for seven years — badly, unwillingly, at ruinous interest. Debt paid in resentment is still debt."

Hazel looked at him sharply. "What do you know about my—"

"I know," said Briarlock, "that you are circling it. You have circled it since the teahouse. Since the Well. You will circle it until this country makes you land, because this country always makes you land." He gestured ahead with one claw, down the long grey vista of the sunken road, toward a bend where the petrified orchard thickened into something darker, denser, trees grown together into walls. "There is a hollow ahead where we make camp. There, tonight, the garden will ask you what you brought it. It asks everyone. Most lie. The garden keeps count of liars."

He trotted on. Wendell drifted closer to Hazel's side, close enough that she caught the smell of him — ink and paper and, beneath it, something green and growing, like a stem snapped in spring. He didn't speak. He simply walked beside her for a while, matching her pace, a companionship so uncomplicated it made her throat hurt.

Then, quietly, without looking at her: "Whatever it is — the thing you keep circling — I'm not going to ask. I just want you to know that when you land, I'll be there."

Hazel stared at the road ahead. The grey light lay flat on everything, shadowless, and she thought absurdly of the Warden compound, of the cell, of the word *evidence*, and of how long a person can carry a thing before the carrying becomes the shape of them.

"Tam Isley," she said, before she could stop herself. The name came out of her like a splinter finally drawn. "His name was Tam Isley. He was eight. I killed myself saving him and I let her hang for it, and then I ran, and I have been running for seven years, and I don't—" Her voice cracked and she pressed her lips together and finished, barely above the sound of the river: "I don't know how to stop."

Wendell stopped walking. He turned to face her fully, and in the shadowless light his face was very pale and very open, and there was something in his eyes that no one in Vell had ever directed at her — not pity, not judgment, not the wary respect people give a charm-seller whose wares actually work. Recognition. As if he had his own sealed room, and knew the architecture.

"Hazel," he said — and paused, and frowned slightly, as if the name had cost him something, as if he'd had to reach for it. "That's your name. Hazel. I— yes. Hazel Coldharbour." He said it twice, carefully, like a man relearning a word in a language he used to speak. Then he shook off the strangeness and took her hand, and his grip was warm and human and slightly trembling. "Then we'll figure out how to stop together. That's what I'm for. I think that's what I'm for."

She wanted to ask what he meant. She wanted to ask why he'd hesitated over her name, why he'd looked at her just then as though checking her against some list only he could see.

But ahead, in the darkening throat of the orchard, something bloomed.

A single flower, out of season, out of reason, out of anything — opening on a branch of stone-dead applewood directly in Wendell's path, white petals unfurling in the space of a breath, turning, as he approached, to follow him like a face.

Briarlock had gone very still. When he spoke, his voice had lost all its courtesy, and what remained underneath was a hundred years old and afraid.

"Master Ash," said the fox-headed herald, "please step away from the tree."
