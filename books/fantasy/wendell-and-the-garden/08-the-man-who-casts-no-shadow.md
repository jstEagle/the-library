## 8. The Man Who Casts No Shadow

Wendell stepped away from the tree.

It was not courage that moved him, nor obedience, but the simple animal logic of a man who has just watched a flower turn its face toward him like a sunflower turning toward the sun — except there was no sun here, only the bruise-colored sky of the Sundered Garden and the grey light that came from everywhere and nowhere at once. Things did not follow light in this place. He was beginning to suspect they followed him.

"Good," said Briarlock, and his tails — Wendell had counted seven, though he suspected the number changed when one wasn't looking — settled slowly along the herald's back like hackles smoothing down. "Very good. Walk on, if you please. Not quickly. Nothing in this garden likes to be hurried."

"What was it?" Wendell asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended. "The flower."

"A bloom," Briarlock said, which was no answer at all.

They walked on through the petrified orchard as the grey light thickened into something like evening. The trees rose around them in their hundreds, their thousands — apple and pear and quince, all of them dead for longer than Wendell had been alive, their branches frozen mid-gesture like dancers caught between movements. Their bark had gone the color of old bone. When the wind moved through the orchard — and the wind did move, though Wendell could not say what it found to move against — the trees made a sound like distant applause, dry and hollow and very far away.

Hazel walked a few paces ahead with her arms wrapped around herself, her chin up, her jaw set in the particular way she set it when she was determined not to be afraid. He had known her for less than a day. He had known her for less than a day, and already he knew the architecture of her fear: how she held her shoulders when she refused to shiver, how she looked slightly to the left of whatever frightened her, as if meeting it head-on would give it permission.

He should have been thinking about the prophecy. About *ash-born* and *runaway of the cold harbour*, about the book that had rewritten itself while they watched, about the tear in the world that had swallowed the teahouse whole. Any reasonable man would have been thinking about those things.

Instead Wendell was thinking about the flower, and about the way Briarlock had said *Master Ash* — not *sir*, not *friend*, but *Master*, the way a servant says it, the way a subject says it — and about the fact that since they had fallen through the hedge, his feet had not once hurt.

That last thought arrived quietly, the way most terrible thoughts do, dressed in ordinary clothes. He stopped walking.

The ground here was not ground as Vell understood it. It was packed grey earth shot through with roots, some of them thicker than his waist, and every step he'd taken since arriving had landed on stone-hard ridges and splinters of petrified wood. Hazel had stumbled twice. Even Briarlock, who moved over this land like water finding its level, had picked his way carefully around the worst of the root-lattice.

Wendell looked down at his boots. They were ruined — good leather, cracked and greyed by the crossing — but beneath them, where he stood, the earth was soft. Dark. Furred with a moss so green it seemed lit from within, spreading outward from his heels in a rough circle perhaps two feet across, like a stain, like a shadow made of growing things.

"Wendell?" Hazel had turned back. She followed his gaze downward, and he watched her face do something complicated — watch the recognition arrive, and then the fear, and then the deliberate packing-away of the fear behind her eyes, the way she must have done a thousand times in seven years of selling charms in a city that hanged people for it.

"That wasn't there before," she said. "When we crossed the orchard edge. I would have noticed."

"I don't think it grows behind me," Wendell said. "I think it grows under me. Wherever I stand long enough."

Briarlock had come back for them. The fox-headed herald stood at the edge of the little circle of living green, and he did not cross it. Wendell noticed that too, then — noticed everything at once now, the way a man notices the details of a room after he's realized the room is on fire. Briarlock's amber eyes were fixed on the moss. His whiskers trembled. And his expression — Wendell had spent three days in the company of a fox-headed creature from another world and had already learned to read that face better than most human ones — his expression was reverence wearing fear like a mask, or fear wearing reverence, and Wendell could not tell which lay underneath.

"You cast no shadow," Briarlock said.

Wendell laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin. "Of course I—"

He turned to look. The grey light of the garden came from everywhere; it threw shadows the way any light would, soft-edged and directionless, pooling beneath every tree, every stone, every figure. Hazel had a shadow, faint but definite, clinging to her heels like a shy child. Briarlock's shadow was enormous and many-tailed and shifted independently of him, which Wendell decided he would think about later or never.

Where Wendell stood, the moss glowed green, and there was nothing else. No darkness clung to him at all. The light simply touched the ground where he stood and kept going, as if he were a window, as if he were a hole cut neatly out of the world.

"It was overcast in Vell," he said, and heard how desperate the excuse sounded even as he said it. "And the teahouse was full of lamps. And the archive — the archive has no windows, you work by—"

"By lamplight," Hazel finished quietly. "I know. I've worked in places like that." She was still looking at the ground, at the border where his living circle met the dead earth, and her voice had the careful flatness of someone handling something sharp. "Wendell. In the teahouse. Your tea went cold, remember? You said it always does. You said you've never once finished a cup warm."

"I drink slowly."

"You drank it in four swallows because you were nervous. I watched you. It went cold anyway." She finally raised her eyes to his, and what was in them was not fear, exactly. It was worse than fear. It was recognition — the look of a woman matching a symptom to a disease she knows far too well. "Cold doesn't settle on you, Wendell. Warmth doesn't stay. Things don't... stick to you. I thought you were just one of those people. Some people are like that. But that's not—" She stopped. Started again. "Living things lean toward you. Did you know that? Plants on windowsills. Moths. My landlady's cat sat on your chair the moment you got up. I thought it liked you."

"I thought so too," Wendell said faintly.

"So do I," Hazel said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

They made camp that night — if night it was; the sky darkened from bruise to deeper bruise and the grey light dimmed to a greyer one, and Briarlock called it night, so night it was — in the shell of a fallen granary, a round stone building whose roof had collapsed inward centuries ago, leaving walls and a floor of tumbled masonry softened by dead moss. Briarlock built a fire from wood he called *greyfall*, driftwood from a river that no longer flowed anywhere, and it burned without warmth, casting a light like moonlight through fog. Hazel curled up near it with her back to a wall and her satchel under her head, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep — the fierce, guarded sleep of someone who has learned to rest the way soldiers rest, fully armed inside.

Wendell could not sleep. He told himself it was the strangeness, the wrongness of the sky, the applause of the trees. He told himself it was the prophecy, the book, the impossible arithmetic of a life that had been ordinary that morning. These were true things. But underneath them, quiet as the moss, was the other thing.

He got up carefully, stepping over Briarlock — who did not stir, though Wendell would have sworn the fox's ear tracked him across the room — and went out through the granary's broken doorway into the garden's night.

The dark here was not empty. The petrified orchard stretched away in every direction, silver-grey under a sky with no stars, and far off, something vast moved — a hill, perhaps, or a sleeping shape, breathing slow enough that each breath took a minute. The air smelled of cold stone and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of apples. Ripe apples, sweet and cidery, though no apple tree here had borne fruit since before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath.

Wendell walked until the granary was a smudge behind him, and then he sat down against a dead tree, and then — because sitting became leaning and leaning became lying, and exhaustion is a country that annexes you without declaration — he slept.

He dreamed of the archive. Of course he did. He dreamed of the long basement stacks where he spent his days correcting maps that disagreed with other maps, and of the shelf in the far corner where the unlisted book had waited for him — thin, brown, smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet. He dreamed of opening it, of the ink crawling like insects startled by light, of the sentence assembling itself letter by letter:

*On the eve of the waning, the ash-born shall meet the runaway of the cold harbour, and the hedge shall open.*

He dreamed that he asked the book, reasonably, politely, the way he asked everyone everything: *Why me?*

And the dream answered, in the rustling voice of pages turning somewhere out of sight: *Because you are the last seed of the first tree, and seeds wake when the ground is broken.*

Then he woke, and the ground was broken, and he was lying in the Sundered Garden, and the world had changed while he slept.

Flowers covered him.

Not climbed over him, not grown around him — *covered* him, the way snow covers a field, the way a blanket covers a sleeper. White blossoms in their hundreds lay across his chest and legs and outstretched arm, and more of them ringed him in a wide spiral, growing directly out of the dead earth, out of the petrified roots, out of the stone itself, in defiance of every law of botany and grief this garden obeyed. They were open. They were all facing him. And the smell — the cold-apple smell he'd noticed earlier — came from them, thick and sweet, so that the air around him tasted like an orchard in October, like cider pressed from fruit that grew on trees that had been dead a hundred years.

Wendell lay very still.

His first coherent thought was absurdly practical: *Hazel will never believe this.* His second was less absurd and much worse: *This is why the book chose me. Books don't choose jokes.*

He sat up slowly. The flowers slid from him without resistance, settling onto the ground around him in a perfect outline of where he'd lain, like a chalk drawing of a man at a crime scene, drawn in blossom. As he rose, the nearest blooms turned — gently, unhurriedly, the way a crowd turns to watch a procession — keeping their faces toward him.

"Don't," he said aloud, to them, to the garden, to whoever was listening. His voice cracked on the word. "Please don't do that."

They kept doing it. What else would flowers do?

He looked down at himself, at his hands, turning them over in the starless light. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained at the nails, a mapmaker's callus on the middle finger of his right hand, a small white scar on his thumb from a knife slip when he was nine. His mother had bandaged it. His mother had — he reached for the memory and found it, solid, warm, real: the kitchen, the sting of the spirit, her laugh. *You'll live, Wendell Ash. You're far too stubborn not to.* He held onto that scar like a rope thrown to a drowning man. Whatever he was, he had a mother who had bandaged him. He had a childhood with scraped knees and winter fevers and all the small damages of being mortal. Hadn't he?

Hadn't he?

He tried to remember being ill. Truly ill, bed-bound, the kind of sickness that marks a childhood — measles, fever, the flu that had swept through Vell when he was eleven and emptied half his street. He remembered the flu. He remembered the empty desks at school. He remembered his mother's worried face at his bedside.

He did not remember feeling sick.

He remembered the worried face, the cool cloth, the chicken soup. He remembered them the way you remember a story someone told you — complete, detailed, correct — but underneath them, where the memory of the thing itself should have been, there was only a smooth blankness, like a page written over so many times the original text had vanished. He searched harder, panic rising in his throat like floodwater. Broken bones? None. Toothaches? None. The measles, the pox, the coughing fevers every child of Vell traded like currency — nothing. Twenty-nine years of life in a city of smoke and damp and bad water, and his body was a ledger with no losses recorded.

Everyone got sick. Everyone bled. Everyone—

He thought of the teahouse, yesterday, a lifetime ago. Hazel's sleeve had brushed the candle flame when she'd leaned forward to make a point, and she'd flinched and sworn and pressed her wrist, and there'd been a small red mark, and he'd felt — he remembered this with total clarity — he'd felt an ache in his own arm, sympathetic, phantom, and he'd wanted to take it from her. And the mark had faded while they talked. Quickly. Too quickly. He'd thought nothing of it. He'd thought nothing of anything; that was the whole trouble with him, he thought nothing, he noticed everything and thought nothing, he had spent twenty-nine years being the gentle, observant, harmless man who noticed things he shouldn't and never once asked why he shouldn't have been able to notice them—

"Because noticing is what a seed does," said a voice behind him. "Before it becomes a tree. It reads the soil. It reads the light. It reads the shape of the sky through however much earth lies over it, and it decides which way to grow."

Wendell turned. Briarlock sat on a fallen wall ten feet away, seven tails wrapped around his feet, watching him with those lamp-amber eyes. How long the herald had been there, Wendell could not guess. How long Briarlock had been anywhere, doing anything, waiting — the fox had said *a hundred years* back in the orchard, and had said it the way you mention the weather.

"How much of that did you hear?"

"All of it. This garden hears everything spoken under its sky. It is mostly deaf to kindness, unfortunately, and perfectly attentive to fear." Briarlock's ears tilted. "You are afraid."

"Wouldn't you be?"

"Yes," Briarlock said simply. "I am. That is rather the point." The herald rose, and the many tails unwound, and he came closer — close enough that the firelight-less grey showed every silver hair, every line of age around those ancient eyes. "May I show you something, Master Ash? It will not hurt. Very little in this garden will hurt you. That is also rather the point."

Wendell should have said no. A sensible man would have said no. But the sensible men were all back in Vell drinking warm tea, and Wendell held out his hand.

Briarlock did not take it. Instead the fox-headed herald lifted his own hand — a hand that was half paw, five-fingered but furred, black-padded — and held it above Wendell's palm, and spoke a word that sounded like a root cracking stone.

Light fell from nowhere onto Wendell's hand. And where it fell, it passed through him — not around him, not off him, *through* him — and struck the ground below, and where it struck, a small green shoot emerged from the dead earth, unfurling two leaves, three, reaching up toward Wendell's palm as if toward the sun.

"The light finds no shadow in you," Briarlock said softly, "because there is nothing in you for it to catch on. Shadow is the body's refusal, Master Ash. Flesh says *no* to light, and the no is called shadow, and every living thing in every world casts one. Every living thing." The amber eyes lifted to Wendell's face. "Except the things that are not barriers. Except the doors. Except the seeds, which are not walls at all, but promises. Light goes through a promise. That is what promises are *for*."

Wendell looked at his hand. The shoot had grown to the height of his wrist now, climbing, its leaves trembling an inch from his skin but never touching, and he understood suddenly, completely, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own diagnosis, that it was growing toward him the way all of it grew toward him — the flowers, the moss, the moths and cats and cold-avoiding warmth of the world — and that it was not affection.

It was gravity. He was not loved by living things. He was *fallen toward*, the way water falls toward the sea.

"When I was born," he said, and his voice sounded like someone else's, "in Vell. Was I born?"

"I do not know," Briarlock said. "I know only what the covenant taught me to wait for: a man who casts no shadow, whom the garden greets as its own. I have waited a hundred years, Master Ash. I have buried six false claimants, kind men all, whose shadows mocked my hope. And then tonight the dead orchard bloomed in your path, and the ground went green beneath your feet, and I knew." The herald bowed his head, and for the first time Wendell saw that the gesture cost him something — saw the fear holding the reverence up like a man holding a roof beam. "I knew the seed had come home. And I do not know what that means for the man. I truly do not. No one has ever asked that question before, because no one believed both of you could exist in the same body."

Behind them, from the direction of the granary, Hazel cried out in her sleep — a short, sharp sound, a name half-spoken, *Mother* — and the sound broke the spell the way a stone breaks a window. Wendell breathed. The night came back: the starless sky, the applauding trees, the far-off breathing hill. The shoot beside his hand had stopped growing, its leaves drooping, as if it too had been listening and did not like what it heard.

"Briarlock," Wendell said. "The book. The prophecy. It named Hazel too. *The runaway of the cold harbour.* What does the covenant want with her?"

The fox was silent for a long moment. When he answered, he looked not at Wendell but at the granary, at the thin thread of firelight showing through the broken doorway, and his voice was older than a hundred years.

"The covenant wants what covenants always want, Master Ash. A price. And the old books are very clear on one point." His ears flattened. "The ash-born is the seed. But it is the runaway who must decide where the seed is planted."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the white flowers that had covered Wendell in his sleep were closing, one by one, folding themselves inward like hands — and at the center of each, where no flower of any world had any business keeping one, a single drop of dew gathered, bright as an eye, and did not fall.
