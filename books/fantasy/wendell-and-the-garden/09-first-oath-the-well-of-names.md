## 9. First Oath: The Well of Names

### Hazel

The Well of Names did not look like a well.

It looked like a wound in the world — a circle of black water set into a shelf of grey stone, ringed by pillars that might once have been trees and might once have been people, so weathered were their faces. The air around it tasted of iron and old rain, of every word ever spoken into it, and when Hazel leaned over the edge she saw not her reflection but something worse: her reflection's mouth moving, whispering, though she herself said nothing.

She stepped back quickly.

"Charming place," she said, to keep her voice from shaking. "Very restful. I can see why they built the first oath here."

Briarlock stood at the rim of the clearing, paws folded, tail still. The fox-headed herald had led them three days through the Sundered Garden — past orchards turned to stone mid-fruiting, past rivers the colour of dishwater, past groves where the branches creaked without wind — and now that they had arrived, the creature would not come closer than twenty paces to the water.

"The Well does not charm," Briarlock said. "The Well *listens*. It has been listening for a hundred years, and no one has come to speak. That is a long time for a thing made of listening to be alone." The fox's amber eyes slid to Hazel, then to Wendell, then back. "One of you must give a true name. Freely given, freely spoken, freely surrendered. That is the shape of the first oath. There is no other shape."

Hazel's hand went to her throat without her permission.

Her name. Seven years she had guarded it. In Vell she was *Haz*, or *the charm-seller on Tallow Row*, or nothing at all — a woman who paid her rent in coin and kept her true name folded up inside her like a letter never sent, because a name was a handle and a handle could be pulled. Wardens could compel a name out of you with hot irons; hedge-witches knew better than to carry one openly. She had spent seven years being almost-no-one, and it had kept her alive.

And now this fox wanted her to pour the last of herself into a hole in the ground.

"No," she said.

"Hazel—" Wendell began.

"You don't understand what you're asking." She rounded on him, and the words came out sharper than she meant, honed on seven years of whetstone. "A name isn't just a sound. It's everything you are, tied in a knot. Give it away and anyone who holds it can pull the string. Unravel you. Command you. *Erase* you, if they're strong enough. I've seen what happens to people who give their names carelessly. I grew up watching it happen."

"Then we should be careful," Wendell said quietly, "not careless."

"It's not careful to give it at all!"

"It is if you choose who holds it."

She stared at him. He stood at the edge of the well with his hands in his coat pockets, this mapmaker who had walked into her life four days ago with ink on his fingers and an impossible book under his arm, and he looked at the black water the way other men looked at open doors — wary, but willing.

"You've known me less than a week," Hazel said. "You'd hand your name to a hole in the ground on my say-so?"

"I wouldn't hand it to the well." Wendell turned to face her fully. "I'd hand it to *you*. You'd hold it. You'd speak it where it needs to go. And then—" he shrugged, that small apologetic shrug she was already learning to read, "—then you'd know where it lives."

"That's not how oaths work," Briarlock murmured from the treeline. "But it is how *trust* works. The Well may accept the difference. The Well is old enough to have learned flexibility, or starved trying."

Hazel looked at the water again. Her reflection was still mouthing something. She caught the shape of it this time, and her stomach dropped: it was saying *run*, the way she had run seven years ago, the way she had been running ever since, from Mother Yew, from Vell, from every hand ever extended toward her.

*Names have power,* Mother Yew used to say, back in the cottage that smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke. *So spend them like coins, girl, and count every one.* But Mother Yew had also said: *a name hoarded rots. A name given in trust grows interest.*

Hazel had forgotten that part. Or perhaps she had chosen to forget it, because the second half required something she had sworn off at seventeen: believing someone would keep their hands steady.

"I can't," she said, and hated how small it sounded. "I'm sorry. I can't. Not mine. It's all I have left."

Wendell nodded slowly. She braced for anger, for hurt, for the withdrawal she deserved — but he only looked at her with a kind of terrible gentleness, as if she were a map with a tear in it and he could see the country underneath.

"All right," he said. "Then it'll have to be mine."

### Wendell

He had thought about this, in the grey hours before dawn, lying awake beneath a canopy of petrified branches while Hazel slept with her back against a stone wall and one hand curled around a knife she probably didn't know she held.

He had thought about his name the way he thought about maps: as a thing drawn by someone else. *Wendell Ash.* His mother had chosen it — or the woman he believed was his mother, the woman whose face was already going soft at the edges in his memory, like a coastline worn down by tides. Ash, for the tree. Ash, for what remains after burning. He had never asked which. Now there was no one left to ask.

And underneath the name, he was beginning to suspect, there was something else. Something the garden knew and he did not. Flowers opened where he slept. His shadow came late in the morning and left early in the evening, as if embarrassed. Briarlock looked at him sometimes with an expression Wendell could only describe as *reverence holding fear's hand*, and it made his skin feel like borrowed clothing.

If his name was a coat someone had put on him, then giving it away cost less than Hazel feared. If it wasn't — if it was truly his, grown from his own root — then it was the only honest thing he owned, and he would rather it buy something real than sit in his pocket while the world frayed at its seams.

He had seen the Hollowed from a distance two days ago: pale shapes drifting between the stone orchards, faces smooth as peeled fruit. He had seen what happened to a world that let itself unravel. Whatever the name cost, the alternative cost more.

"Hazel," he said, "I need you to listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to do it whether you argue or not, and I'd rather you argued *before*."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on the word. "Wendell, don't you dare—"

"*Wendell Ash.*" He spoke it clearly, the way you'd read coordinates off a chart. "That's my name. My whole name. My mother gave it to me and I've carried it thirty-one years and I am giving it to you now, freely, because you're the first person in seven years — maybe ever — who looked at me like I was a person instead of a question." He swallowed. "Hold it. Keep it safe. Speak it where it needs to go. That's the whole of my will in the matter, and I mean it down to the roots."

The Well heard him.

There was no thunder. There was no light. What there was, was *attention* — the sensation of a vast ear turning toward them, of the black water leaning up out of its basin by a finger's width, of every weathered pillar-face in the clearing opening its eyes at once. The air pressure changed. Hazel's hair lifted from her shoulders. Somewhere far below the stone, something exhaled a breath it had been holding for a century.

The surface of the water shivered, and in it Wendell saw his name written out in silver script — *Wendell Ash*, letters flowing like minnows — and then the letters sank, sinking and sinking, down into depths the basin could not possibly contain, and the water went still and ordinary and terribly final.

"Oh," Hazel whispered. "Oh, you fool. You brave, stupid, wonderful fool."

Briarlock made a sound Wendell had never heard from the herald before — a low, keening note, half grief and half relief, the sound of a sentry finally allowed to stand down from one post. "The first oath stands," the fox said formally, though its voice shook. "Given freely, received freely. Two remain."

"Wendell." Hazel crossed the space between them in three strides and took his face in her hands, and her eyes were wet and furious. "What did it take? Tell me what it took. Names always take something. What did it take from you?"

And Wendell opened his mouth to answer —

— and found nothing there.

Not nothing exactly. Not emptiness. But when he reached for Vell — for the city, the archive, the teahouse with its crooked tables and its rain-streaked windows, the moment a nervous woman with charm-seller's calluses had sat down across from him and glared as if daring him to be disappointing — he found the shape of those things without their weight. He knew he had lived in a city. He knew he had met Hazel somewhere before a hedge and a prophecy. But the *first time* — the specific golden hour of it, the taste of the tea, the exact shade of her suspicion softening into curiosity — that was gone. Scooped out clean, like a stone from a peach.

"I don't remember meeting you," he said, and heard his own voice go strange. "I know that I did. I can't remember *how it felt*."

Hazel's face did something he had no map for. Grief and guilt and fury and something warmer and more frightened than all of them, all at once, like weather arriving from four directions.

"That's what it took," she said hoarsely. "That's what it *took*."

"It gave something too." He covered her hands with his own, where they still framed his face. "I can feel it. The name didn't vanish. It's *held*. I can feel where it lives." He looked at her. "It lives with you. Do you feel it?"

She closed her eyes. And he watched — watched the exact moment she felt it, the silver weight of *Wendell Ash* settling into her keeping, watched her shoulders change as something that was not hers became hers to carry. When she opened her eyes again, the fury had burned down to coals, and under the coals was the thing she had been hiding since the teahouse, since the hedge, since the beginning he could no longer quite recall.

"If you ever do anything like that again," she said, "without telling me first —"

"You'll turn me into a newt."

"I'll turn you into something *worse*."

"Then I'll be sure to tell you first." He smiled, and found that the smile was real even if its history wasn't. Some things, apparently, survived the toll. The way she bit her lower lip when she was frightened. The way the garden's grey light made her look like a painting of herself. The fact — and it arrived in him with the certainty of a law of nature — that he trusted her more than he had ever trusted anyone, including whoever he had been before this week.

Briarlock cleared its throat, delicately, the way foxes do everything.

"There will be time," the herald said, "for whatever this is. There will not be time for it *soon*. The dark moon is nine days off. The second oath waits in the drowned chapel, beneath the grey river, and the choir that guards it has drowned a great many pilgrims who came thinking goodwill was a lifejacket." The fox's ears flattened. "And we should move quickly besides. The Hollowed have scented you. Word travels strangely in this place, and there are ears in the orchards that belong to something older than hunger."

Hazel wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, the way she did everything she didn't want to be seen doing. "The drowned chapel," she repeated. "Of course it's drowned. Why would any part of this be simple?"

"Nothing worth swearing is simple," Briarlock said. "Come. We walk north along the river until the river forgets which way is down."

They gathered their things — little enough: Hazel's satchel of charms, Wendell's notebook, the prophecy book that had started everything, its pages quiet now, its ink settled like a sleeping animal. As Wendell slung his pack, he glanced back once at the Well of Names, and saw, in the black water, a single line of silver script rising briefly to the surface before sinking again:

*Held in trust.*

His name, alive somewhere. Safe somewhere. With someone.

It should have terrified him. Instead it felt like the strangest gift of his strange week: proof that he could hand himself to another person and not disappear. Proof that some knots, pulled tight by the right hands, hold instead of unravel.

He turned to follow Hazel and Briarlock toward the river, and did not see — because he was walking behind her, and she walked fast when she was frightened — the way Hazel kept touching her own sternum as she went, lightly, again and again, like a woman checking that a door she'd just locked was really locked.

Or like a woman discovering that something precious had been placed behind it, and that she had no idea what to do except guard it with her life.

Nine days to the dark moon. Two oaths remaining. And somewhere in the stone orchards behind them, pale shapes were already moving, patient as rot, following the scent of living names.
