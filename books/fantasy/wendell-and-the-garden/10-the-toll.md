## 10. The Toll

The name left him like a tooth coming loose.

Wendell had expected pain, or at least resistance — some great wrenching, as though the Well meant to pull his soul up by the roots. Instead it was almost gentle. He knelt at the well's lip, its rim carved from a single ring of grey stone worn smooth as river glass, and he spoke *Wendell Ash* into the dark water below, and the water took it the way dry earth takes rain: quietly, completely, without thanks.

For one heartbeat nothing happened. The garden held its breath around them — the petrified orchards, the colourless sky, the long grass gone silver and stiff as wire. Briarlock stood back with his brush-tail low and his fox ears flat, and Hazel stood closer, her hand hovering near Wendell's shoulder but not touching it, as if she feared what contact might cost either of them.

Then the water shivered.

Not rippled — *shivered*, the way a dog shakes off rain, and something rose through the black depth of it that was not reflection. It was light, or the memory of light, or the idea of light as the garden remembered it from before the Sundering: a slow gold bloom spreading beneath the surface like ink dropped in reverse. And Wendell felt the first thread go out of him.

It was small, at first. He reached for the name of the street where he'd grown up — Marrow Lane, he thought, Marrow Lane, it was definitely — and found a smooth blankness where the word should have been, the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth. He could remember the house itself. The lean of the stairs. The smell of coal smoke and his mother's lavender water. But the street had no name anymore, because the name had been *his* to give, and everything his name had touched had gone into the well with it.

"Oh," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "Oh, I see."

"Wendell." Hazel's hand landed on his shoulder at last, gripping hard enough to hurt. "What's happening? What did it take?"

He wanted to lie to her. He discovered, in that moment, that he still knew how lying worked as a concept, the way you know how a clock works without being able to read one — but the impulse to use it on her simply wasn't there. Perhaps that had gone into the well too. Perhaps honesty had been part of *Wendell Ash*, freely given.

"It takes what the name was attached to," he said. "I think. Everything I was called."

The second thread went, and this one he felt properly — a cold line drawn through the middle of his chest, precise as a paper cut. He saw it leave, almost: an image of the archive where he worked, the long tables under green-shaded lamps, the smell of vellum and dust — except the image was dimming even as he watched it, colours draining out of it like water from a cracked bowl. He grasped for the archive's name and couldn't find it. He grasped for the name of the man who supervised the map room, a kind, stooped fellow who'd once given him half his lunch — and found only a shape, a kindness without a face.

"Stop," Hazel said. Not to him. To the well. She had turned on it with her whole body, arms slightly out from her sides, and Wendell saw for the first time what she must have looked like as Mother Yew's apprentice, before seven years of hiding had taught her to fold herself small. There was iron in her stance. "You've had his name. That's the oath. You don't get to take the rest of him."

The gold light beneath the water pulsed once — not in answer, exactly. In indifference. Wells do not argue. Oaths do not negotiate. The garden went on taking what it had been promised, with the patience of anything that has waited a hundred years.

Briarlock spoke from behind them, his voice a dry rustle like fox feet in dead leaves. "It is done as it is done, hedge-daughter. The Well takes the true name and all that hangs from it. This is the toll. It was always the toll. Your mentor would have told you so, had you stayed to be taught."

"Don't," Hazel said, without turning around. "Don't you dare use her against me right now."

Wendell wanted to tell her it was all right. He opened his mouth to say so and found that the sentence required him to know what *all right* meant in relation to himself, and he was no longer entirely sure. He was kneeling at a well in a dead garden. A woman was holding his shoulder. A fox-headed creature was watching him with ancient amber eyes. These were facts. The connective tissue between them — how he had come here, why, with whom, and what any of it had cost — was going soft at the edges, like a drawing left out in the rain.

He decided to watch while he still could. If he was going to lose it, he would lose it looking directly at things, the way he did everything else. He was a mapmaker. Had been. Was. The habit outlived the profession: *observe, record, hold fast.*

So he looked.

He looked at Hazel Coldharbour and tried to fix her in place the way ink fixes a line. Rain-dark hair escaping its plait in wisps at her temples. A smudge of charcoal still on her jaw from the charm-work she did in the city — the city, which city, there had been canals, he was nearly certain there had been canals — eyes the grey-green of weather coming, a chipped front tooth he'd noticed when she laughed, when she'd laughed, she had laughed tonight, hadn't she, at something he'd said about —

About.

The teahouse went first.

He didn't see it go. That was the terrible elegance of it — there was no moment of loss, no tearing sensation, just a place in his evening where something used to be and now was smooth water. He had been walking somewhere with this woman. Before the garden. There had been a table, he thought — no, there had been a *table*, that much survived, the word and the shape of it, two cups, steam rising — but the room around the table was gone, and the reason they'd been at the table was gone, and the first thing she'd ever said to him was gone, and the first thing he'd ever said to her, whatever small foolish sentence had opened the door to all of this, was gone, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that he would never get it back. Not recalled, not dreamed, not reconstructed. The well drank deep and did not give back.

"Hazel," he said. "Tell me something. Tell me anything about before."

She turned back to him. Her face was doing something complicated — fear and fury and a dawning, horrified arithmetic, the look of a woman counting coins and finding the purse lighter than she'd thought. "Before?"

"Tonight. Before the garden. How did we meet?" He heard his own voice go thin. "I know we met. I know there was a table. I can't — Hazel, I can't find the room."

And he watched her understand.

It moved across her face like weather: confusion, then denial, then the cold arrival of the truth, arriving the way winter arrives — not all at once but everywhere at once. Her hand slid off his shoulder. She pressed both hands over her mouth, and above them her eyes went wide and wet and furious, and when she spoke her voice came out mangled.

"The teahouse," she whispered. "You were early. You were twenty minutes early and you'd brought — you'd brought a map. Of the canal district. Because you didn't know what to do with your hands at a blind date so you'd drawn a map of the walk from my lodgings, in case I got lost, in case you needed to — " Her breath hitched. "You said, you said *I hope you don't mind, I draw when I'm nervous, I drew the whole route twice.*"

"I said that?" He smiled, because it sounded like something he would do, and the smile broke her; she made a sound low in her throat like something tearing.

"You said that. And I thought — I thought, oh no. Oh no, he's *kind*. I'd spent seven years making sure I never met anyone kind." She knelt down in front of him, in the silver grass, and took his face in both her hands, and hers were trembling. "Wendell. Listen to me. The teahouse was called the — it was on the canal, under a willow, the owner was a woman named Sefa with a burn scar on her forearm, and you ordered oolong because you didn't know what else to order, and I lied and said I liked oolong and drank the whole bitter pot anyway because I didn't want the evening to end. Do you hear me? I'm telling you so someone remembers. *Someone has to remember.*"

"I'm listening," he said, and he was, he held every word the way a drowning man holds driftwood, but even as she spoke he felt the edges of her account going strange — not fading, he would not let it fade, but unanchored, a story now rather than a memory, beautiful and secondhand. He could keep it. He could keep it the way you keep a letter from someone else's grandmother. It would never again be the warm weight of having been there.

That was the toll. Not the name alone. The name had been a keystone, and the whole arch of his ordinary life had come down gently, gently, stone after stone after stone, and he sat in the rubble of it while the well glowed gold and satisfied beneath him.

* * *

They did not let him kneel there long. Hazel hauled him up by the arms with more strength than her slight frame advertised, and Briarlock led them away from the Well of Names along a path that wound between orchards of stone fruit — trees carved, or grown, or petrified into perfect grey replicas of apples and pears and things with no mortal counterpart, heavy on branches that had not bent in a century.

Wendell walked and took inventory, because inventory was all he had left.

Things remaining: how to read. How to draw — he tested it mentally, tracing lines in the air, and the skill answered like a loyal dog. His mother's face, though not her name, and not the name of the house or the town or the region it sat in; a portrait floating free of its geography. The feel of a pen in his hand. The taste of oolong, suddenly, absurdly vivid, bitter and floral on his tongue — Hazel's gift to him, spoken aloud at the well's edge, anchoring itself where the original memory had been torn out. Things lost: nearly everything with a label on it. Names were the hooks the world hung on, and his hooks were gone. Faces remained; names did not. Places remained as sensations — brick dust, lamplight, the sound of rain on a skylight — stripped of their addresses.

"I'm a map of a country that's been burned," he said, mostly to himself.

"No." Hazel walked close beside him, close enough that their sleeves brushed. "You're a map with the labels burned off. The country's still there. I'll write them back in. All of them. As many as I know."

"You don't know most of them."

"Then you'll have to make new ones." Her jaw set in a way he was beginning to recognize as the whole architecture of her character: grief converted immediately, furiously, into forward motion. "New names. Better ones. You always struck me as a man who'd enjoy naming things."

He laughed — surprised to find the laugh still worked, that it lived deeper than the well had reached — and Briarlock's ears swivelled backward toward the sound, and the herald said, softly, in that rustling voice:

"Now you begin to understand what was asked of the first three. What was asked of all who keep covenants. The old tales say the Gardener gave his name last of all, and gave it smiling, and that is how the maddest of them began." The fox-creature did not look back at them. "You gave yours first, ash-born. Draw from that what conclusions you can bear."

"What happened to the others?" Wendell asked. "The other two oath-keepers?"

"One died keeping her oath, and the garden grew a spring where she fell. One vanished, and the garden grew thorns where she stood. And one—" Briarlock's tail flicked, once, a sharp punctuation— "one is still here. Tending. Pruning. Waiting a hundred years for the covenant to be renewed by anyone foolish enough to come." The amber eyes finally turned, catching the colourless light. "Rest tonight, nameless one. Tomorrow we walk to the drowned chapel, and the second oath is less gentle than the first."

* * *

They made camp in the hollow of a petrified orchard, where the stone trees wove overhead into something like a roof, and the ground was carpeted in silver grass that did not bend underfoot. Briarlock produced — from nowhere, from fox-magic, from the general implausibility of him — a small fire that burned without smoke and gave off a scent like autumn.

Hazel sat beside Wendell with her knees drawn up, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The silence was not uncomfortable. That was somehow worse. Silence with a stranger should have been awkward, and it wasn't, and he realized that the ease between them had also survived the well — that whatever had made conversation easy at a table he could no longer picture had never lived in his name at all. It lived somewhere deeper. Somewhere the toll couldn't reach.

Unless it reached that too, eventually. Unless this was only the first instalment.

"Tell me the rest of the evening," he said quietly. "While I'm still — while you still can. All of it. Spare me nothing embarrassing. Embarrassing is the best kind."

A ghost of a smile. "You want the humiliating parts."

"I want the parts that were mine."

She looked into the smokeless fire for a while. When she spoke, her voice had gone rough. "You apologized to the chair. When you pulled it out for me, it scraped, and you apologized to it. And I thought, either this man is insane or he's the gentlest person alive, and I genuinely could not tell which." Her hands were fists in her lap. "And then the candles did the thing — the guttering, the wax running sideways — and instead of running you reached across the table and asked me if I was seeing it too. Everyone else pretended. My whole life, everyone pretends. And you just — asked. Like it was the most natural question in the world."

"I wish I remembered asking it."

"I remember it for both of us." She said it fiercely, like a vow, like a ward against the dark. "That's how it works now. You carry the garden, I'll carry Vell. I'll carry the teahouse and the canal and the rain and the way you looked when the book started rewriting itself — you went white as paper and you *still* didn't let go of my hand. I'll carry all of it until we find a way to — " She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "Until we find a way."

There was no way. They both knew there was probably no way. Names given to wells did not return; that was the entire grammar of oaths. But she said *until* instead of *unless*, and he loved her a little for the grammar, and the loving of her was new and enormous and already orphaned — a feeling whose beginning had been confiscated, leaving only the present tense, burning alone.

"Briarlock," Wendell said, raising his voice slightly. "The Hollowed. You mentioned them at the well — things that steal. What exactly do they steal?"

The fox-herald lay curled at the fire's edge, chin on his paws, eyes half-lidded. For a moment Wendell thought he wouldn't answer. Then:

"Fruit," Briarlock said. "Once. In the years after the Sundering, they stole the garden's fruit to feed on its dying magic, and the magic hollowed them into wraiths. Now they steal anything that holds warmth. Bread. Coals. Memories, when they can catch them loose." One amber eye opened fully, fixed on Wendell with unsettling attention. "And names, ash-born. Loose names. Yours is newly given and newly *gone*, and a name that has passed through a well leaves a scent on the air for days. Like blood in water."

Hazel had gone very still beside him. "How long do we have?"

The herald rose, unhurried, and looked out past the stone trees toward the grey distance, where something — or many somethings — had begun, very faintly, to move.

"I would stop asking that question," Briarlock said, "and start putting out the fire."
