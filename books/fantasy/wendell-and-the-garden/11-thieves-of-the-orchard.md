## 11. Thieves of the Orchard

The orchard announced itself before we saw it, the way grief announces itself before you can name it.

First came the smell — not rot exactly, but the memory of rot, sweet and thin, like cider left too long in a warm room. Then the sound: a dry whispering, as if a thousand leaves were being turned by a reader who never finished a single page. And then, rising out of the grey mist that clung to everything in this place, the trees themselves.

They had been apple trees once. I knew it the way you know a face in an old photograph — by what was missing. The branches still held their shape, that particular elbow and reach of orchard stock, but they were white. Not pale. White as candle-wax, white as bone left in sun, every branch fused into a single frozen gesture of giving. And hanging from those waxen boughs, where fruit should have been, hung shapes.

I stopped walking. Wendell, a half-step behind me with his mapmaker's habit of cataloguing everything, nearly walked into my back.

"What is it?" he asked, and then he saw them too, and his voice went quiet in the way it did when something mattered — which, I was learning, was often.

Fruit. Or things that remembered being fruit. They hung heavy and round from the petrified branches, but they were grey as river-fog, translucent at the edges, and inside each one something moved. A slow turning, like a moth trapped under glass. Like a heart remembering how to beat and getting it wrong.

"Apples," Briarlock said. The fox-headed herald sat back on his haunches on a fallen wall, his brush tail curled around his feet, and his amber eyes moved across the orchard with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Caution. "Or what apples become when the covenant thins. They grew here when the garden was whole. Now they grow *wrong*, and things come to eat them."

"What things?" Wendell asked.

Briarlock looked at me instead of answering, and there was something almost apologetic in the tilt of his sharp muzzle. "You'll see them soon enough. Best you see them before dark."

*Best you see them before dark.* That was the sort of thing Mother Yew used to say. *Look at the wound before you bandage it, Hazel-girl. Fear of a thing unseen is always worse than the thing.* I pushed the thought down where I kept all thoughts of her — deep, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted with stones — and made myself walk forward between the first rows of trees.

The ground here was strange underfoot. Soft, then hard, then soft again, as though the soil couldn't decide what it was. Grey grass grew in patches, and where it grew it grew in perfect spirals, unnaturally neat, like hair combed by a careful corpse. Between the spirals the earth was bare and cracked, and in the cracks I saw movement: small pale roots, writhing slowly upward toward the light and finding none worth having, sinking back down.

Wendell crouched beside one of the trees and held his hand near — not touching, just near — one of the grey fruits. It turned toward him. The whole fruit, stem and all, rotated on its branch with a slow wet sound until its inner darkness faced him like an eye.

"Don't," I said, sharper than I meant to.

He withdrew his hand. "It moved."

"Everything moves here." I pulled him up by the sleeve. "That's rather the problem."

We walked on. The rows stretched farther than an orchard had any right to — I counted forty trees down one row before the mist swallowed the rest, and Briarlock said nothing about it, which told me the orchard didn't obey the rules of distance any more than it obeyed the rules of fruit. Somewhere ahead, water dripped. Somewhere behind us, the whispering of unread pages continued, steady as breathing.

It was Wendell who noticed the absence.

"Hazel." His voice had gone very careful. "Where are the birds?"

I listened. He was right. In seven years of city life I'd learned to read Vell's noise the way other people read weather — gulls over the fishmarket, sparrows in the eaves of Tanner's Row, the pigeons that roosted on the Warden towers no matter how many times they were shooed. Silence in a living place is never true silence. It's occupied silence. Something has eaten the song and is listening for more.

"Briarlock," I said quietly. "What eats the birds?"

The fox-herald came down off his wall without a sound, which should have been impossible given his size, and fell into step beside me. "Not eats," he said. "*Took*. Long ago. When the garden began to starve, some of your kind — mortal kind — came through the hedge looking for power. The garden gave it. Freely, foolishly, the way a starving host empties his own cupboards for guests. But the magic here is dying magic, and dying magic doesn't nourish. It hollows." His ears flattened. "They drank and drank and could not stop drinking, and now they are cups themselves. Empty ones. They take fruit because the fruit holds the last sweetness of the old world, and for a moment, holding it, they remember having a self."

"The Hollowed," I said. He'd used the word once before, on our first night, and refused to explain.

"The Hollowed," he agreed.

"And they're coming now," Wendell said. Not a question. He'd gone still in that way of his, head tilted, and I realized he was listening past the whispering, past the dripping, to something underneath. "There's more of them than there were an hour ago. The whispering — it's changed. It's *closer*."

I heard it then too. The dry leaf-turning sound had multiplied, had spread through the mist on all sides, and woven through it now was another noise: a soft, rhythmic *pat-pat-pat*, like bare feet on cracked earth. Many bare feet. Walking without hurry, because nothing that hunts in a dead orchard needs to hurry.

"How many?" I asked Briarlock.

"As many as they need to be."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer this place gives." He drew himself up, and for the first time since I'd met him, the herald looked old — older than his clever fox face, old as the wall he'd been sitting on. "Listen to me, runaway. They will come for the fruit, and they will come for *him*" — his muzzle tipped toward Wendell — "because he smells of green. Of growing. To empty things, he is a feast walking upright. You cannot fight them with iron or fire; they have neither flesh nor fear. But you—" and here his amber eyes fixed on me with uncomfortable weight, "—you are hedge-born. Whatever you swore away, whatever you buried, the craft doesn't leave. It waits. Use it."

"I don't use magic," I said. The words came out flat and automatic, worn smooth from seven years of repetition. "Magic is outlawed. Magic is what got—"

What got Mother Yew taken. What got her chained in whatever dark cell the Wardens kept for witches, while I ran south with soot on my hands and her screams behind me. What would get me taken too, if the Wardens ever caught the charm-seller of Tanner's Row with her wards stripped away.

But we weren't in Vell. There were no Wardens here. There was only the mist, and the pat-pat-pat of bare feet, and the whispering that had stopped pretending to be wind.

"Hazel," Wendell said softly. "Whatever it is you're afraid of — I don't think it followed us here."

And that was the worst thing anyone could have said to me, because he was right, and because being right meant there was nowhere left to hide from it.

They came out of the mist between one heartbeat and the next.

*

They had been people. That was the horror of it — not that they were monstrous, but that they were almost not. Tall, stooped shapes, grey as the fruit, their skin the texture of birch bark, their faces smooth where features should have been except for mouths. Just mouths. Wide, dark, patient mouths that opened as they walked, tasting the air.

There were dozens of them. Maybe more; the mist kept its own count. They moved without urgency, drifting between the rows of waxen trees, and their long fingers — too many joints, the fingers of someone who had been stretched — reached for the grey fruit and plucked them, gently, tenderly, the way you'd lift a sleeping child. Each Hollowed one cradled its harvest against its hollow chest, and where the fruit touched that grey skin, it sank in slightly, like a stone pressed into wet clay, and the creature's mouth opened wider in something that might have been relief.

"They're eating," Wendell whispered. "They're just... eating."

"They're trying to remember," Briarlock said. "It doesn't work. It never works. But they keep—"

One of them turned its mouth toward Wendell.

I felt the change in the air before I understood it — a pressure shift, a wrongness, the sensation of a room full of people all looking at you at once. Every smooth grey face in the nearest three rows rotated toward us. Every wide dark mouth opened. And the whispering, the endless page-turning whisper, resolved into something with words in it, hundreds of voices layered thin as onion skin:

*Green. Green. Growing. Green.*

"Oh," I said. "Oh, that's not good."

The nearest one moved. There was no transition between standing and running — one moment it drifted, the next it crossed twenty feet of orchard floor like a dropped scarf falling, and its too-jointed hand came down toward Wendell's face with terrible gentleness, the way you'd cup a candleflame to shield it from wind.

Everything I had sworn away rose in me at once.

Hedge-craft isn't the high magic of towers and grimoires. Mother Yew taught me that in my first year, when I was eleven and impatient and wanted fireballs. *Hedge-craft is the magic of thresholds, Hazel-girl. Of hedges and doorways and the space between one thing and another. A hedge-witch doesn't build walls. She decides what passes through them.* I had spent seven years using maybe a tenth of what she taught me — luck-charms, ward-knots, little bindings hidden in embroidery thread. I had spent seven years telling myself the rest was gone.

It wasn't gone. It had been waiting, coiled and patient, the way everything in this garden waited.

My hand went out and found the nearest tree — wax-white, petrified, dead as doctrine — and I spoke the threshold-word into its roots.

The word itself doesn't matter; every hedge-witch finds her own, and mine I will not write here. What matters is what it did. The boundary between the tree and everything else — between *is* and *isn't*, between this row of orchard and the next — snapped taut like a drawn wire. And the Hollowed one, mid-lunge, hit that boundary and *bounced*.

It didn't fall. Falling requires weight and intent. It simply stopped, all at once, its outstretched hand hovering an inch from Wendell's cheek, and then it drifted backward as though the air itself had decided it belonged elsewhere. Its mouth worked. Its voice joined the chorus, thinner now, confused:

*Green. Why. Why. Why.*

"Thresholds," I heard myself say. My voice sounded strange — steadier than I felt, deeper, with a resonance I hadn't heard from it in years. "They can't cross a claimed threshold. They never could. That's why they haunt edges — orchards, rivers, walls. Everything they want is on the other side of a line they can't draw anymore."

Briarlock's tail went still. "Yes," he said. "*Yes.* That's the craft. That's the old craft."

More of them were coming. The mist was full of them now, grey shapes pouring between the trees from every direction, and the whispering had risen to a wind-roar of hunger: *green, green, growing, give, give, GIVE.* One of them reached for me and I felt its fingers pass through my hair like cold smoke, seeking the warmth of my scalp, the living heat of me, and finding — I realized with a lurch — that I was already moving, already speaking, my free hand sketching lines in the air that hung there after I drew them, faint silver threads of boundary.

A circle. The oldest working there is. I'd done it a thousand times as a girl, chalk on flagstones, salt on a doorstep, and I'd told myself for seven years that I'd forgotten how. My feet found the pattern without my mind's permission — heel-toe, quarter-turn, the old pacing rhythm Mother Yew had drummed into me until I could walk a circle in my sleep. Around us, around Wendell and Briarlock and me, the silver threads sank into the earth and took hold.

The Hollowed stopped.

All of them. Dozens of grey shapes stood motionless between the rows, mouths open, hands half-raised, and beyond them more came pressing forward and were stopped by the ones in front, who were stopped by the circle, and the whole mass of them quivered like a curtain in a doorway, unable to pass.

Inside the circle, my heart was slamming against my ribs so hard it hurt. Because here was the truth I'd been running from for seven years, and it felt like this: not shame. *Relief.* The magic hadn't left me. The magic had never once left me. All those years of little charms and knotted threads, all those years of telling myself I was only a shadow of what I'd been trained to be — and the whole time, the deep craft had been sitting in my marrow, waiting for me to stop being afraid of it.

And the thrill of it — gods, the *thrill*. My skin sang. The world was suddenly legible again, every tree a sentence, every breath of mist a word, and I stood in the middle of my own circle feeling more awake than I had since I was sixteen years old and stupid and certain the rules were for other people.

That was the danger. I saw it clearly even as I felt it, the way you see the edge of a roof clearly when you're standing on it. This was what the Hollowed had felt, once. This was how it started — the singing in the blood, the sense that the world finally made sense, the certainty that you could drink forever and never drown. They had stood in circles of their own power once, feeling exactly this alive.

"Hazel." Wendell's voice, quiet, at my shoulder. "Your hands are shaking."

"They're shaking because I'm *terrified*," I said, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too bright. "Not of them. Of me."

*

We stood in the circle while the light failed — such light as this place had, a pewter dimness thickening toward a darkness that seemed less like night than like the garden closing its eye. The Hollowed didn't leave. They stood in their silent ranks between the trees, patient as furniture, and every so often one of them would lift its head and taste the air toward Wendell, and its mouth would open a fraction wider, and close.

"We can't stay here," Wendell said eventually. "Can we?"

"No," Briarlock said. "Circles hold, but circles also *show*. You've lit a beacon in a dark country, witch. Everything hungry within a day's drift now knows precisely where you stand. We move before full dark, along the river, and we do not stop."

"Then how do we leave?" I kept my eyes on the nearest Hollowed — a tall one, taller than me by a head, its birch-bark skin cracked at the joints. "If I drop the circle, they—"

"You don't drop it," Briarlock said. "You *move* it. You've been standing in a fixed circle like a girl with a chalk line. The old craft doesn't draw boundaries, Hazel Coldharbour. It *carries* them."

I stared at him. "That's not possible. A circle is a circle. It's anchored—"

"To what? Look around you." His amber eyes glinted. "Anchor it to yourself. Walk your own boundary. It's harder. It costs. But a threshold that travels is a door that opens where you choose — and closes behind you."

It cost. I'll say that plainly, because the books make it look easy and it is not easy. To carry a circle you must hold the entire working in your mind at once — every thread of it, every anchor-point, the whole geometry of your safety — while your body walks and talks and watches the dark. It's like carrying a full cup of water across a shaking bridge while reciting poetry. Sweat ran down my spine. By the twentieth step my temples throbbed. By the fiftieth I tasted copper, and the silver threads of the circle flickered like a candle in draft, and Wendell put his hand under my elbow without being asked and matched his steps to mine, and somehow that helped, though I couldn't have said why.

We moved through the orchard in our travelling bubble of claimed ground, and the Hollowed parted before us — not fleeing, never fleeing, simply unable to cross the line I carried, flowing around our boundary like grey water around a stone. Their whispering followed us. *Green, green, growing, give.* Some of them reached in as we passed, fingers stopping at the invisible wall an inch from Wendell's sleeve, mouths straining wide enough that I could see down their dark throats into nothing at all.

One of them, near the orchard's edge, was smaller than the others. Child-sized. Its mouth was smaller too, and when it pressed its face against my boundary — flattening its smooth grey cheeks against the air like a child against a window — the whispering around it bent into something almost like words, almost like a voice:

*Mine. Was. Mine. Was.*

Then the press of bodies behind it swept it away, and I did not let myself think about what it had been, or when, or whose.

At the orchard's edge the trees thinned, and beyond them lay the grey river, broad and slow and silent, running without reflection. On the far bank, mist. Behind us, the whispering began to fade — not because the Hollowed had given up, but because they had found what they'd come for. As we climbed the riverbank I looked back once. In the last of the light, the creatures stood among the waxen trees, cradling their grey fruit against their hollow chests, mouths open, drinking a sweetness that could not fill them, would never fill them, and the sight of it lodged in me like a splinter.

Because I recognized them. Not their faces — they didn't have faces. I recognized the *hunger*. Seven years I'd lived on scraps of the craft, hoarding my little charms, selling luck by the knot, telling myself that safety was worth the starvation. I had been drinking from a thimble and calling it temperance. And if the Wardens had ever caught me — if I'd spent ten years, twenty, in a cell with nothing but my own fear — wouldn't I have become this? Something smooth-faced and patient, haunting the edge of the life I'd refused, mouthing *mine, was, mine*?

"Hazel." Wendell touched my arm. "You're crying."

"I'm fine."

"You're crying and lying about it, which I'm learning is most of what you do."

I laughed despite myself, wiped my face with the heel of my hand, and kept walking. The river murmured beside us, grey and voiceless. Ahead, Briarlock moved like a shadow with opinions, and behind us the orchard sank back into mist, and somewhere in that mist, among the patient thieves, something child-sized was still whispering *mine, was, mine* — and I carried the sound of it the way you carry a stone in your shoe, refusing to stop and shake it out.

We made camp that night in the lee of a ruined boathouse, and I set the circle wide and strong, and slept — badly, in fits, my hands twitching through old patterns against the ground. Once, near dawn, I woke to find Wendell sitting up, watching me.

"You were practicing in your sleep," he said. "Drawing lines in the air. Your lips were moving."

"Old habit."

"It didn't look like a habit. It looked like coming home." He hesitated, and in the pre-dawn greyness his face was difficult to read. "Hazel — when you closed the circle around us today, and those things stopped — Briarlock looked at you like he'd seen a ghost. A specific ghost. Whose?"

I looked at him for a long moment. The river whispered. Somewhere far off, faint as memory, the orchard went on turning its unread pages.

"My teacher's," I said finally. "She looked like that whenever I did something she hadn't taught me yet. Like she was seeing someone she'd lost." I pulled my blanket tighter. "Go to sleep, Wendell."

He did. I didn't. I lay watching the stars — real stars here, I'd discovered, brighter and stranger than Vell's, arranged in patterns no mapmaker had ever charted — and I thought about the child-sized thing in the orchard, and the tall one with the cracked joints, and the hundred-year-old question I'd been running from since I was sixteen:

If Mother Yew was still alive — anywhere, in any form, in any dark —

what would she see, when she looked at what I'd become?

I didn't have an answer. But the garden, I was beginning to understand, always did.
