## 12. Mother Yew Lives

The Hollowed did not keep their captives far. That was the first cruelty of it — that all this suffering had been happening within an afternoon's walk of the Well of Names, tucked into the ribs of a petrified orchard like something shameful hidden under a bed.

Briarlock led them there at dusk, when the grey light went the color of old pewter and the petrified trees threw long, stone shadows across the frost-killed grass. The fox-headed herald moved with a strange reluctance, ears flat, tail low, and when Hazel asked why, he only said, "You will wish to be braced, runaway. What the Hollowed keep, they keep because they cannot bear to look at it. That does not mean they are gentle with it."

The captives were kept in the orchard itself.

Hazel understood it slowly, in the way one understands a nightmare — piecemeal, each detail arriving separately so that the whole could arrive all at once, terrible and complete. The trees had not always been stone. The Hollowed had pressed their prisoners into the trunks the way a child presses flowers into a book, and the garden — corrupt, starving, mad with its own decay — had done the rest. Arms emerged from bark. Faces surfaced in the wood like drowned things rising to a window, some slack, some screaming in a silence so complete it rang. One old man had been taken to the shoulders; moss grew in his hair, grey-green and soft as a shroud. A woman further in was almost entirely gone, only a hand still visible, fingers curled as if mid-bargain.

The Hollowed drifted among them, thin as smoke, their stolen fruit held against their hollow chests, and they did not notice the three travelers at first. They were busy. That was the worst of it. They were *tending* their captives, smoothing bark from eyes, wiping frost from stone cheeks with hands that had forgotten how to feel what they touched. Whatever they had been before the garden's rot took them, some residue of care remained, twisted past all recognition — gardeners still, in the way a broken clock is still a machine.

"They cannot be cured," Briarlock murmured. "Not by us. Perhaps not by anything. But the ones in the wood — the ones still breathing — those can be cut free. If we are quick. If we are gentle. The wood has grown through them, and them through the wood. It is surgery of a kind no one has performed in a hundred years."

Hazel's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs and made herself look, really look, face by face, because Wendell was already moving down the row with his strange, careful tenderness, laying his palm against the bark and whispering — she could not hear what — to each trapped soul in turn. Where he touched, the stone warmed. She saw it happen: a faint blush of color spreading under the grey, like blood returning to a limb long asleep.

And then she saw the face.

It was half-swallowed. Bark had grown across one cheek and up over the left eye, and the hair — once black as a rook's wing, the black Hazel had envied and imitated and tried to braid charms into when she was eleven and foolish — was white now, white and long, spilling down the trunk like a waterfall frozen mid-pour. The visible eye was closed. The mouth was moving, very slightly, shaping words that had no sound left in them, the same word over and over, worn smooth as a river stone by a century of repetition.

Hazel knew the word. She did not know how she knew it, until she realized she was reading the lips of a woman she had last seen at fourteen, silhouetted in the doorway of a burning lesson-house, and the word was a name.

*Hazel. Hazel. Hazel.*

The world went very quiet. The grey river, somewhere behind them, went on whispering over its stones, but everything else — Wendell's murmuring, Briarlock's breathing, the creak of petrified branches — fell away as if she had plunged underwater.

"Mother Yew," she said. Her voice came out in pieces. "Mother — no. No, she's *dead*. She was taken by the Wardens, she was — I *saw* —"

"You saw what the garden wished you to see," Briarlock said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat gentleness of a creature who has delivered bad news for a hundred years. "Or what your guilt wished. The two look alike, runaway. They always have." He stepped closer to the tree, and his ears angled forward, and something like grief crossed his fox face. "The second oath-keeper did not vanish. She was *taken*. The Hollowed caught her in the first year, when the covenant first began to fray, and they have kept her ever since. The garden does not kill its oath-keepers. It cannot. So it keeps them. The way you keep a tool you have lost the use of but cannot bring yourself to throw away."

Hazel could not move. She understood, distantly, that she should be moving — that Wendell was already prying at the bark with his bare hands, that Briarlock was producing a knife of grey glass from somewhere in his herald's coat, that the Hollowed drifted nearer with every moment — but her body had become one of the trees itself, rooted, stone, a face surfacing in wood.

Seven years. Seven years she had built her whole self on the foundation of that doorway, that fire, that silhouette. *She took the blame for you. She burned for you. You owe a debt you can never pay.* She had carried it like a stone in her chest, had let it calcify, had made of her guilt a kind of monument — and here was the woman herself, not dead, not even free, kept alive by a corrupt garden for a hundred years of waking imprisonment, mouthing her runaway apprentice's name into the dark.

"She thinks I'm dead," Hazel whispered. "She's been saying my name for a hundred years and she thinks —"

"Cut her free," Briarlock said. "Grieve after. The garden's law is the same as the hedge-wife's law, in this one particular: what is done in time is done well, and what is done late is done at all."

* * *

The cutting took an hour, and it was the hardest work Hazel had ever done, and she had once held a child's fever back for three days with nothing but hedge-craft and stubbornness.

The bark did not want to release her. It had grown *into* Mother Yew — through muscle, around bone, fine white rootlets threading her like a needle through cloth — and every cut Hazel made, she felt echo in the old woman's body, a flinch, a small sound like a bird struck by a window. Wendell held the wood warm and pliable with his palms, and it helped, it helped enormously, the stone going soft as candle wax where he touched — but he could not cover the whole trunk, and so Hazel worked with the grey glass knife and her own two hands and a spell she had not used since she was fourteen and learning at her mentor's knee.

It was a healing-song. Not the corrupted garden's magic — she was careful, so careful, to keep it out of her, to draw only on the small hoarded warmth of her charms and the older, humbler current that ran through hedge-witchery like groundwater through chalk. She sang under her breath, the words in the old tongue that Mother Yew had taught her, the words for *open* and *release* and *it is safe now, whatever it was, it is over now* — and she realized halfway through that she was singing her mentor's own lessons back to her, that every technique she used to free the woman had been put into her hands by the woman herself, and she had to stop, once, and press her forehead against the warm bark, and breathe.

"Keep going," Wendell said quietly. He was grey with exhaustion; the warming was costing him something, she could see it in the new hollows of his face. But his eyes were steady. "Whatever it is you're remembering — keep going. She's waited a hundred years. Don't make her wait through your pause too."

So Hazel kept going.

The bark came away in sheets at last, like the pages of a book being peeled apart, and beneath it Mother Yew's body came free by inches — first the arm, terrible and thin as a bundle of kindling; then the shoulder, the other arm, the torso with its ribs like a barrel stave; then, at the last, with a sound like a long-held breath released, the rootlets withdrawing from her face, and she sagged forward out of the tree and into her apprentice's arms, a hundred years old and weightless as a bundle of autumn leaves.

Her eye opened.

It was the same eye. That was the unbearable thing. A century of imprisonment, of bark and frost and the slow stone sleep of the orchard, and the eye that opened and found Hazel's face was the same sharp dark eye that had caught Hazel stealing dried lavender at eleven, that had watched her over the lesson-table, that had been the last thing Hazel saw from the doorway before she ran. It did not wander. It did not look confused. It found her, and fixed on her, and filled — Hazel watched it happen — with a hundred years of things that had no words left.

"Mother Yew," Hazel said. "I — I came back. I'm —"

The slap rang off the petrified trees.

It was not a weak blow. Whatever else a century in the wood had taken from her, Mother Yew's arm remembered its work, and her palm cracked across Hazel's cheek with a force that snapped her head sideways and put the taste of copper in her mouth and left her, for one full second, utterly unable to do anything but stand there with her hand rising slowly to her face and her ears roaring.

Briarlock made a small sound that might have been a cough. Wendell started forward and stopped, because Mother Yew had raised one finger — one crooked, bark-scarred finger — and the gesture was so precisely the gesture of the lesson-house, *stay where you are, this is between us*, that he stopped as if he had been the one struck.

"You *ran*," Mother Yew said.

Her voice was ruin. A century of disuse had left it a rasp, a whisper dragged over gravel — but under the ruin it was still her voice, still the voice that had named herbs and scolded and, once, only once, praised. And now it shook, not with age but with fury, fury banked so long and burned so slow that it had become the only fire left in her.

"A hundred years," she rasped. "A hundred years in the wood, and do you know what I thought? Not *why did she run*. Not *how could she leave me*. I knew why you ran, girl, I have always known why you ran, I took the Wardens' chains with my eyes open —" She stopped. Her jaw worked. The ruined voice climbed, cracked, climbed again. "I thought: *she is alive. Wherever she is, she is alive, and one day she will come back, and I will ask her one question.* One question, Hazel. A hundred years, and I had one question, and I kept it polished, I kept it sharp, I turned it over every single day in the dark of that tree like a stone in a pouch, and here —" her hand shot out and seized the front of Hazel's coat, knuckles like iron, "— here you *are*, and here is my question, and you will answer it before you say one more word, before you apologize, before you weep on me, before any of it:"

She shook her. The whole weight of her fury behind it, and she weighed nothing, and still Hazel rocked.

"*Did you ever once — once — think you might have been wrong to run?*"

The orchard was silent. The Hollowed had drifted to the orchard's edge and stood there in a loose, terrible ring, watching, their hollow faces tilted, as if they too were waiting for the answer.

And Hazel — who had rehearsed this moment for seven years in a hundred sleepless shapes, who had imagined apologies and explanations and confessions, who had composed in her head the speech she would give if she ever found her mentor's grave — found that every word she had prepared was ash. Because the answer was the true one, and the true one was shameful, and it had been shameful for seven years and she had never once said it aloud.

"No," she said. Her voice came out small and steady, which surprised her. "I thought I was right. For seven years, I thought I was right. I saved that child, Mother Yew. I'd do it again. I thought — I thought the only thing I did wrong was let you take the blame for it. And then I thought that so long, and it hurt so much, that I made it into the whole truth. That I was the wronged one. That my guilt made me good." She swallowed. Her cheek burned where the slap had landed, and she found she was grateful for it, grateful for the sting, the proof of contact, of flesh and blood and a woman alive in her arms. "It didn't. It just made me alone. I've been alone for seven years and I called it penance and it was just — cowardice. Wearing a hair shirt I wove myself so I'd never have to take it off and look at what was underneath."

Mother Yew stared at her.

The fury did not leave her face — Hazel doubted anything would ever fully leave that face again; a century had carved its channels too deep — but something moved beneath it, something that had been waiting even longer than the question. Her grip on Hazel's coat loosened, finger by finger, and her hand rose, and for one wild moment Hazel thought she was going to be struck again.

Instead the old woman touched her cheek. The burned one. Her fingers were light as leaf-fall, and they trembled.

"You learned," she whispered. "A hundred years in a tree, and I thought — if she comes back, she will come back still a child. Still running. And you learned. You learned the hardest lesson, the one I could not teach you because I had not learned it myself." Something wet tracked down through the deep lines of her face. "Gods forgive me, Hazel. I did not take the blame to spare you."

The words dropped into the silence like stones into a well, and Hazel heard them fall, and fall, and no bottom came.

"I took it," Mother Yew said, and her voice was going strange now, fraying at the edges, the madness Briarlock had promised beginning to show through like bone through skin, "because a bound apprentice is an apprentice *forever*. Because the debt would hold you to me past the ending of the indenture, past your majority, past death if I could arrange it. Because I could not — I could not bear —" and here the eye lost its terrible focus, skittering away into some middle distance where a hundred years of dark was waiting, "— I could not bear to be *finished*. To have taught everything I had and be set aside. Old women are set aside, Hazel. So I chained you to me with your own guilt, and the gods saw it, and they *laughed*, and they sent me to a tree for a hundred years to learn what a chain feels like from the inside —"

"Mother Yew." Hazel caught her as she swayed. The old woman was lighter than a bundle of sticks and shaking like one. "Mother Yew, stop. Stop. We don't have time. There's a covenant dying, and a dark moon coming, and a Gardener —"

The name went through her like a blade.

Hazel felt it happen — felt the old woman's whole body seize, felt the trembling stop all at once, the way a clock stops. Mother Yew's eye came back from its hundred-year distance and fixed on Hazel with a clarity so sudden and so total that it was worse than the madness. It was the clarity of the woman she must have been before everything, the woman who had been the second oath-keeper, who had stood in the Sundered Garden when it was green.

"You've seen him," she whispered. "The Gardener. You've *seen* him."

"Not yet," Hazel said. "Briarlock's told us. He prunes anything that grows too close to truth. He's mad. He was the third oath-keeper, and he went mad tending the covenant alone —"

"*Alone.*" Mother Yew laughed. It was the worst sound Hazel had ever heard — a century of dark compressed into a single dry rattle, and underneath it, horror. "Yes. That's what the garden tells itself. That's what the *trees* tell themselves." Her hand shot out again, clutching, and this time she pulled Hazel close enough that Hazel could see her own shocked face reflected in the old woman's eye, small and upside down. "Listen to me. Listen, because I have been in the wood a hundred years and the wood *remembers*, and the Hollowed talk to their captives, girl, they talk and talk, and I have heard things —"

"Mother Yew, you're not well, we need to move, the Hollowed —"

"*He was not the third oath-keeper.*" The whisper cut through everything, through the creak of the petrified orchard, through the drifting ring of Hollowed, through the whisper of the grey river. "There were three oaths and three keepers, yes — but the Gardener is not the third. The Gardener was *first*. The first of all of them. The one who sealed the covenant in the beginning, before the Sundering, before everything. And he did not go mad from tending it alone." Her grip tightened, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, and every word came out like a splinter drawn from the wood.

"He went mad because he gave *everything*. His name, his years, his shadow, his self — the covenant took it all, the first time, because no one knew yet that it could be paid in shares. He emptied himself to hold the realms together, and what was left when he was empty —" her eye flicked, for the first time, to Wendell, who had gone very pale and very still at the edge of Hazel's vision, "— what was left has been tending the garden ever since, waiting, *waiting* for someone to come and be emptied the same way. And now the prophecy has brought him a seedling, and a runaway, and a broken old woman, and he will be coming, Hazel. He is already coming. He always knows when something is cut free of the wood."

Far off, at the orchard's edge, every Hollowed head turned at once — toward the grey river, toward the dark coming down, toward the sound, very faint and very far away, of shears opening and closing in a steady, patient rhythm, like a man walking.
