## 13. The Mad Gardener

*She is coming up the hill. She is coming with the ash-born boy and she has not knelt in seven years and I have not slept in a hundred and the shears are still walking.*

Mother Yew sat in the hollow of a petrified apple tree that had once been her home — or had once been a tree, or had once been anything at all; the order of things had gone soft as over-steeped tea — and she counted her thoughts the way she had once counted seeds, one by one, into rows, because rows kept them from tangling.

Row one: *The girl is coming.*

Row two: *She will not kneel.*

Row three: *Neither did I.*

The thought arrived like a thorn under a fingernail, small and unbearable, and she pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw green afterimages, the only green left in this grey country except for the boy who bloomed.

*The boy who blooms.*

That was row four, and it was the important one, and it was also the dangerous one, and she tucked it deep beneath the others where the Gardener's shears could not reach it. He pruned anything that grew too close to truth. That was his whole liturgy now, his whole self: not evil, never evil — he had been the kindest of them once, the first of them, the one who gave everything — but madness does not make a monster so much as a gardener of monsters, trimming and shaping and cutting back every living thing that leaned toward understanding.

She heard them before she saw them. The fox-herald's claws ticking on stone. The girl's boots — Hazel walked like someone perpetually leaving a room, weight forward, always half-gone. And behind them, softer, a third set of footsteps that made no sound at all on the dead ground, and where they fell —

Where they fell, moss.

A thin seam of it, green as an opened vein, trailing up the hillside behind the young man like a ribbon dropped from a maypole.

Mother Yew's hands began to shake. A hundred years of shaking, and it had never once gotten easier.

* * *

They found her sitting among the roots, and she watched Hazel's face do three things in quick succession: recognition, horror, hope. Hope last, hope longest, hope like a candle cupped against wind.

"You're alive," Hazel said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "You're — Mother Yew, you're *alive*, they told me — I thought —"

"You thought what the Wardens wanted you to think." Mother Yew's voice came out rusted. She hadn't spoken to anyone but herself in decades, and herself was poor company. "Sit down, child. Both of you. Not there — *not there*, boy, that root still remembers being a door and doors here open onto nothing."

Wendell Ash stepped back from the root with the careful politeness of a man who had decided the madwoman's warnings were probably sound. Good instincts, she noted. Row five: *the boy has good instincts.* The Gardener would hate him for it.

Hazel sat. She did not sit close. Seven years, and the girl arranged her body like a debt being avoided.

"You were imprisoned," Hazel said. "In the orchard. With the Hollowed."

"I was *kept*," Mother Yew corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference matters, though I'll grant it took me sixty years to find the shape of it. The Wardens didn't put me here, Hazel. They couldn't reach this place. I was put here by the covenant itself — or by what's left of its keeping. When the second oath failed, when the second oath-*keeper* failed—" her mouth twisted— "the garden held me the way a fist holds a coin. Tightly. Meaninglessly. For a very long time."

"Why?" Wendell asked gently. "What did the second oath require?"

And there it was, the question she had circled for a century, the question whose answer she had worn smooth as a river stone from turning it over and over.

"The second oath," she said, "was vigilance. Someone had to watch the border between the realms. Someone had to tend the places where the hedge grew thin and mend them before things crossed. It was my office. I swore it before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath, child, and I kept it for two hundred years, and then—" 

"Then you took the blame for me," Hazel whispered.

"No." Mother Yew laughed, and the laugh frightened her a little, it came out so wrong, too many edges. "No, child. You have carried that story for seven years like a stone in your shoe, and it is time someone took it out and showed you it was never shaped the way you thought."

* * *

Here is what happened, and she told it slowly, in fragments, because fragments were all she had left that were honest:

The child in the well-district had been dying of a wasting fever that hedge-craft could not touch — not because the magic was insufficient but because the magic was *forbidden*, and forbidden magic in Vell curdles when it is used, sours in the casting, and Hazel had known that and done it anyway. Used the deep craft. The craft that touches the Otherworld's edge. She had pulled the fever out of the child and pushed it into a charm-bag and buried the bag in consecrated ground, and the Wardens' instruments had sung like struck bells for a mile around.

"They came for you," Hazel said. "I ran. I let them take you."

"You ran," Mother Yew agreed. "And I stood in the doorway of my cottage with their writs in my hand, and do you know what I felt?" She leaned forward. Her eyes, in this light, were the color of old leaves. "I felt *relief*. Because the moment they took me, the moment I was condemned, I owned you. Forever. Every kindness I'd ever shown you became a chain. Every lesson became a debt. You would spend your whole life running from the shape of my sacrifice, and a person who is running from a debt can never stop looking over her shoulder, and a person who cannot stop looking over her shoulder never looks *forward*, never becomes anyone new. I bound you to me with guilt, Hazel Coldharbour, and I called it love, and the worst part—" her voice dropped to almost nothing— "the worst part is that some of it *was* love, tangled through the leash like bindweed through a rosebush, and neither of us will ever sort which strand was which."

The silence that followed had texture. Wendell had gone very still, the way people go still around wounds.

"That's why you went mad," Hazel said finally. Not cruelly. Wonderingly. "Not the imprisonment. The — the leash failing. I ran anyway. I stopped looking over my shoulder. I built a life without you."

"And the oath broke," Mother Yew said. "Vigilance requires a watcher who watches for love of the watching, freely. Mine had become a watch for possession. The garden knows the difference. The garden *always* knows the difference." She spread her hands, gnarled as bark. "So it kept me. A broken oath-keeper, stored like a tool in a shed, until such time as—" and here she looked at Wendell Ash directly for the first time, and the boy flinched, because her gaze had weight, it had *roots*— "until such time as the seed came home."

"What am I?" Wendell asked. Quietly. The question of a man asking about a diagnosis.

"Not yet," Mother Yew said. "Not while the shears are walking. He hears true names spoken near his work. Understand me: the Gardener was the first oath-keeper. His oath was *sacrifice*. He gave everything — name, shadow, mortality, memory — everything, alone, because in the beginning there was no one to share the burden with. He sealed the covenant with his whole self and it held for an age, and holding it unmade him by inches. Sacrifice given alone curdles, child. It becomes ownership. He tended the border ten thousand years and somewhere in those millennia he stopped tending the *covenant* and started tending his own wound, and now he prunes anything that grows toward wholeness, because wholeness in another is the mirror that shows him what he lost." She swallowed. "He prunes truth. He prunes love. He prunes anything that suggests the burden might be shared."

"He's coming," Hazel said. "We heard the shears."

"He is always coming. He is patient the way winter is patient. But he is bound to his rounds, and his rounds are long, and we have until dark moon. Which gives us—" she calculated, or tried to; her arithmetic had gone feral centuries ago— "days. Perhaps six. Perhaps fewer."

"Then we need the third oath," Wendell said. "The first was at the Well of Names. The second—" he glanced at Hazel— "we think it lies beneath the river. The drowned chapel. What's the third?"

Mother Yew looked at him for a long moment. Moss crept a little closer to his boot, hopeful as a dog.

"The third oath," she said, "is renewal. Given at the Heartwood Gate, where the covenant was first sworn. And it cannot be given by the two of you alone, because renewal requires one who remembers how the oaths were shaped — the grammar of them, the law of them. Freely given, fully paid, witnessed by earth." She tapped the petrified ground. "I remember the grammar. I am the only creature in either realm who still remembers the grammar."

"Then help us," Hazel said simply.

And there it was. The moment she had rehearsed for a hundred years in the theatre of her ruined mind, and in every rehearsal it had gone differently than this.

* * *

Because here was the truth beneath the truth, row six, the deepest row: Mother Yew did not want to help. Helping meant yielding. Helping meant standing beside the girl who had unmade her and admitting that the unmaking had been *necessary* — that the leash had deserved to fail, that freedom had been the right answer all along, that a hundred years of storage in a stone orchard had been the harvest of her own sowing. Pride was the last organ still beating in her. To hand it over was to die a little, and she had died so many little deaths already that she knew each one by name.

But the boy was blooming moss with his footsteps. And the shears were walking. And pride, she reminded herself, was exactly the kind of growth the Gardener pruned.

"There is a price," she said.

Hazel's jaw set. Mother Yew watched her brace for extortion and felt the old possessiveness stir, that hungry thing that wanted the girl indebted, and she strangled it — strangled it with both hands, the way you pull bindweed, knowing the roots remain.

"Not what you think," she said. "Not servitude. Not guilt. Here is my price, Hazel Coldharbour, and it is the only price I will ever ask of you again." She rose. Her joints sounded like twigs. "Kneel."

"...What?"

"Kneel, and finish your apprenticeship. Properly. From the beginning. Not the corrupted craft you cobbled together in alleys — the real grammar, the real law, learned in order, learned *humbly*. Seven years of working alone has warped you worse than you know. Your charms work, yes, the way a fence holds when the posts are set crooked — until the storm. And a storm is precisely what is coming." She looked down at the girl, and let every year of her age show in her face. "I will not help you face the Gardener as you are. You would burn bright and die fast and the covenant would fail. So: kneel. Swear to learn. And when you have learned — when the third gate opens — you and I will stand there as mistress and journeywoman, as *equals*, and not one hour before."

Hazel stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes — fury first, Mother Yew saw it, welcomed it even, fury was honest — and then something else, something more complicated, the expression of a woman recognizing that the trap and the gift had been built from the same timber.

"You're asking me to trust you again," Hazel said. "After you just told me your love was a leash."

"I'm asking you to be strong enough to risk it," Mother Yew said. "That is the entire curriculum, child. Everything else is commentary."

The wind moved through the petrified orchard. Somewhere far off, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the shears opened and closed.

Hazel looked at Wendell. The boy said nothing — wise boy, row seven — but he met her eyes, and whatever passed between them made Hazel's shoulders come down half an inch, which in the language of Hazel Coldharbour was a surrender complete.

She knelt. On stone that had been soil. In front of a madwoman in a dead orchard, with the end of both worlds six days out, Hazel Coldharbour knelt.

"Teach me," she said. "Properly. From the beginning."

Mother Yew's knees nearly went. A hundred years, and the words landed like rain on cracked earth — not healing, not yet, but *soaking in*, going down toward roots she'd believed were dust.

"Then rise, journeywoman-to-be," she said, and her voice only broke a little, "and bring me that boy's hand, because the first lesson begins tonight, and the first lesson is why the shears are afraid of him."

Wendell went pale. "Afraid of *me*?"

Mother Yew smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight.

"Child," she said, "when the Gardener fled his rounds to come hunting you across a dead orchard — did you truly think he was chasing *us*?"

Far below, at the orchard's edge, the rhythm of the shears stopped. Then resumed. Faster.
