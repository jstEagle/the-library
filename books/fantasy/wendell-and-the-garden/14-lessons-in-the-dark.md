## 14. Lessons in the Dark

The first lesson was how to hold a candle.

Not light one — that was trivial, a trick I could have done at six years old with a whispered word and a pinch of stolen tallow. No. Mother Yew set a stub of beeswax on the flat stone between us and said, "Hold it," and when I reached for it she struck my knuckles with her stick, quick as a wren.

"With your craft, girl. Not your hands."

So I sat in the ruined orchard with the grey light coming down through petrified branches like light through dirty glass, and I held a candle with my will alone, and it was the hardest thing I had done in seven years of hard things.

"You're pushing," Mother Yew said. She sat across from me wrapped in three shawls despite the garden's bloodless warmth, her hands folded on her stick, her eyes — clouded now, half-blind, but seeing more than they ever had — fixed on the flame. "You push magic like you push everything else. Like a cart up a hill. That's not how it works. That's never how it worked. You learned wrong and then you learned alone, which is worse."

"I learned well enough to survive," I said.

The flame guttered.

"There." She pointed with her chin. "You felt that. Anger is a wind, and winds snuff candles. Again."

I breathed. I steadied. The flame stood up straight again, small and gold and absurdly bright in all that grey, and for a moment — just a moment — I felt what she wanted me to feel: not mastery but *conversation*. The candle was not an object to be commanded. It was a small warm thing willing to burn, if I only asked properly.

"Better," Mother Yew said grudgingly, which from her was a parade.

* * *

I had forgotten how much of hedge-craft was waiting.

In Vell, my charms were fast things. A knot tied thrice and spoken over, a pinch of ash in a paper packet, a coin warmed in the palm until it took the buyer's luck into itself. Quick magic for quick money, stitched together out of stolen moments and held breath. It worked. I told myself for seven years that it worked, and mostly it did.

But here, kneeling in the black soil of the Sundered Garden while Mother Yew watched from her stone, I was learning that everything I knew was built on a warped foundation, the way a tree grows crooked around some old injury.

"Say the finding-words," she ordered.

I said them. The little rhyme I'd used a hundred times to locate lost buttons and lost coins and once, memorably, a lost husband hiding from his wife's creditors. *What was sundered, seek and see; what went wandering, come to me.*

"Now find the well-water under us."

I cast the spell downward and got — nothing. A blankness, like shouting into wool. I tried again, harder, and this time the magic came back at me sideways, skittering off into the petrified grass and making it twitch like a dying thing.

"Stop. Stop before you bruise yourself." Mother Yew levered herself upright and came to crouch beside me, smelling of camphor and old smoke. "Why do you think it failed?"

"Because the garden's dead. The ground doesn't—"

"The ground is *sleeping*, not dead, and even dead ground answers a polite question. Say the words again. Slowly. And mean them as a question, not a demand."

I said them slowly. And because I was tired, and humiliated, and seven years past any pretense of pride, I actually listened to what I was saying. *Come to me.* Not *show me where you are.* Come to me. In Vell, that phrasing had been efficient — why send your senses wandering when you could drag the answer home? But water did not wander. Water was already everywhere it wished to be. My spell had been asking it to abandon its nature, and the water, sensibly, had declined.

I changed one word. *What lies sleeping, seek and see; what rests below, rise to me* — no, not rise. *Show me.* What rests below, show me.

The answer rose through my knees like cold silk. An aquifer, broad and slow, running beneath the whole orchard, feeding roots that had been stone for a century. I gasped. Mother Yew made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been pruned out of her decades ago.

"Seven years," she said, "of pulling instead of asking. Seven years of taking instead of trading. Do you know what that does to a witch?"

"It works faster," I muttered.

"It *costs* less," she snapped. "That's the trap, girl. Rough magic is cheap magic. It spends nothing today and bills you forever. Every charm you ever sold in that city — every shortcut, every forced binding — left a splinter in you. I can feel them from here. You're riddled with them, like rotten timber."

I looked down at my hands. They looked the same as always: chapped, ink-stained, ordinary. But I thought of the luck-charms I'd sold, dozens of them, each one a tiny theft of fortune from somewhere else, unbalanced, unpaid. I thought of the child I'd saved with forbidden craft, and the bill that had never come due — or had it? Had it come due on Mother Yew instead, a hundred years in a withering garden?

"Can they be removed?" I asked quietly. "The splinters."

"Everything can be mended. Nothing can be unmended. We'll work on both." She settled back onto her stone and pulled her shawls tight. "Again. Find me the oldest living root within a mile. And Hazel—" I paused at the use of my name, still strange in her mouth after so long. "Don't be clever. Cleverness is how you broke it the first time."

* * *

Wendell, meanwhile, was making the garden nervous.

I noticed it on the third morning. He had taken to rising before either of us — Briarlock claimed he slept like something buried rather than something resting — and he would walk the rows of the petrified orchard with his hands behind his back, cataloguing, sketching, doing the quiet mapmaker things that seemed to soothe him the way prayer soothed the devout. He talked to the trees. I'd caught him at it, low apologetic murmurs — *sorry, sorry, I know, I'll move the path* — as if he were a guest disturbing a household.

On the third morning, where he had knelt to examine a crack in the soil, there were flowers.

Small ones. White, five-petaled, the kind that grow in churchyards and waste ground and are too humble to have a proper name. A scatter of them, maybe thirty, ringing the spot like a fairy ring drawn by someone who had only heard about fairy rings secondhand.

"Briarlock," I called, and the fox-headed herald came trotting through the rows with his ears cocked.

He looked at the flowers for a long moment. His brush-tail went very still.

"Curious," he said, in the tone of someone saying *alarming*.

"Is it bad?"

"Nothing the seedling does is bad." He said it automatically, and then his ears flattened, as if the word had escaped without permission. He glanced at me, and I looked back, and neither of us explained ourselves, because we had both privately decided not to know yet. "It is merely fast," Briarlock said. "Growth here has been slow for a hundred years. Slower than slow. And around him—" he gestured at Wendell, visible now in the middle distance, crouched and gentle and utterly unaware, sketching the bark of a petrified pear tree, "—things remember how to hurry."

By evening there was a strip of green along the path Wendell habitually walked, a ribbon of new grass perhaps two hands wide, following his footsteps like a tide following the moon. By the fifth day, one of the petrified apple trees near his sleeping-place had put out a single bud, grey-white and furled, on a branch that had been stone since before my grandmother's grandmother.

Wendell pretended not to notice. I watched him pretending. He would walk past the green ribbon with his eyes carefully forward, the way you pass a beggar you've decided not to see, and then at night I heard him talking to the budding tree through the wall of our shelter, low and urgent: *"I didn't ask you to do that. Please. I don't know what happens to things that grow too fast here."*

The bud opened anyway. It was the first blossom the Sundered Garden had seen in a century, and it was white as bone, and it smelled — Briarlock confirmed this with a reverence that frightened me more than fear would have — it smelled exactly like the Otherworld used to smell, before the covenant cracked.

* * *

"Again," Mother Yew said.

We were working at night now, because my lessons went better in the dark. Light, she said, made me greedy — I wanted to see results, and wanting to see was its own kind of forcing. So we sat in the black orchard with no candle at all, and she taught me to work blind.

"Find the moth."

I closed my eyes. Somewhere in the dark there was a moth — she'd charmed it earlier, marked it with a thread of her own power, sent it fluttering. I reached out the way she'd taught me, not pushing but opening, the way you open a door you hope someone is standing behind.

At first: nothing. The dark was just dark. Then, gradually, other senses woke that I hadn't known I owned. I felt the orchard as a vast slow body around me — stone trees with sleeping sap, soil holding its breath, the aquifer turning beneath us like a great patient fish. I felt the Hollowed far off, a wrongness like a spoiled note, huddled in their ruin somewhere east. I felt Wendell—

I stopped. Because feeling Wendell was like feeling a lit window in a dark street. He was asleep in the shelter, and even asleep he *glowed* against the garden's dimness, a warmth, a green hum, and around him the soil itself seemed to lean toward him the way plants lean toward sun.

"Hazel." Mother Yew's voice, dry as husks. "You've found something interesting."

"I found the moth," I lied.

"You found the boy. Everyone finds the boy. The whole garden finds the boy; that's rather the problem." I heard her shift on her stone. "Now let it go and find the moth. Attachment is another form of force. Even love, grasped too tightly, becomes a fist."

I let go of the window-light, reluctantly, and found the moth — a tiny trembling signature, thread-marked, hiding in the hollow of a stone apple. When I opened my eyes, Mother Yew was watching me with an expression I couldn't read in the dark.

"You hate this," she observed.

"What?"

"This. Being taught. Being wrong. Kneeling." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You fled me rather than kneel, and now you kneel every night and hate every minute, and you think I don't see it."

"I don't hate it," I said, and then, because lying to her had gotten harder as the lessons stripped my rough magic away and left the raw nerve of me exposed: "I hate that you were right. About all of it. The shortcuts, the splinters, the — everything. I built a whole life on broken craft and called it independence, and it nearly killed me, and it *did* kill—" I stopped.

"A hundred years," Mother Yew said softly, "is a long time to be right."

We sat in the dark together. Above us the petrified branches made a lattice against the starless sky — the garden had no stars; that was one of its griefs — and somewhere in the rows, the single blossomed tree stood like a candle in a burned house.

"Why didn't you teach me this way before?" I asked finally. "Before I ran. It was always rules and rote and *don't ask why*. Why didn't you teach me to listen?"

She was silent so long I thought she wouldn't answer.

"Because listening," she said at last, "is how you learn truths your teacher would rather you didn't. I kept you deaf, girl. It was safer for me." Her voice didn't waver, but something underneath it did, deep down, like the aquifer. "That failure is mine. The splinters in you are yours. We will mend what we can mend."

* * *

On the seventh night, I dreamed of the garden dreaming.

I don't know how else to say it. I slept in the shelter with Wendell breathing slow across the room, and I dreamed I was standing in the orchard, except the trees weren't stone — they were alive, heavy with fruit, and the river ran silver, and the sky had stars in it, thousands, arranged in patterns I almost recognized. And the whole dream was suffused with a feeling of *waiting*, of a held breath a century long, of something enormous turned toward a single point of light.

I woke before dawn with my heart hammering and went outside, and the green ribbon along Wendell's path had widened in the night. It was a road now, soft turf edged with clover, running from the shelter door to the blossoming tree and stopping there, as if the garden had laid it down deliberately — a path to an altar, or a threshold.

And the tree had more buds. Dozens. All furled, all waiting, all pointed at the shelter like compass needles.

Briarlock was standing in the grey pre-dawn looking at them, and his tail was lashing, slow and unhappy.

"They're timing themselves," he said, without preamble. "To him. To his sleep, his waking. This is not how gardens behave, Hazel Coldharbour. Gardens do not keep vigil." He turned his sharp amber eyes on me. "The Drowned Chapel lies three days downstream. The second oath waits there. And I think — I *think* — we should go soon. Before whatever is waking in this orchard finishes waking."

Behind us, inside the shelter, Wendell turned over in his sleep, and every bud on the tree turned with him.
