## 15. Second Oath: The Drowned Chapel

The grey river did not move.

Hazel had noticed it first on their second day in the Sundered Garden, walking its bank while Briarlock trotted ahead with his brush-tail held like a banner. The water lay flat as poured pewter, reflecting nothing — not the petrified orchards, not the bruise-colored sky, not even themselves when they leaned over it. It was a river that had forgotten how to be a mirror. She'd dropped a pebble in once and watched it sink without a ripple, without a sound, as though the river swallowed rather than received.

Now they stood on the bank where Briarlock said the chapel was, and Hazel understood why the water refused to reflect anything. Some things should not be seen twice.

"There." The fox-herald's voice was a dry rustle of leaves. He gestured with one clawed hand at a shape beneath the surface — a suggestion of slate roof, a bell tower leaning at an angle no honest architecture would choose, all of it softened by decades of silt into something like a sleeping animal. "The Drowned Chapel. Second oath-site. Your predecessor oaths were sworn there, before the waters rose."

"Before the waters rose," Wendell repeated. He was crouched at the bank's edge, sleeves rolled, studying the river the way he studied everything — with the patient, hungry attention of a man who mapped coastlines for a living and could not stop reading terrain. "Rivers rise from rain, or springs, or flood. This one rose from *grief*, I think. Look at the banks. They're not eroded. They're *slumped*. Like shoulders."

Hazel looked. He was right. The whole valley bent inward around the water, the way mourners bend around a grave.

"How deep?" she asked Briarlock.

"Deep enough." His amber eyes caught the light strangely. "The choir keeps it now. They were the chapel's singers, once. When the covenant began to fail and the garden began to rot, they sang the rites anyway, day after day, until the river took them mid-hymn. They did not stop singing when they drowned. That is the mercy and the horror of them. They still keep the second oath the only way left to them — by never letting the song end."

"And the oath itself?" Wendell asked.

"A gift given in another's keeping," Briarlock said. "The first oath was a name freely spoken. The second is a name *entrusted* — given into someone else's hands, to hold or to spend. Trust, seedling. The chapel tests whether you can bear it."

Hazel felt the words land somewhere under her ribs. A name entrusted. She thought of Mother Yew, half-mad in her cage of thorns, and of the slap that had been waiting for Hazel across a century. She thought of every charm she'd ever sold in Vell with a false smile and a false name.

"I can't swim," Wendell said quietly.

She turned. He wasn't looking at the river anymore; he was looking at his own hands, turning them over as if checking for something missing. Since the Well of Names — since he'd given his true name into the stone-dark water and come up lighter by a lifetime of small memories — he'd had moments like this. Pauses. Little absences behind his eyes where something used to be.

"Wendell—"

"The teahouse," he said. "I know we met in a teahouse. I remember your face across the table and I remember being *afraid* and I remember being happy, both at once, which made no sense. But I can't see the table. I can't taste the tea. There's a hole where that afternoon should be, and I've decided—" he looked up at her then, and his mouth tried for a smile and mostly managed it, "—I've decided to trust that it was good. You have that look about you. Like something good that happened to me."

Hazel's throat closed. She had meant to tell him what he'd paid. She had rehearsed it a dozen times on the road, and every time the words had turned to ash in her mouth, because telling him would make it real, and because some cowardly part of her wanted to be remembered as a beginning rather than a cost.

"You paid more than you knew," she said finally. "At the well. And I let you."

"I know." He said it simply, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Briarlock told me. I'm not angry. I gave it freely — that was the whole point, wasn't it? Payment given freely is the whole of the law." He stood, brushing silt from his knees. "So. A chapel at the bottom of a river that doesn't ripple, guarded by drowned singers, and I can't swim. I suppose we walk in and hope the covenant does the rest?"

"No," Hazel said. "We prepare. Properly this time."

* * *

They prepared for two days, because Hazel insisted, and because Mother Yew — who traveled with them now, wrapped in rags and thorn-scarred dignity, her madness receding and returning like weather — kept muttering corrections to Hazel's knotwork until the girl wanted to scream.

"Air pockets," the old woman rasped, watching Hazel weave breath-charms into strips of waxed linen. "You're weaving them too tight. Magic needs room to expand, child, same as bread. Bind it loose or bind it twice."

"I know how to bind a breath-charm."

"You know how to bind one *fast*, in an alley, with Wardens coming. That is not the same as knowing how to bind one *well*." Mother Yew's clouded eyes fixed on her. "That is the difference between us, Hazel. You learned magic as escape. I am trying to teach it to you as craft. Escape-magic is thin. Thin magic drowns."

Hazel tied the knot again, looser. It held better. She hated that.

Wendell spent those two days at the bank, sketching. He drew the sunken chapel from memory of its outline, then drew it again with the water removed, mapping the probable interior — nave, transept, the choir loft above, the crypt below. "If the choir guards the oath," he reasoned, "the oath is probably at the altar. We go in through whatever door faces downstream, follow the wall on my right hand, and don't cut across open floor. Churches are designed to funnel you toward the altar anyway. Let the building do the work."

"You're treating it like a survey," Hazel said.

"I'm treating it like the only thing I know how to do." He tapped the sketch. "I can't give you spells, Hazel. I can't fight Hollowed things or out-bargain foxes. But I can read ground. Let me do that much."

She wanted to tell him he'd done far more than that — that he'd given his name at the well so she wouldn't have to give hers, that he walked into every horror this garden offered with his chin up and his fear honestly worn. Instead she said, "Stay close to me underwater. If anything goes wrong—"

"Nothing will go wrong," he said, with the particular confidence of a man who has never yet drowned.

* * *

The third morning, they went in.

The river accepted them the way it accepted the pebble: completely, silently, without welcome. Cold closed over Hazel's head like a lid. The grey water was not dark exactly — it was *null*, a colorless medium that erased distance and direction alike. Her breath-charm bloomed warm against her chest, a loose-woven bubble of sweet air, and she saw Wendell beside her, his own charm glowing faintly gold, his eyes wide above the waxed linen bound over nose and mouth.

Below them, the chapel resolved out of nothing. Slate roof first, furred with pale growth. Then the bell tower, its bell hanging motionless, green with age. Then doors — great oak things swollen with a century of submersion, standing open as if the congregation had only just gone in.

Hazel touched Wendell's arm and pointed: the wall, right hand, just as he'd planned. They descended together through water that pressed on her ears like thumbs, past windows glazed with silt that turned their passage into a slow dance of shadows, and crossed the threshold into the nave.

Inside, the chapel was *full*.

Not of water — though the water was there, patient as furniture. Of song. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a hymn in no language Hazel knew, and as her eyes adjusted she saw them: the choir. Figures seated in the wooden pews and standing in the loft, robed in drifts of their own dissolved flesh, their faces smooth as river stones. They had been beautiful once. Now they were the idea of singers, preserved in the amber of their own devotion, mouths open in eternal verse.

One of them turned its head.

The movement was gentle. That was the worst of it — the gentleness. Smooth stone eyes regarded Hazel, then Wendell, and the hymn did not falter, but the note changed. Other heads turned. The song shifted key, and Hazel felt the shift in her teeth, in the wax of her charm, in the water itself, which suddenly had *texture*, a current where no current had been, drawing them down the aisle whether they willed it or not.

*Let the building do the work,* Wendell had said. The building was doing it. The funnel toward the altar had become a funnel toward something else.

They passed pew after pew. Close now, Hazel could see that the choir's robes were stitched with names — thousands of them, embroidered in thread that might once have been gold, names worn to whispers by the water. Names entrusted. Names held. A century of givers and keepers, drowned mid-vow.

The altar rose ahead, a slab of black stone furred white with silt-bloom, and upon it lay a shape that made Hazel's heart stutter: a chalice, overturned, and beneath the chalice a folded scrap of vellum, glowing faintly, patiently, with the same light she'd seen crawling through the prophecy book in the teahouse.

The second oath. Waiting to be taken.

Wendell reached it first. Of course he did — he was taller, and the current favored him, and he had stopped being afraid somewhere back among the pews, replaced by that mapmaker's focus that Hazel both loved and dreaded. He set his hands on the altar's edge. The vellum unrolled itself beneath his fingers, ink stirring like worms in winter soil.

And the choir stopped singing.

The silence was absolute. A century of unbroken hymn, ended in a single instant, and the water seemed to *lean in*. Every stone face turned toward the altar. Toward Wendell. Their mouths moved now without sound, shaping words Hazel couldn't parse, and the current reversed — pulling *away* from the altar, dragging Wendell backward off his feet, out into the open crossing, into the deep of the transept where the water pooled blackest.

He looked at her. Even through the null water, even through the linen over his face, she saw it clearly: surprise. Then the current took him properly, whirled him like a leaf, and slammed him against a pillar hard enough that she heard the crack of bone through the water.

His breath-charm flickered.

"No—" The word came out as a stream of bubbles. Hazel kicked for him, fighting a current that fought back, that had *weight* and *intent*, and the choir watched with their smooth stone faces and their silent mouths, and the hymn's absence pressed on her eardrums like depth itself. Wendell tumbled end over end, arms windmilling, and his charm guttered — gold light strobing, dying — and the linen tore away from his face and the grey water came in.

She reached him as he sank. Got an arm around his chest. His eyes were open, fixed on hers, and they were not afraid — they were *apologetic*, which was unbearable, which broke something in her that had been holding since the teahouse, since the prophecy, since the hedge.

*I'm sorry,* his eyes said. *I've ruined the survey.*

Her vow of caution. Seven years of it. Never draw on your hoard, Hazel, never spend what you've saved, never burn what you might need tomorrow, because tomorrow always comes and it always wants more than you have. She had hoarded charm-magic the way misers hoard coin and cowards hoard excuses — against a rainy day, against the Warden's knock, against the moment she'd have to run again. Years of it. Small savings, knotted charms, luck and warmth and breath and mending, a lifetime of careful little fires banked in her blood.

She opened the hoard and burned all of it.

It went out of her like a sunrise going wrong — upward, outward, *through* her, every knotted charm unraveling at once into raw working. The water around them turned gold. The pressure vanished. The current died mid-pull, and the grey river — for the first time in a hundred years — *rippled*. She felt it ripple, felt the whole drowned valley shudder as her fire ran through it, and she hauled Wendell against her and spoke the working straight into his mouth, breath-charm woven of her own last reserves, her lips against his through the torn linen, air and light and every careful saved thing pouring out of her and into him.

*Live,* she told him, in the old tongue her mother had used for lullabies, in the hedge-craft tongue Mother Yew had beaten into her, in plain desperate Vellish. *Live, live, live.*

The choir began to sing again.

But the hymn had changed. It was her working now, her fire, taken up and harmonized — a hundred stone mouths giving her desperate little charm a grandeur she'd never intended, lifting it, carrying it, and the gold light spread through the chapel like dawn through fog, and the silt-bloom on the altar *bloomed*, actually bloomed, white flowers opening on black stone.

Wendell coughed. Breathed. His hands found her face in the golden water.

"Hazel," he said — her true name, the one she'd never given anyone, the one he must have read in the ink at the altar, or perhaps simply known, the way he knew things he shouldn't. "Your name. It's Hazel. It was always going to be Hazel."

She laughed, or sobbed, or both, bubbles streaming from her mouth. "You nearly drowned and you're *smug*."

"I nearly drowned and you *burned your hoard*." His thumb traced her cheekbone, leaving a trail of gold light. "Seven years of saving. For me. Why would you—"

"Because I love you," she said.

Underwater, in a drowned chapel, surrounded by the dead who never stopped singing, it came out simple. No hedging. No charm-seller's polish. Just the fact, laid bare as the altar stone.

"I think I started loving you in a teahouse I can't remember," Wendell said, "and I know I finished in this river. Hazel Coldharbour. I love you too. I'd have told you on dry land, with better lighting, but the choir seems to want it on record."

Around them, the choir's hymn swelled toward resolution — and resolved. The final chord hung in the water like a held breath, and then the vellum on the altar flared white, and the ink wrote itself in letters of fire:

*A NAME ENTRUSTED. A NAME HELD. THE SECOND OATH IS SWORN.*

The chapel exhaled. The current gentled. And through the high windows, Hazel saw light that was not her fire — pale, cold, moving — moonlight, filtering down through the grey river from a sky that should have been hours from dark.

The dark moon had risen early.

Above them, distant but distinct, came a sound neither of them had heard before and both recognized instantly: the long, dry *snick* of shears closing on empty air.

The Gardener knew they were coming.
