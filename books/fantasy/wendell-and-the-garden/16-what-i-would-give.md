## 16. What I Would Give

*Hazel*

The alcove was a ribcage of stone, some ancient thing the river had swallowed whole and half-digested. Its arches curved overhead like the vaulted ribs of a drowned cathedral, and between them the grey water pressed — not flowing so much as *waiting*, patient as a creditor. But inside, the air was dry. Warm, even, where Wendell sat with his back against the wall, wrapped in every blanket Hazel had thought to charm against damp in her old life.

He looked small in them. That frightened her more than the chapel had.

She had burned seven years of hoarded magic to pull him out of that choir's hands. Seven years of luck-charms and fever-breaks and love-knots sold for coppers in the rain, each one a little knot of power she'd tied off and hidden away like a miser's coins — and she'd spent all of it in one screaming breath beneath the water. She could still feel the hollow where it had been. It ached like a pulled tooth. Like an absence shaped exactly like herself.

Wendell's lips were still faintly blue at the edges. He'd stopped shivering an hour ago, but she kept counting his breaths anyway, the way she used to count coins at the end of a bad market day.

"You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm checking you're alive."

"Ah." A ghost of a smile. "Carry on, then."

She almost laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and turned into something else entirely, and then his hand found hers in the dark and held it, and she let him, because her hands had been empty for seven years and she had forgotten what it was to be held.

* * *

*Wendell*

The water had taken things from him. He was only beginning to understand how many.

Not memories — those were still there, Vell intact behind his eyes: the archive's dust-gold light, the smell of old vellum, the teahouse with its rain-streaked windows and the woman across the table who had looked at him like he was a puzzle she hadn't decided whether to solve. All of it remained. But when he reached for the *edges* of things now — the way the canal had smelled after rain, the exact color of the sky over the map-room — they came up blurred, softened, like places seen through fogged glass. The choir had sung his name while it tried to keep him, and names, he understood now, were not labels. They were anchors. And something had dragged its anchor through his depths.

Hazel's hand was cold. He held it anyway.

"Tell me about the book," she said quietly. "The real version. Not the one you told me over tea, when you still thought I'd laugh."

He should have known she'd circle back to it eventually. She circled everything, his hedge-witch. Wary orbits, never landing.

"It wasn't catalogued," he said. "That's the first strange thing. Everything in the Ashvale Archive is catalogued — there are ledgers of the ledgers. But this book was just... there. On a shelf that shouldn't have existed, between a treatise on canal tolls and a farmer's almanac from before the Wardens. Leather gone soft as skin. No title."

"But it had your name in it."

"It had *both* our names." He watched her face do the thing it did when she was frightened and refusing to show it — a small tightening around the eyes, a stillness that wasn't calm. "'When the runaway of the cold harbour meets the ash-born under a leaking roof, the hedge shall open.' I thought someone was playing a joke. Archivists do that. Bored men with too much learning." His voice dropped. "I went back for it three days later. The shelf was gone. The wall was solid stone, no seam, no dust disturbed. As if the book had grown there overnight and been reabsorbed by morning."

"And you came to find me anyway."

"You were in the book." He said it simply, because it was simply true. "I'd spent my whole life making maps of places I'd never go. Here, finally, was a place I was already written into. Do you know what that's like? To be a footnote your entire existence and then find yourself a heading?"

She didn't answer. Her thumb moved over his knuckles, once, absent, like she was checking he was real.

"Hazel," he said. "What did it cost you? In the water. I saw your face after. I've seen maps of battlefields with less grief on them."

* * *

*Hazel*

There it was. The question she'd been building walls against since she'd hauled him coughing onto the chapel steps, and here it came walking up to her plain as a bailiff.

She could lie. She was good at lying; she'd had practice. *Nothing much. A few charms. Pocket change.*

But he had given his name to a well for her. He had nearly drowned holding a stranger's hand in a drowned church because she'd asked him to trust her. And lying to him suddenly felt like spitting into the one clean well left in her world.

"Everything," she said. "I gave it everything I'd hoarded. Seven years of work. Every charm I ever tied and didn't sell, every scrap of power I pinched off and saved instead of spending. Hedge-witches live small, Wendell. We bank little bits of ourselves in knots and charms, and if we're careful we can live long off the interest." She laughed, and it hurt. "I spent the principal. All of it. To buy you out of that choir's ledger."

His hand tightened around hers.

"What does that mean?" he asked. "Practically. What does that mean for you?"

"It means I'm nearly empty." She made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plain. "I can feel the edges of myself like a house with the furniture gone. I've got maybe — a whisper. Enough for a light-charm. Enough to warm soup. If something comes at us in the night, I can't fight it. I can't ward us. I'm—" Her voice caught on the word she'd been avoiding. "I'm ordinary now. For the first time since I was nine years old, I'm just a woman in a cave."

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the alcove's mouth, the grey river moved with its patient, terrible patience.

"That terrifies you," Wendell said. Not a question.

"Of course it terrifies me." She pulled her hand free and stood, because sitting still with that truth was unbearable, and paced the small space like a fox in a snare. "Magic is the only thing I've ever had that was mine. Not Mother Yew's teaching, not the covenant, not some prophecy — *mine*. I built it charm by charm in a city that would hang me for it. And I poured it out in one breath for a man I met four days ago, and the worst part—" She stopped. Turned to face him. "The worst part is I'd do it again. Right now. Without hesitating. What does that make me?"

"Mine," Wendell said softly.

The word landed in her chest like a struck bell.

"Don't," she whispered.

"Why not? You said it yourself. You'd do it again without hesitating. That's not nothing, Hazel. That's not charity or guilt or debt. That's—" He rose, slow, unsteady, the blankets sliding from his shoulders, and crossed the small distance between them. Up close his eyes were the brown of good tea, of turned earth, of things that grew. "That's choosing. We've both been chosen enough by prophecies and wells and drowned choirs. Let this be the one thing we chose ourselves."

"The covenant may demand more." The words came out of her before she could stop them, the fear she'd been carrying since Briarlock first explained the oaths. "You heard the herald. Two oaths down, one to come, and the dark moon rising, and neither of us knows what the third will ask. What if it asks everything? What if it asks—"

"Then we'll know the price going in," he said. "And we'll pay it together, or not at all."

"You don't know that. You can't promise that."

"No," he agreed. "I can't. I can only promise that whatever it asks, I won't let it take you alone. Not while I have anything left to stand between you and it." His hand rose, hesitant, asking permission, and touched her cheek. His fingers were still cool from the river. "You spent seven years of hoarded magic for a man you met four days ago. I cast my name into a well for a woman who flinched when I smiled. Neither of us is cautious, whatever we tell ourselves. So let's stop pretending."

* * *

*Hazel*

She kissed him. Or he kissed her; afterward neither could say, and it didn't matter, because in a dry alcove at the bottom of a dying borderland, with the grey river pressing its patient weight against the stones and a mad oath-keeper sleeping somewhere above and a Gardener pruning screams out of topiary somewhere beyond — in all of that vast and crumbling world, there was only this: two people who had given pieces of themselves away and found, in the giving, that what remained fit together.

When they broke apart, she was laughing again, properly this time, wet-eyed and helpless.

"We're fools," she said.

"Complete fools," he agreed solemnly. "Mapmakers and hedge-witches. The prophecy really should have warned the realms."

She leaned her forehead against his. Beneath them, impossibly, a thread of green had crept through a crack in the stone floor — a single shoot, pale and new, curling toward their feet like a cat toward warmth. Hazel stared at it. Wendell followed her gaze.

Neither of them mentioned the flowers that bloomed where he slept. Neither of them mentioned the shadow he didn't cast. There would be time — or there wouldn't — for the price still coming. But here, now, in the space between one breath and the next, they chose. Plainly. Deliberately. With open eyes and empty hands.

"Whatever it asks," Hazel whispered.

"Together," he answered. "Or not at all."

The little shoot unfurled its first leaf between their feet, green as a promise, and far off through the stone they both heard it — faint, rhythmic, deliberate:

the snip of shears.
