## 19. Runaways Again

The Hollowed had left their camp scattered like a dropped hand of cards when they fled — a cookfire still smoking, a kettle of grey river-water half-boiled, and three captives huddled under a nettle-cloth tarp who kept looking at Hazel as if she were a door out of a burning house. She had fed them dried plums and told them the road east was clear for a mile at least. She had not told them that the road east led nowhere. In the Sundered Garden every road led nowhere; that was rather the point of it.

Now it was evening, or what passed for evening here — the sky dimming to the color of old pewter, the petrified orchards throwing long shadows that did not quite match the angle of the light. Wendell had built a fire in the lee of a fallen wall, using wood he'd gathered from trees that had been stone for longer than either of them had been alive, and somehow the wood had burned anyway, because the garden bent its rules around him without ever admitting it was doing so. Small white flowers had opened along the seam of his sleeve where he'd leaned against a mossy trunk. He hadn't noticed. He never noticed anymore, and Hazel had stopped telling him, because each time she said *there's another one* something in his face closed like a fist.

She sat across the fire from him with her knees drawn up and her charm-bag in her lap, worrying the drawstring the way she worried everything — between finger and thumb, over and over, until the cord went soft and frayed. Mother Yew slept nearby, truly slept for once, her breath rattling in and out of her like wind through a broken shutter. Briarlock had gone ahead to scout the road to the Heartwood Gate, tail low, ears flat, which was the fox-herald's way of saying he thought they were all walking into a scythe.

Hazel watched Wendell turn a stick in the fire and thought: *I could love this man. I think I already do. And one of us is going to be planted in the ground like a tulip bulb.*

So she said it out loud. Or nearly.

"Wendell." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "What if we didn't?"

He looked up. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind the firelight there was that other thing now, the thing she couldn't unsee since Mother Yew had spoken the truth of him — a green depth, slow as sap, patient as roots. The last seed of the World-Tree, wearing a mapmaker's face and a cardigan with a button missing.

"Didn't what?" he asked, though she could see he knew. He'd been watching her worry the drawstring for an hour. Mapmakers noticed things. It was the first thing she'd liked about him, back in the teahouse, before the ink crawled and the world tore open like wet paper.

"Didn't do any of it," she said. "The gate. The exchange. The Gardener's terms. We walk away. Tonight, while Mother Yew sleeps and Briarlock's out of earshot. There are margins, Wendell. Places where the garden thins so much even the Hollowed don't bother. I've lived seven years in the cracks of a city that wanted me dead — I know how cracks work. We find one deep enough for two, and we stay in it. Let the realms fray. Slowly. Over years. Maybe decades."

"Hazel—"

"Think about it." She was leaning forward now, words coming faster, the way they did when she was selling a charm to someone who didn't know yet that they wanted it. "The fraying isn't a flood. It's dry rot. Canals running backwards, yes, but slowly. Wards going strange, but people adapt — people always adapt. My whole life is proof of that. We could have years. Real ones. Tea on Sunday mornings. You drawing maps of places that actually exist. Me—" her throat tightened around it, "—me growing herbs in a window box instead of a graveyard. Nobody has to be a seed. Nobody has to be erased. We just... opt out."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, one of the petrified orchards groaned — the sound the garden made when its magic shifted, like an old house settling on a failing foundation.

Wendell set down his stick. He did it carefully, the way he did everything, aligning it parallel with the edge of the fire-ring as though order were a courtesy you paid the universe. When he spoke, his voice was gentle, which was worse than if it had been angry.

"The Hollowed," he said. "The ones we fought today. Did you look at them? Truly look?"

"I drove them off, didn't I?" Her jaw was tight. "I know what they are."

"Do you?" He wasn't accusing. That was the terrible thing — he was genuinely asking, with that open, awful sincerity of his. "Because I've been thinking about them all afternoon. The woman with the apple-core face. She looked at me and she *wept*, Hazel. She couldn't remember why she was crying, but she cried. They were people. People who drew on the dying magic because it was there, because they were hungry or lost or desperate, and it hollowed them out like fruit left too long in frost. And the fraying is what made the magic corrupt in the first place. Every year the covenant decays, the garden gets more poisonous, and more people fall through the thin places and get hollowed. Slowly. Like you said." He paused. "Dry rot doesn't kill the house quickly. It just means everyone living in it breathes the spores."

"That's not fair," Hazel said. "You're making it sound like—"

"Like what?"

"Like choosing us is choosing *them*. It isn't. I'm not responsible for every wretch the hedge swallows. I was nineteen, Wendell. I ran because I had to. I've spent seven years keeping my head down and my hands clean and my name out of ledgers, and it nearly killed me, and it taught me one thing worth knowing: you cannot save everyone by feeding yourself to the attempt. All you do is make one more ghost for other people to grieve." She heard her own voice rising and couldn't stop it. "I've been the person someone sacrificed for. Mother Yew took my blame — took a century of imprisonment for what I did — and do you know what it gave me? Not freedom. A debt I can't pay and can't put down. Sacrifice isn't noble. It's just a way of making your absence into a weight other people carry."

"Then let me carry my own weight," Wendell said quietly.

And there it was. The sentence she'd been circling for days, sitting in the middle of the fire between them like a coal.

"You don't know what you'd be carrying," she whispered. "You think planting yourself is — what? Going to sleep in good soil? The Gardener wants to put you in the ground, Wendell. *You.* Your memories, your name, your ridiculous way of lining up sticks, the way you laugh through your nose when something's funny but you're trying not to show it. All of it goes into the dirt so a tree can grow where a man used to be. That's not sacrifice. That's erasure with better PR."

"It might not be."

"Might." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "We're gambling a soul on *might*."

"And you're gambling two worlds on *won't*." For the first time his voice had an edge, thin and bright as a freshly cut stem. He stood up. The flowers on his sleeve trembled. "You want to run. Fine. I understand running — I've read enough of your silences to know you're very good at it. But I've seen the Hollowed up close, Hazel. I held that woman's hands while she cried without knowing why. I saw the man whose shadow moved wrong, who'd forgotten his own children's faces but remembered exactly how many apples he'd stolen — four hundred and eleven, he counted them every night, it was all he had left. If we run, that keeps happening. To someone's sister. To some clerk buying a luck-charm in the rain. To children born in Vell who'll grow up in a city that's forgetting itself one street at a time." He was breathing hard now. "You taught hedge-craft to a child once and let your mentor burn for it. Don't tell me you don't care about strangers. I know that's a lie. I've watched you lie to yourself for weeks."

The words landed like slaps, and the worst part — the part that made heat climb her throat and prickle behind her eyes — was that every one of them was true.

"Don't," she said. "Don't you dare use her against me."

"I'm not using anyone. I'm telling you what I see."

"What you see!" She was on her feet too now, and the firelight threw their shadows huge against the fallen wall — his shadow, she noticed distantly, slightly greener at the edges than it should be, and she hated that she noticed, hated that everything about him had become evidence. "You've been here five weeks. Five! You found out you're a *seed* ten days ago, and you've already decided to be compost for the greater good, because it's easier than sitting with the horror of it. I get it. Believe me, I invented that trick. Run toward the duty so you don't have to feel the fear. But you don't get to dress it up as virtue while you call me a coward for wanting to live!"

"I never called you a coward."

"You didn't have to!"

They stood there, chest to chest across the fire, close enough that she could see the pulse jumping in his throat, and the garden seemed to lean in around them — the petrified branches creaking, the grey river muttering over its stones, the whole withered borderland holding its breath like a chaperone pretending not to listen.

"I love you," Wendell said.

It stopped her cold. He hadn't said it before. Neither of them had, not in words, not plainly, though it had been accumulating between them for weeks the way silt accumulates in a slow river — silently, inevitably, changing the course of everything.

"That's not fair either," she said, and her voice broke on the last word.

"I know." He reached across the fire — not touching her, just laying his hand palm-up on the stones between them, an offering, a map legend: *here is where I stand.* "But I'm not going to weaponize it, and I'm not going to take it back to win an argument. I love you. And because I love you, I can't watch you trade the world for a window box, and you can't watch me trade myself for a tree, and here we are." His mouth twisted. "Both right. Both unbearable."

Hazel looked at his open hand. She thought about taking it. She thought about how easy it would be — how the argument would dissolve into warmth, into the smell of his skin and the flowers that bloomed wherever he lingered, and how tomorrow the question would still be waiting, sharpened, patient, because love doesn't answer questions, it only makes them heavier.

She didn't take his hand. She also didn't step back.

"If we run," she said, slowly, testing each word like ice on a canal, "it's not forever. It's — time. Time to find another way. A third option. There's always a third option; the covenant itself is proof, it was *made*, someone made it, which means it can be remade differently—"

"Or," Wendell said gently, "we run, and the third option never comes, and one morning we wake up in our crack in the world and hear that Vell's canals flow backwards and the Wardens' wards have become cages and nobody remembers anybody, and we'll know we chose that. With our eyes open. For a window box."

"Stop it."

"A window box, Hazel."

"*Stop.*" She turned away from him, arms wrapped around herself, and stared out at the darkening orchard. The petrified trees stood in rows like a congregation that had been turned to salt mid-prayer. Somewhere among them, faint and rhythmic, water dripped from a stone branch into a stone pool — the garden's only clock, counting down to a dark moon neither of them could stop.

Behind her, Wendell sighed, long and quiet, and she heard him sit back down. Heard him pick up his stick. Heard the fire accept it.

"I'm sorry," he said to her back. "For the cheap shots. Both of them."

She didn't answer. But after a while she came and sat down again across from him, because leaving would have been its own kind of answer, and she wasn't ready to give it.

They sat in silence while the pewter sky went fully dark and the first stars came out — real stars, she realized, not garden-magic, the same stars that hung over Vell, which meant the borderland was thinner here than anywhere, which meant the Heartwood Gate was close, which meant the argument wasn't over, only adjourned.

Mother Yew stirred in her sleep and murmured something. Hazel caught only fragments — *the gate... the threshold... hold it, hold it* — and told herself it was just an old woman dreaming.

It was Wendell who finally spoke again, softly, staring into the flames.

"When I was small," he said, "maybe six, I got lost in the archive stacks. Before I worked there — my mother took me to the public reading day. I wandered off and ended up in a section that shouldn't have existed, maps of places with no names, and I remember standing there thinking: *someone has to draw the edges of the world or no one will know where home is.*" He looked up at her, and the firelight and the green light were the same light in his eyes now. "That's all I've ever wanted to do, Hazel. Draw the edges. Keep the borders honest. I can't not do it. It's not duty. It's just — what I am."

Hazel hugged her knees and said nothing, because what could she say? *Then I'll draw them with you*? She wasn't sure yet. She might never be sure. Love against duty, and both sides right, and the dark moon rising like a coin flipped and still spinning.

She was still searching for words when Wendell went suddenly rigid beside the fire.

"Hazel." His voice had changed entirely — stripped of gentleness, stripped of argument. "Something's wrong."

She followed his gaze. He was looking at his own hands, which he had pressed flat against the earth at the edge of the fire-ring — mapmaker's habit, reading ground — and his fingers were spread wide, trembling.

"I can feel it," he whispered. "Through the roots. Through everything. Hazel — the city. Something's happening to *Vell*."

Far to the west, beyond the petrified orchards, beyond the grey river, beyond the hedge itself, a sound rolled across the borderland like the tolling of an enormous bell — except it wasn't a bell, and it wasn't a sound, quite. It was a *pull*. Hazel felt it in her teeth, in the charms sewn into her coat, in the scar tissue of every spell she'd ever cast. The garden's dead branches shivered. The grey river stuttered in its bed, hesitated —

— and began, very quietly, to flow backwards.
