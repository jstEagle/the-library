## 20. The City Fraying

The roots found him before he found them.

Wendell had gone out at grey dawn, as he had taken to doing, telling himself it was to gather kindling when the Sundered Garden offered so little that burned. The truth was simpler and stranger: he wanted to be near the roots. They ran beneath the petrified orchard like veins beneath translucent skin, thick as a man's waist where they surfaced, silver-grey and warm in a way nothing else in this withered country was warm. When he knelt beside them, something in his chest unknotted. When he walked away, it tied itself again.

He told himself that was ordinary. He was learning how many things he had told himself were ordinary.

That morning the largest root had cracked open along its crown, a seam of new wood pale as candle-wax, and from the seam came a smell he knew from nowhere and everywhere — rain on hot stone, his mother's kitchen, the inside of the archive where he'd spent nine quiet years among maps of places no one went. Home-smell. It rose from the wound in the root like breath.

Briarlock had warned him. *Don't put your hands on what remembers you,* the fox-herald had said, tail flicking, amber eyes narrow. *The garden dreams through its roots, seedling, and you are the sweetest thing in its dream.*

But Hazel was still asleep under the hawthorn, her charm-bag clutched to her chest even now, even here, seven years of habit worn into her bones. And Mother Yew sat apart as she always did, muttering to a thornbush as though it were a student who disappointed her. And Wendell Ash — mapmaker, archivist, man who cast no shadow — stood alone in the cold light with a root breathing home-smell into his face, and he reached out, because of course he did. Because some part of him had been reaching for this since before he could name it.

His palm settled against the split wood.

The world folded like a map being closed.

* * *

He was standing on the Ropewalk Bridge over the Vellcanal, and it was raining, and the rain fell upward.

Not all of it. That was the horror of it — not all of it. Some drops fell properly, spattering his shoulders, darkening the cobbles. But between those drops others rose, silver beads lifting off the canal's surface and climbing past his face toward a sky the colour of old pewter, and where they passed through the mist they left streaks, like fingers dragged through wet paint. He watched a drop rise from the water, hang level with his eye, and inside it he saw the bridge reflected — but the reflection showed the bridge whole, bright-painted, busy with people, while the bridge around him was peeling, grey, empty.

The canal itself was running backwards.

He had lived in Vell for thirty-one years. He knew the Vellcanal the way he knew his own handwriting: sluggish, brown-green, drifting always seaward with the tide of the city's waste and rain. Now it slid the wrong way, slow and deliberate, carrying things upstream — an oar, a child's shoe, a garland of wilted flowers that must have been thrown from a bridge downstream, travelling back toward the hand that threw them. A barge moved against the current without sail or pole, sliding smoothly toward the sea-gates, and the bargewoman at the tiller stared straight ahead with an expression Wendell recognized with a lurch: the careful blankness of someone pretending very hard that everything is fine.

"Excuse me," he said, and his voice came out thin. "Madam — do you see it? The water?"

She turned her head. Her eyes were the right colour, a river-mud brown, and they looked directly at him, and there was nothing behind them looking back. Not emptiness exactly. Worse: absence where presence used to be, like a room after the furniture has been carried out but before the dust settles into the shapes.

"The water," she repeated, tasting the words. "The water goes to the sea."

It wasn't an answer. It was a sentence she remembered saying once, kept like a pressed flower, brought out because a stranger spoke to her. She turned back to her tiller. The barge slid on, backwards through time and water both.

*The city is fraying,* said the root, or the dream, or whatever voice the garden kept beneath its bark. It didn't speak in words so much as in understanding, knowledge arriving pre-felt, the way you know in a dream that the figure chasing you is your father. *You are touching the memory of the world-tree, and the world-tree holds the hem of every realm. Look. You asked to look. Look.*

"I didn't ask—"

But he had. He had been asking for weeks, in the only language he had: sleeping among the flowers, walking the rows of dead orchard, pressing his ear to the ground like a boy listening for trains. The garden had simply waited until he touched it willingly.

The bridge dissolved around him and re-formed elsewhere.

* * *

A Warden station. He knew it — the Chancery post on Tanner's Row, sandstone frontage, the iron sigil of crossed keys above the door. In the years since magic had been outlawed, the Wardens had grown from a constabulary into something between a priesthood and a customs service: men and women who could smell a charm at forty paces, who swept the markets with brass censers that smoked white when hedge-craft was near. Hazel had hidden from them for seven years. Wendell had filed their district maps without ever wondering what the little red pins clustered along Tanner's Row meant.

Now he stood in the doorway of the station and watched the wards become cages.

There was a young Warden there, barely older than a clerk, kneeling in the middle of the muster-room with his hands pressed flat to the floor. From his palms spread lines of light — the anti-magic wards, the ones the Wardens laid in every street, every threshold, every drain, a lattice woven over Vell so fine that Hazel had once described it to him bitterly as *a net under the whole city, and we're the fish*. Wendell had thought it a figure of speech.

The lattice was tightening.

He could see it now, from the inside, in the way the dream let him see: a vast web laid over the city like a second sky held just above the rooftops, and every strand pulling inward, contracting by fractions, the way a snare contracts when the animal struggles. And the city *was* struggling. He felt it through the root-memory — ten thousand small magics flaring and dying against the weave. A grandmother's kettle-charm guttering out. A midwife's knot-spell snapping. Somewhere a child's imaginary friend winking out of existence because imagination itself was being taxed at the borders.

The young Warden lifted his head. His nose was bleeding freely, both nostrils, two red threads dripping onto the floorboards, and his eyes were wide and wet.

"It won't hold loose," he whispered. "Sir? Sergeant? It won't hold loose anymore. The wards want to *close*."

An older Warden crossed the room, took the boy by the collar, and shook him once, hard. "Then close them tighter. That's the job. That's been the job for a hundred years." But his voice shook too, and when he turned away Wendell saw why: the older man's shadow was fraying at the edges, dissolving into small grey moths that beat against the lamplight and fell, and beat again, and fell.

*The wards were made to keep magic out,* the root-memory said, gentle as a teacher correcting a child's grip on a pen. *But magic does not live outside. Magic lives underneath, in the roots, in the covenant, in the space between realms that my death has left unstitched. Your Wardens built a net over a hole in the world. As the hole widens, the net pulls. Soon it will pull so tight that it closes — and everything caught inside will be kept. Nothing in. Nothing out. No rain falling upward, no water running anywhere, no one remembering anyone.*

"No," Wendell said. "No, they'd notice. People would—"

*Would they?* 

And the dream, almost kindly, showed him.

* * *

A market square he knew — Cornmarket, where he bought apples every Saturday from a woman named Mrs. Pell who always gave him the bruised ones half-price because she said a growing lad needed feeding, though he had stopped growing at nineteen. The stalls were up. The crowds were there. The noise was there, the ordinary roar of a city morning.

Except that nobody was speaking to anybody.

They moved through the market like figures in a music box, each on their own small track. Mrs. Pell arranged her apples in perfect rows, rearranged them, rearranged them again, her lips moving around words that never came. Two women who had clearly arrived together stood a foot apart, staring at each other with polite, frightened blankness, the way strangers wait at a bus stop. A man dropped his purse; coins scattered; three people stepped around them without looking down, because picking them up would require knowing whose they were, and that knowledge was going, going, the way tide takes a sandcastle grain by grain.

Wendell crouched and gathered the coins himself. His hands passed through one of them — through it, like smoke through smoke — and he understood that he was not really here, that none of this was quite here yet, that the garden was showing him tomorrow dressed as today.

A little girl tugged her mother's sleeve. "Mama, that man's crying."

Her mother looked directly through Wendell. "Who, darling?"

"Mama, the map man. Mama, the man who draws the—"

"Darling." The mother's voice was kind and terrible. "We don't know any map men."

The girl looked at Wendell. She couldn't have been more than six. And she *did* know him — he saw it in her face, the stubborn animal certainty of a child refusing to accept an adult's erasure — and then he watched the certainty flicker, gutter, dim, like the last of a candle drowning in its own wax. Her brow smoothed. She took her mother's hand.

"Can we get apples?" she said.

They walked away. Wendell stayed crouched among the scattered coins for a long time, in a market that had not happened yet, holding a coin that was not quite real, weeping for a child who would forget him by teatime.

*This is what fraying means,* said the root. *Not fire. Not flood. Unpicking. A stitch here, a stitch there. First the water forgets which way it runs. Then the wards forget they were mercy and remember only that they are walls. Then the people forget each other. I held the hem of it all, seedling. I am dying, and the hem is coming loose, and the unraveling has already begun in the place you loved first.*

"Vell," Wendell said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "It started in Vell."

*It started wherever you were,* the root said, and there was something in the way it said it — tenderness, apology, terrible weight — *because you are the last of me, and the world gathers around what remains of its keeping.*

* * *

The dream shifted again, and this time it did not take him to a place. It took him to a person.

Hazel.

Not the Hazel asleep under the hawthorn — a Hazel in Vell, in a room he somehow knew was hers, small and high-windowed, a single bed, a table covered in half-made charms, the walls hung with dried herbs gone brittle as parchment. She sat on the bed's edge in the blue hour before dawn, lacing her boots, and she was talking to herself the way people talk to themselves when they've been alone too long and have forgotten it isn't normal.

"—luck-charm, three coppers, don't take less than four if they haggle badly, that's the rule, that's—" She stopped. Frowned at her own hands. Started again from the beginning: "Luck-charm, three coppers—"

She had forgotten the number. He watched her search for it — watched the small panic move across her face like weather — and find instead a hole where the number should be, and paper over the hole with repetition, and start again.

In the waking garden, miles and years away, Wendell pressed his forehead harder against the root and made a sound he didn't know a body could make.

*She gave pieces of herself away for years,* the root said. *Charms cost. Hedge-craft always costs; that is its honesty. She paid in small names, small memories, small loves, and told herself the toll was fair. But the loom is broken, seedling, and every thread pulled now pulls double. What she spent to survive your city is being called back. What your Wardens wove to cage the magic is closing around everyone. What I failed to hold is slipping through every finger in every realm at once.* A pause, long as a season. *Unless.*

"Unless what." He heard his own voice go flat and hard, and recognized, distantly, that it was not entirely his own voice — that something older spoke through the shape of his mouth even in dream. "Unless someone renews the covenant. Unless someone gives what's owed. Unless—" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Everyone keeps finishing that sentence with *unless someone else*."

*Yes,* said the root, simply, and did not pretend otherwise, and he loved it and hated it for that.

The vision of Hazel's room began to fade — the blue hour brightening to nothing, the walls dissolving into green shadow, her bent head and moving lips dissolving last, and he fought to keep them, clawed at the image the way a drowning man claws at driftwood, and the garden let him hold it for one more heartbeat, one more, and then—

* * *

He came back to himself face-down in the petrified orchard, cheek against soil that smelled of rain on hot stone, his arms wrapped around the great split root as though he'd been trying to hold it together by embrace alone. The seam of pale new wood pulsed faintly under his palms, in time — he realized with a jolt — with his own heart.

"Wendell."

Hazel's voice. He rolled over. She stood above him in the grey dawn with her hair loose and her eyes wide and frightened, still in her sleep-rumpled shirt, and behind her Briarlock paced a tight circle, tail low, and beyond them both Mother Yew watched from her distance with an expression Wendell had never seen on her ruined face: not madness, not contempt, but recognition. The look of a woman seeing an old grief arrive on schedule.

"You were gone," Hazel said. She dropped to her knees beside him, hands hovering over his arms without touching, the way you hover over something you're afraid might be hot. "Two hours. Maybe three. Your eyes were open. You were *looking* at things that weren't there, and you were crying, and when I said your name you said—" She stopped. Swallowed. "What did you say? Tell me what you said."

"What did I say?"

"You said, *'we don't know any map men.'*" Her voice cracked clean through the middle. "Over and over. *We don't know any map men.* Wendell, what did you see?"

And he opened his mouth to tell her — about the rain falling upward, the barge sliding backward, the young Warden bleeding from both nostrils while the net closed over the city, Mrs. Pell and her apples, the little girl whose certainty guttered out like a candle — and what came out instead, first, before any of it, was the thing that had lodged deepest:

"She knew me," he said. "Hazel, there was a child in the market, and she knew me, and then she didn't. It took maybe ten seconds. Ten seconds to forget a person entirely." He pushed himself upright, and the root pulsed under his hand, patient, enormous, dying. "I always thought the stakes were — I don't know. Abstract. Realms, covenants, balance. Words in a prophecy book. But it's not abstract. It's a mother saying *we don't know any map men*. It's your canals running backwards. It's you forgetting the price of your own charms." He grabbed her hands, and hers were cold, and he didn't care. "It's already happening. It's happening *now*, in Vell, tonight, while we sit here deciding whether we're brave enough."

Hazel had gone very still. He knew that stillness — he'd learned it in the teahouse, in another life, watching her decide whether to run. "The wards," she said quietly. "Tell me about the wards."

"They're closing. The whole lattice. The Wardens think it's strength. It's a snare drawing tight, and when it closes—"

"When it closes, nothing gets out." Her face had drained to the colour of the grey river. "Seven years I slipped through that net thinking I was clever. And the whole time it was winding itself shut around everyone I—" She stopped herself, jaw working. Around everyone she'd sold charms to. Everyone she'd protected. Everyone she'd refused to love closely enough to lose.

Briarlock ceased pacing. The fox-herald's ears had swiveled forward, and when he spoke, his voice had lost its usual dry irony entirely. "How long," Briarlock said, "did the dream show you? How far along is the fraying?"

"Early," Wendell said. "Water first. Then wards. Then—" He couldn't say *forgetting* again. It would make it realer, and it was already unbearable. "Then people."

"Then we have until the dark moon," Briarlock said. "Perhaps less. The moon does not wait for courage, seedling. It never has."

Mother Yew rose from her stone. She moved stiffly, a century of sitting in her joints, and she came toward them through the dead grass, and Wendell braced himself for raving, for prophecy-screech, for the madwoman the garden had made of her.

Instead she stopped in front of him and studied his face with her clouded eyes, and said, in a voice scraped almost smooth by madness, almost calm:

"You touched the roots. You touched them and they *showed* you." Something moved across her face — envy, or pity, or both wearing the same mask. "Only the keeper's blood sees the fraying whole. Only the World-Tree's own—" She caught herself. Pressed her lips shut. Turned away. "Well," she said to the dead orchard, to no one, to the garden itself. "So it's true, then. So the book was right. So we really are down to the last of it."

"Down to the last of *what*?" Hazel demanded. "What's true? What did the roots show him that you know about?"

Mother Yew did not answer. But Briarlock had gone rigid, every hair on the fox-body standing, and the herald looked at Wendell — looked *through* him, at the shape of him, at whatever it was that cast no shadow even in full sun — and said, very softly, in the tone of a creature reading its own death warrant:

"The Gardener is going to want to meet you, seedling. And after what you've seen — after what the roots have shown you — I think you need to meet him too. There is something you must understand about who prunes this garden." A pause. "And why he will know your face the moment he sees it."

Far off, beyond the petrified orchards, somewhere along the grey river's bend, a sound drifted to them on the windless air: the rhythmic, unhurried *snick... snick... snick* of shears at work.
