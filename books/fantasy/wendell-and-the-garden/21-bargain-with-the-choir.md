## 21. Bargain with the Choir

The grey river did not flow so much as remember flowing. It moved in slow, reluctant pulses, like a heart that had forgotten why it beat, and where it lapped against the drowned chapel's stones it left a residue of silt that glittered faintly — not gold, Hazel thought, but memory ground fine enough to shine.

She stood on the bank with her boots sunk to the ankle in grey mud and watched the water for a long time before she spoke.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"Of course it knows." Mother Yew sat on a fallen pillar, her back straight despite the century bent into her, her hands folded like a woman waiting for tea. Her eyes — one clouded white, one sharp as a needle — fixed on the water. "The choir has been singing to us since the orchard. You simply weren't listening."

"I was listening. It sounded like wind."

"It sounded like wind because you wanted it to." The old woman's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "That was always your gift, child. Hearing what you wished to hear."

Hazel didn't answer. Seven years ago that sentence would have lit a fire under her skin; she would have snapped back something clever and cruel, something designed to make Mother Yew flinch. Now she only breathed the garden's cold air and let the words settle into her like rain into dry ground. There was truth in them. There had always been truth in her mentor's cruelty, and that was the worst thing about it.

Behind her, Wendell crouched at the water's edge with his sleeves rolled up, his fingers hovering just above the surface. He'd been doing that for an hour — not touching, never touching, but close enough that the river seemed to lean toward him. Where he knelt, the silt had begun to glow a faint, uncertain green, like grass remembering spring.

"Don't," Hazel said quietly.

He looked up. His face was thinner than it had been in the teahouse, a lifetime ago. The Well of Names had taken more than his memories of Vell; it had taken some softness around his eyes, some ease, and left him looking like a man who had woken from a long dream he could no longer describe. But when he smiled at her, the smile was still entirely his.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

"I'm always thinking about it." He withdrew his hand and wiped the mud on his trousers. "It wants me to touch it. I can feel it pulling. Like — you know when you pass a door and you're certain you've left something burning inside?"

"That's not comforting, Wendell."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Briarlock paced the bank behind them, fox ears swiveling, tail low. The herald had been quiet since the chapel's bell tower first broke the surface of their view that morning — a black spire rising out of the grey water like a finger raised in warning. Fox-faced heralds did not pace without reason.

"The choir guards the crossing," Briarlock said at last. Its voice was a dry rustle, leaves over stone. "The third gate lies beyond the river, in the deep wood. No boat crosses. No bridge stands. The drowned sing, and those who would pass must pay them in the only coin they still value."

"Memories," Hazel said.

Mother Yew's head turned sharply. For a moment the madness in her receded, and what looked out through her face was the woman who had taught Hazel to stir a cauldron widdershins, who had smelled of juniper and woodsmoke, who had taken the Warden's lash across her own back so that a runaway girl could vanish clean.

"Who told you that?"

"You did." Hazel kept her voice level. "Last night. In your sleep. You talk to the river, Mother Yew. You argue with it. You've been arguing with it for a hundred years."

The old woman said nothing. The clouded eye wept a single clear tear, which she did not bother to wipe away.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a petrified boathouse, its roof collapsed inward like a ribcage. Wendell slept — and where he slept, moss crept green across the stone floor, and one small flower opened its petals in the dark, pale as a moth. Hazel watched it for a long time. She had stopped pretending she didn't know what he was. She had stopped pretending a great many things since the orchard.

Mother Yew did not sleep. She sat by the dead fire, sharpening a bone knife against a whetstone, and the sound of it filled the boathouse like a slow heartbeat.

"You mean to pay them," she said finally. Not a question.

Hazel pulled her cloak tighter. "You know another way across?"

"There is no other way. That is not the same as there being no other choice." The knife whispered against the stone. "I have heard the choir bargain before, child. A hundred years is long enough to hear many bargains. They do not take what you offer. They take what you love most, dressed in whatever words you give them. You will walk down to that water meaning to trade a memory of your mother's hands kneading bread, and you will rise having given them her voice, or her face, or the way she said your name—"

"My mother has been dead eleven years."

"And you remember her every day. Don't you." It wasn't a question either. "Every charm you sell, you price it the way she priced bread. Every time you count coins you hear her counting. She is the last warm thing from before, and you have carried her through seven years of running like a coal cupped in both hands, afraid to let it go out and afraid to be burned." The knife stopped. "And now you mean to hand that coal to drowning women because it is *efficient*."

Hazel looked at her. Really looked — at the ruined eye, the scarred hands, the bone knife worn smooth from a century of sharpening and never once used.

"When you took my blame," Hazel said slowly, "did you think about what it would cost me?"

Something flickered across the old woman's face — pain, quickly mastered.

"Everything," Mother Yew said. "I knew it would cost everything. I counted on it."

"Yes. I know that now." Hazel turned toward the dark water visible through the broken roof. "But here's what I learned in Vell, in seven years of selling luck to clerks and hiding my craft in cellar-rooms: a payment made grudgingly buys nothing. A payment made in fear poisons the well. My mother taught me that too, though she never said it in words. She gave freely — bread to beggars, coin to liars, hours to fools — and she never once asked what she'd get back. I used to think that was weakness." She paused. "I don't anymore."

"Hazel—"

"Payment given freely is the whole of the law." She said it the way Mother Yew had once made her recite the properties of iron and salt: flat, certain, final. "You taught me that. First lesson, first day. You said I'd forget it. You were right. I forgot it for seven years." She met the old woman's good eye. "I'm not forgetting it tonight."

Mother Yew stared at her for a long moment. Then, softly, in a voice stripped of all its edges:

"You sound like me. Before."

* * *

Dawn came to the Sundered Garden the way it always did — not lightening so much as ceasing to be fully dark, the sky shifting from charcoal to ash. The river waited. Of course it waited. Rivers in this place had nothing but time.

Hazel walked down to the bank alone.

She had considered asking Wendell to come, and rejected it. This was not a thing to be witnessed. Some payments are made in private the way wounds are cleaned in private — not from shame, but because the cleaning itself is intimate, and no one else's hands will do.

The water rose to meet her ankles. Cold, but not cruelly so. The choir began to sing.

Up close, the song resolved into voices — dozens, hundreds, layered like sediment. Women's voices mostly, though not all. Some sang high and clear; some gurgled beneath the surface of the melody like stones rolling in current. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable, was the sound of a market square at midmorning: laughter, haggling, a cart wheel squeaking, someone calling a name across the crowd.

*Come in,* sang the choir. *Come in, come down, come under. We are so hungry. We have been hungry for a hundred years.*

"What do you want?" Hazel asked. Her voice sounded small against the water.

*What you carry,* they sang. *The warm thing. The coal in the cupped hands. We smell it. We have not been warm in so long.*

"I want to cross to the third gate."

*Everyone wants to cross. Crossing is easy. Staying whole is hard.* The water lapped higher, to her knees. *We will take you across, name-witch. We will bear you on our backs like brides. Pay us.*

"I set the price. Not you." She planted her feet. "That's how this works. Freely given means freely chosen. You get what I choose to give, or you get nothing, and I find another way, even if the other way takes until the dark moon rises and falls again."

The song faltered. For a moment the water went utterly still, and Hazel felt the attention of the drowned settle on her like a physical weight — curiosity, ancient and terrible and almost childlike.

*Nobody sets the price,* they murmured. *Nobody has ever set the price.*

"Somebody's setting it now."

Silence. Then, slowly, reluctantly, the choir began to laugh — a sound like bubbles rising through deep water, like a congregation remembering joy.

*Clever hedge-witch. Clever runaway. Very well. Show us what you bring.*

Hazel closed her eyes.

Her mother came to her immediately, the way she always did — not summoned but simply present, patient, the way she had been patient in life. The kitchen in the house outside Vell. Flour on her forearms to the elbow. The particular way she hummed when she worked, slightly flat, never correcting herself. The weight of her hand on Hazel's head, heavy and warm, when Hazel was small enough to stand between her knees.

There were so many memories. Eleven years' worth, hoarded like coins in a jar. The day she died — no, Hazel thought, not that one, never that one, that one stays. The last morning, when her mother had pressed a honey-cake into her hands and said *go on then, trouble, mind the road.* The winter she'd taught Hazel to knit, badly, both of them laughing at the tangled mess. The thousand ordinary evenings that had built the word *home* out of nothing but lamplight and flour and a voice humming flat.

She could not give all of them. She understood that now — understood it the way Mother Yew had tried to make her understand, standing in the ruined boathouse with her bone knife. Give everything and there is nothing left of you to give. But give grudgingly, clutching each memory to her chest, and the payment would curdle, and the choir would take the difference out of her hide.

So she chose.

Not the biggest memories. Not the sweetest. She chose the ones that ached in the old way, the healed-over way — the ones she had already lived from, already grown around, the way a tree grows around a fence post. The winter of the knitting. The afternoon her mother had fallen asleep in the garden chair with a book open on her chest and Hazel, nine years old, had sat watching her breathe for an hour, learning without knowing it what love looked like at rest. The evening she'd come home crying because a boy called her witch-girl, and her mother hadn't comforted her — had laughed instead, bright and fierce, and said *good, let them be scared of something true.*

Those. Those she could spare, because they had already done their work. They had made her. Taking them now was not taking her mother; it was taking the scaffolding after the building stood.

"I'll give three," Hazel said aloud. "Freely. Gladly, if I can manage it. And in exchange you carry me and mine across the grey water to the third gate, and you do not touch the others — not the man who sleeps green, not the madwoman on the bank, not the fox. Sworn on my name, which is the only thing I own outright."

The choir was silent for a long moment.

Then the water began to warm.

*Three,* they sang, wonderingly. *She offers three, and names them hers to choose, and asks protection for the others. Oh. Oh, *that* is freely given. That is the old law. That is the law before the law forgot itself.*

The river rose around her, gentle as hands. Hazel felt the memories lift out of her — not torn, not stolen, but *unhooked*, the way one unhooks a finished garment from its frame. The knitting winter went first, unraveling into warmth and sinking. Then the garden chair, the breathing, the hour of watching. Last went the fierce laughter, *let them be scared of something true*, and as it left her Hazel found she was smiling, because the words remained even as the scene dissolved — she had lived them so long they no longer needed the memory to hold them up.

What remained was the flour on her mother's arms. The flat humming. The honey-cake. The hand on her head. The death-day, safe and sealed. Enough. More than enough. A garden pruned, not felled.

She surfaced on the far bank, gasping, alive, whole in a way she could not have explained to anyone who hadn't paid a price gladly.

Wendell reached her first, catching her elbow, his face white with fear and relief. Behind him Mother Yew stood very still, her bone knife hanging forgotten at her side, and in her one good eye was an expression Hazel had never seen there before.

Respect. Bare, unarmored, and grieving.

"You paid," the old woman said. "Gladly."

"As best I could."

"A hundred years I fought that river in my dreams. A hundred years I clutched every memory I had and dared it to try." Mother Yew shook her head slowly. "And you walked in and *haggled*, like a market woman buying bread." Something cracked in her voice. "My God, child. What did Vell make of you?"

"Nothing I didn't make of myself."

Across the water, the choir was singing again — but the song had changed. It was warmer now, threaded through with a flat, off-key hum, the ghost of a kitchen tune none of the drowned had ever known. They would keep it, Hazel realized. They would keep her mother's humming the way she herself had kept the words, and somewhere in the grey depths of the Sundered Garden, a hundred drowned voices would hum a baker's tune over their own cold songs, and be a little less cold for it.

She stood, brushed the mud from her knees, and turned to face the deep wood — where the third gate waited, and beyond it, she suspected, the Gardener.

"How far?" she asked Briarlock.

The herald's ears flattened. "A day's walk," it said. "Perhaps less, if the wood permits. And Hazel Coldharbour—" it hesitated, and for the first time since the teahouse, the fox-herald looked afraid, "—the trees here are not petrified. Not anymore. Since the well, since the chapel — things have been *growing*. Fast. Toward the gate."

Hazel looked at the treeline. Between the black trunks, in the pre-dawn grey, she could see it: new growth, green and urgent, spreading along the forest floor like spilled ink — all of it flowing in one direction.

Toward the Heartwood Gate.

Toward whatever waited there, pruning shears in hand.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said. "Before the Gardener finishes tending."
