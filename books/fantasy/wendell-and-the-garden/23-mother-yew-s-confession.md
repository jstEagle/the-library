## 23. Mother Yew's Confession

The garden did not sleep, but it dreamed, and in its dreaming the grey light came down through the petrified orchards like water through gauze. Mother Yew sat with her back against a tree that had been stone for longer than she had been alive, and she watched Hazel across the dead fire-ring where no fire had burned for a hundred years.

The girl — no, not a girl anymore; a woman with rain-crow's feet beginning at her eyes and a hedge-witch's calluses on hands that had once been soft as candle wax — was mending Briarlock's snares because Briarlock had asked, and because Hazel had never been able to leave a broken thing unmended. That had always been her flaw and her glory both. Mother Yew had counted on it once. Counted on it the way a spider counts on the geometry of its web.

*Tell her,* said the madness, in its voice like wind through hollow reeds. *Or I will tell her for you, and I have never once told anything gently.*

"I knew you would come back," Mother Yew said.

Hazel looked up from the snare-wire. "What?"

"Here." She tapped her own temple, where the grey had lived so long it felt like bone. "In this place. In this hour. I have known it since the night you ran. The covenant showed me, or the madness did — they taste the same after a century — but I knew. You would come back to me, and I would have to tell you what I did, and you would look at me the way you are looking at me now."

"I'm not looking at you any particular way."

"You are looking at me the way one looks at a door that has been locked all one's life," Mother Yew said. "Wondering what is behind it. Wondering if one wants to know."

* * *

She had been beautiful then. That was the thing no one remembered about her, the thing that had rotted out of every story told in Vell's hidden corners: that Mother Yew, before the Warden's chains and the century of stone orchards, had been beautiful, and had known it, and had spent that knowledge the way misers spend coin — carefully, and only ever to buy more.

Hazel had been nineteen. Nineteen and burning, the way only hedge-talent burns when it comes up wild from the gutters instead of down through proper channels. Mother Yew had found her stealing warmth-charms off the market stall — badly, with the thief's gracelessness of the very gifted — and had thought, watching the child's fingers blur with borrowed heat: *There. There is the vessel.*

She had not thought: *there is my student.* She had thought *vessel*, and she had been honest enough with herself, even then, to know the difference mattered, and dishonest enough to proceed regardless.

"Your magic was never mine to shape," she said now, aloud, to Hazel, who had gone still over the snare-wire. "Do you understand? Every teacher says that. Every teacher lies by saying it, because we shape, we cannot help shaping, it is the whole filthy pleasure of the work. But yours — yours I wanted shaped into something specific. Something that would need me."

* * *

The night of the burning, Mother Yew had been three streets away.

That was the detail that had never made it into any telling. The Warden's ledgers recorded that Mother Yew Coldharbour's apprentice had cast forbidden craft upon a mortal child — a drowning child, pulled from the canal with lungs full of black water, called back across the threshold by a working that traded breath for years — and that Mother Yew had confessed to casting it herself, and had been taken, and had vanished into the Sundered Garden as oath-keeper rather than face the pyre.

A noble lie, the city called it. A sacrifice. They carved it into the story the way ivy carves itself into brick, and the ivy-version was the one Hazel had carried for seven years, and it was the version that had kept her awake at night with guilt like a stone in the belly, and it was — this was the part Mother Yew had to say now, and her mouth went dry as seed-husks around it — entirely wrong.

"I was three streets away," she repeated. "I felt the working go through the hedge-lines like a bell struck under water. I felt your signature on it — yours, unmistakable, that coldharbour tang you could never scrub out of your craft no matter how I taught. And I walked toward it. Slowly."

"Why slowly?" Hazel asked. Her voice was very quiet.

"Because I was counting," Mother Yew said. "I was counting how long it would take the Wardens to reach the canal, and how long it would take you to run, and whether the two numbers would meet. And when I understood that they would — that you were too slow, that you were standing over that drowned child weeping and wringing your hands like an amateur, that the Wardens' lanterns were already turning the corner — I made a choice."

The petrified orchard creaked around them. Somewhere far off, the grey river moved without moving, the way it did.

"I chose to arrive just late enough," Mother Yew said, "to be seen."

* * *

Hazel set down the snare-wire. Her hands were not steady. "You're saying—"

"I am saying that I stood in the mouth of Cooper's Alley and let them find me there, near enough to the scene that no other explanation would serve, and I confessed before they finished asking. And I did it—" and here the madness rose in her like floodwater, and she let it, because the truth needed the flood to carry it out, "—not to save you. Do not give me that face. Do not make me noble in your memory one more time; it has been eating me alive for a hundred years. I did it because a student who owes you her life is a student forever. Because guilt is the strongest binding known to craft, stronger than blood, stronger than true names, stronger than love. Because I had watched you chafe against me for a year — watched you ask questions I hadn't sanctioned, read books I'd shelved high, grow edges I hadn't cut — and I knew you would leave me eventually, the way all bright things leave their keepers, and I could not bear it."

The words came out of her like splinters coming out of flesh, each one leaving a hole.

"So I built you a leash," she said. "Out of debt. Out of shame. I made myself the woman who burned for you, and I knew — I *knew*, Hazel, I planned it in the alley with the lantern-light coming — that you would carry me on your back for the rest of your life. That every charm you sold, every spell you cast, every quiet morning you allowed yourself, would taste of my sacrifice. That you would never be free of me, and that you would come back. Guilt is a homing instinct. I have seen pigeons do less."

* * *

Silence. The kind that has weight and grain and temperature. Hazel's face had gone through shock and out the other side into something flatter, more careful, the face of a woman doing sums.

"The child," Hazel said finally. "The little boy from the canal. Did you even care whether he lived?"

"He lived because of you. Yes, I cared — I am not the Gardener, I have not lost everything human. But caring was not why I came. Caring was not why I stood in that alley. I want that understood. I want it understood by you and by whatever is left of me that will listen afterward." Mother Yew's voice cracked along a fault-line she had not known was there. "I took your blame the way a lender takes collateral. And the worst of it — the part that curdled into this—" she gestured at her own head, at the grey fog behind her eyes, "—is that it worked. For six days it worked. You knelt to me. You wept on me. You swore you would spend your life repaying me, and I lay awake that night warmer than I had been in thirty years, drinking that promise like wine."

"And then?"

"And then you ran."

* * *

"You ran," Mother Yew said again, softer. "Six days after I chained myself to your crime, you climbed out the scullery window with your kit-bag and your stolen luck-charms and you vanished into Vell, and the leash I had built with such care snapped clean through, and I learned the lesson that broke me: that guilt binds only those who stay. That sacrifice given without being asked for is not a gift. It is a theft. I stole your debt from you before you could choose it, and a stolen debt cannot be carried. It can only be fled."

She laughed, and the laugh was terrible, thin as frost on a windowpane.

"Do you know what I did when I found the empty room? I sat on your bed. I sat there until the Wardens came back for me, and I sat there in the cart, and I sat there through the trial, and I sat there in the tear between worlds as it swallowed me, and every mile of it I was thinking: *she is free.* I gave everything — my name, my craft, my century — to bind her, and the binding failed, and she is free, and I am here. And the thinking of it, over and over, year over year, among these stone trees with nothing to tend but my own reflection — that is where the madness began. Not in the garden's corruption. Not in the Hollowed air. In that single unanswerable arithmetic. I paid everything for nothing, and nothing was exactly what I had purchased, because you cannot purchase a person, and I had tried."

* * *

Hazel had risen at some point. She stood at the edge of the dead fire-ring, and the grey light came down on her, and Mother Yew saw that she was trembling — not with rage, which she had expected, and perhaps deserved, but with something worse. With recognition.

"You let me believe it," Hazel said. "Seven years. Every night. I built my whole life on that guilt. I wouldn't take lovers because I didn't deserve ease. I wouldn't use real craft because I'd sworn it away in your name. I starved myself of everything because I thought I owed it to you."

"Yes."

"You could have written to me. Through the hedge-lines, through a dream, through any of the hundred doors a woman of your skill knows. You could have told me the truth any time in a hundred years."

"And what would you have done?" Mother Yew asked. "Would you have come back to me, if I wrote and said: *child, I lied, the debt is void, live your life*? Or would you have simply stopped carrying me at all — dropped me like a stone from your pocket and gone on lighter, and never thought of me except with contempt?"

Hazel said nothing.

"That is what I feared," Mother Yew said. "Not your hatred. Your indifference. I had built my entire self on being necessary to you, and when necessity failed I had nothing left, and so I let the lie stand, even here, even alone, because the lie was the last thing I owned. Madness is only greed that has run out of things to be greedy for. I hoarded your guilt the way the Hollowed hoard fruit, long past rotting, long past feeding anyone."

* * *

The wind moved through the petrified orchard, and it made no sound, because there were no leaves to sound against, and somehow that silence was louder than any accusation Hazel might have spoken.

But Hazel did not speak. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself, and Mother Yew watched her do the arithmetic — she could see it happening behind the woman's eyes, the same arithmetic, the same cold ledger — and she waited for the verdict. Hatred. Contempt. The turning-away. She had earned all of them and braced for all of them.

Instead Hazel said, in a voice gone strange and small: "The night I ran. Why did I run? All these years I've told myself it was fear of the Wardens. But I think—" and her voice caught, "—I think some part of me knew. Some part of me felt the leash go taut and bolted, the way a hawk bolts, without understanding why. Is that possible?"

"It is not only possible," Mother Yew said. "It is the single mercy in this whole rotten account. You were wiser than I was. Nineteen years old and wiser than a woman of sixty years' craft, because wisdom is not craft, it is the willingness to be free, and you had it and I never did." She bowed her head. The grey in her skull shifted like sediment. "I bound you to a crime you did commit, with a sacrifice you did not ask for, and you escaped it anyway, and I have hated you for it and loved you for it in equal measure every day of my exile, and I do not know which of the two has kept me sane, or whether either has."

* * *

Far off across the withered ground, a bell-note sounded — the Heartwood Gate, testing itself, tasting the approaching dark moon. Both of them turned toward the sound. The hour was coming when none of this would matter anymore, when names would be weighed and gates opened, and Mother Yew felt the strangest lightness, as though the confession had been a tumor and speaking it the knife.

"There is one thing more," she said. "Before the gate, before whatever comes. I will not ask your forgiveness. Forgiveness is a coin I have not earned and will not counterfeit. But I will tell you what I have learned, since it is the only inheritance I have left to give: the leash was never on you, child. It never was. A binding only holds if the bound consent to be bound — that is the first law and the last law, the law beneath payment and names and oaths. You consented for seven years out of love, or habit, or wounds I gave you. But you could have walked away any dawn, and the moment you truly knew it, the moment you *know* it now—" she looked up, met Hazel's eyes, and did not flinch, "—the leash turns to smoke. It always was smoke. I only ever had the power you handed me, and you have just taken it back, and I find—" her voice failed, steadied, "—I find I am glad. God forgive me, I find I am glad."

Hazel stepped forward. One step, then another, until she stood over her seated mentor, close enough that Mother Yew could see the canal-water grey of her eyes, the eyes of the runaway of the cold harbour, and in them not awe, not reverence, not the old terrified gratitude —

Something else. Something gentler and more terrible than all of those.

Pity.

"Hazel," Mother Yew began, not knowing what would follow the name, having nothing prepared for the first time in a hundred years—

And Hazel knelt. Down into the ash of the dead fire-ring, down to eye level, and reached out, and took her mentor's ruined hands in her own callused ones.

"No," Hazel said quietly. "Whatever you're bracing for — no. You don't get to decide what this costs you anymore. Neither do I." She glanced up, toward the distant bell-note of the gate, and Mother Yew saw resolve settle over her face like frost forming, swift and complete. "Because I've just realized something, and I need you to hear it before we walk another step toward that gate. If a name given freely is the whole of the law — if consent is the first law and the last — then everything the covenant asks of us, everything the Gardener demands, every sacrifice they've laid out like coins on a table —"

Her grip tightened.

"— none of it is binding unless we say yes. And I am done saying yes to other people's arithmetic."

Beyond the stone orchards, the bell-note rang again — closer now, urgent, and beneath it came a new sound, faint and rhythmic, that Mother Yew had not heard in a hundred years and knew instantly, and feared utterly:

the slow, patient snip of shears.
