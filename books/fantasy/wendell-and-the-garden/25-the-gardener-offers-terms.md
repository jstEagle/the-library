## 25. The Gardener Offers Terms

The Gardener came to us at dusk, and he came the way frost comes to a window: gradually, then all at once.

We had made camp in a hollow of the old orchard, beneath trees so ancient their petrified branches had begun, in the last two days, to bud again — small green fists of leaf clenched against the grey air, as if the whole grove were dreaming of spring and could not quite wake from it. Hazel had built a fire of fallen wood that burned with a scent like crushed rosemary. Mother Yew sat apart, as she always did now, her back against a trunk, her hands moving ceaselessly in her lap — knotting, unknotting, tying invisible threads only she could see. Briarlock had gone ahead to scout the road to the Heartwood Gate, his fox ears swiveling at every sound the garden made, which was all of them, always.

I was mending my boot with a strip of bark-cloth when the light changed.

It is difficult to describe how a place like the Sundered Garden changes light. In Vell, dusk arrives like a tide; here it arrived like a held breath being slowly released. The grey between the trees deepened by degrees, and every degree of it felt deliberate, measured out by something patient. I looked up from my boot and the shadows under the petrified boughs were longer than they should have been, and they were all pointing the same direction — toward me.

"He's here," Mother Yew said. She did not look up. Her hands kept knotting. "Don't run. Running is pruning."

Hazel was on her feet before the word *pruning* finished leaving the old woman's mouth, one hand already full of hedge-light, that green-gold glow she'd been learning to hold without letting it eat her alive. "Wendell," she said quietly. "Behind me."

"Hazel—"

"Behind me."

I did not go behind her. I have never been good at being protected. I stood instead beside her, close enough that our shoulders touched, and I watched the space between the trees where the shadows pooled deepest, and I waited for the Gardener to finish arriving.

He did not walk so much as *become*. One moment there was empty orchard; the next, a tall figure stood among the budding trees as though he had been standing there for centuries and we had simply failed to notice him. He wore robes the color of turned earth, and over them an apron stained with sap older than any tree I had ever mapped. His face was the problem. It was kind. That was the terrible thing about it — it was genuinely, warmly kind, the face of a grandfather at a harvest table, lined and gentle and lit from within by something that might have been love. Only his eyes were wrong: not cruel, but *complete*, as if nothing that happened behind them could ever surprise him again, because everything already had.

"Seedling," he said.

His voice was soft soil and slow water. Hearing it, I understood why Briarlock spoke of him in whispers. There was no menace in it. There was something worse: certainty.

"You've grown," the Gardener said, and took a step closer, and Hazel's hedge-light flared. He paused, considering her the way a man considers weather. "You've both grown. That is the trouble with growing things. They insist on it." He smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, and somehow that made it worse. "May I sit? This old ground and I have an understanding. It will hold me."

He sat anyway, uninvited, on a root as broad as a bench, folding his long hands in his lap. Up close I could see that his fingernails were black with loam and that tiny white flowers grew in the creases of his knuckles, blooming and dying and blooming again in the space of a breath.

* * *

"You are wondering why I have come," he said. "You are wondering why I did not come with shears and take you, seedling, as I could have taken you any night since you first slept beneath these trees. You are wondering what I want."

"We know what you want," Hazel said. Her voice was steady. Her hand was not; I could see the fine tremor in the hedge-light. "You want to plant him."

"I want to plant him," the Gardener agreed, gently, as though she had offered him tea. "And you want me to be a monster when I say it. I understand. Monsters are simpler. But I have been the keeper of this covenant since before your city had a name, child, and I will tell you what I have learned in all that long tending: monsters are rare. Misunderstood gardeners are everywhere."

Mother Yew made a sound — half laugh, half sob. The Gardener did not look at her. Perhaps he could not. Perhaps looking at what he had helped make of her cost him something even his completeness couldn't pay.

"The covenant is dying," he went on. "You have seen it. You have walked its withered miles. Every oath unkept is a vein closing. Your city — Vell, the grey jewel, the city of wards and ledgers — sits on the far edge of those veins, and it does not know how much of its warmth it borrows. When the last oath fails, Vell will not burn. Burning would be dramatic. Vell will *forget*. Its canals will forget their courses. Its people will forget each other's faces. Its Wardens will wall themselves into their own wards and stand there, guarding nothing, forever. I have seen it. I see all endings; it is the burden of the complete."

"Then renew it another way," Hazel said. "There's more than one oath. There's more than one name. We found the riddle in the gate-verse—"

"There is always another way," the Gardener said, "the way there is always another road. And roads take time, and time is the one crop this garden has stopped producing." He turned to me then, fully, and I felt his attention settle on me like snowfall — weightless, total, cold. "So I have come to offer terms. Not threats. Terms. I am told your people respect contracts, seedling. Let us speak contract."

My mouth was dry. "I'm listening."

"Willingly given," the Gardener said. "That is the oldest law, older than the oaths, older than the Well of Names. Nothing rooted by force holds. I could dig you up and set you in the Heartwood Gate tomorrow, and the covenant would drink you dry and die anyway, because coercion makes barren ground. No. The seed must consent. The gardener must ask." He spread his black-nailed hands. "So I am asking."

"And in exchange?" Hazel's voice cracked on the second word. She heard it and hated it; I saw her jaw set.

"In exchange," the Gardener said, "everything you were going to lose anyway — except gently. Hear me. If the seedling consents, the covenant renews whole. The Sundered Garden greens from heart to hedge. The Hollowed wake with their names still in their mouths. Your madwoman—" a nod toward Mother Yew, who had gone very still, "—is released from a century of duty she never chose, free to rest or to teach, as she pleases. And Vell—" he paused, and something almost tender crossed his kind, complete face, "—Vell keeps its canals running forward and its children knowing their mothers' names. No fraying. No forgetting. No Wardens' cages. All of it, bought with one planting."

"One life," Hazel said.

"One *seed*," the Gardener corrected softly. "That is the question, isn't it? What is he? A man who maps coastlines and blushes when complimented and burns his toast? Or the last living seed of the World-Tree, wearing a man the way a bulb wears its papery skin?" He tilted his head. "I have watched him sleep, child. Flowers come up where he dreams. I do not think the skin decides."

* * *

"Show him," Mother Yew said suddenly.

Everyone turned. She had risen, or partly risen, her knotted hands gripping a low branch, her ruined eyes fixed on the Gardener with something that was not quite hatred and not quite pleading. "Show him the vision. You came to show it. Show it and let him choose with his eyes open, the way no one let me choose." Her voice shook. "That is the only mercy left in you, gardener. Spend it."

The Gardener regarded her for a long moment. Then he inclined his head — a small, grave courtesy — and turned back to me.

"May I?" he asked. And gods help me, he *waited*. He asked permission even for this. That was the trap's finest craftsmanship: every door in it stood open.

"Yes," I said.

The world folded.

* * *

I stood in Vell. Not the Vell I knew — a Vell washed clean, morning-bright, its canals running silver and sure beneath bridges hung with flowering vines that had no business surviving northern winters but survived them anyway. Magic moved through the streets openly, the way water moves through open gutters after rain: a woman at a market stall charming her apples to ripeness while customers laughed; children chasing motes of light down an alley; a Warden — a *Warden* — kneeling in the street to mend a broken cartwheel with a word and a touch, his badge repurposed into something like a guild-mark, his face unguarded, even happy.

I knew this Vell. That was the horror of it. I knew it the way you know a place from a dream you've had every night for years. The teahouse by the canal was there, its windows glowing, and through the window I saw Hazel — a Hazel with no shadows under her eyes, laughing at something, her hands wrapped around a cup, wearing magic as easily as a shawl. Beside her sat a young apprentice, a street-kid with wild hair and wilder talent, and Hazel was teaching her, patiently, kindly, all the things Hazel had taught herself alone and badly and at such cost.

There was a charm stall under her own name. COLDHARBOUR, painted on the awning, in cheerful letters.

I walked — or the vision carried me; I could not tell which — down lanes I knew, past faces I knew. Mother Yew sat in a sunny courtyard teaching a circle of students, the madness gone from her like fog off a field, her hands finally, finally still except when they moved to point at a student's misdrawn sigil. Briarlock trotted at her heels wearing a collar of office, herald of a court that had reopened its doors. The Hollowed walked the garden paths restored, blinking in sunlight, remembering themselves.

And everywhere, everywhere, the World-Tree. It rose from the Heartwood Gate like a green sun made solid, its canopy wide enough to shade the whole borderland, its roots drinking from a river that ran clear. And high in its branches, if I squinted, if I wanted to — I thought I could see a shape. A knot in the wood. A grain in the great trunk that might once have been a man who mapped coastlines and blushed when complimented.

Peace. That was what the vision was made of. Peace, layered on peace, on peace. Nobody sacrificed anything ever again. Nobody forgot anybody. The covenant held for a thousand years, and the thousand years after that, and every soul in two realms lived warm under the shade of the tree.

And the tree was me.

"That is the bargain," the Gardener said, and his voice came from everywhere, from the roots of the vision itself. "No one suffers. No one is erased — the seedling does not suffer, seedlings *germinate*. A man is not destroyed by becoming what he was always meant to be. Is the acorn murdered by the oak?" The vision brightened around me, Hazel's laughter ringing from the teahouse, sweet as bells. "They will grieve you for a season. Grief is the toll of every good thing; I cannot waive it, no gardener can. But they will plant gardens in your memory, and the gardens will be *real*, and their grandchildren will live in a world with no Sundering in it at all. One grief, seedling. One season of mourning, weighed against ten thousand years of morning."

I tried to speak. The vision pressed close, warm as a blanket, heavy as a blanket. Somewhere in it Hazel laughed again and I thought: *I could give her that. I could give her the stall with her name on it, the apprentice, the mornings. I could buy her whole life with mine.*

It would be so easy. That was the thing no one warned you about temptation — not that it looks evil, but that it looks like *rest*. Like putting down something heavy you have carried so long you'd forgotten it was heavy. My whole strange life — the shadow that wasn't there, the flowers in my footprints, the prophecy book that wrote itself around my name — all of it resolved, in the vision, into purpose. I had spent thirty years not knowing what I was. Here was the answer, gift-wrapped in everyone's happiness.

"Say yes," the Gardener whispered, and his whisper was the kindest sound I had ever heard. "Say yes, and be the tree. Be the shade. Be the morning. Everything else is only weather."

* * *

I don't know how long I stood inside it. Time in the vision was compost — everything decaying into everything else, seasons turning in a single breath. I remember reaching out, once, toward the teahouse window. I remember the glass was warm.

What broke it was small. That's what I'll tell myself, anyway, for whatever years I have left: that what broke it was not courage but a detail. In the vision, Hazel's charm stall stood under her own name — and above the door of the teahouse hung a little carved sign, a fox's head, Briarlock's mark, the sign of a shop that existed because a herald had settled down. Everyone in the vision had a name and a place.

Except the tree. The tree had a thousand years of morning and no name at all. You do not carve COLDHARBOUR or ASH into a trunk. You carve lovers' initials, small, at eye level, and the tree grows past them, swallows them, forgets them.

The vision ended like a candle pinched out.

* * *

I was on my knees in the orchard. I didn't remember falling. The fire had burned low; the budding trees stood around us in the dark like witnesses. Hazel had my face in her hands, and her eyes were wet and furious, and Mother Yew stood over us both with her arms raised as though she had physically been holding the vision's edges apart.

"Don't answer him," Hazel said. "Wendell. Whatever you saw. Don't answer him yet."

"She showed you the truth," the Gardener said. He had not moved from his root. His kind face watched me with what looked, horribly, like hope. "She showed you because I willed it. I hide nothing, seedling. That is the difference between us and the worlds that broke the covenant: I have never once hidden the price. Ask your mentors what hiding costs."

"He's not a seed to you," Hazel said, rising, turning on him, hedge-light climbing her arm like ivy. "You call him seedling because you can't bear to call him a man. Because a man would have to *consent*, and men say no."

"Men also say yes," the Gardener said mildly. "Free men. Freely choosing. That is all I require — all the old law requires. I will leave you to it." He unfolded himself from the root, towering, patient, and brushed loam from his apron with those flowering fingers. "But hear the terms before I go, and carry them tonight, and tomorrow, and every night until the dark moon. Consent freely given, before the moon dies: and Vell stands, and the madwoman rests, and the girl keeps her name and her stall and her long life. Refuse—" and here his voice did not harden; it softened further, which was infinitely worse, "—and I will not take him. I keep my law. But the covenant fails, and the fraying comes for Vell on its own, and no one will have chosen anything, and everything will be lost *anyway* — only wastefully, only meaninglessly. I offer meaning. That is all any gardener has ever offered."

He turned to go, then paused, and looked back at me over his shoulder, and for one flickering instant the kindness dropped from his face like a mask slipping, and underneath it I saw — not cruelty. *Exhaustion.* Ten thousand years of tending alone, of giving everything alone, of holding a dying covenant together with hands that had forgotten how to be held. It lasted a heartbeat. Then the kindness returned, seamless.

"Think of the morning, seedling," he said. "I have thought of little else for an age."

And the shadows under the trees pointed away from us again, and the space between the trunks was empty, and the fire crackled as though nothing had happened.

* * *

For a long moment none of us moved. Then Hazel's knees gave and she sat down hard in the ash-edge of the firelight, and Mother Yew lowered her arms as though setting down something enormous, and I stayed on my knees because I honestly did not trust my legs.

"You saw it," Hazel said. Not a question. "The vision. What did he show you?"

"Everything," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "Vell, saved. You, happy. Everyone, happy. A thousand years of it." I looked up at her, at the hedge-light still guttering around her fist, at the woman who had already decided, days ago, that she would erase herself to save me — and I saw the same arithmetic starting behind her eyes, the same terrible ledger opening, and I knew with sick certainty what she was about to say.

Because the worst part — the part I would carry into the next chapter of this like a stone in my boot — was that I had almost said yes. Not for the world. For her. For the stall with her name on it. For the apprentice and the mornings and the laughter through the teahouse glass.

"Hazel," I said, before she could speak. "Whatever you're about to propose — whatever plan is forming right now behind your eyes — I need you to hear me first. Because I've seen what a willing sacrifice buys. And I need to tell you why I'm not going to let it be yours."

Her mouth opened. Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Mother Yew's knotted hands went suddenly, completely still — and in the silence of the budding orchard, somewhere far off toward the Heartwood Gate, something began, very faintly, to scream.
