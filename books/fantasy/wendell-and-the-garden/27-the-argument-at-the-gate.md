## 27. The Argument at the Gate

The Heartwood Gate did not look like a gate. It looked like a wound that had learned to be beautiful.

It rose from the garden's root-mass like the ribcage of some god who had lain down here to die and been slowly upholstered by centuries of moss. Two doors of living wood, each taller than the chapel spire they had drowned, met in an archway where pale grain swirled in patterns almost like script — almost like names, Hazel thought, though she could not read them, and suspected no one had been able to for a hundred years. The air around it hummed against her teeth. Not sound. Pressure. The feeling of standing too close to something that was deciding.

Briarlock had gone quiet in the way foxes go quiet — not calm, but coiled. The herald's brush-tail hung low and still, and his amber eyes kept flicking from Wendell to Hazel to Mother Yew as if he were counting them and did not like the arithmetic.

"Two," he said at last. "The gate wants three."

"Then we knock politely," Hazel said, "and hope it's home."

No one laughed. Even she heard how thin it came out.

Mother Yew stood apart, leaning on her staff with both hands, her ruined eyes turned toward the archway as if she could see through it into whatever waited on the other side. A century in the Hollowed's keeping had left her like driftwood — hollowed herself, in a way, light enough that the wind moved her. But when she spoke, her voice still had the old iron under it.

"It will open," she said. "For what you mean to do, girl, it will open wide. Gates always open widest for funerals."

Hazel flinched. She hadn't told her yet. Not in words. But Mother Yew had taught her to read magic the way other teachers taught letters, and Hazel supposed that what she intended was written all over her now — in the way she stood slightly apart from Wendell, in the way she would not let her hand rest in his longer than a breath, in the careful emptiness she was already practicing behind her ribs. An emptied self. A vessel for the garden to root in, so that Wendell need never be planted, never be pruned down to seed and soil and nothing else.

She had rehearsed this. On the road from the choir's river, walking beside him while he talked about the way the new grass grew in spirals, she had rehearsed it the way she'd once rehearsed lies for the Wardens: smooth, simple, over before anyone could object.

"Wendell." She made herself turn and face him fully. The grey light of the dying borderland fell across his face, and she hated how gently it fell, as if even the light had already decided to be kind to him. "There's another way. There has to be. The covenant needs a name given freely — I've read every fragment Briarlock remembers, every scrap Yew can recall. It doesn't say whose name. It says *a* name. So it can be mine."

The words were out. No taking them back.

Wendell went very still. He had a mapmaker's stillness, she'd learned — the stillness of someone used to looking at large things whole — but this was different. This was the stillness of a man watching a door close somewhere inside himself.

"No," he said.

"Let me finish—"

"No." He said it again, softer, which was worse. "You've finished. I heard all of it. Every word you didn't say out loud since the chapel." He looked at his own hands, then at hers, as if comparing them for some quality only he could measure. "You've been carrying it for days. Since the choir took your mother's memories, since you started sleeping facing away from me. You think I don't notice things? I'm a *mapmaker*, Hazel. Noticing is the entire profession."

"You'd fade," she said. "You'd become a tree. That's not salvation, that's—"

"That's my choice." His voice cracked on the last word and he pressed on anyway. "And yours is your choice, and that's exactly why I won't allow it. Because it isn't a choice if you make it to spare me. It's just my sacrifice wearing your coat."

The hum of the gate deepened. Somewhere beneath their feet, roots shifted like sleepers turning over.

Wendell could feel the garden listening.

He had felt it for days now — since the Well of Names, since he'd spoken his name into black water and felt it leave him like a coin dropped down a well. The Sundered Garden paid attention to him. Grass leaned toward his footsteps. When he slept, blossoms opened above him in patterns he refused to look at too closely, because they looked like constellations, and constellations meant intention, and intention meant he was not entirely the man Hazel had met across a teahouse table while rain wrote its long grey signature down the windows.

He loved her. That was the one thing he knew with a certainty that outlasted even his stolen memories. He couldn't remember the date. He couldn't remember the teahouse, or the rain, or the first hour of her laughter — Briarlock had told him about them, secondhand, like a story about strangers. But he remembered loving her, because the love hadn't lived in the memories. It lived deeper, in the place where his name used to be, and it had grown roots there.

Which was why her plan was unbearable. Not because it wouldn't work. Because it would.

He could see it working. He could see her stepping through the gate with her name held out in both hands like bread to birds, and the covenant taking it, and the garden pouring itself into the space she left behind — and Hazel gone. Not dead. Worse than dead. Unwritten. He would wake tomorrow and there would be a shape in the world where she had been, a warmth in his chest with no origin, a love with no beloved. He would spend the rest of his life grieving someone he couldn't remember grieving.

"I know what you're doing," he said. "You're doing the arithmetic. One name against two realms. It balances. It's elegant. It's exactly the kind of solution a woman who spent seven years selling luck-charms to frightened clerks would find — small payments, quietly made, nobody hurt except yourself." He stepped closer. "But I have done the same arithmetic, and mine comes out differently. If the price is a person, then I will pay in myself or not at all. I will not stand in a blooming garden built on your absence and call it victory."

"And I," Hazel said, and her voice shook now, finally, the control cracking like ice on the grey river, "will not spend fifty years being a widow to a tree. Do you understand what you're asking me? To walk back into Vell alone? To pass the canal-side teahouse every day? To grow old knowing that somewhere behind the hedge the man I love is growing rings?" Her breath caught. "I fled one life rather than watch it be spent for someone else's design. I will not stand by and watch yours be spent either. Not even by you. *Especially* not by you."

"So we're agreed," Wendell said bitterly. "Neither of us will permit the other to be noble."

"We are *not* agreed—"

"Hazel." Mother Yew's voice cut between them like a blade through stem-work. The old woman had not moved, but her staff had come down hard on the root-ground, and the garden had gone silent around the sound. "Both of you, stop. You're arguing about who dies. Neither of you has asked the only question that matters."

Hazel turned on her. "Which is?"

"Whether the covenant wants a sacrifice at all." Mother Yew lifted her blind face toward the gate. "I swore one of those oaths once. I stood where you're standing. And I remember—" her voice frayed, mended, frayed again, "—I remember that the old words were never about payment. They were about *giving*. There's a difference, child. A payment is taken. A gift is received. The Gardener has spent a century confusing the two, and so have you."

Before Hazel could answer, a soft sound came from near the gate — a dry, rustling cough, the sound of a fox clearing its throat.

They all turned. Briarlock had sat down. In all the days Hazel had known him, the herald had never once sat down; he stood like a soldier on duty even in sleep. Now he sat with his tail curled over his paws, and his amber eyes were fixed on the swirling grain of the archway with an expression she could only call embarrassment.

"There is," Briarlock said, "a thing I perhaps should have mentioned earlier."

"Briarlock," Hazel said dangerously.

"In my defense, heralds deliver messages. We do not interpret them. Interpretation is above our station." He scratched one ear with a hind foot, entirely unrepentant. "But you have been shouting at each other for the better part of an hour, and the gate is beginning to sulk, and I am a hundred years past caring about my station."

He rose, padded forward, and touched one claw-tip to the pale wood. Where he touched it, the grain-swirls brightened, faintly gold, like embers breathed upon.

"The covenant's founding words," he said. "I heard them spoken. I was young; I carried the summons to the three oath-keepers myself. And the words were these: *let the realms be bound by a name given in love.*" He looked up at them both. "Not a name given *up*. Not a name surrendered, or sacrificed, or paid. Given. As one gives a gift. As one gives—" his tail-tip twitched, "—well. As one gives a hand in marriage, if you take my meaning. The old tongue is more precise than your Common, and considerably more romantic."

Silence. The gate hummed. Somewhere far off, the grey river ran on over its stones, patient as ever.

"A name given in love," Wendell repeated slowly.

"To whom?" Hazel said. "That's the part no one ever—"

"To whom indeed," Briarlock agreed. "The prophecy book in your city — the one that started all this — does it say? I confess I never learned to read your mortal ink."

Hazel's mind was moving fast now, faster than it had moved in seven years of careful, cramped, defensive living. *A name given in love.* Not erased. Not fed into the dark. Given — handed over, entrusted, kept. A name could be given the way a heart is given, and remain a name. Remain a *person*.

"Not to the covenant," she whispered. "To each other."

Wendell stared at her. "What?"

"My name. Yours. We give them — not to the garden, not to the gate, to *each other*. Freely. In love. The covenant takes hold of the bond instead of the bond taking hold of one of us." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her skirts. "It might not work. I don't know if it will work. Names have power, and binding two names together is—" She laughed, a wild, breathless sound with no humor in it. "It's a marriage vow, Wendell. It's the oldest hedge-craft there is. Handfasting. My mother's mother's mother probably did it with knotted cord under a full moon."

"The dark moon rises tonight," Mother Yew said quietly. "If you mean to try it, you try it at moonrise, at this threshold, with no time left to test it."

"Then we test it with our lives," Wendell said. He had crossed the distance between them without Hazel noticing — the garden must have bent its ground to help him, grass parting, roots leveling — and his hands found hers, cold fingers around cold fingers. "Together. Not one paying for the other. Together or not at all."

"Together," Hazel said.

Above the Heartwood Gate, the grain-swirls pulsed once, slow and deep, like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm. And in the forest behind them, from a direction that had no direction, came a sound that froze the blood in Hazel's veins and set Briarlock's every hair on end:

The soft, unhurried snick of shears.

"He knows," Mother Yew breathed. "He felt the gate wake. He's coming — and he will not allow a bond. Order demands a single sacrifice, child. It always has, in his mind. He'll come to prune this before it blooms."

Briarlock was already on his feet, ears flat, body low. "Then we have until moonrise," he said. "And not one breath more."

The gate hummed. The shears whispered closer through the darkening wood. And Hazel Coldharbour gripped Wendell Ash's hands and understood, with terrible clarity, that they would speak their vows tonight with the Gardener's shadow falling across the threshold — and that whether the covenant accepted a bond instead of a body, they would learn in the space between one heartbeat and the next.
