## 28. A Name Given in Love

The dark moon would rise tonight. Hazel could feel it the way she felt weather coming — a pressure behind her eyes, a hush in the petrified orchards, as though the whole Sundered Garden were holding its breath and waiting to see what they would do with it.

They stood at the edge of the Heartwood Gate's clearing, where the ground was warm underfoot like the flank of a sleeping animal. Briarlock had gone ahead to watch the treeline for the Gardener's shears, and Mother Yew sat on a root that had grown into something like a bench, her hands folded, her ruined eyes turned toward the sky she could no longer properly see.

Hazel had not stopped shaking since she'd said it.

*Give my name instead.* She'd said it the way you'd step off a cliff — all at once, before the fear could catch your heels. And Wendell had looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before, not at the teahouse, not in the drowned chapel, not even when the Hollowed had come shrieking out of the orchard rows. He'd looked at her as if she'd proposed burning down the only house either of them had ever loved.

"No," he'd said then. He was saying it again now, quieter, walking the clearing's edge with his hands jammed in his pockets like a man trying to keep them from doing something desperate. "No. I've been turning it over all morning and the answer is still no."

"Wendell—"

"You want me to stand there and let you unmake yourself." He stopped. The light through the canopy came down green-gold and made him look younger, older, both at once. "You spent seven years being nobody, Hazel. Seven years selling luck to clerks under a name that wasn't quite yours, flinching every time a Warden's boot hit cobblestone. And now your grand plan is to be nobody *forever*?"

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" His voice cracked on the word. "Because from where I'm standing, it looks exactly like the thing you already did once. You gave up your name to survive. Now you're giving it up to save everyone else, and you're calling it a lesson learned, and I am telling you—" he crossed the clearing in four strides and took her by the shoulders, gently, always gently, even now, "—I am telling you I will not be saved by your erasure. I won't carry that. I couldn't live in a world you weren't in and know I was the reason."

She wanted to argue. She had arguments; she'd sharpened them all night against the whetstone of her own resolve. But his hands were on her shoulders and his eyes were wet and furious and full of her, and every argument felt like a knife she'd have to use on him.

"The covenant needs a name," she said instead, and hated how small it sounded. "That's the law. A true name, freely given. Yours plants you. Mine empties me. Those are the only two doors out of this room, Wendell, and the dark moon rises in—" she looked up through the branches, gauging the pale disc of the sun, "—ten hours. Maybe less."

"Then we find a third door."

"There is no third door. There's never a third door. That's what magic *is* — it's the absence of third doors. It's tolls and prices and—"

"Hazel." Mother Yew's voice cut across the clearing like a blade through silk. The old woman hadn't moved, but her head had come up, and there was something in her face Hazel hadn't seen since childhood: attention. Real attention, the kind that used to make the whole cottage feel smaller and more alive. "Come here. Both of you. And bring me that book."

"There's no book," Wendell started.

"There is always a book," Mother Yew said. "Briarlock carries the covenant's memory in his skull. Ask him for the original wording of the first oath. Word for word. Not the story of it — the *wording*."

*

Briarlock came at their call, trotting out of the bracken with his brush tail low and his amber eyes narrowed. When Hazel repeated Mother Yew's request, the fox-headed herald went very still, ears swiveling forward.

"Why," he said slowly, "do you want the wording?"

"Because words are doors," Mother Yew called from her root-bench, "and someone built these ones badly. Or well. Depending on whether you're the one standing in the doorway."

Briarlock looked at Hazel. Then at Wendell. Then, last and longest, at Mother Yew, and something passed over his fox face that might have been hope wearing the mask of caution.

"A century I have waited," he said. "A century I have recited the oaths to every fool who wandered in. *A name freely given*, I told them. *One name, given in love*. And every one of them heard what they expected to hear." He sat back on his haunches. "Very well. The covenant's founding words, as spoken at the Heartwood Gate when the World-Tree was young:

*'Let the way between be kept by love freely given. Let a name be given into keeping — not taken, not traded, but given — and let the giving be in love, and let the bond so sealed hold fast while love holds.'*

He paused. "That is the whole of it. That is everything."

Silence. The grey river murmured somewhere below the ridge, patient as ever.

"It doesn't say whose name," Wendell breathed.

"It doesn't say one name," Hazel corrected, and her heart had begun to hammer, because she could feel the shape of it now, the third door, the one that wasn't supposed to exist. "It says *a name given in love*. It says *the bond so sealed*." She turned to Wendell. "It never said the covenant takes a person. We assumed. Everyone assumed. Because sacrifice is easier to imagine than sharing."

Mother Yew laughed — a dry, rustling sound, like wind through winter reeds. "A hundred years mad, and the answer was grammar."

"What are you saying?" Wendell asked, though Hazel could see in his face that he already knew, that he'd known since Briarlock finished speaking, that some part of him had known since the drowned chapel when she'd burned her hoarded magic to pull him out of the water and screamed that she loved him with the last of her breath.

Hazel took both his hands.

"We give our names to each other," she said. "Not to the garden. Not to the covenant. To each other. I give you my name; you give me yours. The covenant takes hold of the *bond* — the thing between us — instead of either of us. Nobody gets planted. Nobody gets emptied. The garden roots in what we make together."

"And if it doesn't work?" His voice was barely above a whisper. "If we're wrong about the grammar? If the covenant takes both names, or takes neither, or takes—"

"Then we'll have lost nothing but time we didn't have anyway."

"That's not true and you know it. If it fails, the realms unravel, Vell unravels, the Hollowed stay hollow, and we'll have spent our one night on a theory." He laughed, short and helpless. "A *theory*. Based on a sentence a fox memorized."

"Do you have a better one?" Hazel asked. "Because I've got an erasure and you've got a tree, and between those two options I'll take the grammar lesson."

Wendell opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked down at their joined hands — hers scarred and charm-marked, his ink-stained and faintly green-veined where the seed in him showed through — and when he looked up again, something had settled in his face. Not certainty. Certainty was too clean a word for it. It was the look of a man stepping off a cliff *knowing* there was no net, and choosing to jump anyway.

"When I found that book," he said quietly, "I thought it was a joke. A prophecy naming two strangers meeting at a teahouse — I brought it to the date half hoping you'd laugh at it with me. Instead it ate the teahouse." He squeezed her hands. "Every impossible thing since then, I've survived by believing there was a plan. Someone wrote the prophecy; someone built the gate; someone knew how this ends. But this—" he gestured at the Heartwood Gate looming behind them, its carved doors fused shut with a century of dead growth, "—this has no plan. This is just us, guessing, on the last night the guess matters."

"Yes," Hazel said.

"And you're sure?"

"I'm sure I'd rather be wrong with you than right without you."

Something moved through the garden then — not wind, exactly, but the memory of wind, stirring leaves that had been stone for a hundred years. Above them, through the gaps in the canopy, the sun was sliding west, and somewhere beyond the sky's rim the dark moon waited, patient as a debt.

*

They spent the afternoon preparing, and the preparation was almost worse than the dread.

Mother Yew drilled them in the old forms — the breathing, the grounding, the way you held a name in your mouth before you gave it away. She was a different teacher now than she'd been in the cottage years; harsher in some ways, gentler in others, and endlessly, exhaustingly precise. Her madness flickered at the edges — she'd pause mid-sentence and stare at a spot six feet to Hazel's left, or address a question to a sparrow that wasn't there — but when she spoke of the craft itself, she was iron and clarity.

"A name is not a word," she said, pacing the clearing with her staff tapping out a rhythm. "A name is everything you are, folded small enough to fit in a mouth. When you give it, you give *all* of it. Every kindness you've done and every cruelty. Every secret. Every shame. Do you understand? There is no partial surrender. The covenant will know a lie."

"Then we won't lie," Hazel said.

Mother Yew stopped pacing. She turned her blind eyes toward Hazel, and for a long moment the old woman simply stood there, and Hazel watched something work its way across her mentor's face — pride, grief, fear, all tangled together like roots.

"No," Mother Yew said at last. "I don't suppose you will. That was always your problem, girl. You never learned to lie properly. It's why you were terrible at running away and why you're standing here now."

*

The sun went down like a coin slipping into a pocket.

They made ready at the foot of the Heartwood Gate. The great doors rose above them, thirty feet of living wood carved with the faces of the three original oath-keepers — the Gardener young and serene, the second keeper whose face had been worn smooth by rain, and the third, whose features someone had deliberately defaced long ago. Dead vines hung from the lintel like funeral garlands. The whole structure smelled of cold ash and old honey.

Briarlock took his place at the clearing's edge, and Hazel saw that the fox-herald had dressed himself in the faded regalia of his office — a collar of copper bells, a sash embroidered with symbols no one living could read. A century of waiting, worn like a uniform.

"If this works," Briarlock said, "the gates open. If it fails..." His ears flattened. "If it fails, I suspect none of us will have long to regret it."

"Comforting," Wendell muttered.

"I am a herald," Briarlock said with dignity. "Comfort is not in my commission. Truth is. And the truth is that I have watched a hundred wanderers stand where you stand, and every one of them tried to pay alone. You two are the first to try to pay together." He bowed, low, the copper bells silent. "Whatever happens tonight — it has been an honor to wait for you."

The dark moon rose.

It didn't look like much — a hole in the star-field, a circle of perfect blackness where the moon should have been, ringed by a thin corona of borrowed light. But Hazel felt it in her teeth, in the marrow of her, in the seed-shaped hollow inside Wendell that she could somehow sense now whenever they touched. The whole garden leaned toward that black disc like a plant toward sun.

"Now," Mother Yew said. "Before the moon passes zenith. Face each other. Hands. And remember — freely given. The moment either of you hesitates, stop. A grudging gift is worse than no gift."

Hazel faced Wendell.

This close, she could see everything she'd learned to read in him over these weeks in the garden — the mapmaker's habit of looking slightly past a person to the horizon behind them, the way his jaw set when he was frightened and refused to show it, the green thread-vines that pulsed faintly beneath the skin of his throat like a second pulse. The man she'd met over tea and prophecy. The man she'd chosen, twice now, with her eyes open.

"I'm frightened," she told him, because lying was forbidden and because he deserved the truth.

"Good," he said. "Me too. Frightened means we understand the price."

She closed her eyes. Reached inward, to the place where her name lived — that small, warm, stubborn core of self that she'd guarded for seven years, hoarded like a dragon's coin, the last thing she owned that the Wardens could never confiscate. *Hazel Coldharbour.* Runaway. Charm-seller. Betrayer. Daughter. Apprentice. The woman who had fled and the woman who had come back and the woman who had knelt in the dirt and finished what she'd started.

She gathered it up in both hands, metaphorically and otherwise, and began to speak.

"Hazel Coldharbour," she said, and her voice rang strangely in the clearing, doubled somehow, as though the garden itself were listening through her. "Runaway of the cold harbour. I give you my name, Wendell Ash. Freely. Fully. In love. Keep it for me."

The name left her like breath leaving lungs after a long dive — a rush, a lightness, a terrible sweet emptiness. For one vertiginous moment she was nothing at all, a shape without contents, a coat hanging on a hook—

—and then something flowed *in*.

Warmth. Weight. *Wendell Ash*, settling into the space her name had vacated like water finding its level. Not replacing it — filling it. She could feel his name wrapped around her now, holding her shape the way a cast holds molten metal, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, she could feel *him*: his steadiness, his wonder, his stubborn gentle refusal to accept that the world had to run on sacrifice.

Across from her, Wendell's eyes had gone wide and bright. His lips were moving.

"—Wendell Ash," he was saying, "mapmaker, seedling, son of no one and everyone. I give you my name, Hazel Coldharbour. Freely. Fully. In love. Keep it for me."

And then his name left him, and hers came home.

She felt it return changed — carried in his keeping, warmed by him, threaded through with everything he was. Her name came back to her wearing him like a garment wears perfume. And she understood, suddenly, completely, why the covenant had demanded love: because a name given in love comes back *more* than it was. Not less. More.

The clearing lit up.

Light poured from the Heartwood Gate like dawn arriving sideways — gold and green and a color Hazel had no name for, a color that felt like the moment after crying when you finally breathe properly. The dead vines on the lintel shivered. Somewhere deep in the gate's grain, something enormous shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns toward morning.

The carved faces on the doors began to move.

The serene young Gardener. The worn-smooth second keeper. Even the defaced third, its ruined features shifting like sand in wind. All three turned their carved eyes downward, toward the two small figures standing hand in hand at the foot of the doors, and Hazel felt the gaze of a century-old covenant settle over them, weighing, measuring—

—and then the gate *recognized* them.

It happened all at once. The fused doors groaned, split, and swung inward onto darkness shot through with veins of growing light. Wind roared up out of nowhere, whipping Hazel's hair, carrying the smell of rain on far soil, of sap rising, of spring arriving early and determined. The dark moon overhead seemed to lean closer, drinking in the sight.

"It's working," Wendell shouted over the wind, and he was laughing, actually laughing, tears streaming down his face. "Hazel — it's *working*—"

And that was when the shears came out of the dark.

They came silently, which was the worst of it — a whisper of steel and a coldness like the space between stars, sweeping toward the joined hands at the center of the light. Hazel had one heartbeat to see them: vast, ancient, silver-bright, moving with the terrible patience of a thing that has pruned worlds.

Then the light around their clasped hands flared white, and the shears recoiled with a sound like a struck bell, and from the treeline came a voice like frost forming on glass:

"*No.*"

The Gardener stepped into the clearing.

He had come without sound, without warning, tall and terrible in his serenity, and his face — the same serene young face carved on the gate — was for the first time anything but serene. He looked at the open gate. At their joined hands. At the covenant-light pouring through the gap like water through a broken dam.

"You were meant to be *planted*," he said to Wendell, and his voice shook with something that in anyone else Hazel would have called grief. "One name. One sacrifice. One clean, ordered ending. That is how covenants are kept. That is how they have *always* been kept."

"Not anymore," Hazel said. She did not let go of Wendell's hand. She did not think she could have if she'd tried; the bond between them hummed like a plucked string. "The covenant never asked for one. We read the words. *Given in love.* You gave everything alone, and it broke you, and you've been pruning the truth ever since so you wouldn't have to look at it."

The Gardener's shears trembled.

For one endless moment, the clearing balanced on a knife's edge — the gate open, the covenant waking, the dark moon at zenith, and the first oath-keeper standing in the light of a solution he had never imagined, because no one had ever offered him one.

Then Mother Yew rose from her bench-root, leaning hard on her staff, and began to walk toward the gate.

"Girl," she said to Hazel, without turning, without stopping, her voice carrying clear across the roaring wind. "Hold the line. Whatever happens next — whatever he does — you hold the line. Don't let go of his hand."

"Mother Yew—"

"He gave everything alone," the old woman said, still walking, her ruined shadow stretching long and strange across the glowing grass. "Alone is the whole disease. So someone must show him otherwise." She reached the threshold. She turned, and smiled at Hazel — a real smile, crooked and fierce and proud. "Someone must stand beside him. Even if he strikes at them. *Especially* then."

And she stepped through the gate's open doors, into the light, toward the Gardener, her staff raised not as a weapon but as an open hand—

—and the Gardener raised his shears.
