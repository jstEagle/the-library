## 32. Aftermath of Green

The light did not end so much as settle.

Hazel had expected a thunderclap, a shattering, some great exhalation to mark the moment the covenant took hold — but instead the world simply *softened*, the way a fist softens when its owner finally sleeps. The Heartwood Gate stood open before them, no longer a gate at all but a doorway with the door removed, and through it poured a green-gold radiance that moved like water finding its level, filling every hollow of the Sundered Garden at once.

She was on her knees. She didn't remember kneeling.

"Wendell." His name came out of her cracked and strange, as though she had not used it in years rather than minutes. "Wendell, look —"

He was beside her, also kneeling, also staring, and for one long heartbeat he looked *fine*. Better than fine. The grey had gone out of his skin; the exhaustion of the drowned chapel and the Gardener's pursuit and the terrible arithmetic of sacrifice had all been rinsed away by whatever the exchange had done. He looked like the man who had sat across from her in the canal-side teahouse, nervous and earnest, turning his teacup because he didn't know what to do with his hands.

Then she followed his gaze upward, and forgot him entirely for a while.

The petrified orchards were blooming.

Not slowly — not the way spring works in the mortal world, coaxing and incremental — but all at once, everywhere, a detonation of blossom that rolled outward from the Heartwood Gate like a wave made visible. Trees that had stood as grey stone for a century split along their grain, and from the cracks came color: white and blush and a deep wine-red Hazel had no name for, petals unfurling with an audible sigh, thousands upon thousands of them opening in unison until the air itself was thick with fragrance and drifting bloom. It fell like warm snow. It caught in her hair. It settled on her shoulders and she found she was laughing, or crying, or both, and could not have said where one ended.

The grey river ran clear.

That was the thing that undid her most, somehow — more than the blossoms, more than the light. She crawled to the bank on hands that shook and looked down into water that had been the color of old dishwater since the day they'd arrived, and saw *sky* in it. Real sky, reflected clean, and beneath the reflection the sudden riot of life returning: silver fish, small and startled-looking, as if they too had been asleep and were waking unsure whether the world would still be here. Reeds uncurled along the bank. A dragonfly — the first living thing she'd seen in this place that wasn't corrupted or mad — lifted off a stone and stitched a bright line across the current.

"It worked," she whispered. "It actually — we *did* it."

Beside her, Wendell said nothing.

She turned, and the joy went cold in her chest like tea left out in winter.

He was watching the garden with an expression she couldn't read — wonder, yes, but underneath it something else, something quieter and far more frightening. And as she watched, a petal drifted down and landed on his hand, and she saw that it did not rest there. It *sank*. Slowly, gently, the way snow sinks into deep drifts, passing through his skin as if it were remembering something it had once been part of.

"Wendell," she said again, and now her voice had an edge to it, fear sharpening the word. "What's happening?"

He looked at her then, and smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it was gentle. Because it was already saying goodbye.

"I think," he said, "we should find Briarlock."

* * *

They found the fox-headed herald at the edge of the first waking orchard, standing very still among the trees, his brush-tipped ears forward, his amber eyes wide in a way Hazel had never seen on him. Briarlock, who had waited a hundred years with the patience of stone, who had guided them through the withered country with dry wit and iron certainty — Briarlock was trembling.

"You did it," the herald said. His voice caught on the words like cloth on a nail. "You actually — after a century, after everything, you *did* it." He dropped to one knee there among the new grass, and pressed his long-fingered hand flat against the ground, and Hazel watched the green spread outward from beneath his palm in a slow ring, moss and clover and tiny white flowers chasing each other into being. "The covenant holds. I can feel it in the ground. All three oaths, renewed — bound not to a person but to—" He stopped. His ears flicked back. When he raised his eyes to Wendell, the reverence in them had curdled into something very like grief.

"Briarlock," Hazel said. "Tell me what's wrong."

"Nothing is wrong." The fox's voice was careful, the way a surgeon's hands are careful. "That is what frightens me. Everything is *right*, mistress Coldharbour. The garden is whole. The realms are knit. And the seed—" He rose, slowly, and looked at Wendell the way one looks at a candle burning down to its final inch: beautiful, and brief. "The seed has done what seeds do."

"I don't understand," Hazel said, and hated how young her voice sounded.

"The World-Tree spent ten thousand years making one last seed." Briarlock walked a slow circle around Wendell as he spoke, and Hazel noticed — really noticed, for the first time — that the grass greened where Wendell stood, that the blossoms leaned toward him like faces toward firelight. "It hid that seed in the mortal world, wrapped in flesh, raised as a man, so that the Gardener could never plant it by force. A seed planted by force grows crooked. But a seed given freely—" He spread his hands. "You gave your names to each other. The covenant took hold of the bond. And the bond needed *root*. Something had to anchor it. Something living. Something—" his muzzle twitched, apologetic, "—something with a tree sleeping inside it."

"No," Hazel said.

"The seed has sprouted," Briarlock said simply. "And when a seed sprouts, Hazel Coldharbour, it does not remain a seed. It becomes the thing it was always going to be. The man was the husk. The husk is being spent."

The word *spent* went through her like a blade through wet paper.

She grabbed Wendell's arm — to shake him, to steady herself, she wasn't sure which — and under her fingers his sleeve felt *thin*. Not torn. Thin, the way a voice goes thin at the end of a long speech. She pushed the sleeve up and gasped. His forearm, which two days ago had been ordinary flesh, faintly freckled from a mapmaker's indoor life, was shot through with veins of pale gold, branching like the limbs of a tree seen against winter sky. Where the patterns converged, his skin had gone translucent, and beneath it she could see — she was almost certain she could see — a faint green glow, pulsing slow and patient, keeping time with nothing human.

"That's not—" Her voice broke. She tried again. "We fixed it. We fixed *everything*. The exchange worked, nobody had to be erased, that was the whole *point*—"

"And nobody was," Wendell said quietly. "Look at me, Hazel. Look at my face. Am I erased?"

He wasn't. That was the horror of it. He was entirely, achingly himself — the same crooked smile, the same ink stain still on his left thumb from the archive, the same warmth in his eyes that had made her trust him over a pot of tea before either of them knew what they were walking into. Nothing had been taken. Everything was simply... thinning. Like a photograph left too long in sunlight. Like a song fading at the end of a long corridor.

"How long?" she asked Briarlock, because someone had to ask, because the question was a wound that would only fester unopened.

The herald's ears flattened. "I do not know. Days, perhaps. A season, if the garden is generous. The seed will grow fastest where the magic is strongest." He hesitated, then added, softer: "Here. Here it will be fastest of all."

* * *

They walked back through the garden because Wendell asked to see it, and Hazel could refuse him nothing now, and would refuse him nothing ever again, and both of those facts sat in her chest like stones.

It was unrecognizable. The Sundered Garden — the name felt wrong already, a relic, like calling a healed limb by the shape of its break — had come alive in the hours since the covenant took hold, and it was coming alive *loudly*. Meadows they had crossed in grey silence now hummed with insects whose names Hazel didn't know, creatures that must have slept dormant in the dead soil for a century, waiting. Streams that had been dry channels ran with water so clear it seemed lit from within. And everywhere, everywhere, the Hollowed were waking.

They came upon the first of them in a petrified apple orchard — except it wasn't petrified anymore, it was heavy with fruit, branches bowed under apples so red they looked painted. The wraith stood among the trees, and it was less than it had been: the tattered shroud-shape of it was unraveling, dissolving from the edges inward, and beneath the unraveling there was *skin*. Human skin. Grey and starved and ancient, but skin, over hands, over a face.

The Hollowed turned as they approached. Its eyes — a person's eyes, hollowed and haunted but a *person's* — fixed on the apples above it, and its mouth opened, and what came out was not the keening shriek they had fled from in the orchard ambush. What came out was a word.

"...hungry," it rasped. "I am — I am so *hungry* — how long — how long has it—"

"A hundred years," Hazel said gently. She stepped forward, past Wendell's warning hand, and took one of the apples down and put it in the creature's shaking hands. "A hundred years, I think. But it's over now. Eat."

The former Hollowed — the former *man* — bit into the apple and wept. Juice ran down his chin and he laughed and sobbed in the same breath, and around him the last of the wraith-stuff burned away in motes of light, and he was only a man, gaunt and ragged and gloriously, ordinarily alive.

"They'll all wake," Hazel murmured. "All of them. They just — they need time. And food. And—"

"Someone to teach them," said a voice behind her, dry as autumn leaves, and she turned to see Mother Yew leaning on her staff at the orchard's edge, watching the weeping man with an expression Hazel had never seen on her mentor's face. Tenderness, perhaps. Or its raw, unpracticed draft. "A century of corruption doesn't wash out in an afternoon, child. They'll wake confused, and frightened, and full of power they don't understand. They'll need—" the old woman's eyes flicked up, met Hazel's, held, "—a teacher. Someone who knows what it is to work magic alone and be warped by it. Someone who knows the way back."

"You're volunteering," Hazel said.

"I'm *retiring*," Mother Yew corrected, with a ghost of her old imperiousness. "There is a difference. Retirement means choosing your work instead of being chained to it." She looked out over the blooming orchard, and her voice dropped. "I have been chained for a hundred years, Hazel. Let me spend what's left of me freely. It is," and here her gaze slid, briefly, terribly, to Wendell, "the whole of the law."

Hazel's throat closed. She nodded, because she could not speak.

* * *

Evening came to the garden for the first time in a century, and it came beautifully — a real sunset, amber and rose, pouring through the new leaves and laying long shadows across grass that had never known shade. They made camp in the lee of the Heartwood Gate, because Wendell wanted to sleep near it, and none of them said aloud why that might matter.

Briarlock kept watch. Mother Yew sat apart, murmuring to the ground in the old tongue, coaxing a circle of night-blooming flowers open around their campfire as if planting a ward. And Hazel sat with Wendell against the Gate's vast root, his head on her shoulder, and watched the firelight move across his face and catalogued everything about it the way a drowning woman catalogs breath.

The gold in his arms had spread to his collarbones. When he turned his head, she could see the branching patterns at his neck, delicate as frost on glass. His pulse, when she touched his wrist, was slower than it should be. Slower, and deeper, and she realized with a lurch that it was keeping time with the green glow beneath his skin — that his heart and the seed were learning to beat together, and that only one of them could win.

"Tell me what it feels like," she said quietly.

He was silent a long moment. "Warm," he said at last. "Like — you know when you've been cold all day, and you get inside, and the warmth starts at your center and works outward? It's like that. All the time. Everything in me is becoming warm and green and growing, and it doesn't hurt, Hazel, that's the strange thing. It feels like *coming home*." He laughed softly, without much humor. "I spent thirty years not knowing what I was. Now I know, and knowing feels like sinking into a feather bed. How is that fair? I should be terrified."

"Are you?"

"Yes." He said it simply, without drama. "But not of dying. Of — forgetting. The Gardener gave everything alone and became a monster. Mother Yew held her duty alone for a century and went mad. I keep thinking — if I fade, if the seed takes me and I become the tree, will any of me stay? Will I remember the teahouse? Will I remember you?" His hand found hers in the dark. "Because that's the version I can't bear. Not ending. *Forgetting.*"

"You won't forget," she said, and heard the desperation in her own voice, the naked refusal. "The exchange — our names are in each other, that's the whole design of it, you're anchored to me as much as to the garden—"

"Hazel."

"Don't." She pulled her hand free and pressed it over her eyes. "Don't say it kindly. Everyone keeps saying things kindly to me today. Briarlock says it kindly, Mother Yew looks at you kindly, and I want someone to say it *ugly*, Wendell, I want someone to stand up and shout that it isn't fair, that we just saved two worlds and the price is *you*, that I finally — after seven years — I finally let someone in, and the universe is charging me for it—"

"Hazel." He took her wrist, gently, and drew her hand from her face. In the firelight his eyes were bright and wet and utterly calm. "Say it ugly, then. I'll listen."

And that undid her completely. She said it ugly. She said it into his shoulder, into his hair, her voice cracking and graceless, all the things she'd never learned to say because her childhood mentor had taught her that feeling was a leak in the hull: *it isn't fair, I love you, I love you, we had one date, one stupid lovely date, we haven't even had a year, I won't watch you turn into a tree, I can't, I did everything right this time, I gave everything freely and properly and it's still taking you—*

He held her through all of it. When she finally ran out, hoarse and emptied, he kissed the crown of her head and said, "There. Wasn't that better than kind?"

She laughed despite herself, a wet broken sound. "You're insufferable."

"I'm a mapmaker. We're trained to observe terrain honestly." He tilted her chin up, and his expression shifted — the calm settling into something firmer, something decided, and she recognized it with a fresh spike of dread because she had seen that exact look in her own mirror. The look of a person building a plan they haven't told anyone yet. "Hazel. Whatever happens next — however long I have — I want to spend it well. Not in fear. Not in bargaining. I've watched what holding-on-alone does to people in this garden. I'd rather learn what letting go *together* does."

"We'll find another way," she said. "There's always another way. I refused to believe that for seven years and I was wrong, and now I refuse to stop believing it, and maybe that's wrong too, but—"

"My love." The endearment landed like a bell struck in an empty room. Neither of them had used it before. "Let tomorrow be tomorrow tonight."

Across the fire, Mother Yew rose stiffly and walked to the edge of the camp, and stood looking north, toward the deep heart of the garden, where the World-Tree's fallen branch lay — the branch Hazel had glimpsed once, vast as a cathedral beam, half-buried in flowering earth. The old woman stood there a long time. Then she turned, and her ancient face was unreadable, and she said:

"Child. Come look at this. And bring Wendell."

Hazel got up, her stomach tightening. "What is it?"

Mother Yew's eyes moved from Hazel to Wendell and back, and when she spoke, her voice was low and strange, carrying something Hazel couldn't name — not grief, not hope, but the trembling ground between them.

"The branch," she said. "It's putting out buds."
