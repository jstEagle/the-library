## 33. The Last Seed

I woke on the third morning after the end of the world, and the first thing I noticed was that my hands were dirty.

Not garden-dirty, not the honest brown of turned soil. *Old* dirt — the kind that gets into the creases of knuckles and stays there, the kind a man accumulates over years of work. I stared at them in the grey-green light filtering through the Heartwood's new canopy and understood, with the strange calm that had settled over me since the exchange, that this was what mortality felt like from the inside. Not pain. Not fear. Just hands that would get dirty every day for as long as I had days, and would one day be still.

The seed was spent.

I had known it before I knew it, the way you know weather is coming by the ache in old bones — except mine weren't old bones yet, and that was rather the point. Somewhere beneath my sternum, where for thirty years something green and patient had slept without my knowledge, there was now only quiet. A room after the furniture has been carried out. I pressed my palm flat against my chest and felt my heart beat, ordinary and mortal and entirely mine, and I found — to my considerable surprise — that I could breathe around it.

"Wendell."

Hazel stood at the edge of the clearing, backlit by a sun that hadn't risen so warmly over this place in a hundred years. The Sundered Garden had spent two days remembering itself: orchards unpetrifying with sounds like ice cracking off a pond, the grey river running silver-bright and loud, grass coming up between flagstones in green fire. She looked like someone standing at the edge of warm water she couldn't decide to enter.

"You're awake," she said. "You've been asleep since—"

"Since the gate." I sat up. Around me, where I'd lain, the ground was a riot of flowers — not the slow, seasonal bloom of an ordinary meadow but a sudden eruption, poppies and evening primrose and things I had no names for, all opened at once, all facing me like small sunflowers finding the sun. Even now, even *spent*, the garden leaned toward me. "How long?"

"Two days. Briarlock wanted to fetch Mother Yew. I wouldn't let her wake you." Hazel came closer, stopping just beyond the ring of flowers, as if they were a fence. Maybe they were. "Wendell, we need to talk about what you are now."

"What I am *now*," I repeated. It wasn't quite a question. We both knew what I had been: the last seed of the World-Tree, raised human in a city of canals and wardens, hidden so well that I had hidden from myself. And we both knew what the exchange had cost. A name given in love, returned in love, rooted in love — the covenant had taken hold of our bond instead of either of us, exactly as Briarlock had promised it might. But bonds need anchoring. Something had to hold the covenant to the earth.

And the garden had chosen its keeper.

"I dreamed about Vell," I said. "Last night. Or I think it was last night. Time moves strangely here when you're sleeping in it." I looked down at my dirty hands again. "I dreamed about the archive. Mrs. Pettibone scolding me for misfiling the tax rolls. The smell of the canal in summer. My little flat above the chandler's with the window that stuck." I smiled, and found the smile was real, which frightened me more than grief would have. "It's all still there. But it isn't *mine* anymore. Do you understand? I reached for it the way you reach for a dream, and it receded. Politely. Like a shopkeeper closing up for the day."

Hazel crouched down across the flower-ring from me. Up close I could see she hadn't slept much; there were shadows under her eyes deep enough to plant things in. Seven years of careful living had taught her face to be unreadable, but I had learned her better than that. Underneath the composure she was doing arithmetic, and I already knew the sum.

"The hedge," she said. "Briarlock showed me. There's a way through now — a proper door, near where we fell in. I can come through. I can visit."

"Yes," I said gently. "*Visit.*"

"And how often? How long can I stay?"

"Hazel—"

"How long, Wendell?"

I told her the truth, because she had earned it and because lying to her had never once worked. "A day. Perhaps two, if the moon is dark and the borderland is drowsy. You're bound to Vell now — truly bound, not just by habit. The covenant runs through you. If you stay past your welcome, the garden will start pulling at you the way it pulled at the Hollowed. It won't mean to. Gardens don't mean anything. They just grow toward water." I spread my hands. "And I'm the deepest well it has."

She was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere behind us, in the recovering orchards, birdsong started up — actual birdsong, tentative and rusty, voices remembering themselves. A hundred years of silence breaking like a fever.

"So that's the bargain," she said finally. "You stay here. I go home. We get—" Her voice caught, steadied. "We get days. Scattered ones. Equinoxes and dark moons and whatever else the hedge allows."

"That's the shape of it."

"It's a terrible shape."

"It's a shape," I said. "I've read enough maps to know that most borders are terrible shapes. That doesn't stop anyone from living on both sides of them."

* * *

Briarlock found us an hour later, trotting out of the birch-gate with her brush-tail high and her fox-face wearing the particular expression of a herald who has delivered bad news before and has opinions about how people take it. She dropped a bundle at my feet — my coat, my satchel, my good drafting pen, all retrieved from the ruins of the teahouse in Vell, which apparently still existed on the other side of everything.

"Your effects," she said. "Also, the Wardens are asking questions in Vell. A teahouse emptied itself of patrons mid-evening and hasn't refilled. The proprietor swears the whole room went soft at the edges, like wax. They're calling it a Warden experiment gone wrong, which is convenient for everyone except the Wardens." Her whiskers twitched. "Your disappearance is being handled. Hers will be too, once she goes back."

"Once I go back," Hazel said flatly.

"The covenant requires an anchor in each realm." Briarlock said it kindly, which somehow made it worse. "That was always the arithmetic, child. Three oaths, three keepers, three tethers. Your bond holds the middle. He holds the garden. You hold the mortal side. Pull either anchor loose and the rope goes slack, and we have all seen what happens when the rope goes slack." She nodded toward the far hills, where the Hollowed were even now waking in the orchard rows — blinking, weeping, clutching stolen fruit they no longer remembered stealing. Some of them had been human once. All of them had been hungry.

"I'm not complaining," Hazel said, in the tone of someone complaining.

"No," Briarlock agreed. "But you're going to want to, and I thought I'd get ahead of it. Herald's privilege." She turned to me then, and her manner changed — the briskness dropping away like a cloak, leaving something older and more careful underneath. "Walk with me, seedling. There are things a keeper should see before he agrees to be one. Properly agrees. With open eyes."

"He's already agreed," Hazel said.

"He's agreed to nothing yet," Briarlock said. "He's agreed to *wanting* to. Those are different animals, and I have been eaten by both."

* * *

She took me first to the river.

The grey river ran clear now, quick and cold over stones the color of rain-clouds, and along its banks grew reeds that whispered in a language almost-but-not-quite words. Briarlock stopped at a bend where the water pooled deep and dark, and gestured for me to look.

"Do you know what this was?" she asked.

"A river."

"A wound. When the covenant broke, the border between realms tore along its length, and everything the tear touched drained of meaning. The water forgot how to be wet. The fish forgot how to be fish. For a hundred years this pool has been a hole shaped like a pool." She crouched and dipped one paw in, and the reflection that came back was hers — sharp-eyed, fox-faced, ancient. "Two days ago it remembered. Because of you. Not because you commanded it. Because you *are* what it drains toward. Every broken thing in this garden flows to you now, Wendell Ash. Every hollow place. Every forgetting thing. Can you feel it?"

I could. I had been feeling it since I woke and calling it something else — a hum under the skin, a pull at the edges of my attention, like standing in a doorway with wind at your back. I closed my eyes and let myself notice it properly, and the sensation resolved into something enormous and patient: the garden's whole wounded weight, leaning against me the way a tired traveler leans against a doorpost.

"It doesn't hurt," I said, surprised.

"It won't. That's the mercy and the trap of it. Being needed feels wonderful right up until you cannot put it down." Briarlock's ears flattened. "The Gardener was the first keeper. Did you know that? Before he was the Gardener he was a man who loved this place, and the place loved him back, and neither of them knew where one ended and the other began. By the end, he couldn't tell his own thoughts from the garden's weather. That is what 'keeper' means here, if you do it carelessly."

"And if I do it carefully?"

"Then you will be a man who lives in a garden, and tends it, and goes to sleep at night still himself." She looked at me sidelong. "You strike me as a careful man. You filed things correctly for a living. There are worse preparations for eternity."

"It's not eternity," I said. "That's the other thing. I'm mortal now. The seed is spent. Whatever I am, I'll be it for one human lifetime, and then the garden will need a new keeper, and I'll be a branch on the World-Tree or a patch of particularly opinionated moss."

"Yes." Briarlock smiled, and it was a strange sight — tenderness and mischief sharing one muzzle. "You will die here, seedling. In time. Among growing things, with work left undone for someone else to finish, which is the only way anyone should ever die. Most people spend their whole lives fleeing that arrangement. You're volunteering for it. I find I respect that more than I can say."

* * *

She took me next to the cottage.

It hadn't existed yesterday. I was nearly sure of that. It stood in a fold of hillside above the orchards, small and stone-walled and smoke-stained as though it had been there three hundred years, with a crooked chimney and a door painted the exact faded blue of Hazel's charm-stall awning, which was either coincidence or the garden eavesdropping, and I had long since stopped betting against the garden.

"This is yours," Briarlock said. "If you want it. Keepers before you lived in grander places — the Gardener kept a palace, once, all white columns and order. Look how that ended. I'd suggest something smaller. Something with a leaky roof you actually have to fix."

Inside, it smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs, and there was a table with two chairs, and a window looking west toward the hedge-door, and on the windowsill someone — the garden, obviously, the garden with its terrible sentimentality — had set a single jar of wildflowers.

I stood in the middle of the room with my dirty mortal hands hanging at my sides and let the choice arrive. Not the dramatic choice, the gate-and-sacrifice choice; that was done, paid for, sealed in light. This was the quieter choice, the one that came after, the one nobody writes prophecies about: whether to grieve what I had lost or turn toward what remained. Whether to spend my one lifetime counting the doors that had closed or sweeping the floor of the one that had opened.

I thought about my flat above the chandler's. The sticking window. The canal in summer. I thought about Mrs. Pettibone, who would mark me absent forever and eventually stop marking me at all. I thought about a city full of people who had never known what held their world together, holding it together anyway, the way Hazel had held her little life together for seven years with charms and caution and a loneliness she wore like a coat.

And I thought about Hazel's face when she'd said *we get days*, trying to make it sound like a sentence being handed down when really it was a sentence being offered — the way she'd offered it, arms open, eyes open, asking me to refuse if refusing was truer.

"Briarlock," I said. "When Hazel comes through the hedge. Where does she arrive?"

"The door opens where the door opens. Near the birches, usually. Sometimes the orchard, if she's thinking hard about apples." The fox tilted her head. "Why?"

"Because I intend to be there when she comes through. Every time. However many times there are." I picked up the jar of wildflowers and set it in the window where the western light would find it. "I'll be the keeper. I'll tend the wounds and wake the Hollowed and learn the river's name and fix the roof. And when the hedge opens and a witch steps through smelling of city rain, I will be standing at that door, and I will not waste one hour of any day she brings me." I laughed — actually laughed, at the absurdity and the rightness of it. "Seven years she spent being careful. I spent thirty years not knowing what I was. Neither of us is going to be careless with the time we've bought. That's the whole of my terms, herald. Write them down."

"Prophecies write themselves," Briarlock said dryly. "Believe me. I've read the book."

* * *

Hazel was waiting at the birch-gate when I came down the hill, which meant she'd been waiting some time, which meant she'd made her own decision while I was making mine. She stood with her satchel packed and her chin up and her hands very still, the posture of a woman who has rehearsed a goodbye in her head until it stopped sounding like dying.

"I leave tomorrow at dusk," she said. "Mother Yew says the hedge is strongest then, and weak hedges make for splinters." She swallowed. "Vell needs its anchor. And the stall — I have charms to sell, apparently, legally now, which is going to take some getting used to. And someone should watch the fraying heal from that side. Make sure it takes."

"All true," I said.

"Don't be reasonable at me, Wendell Ash. I've had two days to be reasonable and I hate it."

"I'm not being reasonable." I crossed the space between us and took her hands — dirty, both of us, mortal, both of us, and I intended to say something eloquent about that but what came out was simpler. "I'm staying. Here. As keeper. With open eyes, as the herald insists. And you're going home, with open eyes. And the hedge opens at dusk, and equinoxes, and dark moons, and whenever the garden misses you — which, fair warning, judging by the flowers, will be embarrassingly often."

Her breath went out of her slowly, like a knot untying. "You're sure."

"I've never been sure of anything in my life. I wasn't even sure I was human until a week ago." I smiled. "But I'm sure of this. The garden chose its well, Hazel. It chose badly if it thinks I'll stand here grieving. I'm going to stand here *growing*. And when you come through — and you will come through, because you are constitutionally incapable of leaving a good thing alone — I'll be at this door."

Behind her, at the edge of the birches, the air shimmered — the hedge-door, learning its hinge, showing a sliver of grey Vell sky and the distant smudge of canal-side rooftops. Home, on the other side of everything. And somewhere in that city, a mapmaker's desk stood empty, and a prophecy book waited in the archive with its ink still moving, still writing, still curious about what two names intertwined might become.

Hazel followed my gaze to the shimmering gap, then looked back at me, and did the bravest thing I ever saw her do: she smiled first.

"Dusk tomorrow, then," she said. "Don't be late, keeper. I hear the garden grows fast around you."

It was already growing. I could feel it under my feet, patient and vast and leaning close, and for the first time in thirty years, I knew exactly what I was.

One human lifetime. One garden. One door that opened at dusk.

I meant to make them all count.
