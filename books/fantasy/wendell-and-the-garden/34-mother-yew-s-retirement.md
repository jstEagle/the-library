## 34. Mother Yew's Retirement

The garden had a new sound in the mornings, and it took Hazel three days to name it.

Birdsong. Not the grey, apologetic chirring of the sparrows that haunted Vell's gutters, but full-throated, quarrelsome, *numerous* birdsong, rolling down from the orchards where the petrified trees had shed their stone like old scar tissue. The Sundered Garden was waking the way a sleeper wakes — slowly, with groans and stretches, one limb at a time — and every morning brought some small resurrection that made Briarlock preen and Wendell go quiet in the particular way he went quiet when something beautiful frightened him.

Hazel noticed the sound because she was awake before it.

She had taken to rising in the blue hour, when the river ran clear as glass and the mist came off the water in slow ribbons, and walking the path along the bank with her hands in her pockets and no purpose at all. It was an extravagance, idleness. She had not permitted herself idleness in seven years. In Vell, stillness was how Wardens found you; you kept moving, kept selling, kept your head down and your hands busy. Here, in this place between places, there was nothing to hide from, and she was discovering — with the awkwardness of a woman learning to walk again after a long illness — what her own thoughts sounded like when they weren't crowded by survival.

Mostly they sounded like Mother Yew.

She found the old woman at the well, of course. Mother Yew had always been at the well. In the cottage of Hazel's childhood apprenticeship there had been a stone basin where rainwater gathered, and Mother Yew would stand over it at dawn with her sleeves rolled, watching the reflections sort themselves out, and young Hazel had thought it was madness or laziness or both. Now she understood it was neither. It was listening. Water remembered everything it touched, and Mother Yew had spent a hundred years learning to hear it.

Except that wasn't right either, Hazel corrected herself, stopping at the edge of the clearing. A hundred years was what the garden had taken. What Mother Yew had actually lived was longer than that, tangled through it, half-mad for most of it, bound to the covenant's failing machinery by a leash she had disguised as love. The confession at the Heartwood Gate was still fresh enough to bruise when Hazel pressed on it. *I did not take your blame nobly, child. I took it so you could never leave me.* And then, worse, truer: *And then you left me anyway, and the leash went slack, and I could not tell which end of it I was.*

But the woman standing at the garden's well now was not the madwoman from the Hollowed's cage. That creature had been all angles and muttering, eyes like chips of flint, hands that shook unless they were working. This woman stood straight. Her hair — white now, truly white, though whether from age or from the covenant's breaking Hazel couldn't say — was braided back in the practical crown she'd worn every day of Hazel's childhood. Her sleeves were rolled. Her shadow lay long and whole on the grass beside her, and Hazel still caught herself checking shadows these days, hers and everyone's, the way a sailor checks the sky.

"You're hovering," Mother Yew said, without turning. "You hover exactly as you did at nine years old. I could hear you thinking from across the river."

"I was being quiet."

"You were being *loud*, quietly. There's a difference." She turned then, and her face was the same and utterly changed: the same sharp planes, the same mouth that had delivered ten thousand corrections, but the fury that had lived behind her eyes for as long as Hazel had known her — the fury of a woman holding up a falling sky alone — had gone out of them. What remained was tiredness, and under the tiredness, something Hazel had only ever glimpsed in fragments: warmth. Banked, careful, rationed like everything else in her mentor's life, but real.

"The Hollowed woke again last night," Mother Yew said. "Three more. The ones from the drowned chapel."

"I know. Wendell sat with them until dawn." Hazel came closer, stepping over the moss that had crept across the flagstones since the exchange of names. Green things grew faster here now, greedily almost, as if the garden were making up for a century of famine in a single season. "He has a way with them. They trust him. I suppose they'd trust the seed."

"They don't trust the seed," Mother Yew said. "They trust the man who sat in the mud with them while they remembered their names." She said it dryly, but Hazel heard the approval underneath, and it landed somewhere in her chest that approval from Mother Yew had always landed — like a coal dropped into a cold stove.

* * *

The waking of the Hollowed was the strangest mercy of the aftermath, and also the hardest work.

There had been perhaps forty of them, once — men and women who had come to the dying garden across a century, drawn by whispers of power the way moths are drawn to a candle that is already guttering. They had drawn on the corrupted magic because it was there, because hunger doesn't ask permission, and the garden had hollowed them in return, wearing their shapes and eating their names until nothing remained but wraiths that stole fruit and fled the light. When the covenant was reforged — when two names given in love flooded the borderland with living green — the hollowing had begun to reverse. Slowly. Painfully. Like frost leaving ground.

Waking was not gentle. The Hollowed came back to themselves in pieces, and the pieces did not always fit. Some wept for days. Some raged. One man, a ferryman who had been lost so long his village on the far side had built him a grave, simply sat by the grey river — the clear river now, Hazel reminded herself, the river ran *clear* — and asked over and over whether his daughter would remember his face, and no one could answer him, because a century is a long time and daughters grow old.

Briarlock had organized them shelter in the petrified orchards, which were petrified no longer but still bore strange fruit, and had appointed himself their herald and defender with the punctilious ferocity of a fox guarding an enormous henhouse of broken people. But shelter was not healing, and the waking needed more than food and fire.

It needed teaching. And there was exactly one person in either realm who had spent a lifetime teaching hedge-craft to stubborn students, and who owed the garden a debt measured in decades.

Hazel had watched Mother Yew arrive at the decision the way she'd once watched her decide weather. No announcement. No ceremony. Just the old woman walking out among the newly-woken one morning, planting herself in front of the ferryman who mourned his daughter, and saying, in the voice that had cowed generations of apprentices:

"Your hands remember boatwork even if your head doesn't. Show me."

And the ferryman, blinking through tears, had shown her. And by evening he was mending a net someone had woven him, and his hands had stopped shaking, and Mother Yew had stood over him with her arms crossed and said, "Sloppy knots. We'll fix that tomorrow," and the *tomorrow* had done more healing than any charm Hazel knew.

By the end of the first week she had eleven students. By the second week, fourteen. She taught them the way she had taught Hazel: ruthlessly, precisely, with an undercurrent of fierce tenderness that she would have denied under torture. She taught them to ground themselves in their own names, spoken aloud each morning, claimed like territory. She taught them to draw magic the slow way, the honest way, through patience and reciprocity instead of theft. She taught them that power taken from a dying thing makes you part of its dying, and power given freely by a living thing makes you part of its life, and the difference between those two things was the entire difference between the Hollowed and everyone else.

Hazel watched from a distance, mostly. She told herself it was respect for the old woman's process. She knew it was fear.

* * *

On the ninth morning, Mother Yew ambushed her.

Hazel came down to the well with her arms full of cut willow — Wendell wanted to try weaving a gate-frame for the cottage-to-be, and willow was generous material — and found her mentor waiting on the bench beside the water, hands folded, expression arranged into terrible patience.

"Sit down, Hazel."

Her name, unadorned. No *child*, no *girl*, no *apprentice*. Hazel sat.

"You've been circling me for nine days," Mother Yew said. "Like a cat deciding whether the dog is asleep. Ask what you came to ask."

Hazel set down the willow. "Are you staying?"

"Yes."

"Just — staying? Here? Forever?"

"Forever is a strong word for a woman my age." Mother Yew looked out over the water, where the morning mist was burning off and the far orchards showed green to their crowns. "I was bound to this place for a hundred years, girl. You'd think I'd run for the hedge the moment the chains came off. Part of me wants to. Part of me walked to the boundary three nights ago and stood there smelling Vell on the wind — bread, and canal water, and horse dung, and all the rest of it — and wept like a fool."

"But?"

"But I looked at those people." She nodded toward the orchards, where the faint sounds of her morning lesson carried — the ferryman's voice reciting a grounding litany, hesitant but steady. "Forty souls who gave a century to this place and got hollowed for their trouble. Forty people who know exactly what it is to be eaten by magic and have to learn to live inside their own skins again. And I am the only teacher within three realms who understands that lesson from the inside." Her jaw tightened. "I did not survive a hundred years of duty to abandon the ones who suffered it alongside me. That is not sacrifice. That is arithmetic."

"It's more than arithmetic," Hazel said quietly.

Mother Yew's mouth twitched — the ghost of a smile, quickly disciplined. "Everything is more than arithmetic. That's the trouble with the world." She turned and looked at Hazel directly, and Hazel braced herself, because direct regard from Mother Yew had always preceded judgment. "I'm staying. And I want to say the rest of it plainly, because I have spent too much of my life saying things sideways and calling it wisdom. I owe you an accounting, child—"

"Hazel."

"—*Hazel*," she amended, and the correction itself was a kind of bow. "I owe you an accounting, and I intend to give it, and then I intend for us to be finished with accounts forever."

* * *

They walked. Of course they walked; Mother Yew had never been able to talk sitting down, claiming that the body's business helped the mind's. They walked the river path past the drowned chapel's tower, which rose from the clear water now like a memory of drowning rather than drowning itself, its bell silent and its choir finally at rest. They walked through the young orchard where the first true fruit since the covenant's renewal was swelling green-gold on branches that had been stone within living memory.

"I told you at the gate," Mother Yew said, "that I took your blame to bind you to me. Let me tell you the whole shape of it, because you deserve the whole shape. When you broke the law to save that child — and it was a good breaking, the best kind, the kind I should have taught you to make — I saw two futures. In one, the Wardens took you, and I lost you to their cells and their quiet executions. In the other, I took the crime onto myself, and you lived, and you were mine. Bound by guilt. Bound by gratitude. Bound so tight you could never fly." She stopped walking. "I chose the second future because I loved you selfishly. That is the confession. Not noble. Selfish. A leash disguised as a gift."

"I know," Hazel said. "You told me."

"And then you flew anyway." Something moved through the old woman's face — grief and pride wrestling to a draw. "Seven years, and not a word. Do you know what that did to me, in that garden, half-mad, holding a failing covenant together with my bare hands? It snapped something. I told myself you were dead. Then I told myself you were a coward. Then I told myself I had deserved it. All three lies, worn smooth as river stones from handling." She resumed walking. "And then you walked back into my life grown, and skilled — self-taught, warped in places, dangerous in others, but *skilled* — and I slapped you, because if I had embraced you I would have wept, and I could not wept in front of the Hollowed. Weakness feeds corruption. Old rule. Wrong rule, probably. Most of my rules were."

They came to the place where the World-Tree's fallen branch lay — the great silver-limbed bough from which Wendell's cottage was slowly growing, fed by his hands and the garden's restored patience. Mother Yew laid her palm against the bark, and Hazel saw the branch lean, very slightly, into the touch.

"I am not asking your forgiveness again," Mother Yew said. "You gave it at the gate, freely, and I have decided to believe you meant it, because the alternative is to keep apologizing until one of us dies, and I refuse. What I am telling you is what comes next." She straightened, and the old authority came back into her spine like a blade returning to its sheath. "I am going to teach these people. All of them. The Hollowed, and any others the garden gathers — strays, runaways, the magically clumsy, the heartbroken. There will always be such people. Both realms are full of them, and both realms have always failed them, and I have a hundred years of penance and a lifetime of skill and nowhere better to spend either. This garden will be a school. Perhaps, in time, a door — a lawful crossing, once your city remembers what magic is for. Someone must teach the ones who come through. I am volunteering. Permanently."

Hazel looked at her — really looked — and saw it clearly for the first time: not the titan of her childhood, not the madwoman in the cage, not the penitent at the gate. A woman. Old, and tired, and fierce, and choosing her work with open eyes the way Hazel had once chosen flight and later chosen sacrifice and finally chosen love. The realization settled over her with an unexpected lightness.

"You're happy," Hazel said, wonderingly. "You're actually — this is you, choosing. Nobody's ever let you choose before, have they? The covenant chose you. Duty chose you. Even taking my blame was just another duty you weaponized."

Mother Yew was quiet a long moment. "No," she said at last. "No one has ever let me choose. I find I'm rather good at it." And then, before Hazel could respond to that astonishing sentence: "Which brings us to the matter of *you*."

"Me?"

"You are going home." It was not a question. "To Vell. To your stall, your landlady, your canals. The fraying is healed; the wards are coming down; the city will need charm-sellers who know what they're doing, and soon it will need teachers of its own. You belong there. Not because I say so — because you do. I watched you walk this garden for weeks. You love it. And you are always looking toward the hedge." Her eyes narrowed. "Also, your knotwork is atrocious and your grounding technique is self-taught rubbish, and I cannot abide the thought of you corrupting a generation of Vell children with it. So."

"So?"

"So before you go, you will spend one week here. As my student. Properly. We will unlearn the warping, mend the gaps, drill the fundamentals until your craft stands on honest ground. Not because you need finishing—" her mouth twitched again, "—though you do. Because I owe you the lessons I never gave you, and you owe yourself a teacher who does not lie to you. One week. Then we part."

"Part," Hazel repeated.

"As equals." Mother Yew said it steadily, but Hazel saw her hands, folded hard in her sleeves. "That is the word I have been circling for nine days, girl, same as you circled me. I have been your master, your judge, your jailer-by-proxy, your ghost. I will not be your conscience. Consciences are internal organs; grow your own. What I will be, if you'll have it — and I would understand entirely if you wouldn't — is your colleague. Your fellow practitioner. Two women who know the same craft, trading letters across a hedge, arguing about theory until we die." She lifted her chin. "Equals. It is not a word I have ever earned. I would like to earn it in the week remaining."

Hazel found, to her horror, that her eyes were burning. Seven years of calcified shame, a slap, a confession, a forgiveness at a gate — and it was this, this plain offer of ordinary collegiality, that undid her. She laughed, wetly, and wiped her face with her wrist like a child.

"One week," she said. "And you're fixing my knotwork first. The ferryman's knots are better than mine and he was a wraith a month ago."

"He was a *boatman* a month ago," Mother Yew said, already turning toward the orchards, brisk as spring. "Boatmen know rope. Come along, apprentice."

"Not apprentice."

"No," Mother Yew agreed, and paused, and glanced back with something that was almost, nearly, entirely a smile. "Colleague. Come along, colleague. Daylight's wasting, and I have forty students and one week to make a hedge-witch of you worthy of the name."

* * *

The week passed the way good weeks pass — too fast, and only visible in retrospect.

Hazel learned more in seven days than in seven years of solitary scraping. Not new magic — older magic. The fundamentals she'd skipped, the theory she'd mocked, the patient disciplines that made power flow *through* a witch rather than *out* of her. Mother Yeu corrected her grip on the willow wand she'd cut her first morning, corrected it four times, and on the fifth attempt something clicked in Hazel's wrist and a simple growth-charm bloomed through the cutting so cleanly that the willow whip put out roots in her hand.

"There," Mother Yew said, satisfied. "That's what honesty feels like. Remember the feeling. Everything you ever cast crooked, you cast because you were afraid of this feeling — of power moving through you without friction. Fear makes witches grab. Trust lets them open their hands."

At night they sat by the fire with the waking Hollowed and drank tea brewed from the garden's first mint, and Mother Yew told stories — real ones, unarmored — about her own apprenticeship a lifetime ago, about mistakes Hazel had never imagined the titan capable of making. And Hazel told stories too, about Vell, about the stall, about a blind date at a canal-side teahouse with a mapmaker who noticed things he shouldn't, and Mother Yew listened with her chin in her hand and said, at the end, "You chose well. Stubbornly, dangerously, against all sense. Well."

On the seventh evening, they stood together at the hedge — the ragged boundary where the mortal realm showed through like sky through leaves, smelling of rain and coal smoke and the distant bells of Vell.

Mother Yew held out her hand.

Not a blessing. Not a command. A hand, offered level, palm to palm, the grip of one practitioner greeting another across a workbench.

Hazel took it.

"Write to me," Mother Yew said gruffly. "Argue with me about theory. Send me your failures; I enjoy failures, they're instructive. And when the school grows — and it will grow, gardens and schools always do — send me your difficult students. The angry ones. The frightened ones. The ones who remind you of yourself." Her grip tightened, briefly. "I'm very good at the ones who remind me of myself."

"I know," Hazel said. "You had practice."

The old woman snorted — an actual, undignified snort — and released her hand, and stepped back into the green shade of the garden that had been her prison and was now, at last, her home.

Hazel turned toward the hedge. Toward the smoke-smell and the bell-sound and the city that had been her hiding place and was now, at last, her own.

Behind her, faintly, she heard Mother Yew call the evening lesson to order — forty voices answering, ragged but growing steadier, a litany of names claimed back from the dark.

Ahead of her, through the branches, she caught a glimpse of the path home — and, at the edge of it, a familiar figure waiting with a basket of seedlings and a smile she could feel from thirty feet away.

Wendell had come to walk her to the hedge. Of course he had.

She squared her shoulders, checked her shadow — whole, dark, hers — and went to meet him, colleague of the garden, witch of Vell, a woman with her own name in her own mouth and, at last, nowhere left to run from it.

Only toward.
