## 35. Homecoming

The road into Vell smelled different.

Hazel noticed it before she saw anything else — before the walls rose out of the morning mist, before the canal-gates swung open at the tollhouse, before the first face turned toward her on the cobbled way. For seven years the city had greeted her nose with tallow smoke and wet stone and the sour undertone of fear, that low animal reek every city gives off when its people are afraid of something they cannot name. Now there was something green threaded through it. Something growing. It came up through the cracks in the flagstones in thin blades of grass that no one had bothered to pull, and it hung from window-boxes where geraniums bloomed out of season, and it drifted down from the rooftops where moss spread across the slates like a slow, deliberate blessing.

The Sundered Garden's healing had not stayed behind the hedge. It had followed her home.

She walked through the gate without showing papers to anyone. The Warden who stood at the post — a young woman with a bored expression and a sapling growing out of a crack beside her boot — glanced at Hazel's travel-worn cloak and nodded her through as if she were any merchant's wife come back from the country. No questions about charms. No pat-down for talismans. The anti-magic wards that had once hummed over every archway in Vell, tasting the air for the wrongness of working, were gone. In their place, someone had hung wind-chimes made of bent iron nails and river glass, and they sang a small broken music as Hazel passed beneath them.

Her hands shook on the strap of her satchel.

It was absurd. She had faced the Hollowed in the orchards. She had knelt in a drowned chapel while a choir of the dead sang her mother's name back at her in voices like water over gravel. She had spoken Wendell's name into the Heartwood Gate with the Gardener's shears whistling past her ear, and she had felt the covenant take hold of them both like two hands closing around a single flame. Compared to all of that, walking into a city should have been nothing.

But she had left this city as a fugitive with a false name stitched into her collar, and she was coming back to it under her own.

*Hazel Coldharbour.* She said it silently, testing it, the way you test ice before putting your weight down. Seven years of being *Maren*, of being *the charm-girl*, of being *you there, keep moving*. Seven years of flinching when Wardens swept the market rows, of palming her luck-charms into sleeves, of selling hope out of a hatbox while pretending it was only pretty junk. The name fit strangely now, like a coat returned from storage — hers, but cut for a younger woman.

The canals ran forward again. That was the thing that nearly undid her, more than the grass or the chimes or the missing wards. In Wendell's vision, in those terrible minutes when he'd touched the garden's roots and seen what the fraying was doing to Vell, the canals had run backwards, carrying boats upstream against their own wakes, carrying drowned things up toward the light. Now the water moved the way water ought to, brown-green and slow and honest, and bargemen called to each other across it in the easy cadences of men whose world still obeyed itself. A child sat on the towpath dangling a string and a bent pin into the current, and when Hazel passed, the child looked up and smiled at her with the unguarded ease of someone who had never once been afraid of the water.

Hazel smiled back. Her eyes stung. She blamed the wind off the canal, which was lying, because there wasn't any wind.

* * *

The Gullwing Row market opened at eight bells, and Hazel arrived at seven, the way she always had, to claim her pitch before the fishwives did.

Except everything was arranged differently now. The old stalls — the ones that had huddled together in the shadow of the customs house, backs to the wall, everyone watching the alleys for grey cloaks — had been rebuilt facing outward, open to the light, awnings strung between poles like festival bunting. There were new pitches too, whole rows of them, and half the sellers were people Hazel didn't recognize: a broad-shouldered woman laying out bundles of dried herbs with the confident economy of a trained herbalist; a boy of perhaps fifteen arranging candles that burned with flames of six different colors and charging honest coin for them; an old man with a sign reading BONES CAST, FORTUNES TOLD, NO REFUNDS, laughing with his customers instead of glancing over his shoulder mid-sentence.

Magic, sold in daylight. Magic, haggled over. Magic, complained about, when a candle guttered wrong or a fortune came out vague. Ordinary. That was the miracle of it — not the wonder but the ordinariness, the sheer domestic fact of enchantment taking its place among the cheese wheels and the rope and the secondhand boots.

Hazel stood at the edge of the row with her satchel clutched to her chest and could not make her feet move.

"You're blocking the light, love," said a voice, and she turned to find the herb-woman regarding her with frank curiosity. "New? You've the look of someone coming home from a long way."

"Something like that." Hazel's voice came out rusty. "I used to sell here. Before."

"Before." The woman said the word the way everyone in Vell said it now — a shorthand for the years of fear, the fraying, the time when the canals ran strange and the wards bit and people forgot each other's faces in the streets. The time before the night the sky over the borderland had filled with green light, visible even from the city walls, and every ward in Vell had gone dark at once, gently, like lamps blown out by a kind hand. "Well then. You'll want a pitch. Talk to Mistress Ferrow at the tollhouse — she does the assignments now. And mind—" the woman leaned in, lowering her voice with the air of imparting scandal, "—she'll ask your name. Your true one. It's the custom since the mending. Everything sold here is sold under true names. Keeps the crooked honest, they say."

Hazel's throat tightened. "And if a person's been using a false one a long while?"

"Then I expect she's earned saying the right one out loud." The herb-woman smiled, gap-toothed and kind, and went back to her yarrow.

* * *

Mistress Ferrow turned out to be a compact, formidable person with ink-stained fingers and a ledger the size of a paving stone, and she looked Hazel up and down with the assessing eye of a woman who had spent thirty years pricing other people's goods.

"Pitch nineteen's open. Corner spot, good foot traffic, near the fountain." She licked her thumb and turned a page. "Name?"

Hazel set down her satchel. Straightened. Somewhere beyond the market roofline, far to the east, the hedge waited — the tear in the world that was no longer a wound but a door, tended now, kept now, watched over by a man who cast no shadow and slept in flowers. She would go back to it. She went back every week. But first—

"Hazel Coldharbour."

Ferrow's pen paused above the ledger. She looked up. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, or the shape of recognition, the story everyone in Vell knew by now whether or not they admitted it: the runaway apprentice, the prophecy book, the blind date at the teahouse that had ended the old world. Ballads were already being sung about it, badly, in taverns along the canal. Hazel had heard three verses through an inn window on her walk in and had wanted to crawl under the table.

"Coldharbour," Ferrow repeated. "Any relation to Mother Yew's Coldharbour? The hedge-witch who—"

"I was her apprentice." Hazel lifted her chin. "Seven years ago. I'm the one who ran."

A silence. Around them the market went on — coins, laughter, the colored candles smoking sweetly — but in the little bubble around the tollhouse desk, the silence held its breath.

Then Ferrow wrote the name in the ledger, in careful letters, and turned the book so Hazel could see it. *Pitch nineteen. Hazel Coldharbour.*

"There," Ferrow said briskly, as if she had settled some great matter. "Been waiting for that name to come back to my book a long while, though I didn't know it till just now. Nineteen's yours. Rent due at each quarter moon, payable in coin or in charms — we're flexible, these days." She almost smiled. Then, quieter, studying Hazel with those shrewd ink-stained eyes: "They say you gave your name to the covenant. That it took you clean out of the world, and the garden gave you back."

"It's complicated," Hazel said.

"They usually are." Ferrow closed the ledger. "Go set up your stall, Coldharbour. Daylight's wasting."

* * *

Pitch nineteen had a broken shutter and a wobbly table and a view of the fountain, where water leapt and caught the sun and threw small rainbows across the cobbles. Hazel unpacked her satchel with hands that had finally stopped shaking.

The charms came out one by one, and she looked at each of them as if greeting old friends — which, in fairness, she was. The luck-knots braided from canal-grass and red thread. The sleep-sachets stuffed with valerian and a whisper of hedge-craft. The little glass bottles of courage, which contained nothing but spring water and a word spoken over them at dawn, and worked anyway, because payment given freely was the whole of the law, and Hazel had learned that lesson in a drowned chapel with her mother's memories going out of her like tide off a sandbar. She had gotten most of those memories back when the covenant mended — the garden had returned what it had taken, plus interest, plus a few memories of her mother laughing that Hazel had never known she'd lost. But she remembered the giving. She would always remember the giving. It was the difference, she supposed, between a girl who hoarded power and a witch who understood what power cost.

She laid everything out in the morning light. Then, after a moment's hesitation, she reached into the bottom of the satchel and brought out the last item: a small wooden sign, blank, which she had carried through the hedge and across the hills without quite admitting why.

She borrowed a pencil stub from the candle-boy and wrote on it in her best hand, the hand Mother Yew had drilled into her over long evenings of copying receipts and remedies:

*HAZEL COLDHARBOUR — CHARMS, CURES, SMALL MIRACLES. HONEST WORK AT HONEST PRICES.*

She stood the sign at the front of the stall and stepped back.

There it was. Her name, in public, in daylight, where any Warden could have read it once — where any Warden *would* have read it, once, and come for her in the night with irons and a warrant and the quiet apology of men who enforce laws they don't understand. Now a pair of children ran past and read it aloud, stumbling over *Coldharbour*, giggling, and ran on, and nothing happened except that the fountain threw rainbows and the morning went on being beautiful.

Hazel sat down on her stool and put her face in her hands and laughed until she cried, quietly, behind the shelter of her own stall, while the first customer of the day — an old woman wanting a charm for a bad knee — waited patiently and pretended not to notice.

* * *

The day passed the way good days pass, which is to say too quickly and full of small things.

She sold a luck-knot to a bargeman whose daughter was sitting her guild exams. She sold a sleep-sachet to the herb-woman from the next pitch, who introduced herself properly — Dagny, three sons, one goat, opinions on everything — and pronounced Hazel's valerian blend "better than mine, curse you, where do you source it?" She fixed a love-charm that another seller had botched, untangling a working done with grasping intent rather than free offering, and explained to the chastened seller, gently, why it had gone wrong, and watched understanding dawn in the woman's face like sunrise. Word traveled fast in a market. By afternoon she had a small crowd at her pitch whenever she worked, people craning to watch a hedge-witch do openly what half of them had secretly paid for in alleys for years.

"You're the one from the ballad," said a girl of about ten, planted at the front of the crowd with the fearless entitlement of the very young. "The one what kissed the shadowless man and saved the world."

"The ballad has it wrong," Hazel said. "I didn't save the world. We traded names. He got mine, I got his. It's more romantic that way, if you think about it — somewhere out east there's a man who introduces himself as Hazel."

The crowd laughed. The girl did not. She leaned closer, dropping her voice, and her eyes were sharp and hungry in a way Hazel recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting.

"That's not what I heard," the girl said. "I heard you gave your name away to death and he pulled it back out. I heard you burned up seven years of magic in one minute to save him from drowning. I heard you knelt in the dirt to a madwoman and finished your apprenticeship with the whole garden watching." Her chin lifted. "I want to learn how."

The market noise seemed to recede. Hazel looked at the girl — the patched dress, the quick hands, the wildness banked behind her eyes like a fire in a stove — and felt seven years fall away, felt herself standing in Mother Yew's cottage at that same age, asking the same question with the same terrible hunger, and felt also the weight of everything that hunger had cost: the flight, the betrayal, the blame, the leash disguised as sacrifice, the century of madness, the Gardener alone with his shears. Power asked. Power always asked. The only question was whether you learned to hear the price before you paid it.

"Come back tomorrow," Hazel heard herself say. "At dawn. Bring nothing. If you're serious, we'll talk about what learning costs." She held the girl's gaze. "And if you're not serious, don't come at all. I'll know."

The girl grinned — a wild, bright, dangerous grin — and vanished into the crowd, and Hazel sat back on her stool with her heart hammering and the afternoon sun warm on her shoulders, wondering what exactly she had just done.

* * *

She closed the stall at dusk, as the lamplighters came through and the candle-seller's six-colored flames made the whole row glow like something out of the Otherworld, which, Hazel reflected, it very nearly was these days. She ate supper at a canal-side counter — fried fish, bread, small beer — and listened to the talk around her, which was about rents and barges and a wedding and the price of eels, ordinary talk, unhaunted talk, and she thought: *this is what we bought. This is what the exchange purchased. An evening where nobody is afraid.*

Afterward she walked the long way home, along the canal, past the teahouse.

It had reopened. Of course it had. New paint, new awning, tables set out under strings of lanterns, and there — third table from the water, the one where a nervous mapmaker had once confessed to finding a book that shouldn't exist — sat a young couple leaning close over their cups, talking with the shy intensity of people at the beginning of something. Hazel stopped and watched them for a moment from the shadows of the opposite quay, and pressed her hand flat against her sternum, where the ache lived, the permanent one, the one shaped like a man who slept in flowers.

One day a week. That was the bargain, and he had chosen it with open eyes, and she honored it with open eyes, and neither of them pretended it didn't hurt. But tonight the ache was bearable, because tonight she had a name in a ledger and a pitch in a market and a girl with hungry eyes coming at dawn, and because tomorrow — no, four days hence, she was counting, she was always counting — she would walk out the eastern gate to the place where the hedge grew, and the hedge would open for her like a held breath released, and he would be standing there among the apple trees with no shadow and dirt on his hands and that look on his face, that look that said *you came, you always come, I never quite believe it until you do.*

She turned for home. Behind her, the teahouse lanterns swayed, and the couple at the third table laughed at something, and the canal carried its honest water onward through the healed and waking city.

Halfway home, she realized she was humming the ballad. Badly. All three verses.

She let herself laugh at that, alone in the lamplit street, and walked on — Hazel Coldharbour, charm-seller, hedge-witch, home — while somewhere far to the east, past the walls and the hills and the border of the world, a garden grew, and a man without a shadow tended it, and waited for her, and the seed in the ground beneath his feet put out, in the dark, its first pale root toward a door that would one day never close.
