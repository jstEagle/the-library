## 36. The Mapmaker's Letters

*The first letter, found folded inside a hollow acorn on the Vell side of the hedge, in Hazel's handwriting:*

---

Wendell —

I have written this letter eleven times. I know because I kept the failures, and they are stacked under my bed like kindling, and sometimes at night I take one out and read the ways I failed to say anything true.

*Dear Wendell, I hope the garden is well.* Absurd. The garden is you now; I might as well write *I hope you are well* and mean it plainly, which is apparently the hardest sentence in any language.

So: plainly. I miss you. Not the way I expected to — not like an ache or a wound. More like a room I keep walking into even though the door has moved. I'll be measuring out dried nettle for a fever charm and my hands will remember the weight of your compass case, and there you are, in the middle of a Tuesday, entirely uninvited and entirely welcome.

Vell is learning its own name again. That sounds strange, but it's the truest thing I can tell you. For seven years magic here was a whisper, a crime, a thing done with shutters drawn. Now the Wardens have become something like librarians — stern, overworked, faintly embarrassed about the old cages. There's a girl on Tallow Street who can coax light out of lamp-oil without a wick, and last week the canal by the Weavers' Bridge ran clear for a full hour and everyone stood on the banks and cried as if they'd witnessed a coronation. Maybe they had.

I reopened the stall. Under my own name. *Hazel Coldharbour, Charms & Remedies,* painted on the board in green because I couldn't help myself. The first week I flinched every time someone read it aloud. The fourth week a woman brought her daughter to be tested for the gift, and asked me — me — whether I'd take an apprentice someday. I said I was thinking about it. I am thinking about it. Mostly I think about how Mother Yew would have answered, and then I try to answer better.

Tell me what the garden is doing. Tell me what the river looks like where it runs through your maps. Tell me something small and specific, because small and specific is how I hold you: the ink stain on your left cuff, the way you say *hmm* before you disagree with someone, the smell of the teahouse rain.

Eleven drafts, and it comes down to that. Write me something small.

— Hazel

---

*His reply, delivered by Briarlock, who left it in the crook of a petrified apple tree and pretended not to watch her find it:*

---

Hazel,

Something small, then. The moss on the north wall of the ruined chapel has come back the colour of kingfishers. I did not know moss could do that. I have made three attempts to map it and each time the colour runs off the page and onto my fingers, so for now the map simply says HERE THE WORLD IS SHOWING OFF, which Briarlock says is not cartography, and Briarlock is right, but Briarlock also once mapped a river as "wet," so we are neither of us beyond reproach.

You should see the orchards. You *shouldn't*, actually — you should wait until equinox and see them properly — but I cannot help describing them, because describing things is the only way I know to give them to you. The petrified trees are waking from the outside in, like people thawing. Bark softens first, then the branches remember how to bend. Last month a tree that had been stone since before the Sundering put out blossom overnight, white flowers with grey veins in the petals, and when the wind came through the whole grove sounded like a crowd exhaling. I sat under it until dawn. I thought: she would want to know exactly how many blossoms. So I counted. Four thousand two hundred and six, give or take the ones the bees were arguing over.

The bees are back too. Nobody sent them. They simply arrived one morning from wherever bees go when their world dies, and Briarlock wept, which for a fox-headed herald means standing very still with his ears flat and pretending to inspect the horizon.

Here is the part I keep circling and failing to say: being mortal again is stranger than being a seed ever was. I sleep now. Deeply, heavily, the way stones must sleep. I dream of nothing, most nights, and wake up hungry, and eat breakfast, and the ordinariness of it undoes me. I spent twenty-odd years human without knowing what it cost, and now I spend my days mapping a borderland and my evenings feeling the cold come into my bones like an honest tenant paying rent, and I have never been happier or more frightened. Mortality is a deadline. Everything I do now, I do *before*. Before the frost. Before the year turns. Before—

Well. Before.

The garden gives me work instead of grief, which I suspect was the design. Every morning there is something new to chart: a spring where the grey water runs clear, a stand of birch grown knee-high in a single night, a hollow where the Hollowed used to huddle and where now there is only warm earth and the print of small returning feet. I map the regrowth the way you once sold luck — carefully, honestly, one small miracle at a time.

Mother Yew says hello, in her fashion. Her fashion is assigning me essays on root-structure. She has taken on eleven of the waking Hollowed as students and terrifies all of them thoroughly, and they adore her. Yesterday I watched her teach a man who had been a wraith for sixty years how to hold a trowel, and she held his hands steady the whole time, and pretended she wasn't. You come from her, Hazel. I hope you know that whatever else she was, that steadiness is hers, and it's in you, and I have felt it.

Four thousand two hundred and six. When you come at equinox, I will show you the tree, and you will tell me I counted wrong, and I will show you my notes, and you will kiss me to end the argument, and I will let you win.

Yours, entirely and specifically,
Wendell

---

* * *

*Her next letter, some weeks later:*

---

Wendell,

You counted wrong. It was four thousand two hundred and eleven. I checked twice while you slept under it, which you do now, in the open air, like a creature with no enemies, and watching you sleep may be my favourite of all the things the new covenant lets me do.

Now my news, because you asked and because I promised myself I'd stop hoarding Vell the way I used to hoard charms — keeping everything close, sharing nothing, safe and starved.

The city is holding its first Open Market since the Sundering. Not a hidden market in a cellar behind a fishmonger's — an actual market, in Founder's Square, stalls in daylight, children everywhere. The Council fought it, of course. The Council fights everything except its own reflection. But the Wardens' new captain is a woman named Odile Voss whose brother was Hollowed — no, don't look like that, he came back, they're nearly all coming back now, the garden's healing reaches further than anyone guessed — and she stood in front of the Council chamber and said, "You can outlaw a river, but it will still be wet." I have decided to forgive the Wardens collectively on the strength of that sentence alone.

I taught my first lesson. Not an apprenticeship — just a lesson, in the back of the teahouse where we met, which has been renamed, unbearably, *The Ash & Ivy.* The landlady swears it was always called that. Magic makes people sentimental and then gaslights them about it.

Six students. A baker with a talent for warmth-charms, twin sisters who can hear storms coming three days off, a retired ferryman whose hands make rope knots untie themselves, and a boy of maybe nine who floated his mother's thimble across the table and then looked terrified, the way I looked terrified, the way everyone looks when the secret finally gets to breathe. I stood in front of them and heard Mother Yew's cadences coming out of my mouth — *the gift is a garden, not a weapon; tend it or it grows wild* — and afterward I sat alone for a long time with my tea going cold.

Not from sadness. From the strangeness of a circle closing without cutting anyone.

I used to think leaving her was the worst thing I ever did. Then I thought letting her take the blame was the worst thing. Now I think the worst thing was smaller than either: it was deciding, at nineteen, that I was the kind of person who destroys what she loves, and then arranging my whole life as evidence. Seven years of building a case against myself. The covenant didn't free me from that, exactly. You did. Or loving you did. Same thing, if you ask me, and you can't argue, because you're not here, which is the entire problem with this arrangement.

Which brings me to the complaint portion of this letter: the hedge lets letters through and lets me through one day a season, and I understand why — borders need tending, love needs tending, nothing given freely should become a cage — but understanding a rule and resenting it are not mutually exclusive, and I reserve the right to do both.

The night after the Open Market, I walked past the spot where the tear opened. It's gone, of course — sealed, smooth cobbles, a flower-seller stands there now. But I put my palm on the stones anyway, the way you'd touch a scar, and I swear the cobbles were warm.

Come spring, I'm bringing you honey-cakes from the baker. Don't argue. You've lost weight; Briarlock wrote and told me, the traitor, the darling.

Counting blossoms and counting days,
Hazel

---

* * *

*His reply:*

---

Hazel,

Four thousand two hundred and eleven. Of course it was. You have never once let me win an argument except the ones you decided to lose in advance, and I have learned to treasure those like rare coins.

I showed your letter to Mother Yew. She read it standing in her doorway with the light behind her, and when she reached the part about the thimble-boy she made a sound I had never heard from her — short, startled, unmistakably a laugh — and then she cleared her throat and said, "Tell her the boy will need grounding exercises or he'll float himself into the eaves," and went back inside. From her, that is a sonnet. I transcribed it faithfully.

Briarlock has asked me to inform you that he is not a traitor, he is a *correspondent*, and that he considers himself the finest postal service in either realm, a claim I cannot dispute since he is also the only postal service in either realm. He carries your letters pressed flat against his chest beneath his coat, and I have noticed he walks differently on delivery days — slower, ceremonial, like a priest with a relic. I think he waited a hundred years for us and has decided that carrying our words is the good part of the prophecy. Let him have it. Some duties are rewards wearing disguises.

Now, my confession, since yours was so brave: I have started a second map. Not of the garden. Of the year.

It sounds mad. Briarlock certainly said so, and then helped me draw it, which is his way of saying yes to everything. Here is the idea: mortality gave me a deadline, and I have decided to spend the deadline deliberately. So I have drawn the four seasons as four territories, and I am surveying them the way I'd survey any country — noting where the light falls, what grows there, who lives there. Spring is marked with your name. Equinox, the hedge-day, the cottage. I have begun annotating it already, in advance, the way you'd mark a place you intend to return to: *here she argued about blossoms; here she fell asleep mid-sentence and I carried her to bed and she denied it in the morning; here the honey-cakes.*

A map of a life, Hazel. Made of visits. Small territory, fiercely claimed. I used to think devotion meant giving everything at once — I was raised on prophecies, after all, and prophecies are greedy. But the garden is teaching me what you always knew: that a thing tended daily outgrows a thing sacrificed grandly. The Hollowed are learning it too. One of them — Marta, who was a wraith for forty years — told me last week that she doesn't miss her old life, she misses *ordinary Tuesdays*, and so now every Tuesday we sit together and do absolutely nothing remarkable, on purpose, as practice.

So consider this letter an ordinary Tuesday, posted across a border. Nothing remarkable in it. Only that I am here, and you are there, and the words get through, and that is enough to build a country on.

One more thing, because I promised myself I'd stop circling it: I am not afraid anymore. Of the dying, I mean. I thought mortality would taste like loss, and mostly it tastes like attention. Every season I get is a season I *know* I'm getting. When you walk through the hedge in spring, I will not waste a moment of it bracing for autumn. This is the seed's last gift to me, I think — it taught me how to be planted, and now I get to practice being harvested, and both of them, somehow, are forms of love.

Bring the honey-cakes. Bring the thimble-boy's progress reports. Bring yourself, which is the cargo all the rest is wrapped around.

Mapping the year, and finding you in every quarter of it,
Wendell

---

* * *

*Her reply, shorter, written fast, in ink still wet when Briarlock took it:*

---

Wendell —

Short one, because Briarlock is tapping his foot and pretending he isn't, and because some things shouldn't be drafted eleven times.

The thimble-boy's name is Fen. He followed me home from the market last week — literally followed, all the way through three districts, too proud to admit he was lost and too stubborn to leave. Wild talent, no training, parents gone, sleeping in the loft above the stall. Do you see it yet? Because I didn't, for two full days, and then I woke at dawn and looked at him asleep among my drying herbs and felt the ground tilt.

He asked me this morning, not meeting my eyes, the way I once didn't meet hers: "Is it true there's a teacher in the other place? The fox-man's mistress says there's a lady who teaches the broken ones."

I said yes.

He said, "Could I learn? Both places?"

And Wendell — I said yes to that too.

I don't know what I'm doing. I know exactly what I'm doing. Both at once, which Mother Yew would say is the definition of a hedge-witch. He'll need grounding exercises or he'll float into the eaves; I'll tell him you said so and watch him stand straighter, because children straighten when they learn someone far away is paying attention.

Equinox soon. The honey-cakes are baked and wrapped and I have caught myself checking the parcel twice a day like a woman expecting a letter, which I suppose I am, in a way — expecting the answer to one I mailed years ago, back when I was nineteen and running, asking the world whether anyone could love the thing I actually was.

The world is answering slowly. In blossoms. In letters. In a boy asleep in my herbs.

Save me the space under the flowering tree. I'm bringing an apprentice to meet his teachers.

Always,
Hazel
