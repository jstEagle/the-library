## 38. The New Apprentice

The hedge had grown a door in the three years since the covenant was remade — a proper one, of hawthorn and honeysuckle, standing in a courtyard behind the old tannery where no door had any right to be. It opened for Hazel the way a well-trained dog opens its eyes when its person comes home: not all at once, but with a slow, green attention that she had never stopped finding miraculous.

She stepped through on a morning in late autumn, her satchel heavy with things Vell wanted and the garden needed: salt from the Wardens' new cooperative kitchens, a bundle of letters tied in red string, two loaves of rye bread because Wendell could not bake bread to save his life and would never admit it. The Sundered Garden received her the way it always did now — with warmth, with birdsong, with the smell of rain that had fallen somewhere else and come here to finish falling.

The grey river ran clear these days. She still caught herself looking at it sideways sometimes, expecting ash and silt, expecting the drowned chapel's bells tolling under the current. Old habits. Seven years of hiding left grooves in a person, and though the city had changed around her — magic taught openly in the archive halls now, charm-sellers licensed instead of hunted, the Warden sweeps replaced by something gentler called the Registry of Gifts — Hazel still woke some mornings with her hand curled around a charm that wasn't there, ready to run.

But not this morning. This morning she walked the petrified orchard road, which was petrified no longer, past apple trees whose bark had softened back to living wood, their branches heavy with fruit that glowed faintly gold when the light touched it right. The Hollowed who had once haunted these rows were mostly awake now — thin, bewildered people learning to eat and speak and weep again, tended by students of Mother Yew's growing school. One of them, a former wraith named Colm who had spent forty years as a fruit-stealing shadow, waved at her with an arm that was still regaining its color.

"Hazel Coldharbour," he called, his voice like a hinge remembering how to swing. "Your bread's burning."

"It's not burning, it's *carrying*." But she checked anyway, and laughed, and walked on.

The cottage stood where the World-Tree's fallen branch had rooted itself — a small crooked house of silver-grey wood, its roof shingled in living moss, its windows lit even at midday because Wendell liked lamplight and saw no reason to ration it. Smoke curled from the chimney. Somewhere behind it, she could hear him humming, off-key, the way he always hummed when he was drawing.

Home, said something in her chest that had been silent for seven years and then, briefly, forever, and was learning to say it again.

She was twenty paces from the door when she heard the second set of footsteps.

* * *

Hazel had survived seven years in Vell by noticing things other people didn't. It was the first lesson Mother Yew had ever taught her, before everything went wrong between them: *magic is mostly attention, girl. The rest is just manners.* And so she had noticed, over the years, the small strangenesses that clung to certain children like burrs — the ones who made lamps flicker when they lied, who found lost coins in gutters no one had swept, who dreamed true and hated it.

She had been one of those children. She knew their footfalls the way a shepherd knows the sound of a lamb that has found a hole in the fence.

This one was good. Better than good. The footsteps behind her were nearly silent — not trained silence, but the natural quiet of someone whose body simply agreed with the ground — and yet Hazel felt them the way you feel weather coming. A pressure. A hum. The charms in her satchel, harmless little things, had begun to whisper to each other.

She didn't turn around. She kept walking, unhurried, and said to the empty air, "You've been following me since the tanner's yard. That's half a mile on a bad ankle, by the sound of your left step. You can come out or you can keep limping."

Silence. Then, from the shadow of a reborn pear tree, a voice — young, scraped raw at the edges, trying very hard to be flat and unimpressed and failing at both:

"You're the witch. The one from the stall. Coldharbour."

"I'm the charm-seller from the stall. Witch is a word people use when they want to arrest you or marry you, and I'm too busy for either." Hazel turned.

The kid was maybe eleven, maybe twelve — street-age was a guesswork science in Vell, where hard winters aged children like wood. Small, sharp-faced, hair cropped short with what looked like a knife and no mirror. A coat three sizes too big with the sleeves hacked off. Eyes the colour of pond ice, and around those eyes — this was what made Hazel's breath catch — a faint shimmer, like heat-haze, like the air itself couldn't quite decide whether to believe in her.

Wild talent. Untrained, unshielded, leaking everywhere. Hazel hadn't seen it that naked since she'd seen it in her own reflection, thirty years ago, in Mother Yew's scrying bowl.

"What's your name?" she asked.

A shrug, too casual to be casual. "People call me Sparrow."

"People call me a lot of things. What does your mother call you?"

"Don't have one." Flat. Practiced. A door slammed and painted over so many times the paint had become the wood. "Don't have a father either, before you ask. Don't have anybody. I sell matches. I'm good at it. I don't need anything."

"Mm." Hazel set down her satchel, deliberately slowly, and took out the rye bread. The smell of it drifted across the orchard road, warm and honest. "You followed me through a door that doesn't exist for most people. Do you know why you could see it?"

"Because I'm— " The kid stopped. The shimmer around her eyes flickered, and for a moment she looked less like a hardened match-girl and more like exactly what she was: a child standing very far from home, hoping desperately not to be sent there. "Because I'm wrong. Something's wrong with me. Things happen. Lamps do things. I dreamed about this place before I ever saw it — the grey river, the dead trees, a fox wearing clothes. I dreamed it for years and everyone said I was mad, and then yesterday I saw you buying apples and you smelled like—" She gestured helplessly at the whole blooming world. "Like here. Like *before*. So I followed you. And if you're going to turn me in to the Registry, do it quick, because I'm not going back to the Foundling House and I'll run first."

Nobody had mentioned the Registry. Nobody had threatened anything. Hazel looked at this scrap of a child, vibrating with borrowed lightning, lying badly about needing nothing, and felt thirty years fall away like leaves in a high wind.

There had been a night, long ago, when a child named Hazel had used forbidden magic to save another child's life, and fled rather than let her mentor pay for it — except her mentor paid anyway, for a century, in a garden gone grey. There had been a night, more recently, when Hazel had knelt in the dark beside a madwoman and learned that love disguised as sacrifice is still a leash, and forgiveness given freely is the only key that opens it. And there had been a moment at the Heartwood Gate when she had offered up her own name, her whole self, to be erased — and been saved instead by a man who loved her enough to share the cost.

Every lesson she had ever learned, bought at terrible price, came down to this one small truth standing in front of her in a stolen coat:

You cannot leave a child alone with wild magic. It will eat her alive, or the world will, and either way the loss is the same.

"Sparrow," Hazel said quietly. "Come here. Sit down. Eat some bread. And then I'll tell you a story about a girl who ran away from her teacher because she thought running was freedom, and what it cost, and how long the road back was." She held out the loaf. "Nobody's turning you in. Nobody's sending you anywhere. But I am going to teach you, if you'll let me — properly, the way I should have been taught, the way nobody should have to learn alone. First lesson's free."

The kid stared at the bread like it might be a trap. Then at Hazel. The shimmer around her eyes steadied, just slightly, the way a candle flame steadies when a hand cups around it.

"...What's the catch?"

"The catch is lessons are hard, and I'm strict, and there's an old woman here who will make you weed the herb beds until your hands smell of thyme for the rest of your life." Hazel smiled. "Her name is Mother Yew. You'll hate her for a year and then you'd die for her. That's how it works. That's how it's supposed to work."

Sparrow took the bread.

* * *

Mother Yew met them at the schoolhouse — a long low hall built into the roots of the World-Tree's living trunk, its walls hung with drying herbs and star-charts and, in the place of honor where a century ago a prophecy book had hung, a simple wooden sign that read *ASK ANYTHING. PAY FREELY. THAT IS THE WHOLE OF THE LAW.*

The old woman had not aged a day since the covenant's renewal — or rather, she had finally begun aging normally, which after a hundred years of frozen madness was its own kind of miracle. Her hair was white as birch-bark now, her hands spotted, her eyes clear in a way they had not been clear since before Hazel was born. When she saw the child trailing Hazel through the door, leaking magic like a cracked jug, her eyebrows rose.

"Well," she said. "You've brought me a feral one."

"She brought herself. I just failed to lose her."

"They usually do." Mother Yew crouched, slow in her joints, to look Sparrow in the eye. Hazel watched the old woman's gaze move over the shimmer, the too-big coat, the knife-cropped hair, and saw — not pity, never pity; Mother Yew had learned better than anyone alive what pity did to pride — but recognition. "What did you dream, child? Before you found the door. Tell me true."

Sparrow's chin came up, defiant, but her voice wobbled. "Grey trees. A river the wrong colour. A fox in a red coat who talked. And..." She hesitated, glanced at Hazel, looked away. "A woman made of light. Holding hands with a man who had flowers growing out of his sleeping bag. I thought I'd invented them."

"You didn't invent us," Hazel said. "We're realer than we have any right to be."

Mother Yew straightened, and something passed over her face — the ghost of a century of guilt, met and mastered. "Then we begin tomorrow at dawn. Herb-lore first. Grounding second. You will learn to hold your gift the way you'd hold a bird: firmly enough that it stays, gently enough that it lives. And you will learn it *here*, among others, with teachers who owe you nothing and therefore owe you honesty." She fixed the child with a look that had cowed generations of apprentices. "I will not lie to you, Sparrow. I will not bind you. I will not pretend sacrifice is love. Those promises I broke once, long ago, to someone standing right there—" a nod at Hazel, "—and I have spent a hundred years learning better. You will get the better version. That is my debt, paid forward."

Sparrow blinked. Hazel suspected the child understood perhaps a third of it. The important third, though — the part that said *you are safe, and you are free, and those are not opposites.*

"Okay," said Sparrow, in the tone of someone accepting a job offer while planning to rob the employer. But her hand had crept, without her noticing, to grip the hem of Hazel's sleeve.

Hazel pretended not to notice. Some things you let a child keep.

* * *

Wendell was waiting at the cottage when they came back at dusk, and Hazel watched him watch the kid — watched the mapmaker's eye take in the shimmer, the lean hungry watchfulness, the way fireflies had already begun gathering around Sparrow's head like a crown she didn't know she wore.

"Another one?" he asked softly, when Sparrow had fallen asleep in the armchair mid-sentence, mid-question, mid-everything, worn out by wonder and bread.

"Another one."

He came to stand beside her at the window, his shoulder warm against hers, his hand finding hers the way it always did now — easily, without ceremony, a name given in love becoming a life lived in it. Outside, the garden breathed. Somewhere far off, Briarlock's fox-fire lanterns bobbed along the river path, lighting late-walking Hollowed home.

"Do you remember," Wendell murmured, "when you told me you'd sworn off teaching? That you'd never put anyone through what you went through?"

"I remember being wrong about a great many things. It's one of my talents."

"One of your best." He kissed her hair. "She'll be all right, you know. With you. With Mother Yew. With—" he nodded at the sleeping child, at the fireflies settling on her cropped hair like tiny green stars, "—with whatever she turns out to be. We didn't get that. She gets to."

Hazel leaned into him and watched the last light go amber over the orchards, over the clear river, over the little schoolhouse where a lamp was already lit for tomorrow's dawn lesson. Thirty years ago a girl had run from a teacher and broken the world a little. Today a girl had run *toward* one, and the world had opened a door.

That was the whole of the law, really. Payment given freely. Doors opened on purpose.

Sparrow stirred in the chair and spoke without waking, the way dreaming children do — and the words dropped into the quiet room like a stone into a still pool:

*"The tree says there's another one coming. Older. And she's carrying something that used to be a book."*

Hazel went cold. Wendell's hand tightened around hers.

In the archive at Vell, in a glass case where scholars now studied it openly, a prophecy book lay with two names intertwined on its final page — and Hazel had sworn, more than once, that its ink had finished moving.

Apparently, it disagreed.

Outside, deep in the roots of the World-Tree, something old turned over in its sleep — and every firefly in the garden rose at once, like a held breath.
