## 1. The Keeper of Small Prayers

The trouble with being the last keeper of the Hollow Gate, Paloma Sterling had long ago decided, was that nobody ever checked your work.

There were no superiors to impress. No inspectors from some distant ministry to nod approvingly at her ledgers. The valley elders came by twice a year to make sure she hadn't starved or gone mad, and even then they mostly wanted to complain about the price of wool. So when she swept the gate-stones at dawn, oiled the iron hinges that had not swung in a hundred years, and recited the Nine Verses of Keeping in a voice pitched somewhere between reverence and boredom, there was no one to notice if she skipped a verse. Which, on cold mornings, she did.

She never skipped the ninth, though. The ninth verse was the one that said *I am here*, and Paloma had a private superstition — one she would have denied under torture — that as long as somebody said *I am here* to the gate every day, the country on the other side would stay politely unmade.

"Good morning," she told the gate, because her grandmother had told it good morning, and her grandmother's mother before that, all the way back through a line of women who had apparently believed that ancient seals responded well to courtesy.

The Hollow Gate did not look like much. That was the first thing strangers always said, back when strangers still came, and the last thing they ever said, because after looking at it properly they tended to go quiet. It stood in the narrow throat of the valley where the hills pinched together like a hand closing: two pillars of grey stone, an arch between them, and beyond the arch — nothing. Not darkness. Not mist. Nothing, the way a word is nothing on the far side of your lips. The eye slid off it. The mind filed it under *later* and moved on.

Magic could not follow you through the valley. That was the whole point of the place, though the elders had let the reason wear off the way lettering wears off old coins. A candle burned here and gave only light. A blessing spoken here was just breath. Paloma had tested it once, at nineteen, with a charm her grandmother had taught her for finding lost things — a small, homely bit of magic, the kind grandmothers keep in their apron pockets. She had stood three feet from the arch and whispered it, and felt it go out of her like warmth out of an open door, and heard nothing answer.

That had frightened her more than the Nothing itself, if she was honest. The Nothing at least kept to its side of the gate.

* * *

Her days had a shape to them, and she loved the shape even when she grumbled at its contents. Dawn: sweep, oil, verses. Morning: the ledger, in which she recorded the gate's condition in handwriting so meticulous it was practically calligraphy, because if no one would ever read it, it might as well be beautiful. Midday: the walk down to Millbrook for flour, gossip, and the particular pleasure of Mrs. Aldous telling her she looked thin.

"You look thin," Mrs. Aldous said, weighing flour with the gravity of a judge passing sentence.

"I look exactly the same as I did last week."

"That's what worries me." Mrs. Aldous leaned across the counter. "Any trouble up at the gate?"

"No trouble."

"The Henderson boy swears he saw lights past the pillars last month."

"The Henderson boy also swears he wrestled a river spirit, and we both know he fell in the ford drunk."

Mrs. Aldous laughed, but she weighed the flour slowly, and her eyes went to the window, toward the pinch in the hills, before she handed the parcel over. Everyone in Millbrook did that eventually. They looked at the gate the way you look at a sleeping dog whose breed you're not sure of.

Paloma carried her flour home and made bread, and the bread rose, because yeast is honest work and not magic, and she ate half a loaf standing at her window watching the light die off the western hills. This was the hour she liked best. The valley went gold, then violet, then blue as ink, and the gate-stones caught the last of it and held it longer than anything else, as if the stone were greedy for a world it had spent a century keeping out.

She should have gone to bed. Instead she found herself walking up the path in the dark, which she almost never did, because night rituals were for emergencies and anniversaries, and this was neither. It was simply that tomorrow was the first of Thawmonth, and the first of Thawmonth was the day her grandmother had died, eleven years ago, and grief keeps its own calendar whether or not you subscribe to it.

At the gate she lit the small lamp — allowed, since lamplight is not magic either — and set it on the keeper's shelf, a niche worn into the left pillar by a hundred years of leaning elbows. Then she did something she had not done since she was a girl and still believed prayers were letters that got delivered.

She spoke aloud.

"Grandmother," she said, to the air, to the stone, to whatever portion of the dead might be on duty. "I kept it another year. The verses, the oil, the sweeping. The ledger's full. My handwriting's improved, you'd be pleased." She paused. "I'm lonely. There. I said it. You always said a keeper shouldn't want things, and I don't want things, exactly. I just — I'd like someone to tell about my day. Someone who'd know why the ninth verse matters. That's all. That's a small prayer. You can't tell me that's greedy."

The valley held its silence, as valleys do.

And then the silence answered.

"*Small,*" said a voice, "is a matter of scale."

Paloma did not scream, which she counted later as her single greatest professional achievement. What she did was go very still, the way you go still when you realize the sleeping dog has opened one eye, and turn her head by degrees toward the arch.

The Nothing was still nothing. But it had — texture, now. Attention. She had spent her whole life learning the gate the way you learn a face, and she knew with absolute certainty that it was *looking* at her. Something behind the unmaking had leaned its weight against the door and listened, and having listened, had decided to speak.

"You are the keeper," the voice said. It was not loud. It was not unkind. It sounded like a bell heard through a wall, like a name called from very far away by someone who loves you. "You are the one who says *I am here*."

"Yes," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Who's asking?"

"A prayer was offered at the threshold. Prayers at the threshold are mine to answer."

"That was a complaint, not a prayer."

"All petitions are prayers. The distinction is administrative." A pause, and then, with what she could only describe as mild surprise: "It has been a long time since anyone petitioned. The queue was very long. I am only now reaching the beginning of it."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry, but her grandmother's training surfaced through the fear like a cork: *when the impossible happens, take notes.* She reached for the little notebook in her coat pocket and her fingers shook so badly she dropped it, and picked it up, and opened it against her knee.

"What do I call you?" she asked.

Another pause, longer. When the voice came again it was quieter, and sadder, and somehow that was worse than everything before it.

"I had nine names," it said. "I had a country that spoke them. I am the god of the Hollow Gate, keeper, and I have been asleep inside your ninth verse for a hundred years, and you have been saying *I am here* to me every morning of your life, and I have only just woken enough to say it back."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no wind.

"I am here," the god repeated, tasting the words. "Do you know, I think that was the whole of the prayer. All these years. Every keeper, every morning. Not a spell. A roll call. We were answering each other, and neither of us knew."

Paloma wrote, in wobbly handwriting, *it talks*, and underlined it twice, and then made herself ask the question that mattered, the one whose answer she already dreaded.

"If you've been asleep a hundred years — why wake now? Why tonight? Why me?"

"Because the seal is failing," the god said gently, the way you tell someone a house is on fire. "Because the thirteenth moon is rising, and it rises thin, and it will rise thirteen times before it is full. And because you asked aloud, keeper, and I could not bear, on the first night of waking, to leave even a small prayer unanswered."

Somewhere down in Millbrook, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then all of them, a ragged chorus rolling up the valley as if every animal in the world had smelled smoke.

Paloma closed the notebook. Her hands had stopped shaking, which surprised her; what remained instead was a cold, clear, wide-awake feeling, the feeling of standing on a floor and hearing, beneath it, the first hairline crack.

"There's no one I can ask about this," she said. "Do you understand? Everyone who knew what the gate really was is dead. I'm the last keeper. Whatever happens next, I'm the only one holding the manual."

"No," said the god.

Paloma went very still again.

"No," the god said again, and behind the arch, in the middle of the Nothing, something moved — a shape, upright, human-shaped, walking out of the unmade dark with the unhurried gait of a man arriving precisely on time. "You are not the only one. He is coming. He remembers everything."

The figure stepped into the lamplight, wearing clothes from a country that was not on any map, and smiled at her like a man greeting the last page of a very long book.

"Keeper," said Wendell of Vessany, "we need to talk about your grandmother."
