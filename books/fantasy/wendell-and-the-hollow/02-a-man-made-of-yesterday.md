## 2. A Man Made of Yesterday

I had been walking through the mist for eleven years, and I want to be honest about that number, because honesty is the only trade I have left.

Eleven years, give or take the days I lost. The mist does not keep time, and neither did I, after a while. I kept other things. That was my whole profession, once: keeping. In Vessany they called us memory-keepers, and children thought we were magicians, and magicians knew better and were politely jealous. We carried what a country could not carry for itself — every song, every grudge, every recipe for plum brandy, every name of every dog that ever slept on a Vessani hearth. A country is too heavy to remember itself. That is why it hires people like me.

And then one morning my country decided to stop existing, and I became the only archive left standing, which is a very grand way of saying I became a man with nowhere to put anything down.

The mist had been thinning for three days before I saw the gate. I noticed it the way you notice a toothache — gradually, then all at once, then with a kind of horrified gratitude. Thin mist meant edges. Edges meant somewhere. And somewhere, at last, meant her.

I had been watching Paloma Sterling for some time, if watching is the right word for a man peering through the gauze of an unmade nation at a woman who cannot see him back. She tended the Hollow Gate the way I imagine people tend graves of people they loved but never met: faithfully, thoroughly, and without any expectation of thanks. Every dusk she walked the seal-stones. Every dusk she spoke the old words in an accent that would have made my grandmother weep, first with laughter, then with something else. Every dusk she poured out a thimbleful of honey at the gate's foot, which was not in any rite I remembered, which meant she had invented it herself, which meant she was the sort of keeper I had hoped still existed.

Keepers who invent small kindnesses are the ones worth finding.

So when the mist finally let me through — when it parted around me like a curtain drawn by someone tired of the play — I stepped onto valley stone wearing the last clothes of a country that no longer appeared on maps, carrying everything it had ever been inside a skull that was, I regret to report, beginning to leak.

* * *

She found me at dawn, sitting on the third seal-stone from the left, eating the honey.

I want to defend myself here. It had been eleven years since I tasted anything that wasn't mist, and the honey was Vessani clover-honey, or close enough that my eyes watered, and I am only a man. Also, I had reasoned it out: she poured it as an offering. Offerings are invitations. I had simply accepted one, eleven years late.

"You're eating the offering," she said.

Her voice was exactly as I'd imagined and nothing like it at all. Tired. Younger than her duties. The voice of someone who has spoken more to stones than to people and has made her peace with it, mostly.

"I'm accepting the invitation," I said. "Though I'll grant the distinction is academic now."

She had a staff, and she held it the way her ancestors had held theirs — wrongly, but with conviction. "The law says strangers at the gate are turned away."

"The law also says the gate will hold forever," I said, "and here I am, so perhaps we shouldn't lean too hard on the law this morning."

That was cruel of me, and I knew it even as I said it, and I said it anyway, because I had learned over eleven years that the truth arrives better dressed as rudeness than as pity. She didn't strike me with the staff. She looked at me instead — really looked, the way keepers look, cataloguing — and I watched her cataloguing reach the same conclusion mine had reached about her.

*You're one of me,* her face said. *God help us both.*

"Your coat," she said at last. "I know that cut. I've seen it in the oldest drawings on the seal-stones. That's Vessani work."

"It is."

"Vessany doesn't exist."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't. I'm what's left of it. All of it, actually. Every street in Callowmere, every bell in the Sept of Nine Sorrows, the name of the baker on Tallow Row — Ostrel, his name was Ostrel, and he burned the morning loaves every single day for forty years and blamed his oven — I have all of it up here." I tapped my temple. "I am, at present, the entire national library of a country that was unmade, and I have come to inform you that the library is on fire."

She sat down on the second seal-stone. Not because she meant to; her legs simply decided for her. I recognized the gesture. It is what happens when a keeper hears the thing she has spent her life half-believing spoken aloud by a stranger in an impossible coat.

"The gods," she said carefully. "They've been silent for a hundred years. Everyone knows why. The gate—"

"The gate didn't silence them," I said. "The gate is a bandage over a wound your history books were never allowed to describe. The gods went quiet because there was no country left to pray to them *from*. Prayer needs two ends, keeper. One end was erased." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's hat. "But prayers need somewhere to live while they wait. Guess where yours have been living."

"In me," she whispered.

"In you. In the honey. In the stones. In every ritual performed without quite believing, which is the most powerful kind, though no one writes that down either." I leaned forward. "Three nights ago, you spoke a prayer aloud. First time in years, yes? Something small. Something you didn't think anyone heard."

The color left her face in a way that told me I had guessed right, and I hated being right, which is the occupational hazard of my profession.

"And it was answered," I said gently.

"How do you know that?"

"Because the answering woke *me*, and I was very comfortably dying, and I would like to register a formal complaint about it." I spread my hands. "Here is the arithmetic, Paloma Sterling, and I'll give it to you plainly because you strike me as a woman who prefers plain arithmetic. A memory-keeper can hold a country for roughly a lifetime. I have held mine for a hundred and thirty years, which should be impossible, and is, and yet here I sit, eating your honey. I am dying. Have been dying for eleven years, slowly, the way a candle drowns in its own wax. And every piece of Vessany that slips loose from me as I go—" I tapped my temple again. "It doesn't vanish. It leaks. Through your gate. Back into the world."

"That's not possible."

"Neither am I, and yet."

* * *

We talked until the sun was properly up, which neither of us noticed, both of us being creatures of dusk by long habit. She asked questions in the manner of an interrogator who has decided the prisoner might be innocent but intends to verify. I answered in the manner of a man who has rehearsed this conversation for eleven years and keeps getting the rehearsal wrong.

"You said the gods are waking because you're dying," she said. "Say it again. Slowly. I want to hear how it sounds in daylight."

"It sounds worse in daylight," I admitted. "Very well. I am the vessel. When the vessel breaks, the contents spill. But a country is not wine, keeper — it does not spill all at once. It spills in turns. Moons. Each moon that wanes, a little more of Vessany comes back through your gate. First the small things — flowers, weather, dead lambs standing up, that sort of thing. Then the people. Then—" I paused, because the next part deserved its own silence. "Then the gods. All of them. Including the ones your ancestors sealed the gate to keep out."

"My ancestors sealed it to protect the valley."

"Your ancestors sealed it to end a war," I said, "and protection was the story they told afterward, and I have no doubt they believed it, because everyone believes their own story eventually." I watched her jaw tighten and thought: *there it is.* "I'm not telling you this to wound you. I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from a keeper and not from a god."

"You could be lying."

"I could. Test me. Ask me anything about Vessany. Ask me the color of the door on the house where the queen was born."

She hesitated, then: "What color?"

"Blue. Not sky-blue, not sea-blue — a blue they called *vesseleth*, which translates badly. 'The blue of a promise you intend to keep.' They painted that door fresh every spring for six hundred years, and the last person to paint it was a girl named Maren who was seventeen, and she got the paint on her good shoes, and she swore, and her mother laughed, and I keep that laugh." My voice did something I hadn't authorized. "I keep all of them. That's the trouble."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. The wind came down the valley and moved through the gate without stopping, the way wind does, indifferent to seals and laws and the whole apparatus of keeping.

"If what you say is true," she said slowly, "then the gate isn't failing. The gate is doing exactly what it was built to do — holding. It's you that's failing."

"Yes," I said. "Aren't families wonderful?"

"And when you're gone—"

"When I'm gone, everything goes. Thirteen moon-turns, by my count, though I confess my counting has been affected by the dying. On the turn of the thirteenth moon, whatever hasn't leaked will pour. The whole country. Its gods, its wars, its tyrants — oh yes, we had tyrants, your histories were spared those too — all of it, home again, whether the world is ready or not."

"So we stop it." She said it fast, the way people say things when they haven't finished thinking them. "We find another vessel. Another keeper. We move what you're carrying into—"

"Into what? Into whom?" I shook my head. "Keeper, do you know what it takes to become a memory-keeper? Years. Decades. You don't pour a country into a person like water into a jug. And even if we could — even if we found some poor soul willing to spend their one life as a warehouse for the dead — would that be justice, or just a slower version of the same crime?"

"Crime," she repeated. "You said crime."

I had. I heard it hanging in the air between us, and I decided, in the way of men who have run out of cleverness, to leave it there. Some truths you hand over in installments. That one could wait for chapter and verse.

Instead I stood, and my knees announced their objections, and I offered her the only thing I had that wasn't memories.

"A bargain," I said. "You hide me — because your elders will want me dead within the hour of seeing this coat, and we both know it. And in exchange, I teach you Vessany. The songs, the names, the true shape of the thing your gate holds shut. So that when the thirteenth moon rises thin, you won't be facing it blind."

"Why would you teach me? If I know too much, I might try to stop you. To stop it."

"Because," I said, "you are a keeper, Paloma Sterling, and keepers do not destroy what they understand. They keep it. Even when it hurts. *Especially* when it hurts." I held out my honey-sticky hand. "Besides. I have been alone with a hundred thousand souls in my head for eleven years, and I find I would very much like to talk to one person who is real."

She looked at my hand for a long time. Then, somewhere behind her eyes, I watched a small private war conclude itself — duty losing ground, curiosity advancing, loneliness flanking both.

She took my hand.

"One condition," she said. "You start with the songs. If I'm going to betray everything I was raised to believe, I want to at least learn the music first."

"Madam," I said, "you have just described the entire Vessani approach to catastrophe. We sang at funerals. We sang during the war. We sang, I am reliably informed, during the actual unmaking, though I was not there to hear it, and that absence is the hole in me where everything else used to fit."

I taught her the first verse that evening, there on the seal-stones, our voices low so the valley wouldn't hear. It was a walking song, a road song, the kind mothers hummed to restless children. Halfway through the second line, Paloma stopped.

"Wendell," she said — she had demanded my name earlier, twice, suspiciously, as though I might have borrowed it — "this song. The valley has a version of it. Almost the same words. My grandmother sang it."

"Of course it does," I said. "Where do you think your valley learned its lullabies? Countries leave fingerprints, keeper. Yours is printed all over in Vessani ink."

She stared at me, and I watched the first crack travel up through a hundred years of inherited certainty — a hairline fracture, no wider than a song.

And far off, past the gate, past the mist, something ancient turned over in its sleep and, for the first time in a century, dreamed toward the sound of its own name being sung.
