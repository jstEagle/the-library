## 3. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The morning the valley broke its own rules began, as most mornings did, with Paloma arguing with a goat.

"You cannot eat the ritual candles," she told him. "They are consecrated."

The goat, whose name was Turnip because her father had named all their animals after foods he wasn't allowed to have during fasting weeks, regarded her with the flat contempt of his kind and ate the candle anyway.

"Fine," said Paloma. "Burn from the inside. See if I care."

It was a good morning, right up until it wasn't. She had slept — actually slept, not the thin watchful doze of the past two nights since the voice in the mist had answered her prayer. She had woken expecting to feel foolish about the whole thing, the way one feels foolish about a dream where one's grandmother gives financial advice. Instead she felt something worse: awake. The world had a texture to it that it hadn't possessed before, like a blanket someone had secretly re-woven overnight.

She noticed the first wrongness on her walk down to the gate. The blackthorn hedge along the keeper's path was blooming.

Blackthorn bloomed in early spring, grimly, before its leaves came, like a person who answers the door half-dressed. It did not bloom in late autumn, and it certainly did not bloom *enthusiastically*, which is the only word for what it was doing now — every branch crowded white, petals falling so thickly they hissed on the frost like warm rain.

Paloma stood very still and did the arithmetic she had been taught at seven years old, kneeling on this same path while her mother tapped each stone with a hazel wand. *Magic does not follow past the gate. Nothing blooms out of season inside the Hollow. If it does, you will know the seal is failing, and you will tell no one, and you will pray harder.*

She had always privately thought that last instruction was a bit useless. Praying harder seemed like bringing a spoon to put out a house fire.

Still. She knelt, out of habit more than faith, and pressed her palm to the path-stone. It was warm. Stones in late autumn were not warm. Stones were never warm, except the ones in the hearth, and those only earned it honestly.

"Oh," said Paloma, to no one. "Oh, that's not good."

* * *

By noon there were three more impossibilities, each less deniable than the last.

The well water ran sweet instead of mineral-bitter, which would have been lovely if sweet water weren't, according to the old ledgers, a sign of "the near country's breath upon the deeps." A flock of starlings flew in a pattern over the village green that was unmistakably, deliberately, a spiral — the mark of Vessany's second dynasty, which Paloma knew only because Wendell had drawn it in the dirt two nights ago while explaining, with the air of a man describing an embarrassing relative, how that dynasty had fallen.

And then there was the lamb.

She heard about it from Tam Broadhollow, who came pounding up the keeper's path red-faced and hatless, which among the valley folk was roughly equivalent to arriving naked and screaming.

"Widow Marsh's lamb," he gasped. "The dead one. From Tuesday."

"What about it?"

"It's standing up, Keeper." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Standing up and looking at people. Not eating. Just — standing. And looking. Widow Marsh won't go near the pen. She says it has her husband's eyes."

"That's absurd," said Paloma. "Lambs don't have anyone's eyes but other lambs'."

"I know that," said Tam, with dignity. "But try telling her. She's throwing shoes at it."

Paloma went. Of course she went; that was the job. Keeper of the Hollow Gate meant being the person who went, whether the crisis was a broken seal-stone or a widow hurling footwear at a resurrected sheep.

The lamb did, admittedly, look at her strangely. There was a quality of attention in its flat dark gaze that lambs generally reserved for bottles of milk. It stood in the straw with the patient stillness of something waiting for instructions, and when Paloma murmured the old calming-words her mother had taught her — words that had never once done anything, that were pure ceremony, comfort-shaped sounds — the lamb lay down.

The whole yard went quiet. Widow Marsh lowered her third shoe.

"Keeper," she said slowly, "those words. My gran used to say them. Said they were Vessany words, from before."

"They're just old words," said Paloma, hearing how unconvincing she sounded. "Ceremony. Habit."

"Mm," said Widow Marsh, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later, which was the most dangerous thing a valley woman could do with information.

* * *

The elders convened that evening in the meeting-house, by lantern light, with the door barred against the unseasonable warmth outside. Paloma sat at the head of the table because the keeper always did, though tonight it felt less like a seat of honor and more like the dock.

"The law is clear," said Elder Corren, who was seventy, deaf in one ear, and made up for it by speaking at a volume suitable for both ears and several neighbors. "A stranger found within the Hollow must be turned away at the gate. If he will not turn, he must be killed. That is the law your mother kept, and her mother before her, back to the First Keeping."

"The law was written," Paloma said carefully, "for a time when magic could not cross the threshold. When a stranger was just a stranger. Cold, hungry, ordinary."

"And is this one ordinary?" Corren leaned forward. "The night he came, the mist spoke. The next day, blackthorn blooms. Today, a dead lamb stands up wearing a dead man's expression. You'll forgive me, Keeper, if I decline to offer him soup."

"He hasn't done anything," said Paloma. "He's sick. Dying, maybe. He walked out of the mist with nothing but memories and a coat full of holes."

"Then turn him out mercifully," said Elder Sennet, gentler than Corren but no softer underneath. "Let him die beyond the edge, where his dying can't — *leak*." She said the word reluctantly, as if it had arrived in the valley without papers, like everything else lately.

Paloma looked around the table. Nine faces, lantern-lit, frightened in the specific way of people who had inherited their fear along with their furniture and had never thought to question either. She loved these people. She had grown up on their bread and their gossip and their stubborn, sensible kindness. And she understood, truly, why they wanted the stranger gone: because the alternative was admitting that everything the valley had built its peace upon — the silence, the rituals, the careful smallness of a life without miracles — might be ending.

But she also saw, with terrible clarity, a man lying under her roof wrapped in every blanket she owned, shivering, apologizing between shivers for the inconvenience, saying things like *I'm sorry, I know I'm heavy, a hundred years of memories sits poorly on a body.*

"He's not leaving," Paloma heard herself say.

Corren's eyebrows climbed toward his bald crown. "Keeper—"

"No. Listen to me." Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "You want me to enforce the law. Fine. Here is my enforcement: the law says strangers at the gate are turned away or killed. This man is not at the gate. He is in my house, under my keeping, and the oldest law of all — older than the First Keeping, older than your grandfathers' grandfathers — is that a keeper shelters what crosses her threshold. Hospitality. Bread and salt. Even the Vessany kept that law, and we learned everything cruel we know from fighting them."

"That's sophistry," said Sennet.

"It's *lawyering*," Paloma corrected. "There's a difference. Sophistry is when you're wrong cleverly."

Someone at the far end of the table — young Maren, the miller's daughter, who mostly attended elder meetings for the biscuits — snorted into her sleeve.

Corren's jaw worked. "You're hiding something, Keeper. I've known you since you were in leading-strings. You've got your mother's face when she lied about the state of the jam stores."

"My mother never lied about the jam."

"She absolutely did. The year of the bad plums, the whole valley ate lies about jam." He sighed, and some of the iron went out of him, replaced by the tiredness of a man who had been old a long time. "We give you three days, Keeper. Three days to decide what you're keeping — the gate, or the man. After that, the valley decides for itself, and I'd not stand in front of what comes up that path if it decides badly."

* * *

She brought him supper pastries stolen from the meeting-house tray, which she felt was a fitting use for elder biscuits.

Wendell was sitting propped against the wall where she'd left him, wrapped in blankets, looking like a manuscript someone had left out in the rain. His eyes lit at the pastries with an eagerness he clearly considered undignified.

"Honey and blackcurrant," he said reverently, after the first bite. "Vessany had a pastry like this. Less honey, more grief. Everything there had more grief in it, food included."

"Eat your grief pastry."

He ate. Outside, through the shutter-cracks, the light was doing something wrong again — too golden for the hour, gilding the frost, and somewhere down-valley a bird was singing a song Paloma didn't recognize, in a key that made her chest ache for reasons she couldn't name.

"They gave me three days," she said.

Wendell stopped chewing. "Three days until what?"

"Until they come for you. Or until I choose, depending on how you look at it." She sat down across from him, elbows on knees. "So I need you to stop editing, Wendell. Whatever it is you keep smoothing over when you tell me things — the parts where you get careful and your voice goes soft — I need all of it. Tonight. Because tomorrow I may be choosing whether to die for you, and I refuse to do it for a curated version."

He looked at her for a long moment. The wrong golden light came through the shutters and lay across his face like something spilled.

"There are things I haven't shown you," he said quietly, "about the gate-keepers. About what your line did, before the unmaking. About what they *helped* do."

Outside, the singing bird fell silent all at once, as if listening.

"Then start at the beginning," said Paloma, "and leave nothing out."
