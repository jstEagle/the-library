## 4. The Thirteenth Moon Rises Thin

Wendell had counted the moons of his dying the way other men counted coins: carefully, and with a growing dread of running out.

He sat on the cold hearthstone of Paloma's cottage — she had not invited him to a chair yet, but she had stopped standing between him and the door, which in her language was nearly an embrace — and he watched the thirteenth moon rise through the window. It came up thin as a paring of fingernail, a sliver of silver with its back turned to them, and it made his chest ache in a way he could not explain to anyone who had not carried a country inside their skull for a hundred years.

"You're doing that thing again," Paloma said from the doorway. She had a shawl around her shoulders and a lamp in her hand, and she looked at him the way she looked at everything: as though it might need correcting. "The thing where you stare at nothing and your face does arithmetic."

"It's not nothing," Wendell said. "It's Vessany."

"Through the window?"

"Through everything." He patted the hearthstone beside him. She considered this invitation at length, the way she considered all invitations, then sat — at the far end, within easy reach of both the door and the fire poker. He had come to find this charming. "Look at the moon. Properly look."

"I've seen the moon before."

"Not like this one. Count its turns since I walked out of the mist."

Her lips moved faintly. He loved that about her too — that she counted things, that she trusted numbers more than people, because numbers had never lied to her. People had; the histories had; the whole tidy inheritance of the valley had.

"Three," she said. "Nearly four."

"Mm." Wendell held out his hand, palm up, and let her see what lay in it: a small silver disc, worn smooth, marked with thirteen notches. Twelve of them were filled in with something dark. "This is my life expectancy. Also my country's. Also, if we're being honest about it, yours."

Paloma did not take it. She looked at it the way she'd looked at the dead lamb standing up in its pen, three nights ago — with horror held very still behind her eyes, like water behind a dam. "Explain it plainly. I'm a keeper, not a scholar."

"You're both. You just weren't told the second half." He closed his fingers over the disc. "Then plainly. When Vessany was unmade, it didn't stop existing. It stopped *arriving*. Everything in it — the streets, the rivers, the people, the gods — was pushed back through the Hollow Gate and held there, on the far side, in the place that isn't anywhere. And something had to hold the door shut from the outside. That's you. Your line. A hundred years of Sterling hands on Sterling stone."

"And you?"

"I'm what holds it shut from the *inside*." He smiled, and knew it was a poor smile. "A memory-keeper carries the country so it doesn't fray. Every street I can name stays named. Every song I remember keeps its tune. The gods sleep as long as someone remembers them sleeping. I am, in the most literal sense available, the last wall between your valley and everything your ancestors locked away."

The fire popped. Paloma's eyes went to the window, to the thin moon, then back. "And when you die—"

"The country forgets itself. And a country that forgets itself stops waiting politely to be let in. It comes through." He tapped the disc through his closed fist. "But I'm not dead yet, so here's the interesting part. My memory isn't holding the way it used to. I'm old, Paloma. Older than I look, and I look like a man who argues with geese. Each moon turn, a piece of Vessany slips past me and pools against the gate. First turn, it was small things. The smell of bread I haven't baked in a century. Second turn, a street name. This turn—" he gestured vaguely at the window, at the world beyond it, where somewhere a lamb that should be dead was chewing grass with indecent vigor, and flowers that should be frost-killed were opening their faces to a winter sky, "—this turn, it's started being *alive*."

"That's what the elders are calling an abomination."

"The elders are right to be frightened and wrong about everything else, which is traditional." He said it gently, and saw her mouth twitch despite herself. Good. He needed her mouth to keep wanting to twitch. There were hard months ahead, and humor was the only rope bridge across them. "Here is the arithmetic your face was asking after. Thirteen turns of the moon from the night I walked out of the mist. On the thirteenth, when it wanes full — not waxes, *wanes*, the country has always preferred doors that close rather than open — the last of me goes out like a candle. And everything I've been holding comes home. All of it."

"All of it," she repeated quietly.

"The cities. The wars. The kings who thought a god could be edited. The gods themselves, waking mid-scream, with no idea a hundred years have passed." He watched her absorb it. She absorbed things the way stone absorbs rain — slowly, completely, and with visible weight added. "Your valley sits at the drain-hole, Keeper. When the tub empties, you'll be standing in it."

* * *

She did not sleep, and neither did he, and so they ended up by the fire again in the smallest hours, and it was there — because grief always wants a task, and teaching was the only task he had left — that Wendell began to teach her the songs.

"This one is a counting song," he said. "Children sang it skipping rope in Sennet's Wake. You'll want to hate it. Everyone does, by the fourth verse."

He sang. His voice was a ruin, cracked and thin, but the tune came out whole, because tunes were the one cargo he had never once dropped. It was a silly song about a fishmonger's wife who kept losing count of her eels, and the numbers went wrong on purpose, and by the third verse Paloma — stern, dutiful, poker-within-reach Paloma — was frowning at herself because her foot was tapping.

"Why teach me this?" she asked, when it ended. "If it's all coming back anyway."

"Because what comes back will be *my* Vessany. The one I managed to hold onto. And I have held it alone for a hundred years, and alone is a kind of crookedness." He fed a stick to the fire. "A country remembered by one man is a country seen through one window. I want it remembered by two. If I get a verse wrong — and I have, Paloma, I know I have, there are songs where I can feel the wrongness sitting in them like a stone in a shoe — I need someone else's ear to catch it. Someone who never heard the original. Someone whose silence will notice."

"That's a terrible plan. My silence notices everything."

"Yes," he said warmly. "That's why I chose you."

She gave him a long look over the fire. Then, grudgingly, in a voice like a woman confessing to theft, she sang the second verse back to him. Her voice was low and true and entirely unadorned, and when she reached the part where the eels became seven instead of nine, she corrected it without being told, because Paloma Sterling could not abide a wrong number even in a nonsense song.

Wendell laughed until his ribs hurt, which was either joy or the leaking, and these days he honestly could not tell the difference.

They worked until grey light. He taught her the harvest rounds and the lament for the drowned bell of Coriel, and the song the gate-keepers' own order used to sing at the turning of the year — at which she went quiet, because no one had ever told her gate-keepers had an order, or a song, or a history older than the gate itself.

"Wendell." She stopped in the middle of the lament, her hands folded tight. "When the thirteenth moon wanes. The tyrants you mentioned. The ones who caused the unmaking. Are they held on the far side too? With everyone else?"

He should have lied. He was good at lying; he'd had a century of practice, and most of it had been kindly meant. But she was looking at him with her silence noticing everything, and he found the lie would not sit in his mouth.

"They're held," he said. "Sleeping, like the rest. Waking, like the rest. Coming home, like the rest."

"Then sealing the gate harder won't save anything."

"No," he agreed softly. "It never could. Your ancestors knew that. It's why they chose the door they chose." He heard his own words and winced inwardly — *the door they chose*, as though it had been a simple purchase, a matter of taste — but Paloma was looking at the thin moon through the window, and did not catch it.

Outside, the first birds were starting, and one of them — he was almost sure — was singing a Vessany dawn-round that he had never taught it.

Twelve notches filled. One empty. And the moon above the valley rising thinner each night, like a door being pared down to its hinge.
