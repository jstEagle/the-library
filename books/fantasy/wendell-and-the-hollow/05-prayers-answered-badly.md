## 5. Prayers Answered Badly

The trouble began, as most trouble in the valley did, with the Widow Marsh.

Paloma had known her all her life — a small, iron-spined woman who sold honey at market and had not spoken her husband's name aloud in eleven years, because speaking it hurt, and because there was no one left to answer anyway. That was how things worked in the valley. You kept your prayers small and your griefs smaller, and you certainly didn't say dead men's names into the dark.

So when Paloma came down the hill on market morning and found a crowd gathered outside the Marsh house, silent in that particular way crowds are silent when they are afraid, she knew before she reached the gate.

He was standing in the dooryard. Tam Marsh, dead these eleven years — drowned in the millrace, pulled out gray and swollen while his wife held his boot like it might still hold him. He was standing in the dooryard wearing the same coat he'd drowned in, dry now, whole now, smiling at the widow with a smile that was almost right. Almost.

"Tam," the Widow Marsh said, from the doorway, in a voice like a woman stepping onto ice. "You've been gone."

"Have I?" he said pleasantly. "I don't remember gone. I remember you asked me to come home. So I came home."

And that was the thing, Paloma thought, pushing through the crowd with her heart going cold and quick. He *had* come home. Faithfully. Exactly as asked. The prayer was eleven years old and spoken once, half to herself, over a grave: *come back to me.* And something had heard it at last, and something had answered, and the answer had no idea what death was, or what eleven years were, or why a man should not be stitched back out of water and wish.

"Paloma Sterling." The Widow Marsh saw her and grabbed her wrist with fingers like bird claws. "You keep the Gate. You tell me what this is."

What it was, was magic. Magic in the valley, where magic could not follow — except that it plainly could now, seeping under the door of the world like meltwater under a sill. Paloma looked at Tam Marsh's face, which was her neighbor's face, and found she couldn't say any of it.

"I'll look into it," she said, which was what keepers said, and had always been a way of saying *nothing can be done.*

Behind her, hidden up at the cottage, Wendell would know. Wendell, who remembered Vessany better than its own ghosts did — and here, apparently, were the ghosts to prove it.

* * *

"You prayed," Wendell said that evening, pacing the length of her kitchen like a caged heron. He'd taken to pacing lately; he said his head felt crowded, though he wouldn't say crowded with what. "Someone prayed, and it answered. Do you understand what that means? For a hundred years nothing in Vessany has heard so much as a cough. The gods went quiet when the country was unmade. Quiet as stones. And now—"

"Now it's answering prayers it doesn't understand."

"It's answering them *perfectly*," Wendell said, and stopped pacing, and looked at her with an expression she was beginning to dread, the one that meant he was about to be right about something awful. "That's the horror of it, Paloma. It isn't careless. It's faithful. A god that answers exactly what is asked, word for word, without knowing what words cost. Like a child reciting a blessing it memorized before the war."

"Before the war," Paloma repeated. "So it's old. It remembers the old prayers."

"It remembers everything you people ever asked it for and nothing about what happened after." He sat down heavily across from her, and for a moment he just looked tired — tired the way only a man carrying a hundred years of someone else's memories can look. "A god waking up mid-sentence. Reaching for the last thing it heard."

The last thing it heard. Paloma turned her cup slowly on the table. Her own prayer, three weeks ago, spoken aloud for the first time in years — *let me not be alone in this* — and answered by a voice in the mist, and then by a man made of yesterday walking out of it. She had thought herself chosen. Now she wondered if she'd simply been the nearest open ear.

"Wendell. The child's wish. Have you heard?"

His face went carefully blank. "Tell me."

"Little Sorrel Fenn, up past the orchards. Wished for snow. Children wish for snow; it's practically a law of nature." Paloma's mouth was dry. "It snowed last night. In high summer. Just on their farm, just on their fields. Six inches of it, fine and clean and beautiful, and every green thing under it is black this morning. The barley. The beans. The Fenns' whole year, killed in one night, and Sorrel stood in the middle of it crying because she only wanted to see it fall."

Wendell closed his eyes. "Faithful," he said quietly. "Blindly faithful. Mercy without judgment."

"And the valley will want someone to blame."

"The valley will want *you*," Wendell said. "You're the keeper. Keepers are for blaming. It's practically in the job description."

Despite everything, Paloma snorted. "You've met my elders, have you?"

"I've met a hundred years of elders," he said. "They're all the same elder, really. Somewhere under the different hats."

* * *

The elder under the hat this time was Maren Ostler, who ran the valley council the way a millstone runs grain — thoroughly, and without much interest in what got crushed. She summoned Paloma to the meeting house two days later, and Paloma went, leaving Wendell behind with strict instructions to be invisible, which he took to mean reading her father's old almanac by the fire and asking questions about crop rotation.

The meeting house smelled of tallow and fear. Every farmstead had sent someone. They sat in rows like a jury that hadn't decided yet whether the accused was Paloma or the world.

"The Marsh woman's husband," Maren said, without preamble. "Is it him? Truly?"

"He has Tam Marsh's face and voice and memories up to the day he drowned," Paloma said. "Beyond that I can't say. None of us can."

"And the frost."

"Was not my doing, if that's the question underneath the question."

Maren's eyes narrowed. "Then whose?"

*A god's,* Paloma did not say. *An old god of a country that no longer exists, waking up blind and eager and answering a century of stored-up wishes like a well overflowing.* What she said was: "Something is coming through the Gate. Something that grants what's asked of it. And I'm asking you — I am *begging* you — to stop praying. All of you. Whatever you've whispered into your pillows, whatever you've wished on stars, unsay it. Because it's listening now, and it does not know what mercy costs."

Silence. Then, from the back, old Hobb Tanner: "My knee stopped aching Tuesday. Forty years it ached. I thanked Saint Aldery for it." He paused. "Wasn't Saint Aldery, was it."

"No," Paloma said. "I don't think it was."

Another silence, longer, and in it Paloma could feel the terrible arithmetic happening all around the room. Because a healed knee was a healed knee. A husband home was a husband home. People were already doing it — she'd seen it at market, the sidelong glances, the lips moving over old grievances. *Fix my roof. Bring my son back from the coast. Let me win at cards.* A hundred years of small stored-up wants, and something patient and loving and utterly without sense beginning to pay them out one by one.

"They won't stop," said a quiet voice near the front — Tamsin Fenn, Sorrel's mother, whose fields lay black under summer snow. Her face was gray with grief but steady. "You know they won't. Half of them are already praying harder. Why wouldn't they? It *works.*"

"It works the way a flood works," Paloma said.

"A flood doesn't bring back husbands," someone muttered, and heads nodded, and Paloma felt the room tilt away from her — toward the wishing, toward the warm easy miracle of it, away from the cold arithmetic of what each miracle ate.

Maren raised a hand and the room stilled. "Keeper. The old law says the Gate must hold. It says anything that comes through is turned away, and if it cannot be turned—" she let the pause do its work, "—dealt with. There are those among us asking whether the Marsh man ought to be dealt with. Whether whatever's granting these wishes ought to be dealt with. And whether the keeper who's been seen talking to strangers at the Gate ought to explain herself first."

There it was. Paloma kept her face still and her hands loose on the table, and thought of Wendell by her fire asking earnest questions about barley, and said, "I'll deal with what comes through the Gate. That's my charge. As for explaining myself — when I have something to explain, Maren, you'll be the second to know."

"Second?"

"After me."

A few people laughed, briefly, the way people laugh when they're frightened and grateful for the excuse. But Maren didn't laugh, and Tamsin Fenn didn't laugh, and Paloma walked home under a sky so clear and blue it felt like a lie.

* * *

Wendell was waiting, and he knew before she spoke — she could see it in the way he'd set down the almanac and squared his shoulders, a man bracing for weather.

"Well?" he said.

"She suspects you. Half of them suspect me. And they're starting to pray on purpose, Wendell. Not little wishes — *requests.* One of the Coopers asked it for rain on his own field and no one else's. It obliged. His neighbor's field burned." She dropped into her chair. "It's going to get worse. Every answered prayer teaches them to ask bigger."

"Yes." Wendell stood at the window, looking down the valley toward the Gate, and when he turned around his face had gone strange — not afraid, exactly. Grieved ahead of schedule. "Paloma, there's something I need to tell you, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything."

"That sounds ominous."

"It is." He crossed the room and knelt — actually knelt, which alarmed her more than anything else he might have done — and looked up at her with those eyes that held too many funerals. "The god answering your valley. It isn't leaking through by accident. It's following a path. Old paths, laid before the unmaking, and they all run through one place. There's a Door, Paloma. Not your Gate — older than your Gate, deeper, the true threshold. And it lies beneath the Unmade Court, in the heart of what's coming back."

Paloma sat very still. Outside, the evening light lay gold and gentle on a valley full of blackened fields and walking dead men, and somewhere in it, she knew, dozens of lips were moving, whispering their wants into the willing dark.

"And you've known this," she said carefully, "for how long?"

"Long enough to be ashamed of the waiting," Wendell said. "Will you come with me?"

She looked at him a long moment — this impossible man made of yesterday, kneeling on her kitchen floor, offering her the one road the valley's law could not follow her down.

"Get up off my floor," she said, "and start talking."

But even as she said it, even as warmth crept back into the room, Paloma heard it — faint, from down the lane, from the direction of the Marsh house — the sound of the Widow Marsh weeping, and beneath the weeping, low and contented, Tam Marsh's voice saying over and over, gentle as a lullaby:

"You asked me to come home. You asked me to come home. You asked me to come home."
