## 7. Border of the Unmade

The gate behind us had never looked so small.

Paloma stood at the edge of her valley with her pack on her back and her keeper's iron in her hand, and she made herself not look back at it. If she looked back, she would think of the elders' faces when they'd found her empty house and cold hearth. If she looked back, she would think of the law, which said what happened to keepers who abandoned their post, and it was not a gentle thing to think about before breakfast.

"You're doing the thing," Wendell said beside her. "The looking-back-adjacent thing. Your shoulders do it."

"My shoulders are my own business."

"They're very expressive shoulders. In Vessany we had a saying: a woman's shoulders tell the truth her mouth is too polite for."

"Did you have a saying for men who talk too much at dawn?"

"Hundreds." He smiled, and the mist ahead of them seemed to lean toward the smile like a plant toward light. "Mostly unflattering. Shall we?"

She shouldered past him — expressively — and stepped over the boundary stone.

She felt it happen. That was the worst part. Not a wall, not a fall, just a sensation like walking through a curtain of cold water, except the water was *elsewhere*, and when she came out the other side the air tasted of things that had no business existing: woodsmoke from a fire lit a hundred years ago, river water that had never touched this valley's stones, and underneath it all something sweet and green and old, like the smell of a book opened for the first time in a century.

"Oh," she said.

"I know," Wendell said softly.

"It smells like—" She stopped, because she didn't have the word. She had never had the word. Her whole life she'd kept a gate against a country whose name was on no map, and now the country was breathing on her face.

"Home," Wendell supplied. "It smells like home. Though I'll grant I'm biased. I've been carrying it around in my head for a hundred years; it's had time to improve."

The road under their feet was real where Wendell walked on it. Paloma noticed this within ten steps, because her own boots kept finding different degrees of ground. Where he trod, the cobbles were solid, worn smooth, honest. A pace to either side, the road thinned into suggestion — a gray shimmer, a rumor of paving — and beyond that, the hills of her own valley showed through like bones through skin.

"The streets exist when they're remembered," Wendell said, watching her watch the road fail around him. "I'm sorry. I should have warned you it would feel strange underfoot. Most visitors find it—"

"There are no most visitors, Wendell. There's me. And I'm finding it like walking on a liar's promise."

He winced. "A fair description. Try to stay close to me. The road holds better near a memory-keeper. It's rather the whole point of me."

So she stayed close, and the road held, and Vessany assembled itself around them piece by piece as Wendell remembered it into being.

It was the most beautiful thing Paloma had ever seen, and she resented every second of it.

Lamplit streets built themselves out of nothing as they passed — first the glow, then the posts holding the lamps, then the cobbles drinking the light. Houses rose in rows with their shutters half-open, laundry strung between windows that existed only because Wendell recalled exactly which widow had hung which sheet. A fountain stood in a square, dry until he glanced at it, then running, silver and loud, startling a bird that may or may not have been a bird.

And the people.

They came out of doorways and down lanes, and they were not ghosts, whatever the old stories said ghosts were. They were more than ghosts and less than people. A baker nodded to Wendell from his doorway, flour to his elbows, and his smile was warm and his eyes were distant, like a man greeting a friend through deep water. Children chased a hoop along a street that only existed while they ran on it, laughing sounds that arrived a half-second after their mouths moved.

"They don't see us properly," Paloma murmured.

"They see what they hope to see. They've been waiting a hundred years without knowing they were waiting. You'd be a little unfocused too." Wendell lifted a hand to the baker. "Morning, Tam!"

"Morning, Keeper!" the baker called back, cheerful and hollow, like a bell heard through a wall.

Paloma stopped walking. "Keeper," she repeated. "They know you."

"Everyone knows the memory-keeper. That was rather the job."

"No — Wendell." She turned to face him fully, and the street dimmed slightly where her attention left it, which was somehow worse. "You said you were the last one. You said you survived by carrying everything. But these people greet you like you never left. Like you were here yesterday. How long were you here, Wendell? Before the unmaking? What were you *to* them?"

"A friend," he said, too quickly. "I was a friend, and I'm tired, and there's a long way yet to the Court. Can questions wait until—"

"No," Paloma said pleasantly. "Questions can't. Questions are the entire reason I abandoned my post, broke my oath, and made my shoulders very expressive indeed."

* * *

They argued their way up a hill that remembered being terraced with vineyards, and lost the argument to exhaustion somewhere near the top, and made camp in an inn that Wendell remembered into standing — three walls, a roof, a hearth that burned without wood because he recalled the shape of its burning. The innkeeper, a broad woman named Maret who looked through them both like weather, set out bread and cheese that tasted almost entirely real if you didn't think about it too hard.

Paloma thought about it extremely hard, out of spite, while Wendell spread a map across the table. The map was drawn on paper that flickered at the edges, and it showed Vessany whole: rivers, roads, cities with names like bells.

"The Unmade Court is here." His finger landed on a city at the map's heart, drawn in ink darker than the rest. "Two days' walk, if the roads remember themselves kindly. The Door is beneath it. The original threshold — the one your gate was copied from."

"Copied," Paloma said. "That's an interesting word for it."

"It's an accurate word for it." He smiled his distracting smile. "Your valley's gate is a lock. The Door is the hinge. Locks can be mended anywhere. The hinge is where it's actually—"

"Wendell."

"Mm?"

"Why did the gate-keepers help build the lock?"

The hearthfire stuttered. Just for a moment — a hiccup in the flame, like a skipped heartbeat — and Wendell's finger went still on the map.

"What makes you ask that?"

"Because today a baker greeted you by title. Because Maret set two places without being asked, though she can't properly see us. Because every soul in this country treats you like family, and family keeps *stories*, Wendell, and you've been feeding me stories for weeks now like a careful cook trimming the fat." She leaned forward. "I'm not stupid. I'm a keeper. Keeping things is what I do, and I know curated goods when I see them. So tell me what you've been leaving out."

For a long moment he said nothing, and the inn around them grew quieter, as if the building itself were leaning in. Then he began rolling up the map with great care, as though tidiness could postpone truth.

"The roads are dangerous past the vineyards," he said. "We should start early."

"Wendell."

"There are things in the borderlands that aren't citizens. Remnants. Things the unmaking left behind, angry and unfinished. I'll protect you as best I—"

"*Wendell.*"

He looked up. And Paloma watched something happen in his face that she had seen once before, years ago, in a mirror, the night she'd first lied to an elder to protect a stranger at her gate: the exact moment loyalty loses to honesty, and knows it's losing, and surrenders anyway.

"There was a betrayal," he said quietly.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Somewhere below them, in a cellar that existed only because he remembered it, something seemed to echo.

"The gate-keepers of old," Wendell went on, each word measured out like medicine, "were not what the songs say. They weren't guardians who fought to the last. They were—" His jaw worked. "They were promised something. By the other side. By the ones who unmade us. And they took the promise, and they opened the way from inside, and my country died believing its own keepers had betrayed it."

The hearth had gone quite still. Even the fire remembered only silence.

"And my line?" Paloma asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "My line of keepers. The Sterlings. The ones who've guarded the gate ever since, generation after generation, praying to silent gods."

Wendell closed his eyes.

"Paloma—"

"*What did my line take?*"

When he opened his eyes again, he couldn't hold hers, and outside the remembered window, in the darkening streets of a dead country, the half-ghost citizens had come out of their houses and were standing in the lamplight, all of them, every one, facing uphill toward the little inn that shouldn't exist.

They were looking at her.

"Paloma," Wendell said, very gently, "they know whose blood you carry. And I think — God forgive me, I think they've been waiting a hundred years to ask you the same question."
