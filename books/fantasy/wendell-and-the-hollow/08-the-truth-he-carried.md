## 8. The Truth He Carried

Wendell had carried ten thousand memories of Vessany inside his skull for a hundred years, and he could tell you the exact weight of each one if you asked — the smell of the tanneries in Lower Sable, the sound of the bell at Marrowbridge that rang flat because a swallow had nested in it and no one had the heart to move her, the taste of honeyed pear wine that his mother watered for him when he was small enough to sit on counters. He had carried them all faithfully. He had carried them so long that they had worn grooves in him, like water wearing stone, until there was very little Wendell left and mostly Vessany.

And in all that time, in all those grooves, there was one memory he had never once let surface where Paloma Sterling could see it.

She was standing across the fire from him now, and she had asked.

They had made camp in the lee of a collapsed aqueduct, three days into the Unmade country, where the world flickered like a candle in a draft. Paloma had her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way she had — the way of a woman who had spent her whole life being lied to by silence and had finally learned to hear it.

"The gate-keepers," she said again. "In the memory you showed me yesterday. The one with the procession to the Hollow Gate. You cut it short. I felt it end too soon, the way a song ends wrong. What came after?"

"Paloma."

"Don't." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. She never raised her voice; she simply stood in front of you like a door you couldn't get past. "You've been editing since the border. I've been counting. The tanner's quarter — you skipped it entirely. The names on the Court of Records — you walked me past them fast, like a man hurrying past a grave he dug himself. And now this. Tell me what came after the procession, Wendell."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the half-real dark, a streetlamp that hadn't existed an hour ago buzzed and guttered, remembering itself.

He thought about lying. It would have been easy. It had always been easy; that was the whole trouble with being a memory-keeper. You didn't just keep memories. You kept *versions* of them. You decided what order people saw things in, what light fell across them, what was left in shadow. For a hundred years he had told himself that curation was kindness. That the truth about Vessany's ending was a heavy thing, and Paloma already carried enough heavy things.

But she was looking at him the way she looked at prayers answered badly — with that terrible, patient grief — and Wendell understood, all at once and completely, that every kind lie he had ever told her had been a small unmaking of its own.

"What came after," he said, "is that your ancestors were not heroes."

* * *

He sat down. His knees had opinions these days; dying did that to a man. He held his hands out to the fire because they were shaking and he wanted, selfishly, to blame the cold.

"You know the story your valley tells," he said. "The god-war came, and the gate-keepers stood against it, and Vessany was lost despite their courage, and the last keeper sealed the Hollow Gate with her own two hands to save the world from what was inside. Yes?"

"Yes," Paloma said. "That's the liturgy. I've said it every dawn of my life."

"It's a lovely liturgy." He smiled, and hated how thin the smile came out. "I helped write parts of it, actually. Before the end. We were all very good at writing lovely things. It was practically a national industry."

"Wendell."

"The war wasn't gods fighting each other." He made himself look at her. "Not at first. It was gods fighting *us*. Or rather — it was us fighting over them. Two factions, the Sunsworn and the Deep-Rooted, each certain their god alone should speak for Vessany. And the gods, poor blind things, answered whoever prayed loudest. You've seen what answering without judgment does. The frost on the barley. The widow's husband, come back wrong. Now imagine that at the scale of a nation. Imagine twenty years of it. Cities praying themselves into ruin. Children conscripted into prayer-houses. Rivers that ran backward because someone wished hard enough and no one wise enough said no."

The firelight moved on her face. She hadn't sat down. Keepers of gates, he thought, don't sit while they're being told something.

"So the gate-keepers met," he went on. "All twelve lines, in the Unmade Court, before it was unmade. And they made a decision. Not to fight the war. To *end* it. And the only way to end a war fought through gods who answer everything..."

"No," Paloma said quietly.

"...was to take away everyone the gods had to answer *through*." He nodded. "The gate-keepers didn't fail to save Vessany, Paloma. They unmade it. Deliberately. With rites we have spent a century pretending were lost. They folded the country up like a letter and slid it behind the Hollow Gate, gods and tyrants and tanneries and all, and they did it to stop the dying. And it worked. It worked beautifully. No more war. No more prayers answered blindly. A hundred years of quiet."

"And my line?" Her voice was still level. Still door-steady. "The Sterlings. The first keepers. The ones the liturgy says—"

"Your great-great-grandmother drew the first seal." He said it plainly, because softening it now would be the final cruelty. "Ilse Sterling. She didn't stand at the gate and hold it against the dark. She *built* the dark. She chose which prayers would go unanswered forever, and she signed the rite, and she taught her daughter to tend the lock as though it were a wound instead of what it actually was."

"Which is?"

"A door," Wendell said, "that her family locked from the outside, on a country full of people who were still alive when it closed."

* * *

The silence after that lasted a long time. Out in the dark, the half-made city went on remembering itself — a shutter banging, a fountain starting up somewhere, the ghost of a market bell. Vessany, leaking back into the world one remembered detail at a time, indifferent to confessions.

Paloma sat down at last. Slowly, the way people sit when their legs have stopped consulting them.

"All those dawns," she said. "The liturgy. The vigil. My mother teaching me the seals before I could write my own name. She used to say we were the wall between the world and the storm." She laughed — one note, no humor in it at all. "We weren't the wall. We were the ones who made the storm necessary."

"Yes."

"And you knew. All this time. You walked out of the mist, you let me hide you from the elders, you taught me the songs—" Her voice finally cracked, just once, and she pressed her lips together until it steadied. "You taught me the harvest songs, Wendell. You watched me sing them like a woman honoring martyrs."

"I did." He didn't flinch away from it. He owed her that much — to be looked at while she hated him. "And every time, I told myself: not yet. She needs to trust me first. She needs to be strong enough. She needs—" He stopped. Shook his head. "No. Those were excuses dressed in better clothes. The truth is I loved having one person who believed the beautiful version. After a century alone with the ugly one, I wanted — I *needed* — someone to share the liturgy with. Your faith was the warmest thing I'd touched in a hundred years, and I let it stay warm by keeping the truth out of it. Loyalty, I called it. It was cowardice wearing loyalty's coat."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "Your loyalty was to Vessany. To its memory. I understand keeping secrets for a country."

"My loyalty was to *you*, you stubborn woman, and that's precisely the problem." He leaned forward, firelight catching the deep lines of his face. "A memory-keeper's first vow is truth. Every memory, whole, in its proper light. I broke that vow the day I met you and curated our friendship. And here's the joke of it, the bitter little joke: I survived the unmaking of a nation by refusing to forget a single thing, and then I spent a year carefully helping the last gate-keeper forget the most important one."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone somewhere he hadn't heard before — past anger, past grief, into some cold clear country underneath both.

"The elders' law says strangers at the gate must be turned away or killed. I broke that law for you. My whole inheritance says my line died defending the world from Vessany. You're telling me my line *murdered* Vessany to defend the world." She looked up. "So tell me the rest, Wendell. All of it, in its proper light, as your vow demands. If the gate-keepers did the unmaking — who gave them the right? Who *asked* Vessany?"

And Wendell felt the last locked room in his chest swing open, because that was the question, wasn't it? The one he had dodged for a hundred years even inside his own head. The one that made Ilse Sterling's signature on the rite not tragedy but something closer to crime.

"No one asked," he said. "Twelve keeper-lines voted. Eleven million people were never consulted. And the worst part — the part I've hidden even from myself, until you made me say it aloud — is that many of them *would have said yes*. Given the choice. Given the truth. But the keepers never gave them the choice. They decided that a hundred years of silence was better than a war, and they never once considered that silence might be a kind of death too." He spread his hands. "They took justice into their own hands and called it law, because there was no law left that reached high enough. Does that sound familiar, Paloma? Because it should. It's exactly the choice waiting for you at the Door."

Paloma rose. She stood over the fire with the light climbing her face, and Wendell saw something settle in her — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever — but purpose, arriving like a blade finding its balance.

"Then tomorrow we walk faster," she said. "Because I'm going to give them the choice my ancestors didn't."

She rolled into her blanket with her back to him, and the fire burned low, and Wendell lay awake under the flickering stars of a country that wasn't there, lighter than he had felt in a hundred years and more afraid than he had ever been — because the truth was out at last, and the truth was a door too, and he had no idea what would come through it in the morning.
