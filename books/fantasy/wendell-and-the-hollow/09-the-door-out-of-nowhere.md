## 9. The Door Out of Nowhere

The Unmade Court had been a palace once. Paloma could tell because palaces were the only buildings foolish enough to have this many doors.

They stood in a hall the size of a cathedral and a wheat field at once — the proportions kept shifting when she wasn't looking directly at them, like a dog trying to fit through a gap it had already decided it could fit through. Columns rose into a ceiling that was either painted with stars or was stars, depending on how honestly you squinted. The floor was white marble shot through with veins of gold, except where it was packed earth, except where it was the grey ash of a country that had been told, very firmly, to stop existing.

And in the center of it all: the Door.

Not her gate. Her gate — the Hollow Gate she'd tended for nineteen years with oil and salt and half-believed prayers — was a child's drawing of this. This Door was tall as a cliff face and made of something that wasn't wood or stone but *decision*, dark and grained and warm to look at, the way a hearth is warm before you've lit it. It stood ajar by exactly one hand's width, and through that gap Paloma could see morning.

Not valley-morning. A different morning. One with two suns low on the horizon and birds flying in shapes too deliberate to be accidental.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated how small her voice came out.

"It's Vessany," Wendell said beside her, "which is the same thing, if you ask me, though I admit I'm biased. I'm biased about everything. It's my whole personality."

She almost laughed. That was the thing about Wendell — even now, walking into the belly of a waking god with his memory-keeper's coat fraying off him like autumn leaves, he could put a crooked grin on catastrophe. But his hands were shaking. She'd noticed it three miles back and hadn't said anything, because some mercies are just lies with better posture.

"Stay behind me," she said.

"You're not armed."

"I'm a keeper of the Hollow Gate. I've spent nineteen years being not-armed at things until they went away. It's a whole discipline."

He didn't laugh at that one, which told her more than laughter would have.

* * *

The god did not arrive so much as remember itself into being.

One moment the hall was empty; the next, there was a figure standing where the light from the Door's gap fell longest — a shape like a person, if a person were drawn by someone who had only ever had prayers described to them. It wore robes the color of harvest. Its face was kind the way weather is kind: sincerely, and without any particular interest in what kindness cost.

Paloma knew it. Of course she knew it. She'd been talking to it for months without knowing — every answered prayer, every wrong mercy, every returned husband and killing frost. The voice from the gate. The faithful, blind thing that had heard her loneliness and answered it with a stranger made of yesterday.

"You are the keeper," it said. Its voice filled the hall the way water fills a cup — completely, and taking the shape of its container. "Daughter of Sterling. Daughter of the line that closed me out."

"We need to talk about your customer service," Wendell muttered.

"*Wendell.*"

"I'm dying, Paloma. I get to be rude to gods. It's one of the few perks left."

But the god wasn't looking at Wendell. It was looking at her, and its gaze had weight, like a hand laid gently on the crown of her head.

"I offer the lawful choice," it said. "As I offered your ancestors. Seal the Door. Speak the closing words your blood has carried for a hundred years, and the Door shuts forever. Your valley is saved. Your people sleep safe. The dead of Vessany rest unbothered, and I go back to silence, which I am told is peaceful." A pause. "I would not know. I have never tried it."

Paloma's mouth went dry. "And if I refuse?"

"Then the thirteenth moon wanes full, and everything returns whether anyone wishes it or not. The wars. The tyrants. The god-war that taught your ancestors what closing a door costs." The god tilted its head, and the gesture was horribly human. "Those are the choices. Close it, or lose everything. Your law is very clear. Your law wrote itself in ash."

"That's not true," Paloma said quietly.

The hall went still. Even the shifting proportions stopped shifting, as if the building itself wanted to hear.

"The law says strangers at the gate must be turned away," she said. "Or killed. My elders read that to mean everyone who isn't us is a threat. But I've read the older texts. I hid in the archive for six days while we traveled, Wendell thinks I was sleeping—" 

"I was *worried*—"

"—and the oldest version doesn't say *strangers*. It says *the uninvited*. There's a difference. A stranger can be invited. My ancestors just stopped inviting anyone, because inviting is hard and killing is simple." She lifted her chin. "Your law isn't clear, sir. It's just tired. And tired laws shouldn't get to decide who lives."

The god regarded her for a long moment. Then, terribly, it smiled.

"Good," it said. "You argue. Your ancestors groveled beautifully, but they never argued. Arguing means you think I can be reasoned with. Do you?"

"No," Paloma said honestly. "I think you're answering prayers blind, like a man handing out bread in the dark — generous, and breaking teeth. I don't think you can be reasoned with yet. I think you need witnesses first. Judgment needs someone watching, or it's just reflex with better vocabulary."

"And so?"

"So I'm not going to close the Door." She stepped forward, past Wendell's suddenly clutching hand, past the safe edge of the marble, onto the ash that remembered being a country. "And I'm not going to let it blow open either. I'm going to hold it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about claiming a keeper's authority: it isn't a ceremony. There's no incantation, no glowing sigil, no choir of ancestors rising up to hum approval. There is just a girl standing in front of an impossible Door, saying words no one asked her to say, and meaning them so hard that reality has to renegotiate.

"I am Isandro—" no. Wrong name, wrong story, she caught herself. "I am **Paloma Sterling**, keeper of the Hollow Gate, last of the invited line. And I claim the threshold."

The god's smile faded into something older. "You cannot hold what I hold."

"Watch me."

She put both hands flat against the Door.

It was warm. That undid her a little — she'd braced for cold, for horror, for the touch of a country that had been unmade, and instead it felt like a kitchen door in summer, like the inside of somewhere people had been happy. Through the gap she could smell bread. Actual bread. Somewhere on the other side, someone was baking, because even countries bleeding back from nonexistence still need breakfast.

The Door pulled at her. Not violently — patiently. It wanted open the way a river wants the sea, and it was very willing to wait, and it had a hundred years of patience banked. She felt her arms begin to ache with a weight that wasn't weight. She felt the thirteenth moon overhead, thin as a paring of fingernail, leaning on her shoulders.

"Paloma." Wendell's voice cracked right down the middle. "There has to be another way. Let me carry it — I've carried a whole country in my head, I can carry a door—"

"You're dying, Wendell. If you hold it, the moment you go, it opens anyway. If I hold it—" she gritted her teeth against the strain, "—then it stays held as long as I do. And I intend to be extremely stubborn. Ask anyone. Ask the elders. They have a whole filing system about it."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny. You should see your face. You look like a man watching someone juggle knives who's just realized they're real knives."

"Because they ARE—" He stopped. Breathed. And then, because he was Wendell, because he was the last memory-keeper of Vessany and had made a life's discipline of carrying unbearable things, he stepped up beside her and put one hand on the Door next to hers. Just one. Not holding. Helping.

"Together, then," he said softly. "Like the old keepers should have done. Like they forgot to do."

The god watched them both. And Paloma saw — she would swear this later, under oath, to anyone who asked — the moment the reflex broke. Something moved behind those weather-kind eyes, slow as a season turning. Not understanding, not yet. But the *want* to understand. The first crack of judgment in a hundred years of blind mercy.

"What do you want, keeper?" it asked, and for the first time it sounded like a question rather than a verdict.

"I want you to stop deciding for them," Paloma said, jerking her chin toward the gap, toward the two-sunned morning, toward whatever remained of Vessany's people hovering between return and rest. "Open it wide enough to choose. Not wide enough to fall through. Let them come home if home is what they want. Let them rest if rest is what they need. You've been answering their prayers for months — start asking them questions instead."

"That is not lawful."

"No," Paloma agreed, and pushed, and the Door swung wider by exactly the width of a choice. "It's justice. Different department entirely."

* * *

The change came like dawn — gradually, then all at once.

Through the widening gap, figures began to gather on the far side. Half-ghost, half-hope, the citizens she'd met in the leaking streets of Vessany's edges: the baker whose bread she could smell, the soldier who'd saluted a flag that hadn't flown in a century, the little girl who existed only on Tuesdays. They stood at the threshold of themselves, looking through at her — at the strange keeper holding their country's Door with bleeding hands and a stubborn chin.

And they began, one by one, to decide.

Some stepped forward. Some turned away, gentle as snowfall, toward whatever rest waited for the finished. Neither side looked afraid. That was the part that made Paloma cry, though she'd deny it: nobody looked afraid. For the first time since the unmaking, somebody had asked them instead of telling them.

"Hold," Wendell whispered, feeling her sway. "Hold, Paloma. You magnificent, impossible woman, *hold*—"

"I'm holding," she gasped. "I'm holding, I'm — oh, that's a lot of country—"

The moon leaned harder. The Door pulled deeper. And deep beneath her feet, in the ash and the marble and the star-or-painted ceiling, the Unmade Court began to settle around its new hinge — around *her* — the way a house settles around its cornerstone, accepting the arrangement, making room.

The god knelt. Actually knelt, robes of harvest pooling on ash, and bowed its weather-kind head.

"Witness me," it said — and it was a prayer, she realized. After a century of hearing prayers, the god was finally learning to say one. "Judge me. Teach me the difference between giving and giving *well*."

Paloma, arms burning, heart hammering, tears freezing on her cheeks in air that belonged to two mornings at once, laughed — a wet, exhausted, triumphant sound.

"Oh, we're going to be *so busy*," she said.

And far above them, thin as a fingernail, patient as a promise, the thirteenth moon began — at long last — to set.
