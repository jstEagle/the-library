## 10. Thirteen Turns, Then Morning

### Paloma

The thirteenth moon rose thin as a paring of bone, and Paloma stood in the doorway of herself and waited.

She had not slept in three days. Sleep, it turned out, was one of the many things a Door did not do. She had also stopped eating, stopped shivering, stopped feeling the cold that had settled over the valley like a held breath. Her body still stood at the threshold of the Unmade Court, hands braced against the frame of the true Door, but her self had spread outward into the hinge of the thing — into the wood and the dark and the country pressing against the other side like a face against a window.

It should have hurt. She had expected it to hurt.

Instead it felt like standing in her own doorway on a winter morning, watching someone she loved come up the path.

*They're coming,* said the god's voice, everywhere at once, careful as a child carrying water. *Paloma-who-is-the-Door. They are coming to choose.*

"Then let them choose," she said. Her voice came out of the Door itself, from every grain of it. "And you — you wait. You listen. You don't answer anything until they've finished asking."

The god had learned, these last three days, to be quiet when told. It was learning a great many things. That was the whole point of her, after all. A gate only opened or shut. A door could stand ajar, and through the gap, both countries could see each other plainly — and seeing, she had discovered, was the beginning of judgment, which was only mercy that had finally grown eyes.

The first of them came up the long road out of Vessany just past midnight: an old woman in a coat the color of rust, walking with the deliberate care of someone who has rehearsed this moment for a hundred years and is terrified of getting it wrong anyway.

"Ilyen," Wendell said beside her, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice.

"You're supposed to be dead," the old woman said. "I remember your funeral. I remember it because I invented most of it. There were lilies."

"There were no lilies?"

"I made them up. I felt the occasion required them." She looked at him for a long moment, and then at Paloma, and then at the Door, which was also Paloma, which was also looking back at her. "Well," said Ilyen. "This is stranger than my funeral."

One by one, they came. And one by one, they chose.

Some walked through — through Paloma, which felt to her like being sung, like every hymn she'd ever half-believed in happening all at once — and stepped down into the valley where the frost was lifting and the grass remembered how to be green in the wrong season, gloriously wrong, defiantly wrong. Some stood before the threshold a long while and then turned aside, down the other road, toward rest, toward whatever country lies behind sleep. The god watched each choice with its terrible new attention and answered nothing until asked, and even then asked questions back, clumsy and earnest, like a giant trying to learn to write with a pen made for smaller hands.

A boy of perhaps nine came last of all the walkers-through, and paused in front of Paloma's doorframe-self.

"Are you the Door?" he asked.

"I'm the keeper," she said. "The Door and I have an arrangement."

"My mother says there used to be a law about doors."

"There did." She thought of her elders, of the old law, of the hundred years of small prayers kept like coins in a jar. "Laws are what people make when they can't be trusted with each other yet. Someday, maybe, you won't need me. But today you do, so — mind the frost, and be kind to the valley folk, because they'll be frightened of you, and frightened people say stupid things. Both sides. Believe me. I've been both."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, nodded once, and went through.

* * *

### Wendell

Wendell had spent his whole life being full. Full of Vessany — its songs, its street names, the exact blue of its winter sky, the recipe for honey-bread his grandmother ruined on purpose because she liked it burnt. He had carried a country in his skull the way other men carry guilt, and now, as the last of the choosing trickled through the Door, he felt the weight going out of him like water from a breached dam.

It did not leave him empty. That was the surprise. It left him *light* — light as a man who has set down furniture he'd forgotten was furniture.

"You're crying," said Ilyen, who had chosen to stay on the Vessany side and was now sitting on a fallen pillar eating something she'd conjured out of pure stubborn memory. "You never cried at my funeral either. Which, again, I invented. You owe those lilies an apology."

"I'm not crying," Wendell said, crying. "I'm leaking. It's different. It's a keeper's ailment."

"Mm." She chewed. "And her?" She nodded at the Door, at Paloma, who stood in the grey hour before dawn with frost melting off the stones around her feet in a slow, spreading ring, as if the ground itself were glad she was there.

"She stays," Wendell said. "That was the bargain. Not mine to make for her, though I'd have argued. She made it herself."

"And you? What does a memory-keeper do when the memories all have somewhere to live again?"

Wendell looked at his hands. They were just hands now. Ordinary. Slightly ink-stained, because some habits outlive their purpose.

"I expect," he said slowly, "I'll find out."

* * *

### Paloma

Morning came the way it always comes — without permission, without ceremony, sliding grey-gold across the valley while everyone was busy looking elsewhere. The thirteenth moon set thin and unfinished, and nothing ended, and nothing began, and the world simply continued, which Paloma decided was the most miraculous thing it had ever done.

The Door remained. She remained. Neither open nor shut. When Vessany's people wished to cross, the way was there; when the valley needed the border closed against a hard wind or a harder history, the Door could narrow to a crack, and she could feel every inch of it, the way you feel a held door with your shoulder.

The god came to her at first light. It had taken to arriving quietly, which was new for gods, and it stood at the edge of her awareness like a large animal hoping to be fed.

*I answered a prayer wrongly yesterday,* it said. *The widow Marta asked for her husband. I gave her the husband she described. He was wrong, and she wept, and I unmade him, and she wept more, and I do not understand which part I did badly.*

"All of it, a little," Paloma said gently. "But you unmade him when she asked. That was the right part. That was judgment. A year ago you wouldn't have known to stop."

*Judgment,* the god repeated, tasting the word. *It is slower than answering.*

"It's much slower than answering. That's how you know it's working."

The god was silent a while. Then, in a voice gone small and wondering: *There is a child in the eastern valley who prays every night for her father to come home from a war that ended before she was born. What should I tell her?*

Paloma felt that one in the wood of her. She thought of a hundred years of small prayers, hers among them, rising unanswered off the valley floor like heat shimmer. She thought of answers given blindly, faithfully, terribly. She thought of justice, which was not a rule but a conversation, and conversations required two parties willing to be changed by each other.

"Tell her the truth," she said. "Tell her the war took him, and that it wasn't her praying wrong. Tell her grief isn't a request that failed. And then—" she smiled, and the frost flowers on the Door bloomed open, white and brief and alive— "and then ask her what she needs. Not what she prayed for. What she *needs*. Then come back and tell me what she says, and we'll figure it out together. Slowly."

*Together,* said the god, as if the word were a door too, and went away to practice.

* * *

### Wendell

They sat together at dusk — the keeper who was a Door and the man who was no longer a library — on the steps of the Unmade Court, sharing bread that Ilyen had sent through with instructions ("burnt, as tradition demands") and tea that Paloma could no longer drink but liked to hold, for the warmth, for the ritual, for the look of things.

"So," Wendell said. "Empty-handed, unemployed, and exiled from my own country by my own choice. Most men would call that a bad month."

"You forgot 'surrounded by people who think you're a legend,'" Paloma said. "Half the valley wants to build you a statue. The other half wants to interrogate you about the betrayal, and honestly, Wendell, the statue seems crueler."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising him on its way out. "I keep waiting to feel hollow. Everyone warned me I would. Carrying Vessany was the whole of me, they said. Take it out and there's nothing left."

"And?"

"And I found a poem under everything. Just one. Four lines. I don't know who wrote it. It survived every editing I ever did — I couldn't cut it, didn't know why, kept it like a stone in my shoe." He recited it softly, in the old tongue, then translated: '*What is carried is heavy. What is shared is bread.*'"

Paloma was quiet a moment. "Your grandmother's honey-bread," she said. "Burnt on purpose."

"Burnt on purpose." He broke the loaf and handed her the heel, which she could not eat, and which she accepted anyway, holding it in her two steady, impossible, door-made hands.

Below them the valley lights were coming on, one by one, and beyond the Door the lights of Vessany answered, and between the two countries the evening lay soft as a held breath finally let go.

"Wendell," she said. "Stay. Not as a keeper. There's nothing left to keep. Stay as — a neighbor. A nuisance. Whatever you like. The Door gets lonely, and Doors can't go visiting."

He looked at her — at the woman who was a threshold, who had chosen justice over law and paid for it in flesh and breath and would pay for it every day forever, and who had still saved him the heel of the bread.

"I was going to stay anyway," he said. "I just hadn't worked out how to ask."

"You didn't need to pray," said the Door, warm as morning. "You only had to knock."

And somewhere far off, very carefully, a god wrote down four lines of poetry it had heard a dying man carry across the end of the world — and got every word exactly right.
