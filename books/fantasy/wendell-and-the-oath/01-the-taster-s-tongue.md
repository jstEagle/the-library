## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first cup was always the worst.

Beatrix Ashgrove had learned this over six years of service, and she had learned it the way she learned everything: slowly, precisely, and with her body paying the tuition. The first cup was the worst because her tongue was cleanest then — uncoated by wine, unbored by routine, every nerve in it awake and listening like a dog that had never once been allowed to sleep.

She lifted the goblet of spiced honey-wine and let it sit against her lower lip for three heartbeats. This was not ceremony. Ceremony was what the courtiers watching from the long table believed they saw, the pretty ritual of a girl in grey silk performing her small office before the King's representative dined. What Beatrix was actually doing was listening. Wine spoke first through the nose — clove, burnt sugar, the sour ghost of the barrel — and if there was anything beneath that voice, anything whispering, she would hear it as a wrongness at the edge of sweetness, like a note played just flat enough to make a dog howl.

Nothing. Clove, honey, oak, and the faint metallic thread of the copper goblet itself.

She drank. A mouthful only, held on the tongue for a slow count of ten, then swallowed, then waited. The hall waited with her — or pretended to. Forty nobles of the provincial court of Vellmark sat beneath banners gone soft with age, and forty pairs of eyes flicked to her in the way people watch a thing they hope will die so they can stop thinking about it.

Beatrix had long ago stopped minding the looking. It was easier to think of herself as furniture that happened to swallow.

"Clean," she said, and set the goblet down with its handle turned toward the King's steward, Lord Aldous Vane, whose silver-grey eyes did not blink often enough.

Vane nodded once and gestured, and the first course came out on platters of polished tin: eel in green sauce, river-caught, dressed with sorrel and something sharper underneath that made the back of Beatrix's throat prickle with interest even before she touched it.

*Mustard seed,* she catalogued, as the plate arrived before her. *Old mustard seed, ground coarse, kept too near the smokehouse.* She took up the small silver spoon reserved for her alone and tasted the sauce first, because sauces were where cowards hid their work. Sorrel, vinegar, mustard gone slightly bitter with age, salt scraped from a pan rather than cut fresh — lazy kitchen, but not murderous. Then the eel itself, flaked from the spine, pressed to the roof of her mouth. River mud, fat, sweet flesh, no bitterness of hemlock, no almond ghost of cyanide, no numbing ring of nightshade beginning at the lips.

She worked through the plate methodically while conversation resumed around her, cataloguing flavors the way a priest might count beads. This was the secret of her trade, the one no one at court understood: tasting was not eating. Tasting was prayer. Each flavor named and set down, each absence noted — *no bitter almond, no hay-sweetness of hemlock, no metal of arsenic, no pepper-burn that does not belong* — until the whole dish had been confessed and absolved, and only then could the King's man eat.

Not that the King himself was here. King Aldric had not left Corvane in four years, and the banquets of the provinces were graced instead by stewards and seneschals and men like Aldous Vane, who wore the crown's authority the way a crow wears another bird's feathers — borrowed, glossy, and faintly wrong. But poison did not care whether the man it killed was royal. Poison was democratic that way. So the tasters went out into the provinces like missionaries of a faith no one wanted, and Beatrix had been sent to Vellmark because she was the best the guild had, and being the best meant being used until you were used up.

She swallowed for the crown. That was the whole of her office, written plainly in the guild rolls: *the taster swallows, that the realm may eat.*

Her mother had said it differently.

*You were born in the one place in the world where nothing can follow you, Bea. Not soldiers, not sickness, not magic. The valley keeps us. Promise me you'll never leave it.*

Beatrix turned her spoon over and looked, briefly, at her own reflection in the tin of the platter — a narrow face, dark hair pinned severely back, eyes the brown of river water in autumn. Her mother's face, twenty years younger. Marta Ashgrove had been born in the Ashen Valley and would die there, in the stone house below the terraced barley fields, and she had raised her daughter on two gospels: bread must be kneaded until it stops fighting you, and the valley's borders are the edges of the world.

Everyone in the valley believed it, or said they did. The Ashen Valley lay folded between two ridgelines of grey mountain like a coin dropped in a purse, and nothing grew strange there and nothing behaved strangely. No witch-fires on the fells. No charms on the livestock. No traveling hedge-mages selling luck in paper twists. The old story — told in the same breath as the names of the dead — was that long ago something had happened at the valley's head, some burning or breaking, and ever after the valley had been *ashen*: swept clean, scoured, empty of the power that ran everywhere else in the kingdom like water under a field. Magic could not enter. Magic could not follow. A child could fall asleep on the border stones and wake unchanged.

It was, Beatrix thought now, watching Vane tear his eel with appetite, the safest place in the world.

And she had left it.

Not far. Never far. Vellmark sat a hard day's ride below the valley's southern pass, close enough that on clear evenings she could stand at her window in the taster's quarters and see the mountains standing shoulder to shoulder, grey and patient, like elders waiting for a prodigal to come home. She had left because the valley was safe the way a grave is safe, and because her mother's gospel had a cost written in letters too small to read from inside the walls: safety was all the valley offered, and survival was all it asked, and Beatrix had been nineteen years old when she understood that a life spent merely surviving was a life spent kneeling.

So she had knelt anyway — but on marble floors, in silk, swallowing poison for wages that bought her mother a new roof beam and a winter's coal. It was not freedom. It was not even courage, really. It was resignation wearing a livery of grey silk, and she knew that, and she wore it neatly.

The second course came: roast capon glazed with quince.

*Quince, honey, rosemary scorched at the edge — careless basting, no malice. Skin: rendered fat, salt, woodsmoke. Flesh beneath the second joint:* she pressed the meat to her palate and held it there, eyes half-closed, reading. *Nothing. Clean.*

"Clean," she said again, softly, and Lord Vane ate.

There was a rhythm to it, a liturgy. Taste, name, wait, pronounce. Between courses she drank barley water to rinse her palate and kept her hands folded and let the hall forget her, which was the other half of her craft. A taster who was watched too closely became a target twice over — once for the poisoner, once for the ambitious lord who wished to be seen defying the crown. Better to be furniture. Furniture survived.

But tonight her tongue would not settle.

It was nothing she could name, which troubled her more than any flavor could have. Six years of training had taught her that the tongue never lied; it might be mistaken, startled, exhausted, but it did not invent. And all evening, beneath the honest flavors of the meal, there had been a texture she could not place — not in the food, exactly. In the air. In the wine's afterbreath. Something faintly mineral, like licking a slate, like the smell of a storm that has decided not to break.

She had tasted it before, once or twice, in Corvane itself — a ghost of ash on dishes that should have been innocent. She had mentioned it to the guild-master once, early on, and he had looked at her for a long moment with an expression she had never been able to read, and had said only: *"Some flavors are not poisons, girl. Some are just the taste of the times we live in."* Then he had changed the subject, and she had learned not to ask.

Tonight the slate-taste was stronger than she'd ever felt it outside the capital. It lay on the capon like dust on a coffin lid.

She pronounced it clean anyway. There was nothing in it that would kill. Whatever else the flavor was, it was not her office to name it, and naming things beyond her office was how tasters ended up dismissed — or worse, noticed.

* * *

The banquet broke apart by degrees, as such gatherings do — the wine deepening, the laughter loosening, the candles drowning in their own wax. Beatrix stood at her post beside the high table with her hands folded, invisible, running her tongue along the inside of her cheek where a small callus had grown years ago, the taster's mark, the way a rower's palms go hard.

Lord Vane approached her near midnight, when the hall had thinned to the stubborn drinkers. He was a tall man, elegant, perhaps fifty, with the kind of face that seemed carved for coinage. His eyes were the pale grey of frost on a windowpane, and they rested on her without warmth and without contempt — simply weighing, the way a merchant weighs goods he already owns.

"You found nothing," he said. Not a question.

"The meal is clean, my lord."

"And yet you look troubled, Mistress Ashgrove. Do I offend you? Is the wine poor?"

"The wine is excellent, my lord." She paused. It was imprudent, and she heard herself doing it anyway, the way one hears oneself stepping onto ice. "There is a flavor in the hall tonight that I cannot name. It isn't in the food. It may be nothing."

Something moved behind those frost-grey eyes — not alarm. Interest, perhaps. Or recognition.

"It is nothing," Vane said pleasantly. "The provinces have their smells. Smoke, wet wool, politics." He smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You are from the Ashen Valley, I'm told. The quiet country. Nothing follows you there, they say."

"No, my lord. Nothing follows."

"How restful that must be." He said it lightly, but he said it the way a man says *how restful* about a prison cell, and for one strange moment Beatrix felt certain he was not mocking her at all — that he envied her, or pitied her, or both. "Rest well, mistress. You ride back toward your mountains tomorrow, I understand? There is a mill on your road. Millrace village. Buy your flour there — the crown's purser has an account."

"I know the mill, my lord."

"Of course you do." He inclined his head, precise as a clock striking. "Travel safely, Beatrix Ashgrove. The roads grow interesting this season."

He walked away into the candle-gloom, and Beatrix stood very still, listening to the words settle in her chest like stones dropped down a well. *Interesting.* Courtiers did not say *interesting* about roads. They said *dangerous*, or they said nothing at all. Interesting was a word men used when they knew something and were deciding whether you needed to.

She finished the night as she always did: rinsing her palate, cataloguing the day's tastes in the little leather book the guild required — *honey-wine, clove, oak; eel, sorrel, aged mustard; capon, quince, rosemary, ash* — and pausing over that last word before writing it, because writing it made it real, and real things demanded explanations she did not have.

*Ash.*

Outside her narrow window, the mountains stood black against the stars, and somewhere beyond them the valley slept in its enforced innocence, and her mother slept in the stone house believing her daughter safe because her daughter had never strayed beyond reach of home.

*I'll come up for the harvest,* Beatrix had written last month. *I'll bring honey and cloth. Save me the bed under the south window.*

Tomorrow she rode north. Tomorrow, the mill at Millrace, the purser's account, a sack of flour ground from valley wheat.

She blew out her candle and lay down in the dark, and her last thought before sleep was the thought she carried to bed most nights, worn smooth as a river stone from handling: *survival is a life's whole work, and I am good at my work, and that will have to be enough.*

She did not yet know that a man was dying on the road ahead of her — bleeding his life into the dust a mile past the millrace, with a map sealed in wax pressed against his ribs and four words left in him, four words that would fall like a lit match into all the dry, careful years of her surviving:

*The crown eats him.*
