## 2. A Stranger at the Millrace

The Ashen Valley had no magic in it, and Beatrix had learned to love it the way one loves a plain room — not for what it held, but for what could not enter.

She was thinking about this as she walked the mill path on a morning so ordinary it felt almost like an apology. Mist clung to the millrace in long gray ribbons, and the water turned the wheel with the patient, grinding patience of a thing that had done its work for two hundred years and would do it for two hundred more. The smell of wet stone and cut grain hung over everything. Somewhere upstream a heron stood in the shallows with the stillness of a creature that had never once been lied to.

Beatrix carried an empty flour sack and a small purse of coins, and she counted both out of habit. Counting was another kind of safety. She counted steps, counted breaths when the poisons sat badly in her stomach, counted the days since her last bad reaction — eleven, today, which was good, which meant her body was still learning the alphabet of toxins the crown's stewards fed her in measured doses. Arsenic on the first of the month. Wolfsbane midweek. A tincture of blisterwort that tasted of copper and old churches and made her tongue swell until she could not speak for a day. Her body was a ledger of everything that had tried to kill it, and every entry made her more valuable and less herself.

*Survival is a life's whole work,* her mother said, whenever Beatrix came home pale from the palace kitchens. *You are alive. That is enough.*

Her mother had never once asked what the work cost.

The mill stood where the river bent, low-roofed and dark-wooded, its wheel throwing up cold spray. Old Harrow the miller was already at his door, arguing with a sack of barley as though the barley had opinions, and Beatrix lifted a hand to him and opened her mouth to call a greeting —

— and then the stranger came out of the mist.

He did not walk so much as fall forward in stages, each step a negotiation with gravity he was losing. He wore riding leathers gone dark with something that was not mud. One hand pressed against his side, and between his fingers, black in the gray light, blood moved in a slow pulse. His horse was nowhere. His eyes were fixed on Beatrix with the terrible focus of a man who has been carrying something too far and knows he cannot carry it further.

"Harrow," Beatrix said quietly, without turning. "Fetch —"

But the man had already seen her, only her, as if the miller were smoke and the wheel were smoke and the whole valley were smoke and she alone stood solid in the middle of it. He crossed the last ten yards in a lurching run and caught her arm with fingers that burned cold through her sleeve.

"You're —" He looked at her face, at her hands, at the plain wool of her dress, searching for something. "You're not warden."

"No," Beatrix said. Her voice came out steady, because her voice always came out steady; twenty years of swallowing poison taught you where to keep your fear. "You're hurt. Sit down before you fall down."

"I don't have time to sit." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought red to his lips, and Beatrix understood with a lurch that this was not a wound a person recovered from. This was arithmetic. He had perhaps minutes, and he knew the sum already.

"The wardens," he rasped. "Where are the wardens?"

"Half a mile, maybe. The watch-house is by the chapel." She turned her head. "Harrow! *Harrow!* Run for the wardens — now!"

Old Harrow dropped his barley and ran, bless him, his boots slapping the millstones, his cry going up thin and cracked across the water. And the stranger sagged with relief, actually relief, as if the running of that one foolish old man toward help was the best news he'd had in weeks.

"Good," he whispered. "Good. Then there's time. There might be time." His hand left her arm and went into his coat, and Beatrix tensed — she had seen men reach into coats before, in the capital, in the years before the valley, and the reaching always preceded either a blade or a bribe — but what he drew out was neither.

It was a scroll case, no longer than her forearm, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with sweat and rain. Around its middle was wound a cord, and sealing the cord was a disc of dark wax stamped with a sigil she did not recognize: a crown, yes, but a crown drawn wrong, its points elongated into something like teeth, or roots, or fingers closing.

"Take it," the man said.

"I can't take — I don't know what it is. Wait for the wardens, they'll —"

"They'll bury it with me." He tried to smile. It was a young man's face under the grime, she saw now, younger than hers, and that made it worse. "Listen. Listen to me. My name is Corvin. I rode from Corvane. Eleven days. They were behind me at the pass, they'll be behind me here, do you understand? Whatever happens, whatever they tell you, whoever comes asking — this cannot go back to the crown."

"The crown?" Beatrix heard her own voice go flat and careful. "This is the king's business?"

Something moved behind his eyes — pity, or horror, or both wearing the same face.

"The king," Corvin said, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, to a secret shaped for one pair of ears, "is not the king anymore. Do you hear me? The crown eats him. It has been eating him for years, and everyone smiles, and everyone bows, and the thing that wears him gets stronger every season. I carried proof. I carried the way to unmake it. And now I'm dying in a flour mill in the one place in the world it can't follow, and you—" his grip found her wrist again, weak as a child's, "—you have hands. You have hands and you're standing here. Take it."

The world had gone very quiet. The wheel still turned. The heron still stood in the shallows. Mist still unwound along the millrace like thread from a spindle, and Beatrix stood in the middle of all that ordinariness holding a dying man's arm and feeling the ground shift beneath a life she had built stone by patient stone.

"I'm a food-taster," she heard herself say. Foolish, irrelevant words. "I taste for the steward of the valley court. I'm nobody. I'm not — I can't be part of whatever this is."

"Nobody." He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something. "Nobody is exactly who it needs. Nobody can carry it where somebody would be searched." He pressed the scroll case into her hands, folding her fingers closed around it with his own, and the wax seal touched her palm through the oilcloth.

It was cold.

Not cool — cold, the specific biting cold of iron left out in winter, of a well's depths, and it sank through her skin into the bones of her hand and up her wrist, and Beatrix gasped despite herself. Corvin watched her face and something in his own eased.

"It knows you," he murmured. "Or it will. Keep it against your skin. Don't let them take it off you. Promise me. Swear it, even if you swear nothing else ever again."

"I don't — I don't know how to do what you're asking. I don't know anything about kings or crowns or —"

"You know how to survive," he said. "I can see it. It's written all over you — the way you stand, the way you count things with your eyes. Doors, hands, distances. You've spent your life being poisoned by powerful people and learning their flavors anyway." His breath rattled. "That's the whole job, taster. That's the whole war. Learning the flavor of the thing that's killing you."

Boots on the road. Voices. The wardens, coming fast — she could hear Warden Cade's bark carrying across the water, calling for the injured man to show himself.

Corvin heard it too, and the relief drained out of his face, replaced by something urgent and final.

"Not yet," he breathed. "Not them first. Me first. Say you'll keep it."

And Beatrix, who had sworn oaths to no one, whose mother had raised her on a single commandment — *never leave, never promise, never give them a reason to look at you* — looked down at the scroll case burning its cold into her palms, and heard herself say:

"I'll keep it."

* * *

Warden Cade arrived with three others and took charge of the dying man the way wardens take charge of everything: loudly, thoroughly, and too late.

They laid Corvin on the flat stones by the millrace door. Someone fetched blankets. Someone else fetched the valley's one physician, a stooped woman named Maren who smelled of camphor and resignation, and Maren knelt, and looked, and shook her head with the economy of long practice.

Beatrix stood back among the sacks of grain and watched, and told herself the shaking in her hands was the morning chill.

Cade was a broad, ruddy man with a warden's talent for making questions sound like kindness. He crouched beside Corvin and spoke gently. "Who did this to you, son? Where's your horse? What are you carrying?"

Corvin looked at the sky. The mist was thinning; a pale smear of sun was trying to happen above the eastern ridge. He seemed to find it interesting.

"Tell me your name," Cade pressed. "Your house. Who sent you."

"My name," Corvin said clearly, and his eyes slid sideways and found Beatrix's, and held them, and there was no fear in them at all now, only a fierce and exhausted certainty, "was Corvin of the Lowmarsh road. I was a courier. I delivered what I was given to deliver." A pause. A bubble of blood at the corner of his mouth. "The rest is the taster's business now."

And he died between one breath and the next, politely, the way couriers die in stories — having said exactly enough and not a word more — while the millwheel turned and the heron finally spread its wings and went up the river into the brightening gray.

* * *

They asked her questions for an hour.

Cade was not cruel about it. That was somehow worse. He sat her on a bench outside the mill with a cup of cider she didn't touch, and he asked her everything twice in different words, the way hunters circle: What did he say? Word for word. Did he give you anything? Are you certain? May I see your hands?

She showed him her hands. Empty. Steady. Twenty years of practice kept them steady, and the lie came out of her smooth and unblemished, and she marveled a little at herself even as she told it: *He grabbed my arm, he asked for the wardens, he said nothing else, he died.* The scroll case was inside her dress, bound flat against her ribs with the sash of her apron, and it was so cold against her skin that she could feel the exact shape of it, every edge, like a brand laid on backwards.

Cade studied her a long moment. He had kind eyes, Warden Cade. Everyone in the valley said so.

"He came a long way to die here," Cade said at last, almost to himself. "Out of all the valleys, all the roads. Into the one place nothing unnatural can cross." He rubbed his jaw. "You believe that, girl? About the valley?"

"Everyone believes it," Beatrix said. "It's why we're here."

"Mm." He looked out at the millrace, at the turning wheel, at the ordinary blessed water. "My grandfather helped raise the border stones. Said the founders paid a price for it — a bargain, a binding, something old. Magic can't follow us in, he said. But he never said what couldn't follow us *out*." He smiled then, quick and warm, and stood, and the moment folded itself away like a letter. "Go home, Beatrix Ashgrove. Eat something. Sleep. If you remember anything else, you'll come to me."

"Yes, Warden."

"And Beatrix." He paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the brightening water. "Whatever a dying man presses into your hands in his last minute — that's a heavy thing to carry alone. People break under less. Remember the wardens are here. Remember the valley keeps its people safe. That's what it's *for*."

She walked home the long way, past the chapel, past the watch-house with its green-shuttered windows, past children chasing a dog through the square where she had bought apples every market day of her life. Everything looked the same. That was the horror of it — everything looked exactly, perfectly the same, and she was different now, she was a woman walking through her own town with a dead man's secret strapped against her heart, and the secret was cold, so cold, colder than the morning, and it did not warm. All the way home it did not warm.

Her mother was in the garden when she arrived, kneeling in the bean rows, her gray hair pinned up and her sleeves rolled, and at the sight of her Beatrix felt her throat close with a grief she could not name, because nothing had happened, nothing had changed, except everything.

"You're late," her mother said, not looking up. "The palace sent for you. Steward Vess wants you at the noon tasting — there's a delegation from the capital, and he wants your tongue on everything they bring."

"The capital," Beatrix repeated.

"Crown officials." Her mother straightened, brushed soil from her knees, and only then looked at her daughter properly, and frowned. "Beatrix. You're white as flour. What happened on the mill road?"

"A courier died," Beatrix said. "At Harrow's mill. The wardens have him."

Her mother went very still. It lasted half a heartbeat, no longer — a stillness like a held breath, like a door closing softly somewhere deep in the house — and then she was moving again, brisk and ordinary, gathering her basket, saying something about soup, about eating before the tasting, about standing up straight in front of capital men.

But Beatrix had spent her life reading faces for the flicker of poison, for the micro-tremor of a lie in a server's mouth, for the truth people swallowed along with their meals. And she had seen her mother's face go still.

Against her ribs, under the apron sash, the scroll case lay its line of cold along her skin, patient as a seed.

*The crown eats him,* a dead man had said. *It knows you. Or it will.*

Noon was coming. The capital was coming. And Beatrix Ashgrove climbed the steps to her mother's kitchen carrying the first secret of her life, and understood, with the clarity of a tongue that had never once been fooled, that the valley had stopped keeping her safe the moment she said *I'll keep it* — and that there was no road back to the woman who had walked to the mill that morning with nothing heavier than an empty sack.
