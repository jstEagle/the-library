## 3. What Cannot Follow

The warden-captain's name was Doss, and Beatrix had known him since she was seven years old, when he'd fished her out of the millrace after she slipped on the wet stones chasing a runaway hen. He had carried her home on his shoulders, her soaked skirts dripping down his back, and told her mother she had the grip of a river eel and the sense of a startled rabbit. He was not a cruel man. That was what made it so much worse.

He stood in the doorway of her mother's house at dawn, filling most of it, his gray uniform brushed and buttoned to the throat, his face arranged into something he probably believed looked like sympathy. Behind him, in the lane, stood four other wardens with staves, and beyond them half the village had gathered under the pretense of fetching water, which meant they were all watching her door.

"Beatrix." His voice was gentle, the voice he used for delivering death-notices. "You know why I'm here."

She did. She had known since midnight, when she'd heard the boots in the lane and blown out her candle and sat in the dark with the map pressed against her sternum like a second heart, listening to them search the mill first, then the courier's body — they had taken him away on a cart, she'd learned, though no one would say where — then working their way up the street, house by house, patient as rot.

"The man who died," Doss said. "The courier. Before the wardens reached him, someone spoke with him. The miller's boy saw a woman crouched over him by the wheel."

"Someone should have," Beatrix said. "He was dying."

"He was dying of things that don't happen here." Doss stepped inside without being asked, which was itself an answer to a question she hadn't voiced yet: *am I suspected?* Yes. Deeply. "No wounds on him that could bleed that much. No sickness our healers knew. And his hands—" The captain paused, choosing words the way she chose which poison to swallow first, smallest risk before larger. "His hands were burned, Beatrix. From the inside. Like something had been written on him and then erased."

The map lay beneath the loose floorboard under her bed, wrapped in oilcloth, and even now, even through floorboards and wool and her own ribs, she could feel it. Not warm. Not exactly cold either. It felt the way the first sip of wine felt on a tongue numbed by arsenic trials — a faint wrongness, a flavor where there should have been none. It had kept her awake all night, that taste. It had kept her awake whispering.

"I held his hand while he died," she said. "That's all."

"That's what I told the elders." Doss's eyes went around the little room — the herb bundles drying from the rafters, her mother's loom shrouded in its sheet, the single window with its view of the valley walls rising green and safe on every side. "I said, that girl has swallowed more poison than any three people in this valley and never once lied about what she tasted. If she says she only held his hand, she only held his hand."

Something loosened in her chest, briefly, foolishly.

"But the elders didn't believe me," Doss continued, "because the elders already know what he carried. They've known for longer than you've been alive. Longer than I've been wearing this coat." He turned to face her fully, and she saw now how tired he was, how the gentleness in his face was costing him something. "They sent me for the map, Beatrix. Not to ask. To collect. They want it brought to the moot-hall tonight, and they want you there to swear on the valley stone that you touched nothing else of his."

"And if I say I found nothing?"

"Then I tell them I searched your house and found nothing, and we both sleep badly for the rest of our lives, and the valley stays asleep too." He said it flatly, without threat, which somehow made it more frightening. "Or you give it to me now, and I carry it to the moot-hall, and the elders do what elders do."

"Which is?"

Doss was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a rooster crowed, absurd and ordinary, and somewhere the millrace turned and turned.

"They burn it," he said finally. "In the warding fire, with the seal broken and the ashes salted. Whatever it is, whatever it leads to — they will make sure it can never be followed. By anyone. Ever again."

*The crown eats him.*

The courier's last words rose in her like water in a flooded well. She thought of King Aldric, whose face was stamped on every coin she'd ever earned tasting death for the crown's distant cousin, the royal household — a strong-jawed man on a horse, always on a horse, always riding away into some engraved horizon. She thought of the way the courier had gripped her wrist with those burning hands, strength pouring out of him like the last heat out of a cooling forge, and how certain his eyes had been. Not desperate. Certain. As if he had spent his whole life looking for exactly one person and had run out of road two feet short of her door.

"Why?" she asked. "Why burn it? What does it show?"

"You know I can't—"

"You just told me the elders have known about the king for twenty years or more. Known *what*, Captain Doss? Say it. Say it out loud in my mother's kitchen, where I learned to count flavors before I learned to count coins."

He flinched. Actually flinched, this man who had carried dead children off mountainsides without changing expression. And that flinch told her more than any answer would have.

"It isn't ours," he said. "Whatever's wrong with the king, whatever that map opens — it isn't ours. We are the Ashen Valley. Magic cannot follow us across the border stones. That is the whole of our covenant and the whole of our safety. The elders' grandmothers made a bargain so their grandchildren could live plain lives, and the price of that bargain is that we keep our eyes on our own fields. A kingdom's sickness is not a valley's debt."

"A kingdom's sickness." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and cracked in the quiet room. "A man is dying of it. A king is dying of it, maybe, if the courier wasn't mad. And the answer to it walked through the mill gate yesterday and put it in my hands, and your elders want to burn the answer because it isn't *ours*."

"Yes," Doss said simply. "That is precisely what they want. And Beatrix — listen to me, because I am going to say this once, man to girl, and then never again." He came closer, close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and leather on him, the smell of every walk home from the mill of her childhood. "Give me the map. Not because they command it. Because I have seen what happens to people who leave this valley. Your mother crossed those stones once, carrying more than she could bear, and look what it cost her. Look what it cost *you*. There are things outside that eat oath-keepers and tasters and couriers and kings, and there is nothing in this valley that can follow you across the stones except your own conscience, and conscience, girl, is survivable. I promise you that. I have carried mine forty years and I still sleep."

The floorboard under her bed might as well have been glowing.

She thought about saying yes. She thought about it honestly, the way she tasted everything — holding it on the tongue, cataloguing the notes. There was safety in it, thick and sweet as clover honey. Give Doss the map, swear the oath, stand in the moot-hall tonight among people who had known her whole life, and let the world outside go on eating its kings without her. She could live. She was very good at living; it was the one art her whole life had trained her in. Swallow, catalogue, survive. Swallow, catalogue, survive.

But underneath the honey there was ash. She knew ash better than anyone alive. It was in everything the court sent her, faint but unmistakable to a tongue like hers, and once you had learned its taste you could never unlearn it, and it was in Doss's kindness now, and in the elders' bargain, and in the word *covenant* spoken in a room where a dying man's blood had probably soaked into the millstones not thirty hours ago.

"Doss," she said quietly. "When the elders knew — all these years — did they know the courier would come? Did they know someone would come looking?"

The silence stretched.

"Because I think they did," she went on, and her voice steadied as it went, the way it did when she named a poison correctly, the moment the fear became knowledge. "I think they've been waiting for this map the way you wait for a fever to break. I think every year at the moot they light the warding fire and thank the stones that magic can't cross, and nobody asks what the stones are keeping *in* as well as out. I think my whole life, everyone's whole life, has been built on top of a locked door, and yesterday somebody knocked, and today the elders want to burn the key rather than open it."

"You don't know what's behind that door."

"No," she agreed. "But I know what's behind yours. I've lived behind it twenty-six years, and it tastes like ash, Doss. Everything here tastes like ash to me now. Maybe it always did, and I only just got the tongue for it."

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, the neighbors were losing interest in their water-fetching; she could hear them murmuring, shifting, the crowd beginning to decide what it believed. Doss closed his eyes briefly, and when he opened them, something had gone out of them — or perhaps into them, some final settling of a weight he'd been carrying since he knocked.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I have to report that I searched the house."

And he did. She stood in the middle of her own kitchen while he moved through it with the thoroughness of a man who loved the place he was violating — lifted the mattress, opened the flour bin, ran his fingers along the window frame. She watched his hands and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that he was searching slowly. That he was giving her time. That every extra second he spent examining a bundle of dried yarrow was a second bought for her, and that when he found the floorboard — and he would find the floorboard, he had taught her to fish, he knew every trick of hiding this valley had — she would have to choose in front of him, in front of the wardens, in front of the whole watching village.

Unless she wasn't there when he found it.

It came to her the way flavors come to her tongue: not as a decision but as a recognition, a taste she had swallowed long ago and only now identified. Her mother's warning — *never leave, never leave, the valley is the only place it cannot follow* — and beneath the warning, all these years, the question her mother had never answered: *follow where? What did you see, Mama, that made you build your whole life into a wall?*

Doss knelt by the bed.

Beatrix moved. Not fast — fast would draw the eye of the wardens at the door. She moved the way she did at banquets, unhurried, precise, a woman doing nothing worth noticing, drifting toward the back of the house where the wash-water stood, and she took up the jug as if to fetch the morning's water, and she walked out through the rear door into the garden, and from the garden into the goat-path behind the tanner's yard, and from the goat-path into the alder woods that climbed the western wall of the valley, and she did not run until the house was out of sight, and then she ran.

* * *

The map was against her skin. She had taken it from under the floorboard in the six heartbeats between Doss kneeling and Doss lifting, a sleight of hand learned over ten years of swapping poisoned cups for clean ones under the eyes of suspicious stewards, and now it rode against her breastbone inside her shirt, wrapped in oilcloth, cold as a coin left overnight in snow. Cold, and tasting faintly of ash, and — she admitted it now, running through the alders with her lungs burning — *alive*. It had a pulse of wrongness that quickened when she ran, as if it liked the running, as if it had been waiting twenty years for legs.

Behind her, faint and fading, she heard Doss's voice raised in the lane, calling her name. Not the wardens' whistle. Not the alarm-cry. Just her name, twice, in the voice of a man who had fished a seven-year-old out of a millrace and had just watched her dive back in.

She climbed.

The western path was a goat-track mostly, switchbacking up through bracken and scree toward the notch in the valley wall that the old folk called the Shepherd's Cut — the one place the walls dipped low enough to pass on foot, the one place the border stones stood in a ragged line like pulled teeth. She had never come this far. No one came this far. Children dared each other to touch the nearest stone and ran back screaming with delight, and adults spoke of the Cut the way they spoke of the dead, respectfully and rarely, and her mother had never once in twenty-six years looked at the western wall without something moving behind her eyes like weather.

The trees thinned. The air changed first — she tasted it before she saw anything, because of course she did; her tongue had always been the sharpest part of her. The valley air tasted of loam and woodsmoke and clean water, ordinary and good. But as she climbed, another flavor threaded in, thin as a wire: mineral, ancient, faintly metallic, like licking an old key. Magic, she thought. This must be what magic tastes like. It had been standing outside the valley her whole life like weather against glass, and now she was walking out into it.

The border stones appeared through the bracken, gray and lichen-eaten, each taller than a man, each carved — she saw as she drew close, breath sawing — with the same mark worn nearly smooth: a circle with a line struck through it. *What cannot follow.* She stopped an arm's length from the nearest one and put her palm flat against it, and the stone was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm like a sleeping animal, like a hearth banked for the night, and beneath the warmth she felt a hum too low to hear, felt it in her teeth, in the map against her sternum, which had gone suddenly, eagerly cold.

Beyond the stones the land fell away into morning mist, and somewhere down in that mist were roads, and rivers, and the city called Corvane where a king sat on a throne with something ancient wearing his crown like a hand in a glove. Somewhere down there was whatever had burned a courier alive from the inside to get this map to the one person in the world whose tongue could not be fooled and whose blood could not be touched. Her mother had crossed these stones pregnant and afraid and had never once spoken of why, and the elders had lit their fires for sixty years thanking the stones for the silence, and Doss had carried his conscience forty years and called it survivable.

Beatrix Ashgrove stood at the edge of the only safe place in the world, and understood that she had already left it. Not with her feet. With her hands, yesterday, at the millrace, when she had knelt in the blood of a stranger and listened instead of walking away. Everything since had just been her body catching up.

*Survival is a life's whole work,* she had believed. Her mother's creed, the valley's creed, the whole quiet architecture of her days. But survival for whom, and at what cost, and paid to whom? She had spent ten years swallowing poison so that a crown she'd never seen could keep feeding on a kingdom she'd never visited, and she had called that a life. The courier had spent his last mile making sure the map reached her, and she had called that an accident.

The mist below shifted. And in the mist — not near, not yet, but on the road that wound up toward the Cut — she saw movement. A figure, walking up. Walking steadily, unhurried, as if he had been waiting at the bottom of this pass for a very long time and had seen her coming the moment she crested the ridge. A man in a travel-stained coat, gray at the temples, leaning on a staff, and even at that distance something about the set of his shoulders was familiar in a way that made no sense, like a flavor she knew from a dish she'd never eaten.

The map pulsed against her heart, cold and insistent, like a second pulse learning hers.

Beatrix stepped over the border stones.

The magic did not follow her. It had never needed to. It had simply been waiting for her to come to it — and the man in the mist raised his head, and began, quietly, to climb.
