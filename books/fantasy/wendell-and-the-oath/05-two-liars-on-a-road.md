## 5. Two Liars on a Road

**Beatrix**

The man who called himself Wendell walked like someone who had learned to apologize to the ground for touching it.

Beatrix watched him from three paces back as they followed the old drover's road east out of the valley's shadow, cataloguing him the way she catalogued everything — by what it might do to her if she let it past her lips. His boots were good leather gone soft at the seams, a soldier's boots worn past soldiering. His cloak had been dyed once, expensively, and then dyed again in cheap brown to hide it. And his hands — his hands never stopped moving. Thumb along the seam of his sleeve. Knuckle against his jaw. A man counting something over and over and never reaching the end.

He hadn't asked her name since the border stones. He'd asked instead where she'd gotten the map, whether anyone had seen her take it, whether the wardens had searched her lodging, whether she could ride, swim, run, lie. She had answered every question with a smaller question of her own, and by the second mile he had understood the game and stopped playing it aloud.

Which meant he was dangerous. Honest men asked questions until they got answers. Liars asked questions to see how you lied.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm favoring my left knee." He didn't look back. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"A limp is an accident. Favoring is a choice." He stepped over a fallen branch without breaking stride. "You'd know that if you'd ever marched anywhere."

She let that pass. The road climbed through birch scrub, the valley falling away behind them in folds of grey-green, and with every step the ache in her chest changed shape — grief, she supposed, or its cousin. She had lived twenty-six years inside the Ashen Valley, inside the one place in the world where magic could not follow, where her mother had carried her as an infant across those same border stones and never once looked back. Her mother's voice was still in her ear. *Nothing out there will love you, Bea. Nothing out there will even see you. Stay where you are invisible.* And Beatrix had stayed, had swallowed poison on schedule for a king she'd never met, had built a whole life out of small survivable doses — and had thrown it away in an afternoon because a dying stranger put cold wax in her palm and said *the crown eats him*.

The map rode against her sternum now, sewn into the lining of her jacket where she'd moved it the first night while Wendell slept. He thought it was still in the oilskin packet in her satchel. She intended for him to go on thinking so.

"Water," Wendell said, and turned off the road toward a stream cutting through the birches.

She watched him crouch at the bank and do something curious: he filled his cupped hand, held it, and waited. Counting. Then he drank.

"What are you waiting for?" she asked.

"For the water to settle. If something dead is upstream, it shows." He glanced up at her, and there was something almost gentle in the dryness of it. "Old habit. You learn it in certain company."

*What company?* she wanted to ask. Instead she knelt downstream of him, filled her own flask, and did not drink. She never drank water she hadn't boiled herself, hadn't since she was nine years old and her mother caught her drinking from the millrace and made her sick on purpose, afterward, with a tincture of ipecac and shame, so she would understand what trust cost. "I'll boil mine tonight," she said.

"Suit yourself." He rose, shaking off his hands. "We make the crossroads by dark. There's a shrine there — roofed, dry. We sleep in shifts."

"Shifts," she repeated. "You think I'll rob you in your sleep?"

"I think everyone robs everyone eventually." He said it lightly, the way people say true things when they can't bear to say them any other way. "I'd just rather be awake for it."

**Wendell**

She lied beautifully. That was the first thing he'd decided about her, and the decision had settled into him like a stone into mud.

Wendell had spent twenty years around liars of every grade — courtiers, kings, oath-breakers, and worst of all honest men forced into dishonesty by their masters — and he knew the spectrum the way a farrier knows horses. Most liars overbuilt. They gave you too many details, too much sorrow, too steady a gaze. This girl — this *taster*, though she hadn't said it and he hadn't asked, because the way she held her flask told him enough — lied by subtraction. She answered exactly what was asked and not a syllable more. When he'd asked what work she did for the crown, she'd said, "Deliveries." True, in the way that arsenic is a delivery. When he'd asked why the courier chose her mill, she'd said, "I don't know." Also true, probably, but delivered with the flatness of a woman who had decided not to wonder.

And she'd moved the map. He was nearly sure of it. The first night, half-dreaming by the fire, he'd heard the small wet sound of thread parting and rejoining, and in the morning the oilskin packet sat in her satchel with a neatness that looked rehearsed. He hadn't said anything. Let her keep her little secret; he was keeping a cathedral of them.

The largest one walked beside him now wearing his silence like armor: the map required a bearer bound by oath. Not paper and wax alone — the thing was keyed, forged into the First Hearth's own fire, and it would open its instructions only to someone sworn and willing. He had drawn it twenty years ago for King Osric's bearer, a quiet man named Tam Voss who had died in a cellar in Corvane with the map unopened in his fist, because at the last moment he had refused. Refused what, Wendell had never learned. Refused the cost. There was always a cost. That was the whole architecture of oaths: they were bridges, and bridges took weight, and someone always paid the toll.

If the girl learned that, she might turn back. Worse, she might do something brave. In his experience bravery was just ignorance moving quickly, and he had buried enough of it.

"The crossroads," she said, pointing ahead with her chin. "That's wrong."

He followed her finger. The shrine stood where it should, a low stone hump with a leaning roof, and beside it the milestone, and beyond them the road forked north toward the river towns. What was wrong — and it took him another three heartbeats to see it, which shamed him — was the birds. Or rather the absence. The birches around the clearing stood empty. No finches on the shrine roof. No crow anywhere in the grey sky.

"Hunters?" she asked quietly.

"Maybe travelers. Maybe nothing." But he was already unslinging his pack, already moving them both off the road into the bracken, his hand finding the worn grip of the short sword under his cloak. "How many men does the crown send after a stolen map?"

"More than two," she said. "Fewer than ten. The valley wardens send riders to Corvane within a day of anything unusual. By now there's a description of me in three garrisons."

"And of me?"

"They don't need one. You look like trouble that's been left out in the rain."

Despite everything — despite the empty trees and the taste of old failure in his mouth — Wendell laughed. It came out rusty, a hinge unused for years. "Fair," he said. "Stay behind me. If I fall, take the road south and don't—"

"Don't give a speech," she said. "I've tasted better last words than yours would be."

They came around the shrine through the bracken, low and slow, and Wendell saw them: four men, maybe five, positioned with a competence that ruled out bandits. Two flanking the road behind the milestone. One at the tree line with a crossbow resting easy. One sitting on the shrine steps eating an apple, unhurried, which meant he was the one who thought the trap already worked. Crown hunters wore no livery — that was the point of hunters — but they all ate the same way, watching the road while they chewed, feeding themselves like it was a chore between chores.

The apple-eater saw them before they could withdraw. Of course he did. He smiled with the unhurried confidence of a man holding four aces and stood, tossing the apple core into the grass.

"Mistress Ashgrove," he called pleasantly. "And a friend. The warden-captain sends his regards — and his reimbursement. Hand over the map and walk home. Nobody bleeds today."

**Beatrix**

Beatrix's mouth went dry, and her tongue — her treacherous, magnificent, ruined tongue — began working without her permission.

It had started as a child's game and become a profession and become simply the way she saw: the world arrived to her as flavor first. The air near the hunters tasted of iron and horse sweat and, underneath, faintly, sweetly rotten — old blood dried in cloth, a wound somebody hadn't cleaned. The apple-eater's breath, even at thirty paces, carried something sharper beneath the fruit: bitter almond, thin as a needle. She knew that taste the way priests know scripture. Cyanide, or its cousin. Carried in a ring or a locket, held under the tongue. Hunters who carried suicide doses for themselves, in case capture came.

Which meant these were not garrison men following a warden's letter. These were professionals. Which meant someone in Corvane wanted the map badly enough to spend real coin.

"Wendell," she breathed. "The one on the steps. Bitter almond. Under the apple."

His head turned a fraction. "What?"

"He's carrying poison in his mouth. For himself, if it goes wrong. Men who plan to die before talking don't plan to take prisoners." She kept her voice below the wind. "And the crossbowman's string is waxed with something — I can smell it from here, rancid, like fish oil gone over. Old stock. His bolts have been stored damp. Half of them won't fly true."

For a moment he stared at her as if she'd spoken in a language from before the valley. Then, slowly, something rearranged itself in his face — not belief yet, but the scaffolding of it. "You're not a courier," he said softly.

"I deliver things," she said. "Shut up and tell me how you want to die."

He huffed — almost a laugh, strangled by circumstance — and his mind visibly began to move, fast, using what she'd given him. "The crossbowman's your problem, then. If his bolts are soft, he needs close range to be sure. He'll try to move to the milestone when we run." He drew the short sword without flourish. "The other two will come straight at us. The talker stays put — talkers never dirty their boots."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're dead, and it stops mattering whose fault it was." He glanced at her sideways, and in the flat grey light his eyes were very clear. "Run for the north fork on my word. Don't stop for me. Don't be noble. Noble people are heavy to bury."

"On three?" she said.

"On *now*—" and he burst from the bracken screaming like something out of an old story, sword high, utterly committed, and Beatrix ran.

The world narrowed to the grammar of survival. Bracken whipping her calves. The birch trunks strobing past. Behind her the crash of bodies — Wendell meeting the first hunter with a sound like an axe in wet wood — and ahead, at the edge of her vision, the crossbowman scrambling down from the tree line toward the milestone, exactly as Wendell had read him, closing to forty paces, thirty, kneeling, raising—

Beatrix swerved. Not away. Toward. She cut hard left through the trees, crashed through a hazel stand, and came out on the hunter's flank at fifteen paces just as he leveled the weapon at the fleeing figures on the road. She snatched the first thing her hand found — a dead branch, thick as her forearm, rotten-hearted — and threw it with all the graceless fury of a woman who had never thrown anything in her life.

It wasn't a good throw. It didn't need to be. It cracked against a trunk two feet from his head, and he flinched — a full-body flinch, the bolt going wide and low into the dirt — and that half-second was enough for her to reach him.

After that it was ugly and brief and nothing like the stories. She had no skill for fighting; what she had was worse. She had twenty years of knowing exactly where bodies were weakest, because her whole education had been about what entered a body and what it did there. She drove her knee into the same place she'd seen a dying dog kicked, and when he folded she brought her elbow down on the back of his neck with her whole weight behind it, and he went into the leaves and did not get up. She stood over him shaking, tasting copper that wasn't hers, tasting his fear-sweat sour in the air, and discovered she was breathing in counts — four in, seven hold, four out — the way she breathed through the king's banquets, through the courses that might kill her.

*Survival is a life's whole work,* her mother had said. Apparently her mother had undersold it. Survival, it turned out, was also a weapon.

On the road, Wendell stood over the second hunter, chest heaving, sword red. The third lay crumpled by the milestone, groaning, alive. The apple-eater was gone — fled at the first clash, sensible to the last, and somewhere out in the world he was already telling someone what he'd seen.

Wendell limped toward her through the bracken, and she braced for the questions — *what are you, who taught you, what else have you hidden* — the reckoning she'd been dodging since the border stones.

Instead he looked at the unconscious hunter, then at her bleeding knuckles, then at her face, and said quietly: "You counted his breath."

"What?"

"When you came out of the trees. Four in, seven hold. I do the same thing before a fight. Only ever met one other person who breathes like that." He wiped his sword on the grass, not looking at her, giving her the mercy of not being looked at. "You've been swallowing poison for the crown your whole life, haven't you. That's the deliveries."

It wasn't a question, and she found she couldn't build a lie fast enough to meet it. "Since I was twelve," she said.

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and then he did something she did not expect. He bowed his head — briefly, awkwardly, a man out of practice at reverence — and said, "Then I owe you an apology, and a truth, and I'm not sure which will anger you more. But not here. The talker ran, which means by nightfall there will be more of them, and better ones."

They walked north through the failing light, side by side now instead of in file, and the silence between them had changed texture entirely — no longer the silence of mutual surveillance but of two people standing in the same burning house, deciding whether to say so.

At dusk, when they finally halted in a hollow under a granite shelf, Wendell built a small fire and fed it one stick at a time, and Beatrix boiled her water, and he watched her do it with new eyes. When the tea was done she poured two cups and handed him one, and he took it, and neither of them pretended it wasn't a treaty.

"The map," he said, staring into the flames. "You should know what it costs before we go another mile. Because I drew it, twenty years ago, for a bearer who died refusing to pay — and I swore I'd never watch that happen twice." He turned to look at her, firelight moving in his face, and she saw that underneath the rain-worn pragmatism he was terrified. "It doesn't open for thieves or heroes, Beatrix. It opens for someone bound by oath. Someone willing. And what it asks of the willing—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "What it asks, no one has ever paid and come home the same."

Beatrix held her cup and felt the map's cold presence against her sternum, patient as a seed, and far to the south, past the dark miles, the towers of Corvane stood lit against the night like a row of candles around something feeding.

"Tell me anyway," she said.
