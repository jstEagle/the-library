## 6. The City That Remembers

Corvane announced itself three hours before they saw it.

Beatrix tasted it first — a change in the wind coming down off the northern hills, a faint mineral bitterness riding the air like iron shavings on the tongue. She had been chewing a sprig of dried yarrow to keep her mouth honest, and the yarrow turned sour against her teeth, as though the plant itself had flinched.

"What is that?" she asked.

Wendell didn't look up from the road. He had walked with his hood up for two days, ever since the ambush at the ford, and he answered questions the way a man answers when he is counting something in his head. "The city."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He pulled the hood back just far enough to show his eyes, gray and tired. "Corvane's big enough to make its own weather, its own smells. Tanneries, coal smoke, the river. You'll taste all of it. Try not to catalog it."

But that was like telling her heart not to beat. Beatrix had spent eleven years cataloging flavors the way monks cataloged sins — methodically, obsessively, because her life depended on knowing which bitterness was almond and which was merely bitter. The air of Corvane arrived in her mouth like a dish she hadn't ordered: smoke and wet stone and horse, yes, but underneath it something else. Something old. It reminded her of the ash pit behind her mother's cottage after a long winter — the smell of things burned so thoroughly that even the memory of them being alive was gone.

She said nothing more. Wendell was watching her too closely these days, and she had learned that when Wendell watched her closely, it meant there was something he wasn't saying.

They crested the last hill as the sun went down, and Corvane opened beneath them like a wound in the world.

She had imagined cities from travelers' talk — the miller's cousin who'd seen Vellamark, the warden who once escorted prisoners to the coast. She had imagined noise and crowding and lights. What she had not imagined was the size of it, the way the walls swallowed the horizon, the way ten thousand chimneys breathed their smoke into one gray column that rose and rose until it became indistinguishable from the clouds. And she had not imagined the crown of it: the royal keep rising from the city's heart on its hill of black stone, taller than anything made by hands should be, its spires catching the last red light like blades dipped in blood.

Atop the highest spire flew the king's banner — a silver crown on black — and Beatrix felt the map shift against her ribs where it was sewn into her coat lining, cold as a fish slab in winter.

"The crown eats him," the courier had whispered, dying at her feet in the flour dust. She had carried those words across half a kingdom, and now she stood looking at the thing they described, and her mouth went dry.

"We go in at dawn," Wendell said. "Tonight we sleep in the outer wards, among the carter folk. In the city, you're my sister. We're come up from the salt country looking for work. You don't speak unless spoken to, and when you do, you speak dull."

"I'm very good at dull," Beatrix said. "I've spent my life pretending food tastes fine when it doesn't."

Something moved through his face — quick, quickly hidden. "That," he said, "is exactly what worries me about bringing you here."

* * *

The outer ward swallowed them at first light.

Beatrix had expected gates and guards; what she got was a tide. The road funneled carts and goats and pilgrims and peddlers through a gatehouse wide as a barn, and the guards there barely looked at faces — they looked at loads, at wheels, at the color of coin. A bored sergeant waved their cart through with two fingers, already turning to shout at a man whose axle had cracked, and then the city closed over them like water.

It was loud. It was overwhelming. It was also, Beatrix realized within a hundred paces, the most honest place she had ever been — and the least.

Honesty lived in the lower streets: bread that smelled of real wheat, onions frying in honest fat, tallow and sweat and live animals, the green tang of the river where laundry was beaten on stones. Her tongue read it all effortlessly, a thousand small truths. *No poison here. No rot here. This woman waters down her ale but doesn't hide it. This butcher's meat is three days old but sound.* Eleven years of training had made her tongue a ledger, and the ledger balanced.

Then they climbed.

The streets rose toward the keep in terraces, and with every terrace, the honesty thinned. The houses grew finer. The smells grew subtler. And the people changed.

She noticed the eyes before she understood them.

A tax clerk on the steps of the customs house, checking seals with a stylus of pale metal — his eyes were silver. Not gray, not pale blue. Silver, like coins left too long in vinegar, like the back of a spoon. He did not blink as often as a man should. When he looked up at the crowd, his gaze passed over everyone with the flat patience of a scale weighing nothing in particular.

"Wendell," she murmured. "The clerk. His eyes."

"I see them." His voice was low and even. "Don't stare. They notice staring."

"Them?"

He tilted his chin, almost imperceptibly, toward the terrace above. A woman in a magistrate's sash was reading a proclamation to a knot of merchants. Silver eyes. Two liveried runners flanking her — silver. An old man selling candied fruit who bowed too deeply as she passed — silver, though his rags said he should have had no business anywhere near a magistrate.

"How many?" Beatrix asked.

"In the upper city? More every year. Twenty years ago you could count them on one hand. Now—" He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "Now the civil service is very efficient. Nothing is lost. Nothing is late. No one complains, because complaints are heard perfectly and answered perfectly and nothing ever changes."

They climbed another terrace. The food stalls here sold things Beatrix could not name — pale jellies, glazed birds, sugar work shaped like crowns. She bought a honey cake from a vendor with ordinary brown eyes, mostly to have a reason to stand still, and bit into it while Wendell haggled over a room at a carter's inn.

The cake was beautiful. Golden, fragrant, dusted with sugar like new snow.

It tasted of ash.

Not burnt ash, not carelessness — she knew careless baking by heart. This was ash underneath the sweetness, ash woven through it, as if the flour itself had grown in burned ground, as if the honey had been drawn from flowers that fed on cinders. It was the taste from the wind on the road, concentrated, deliberate. And yet the vendor's customers ate and smiled and asked for more, and the vendor himself beamed with genuine pride.

Beatrix spat the rest into her palm, wrapped it in her kerchief, and walked away without running.

* * *

The carter's inn was called the Loaded Wain, and it was loud, greasy, and blessedly ordinary. The stew was mutton and barley and turnip, oversalted and undercooked, and Beatrix ate it with something close to joy, because every flavor in it told the truth.

Wendell took the corner table, back to the wall, and paid for the room in clipped copper without meeting anyone's eyes. Only when they were upstairs, in a room with one bed, one stool, and a window overlooking the stable yard, did he speak freely.

"Now you understand why I needed you," he said. "Or part of why."

"Do I?" Beatrix sat on the bed, arms around her knees. "Because from where I'm sitting, I understand less than when we started. What are the silver eyes, Wendell?"

"Marking." He lowered himself onto the stool, joints cracking. "When the thing in the crown takes a deeper hold of someone — when it decides they're useful — it changes them. Slowly. The eyes first. Then the taste for ordinary food goes. They eat, but it doesn't matter to them anymore. Eating becomes ceremony." He paused. "You spat out that cake."

"You saw."

"I see everything you eat. Have since the ford." He rubbed his jaw. "Here's the thing, Beatrix. Everyone in this city eats that cake. Everyone drinks that wine, breaks that bread at the king's table. To them it tastes fine. Better than fine — they love it. There's something in it, or in the ground it comes from, or in the air of this city, that the Regent puts its thumb on. And nobody can tell. Nobody has ever been able to tell. Except—"

"Except me." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Except you." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Eleven years swallowing poison for a crown that isn't even the right crown. Your tongue is the only instrument in this kingdom that reads true inside these walls. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Tired," she said.

He almost smiled. "Valuable. Dangerous. Terrified, probably. All of it. Every official in the upper city could be marked or unmarked and there's no way to know — except you. Every cup poured for the king could be clean or could be a leash, and there's no way to know — except you. The court hasn't had a taster who could actually taste in twenty years. They stopped needing one. Nothing poisons the king anymore." His voice dropped. "Because everything already belongs to it."

Beatrix looked at the window, at the slice of gray sky and the black spire beyond it. Her whole life she had believed her tongue was a curse — a thing done to her, dose by dose, until she was more instrument than girl. Her mother had called it survival. The crown had called it service. She had called it nothing at all, because naming it would have meant admitting what it cost.

And now, sitting in a cheap room in the enemy's city, she felt the curse turn over in her chest like a living thing and show her its other face.

Every silver-eyed clerk. Every ash-tasting cake. Every cup in every hall of that black-keep — invisible to ten thousand people, legible only to her. She was not a taster here. She was the only witness the kingdom had.

"I hate this," she said quietly.

"I know."

"No, I mean—" She unfolded herself and stood, pacing the three steps the room allowed. "In the valley, my tongue kept me alive. That was the bargain. Poison in, immunity out, and someday I'd be so well-armored nothing could touch me, and I'd grow old in Ashen Valley and die in the house I was born in. That was the whole shape of my life, Wendell. Small and safe and mine." She stopped at the window. "And now you're telling me the thing I hated most about myself is the only reason anyone should bring me within a mile of that keep."

"Yes."

"And if I'm wrong about a single flavor — if I mistake ash for spice, if my tongue fails me once—"

"Then you die," Wendell said simply. "Same as any taster. But hear me, Beatrix — the Regent doesn't poison. That's the horror of it. Everything offered to you will be genuinely, verifiably clean. Its weapons are invitations. Its traps are jobs. If it takes you, it will be because you said yes to something."

She thought of the valley then — suddenly, violently. The millrace in spring, the specific squeak of her mother's gate, the way the light came slantwise through the smoke-pines at evening. Magic couldn't follow her there. Nothing could follow her there. She had traded the whole world for that one small certainty, and she had never once regretted it, because regret required imagining alternatives.

Now she stood in the alternative, and it tasted of ash, and somewhere in the black keep a king wore a crown that ate him, and dying courier's blood was still — she was sure of it — under her fingernails, no matter how many times she scrubbed.

Below in the yard, a servant girl was drawing water, and the bucket's squeak was almost, almost the squeak of her mother's gate. Beatrix held onto that sound like a rope.

"All right," she said. "Tell me how a carter's sister gets into the royal kitchens."

* * *

The answer, it turned out, was bread.

The palace bought its ordinary loaves — the servants' bread, the guardroom bread — from a ring of licensed bakers in the third terrace, and the bakers hired extra hands each market day. Wendell knew this because Wendell, it seemed, had known Corvane intimately in another life, and every time he mentioned some detail of the city's workings — which gate took grain deliveries, which steward kept the kitchen rolls, which alley cut between the customs house and the keep's service stair — he spoke a little faster and then went quiet a little longer, as though the knowledge cost him something to spend.

On the fourth morning, Beatrix stood in the steam and roar of a baker's cellar with flour to her elbows, hauling trays, and listened.

Bakers' cellars were better than taverns for news. Hands worked, mouths ran. She learned which steward drank, which guard captain owed money, which under-cook had been summoned to the keep twice in one week and come back both times with his eyes a shade lighter. She learned that the king took his meals alone more often than not, and that the servers who attended him rotated constantly because, as one scullery maid put it with a nervous laugh, "they get strange, serving him. Quiet. Stare at the middle distance. Old Marta swears the soup talks back."

Everyone laughed. Beatrix did not.

And she learned, from a delivery boy complaining about his tips, that the palace was seeking a new assistant taster. The old one had retired — "or got promoted, or got eaten, depends who you ask" — and the Master of Household wanted someone vetted by month's end. References from the provinces preferred. Someone *unremarkable*.

Someone, Beatrix thought, tying off a flour sack, exactly like her.

That night she laid it out for Wendell on the inn room floor, tracing the keep's service ways in spilled flour: the bake-house gate, the tasting chamber off the buttery, the stair that supposedly led up toward the king's own table.

"They test candidates," Wendell said. "How will you pass a tasting trial without revealing what your tongue can do?"

"I won't reveal it. I'll hide it. That's the trick, isn't it?" She met his eyes. "Twenty years ago, whatever you did or didn't do for the last king — you've never said, and I'm not asking. But I know oaths, Wendell, better than you think. I know what it is to be bound to a role so long you can't remember who you were before. So trust me in this at least: the best way to hide a gift is to make it look like diligence. I'll taste carefully. I'll hesitate in the right places. I'll be good — good, not miraculous. Miraculous gets remembered. Good gets hired."

He studied her for a long moment, firelight moving in his gray eyes, and whatever war he was fighting behind them, she watched him lose it.

"There's a phrase," he said finally, "that the marked use among themselves. A greeting. If anyone says it to you — anyone — you come find me before you answer. Promise me."

"What phrase?"

"*The table is set.*"

Cold walked down Beatrix's spine, and she was suddenly, absurdly, back at her mother's table in the valley, age nine, learning that the difference between safe and deadly could hide in a spoonful. "What does it mean?"

"It means they know what you are," Wendell said. "And it means the next thing you taste might be the last thing you choose."

Outside, the bells of Corvane rang the hour — nine deep strokes rolling down from the keep, and beneath them, so faint she might have imagined it, Beatrix's tongue caught the taste of ash on the night wind, patient as a held breath.

Three days later, with forged references in a dead woman's name and flour still ghosting the creases of her knuckles, Beatrix Ashgrove walked through the bake-house gate of the royal keep, and the doors closed behind her with a sound like a swallow.
