## 7. An Audience of Ash

The kitchens of Corvane began before dawn, and Beatrix learned their rhythm the way she had once learned her mother's breathing through a thin wall — by listening in the dark until the dark told her its secrets.

She had been in the palace four days. Four days of flour-dusted aprons and lowered eyes, of answering to *Taster Ash*, a name she had not chosen but which fit her like an old glove. The master of the household, a silver-eyed man named Corvel who smelled of cold stone and something sweeter beneath it, had accepted her papers without reading them. Wendell's forger was good. Better than good. But Beatrix suspected the papers were never truly examined at all — that the palace took in new tasters the way a mouth takes in food, without much caring what it swallowed.

That thought did not comfort her.

She stood now in the scullery's pre-dawn chill, hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea, watching steam rise and vanish. Around her, the great kitchen woke: boys hauling water, women lighting ovens whose heat would soon press against the walls like a living thing, a butcher breaking down a carcass with the weary precision of long habit. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary smells. And threaded through all of it, faint as a hair in milk, that taste.

Ash.

Not woodsmoke, not char, nothing so honest. It was a sweetness gone wrong, a flavor like the memory of sugar burned past rescue. She had tasted it first on her second morning, in a pot of leek soup destined for the steward's table. No one else had noticed. The cook had sworn by every saint in the calendar that the leeks came fresh from the river terraces. Beatrix had held the spoon in her mouth for a full ten seconds, cataloguing the way her mother had taught her to catalogue — top note, body, finish — and the finish was ash, ash, ash, spreading across her tongue like frost across a window.

The food itself was clean. She was certain of it. Which meant the corruption was somewhere else — in the air, in the walls, in the mouths of the silver-eyed men who walked the corridors as though they owned the stones beneath them.

*Something is feeding here,* she thought, and the thought made her stomach clench around the tea. *And I am standing inside its mouth.*

"Taster." A kitchen girl, round-faced, breathless, one of the few whose eyes were still brown. "They want you in the west passage. His Majesty breaks his fast within the hour."

His Majesty. King Aldric. The man on the bloodstained map. The prisoner, if the courier had died telling the truth, bound to something ancient that wore his crown and spoke through his smile.

Beatrix set down her cup. Her hands were steady. Twenty years of swallowing death in small measured doses had taught her body a discipline her mind could only envy.

"Lead the way," she said.

* * *

The king's breakfast was served in a small chamber off the royal apartments, not the great hall — a room of pale stone and tall windows where morning light fell in long gold bars across a table set for one. One chair. One place. Beatrix noted this the way she noted everything now, automatically, the taster's inventory running beneath her thoughts like a second pulse: *one seat, no consort, no family, no guests. A man who eats alone in a kingdom of ten thousand tables.*

There were three dishes. A porridge of honey and cream. Smoked river trout, flaked. Bread still warm enough to steam when the knife went in. And wine, dark red, poured from a decanter of green glass.

Beatrix knelt at the sideboard where the under-steward indicated, arranging the tasting spoons in their row: silver, each one polished to a dull shine, each one a small altar at which she had worshipped her whole life. Behind her she heard the door open, heard footsteps — unhurried, heavy, the footsteps of a large man carrying something heavier than his body — and felt the temperature of the room change.

Not colder. That was the terrible thing. *Warmer.* As though something exhaled.

"Your Majesty," the under-steward murmured, "the new taster, sent up from the southern provinces. Taster Ash."

Silence. Long enough that Beatrix's neck prickled beneath her coif.

Then: "Let her work."

The voice was wrong. She knew it immediately, knew it the way her tongue knew poison — not by any single feature but by the whole of it, the way the notes sat together. It was a fine voice, deep and resonant, a voice built for command and kindness both. But underneath the resonance ran a second sound, a harmonic too low for ordinary ears, a vibration like a bell struck in another room. When the king spoke, two things spoke.

Beatrix bowed her head and picked up the first spoon.

*Porridge.* Cream from the palace dairies, honey from hives she could name the gardens of, oats cooked soft and salted with a careful hand. Clean. Clean. And beneath the clean — there. The ash-sweetness again, stronger here than anywhere else in the palace, rising off the king himself like heat off a forge. It clung to the spoon after her lips touched it. It lay over the porridge like a veil laid over a face.

She swallowed, held, waited the customary count. "The porridge is sound, Your Majesty."

"And the trout?"

*The trout* — smoked over alder wood, she guessed, with a rub of juniper that spoke of a cook who loved his craft. She tasted it twice. Clean. She said so.

The bread. The wine. She worked down the row of spoons with the litany she had spoken ten thousand times in the Ashen Valley, the prayer her body knew better than any scripture: *top note, body, finish; hold; swallow or spit; speak.* And all the while she felt him watching her. Not the way masters watch servants, idly, the gaze sliding off. He watched her the way a drowning man watches a rope thrown into water he has stopped believing can hold him.

When she reached the last dish and pronounced it sound, the under-steward nodded and withdrew to the doorway, and the king sat, and lifted his spoon, and ate.

Beatrix should have looked down. A good servant looks down. She fixed her eyes on the sideboard, on the row of spoons, on her own reflection bent dull and wavering in the silver —

— and in the reflection she saw his face.

He was perhaps forty-five, broad through the shoulders, with the kind of frame that had been built for armor and was being slowly repurposed for ceremony. Grey had taken his temples early. There were lines around his eyes that might have come from laughing, once, in a life before this room. And his eyes themselves were brown — brown, not silver, the last brown eyes she had seen in this palace outside the kitchens — and they were fixed on her reflection in the window glass with an expression she recognized the moment she saw it because she had worn it herself, every day, for twenty years.

It was the expression of someone eating poison on purpose.

Her breath caught. She kept her head bowed, kept her hands moving, stacking spoons, folding the cloth. In the window, the king chewed, and swallowed, and his throat moved, and his face did the thing that faces do when the body rebels and the will overrides it — a flicker, a flinch pressed flat, a small dying behind the eyes.

"The porridge is excellent," King Aldric said. "Tell the cook he has outdone himself."

*It tastes of ash,* said the look in his eyes. *Everything tastes of ash. Do you taste it? Can you taste it?*

"I will convey your pleasure, sire," Beatrix heard herself say.

He ate the trout. He praised the trout. He tore the warm bread and buttered it with slow, deliberate motions, a man performing the role of a man eating breakfast, and between each bite his gaze returned to her — always to her, always through the glass, always with that silent, screaming fluency that needed no words. *Please. Please. Please.* Not *help me*, even — he had perhaps passed beyond asking — just *please*, the way prisoners tap on walls, not expecting rescue, only wanting the wall to know someone is there.

Beatrix stacked the last spoon. Her fingers trembled, and she stilled them the way she stilled them before announcing hemlock, the way she had stilled them over a dying courier's map. *Steady. Steady. You are a tool, and tools do not shake.*

But her heart was not a tool. Her heart was doing something it had not done since the millrace, since the warden-captain's smooth, silver-eyed lies about what the elders knew. It was breaking — quietly, along old seams, the way things break when they have been broken before and mended badly.

*I know you,* she thought at him, across the sunlit room, through the glass, through the crown. *I know exactly what you are. I have been you. I am you. They feed us and call it service, and we swallow, and we say thank you.*

The king set down his napkin. Rose. The movement carried that doubled quality, flesh and something else moving in imperfect unison, like a puppeteer walking inside his own marionette. For a moment — one moment, as he turned toward the window, toward the light — his face was only a tired man's face, and his mouth moved around words that made no sound, shaping them carefully, deliberately, a message rehearsed in whatever privacy remained to him:

*Run.*

Then the other thing settled back over him like a lid, like a crown, and Aldric smiled the Regent's smile, warm and gracious and utterly empty, and said, "You have a careful tongue, Taster Ash. I hope you will serve at the evening meal as well."

"It would be my honor, sire."

He left. The door closed. The sunlight lay across the empty table like spilled honey, and Beatrix stood alone among the spoons with her heart hammering and her mind finally, horribly clear.

*He begged me,* she thought. *The King of Corvane looked at a servant girl over breakfast and begged.*

* * *

She found Wendell at midday, in the shrine-yard behind the tanners' row where the palace's cast-off wax and candle-ends were melted down — a place of smell and steam where no one lingered who didn't have to. He stood with his hood up, looking like what he pretended to be, a pilgrim leaving an offering, and only his eyes moved when she sat beside him on the low wall.

"Well?" His voice was rough. He hadn't slept; she could see it in the grey of his lips, hear it in the scrape of it. Twenty years ago he had drawn the map that lay hidden against her ribs, and twenty years of failure sat on him like wet wool.

"He's alive," Beatrix said. "He's in there. All of him."

Wendell went very still.

"The thing doesn't wear him like a mask, Wendell. It wears him like a coat — and he's still inside it, feeling every button. He tastes his own food. He knows what it's become." Her voice caught, and she hated it, and let it. "He praised the soup, Wendell. He praised it while his eyes were begging me to run. And the worst part—" she pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, against the ache there, "—the worst part is that I understood. Every day of my life, I put poison in my mouth and called it duty. He does the same thing. Only his poison talks."

Wendell stared at the melting wax, at the fat grey drips running into the trough. When he spoke, his voice was so quiet she had to lean close. "The last king was the same. Right up to the end. He'd look at me across the council table and I'd think, *he's still fighting, he's still in there* — and then I'd tell myself I was imagining it, because imagining it meant I had to act, and acting meant—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I waited too long to believe my own eyes. Don't you do the same."

"I'm not waiting." She pulled her coif lower against the wind. "Tonight I serve at the evening meal. The Regent's inner circle eats there — Corvel, the Lord Chancellor, the ones with silver eyes. If the binding shows in the food anywhere, it will show there. And Wendell—" she hesitated, then said it, because he deserved the truth and because saying it made it more real, "—when the king said *run*, it wasn't fear in his face. It was courtesy. He's been in there so long that his first instinct toward a stranger is to protect her. That's what's left of him. That's what the thing couldn't eat."

Wendell looked at her then, fully, and for a moment she saw the oath-keeper he must have been before the breaking — the steadiness of him, the weight. "Twenty years," he said, "I've told myself the man I failed was already lost. It was easier. It let me sleep." His jaw tightened. "You've given that lie back to me in one morning. I don't know whether to thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Beatrix rose, brushing wax-dust from her skirt, and felt the map shift against her ribs, cool as ever, patient as ever. "Tonight I taste for the Regent's own table. And I need you to understand what I tasted this morning, Wendell, so you can tell me if I'm mad."

"Say it."

"The binding isn't a chain. Chains rattle. This is quiet. It's *fed*." She wrapped her arms around herself against a chill that had nothing to do with the wind. "This morning, kneeling next to that food, I finally understood what the ash-taste is. It's not corruption leaking out of the king. It's the flavor of the binding itself — and it's strongest near him because that's where it feeds. Something ancient is sitting in that palace eating a king one day at a time, and it has grown so patient, so *accustomed*, that it lets him beg strangers over breakfast because it no longer believes anyone can hear him."

She turned to go, then stopped.

"One more thing. The evening meal — the invitation came through Corvel himself. He chose me. Out of eleven tasters, he chose the newest one, the one nobody knows." She met Wendell's eyes. "Either I've been very lucky, Wendell, or something in that palace already knows exactly what I am."

Behind them, the wax-trough steamed, and somewhere above the tanners' row, in a tower of pale stone, a king who tasted ash raised a cup to lips that were not entirely his own, and smiled, and smiled, and smiled.
