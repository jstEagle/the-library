## 8. The Oath-Keeper's Confession

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Wendell finally spoke.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, three days out of Corvane and two days of silence since the ambush on the river road — two days in which Beatrix had asked him nothing, which was worse than any question. She sat across the coals now, mending a tear in her sleeve with small, precise stitches, and she did not look up when he cleared his throat. But her hands went still. He noticed that. He had begun, without meaning to, to notice everything about her: the way she tasted the air before drinking from a stream, the way she counted exits in every room they entered, the way she watched him with those gray eyes that seemed to weigh whatever he said against some private scale and find it wanting.

She deserved better than wanting. She deserved the truth, and he had been rationing it like a coward rations bread.

"The map," he said. "You've been carrying it for weeks now. You've never once asked me where it leads."

Her needle paused mid-stitch. "I assumed you'd tell me when you were ready."

"That's generous."

"I'm not being generous." She bit the thread and looked at him at last across the firelight. "I'm being careful. There's a difference. You flinch every time I touch the satchel it's in. You sleep with your back to it. Whatever's at the end of that map, you're afraid of it — not for me. *Of* it. And I wanted to see how long you'd let me walk blind before your guilt got louder than your shame."

Wendell laughed — a short, startled sound, half pain. Twenty years an oath-keeper, twenty years a liar, and this poison-taster girl from a valley with no magic in it had read him in a fortnight.

"Longer than this, apparently," he said. "All right. All right." He fed a branch to the coals and watched the bark curl and blacken, because it was easier to speak to fire than to her face. "Do you know what an oath is? Not the word people say at weddings. Not the pretty vow a knight makes with a sword on his shoulder. I mean the true thing underneath."

"Something that binds," she said. "Like a knot. Like a contract written into the blood."

He stared at her. "How—"

"I taste things," Beatrix said simply. "When we passed through Marrowford, the well water tasted of iron and old promises. When the crown hunters caught us on the road, their blades tasted of something sweeter than steel — something that *wanted*. I thought everyone could taste it until I learned to stop mentioning it." She folded the mended sleeve over her knee. "So tell me what an oath is, Wendell. Tell me what I've been tasting."

He told her.

He told her about the First Hearth — though he did not yet say its name, circling it the way one circles a wound. In the beginning, he said, before kingdoms had borders or names, there had been a forge built where the world's first promise was spoken. Not stone and iron, not exactly. A place where words took weight. Every binding oath ever sworn by crown or covenant had been forged there, hammered out on an anvil older than the mountains, and every oath carried a spark of that first fire down through the centuries. Kings were crowned upon it. Kingdoms were sealed with it. And kings, sometimes, were bound by it — willingly, desperately, in ages when the alternatives were worse.

"Aldric's grandfather bound something to protect the realm," Wendell said. "A hunger older than the Hearth itself, something that had eaten its way through three dynasties before anyone thought to cage it rather than kill it. It couldn't be killed. So they bound it to the crown — chained to the throne, fed on ceremony, starved of everything else. And for sixty years it worked. For sixty years the thing in the crown slept, dreaming of silver, and the kingdom forgot it was ever in danger at all."

"And then?"

"And then Aldric did what good kings do." Wendell's voice went flat. "He renewed the binding. It has to be renewed each generation — fresh consent, freely given, or the chain rusts through. He knelt at the Hearth and swore himself as anchor, thinking it would be a formality, a ceremony, a line in a chronicle. But the thing had been patient for sixty years, and patience is the one weapon no binding accounts for. It had thinned the old words. Worn grooves in them, the way water wears stone. When Aldric spoke his oath, the chain didn't hold him outside the hunger. It pulled him *into* it. Crown and king and creature, all one knot now. The Regent, the court calls it, when they call it anything. Silver eyes. Perfect manners. A voice like a locked door."

Beatrix had gone very still. "And the map."

"The map is the way back to the Hearth." He reached into his coat — slowly, so she could see his hands, though she had never once flinched from them — and drew out the second object he had carried for twenty years: a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around a broken seal, the twin of the one on her map, cracked clean down the middle. "I know because I drew yours. Or its parent. Twenty-two years ago, for a man named King Osric, who held the throne before Aldric's father. The binding was failing even then. Osric commissioned a map to the First Hearth so that a bearer could carry the unbinding rite when the time came. I was his cartographer. I was also—" His throat closed. He made himself go on. "I was also the last person to swear myself as his oath-keeper. Bound to him, body and word, until the task was done or I was dead."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark beyond the hollow, an owl called and got no answer.

"There was a bearer," Beatrix said quietly. "Before me."

"There was a bearer." Wendell turned the broken seal over in his fingers. "His name was Tam Vesset. He was Osric's chamberlain — a soft-spoken man who liked cats and bad poetry, and who loved his king the way you love a brother. We reached the Hearth together, the two of us, in the winter of the year the Regent's agents burned every road behind us. And at the forge, Tam read the rite, and the rite was simple, Beatrix. That's the cruelty of it. It has always been simple. A king can be unbound only if another soul willingly takes the oath in his place. One binding exchanged for another. One life anchored against the hunger instead of his. That's all it has ever cost. That's all it will ever cost."

He heard her breath catch — a small sound, quickly mastered, but he had spent twenty years listening for sounds like that.

"Tam refused," Wendell said. "Of course he refused. Any decent person refuses. He stood in front of that ancient anvil with the rite in his hands and he said, *no, there must be another way, there is always another way,* and he turned to me and asked me to help him find it. And I—" The words came hard now, dragged up from somewhere deep and packed shut. "I told him there wasn't one. I told him I'd read every record, every fragment, every marginal note in the Hearth's archive. I told him the math was merciless: one willing soul, freely given, or the hunger stays on the throne and eats a kingdom a generation at a time. And he looked at me and said, *then let it eat me slowly too, Wendell, because I will not hand it a new meal.* And I hated him for it. God forgive me, in that moment I hated him, because I could see the future he was choosing, and it was full of ash."

"What happened to him?"

"The Regent's hunters found us before we left the mountain." Wendell's hands had gone steady now, terribly steady, the steadiness of a man holding something shut. "They came for the map. Tam burned it — burned my work, twenty years of my life in ink, burned it in the snow — and he bought me time to run. Me. The oath-keeper whose whole purpose was to stand between. He died on that mountain so that I could carry the knowledge out, and I have spent twenty years telling myself that someday I would find someone brave enough to do what he wouldn't, and that this would make his death mean something." He looked up at last, and let her see all of it, the whole ruined country of his face. "It doesn't make it mean anything. Nothing makes it mean anything. He refused, and he died, and the hunger kept the throne, and I broke my oath by surviving when my king needed me to stay. That's what a broken oath tastes like, if you're wondering. It tastes like nothing. Like a mouth full of cold water, forever."

* * *

For a long time Beatrix said nothing. She had picked up her needle again without seeming to realize it, and the stitches she made were crooked, the first crooked thing Wendell had ever seen her do.

"You said the rite needs a willing bearer," she said finally. "Not a willing victim. A bearer. What does that mean, exactly? What does the bearer become?"

"The anchor." He said it plainly; she had earned plainness. "The hunger needs a soul to root in — a living mind to wear the way a hand wears a glove. The binding doesn't kill the bearer. That would be mercy, and the Hearth has none. It means you feel it. Every day. Every hour. A patient, ancient appetite curled around your heart, whispering, always whispering, and your whole life becomes the work of saying no. Tam understood that. That's why he refused — not from cowardice. From clarity. He knew exactly what he was being asked to become, and he judged that no one should be asked." Wendell shook his head. "He was wrong, I think. Or right. I've gone back and forth for twenty years and I still don't know. But someone must be asked, or Aldric rots on that throne until there's no Aldric left at all, just the Regent wearing his face and eating his years."

"And the bearer has to be willing," Beatrix said. "Truly willing. The Hearth would know a lie."

"The Hearth knows everything about a soul that matters. It rejected a dozen false volunteers in the old records — men who came to bargain, to trade, to die dramatically for glory. The forge reads intent like I read terrain. If the willingness isn't real, clean, unforced — the rite simply fails, and the failure kills. That's how most of the false volunteers died. Not martyrs. Just mistakes."

"So it can't be forced on anyone. It can't be tricked onto anyone." Her needle had stopped again. "It has to be chosen."

"Yes."

"Then why am I here, Wendell?" She set the mending down and looked at him across the coals, and her voice was not angry, which somehow made it worse. "Why have you led me halfway across the kingdom toward a forge that requires a willing soul, if you knew all along what it costs? Did you think I'd volunteer? Did you think a food-taster from the Ashen Valley would hear *ancient hunger rooted in your heart forever* and say, yes please, where do I kneel?" She laughed, short and sharp. "Or did you bring me because you couldn't do it yourself, and you needed someone to fail in your place one more time?"

The accusation landed where she had aimed it, dead center, and Wendell found he could not even flinch. He had aimed it at himself for two decades; her version was cleaner.

"I brought you," he said, "because you were already carrying the map when I found you, and I told myself that meant fate, and fate is the lie cowards tell about coincidence." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough to the coals that the heat stung his face. "But I'll tell you the truth underneath the truth, since we're spending truth tonight. I brought you because when I saw you take that map from a dying man's hands — a stranger, a nobody, a girl with flour on her sleeves — you didn't run. Everyone runs. I've watched soldiers run, priests run, kings run. You knelt down next to him and closed his eyes. And I thought: *there. There is someone who does not look away from what's put in front of her.* And then I spent every mile since hating myself for what I was walking you toward, and telling myself I'd find another way before we arrived, and knowing there isn't one."

"There might be," Beatrix said. "There's always another way. Tam was right about that much."

"No."

"No?" She raised an eyebrow. "You told me the math was merciless. I've done math my whole life, Wendell. Rations, doses, the exact grain of arsenic that kills and the exact grain below it that only teaches the body to endure. Math is never merciless. Math is just honest. And honest math says: if the binding requires a willing soul, then the variable isn't the soul. It's the willingness. Someone chose to bind the hunger in the first place. Someone chose to renew it. Choices built this cage, and choices can be rebuilt." She stood, brushing pine needles from her skirt, and looked east, toward the dark line of the mountains where the First Hearth waited like a held breath. "You want my forgiveness for leading me here. You don't need it. I'm not Tam Vesset. I'm not going to refuse and die nobly on a mountain, and I'm not going to kneel at your forge either — not yet, not because a sad old liar with a broken seal tells me the math leaves no other answer. I'm going to that Hearth because I want to *see* it. I want to read the rite with my own eyes. And then I'll decide."

Wendell felt something crack open in his chest — not pain, or not only pain. Hope, maybe, which after twenty years felt almost identical.

"That's more than Tam ever gave the forge," he managed.

"Tam gave it his life," Beatrix said. "I intend to give it less and get more. Now sleep. You've talked enough for one night, and tomorrow we climb." She settled back against the oak roots, the map-satchel tucked under her head like a pillow, and closed her eyes as if the fate of a kingdom were a thing a body could simply set aside until morning.

Wendell did not sleep. He sat watching the coals and turning over the thing he had not told her — the last truth, the one he had swallowed with the rest and kept down. The records were clear that the Hearth required a willing, unsworn, unbound soul. They were equally clear on what disqualified a candidate: any existing oath, any prior binding, any debt already written into the blood. He had known that for twenty years. He had known it the moment he met her, and he had said nothing, because the knowing changed everything — because a girl raised in a valley where magic cannot follow, who had sworn no oaths and borne no bindings, whose whole life had been a fortress of refusal —

Because she was the only candidate left in the world.

The owl called again, closer this time. Wendell fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb toward the indifferent stars, and told himself, as he had told himself every night since the millrace, that he would find a way to spare her.

He was running out of road to believe it on.
