## 9. Silver Eyes

The summons came at dusk, carried by a page whose eyes caught the last of the light and held it too long.

Beatrix had been in Corvane for six days, wearing a servant's grey and a taster's silence, learning the rhythms of the palace kitchens the way she'd once learned the rhythms of her mother's garden — by watching, by waiting, by tasting what no one else would taste. She knew now which of the undercooks watered the wine. She knew which of the stewards skimmed from the market carts. And she knew, with a certainty that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone, that three of the king's privy counselors were not men at all.

Their food tasted of ash.

Not the honest ash of a hearth left untended — she'd grown up with that flavor, in the valley where magic could not follow, where everything burned clean and ordinary. This was ash with something underneath it, something sweet and slow and *patient*, like honey poured over a grave. When those men ate, something else ate with them. She could feel it on her tongue: a second appetite, vast and old, feeding through their mouths.

She had told Wendell as much, huddled in the loft above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and secrets. He had gone very still, the way he always did when the past reached up to touch him.

"Then it knows you're here," he'd said. "It was only ever a question of when."

The page found her in the scullery, of all places, up to her elbows in dishwater — a disguise so humble it had felt inviolable. The boy couldn't have been more than twelve, and his livery was immaculate, and when he spoke her name — not her false name, her *true* one, the one she'd buried at the valley's border stones — the water in the basin went cold around her wrists.

"Lady Ashgrove," he said, though she was no lady and had never been one. "The Regent requests your presence. Alone." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "He knows about the map."

* * *

They walked through corridors that grew quieter as they climbed, as if sound itself had been dismissed from service. The palace of Corvane was beautiful the way a preserved specimen is beautiful — every surface polished, every tapestry vivid, every candle burning at exactly the same height, and none of it alive. Beatrix catalogued it all out of habit: the faint bitterness of beeswax mixed with something mineral; the ghost of lilies, which she distrusted because lilies were funeral flowers and someone had chosen them deliberately; beneath it all, threading through the stone like groundwater, that sweetness-over-grave again.

The Regent was everywhere here. Or rather, its *appetite* was.

The chamber they brought her to was small, which surprised her. She had expected a throne room, all marble and menace. Instead: a study. Bookshelves. A fire burning low and clean. A table set for two, with a covered dish, two glasses of wine the color of garnets, and a man standing at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the city kindle into evening.

He turned, and Beatrix understood why they called them silver-eyed.

His eyes were not blind-white like a statue's. They were molten — quicksilver moving slowly behind the iris, catching the firelight and returning it changed, colder, older. His face was handsome in the way of coins: regular, stamped, worth exactly what it claimed. King Aldric's face. But worn differently. Worn *longer*.

"You're smaller than I expected," the Regent said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, but pitched lower, unhurried, the voice of something that had never once needed to raise it. "The couriers described a giantess. They described a warrior. They described, in one memorable case, a woman who breathed fire." It smiled, and the smile fit the face the way a glove fits a hand — correctly, and yet somehow wrong. "You are none of these things. You are a girl who washes dishes."

"I'm a girl who's still alive," Beatrix said, "which is more than I can say for your couriers."

Something flickered behind the silver. Not anger. *Interest.*

"Yes," the Regent said softly. "There it is. Sit down, Lady Ashgrove. Please. I've been looking forward to this conversation for longer than you've been alive, and I find I'd rather enjoy it than endure it."

She did not sit. She stood beside the chair instead, hands loose at her sides, weight balanced — the posture Wendell had drilled into her on the road without ever calling it training. *A taster stands where she can leave,* he'd said. *Always.*

The Regent observed this too, and its smile deepened by a degree.

"Sensible," it murmured. "Wendell taught you that. He was always sensible. That was his tragedy — he survived everything except his own conscience." It gestured, and the cover lifted from the dish on its own, steam rising: pheasant in a dark sauce, the smell of it rich and perfect and utterly without ash. "Eat with me. Or don't. I promise you nothing on this table will harm you. Harm is such a blunt instrument, and we are long past bluntness, you and I."

"We aren't anything, you and I."

"No?" The Regent finally sat, pouring the wine with a human grace that made her skin crawl precisely because it was flawless. "Then let me tell you what we are. You are the daughter of Maren Ashgrove, who was born Maren Vael, who took an oath at a forge older than this kingdom and then ran from it with my mark still warm on her skin. You are the woman who carries a map drawn in the blood of the last free court of Corvane. And you are—" it paused, savoring, the way she herself savored a first taste, "—the only creature in this kingdom whose tongue can tell my servants from my food. Do you know how that feels, from where I sit? To have built twenty years of perfect quiet, and to learn that one girl in a scullery can hear the clock ticking?"

Beatrix kept her face still. Inside, her heart was doing arithmetic. *It knows about Mother. It knows about the map. It knows what I can taste.* Every secret she and Wendell had hoarded like coins, spent already, laid out on this table between the pheasant and the wine.

"You talk a great deal," she said, "for something that's supposed to be patient."

The silver eyes brightened with what might have been genuine delight.

"Oh, I *like* you. Aldric would never have dared speak to me that way, even in the first years, even before he understood what he'd invited in." It turned its glass slowly on the tablecloth. "But you mistake me. Patience is not silence. Patience is knowing exactly how long you can afford to wait — and knowing, just as exactly, when waiting becomes waste. Which brings us to why you're here."

* * *

From inside, the thing that wore the king considered the girl across the table.

It had worn many faces over the centuries — this was merely the latest, and the finest, a body crowned young enough to shape and strong enough to last. It had eaten kingdoms before this one. It knew the recipe by heart: hollow the throne first, then the lords, then the priests, then the granaries, and let the people forget themselves last, gently, the way sleep comes. Corvane had been almost finished. Another decade, perhaps less. The village experiments alone had been exquisite — a whole town scraped clean of names, of memory, of the sticky human habit of *mattering*, and no army marching, no banner raised, no evidence but absence.

And then a courier had died at a millrace in the one place its reach could not follow, and a food-taster with poison in her bones had picked up a map.

The Regent did not fear armies. Armies were weather; you waited them out. It did not fear the Hearth — the forge was cold, its keepers dead or scattered, its door sealed by grief. What it feared was rarer. It feared the instruments it could not corrupt, because corruption was its entire method, and a tongue calloused against every poison was a tongue that could not be bribed with sweetness. One girl. One palate. The single thread by which its whole tapestry could be found and pulled.

So it would not threaten her. Threats made threads snap. It would offer.

* * *

"Here is what I know about you, Beatrix Ashgrove," the Regent said, and her name in its mouth sounded like a coin dropped in a well. "You grew up in the valley eating safe bread and drinking safe water, and you hated it — don't look surprised, hatred has a smell, and yours is very faint but very distinct. You hated it because safety is a wall, and walls are for things that are kept. So when the crown offered you employment as a taster — a position in which you would be *paid*, deliberately, to swallow ruin — you accepted. You have spent ten years making yourself a vessel for poison so that you could matter to people who will never remember your name. Your mother begged you not to go. You went anyway."

Each sentence landed like a physician's finger finding a fracture. Beatrix stood very still and let them land, because flinching was information too, and she would not give it.

"So understand," the Regent continued, gentler now, almost tender, "that I am not offering you a bribe. Bribes are for the corruptible, and you are not corruptible — it's why you frighten me, and I confess it freely, because honesty disarms far better than menace. No. I am offering you a *trade*. An exchange between professionals."

It rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the city — at the sprawl of roofs going gold in the dying light, the smoke rising straight and peaceful from ten thousand chimneys.

"The map leads to the First Hearth. You believe that. Wendell believes it harder, because he needs it to be true — guilt is a compass that always points at the bearer. But consider what the unbinding costs. The oath must pass to a living bearer. Someone must stand where Aldric stood and take what he took. Someone must wear, in whatever way the forge decides to interpret the word, the crown." It turned back to her. "You already know this. You've known since the night you read the map by candlelight while Wendell slept. You looked at him and thought: *not him. He's paid enough.* And then you thought of yourself, and you thought: *I am already a vessel. What is one more filling?*"

Her breath went thin. Because it was true. All of it, true, and hearing it spoken aloud in a dead king's voice was like seeing her own heart lifted dripping from her chest and held up to the light.

"So here is my trade," the Regent said. "Leave the map. Leave Wendell — he'll grieve, and then he'll drink, and then he'll survive, because that is the one thing he does superbly. Go home. Not to the valley — the valley is closed to you now, you must know that, the wardens saw your face and the elders do not forgive — but anywhere else. The southern coast, where the oranges grow. I will give you a house. Land. A name that opens doors. Money enough that your grandchildren's grandchildren will live like minor royalty and never know whence it came. And I will give you the one thing no gold can buy: I will make your tongue *quiet*."

Beatrix frowned. "What?"

"The ash-taste. The second appetite you feel when my servants eat. The constant low hum of wrongness you've been hearing since you crossed the border — yes, I know about that too, it's written all over you, you flinch at lilies." The Regent smiled again, and this time there was something almost wistful in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "I can take it away. Not dull it — *take* it. You would eat your meals like any other woman and taste only dinner. Ten years of poison-callus, and silence after. You would be, at long last, safe. Isn't that all you ever wanted? Isn't that the prayer you've been saying since you were a child watching your mother bar the door?"

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, the city murmured on, unaware.

And Beatrix — who had catalogued flavors like prayers, who had swallowed death in a hundred small doses so that others might eat unmolested, who had believed for thirty years that survival was a life's whole work — found that she was not tempted.

That was the revelation. Not the offer; the *absence* of temptation. She examined herself the way she examined a suspect broth, turning the feeling over, checking for hidden bitterness, and found none. The house meant nothing. The money meant less. Even the quiet tongue —

Even the quiet tongue.

"That's the flaw in your trade," she said slowly, and watched the silver eyes sharpen. "You think my tongue is my affliction. It isn't. It's the only part of me that's ever been *true*. Everything else — the valley, the job, the safe bread — everything else was a wall. My tongue is the window. And you're offering to brick it up and call it mercy."

The Regent said nothing. The firelight moved in its eyes like fish under ice.

"And another thing," she went on, warmer now, the words coming with the ease of long-swallowed truth. "You said harm is a blunt instrument. Fine. Then hear this plainly: I have tasted your servants. Three counselors, two captains, a bishop. I know the flavor of what you are. And I will spend the rest of my life — however long you allow it to be — tasting for everyone who can't. You can buy my silence. You cannot buy my *palate*. It's not for sale because it isn't mine to sell. It belongs to every mouth in this kingdom but mine."

For the first time, the Regent's smile failed.

It was a small failure — a half-second where the expression simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and behind it she glimpsed not rage but calculation, rapid and cold, a mind revising a ledger it had believed balanced. Twenty years of patience meeting its first immovable object, and the object was a dishwater girl with poison in her bones.

"You misunderstand your position," the Regent said at last, quietly.

"No," Beatrix said. "I really don't. I just don't care anymore. That's the part you didn't price in."

* * *

Later — much later, when the guards had escorted her back down through the beautiful dead corridors and released her into streets that suddenly smelled wonderfully, gloriously of ordinary filth — Wendell gripped her shoulders in the chandler's loft and searched her face by candlelight.

"What did it want? What did it say? Beatrix, *what happened*?"

"It offered me everything," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Underneath the steadiness, something enormous had shifted and settled, like a foundation stone finding bedrock. "My old life back. A new one, better. Safety. Silence." She looked at him — at the scarred, tired, faithful wreck of him — and felt the last of her hesitation fall away like a shed skin. "It showed me exactly what I am, Wendell. It thought that would break me."

"And did it?"

"It armed me." She turned toward the window, toward the distant bulk of the palace black against the stars, and felt the map's weight against her ribs where it rode beneath her shirt, warm now instead of cold. "It's afraid of me. Of *this*." She touched her own lips, absurdly, and almost laughed. "Twenty years of eating a kingdom, and the thing it fears most is a tongue."

Wendell stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to grin — the first real grin she'd ever seen on him, crooked and exhausted and alive.

"Then we'd best not waste it," he said. "The Hearth is nine days' ride. And Beatrix — after tonight, it won't send invitations again."

She was already reaching for her cloak.

Outside, high on the palace hill, a single window blazed silver against the dark — and did not go out until they were gone.
