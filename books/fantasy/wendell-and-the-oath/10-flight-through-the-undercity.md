## 10. Flight Through the Undercity

The Regent's offer still sat on Beatrix's tongue like a coin held too long in the mouth — cold at first, then warming, then tasting of nothing at all.

She had said no. She was almost certain she had said no. The word had come out of her small and flat, the way a stone drops into a well, and the thing wearing the Seneschal's face had smiled with the Seneschal's mouth and said, *How like your mother,* and everything after that had happened very quickly.

Now there were boots on marble above her and a hand around her wrist and Wendell pulling her down a stair that smelled of tallow and old rain.

"Not the servants' way," he breathed. "They'll have sealed it. Down here."

"Down where?"

"The city keeps its bones under its skin." He moved through the dark as though the dark owed him familiarity, one hand trailing the wall, the other never quite leaving her arm. "Every capital is built on top of an older, less honest version of itself. Corvane most of all."

She could hear the pursuit behind them — not shouting; the Regent did not shout. It sent silence instead, the particular silence of men who had been told not to damage what they carried back. Silver eyes did not need torchlight. That thought kept her moving when her lungs began to burn.

*You could have said yes.* The temptation had been made so gently, that was the horror of it. Not gold flung across a table but a quiet room, a cup of tea that tasted of nothing — of *clean*, of the Ashen Valley's magicless air rendered in porcelain. *Your valley. Your mother. Your small safe life, returned to you intact. All you must do is forget what you tasted in the king's soup.*

She had spent her whole life believing survival was a life's whole work. The Regent had simply offered to let her retire from it.

And she had said no, and now she was running through the dark with a disgraced oath-keeper whose breath came ragged beside hers, and she could not decide whether she had refused out of courage or out of spite or because some poisoned, calloused part of her had finally learned to recognize sweetness offered too freely.

"Here." Wendell stopped at a wall that looked like every other wall and pressed his palm against a seam she couldn't see. Stone ground against stone. Cold air rolled out, wet and mineral, tasting of iron and deep places. "The cisterns. Old Corvane drank from these before the kings taught it to drink from lies."

"You've been down here before."

"I drew maps of it once." His voice did something complicated. "For a king who wanted to know if his own palace could be breached. It could. It can. Come on."

* * *

The undercity was a cathedral turned upside down. Pillars rose into darkness instead of descending from it; water dripped somewhere with the patience of a metronome counting out centuries. Their footsteps echoed wrong — doubled, tripled, as though other runners kept pace just out of sight.

Beatrix catalogued the air the way she catalogued everything now, the habit sunk so deep into her it had become the shape of her thinking. Wet stone. Rust. Rat. Somewhere far off, the sweet-rot of a drain gone septic. And beneath it all, faint as a hair in soup: that ash-taste. The Regent's flavor. It was in the water here. Diluted, ancient, but present — the way a poison lingers in a pot long after the meal has been served.

"It's been feeding on this city for generations," she whispered.

Wendell glanced back. "What?"

"The water. There's something in it. Something that tastes—" She searched for the word and hated that she needed one. "Patient."

He was quiet for three steps, four. "That's how it holds a kingdom," he said finally. "Not with armies. Armies are loud. It holds them the way rot holds a beam — from inside, slowly, until the whole house stands only because no one has leaned on it yet."

Ahead, a faint grey light marked where the cisterns opened toward the river gates. Beatrix felt her chest loosen half a degree. Beyond those gates, the salt road. Beyond that, the First Hearth, and whatever terrible arithmetic waited there.

Then a lantern bloomed behind them, soft and golden, and a voice she knew said her name.

"Liss," Beatrix breathed.

The servant girl stood at the mouth of the side passage with a lamp raised in one hand, and for one absurd moment Beatrix's heart did the foolish thing hearts do — it leapt. Liss, who had smuggled her bread in the palace kitchens. Liss, who had told her which corridors the silver-eyed ones avoided, who had pressed a key into her palm two nights ago and said *the west stair, my lady, go quickly and don't look back*. Liss, who had wept when Beatrix thanked her.

"I brought you this way on purpose," Liss said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. Rehearsed-steady, the steadiness of someone who has said a sentence over and over until it stops meaning anything. "They know, Bea. They knew before you ever reached the king's table. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. They have my brother."

Beatrix understood then, all at once and forever, the way one understands a poison only after swallowing it. The kindness had been real. That was the cruelest engineering of it. The Regent did not buy people with threats alone; it bought them with true things wrapped around false ones, a brother genuinely imprisoned, tears genuinely shed, a knife genuinely hidden in the sleeve of genuine love.

"They promised he'd live," Liss said, and now her voice broke, and the lamp trembled, and Beatrix saw the glint of steel drop from the girl's left sleeve into her waiting hand. "Please don't make me—"

"Liss—"

The girl moved faster than her weeping suggested she could.

Later, Beatrix would replay it a thousand times and always arrive at the same fraction of a second too late. She saw the blade come. Her body — trained by ten years of poisons to read danger in the smallest signals — saw it, named it, and froze anyway, because the hand holding it belonged to someone who had cried over spilled flour with her, and her training had never covered this. No taster's discipline prepared you for a wound delivered by a friend.

Wendell's body covered it for her.

He stepped between — not gracefully, not heroically, just *immediately*, the way a wall is where it is — and the blade went in under his ribs with a sound Beatrix felt in her own body, a wet, intimate sound, the sound of a door opening that should stay shut.

Liss screamed and dropped the lamp. It guttered on wet stone. In its dying light Beatrix caught the girl's face — horror, real horror, she hadn't known, perhaps she truly hadn't known they meant killing — and then footsteps, many of them, coming fast along the passage the girl had led them to, and Liss fled into the dark crying her brother's name like a prayer she no longer believed in.

"Wendell." Beatrix caught him as he folded. He was heavier than he looked, or fear made him so. Blood spread warm and shocking through her hands. "No, no — stay with me, keep pressure, look at me—"

"Gate's—" He coughed. "Thirty paces. Grey light. Go."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You have to—" Another cough, worse. "Map needs a bearer. Doesn't need me. Never did. I'm just—" his mouth twisted into something that wanted to be a smile, "—the man who knows the way, and I've got us lost twice already, so honestly—"

"Shut up." She hauled his arm across her shoulders. He was right about the distance; thirty paces had never seemed so long. Each one cost him a sound he tried not to make, and each sound landed in her chest like a debt accruing interest. Behind them the boots grew louder, unhurried. The Regent's men did not hurry. Why hurry? The city was theirs down to its bones.

*This is your fault,* said a voice in her that sounded like the Regent, smooth and reasonable. *You refused. You chose this road, and now a man bleeds out on it, and a girl will carry her brother's ransom like a stone for the rest of her life. Say yes next time. Next time say yes.*

She put the voice away the way she'd learned to put away flavors that didn't belong — firmly, without ceremony — and dragged Wendell through the gate.

* * *

The river took them.

Not romantically; there was nothing romantic about clinging to a leaking skiff while a man shivered blood into the bilgewater below her and the current shoved them past the drowned foundations of old Corvane. But it took them, and it hid them, and by the time the walls fell away behind and the banks went soft with reeds, the pursuit had thinned to distant lanterns that gave up one by one, like stars going out before dawn.

Beatrix worked by feel and memory. Tourniquet — no, pressure, tourniquets killed more than they saved below the ribs. Clean cloth from the smuggler's bundle Wendell had cached in the boat, because of course he had cached supplies in the boat, because the man planned for failure the way other men planned for breakfast. She packed the wound, bound it, made him drink watered wine from the flask, and all the while her hands performed their work with a steadiness that felt like someone else's, while the rest of her shook.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Twice now. Once in the ambush, once tonight. You're statistically impossible."

"Oath-keepers are hard to kill." A whisper. "It's the keeping part that does for us."

"Don't joke."

"Wouldn't dream of it." His eyes found hers in the dark, fever-bright. "Beatrix. The girl. It wasn't her doing. Whatever you're carrying in there—" he tapped her sternum weakly, "—put it down. The Regent builds betrayals out of true things. That's its whole craft. You can't blame the brick for the architect."

"She cried."

"So did I, once, holding a knife I didn't want." He closed his eyes. "Everyone serves it a little, before they understand what they serve. The sin isn't serving. The sin is what you do the morning after you understand."

She looked at her hands. In the faint grey pre-dawn they were black with his blood to the wrists, and she thought: *I have tasted a hundred poisons and none of them ever got under my skin like this.* Guilt and tenderness had tangled themselves so thoroughly in her chest that she could no longer tell which was which. Duty said: keep him alive, he is the guide, the map requires his knowledge. Love — and she flinched from the word even in the privacy of her own skull, *love*, when had that happened, somewhere between the ambush and the wine flask, somewhere she hadn't been watching — love said the same thing in a different voice, and she could not separate the voices anymore, and maybe she didn't want to.

"Hold on," she said, which was not duty and not love but somehow both, and bent to the oars.

* * *

Dawn found the smugglers' caravan two miles up the salt road, and found Beatrix sitting in its straw-strewn bed watching Wendell breathe.

He slept badly, thrashing at dreams she suspected were twenty years old. But he breathed. The caravan's owner, a woman with a smuggler's indifference and a healer's hands, had pronounced the blade clean and the man stubborn, and had charged them an obscene sum for both observations.

Beatrix rationed their food with a taster's precision — she did not examine why, not yet, not fully — and kept watch, and watched him breathe, and counted the breaths like prayers.

Somewhere behind them, Corvane woke and ate its ash-flavored breakfast and praised its king. Somewhere ahead lay the First Hearth, and the truth about the map, and a price she could feel approaching the way animals feel weather.

In the map's lining, against her ribs, something pressed cold as a coin held too long in the mouth.

She reached for it without deciding to.
