## 12. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The road stopped being a road three days out of the smugglers' camp, and after that it was only a suggestion — a thread of packed earth winding between hills the color of old pewter, where the wind came down off the high country smelling of iron and cold stone. Beatrix walked with her hood up and her tongue pressed to the roof of her mouth, cataloguing the air the way she had learned to catalogue everything: *loam, pine resin, distant smoke, and beneath it all, faint as a fingerprint on glass, that taste of ash.*

It was everywhere now. She had hoped, leaving Corvane behind them, that the ash-taste would thin. It did not. If anything it grew more insistent as they climbed into country that no map in their possession bothered to name, and by the second morning she had stopped remarking on it to Wendell, because there was nothing either of them could do about it, and because saying it aloud made it heavier.

Wendell's wound was knitting. Slowly, badly, the way wounds knit on men who refused to rest them. He carried his arm bound against his chest and drove himself forward anyway, one foot and then the other, and when she caught him wincing he would say, "It's a long way to the Hearth," as though distance were an argument against pain.

"It's a long way to anywhere," Beatrix said. "That's not a reason."

They crested a rise in the late afternoon of the third day, and below them, cupped in a fold of the hills like water in a stone, lay a village.

It should not have been remarkable. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys; goats moved along a lane; a woman beat a rug over a fence rail with slow, patient strokes. There was a mill — smaller than the one at home, its wheel turning in a stream the color of weak tea — and fields beyond it, and a church-house with a bell that did not ring, or perhaps simply was never rung.

But Beatrix stood on the ridge for a long time before she could make herself go down toward it, and Wendell, watching her face, asked what she saw.

"Nothing," she said. And then, because that wasn't honest: "No roads leading out. Look. There's the track we came on, and there's the stream, and there are paths between the houses. But nothing going away from here. No cart-road east. No drovers' way south." She turned a slow circle. "Every village I've ever seen is a knot with threads coming out of it. This one has no threads."

Wendell studied it with the flat, assessing look of a man who had spent twenty years reading landscapes for ambushes. "Old places sometimes lose their roads," he said, but he didn't sound convinced, and neither of them pretended he was.

They went down anyway. The night was coming on cold, and Wendell's fever had been climbing since noon, and pride was a luxury they had stopped being able to afford somewhere back on the White Pass approach.

* * *

The first person to speak to them was a boy of perhaps ten, sitting on a wall near the millrace, whittling a stick into something that might have been a bird or might have been nothing at all.

"Evening," Beatrix said.

The boy looked up. His eyes were brown and ordinary and entirely empty of curiosity, which was the first wrong thing, though she did not know yet that it was wrong. Children looked at strangers. They stared, they hid, they demanded to know where you'd come from and whether your horse was yours. This boy looked at her the way a man looks at a fencepost.

"Evening," he said. "Are you staying?"

"We're hoping to. One night, if there's room. My friend is hurt."

"Is he?" The boy glanced at Wendell without interest. "There's beds at the longhouse. Mother will know which ones are free." He went back to his whittling.

"What's your name?" Beatrix asked.

The knife kept moving. Shave, shave, shave. "I don't have one," the boy said, pleasantly, the way another child might say *it's Tuesday*. "Nobody has names here. We tried them once but they wouldn't stay."

Beatrix felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs and sit there, cold. "They wouldn't stay," she repeated.

"They slide off," the boy said. "Like water off a duck. Father says it's easier not to bother." He held up his carving, considered it, and set it down on the wall beside him, next to a dozen others just like it — birds, or nothings, each abandoned at the same unfinished stage. "You can have one if you want. A name. We don't use them."

He said it kindly. That was the worst part, Beatrix thought later. There was no grief in him at all.

* * *

The longhouse was warm and dim and smelled of barley soup and tallow, and the people in it were — this was the thing Beatrix kept circling and could not stop circling — *kind*. An old woman with hands like knotted oak took one look at Wendell's grey face and bustled him onto a bench by the fire, clucking over the bandage, calling for hot water. A broad-shouldered man brought bread and hard cheese without being asked. A girl of fifteen or so sat down across from Beatrix and studied her with mild, bovine attention.

"You're from outside," the girl said.

"Yes."

"Is it nice outside?"

"It's—" Beatrix hesitated. "Some of it is. There are cities. Big ones, with towers. There's a sea, if you travel far enough. Have you never wanted to see it?"

The girl considered this with the unhurried patience of someone considering the weather. "I don't think so. Is there a reason to?"

"There's everything. Ships. Mountains taller than these. The capital—"

"What's the capital?"

"Corvane. Where the king lives."

"Oh." The girl nodded slowly. "We have a king, I think. Or we had one. Someone said so once." She frowned, and Beatrix watched the frown happen — watched the thought form and then dissolve behind the girl's eyes like sugar in warm water, leaving nothing. "Was that important? Having a king?"

And there it was again, under the tallow-smoke and the kindness: the taste of ash, stronger here than anywhere, so strong that Beatrix could barely taste her own soup past it. She set down her spoon.

"How long," she asked carefully, "has your village been here?"

"Always," the girl said.

"And before that? Who founded it? What was it called?"

The girl's brow furrowed again, that same gentle dissolution. "It doesn't — I mean, it isn't —" She laughed, a small confused sound, and gave up the effort the way one gives up lifting something too heavy. "It doesn't matter, does it? Nothing here matters very much. That's rather nice, actually."

*That's rather nice, actually.* Beatrix excused herself and went out into the yard, into the dark and the cold, and stood breathing until her stomach unclenched.

Wendell found her there, leaning against the well, wrapped in a blanket despite the fever, his eyes too bright.

"You tasted it," he said. Not a question.

"The whole village tastes of it. The well water, the grain, the soil. Like something steeped here for generations." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Wendell. What happened to these people?"

He was quiet a long moment. Firelight spilled through the longhouse door and lay in a yellow stripe across the frozen mud. "When I served the last king," he said finally, "there were stories. Places the crown's men visited and then — weren't quite themselves afterward. Records that went blank in the archives. A whole barony whose taxes stopped being collected because no one could remember it owed any." He rubbed his good hand over his face. "I thought they were stories. Drunk soldiers' tales. But the Regent has been wearing the crown for twenty years, and before that it wore Aldric's father, and before that — how far back does a thing like that reach, Beatrix? How many villages does it prune? Places that saw too much, or knew too much, or simply stood in the way of some arrangement it preferred forgotten."

"But why forget *names*? Why children's names?"

"Because a name is a claim," Wendell said. "A name says you belong to someone, to somewhere, to a story. You can't grieve what you can't hold onto. You can't rebel for a place you can't name." He looked back at the longhouse, at the firelit windows and the slow, contented shapes moving behind them. "It doesn't kill them. Killing leaves bones, and bones leave questions. It just — files them smooth. Rounds off every edge that could catch a memory."

Beatrix thought of her mother's warning, spoken a hundred times in the kitchen in Ashen Valley: *Never leave. Magic cannot follow you there. Promise me.* She had always imagined the danger as teeth — hunters, blades, the crown's men on the road. She had never imagined it like this: a village full of gentle, smiling people, whittling the same unfinished bird forever, while something vast and patient drank the story out of them one generation at a time.

"They're happy," she said, and hated how her voice shook. "That's the horror of it, Wendell. They're *content*. That girl — she thinks it's nice that nothing matters."

"That's the design of it," Wendell said quietly. "Fear fades. Contentment keeps."

* * *

She did not sleep. Toward the small hours she sat by the longhouse fire with the map open on her knees, and the wax seal — broken now, but still hers, still humming that faint cold note against her fingers — seemed louder than usual, as if it too could taste the ash in the air and wanted no part of it.

An old man shuffled over and lowered himself onto the bench beside her. He had the mildest face she had ever seen, seamed and pleasant, with watery eyes that reflected the fire.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"No."

"I sleep fine," he said, in a tone of mild wonder. "Everyone here sleeps fine. Isn't that something?" He warmed his hands. "My wife used to say I talked in my sleep. Used to say I called out names in the night." He smiled at the fire. "I don't anymore. Haven't for years. Funny, the things you grow out of."

Beatrix's throat closed. "Did you have children?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

"Oh, yes. Houseful, once." He said it cheerfully. Then the cheerfulness paused — a hitch, a skipped stitch — and for one instant something moved behind his eyes, something vast and drowning reaching up through deep water toward light. His hand trembled against his knee. His mouth opened.

"Granddaughter," he whispered. "There was a — she had a — it started with—"

The moment passed. The water closed over it. He blinked, and the mildness returned like frost reforming on a window, and he patted her hand kindly.

"Funny," he said. "Lost my thread. Happens all the time." He stood, joints cracking, and shuffled off toward his bed, contented, emptied, *filed smooth*, and Beatrix sat by the fire with tears running silently down her face and the map burning cold against her knees, and understood — with a clarity that felt like a blade laid flat against the skin — exactly what waited at the end of the Regent's patience.

Not conquest. Not fire. Just this: a kingdom of kind, sleeping faces, whittling the same unfinished bird, unable to say why they were weeping.

* * *

They left before dawn. Wendell argued — his fever was worse, the road harder — but Beatrix was already packing, movements sharp and fast, and when he saw her face he stopped arguing.

At the edge of the village the boy with the knife was waiting on his wall, in the blue-grey light, whittling.

"You're going," he said.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"Away. To fix something."

The boy considered this. "Will it work?"

"I don't know," Beatrix said honestly. "But I'm going to try."

"That's good," the boy said, and meant it, and returned to his carving. Then, as they turned to go, without looking up: "If you find any names out there — spare ones, that nobody's using — could you bring some back? We're short."

Beatrix walked fast after that, up out of the fold of the hills, and did not let herself look back, because she knew that if she did she would see the whole village waking to a day identical to every day, and she would want to burn it down or die for it, and neither would save a single child who could not even be called by name.

Behind them the sun came up over the nameless place, and the millwheel turned, and turned, and turned.

They had made maybe two miles, the village lost behind the shoulder of the hill, when Wendell stopped so abruptly she nearly walked into him. He was staring at the map, unrolled across his good forearm, and his face had gone the color of the morning frost.

"Beatrix," he said. "Look at the route. The First Hearth — the pass we need —" His finger traced the line and stopped. "This village sits directly on it. Which means the Regent didn't erase this place because it stood in the way." He looked up at her, and his eyes were terrible. "It erased it because of what's buried underneath. Something it never wanted anyone to reach. We've been sleeping on top of the one secret it's killed a whole village to keep."

Far behind them, faint on the wind, the church bell that was never rung began, very softly, to toll.
