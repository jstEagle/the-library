## 13. What My Mother Knew

The village had no name, and so Beatrix thought of it as the place where the bread was sweet.

That was how she catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything now — by taste, by the small honest signals of her tongue. The bread here was made with honey and a wildflower that grew only in the shadow of the shrine, and it tasted of nothing but itself. No ash beneath. No silver aftertaste. Whatever the Regent had done to erase this place from the kingdom's memory, it had not yet poisoned the ground.

They had been given a room above the shrine, a narrow cell with a straw pallet and a window that looked out on a courtyard where children played a game with knucklebones and no names for anything they owned. Wendell slept in the room below, or pretended to. His wound had closed into an angry red seam along his ribs, and he walked like a man carrying water in both hands — carefully, and aware of every step.

Beatrix sat cross-legged on the pallet with the map unrolled across her knees.

She had done this every night since the White Pass. Unroll it, study it, roll it up again. The route to the First Hearth was burned into her memory now; she could trace it blind, could feel the way the inked lines bent toward the mountains like iron filings toward a lodestone. There was nothing left to learn from it. She told herself she kept unrolling it because thoroughness was a habit of survival, and survival was a life's whole work, and some lessons did not unlearn themselves.

But that was a lie, and she was getting better at catching them.

The truth was that the map felt different since the pass. Heavier. Not in the hand — in the *attention*. When she smoothed it flat, something in the parchment seemed to smooth back, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight when you sit down beside it. And there was another thing, smaller, stranger: a thickness at the map's lower edge, along the border where the cartographer's flourish ran off the parchment's margin. A ridge beneath the ink, like a vein under skin.

Tonight, with the candle burning low and the village's silence pressing at the window, she stopped pretending.

She took the little knife she used for paring fruit — a taster's knife, short and curved, kept honed to a whisper — and set the blade's tip against the ridge. Then she paused. Twenty years of careful hands, and still she hesitated, because some part of her understood that once you opened a thing you could not close it again, and she had spent her whole life being the person things were *done* to rather than the person who did them.

*The crown eats him,* the courier had said. *The map is the only key.*

She cut.

The lining parted along a seam so fine she had missed it a hundred times, stitched with thread the color of the parchment itself. Inside lay a second sheet, folded eight times, thin as onion skin, and pressed so flat against the map's backing that it had taken on the map's faint warmth and would not give it up.

A letter. In handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her mother's hand. The tall, slanted letters; the way the *r*s crossed their own tails; the little hook she put on the end of *y*, like a question mark that had given up halfway. Beatrix had seen that hand on market lists and medicine labels and one letter, years ago, that had come from an aunt in the lowlands and which her mother had read twice and burned.

She did not open it immediately. She held it, and her hands — steady enough to lift poison past her lips without a tremor — shook.

Then she unfolded it, and read.

---

*My daughter, if you are reading this, then the map has found you, which means I have failed in every way that matters, and succeeded in the only way I ever prayed for.*

*I do not know if you remember me telling you about your grandmother's hands. You were small. I said she had baker's hands, and you asked why yours were not baker's hands too, and I said because you were going to be something safer. That was a lie. Most of what I told you was lies, and I have decided that if these words reach you, you deserve the truth even if it costs you the comfort of me.*

*I was an oath-bearer. Before you were a seed in me, before the valley, before any of it, I carried the same seal that is now pressed into that map. I swore at the First Hearth when I was nineteen years old, and I meant it the way the young mean things — completely, and without understanding what complete means.*

*You will want to know what my oath was. It was this: that should the king ever be bound, I would take the binding into myself and free him. That is the whole of the office. That is why there are so few of us, and why the crown let the order die. An oath-bearer is a door built in advance of the fire.*

*Twenty-two years ago, the binding came. I felt it the way you feel weather changing — a pressure behind the teeth, a wrongness in every meal I tasted. I went to the Hearth. I stood before it. And I refused.*

*Understand me: I did not fail. Failing is trying and falling. I looked at the fire that asks everything, and I said no. I said no because I had just learned I was carrying you, and I knew — the way you know the weight of a cup before you drink it — that the oath would not leave enough of me to raise a child. It takes the bearer's immunity, her home, her future. It leaves the oath and nothing else. I chose you over the kingdom, and I have never once regretted it, and I have regretted it every single day, and both of those things are true, and you may hate me for either or both.*

*I fled to the valley because magic cannot follow there. I thought that was the end of it. But oaths do not end, my love. They wait. The one I refused passed to the next bearer, a good man named Wendell Hale, who did not refuse — who went to Corvane and tried, and broke instead, and has carried that breaking for twenty years while believing himself the failure. He is not the failure. I am. Or neither of us is. I no longer know the word for what we are.*

*If you hold this letter, then the refusal has come down to you like eye color, like the shape of your chin. I am sorry. I wanted to give you safety and I gave you a debt instead, because inheritance is never what we are given. It is what we refuse to pass on — and I failed to refuse it. I wrapped it in a map and hid it in my own guilt and called it protection.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop, because my hand is tired and the candle is dying and you deserve better than a mother's excuses.*

*Your tongue is not a curse. I know they told you it was the price of the valley's safety, the slow poisoning that buys your immunity. That is true, but it is not the whole truth. Tasters' tongues were bred for this work, generation upon generation, by oath-bearers who needed to taste binding-magic in a king's food and know it for what it was. You are not an accident of the valley. You are the last instrument of an order everyone believes is dead. The Regent fears your tongue more than its hunters fear anything, and if you have gotten far enough to read this, you already know it.*

*I love you. I chose you. If you forgive me, forgive me for the choosing, not for the hiding. The hiding was cowardice. The choosing was the only clean thing I ever did.*

*— Your mother, who had another name once*

---

Beatrix read it three times.

The first time, she read it as words. The second time, she read it as a confession. The third time, she read it as a recipe — ingredient by ingredient, measure by measure, the way she read everything, because that was how her mind worked and her mother had known it would be, because her mother had the same mind, and had used it to swear away kingdoms and then to bake bread in a valley where nothing magical could reach.

When she finished the third reading, she discovered she was standing. She had no memory of rising. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width and the letter trembled in her grip, and the fury came up through her like heat through a kettle — not the hot flash of anger but the deep, rolling boil of betrayal long delayed.

*Every lie.* Every warning never to leave the valley. Every story about grandmothers with baker's hands. Every evening her mother had watched her swallow poison for the crown and said nothing, *said nothing*, while knowing — knowing — that the poison was not making her safe but making her *ready*. That the valley had not sheltered her. It had *stored* her, like a blade wrapped in oilcloth, like a vial stoppered and shelved against a need someone else would decide.

And Wendell. All those miles of road, all his silences, his careful half-truths about the map and its requirements — and he had known her mother. Had known her mother *by name*, had drawn the map for her, had watched Beatrix's face for twenty days looking for another woman's face in it, and had said nothing.

She was at the door before she knew she was moving, barefoot on cold boards, down the stairs, hammering on the door of the room below with the flat of her fist.

Wendell opened it with a knife already in his hand, because that was who he was, and then saw her face and lowered it. "Beatrix—"

"You knew her." Her voice came out low and terrible, a taster's voice, the voice she used to name a poison to a man's face. "You drew the map for her. You've known since the millrace — since the *border stones* — and you looked at me every day and said nothing."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part. He simply stood aside and let her into the room, and set the knife down on the table between them like a man laying down a card he couldn't win with.

"I knew her," he said. "Maren Ashgrove. Oath-bearer, third rank, sworn at nineteen. The bravest woman I ever met, until the night she wasn't." He rubbed his eyes with a hand scarred across the knuckles. "I drew that map for her, yes. Twenty-two years ago. She was supposed to carry it to the Hearth and speak the oath. Instead she disappeared, and I spent two decades thinking I'd failed alone. Then a courier dies at a millrace and presses that seal into a girl's hands, and I look at her, and I see Maren's chin, Maren's stubborn mouth, and I think — *the refusal has a daughter.*"

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because telling you would make it yours," he said simply. "And it was already yours. I've watched enough people be handed a fate they didn't ask for. I wasn't going to be the hand that did the handing. Not again."

"That wasn't your choice to make!"

"No," he agreed quietly. "It was hers. And she made it, and hid it in a letter, and now you've read it, and I'm sorry for the part I played in the silence. All of it. But Beatrix—" He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and for the first time since she'd known him he looked his age and older, worn down to the grain. "Read the letter again. Not the accusations. The last part. About your tongue."

"I read it."

"Then you understand what you are. Not a taster. Not a victim of the valley. The last instrument of a dead order, walking around with the one weapon the Regent actually fears, raised and sharpened and *stored* for twenty-six years." He shook his head slowly. "Your mother didn't betray you. She armed you. The difference between those two things is the whole history of mothers and daughters, and I'm not fool enough to referee it."

Beatrix laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it, surprise at herself more than anything. She sank onto the stool across from him, and the fury was still there, boiling, but underneath it something else had begun to move, the way ice moves before it breaks: forgiveness, arriving unwanted, ahead of schedule.

"She chose me," Beatrix said. "Over the kingdom. And then she spent my whole childhood teaching me to survive as if survival were the point, when really she was—" Her voice caught. "She was keeping the instrument safe. Keeping *me* safe. Both at once. And she couldn't tell me which one she was doing, because she didn't know herself."

"No one does," Wendell said. "That's what oaths teach you. Every choice costs a self. You just pick which self you can bear to lose."

They sat in silence while the candle drowned in its own wax. Somewhere below, the nameless villagers were banking their fires, and the smell of tomorrow's bread was already rising through the floorboards — honey and wildflower and nothing else, honest as stone.

At last Beatrix folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, the way her mother must have folded it, and slid it inside her shirt against her skin, where the map's cold used to burn.

"When we reach the Hearth," she said, "I'll speak the oath. I decided on the pass, maybe before. But I want you to know it isn't her debt I'm paying. It's not even Aldric's freedom, though God knows he's earned it." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and clear and very steady. "It's mine. She refused, and the refusal became mine, and I'm refusing *her refusal*. That's the only inheritance worth passing on — the choice itself. Everything else is just what we're given."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod at things too large for speech.

But as she rose to go, his head lifted sharply — the old soldier's reflex, the listening posture — and outside, far down the valley road, a sound reached them both through the shuttered window.

Hooves. Many of them, moving fast, and beneath the drum of them a thinner note that Beatrix's trained tongue could almost taste on the air: the high, silver ring of the Regent's hounds, loosed at last, and closing on the village that had forgotten its own name.
