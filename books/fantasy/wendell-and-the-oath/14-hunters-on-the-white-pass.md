## 14. Hunters on the White Pass

The snow began an hour before dawn, and Wendell counted it a mercy.

Snow meant tracks filled in. Snow meant scent killed, breath muffled, the world reduced to what a man could hold in his two hands and no more. For three days they had climbed out of the forgotten valley with its nameless dead, up switchbacks that clung to the mountain's flank like old scars, and for three days he had felt them behind — not seen, not heard, but *known*, the way one knows a storm has turned on the far side of a ridge. The Regent did not send ordinary hunters after oath-bearers. It sent the Vane Company, six of them, silver-eyed, patient as frost.

He paused at the lip of the pass and let the wind shove at him, testing it like a man tests a door. Above, the White Pass narrowed to a throat between walls of blue ice; below, the trail fell away into cloud. Somewhere past that throat lay the First Hearth, and somewhere behind him lay everything he had failed.

"Beatrix." He said her name quietly, because the mountain carried sound strangely. "How is your breathing?"

"Shallow. Fast. Same as yours." She came up beside him, hood rimed white, cheeks scoured red by wind. She had grown leaner on the road, harder-edged, but her eyes had not changed — those taster's eyes that weighed everything and gave back nothing until they were ready. "You're going to tell me we should have taken the low road."

"The low road is a river now."

"Then I'm going to tell you we should keep climbing."

He almost smiled. Almost. "There's a shelf above the narrows. Old shepherd track. If we can reach it before—"

The wind shifted.

Wendell went still in a way that twenty years of bad living had taught him was worth more than any sword drill. The wind had come around from the south, carrying pine and cold stone and — beneath it, faint as a hair across the tongue — something else. Ozone. Hot metal. The smell of a forge burning wet.

*Silver eyes smell like that,* he thought. *I'd forgotten.*

"They're close," Beatrix said.

He looked at her. "You can't possibly—"

"I've been tasting the air since the valley," she said simply. "It's how I taste everything. There's a bitterness on the wind that wasn't there yesterday. Like copper left too long in milk." She pulled her hood back, letting the snow land on her bare face, and breathed again, deliberate, the way she did over a king's cup. "Two hours behind us. Maybe less. They don't sleep, do they?"

"No."

"And they don't eat?"

"Not anything we'd recognize."

She nodded slowly, and he watched her do the arithmetic she always did — not of distance or numbers, but of bodies and needs. He had watched her ration their food through four provinces with a precision that had first seemed fussy and then seemed holy: this much salt, this much dried plum, this many mouthfuls of water, measured against the work required of them. She fed herself the way an apothecary feeds a fever, exactly enough and never more. He understood now that she had been doing it all her life. That survival was not something that happened to Beatrix Ashgrove. It was something she performed, daily, like a prayer said in a language only she remembered.

"Then we run for the narrows," she said. "And we don't stop."

They ran.

* * *

Beatrix's world shrank to rhythm: boot, breath, boot, breath, the crunch of crusted snow, Wendell's grey cloak flickering ahead of her like a moth against the dark. The pass rose around them, walls of ice groaning under their own weight, and the sky above the notch burned the color of a healing bruise. Her lungs were knives. Her legs were someone else's legs, borrowed and badly fitted. But her tongue — her tongue was clear, clearer than it had been in days, tasting the air the way she had once tasted soup for a king who was not a king.

Copper-in-milk. Closer now.

"Wendell." She caught his sleeve. "Stop. *Stop.*"

He turned, chest heaving, and she saw him read her face and go quiet.

"There's water here." She crouched beside the trail where meltwater threaded down the ice wall in a bright, glassy vein, pooling in a hollow of blue stone before spilling onward down the mountain. It was beautiful. It was the kind of thing a thirsty traveler would kneel to without thinking. She dipped two fingers into it, touched them to her tongue, and held still.

Bitterness bloomed at the back of her throat — not copper now but something greener, sharper, like crushed yew needles steeped in wine. She knew it. Gods help her, she knew it. She had tasted its cousin at court, in a cup of mulled wine meant for the ambassador from Selharrow, and had spent three days retching while the physicians marveled that she lived.

"Hemlock-kin," she said. "Something bred from hemlock. Someone dosed the meltwater above us. All of it — look, the whole vein runs through it." She wiped her fingers on the snow, heart hammering in a way that had nothing to do with the climb. "This isn't a trap on the road, Wendell. This is a trap on the *mountain*. They poisoned the pass itself. Every stream, every seep, every pool from here to the summit, I'd wager. They want us thirsty enough to drink, or weak enough to be taken when our throats close."

Wendell stared at the bright thread of water as if it had spoken. "That's why they're two hours behind instead of one. They climbed ahead in the night. Seeded the snowmelt." He laughed once, short and ugly. "Twenty years ago the Vane Company would have just cut your throat in your bed. Now they season the mountainside. The Regent's grown *patient*."

"It's had time to practice." She stood, and a thought arrived in her the way thoughts sometimes did — fully formed, cold, wearing its own consequences like a coat. "Wendell. How much of this poison would you say is in the water? A strong dose? Enough to kill six men?"

"Enough to kill sixty travelers. Why—"

"Because we're not travelers." She was already untying the small skin at her belt — the one she used for rinsing her mouth after court tastings, the one Wendell had teased her about in three languages. "And because I spent fifteen years learning to survive things built to kill kings. And because—" her hands were steady, she noticed distantly, steadier than they had ever been at court, "—because they expect us to be afraid of it. Nobody expects the quarry to bottle the trap."

Understanding moved across his face slowly, then all at once. "No," he said. "Absolutely not. You don't know the dose. You don't know the—"

"I know poisons the way you know oaths. By their weight, their temper, what they promise and what they actually deliver." She was filling the skin from the pool, watching the water catch the bruise-colored light. "This isn't pure hemlock-kin. There's a bitter top note — hellebore, maybe, or something crossed with it. Which means it acts slow on a full stomach and fast on an empty one." She almost smiled. "Good thing I've been starving us precisely, isn't it? Our stomachs are empty. Theirs won't be — hunters carry rations, real ones, meat and bread. They'll be slow-blooded. Heavy."

"You'd be poisoning armed men with water you scooped off a rock."

"I'd be doing what I've done my whole life, Wendell. Tasting what's in the cup and deciding who drinks." She corked the skin and slung it across her back, and found that her hands wanted to shake now that the decision was made, so she pressed them flat against her thighs until they obeyed. "Unless you have a better plan. Unless you'd like to explain to the Regent's silver-eyed butchers why we died politely."

For a long moment he said nothing. Snow gathered on his shoulders. Then he reached out and gripped her arm, hard, once, and let go.

"We get above the narrows," he said. "We find where the shelf overlooks the trail. And we make them come to us."

* * *

The shelf was exactly what a dying shepherd track promised: a ledge of broken granite beneath an overhang of ice, wide enough for two people to lie flat, high enough above the trail that stones loosened by a boot could fall a long, long way. Wendell stacked a cairn of loose rock at the edge with the calm of a man building a wall he intended to hide behind forever. Beatrix watched the trail below, and tasted the wind, and waited.

They came an hour past full light.

Six figures moving up the pass in a staggered line, unhurried, their grey-and-white gear melting into the snowfield so completely that Beatrix's eyes kept losing them even when she knew where to look. Only their faces betrayed them — pale, smooth, wrong, with eyes that caught the daylight and threw it back silver, like coins at the bottom of a well. The lead hunter stopped at the poisoned pool. Dipped two fingers. Tasted.

And smiled.

"They know we know," Beatrix breathed.

"Then they know we didn't drink." Wendell's voice was flat. "Which means they know we took some with us. Which means—" He exhaled. "They're deciding whether to rush us or starve us."

Below, the lead hunter raised one hand. The line split. Four continued up the trail toward the narrows. Two peeled away, climbing the eastern wall with a horrible, easy grace, finding handholds where Beatrix could see none.

"Flanking," she whispered. "They're coming up both sides."

"Yes." Wendell drew his knife — the plain, nicked knife he had carried since she'd known him, the knife of a man who had sold his sword years ago along with everything else. "Listen to me. When they reach the shelf, there will be a moment — a few heartbeats — when they have to commit. Come over the edge or fall back. Everything happens in that moment. You stay flat. You stay small. You let me do the rest."

"Wendell—"

"You wanted duty," he said, and his voice cracked on the word like river ice. "Well. This is mine. I stood at a door twenty years ago and let a king walk through it to die. I am not standing at another one."

"That's not duty," she said fiercely. "That's penance, and penance is just pride wearing sackcloth, my mother used to say, and she was right, and so am I — this isn't yours alone, you stubborn, broken, *brave* fool—"

The first hunter came over the edge.

It happened fast then, the way violence does — faster than telling, faster than thought. A silver-eyed shape rising out of the snow-dazzle like a fish breaking dark water, and Wendell meeting it with the cairn, shoving a hundredweight of stone over the lip into the second climber's face, and the mountain itself seemed to flinch at the crack of it. The first hunter landed on the shelf, blade already moving, and Wendell took the stroke on his forearm because there was nowhere else to take it, and Beatrix heard him grunt — not scream, *grunt*, as if pain were a tax he had budgeted for — and then they were locked together, rolling, the knife flashing, blood shocking-red on the white stone.

The second hunter was climbing. The third. Below, the four on the trail had begun to run.

*Now.* Beatrix rolled to the skin at her back, wrenched the cork free with her teeth, and waited — one heartbeat, two — until the third hunter hauled itself onto the shelf and turned toward her with those coin-bright eyes widening in something that might, in a human face, have been surprise.

She threw the water into its face.

It recoiled, gasping, and she saw the poison go to work the way she had known it would — fast, on empty lungs and open membranes, the green bitterness seizing breath and sight. The hunter staggered, clawing at its own eyes, and blundered backward off the shelf with a sound that stopped abruptly far below.

But the second one was through. It came over the rocks like winter itself, silent, and its blade found Wendell before he could turn — a long opening down his side, deep, deeper than deep, and Beatrix heard herself make a sound she had never made before, wordless, animal.

Wendell went down. The hunter stood over him, raising the blade.

Beatrix did not think. Thinking was for banquets. She seized the largest stone from the ruined cairn — a slab of granite half the size of her torso — and swung it in both arms into the hunter's knee, and felt something give with a crunch she would feel in her own bones forever. The hunter folded. She hit it again. And again. And when it finally lay still she was kneeling in the trampled snow, shaking, her hands red to the wrist, saying words she couldn't hear over the roaring of her own blood.

Below, the four runners had stopped. The lead hunter stood at the base of the shelf, looking up — at the fallen one, at the poisoned one, at Beatrix crouched over Wendell with a bloody stone still in her grip. Silver eyes met hers across forty feet of thin mountain air.

Then, deliberately, the hunter raised one hand — not in threat, but in something like acknowledgment, a duelist's salute — and the four of them withdrew, down-trail, into the cloud, unhurried, patient as frost.

They would regroup. They would report. The Regent would learn that the taster bottled its poison and broke its hunters on a mountainside, and next time—

Next time later. Beatrix dropped the stone and crawled to Wendell.

* * *

He was pale in a way that had nothing to do with cold, and the wound along his ribs was long and ugly, welling steadily despite the pad of cloth she had jammed against it. His eyes found her, focused with effort.

"Did we win?" he rasped.

"You nearly died holding a line I told you not to hold."

"Sounded... braver... the other way." A bubble of laughter cost him dearly; she saw the pain of it cross his face like weather. "Beatrix. Listen. If I—"

"Don't." She was binding the wound with strips torn from her own cloak, knotting them with hands that refused, absolutely refused, to tremble. "Don't you dare give me last words on a mountainside, Wendell of the Broken Oath. You haven't earned them. Last words are for men whose stories are finished, and yours—" her voice betrayed her, cracked clean through, "—yours isn't finished. Do you understand? I have watched you carry your failure for twenty years like a stone in your belly, and I have decided — *I* have decided — that you don't get to die until you put it down. So put it down. Live. Those are your orders."

His hand found hers, slick with his own blood, and gripped.

"This was never duty," he whispered. "Not since the millrace. Not since you looked at me with that map burning your hand and I thought — gods forgive me — I thought, *there she is*. The one I've been waiting to fail better for." His breath hitched. "I'm not keeping faith with a kingdom anymore, Beatrix. I'm keeping faith with you. Say you know that."

The wind came down the pass and scattered the snow around them like ash, and Beatrix Ashgrove, who had catalogued ten thousand flavors and given back nothing until she was ready, found she was ready now.

"I know it," she said. "I stopped pretending before you did. I just hadn't said it aloud." She bent and pressed her forehead to his, blood and snow and breath mingling. "We'll say it properly when you're not leaking. Come on. Up. There's a smuggler's caravan on the Salt Road below the pass, and you're going to hate every mile of it, and you're going to live."

He laughed again, weaker, and let her haul him upright, and together — leaning, limping, bound to each other more honestly than any oath could manage — they started down the southern slope, toward the cloud, toward the Hearth, toward whatever the Regent sent next.

Behind them, high on the shelf, the blood in the snow was already freezing.

And far below, in the white silence of the pass, Beatrix tasted the wind one last time — and caught, faint and unmistakable, riding the south wind like a whisper: smoke. Woodsmoke. A fire where no fire should be.

Someone was waiting for them in the valley below.
