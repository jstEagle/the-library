## 16. Wendell's Bargain

The First Hearth did not look like the end of anything.

That was the first cruelty of it. Wendell had carried the image of this place in his chest for twenty years — a forge of black iron and white fire, a cathedral of oath-stone where kings had knelt and risen changed. He had imagined it vast, terrible, humming with the weight of every promise ever spoken into it. Instead they came down through a collapsed colonnade into a hollow in the mountain's root, and found a room no larger than a granary, its ceiling lost in shadow, its walls streaked with soot that might have been centuries old or might have been yesterday.

At its center stood the hearth itself: a ring of stones no higher than his knee, cold as riverbed rock, holding nothing but ash.

"It's dead," Beatrix said behind him. Her voice echoed strangely, flattened, as though the room swallowed sound rather than returned it. "Or sleeping. Which is it?"

"I don't know." He set down the pack. His hands were steady. He had made them be steady for twenty years, and they obeyed him now out of long habit, the way an old dog obeys a master it has stopped loving. "When I was here last, it wasn't like this."

"When you were here last," she said, not unkindly, "a king died."

He didn't answer that. There was nothing to answer with that he hadn't already said on the Salt Road, in the smuggler's caravan, in the forgotten village where the people had smiled at them with empty eyes. The story had been told so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and still it cut him every time he picked it up.

He knelt at the hearth's edge instead and drew the map from his coat.

It had changed again. It had been changing since the White Pass — the ink migrating across the vellum like frost creeping up a window, lines dissolving and reforming, until what remained was less a map than a sentence. He spread it flat on the stone before him and read it aloud, because some things needed breath to be true:

*Here the first word was given. Here only a willing mouth may take it back. A king bound may be loosed by one who binds himself in the king's place — freely offered, fully spoken, never taken back.*

Below the words, in ink that seemed to darken even as he watched, was the oath itself. He knew it. Gods, he knew it. He had written these lines twenty years ago in a scriptorium that smelled of tallow and fear, copying from a source text so old the parchment had gone to lace. He had spoken them once, kneeling where Beatrix stood now, and King Maren had spoken them after him, and the Hearth had roared up white and hungry, and they had all believed —

And then Maren had flinched. At the last line. At *never taken back.* And the binding had slipped sideways, off the crown-eater and onto the king himself, and Wendell had spent two decades learning precisely how much a single hesitation could cost.

"Read it to me," Beatrix said.

"You've read it."

"Read it anyway. I want to hear how it sounds in the mouth of someone who's said it before."

So he read it. His voice filled the little hollow and died there. When he finished, the ash in the hearth stirred — just barely, just enough — and both of them went still as hares.

* * *

They made camp in the colonnade above, because neither of them could bear to sleep in the Hearth's presence. Wendell built a small fire with the practiced economy of a man who had built ten thousand of them alone, and Beatrix rationed their food with those precise hands of hers, dividing dried meat and hardtack into portions weighed to the half-ounce. He had stopped being surprised by it. Somewhere on the Salt Road he had understood that she had been at war her whole life, one measured swallow at a time, and that she would go on fighting this way until something killed her or she won.

He meant to tell her in the morning. He rehearsed it through the night watch while she slept sitting up against a fallen pillar, chin tucked, breathing light as a bird's. *The oath needs a bearer who is unsworn and unbound. I am neither. You are.* He turned the sentences over like coins, testing their weight, finding each one heavier than the last.

Because there was another sentence underneath them, the one he refused to say: *I will do it myself, if the Hearth will let me.*

In the grey of dawn he rose without waking her and went down alone.

* * *

The Hearth was colder in the morning, or seemed so. He stripped off his coat, his sword belt, the signet he had kept hidden against his skin for twenty years — Maren's signet, which he should have surrendered to the crown and had not, because surrendering it felt like admitting the king was truly gone. He laid everything in a neat row, the way a man prepares for burial, and knelt at the ring of stones.

"I know what I am," he said to the ash. His voice sounded foolish in the empty room. "I know what I'm asking."

Nothing moved.

He had expected that. He spoke anyway, because hope is a thing that must be spent to be spent honestly, and he began the oath from memory, the old words rising in him like water into a dry well:

"Hearth of first speaking, hearth of last hearing — I come unwilled by any hand but mine own. Take from me what I have left unkept, and let my keeping begin here—"

The ash shivered.

It was not much. A tremor across the surface, fine as sand on a drumhead. But Wendell had knelt in this room when it burned white-hot, had felt the Hearth's attention come down upon him like weather, and he knew the difference between a fire considering a supplicant and a fire turning away. This was the turning away. The ash settled. The cold deepened. And somewhere beneath the floor, in whatever marrow of stone the Hearth drew upon, he felt — distinctly, unmistakably — refusal.

He kept speaking. That was the worst of it; he kept speaking, line after line, his voice cracking on the old cadences, pouring himself into the ring of stones like a man bailing the sea with a cup. *Freely offered, fully spoken, never taken back.* He said the last line three times. He said it until his throat tore. He said it the way he had failed to say it for Maren, clean and whole and without flinching, giving the Hearth everything he had withheld twenty years ago and more besides.

The ash did not so much as lift.

When he finally stopped, the silence was total. Even the drip of water somewhere in the dark had ceased, as though the mountain were holding its breath around him. He knelt there a long time, forehead nearly touching the cold stones, and understood.

Broken oaths cannot hold new ones. He had known it, in the way a man knows the thing he cannot afford to know. An oath was not a spell to be cast but a vessel to be filled, and his had cracked the day Maren hesitated — no. Before that. The day Wendell had drawn the map and felt, in some chamber of himself he had never opened since, the first small doubt about whether any man could give what the Hearth asked. The crack had been in him all along. Maren's flinch had only shown it to the world.

You cannot pour new wine into a broken vessel. You cannot build a wall on a failed foundation. And you cannot ask a man who has already given his word and lost it to give it again, because the Hearth does not weigh intentions. It weighs capacity. And his capacity for keeping was spent, paid out over twenty years of penance that had never once been accepted, and there was nothing left in him to hold.

He laughed. It came out wrong, a dry rattle, and he pressed his fist against his mouth to stop it, and knelt there shaking in the dead forge where his life had ended twice.

* * *

Beatrix found him there. Of course she did. She had a taster's instincts for poison in a room, and he supposed that at this point he qualified.

She didn't speak at first. She came and stood beside him, looking down at the row of his belongings laid out like grave goods, and then she crouched, unhurried, and gathered them up piece by piece — coat, belt, signet — and held the signet out to him on her open palm.

"You tried," she said.

"The Hearth rejected me." His voice was gravel. "Twenty years of carrying this, and it took the Hearth four lines to tell me what I already—" He stopped. Started again. "A broken oath can't hold a new one. That's the arithmetic of it. The vessel's cracked. I'm cracked. There's no redemption in this room for men like me. There was never going to be."

"Wendell—"

"Do you know what I thought redemption looked like?" He got to his feet because staying kneeling felt too much like prayer and he had lost the right to pray. "I thought it looked like this. Coming back. Finishing it. Saying the words properly this time, taking the binding myself, and dying in a king's place the way I should have died in Maren's. Clean. Simple. A debt paid in full." He gestured at the cold ash. "And it won't even take the payment. It looks at me and sees a hole where a man used to be."

"That isn't what it sees."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've spent my whole life being looked at by things deciding what I'm worth," she said quietly. "Kings. Wardens. Poisoners. I know the look." She closed her fingers over the signet and pressed it back into his hand. "It doesn't see a hole, Wendell. It sees a man who already gave everything he had. There's a difference."

"There isn't. Not to the Hearth."

"No," she agreed. "Maybe not to the Hearth."

And then she was quiet, and he watched her turn toward the ring of stones, and the understanding arrived in him slowly, the way floodwater arrives in a valley — filling the low places first, then the fields, then rising past everything he had built to keep it out.

An unsworn person. An unbound person. Someone whose word had never been given and therefore could still be given; someone whose self belonged to no crown, no order, no valley, no vow. He had assumed, walking down here in the dark, that such a person could not exist — that everyone alive was bound to something, some debt or duty or love. Everyone except—

"Taster," he said. The old word, the dismissive one, and it broke in his mouth. "Beatrix."

"Don't." She didn't turn around.

"The oath needs someone the Hearth can hold. Someone unsworn. Unbound. Someone who has never—" His voice failed and he hated it. "There are perhaps five people in all the kingdoms who qualify. Three of them are children. One is a hermit in the Thornwood who couldn't survive the road. And the fifth is standing in front of me, and she is the only reason we made it this far, and I would sooner cut out my own tongue than say what I'm about to say."

"Then don't say it." Now she turned, and her face in the grey light was calm in the way he had learned to dread, the calm of a woman who had tasted a hundred poisons and catalogued every one. "Because I've already read the map, Wendell. I read it while you slept. I've known since the White Pass what it asks for. I was waiting to see whether you'd try to take it yourself first."

"And?"

"And now I know." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or its close cousin, relief. "You tried. That matters. I needed to know you'd try."

"Beatrix, listen to me." He crossed the space between them and took her shoulders, and she let him, which was somehow worse than if she hadn't. "Do you understand what it takes? Not death. Death would be mercy. It takes *everything you are*, poured into the binding and sealed there, forever. No valley. No mother. No garden, no morning bread, no name anyone speaks kindly. You'd become what Aldric has been — a mouth for something ancient to speak through, a prisoner wearing your own face. You told me yourself what you saw in him. You'd be telling it from inside."

"I know what I saw."

"Then you know why I can't—"

"You can't what?" Her voice stayed gentle, and gentleness, from her, was the sharpest blade in the room. "Stop me? Chain me? Carry me down the mountain and find one of the other four?" She shook her head. "Wendell. I have spent thirty years swallowing things so that other people wouldn't have to. My whole life, my whole *body*, built for exactly this — for taking in what would destroy anyone else and surviving it. Do you think it's a coincidence the map came to me? Do you think the courier stumbled?" She touched her own sternum, lightly, the way she did when she was naming a truth. "My mother ran from this. She hid me in the one place magic couldn't reach, and she called it safety, and it was — for a while. But the kingdom kept eating its kings, and the poison kept needing a tongue, and every road I could have walked led here eventually. Every single one."

"There has to be another way."

"There isn't. You taught me that. You're the one who says oaths are vessels." She stepped back, out of his hands, and looked at the cold hearth with something almost like tenderness. "And mine hasn't been spent on anything yet."

The silence that followed was the longest of his life. In it, Wendell heard every argument he owned and recognized, one by one, that none of them were honest — that what he was really saying, beneath the strategy and the sacrifice and the arithmetic of candidates, was simply *not you, please, gods, anyone but you,* and that this was not love's wisdom but love's cowardice, and that she deserved better than a coward's plea dressed as a keeper's caution.

"The Regent will feel it," he said at last, because it was the only true thing left. "The moment the Hearth wakes, it will feel the binding shift. It'll come for this mountain with everything it has. We'll need days, maybe. We have hours."

"Then we shouldn't waste them arguing." She was already moving toward the hearth, rolling up her sleeves, and there was something in the set of her shoulders he recognized from the banquet halls she had described to him — the taster at the table, lifting the spoon, knowing exactly what the cup contained.

"Wait." His voice cracked apart. "Let me at least — let me stand with you. Let me say the words with you, even if the Hearth won't hear mine. Let me do that much."

She paused. Looked back at him. And for the first time since the millrace, since the bloodstained map and the dying courier's whisper, she smiled — small and tired and utterly without fear.

"You can hold my hand," she said. "If the Hearth objects, it can refuse us both."

She knelt at the ring of stones. She smoothed the map flat before her. And Wendell of the Broken Oath knelt beside her, and reached for her hand, and felt beneath his own cracked palm the first faint warmth rising out of the ash — the Hearth, waking, turning its ancient attention upward, considering.

It was not looking at him.

It was looking at her.
