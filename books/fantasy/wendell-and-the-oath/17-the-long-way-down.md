## 17. The Long Way Down

The mountain gave them one last morning of kindness, and Beatrix spent it writing.

She sat on a flat stone above the smugglers' camp with the caravan's ink pot balanced on her knee — a chipped thing that leaked a little, so that every word she wrote was ringed in a faint blue bruise, as though the letters themselves had been struck. The paper had come from Wendell's pack, three sheets wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and when she'd asked him where a disgraced oath-keeper kept such fine paper he had only said, *For confessions. Everyone who does what I do should carry paper.* He hadn't asked what she meant to write. He had looked at her face for a long moment instead, the way he sometimes did, and then gone down the slope to argue with the caravan master about axle grease, which was how Wendell argued with everything he could not fix.

The first letter was the hardest, and it was to her mother.

*Mother.*

She stopped there for a long time. The pen hovered. Below her, the camp stirred — the smugglers banking their cookfire, a mule complaining at its tether, Wendell's grey head bent over the wagon tongue with his sleeves rolled up, doing work he was too proud to admit he enjoyed. Somewhere below all of that, two days' hard ride and a river crossing away, Corvane sat in its bowl of smoke like a spider in a web it had woven out of other people's lives. She could not see it from here. She could feel it anyway, the way you feel a storm behind a hill: a pressure in the ears, a taste at the back of the throat. Ash. It was always ash now, fainter than in the city but never quite gone, a flavor that had followed her since the throne room, since the Regent had leaned close and breathed silver at her and offered her whole life back to her like a merchant offering a bolt of cloth.

*You told me never to leave the valley,* she wrote. *You were right. I want you to know that first, before anything else, because I have thought about it every day since the millrace. You were right the way mothers are right — not because you knew what would happen, but because you loved me enough to want nothing ever to happen to me at all.*

The ink bled. She waited for it to dry before going on, because a taster learns patience the way other people learn to walk — early, painfully, and forever.

*I found your letter. In the lining of the map. I am not angry. That is a lie — I was furious for two days, and Wendell let me be furious, and then somewhere on the Salt Road the fury wore through and underneath it was something else. You carried an oath meant to save a king, and you put it down, and you ran, and you made me. Mother, I have stood where you stood. I have held the same choice in my hands, and it is heavier than any map. I understand now that you did not abandon the kingdom. You chose me over it. There is no word for that kind of courage except mother, and I did not earn the right to use it until this week.*

*So here is what I choose, and why you must not come after me.*

She told her plainly, because her mother had never once tolerated being protected from truth. She told her about the First Hearth, the ruined forge under the mountain where oaths had been hammered into the world before there were kingdoms to swear them. She told her about the instructions the map had resolved into at last, the old words surfacing through the bloodstains like bones through soil: *a king bound may be loosed only by another who takes the binding willingly, freely, with full knowledge of its cost.* She told her about Wendell standing in that cold place with his hands open and his heart offered up, and the Hearth turning him away — *broken oaths cannot hold new ones*, the words had said, or perhaps the silence had said them, the Hearth spoke mostly in silence — and the look on his face when he understood what that meant. Who was left. Who had been left all along.

*It has to be someone unsworn,* she wrote. *Unbound. Someone who has never given their word to anything bigger than themselves. Do you see? All those years you kept me in the valley, kept me small and safe and magicless — you were not hiding me. You were keeping me empty. Keeping me available. Whether you knew it or not, whether you meant it or not, you were forging the only weapon in the world that can kill the thing wearing Aldric's crown, and the forge was my childhood.*

*And I forgive you. I need you to read that sentence twice, because I needed to write it twice.*

*I am going down to Corvane. I am going to stand in front of the Regent and keep it talking while Wendell does the dangerous part beneath the floor, and then I am going to say words older than either of us, and take into myself what no one should have to hold alone. Maybe I die. The Hearth was honest about that, in its silent way. Maybe worse than dying — maybe I become what Aldric has been for twenty years, a mouth with something else speaking through it. But I have tasted every poison the crown ever sent to a banquet table, and I am still here, and I have learned the one thing a taster knows better than anyone alive:*

*Nothing kills you that you refuse to swallow.*

*Except this time I swallow it on purpose. That is the difference between us, Mother, and I do not mean it as a wound. You refused, and I lived. I accept, and maybe everyone else will. We are both right. Love is just choosing who you bleed for.*

*Do not cross the border stones. If I live, I will explain why someday, and you will laugh, or cry, or both, the way we do. If I don't live — plant something over my grave that tastes bitter. I would hate to be sweet. I was never any good at it.*

*Your daughter,
Beatrix*

She read it over once, folded it along the creases the paper already remembered, and sealed it with a drop of candle wax pressed under her thumb because she had no signet and no house and no name worth stamping. Just her thumbprint, which was, she supposed, the oldest seal there was.

* * *

The second letter took less time, though it was the one her hand shook over.

*Wendell.*

He was twenty feet away, arguing about axle grease. She could hear his voice rising and falling in its patient, stubborn rhythm, the voice of a man who had failed once, catastrophically, publicly, and had decided that the correct response was to spend the rest of his life being extremely careful about small things. Axles. Rations. The angle of a bedroll against the wind. Twenty years ago he had drawn the map in her pocket — had drawn it for a king he swore to save and watched die anyway — and every day since, she suspected, he had woken up and checked the small things, because the large thing had already broken him once and he would be damned if it broke him twice.

*By the time you read this I'll be gone.* No — she crossed it out. Too dramatic. He would make a face. *I've gone ahead. Don't chase me; you're slower than you think and I've had a head start.*

Better.

*Here is what I could not say at the Hearth, because if I said it out loud I would have talked myself out of everything, and I cannot afford to talk myself out of anything anymore.*

*Twenty years ago you drew a map to save a king. It didn't work, and you have carried that failure like a stone in your boot ever since, limping through every day of your life on it. I know what that's like, carrying a thing in the body that never stops hurting. I have swallowed poisons for six years, Wendell. Small doses, measured, deliberate, until my blood learned each one and turned it to nothing. That is what you did with your failure. You took it in small doses, daily, until you could carry it without dying. I used to think that was survival. Now I think it was a kind of loyalty — the ugliest, most faithful kind. You never stopped serving the king you lost. You just changed the shape of the service.*

*But hear me: the Hearth did not reject you because you are broken. It rejected you because you are already spoken for. Every oath you have left in you belongs to the dead. That is not a flaw. That is fidelity. And I will not let you spend your last coin buying back a debt that was never yours — the last bearer died refusing, yes, but you did not refuse. You came. Twenty years late, half-starved, hunted, with a map you drew yourself and a stranger you owed nothing to. You came. Whatever happens under Corvane, that settles the account. I settle it. I'm the taster; tasting is knowing; and I know you are clean.*

*Which brings me to the part I actually sat down to write.*

*If I live, I will be changed. The oath will burn my immunity out of me like fever burns a fever out, and what grows back will be worse — I'll taste magic everywhere, they say, always, in everything, forever, and there is no valley far enough to escape it. I may not be able to stand beside you without flinching at the taste of your own oath-keeping. I may not be pleasant company. I may spit at sunsets for the rest of my life because even light has a flavor now.*

*Come find me anyway.*

*That's the whole letter, really. Everything above it was just me being brave enough to write the last line. Come find me anyway. Not because I need saving — I am done needing that, and besides, you're slower than you think. Because I have spent six years cataloguing flavors like prayers and none of them ever tasted like home, and then some grey-haired liar with a broken oath shared a fire with me on a cold road and lied to me about rations and nearly died taking a blade meant for me, and I have not been able to tell duty from love since, and I have decided I am done trying to tell them apart. They were probably the same thing all along. Every taster knows the deadliest poisons are the ones that taste almost exactly like something good.*

*Don't read this until I'm gone. I mean it. I will know. I have a very good tongue.*

*B.*

She sealed it the same way, thumbprint in wax, and sat holding it a moment longer than the first. Then she tucked both letters into the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, where the map had ridden for so many weeks that the fabric had worn smooth in its shape. The map itself was blank now — spent, its secret delivered, its ink faded to ghost-lines — but she kept it anyway. Some things earned the right to be carried.

* * *

The third letter was the shortest, and she wrote it last, and it was the one that finally made her eyes sting.

*To the Ashen Valley, and everyone in it:*

*Thank you for keeping me safe. Thank you for the millrace and the bread ovens and the wardens' lanterns and the way the fog comes down the valley walls at dusk like a blanket pulled up by careful hands. Thank you for being the one place magic could not follow, and thank you for lying to me about why, because the lie was made of love even if the silence underneath it was made of fear.*

*I know now that the elders always knew about the king. I know you chose your peace over his freedom, year after year, generation after generation, and I know how easy that choice looks from inside warm walls. I made the opposite choice, but I am not writing to say you were wrong. I am writing to say: someone has to stay safe. Someone has to keep the bread ovens lit and the millrace turning and the fog coming down at dusk. A kingdom full of people like me would burn itself down in a week. Valleys full of people like you are the reason there is anything worth saving at all.*

*Keep being safe. Keep being soft. Keep feeding each other. When the story reaches you — and it will reach you, stories travel faster than armies — remember that the girl who left was not brave. She was terrified the entire time. She simply discovered that terror is not a reason to stop. It is just the flavor fear leaves in the mouth, and she had a tongue trained to notice flavors and keep swallowing anyway.*

*That is all a taster ever really does. Notice, and swallow anyway.*

*Beatrix*

She sealed it, and then she sat very still on her flat stone with three letters against her ribs and the wind coming down off the White Pass behind her, smelling of snow and iron and distance, and she realized that her hands had stopped shaking somewhere around the second letter and had not started again.

That was new. For six years her hands had shaken — at banquets, in the taster's alcove, whenever the steward set the covered dish in front of her and the whole room went quiet to watch her swallow. Fear had been her constant companion, her oldest colleague, the co-worker she ate every meal with. And now, on the morning she rode toward the worst thing in the world, her hands were steady as millstones.

Because — and the understanding arrived not as a thought but as a taste, clean and unmistakable, like cold water after years of wine — because she had already done the hardest thing. The choice was made. It had been made in the Hearth's cold silence, made again in the letters, made a third time just now in the act of folding them away. Everything left was only consequence, and consequence was just weather. You dressed for it. You walked into it. You did not pretend you could argue with it.

Choosing, she discovered, felt exactly like freedom. Nobody warned you that freedom would feel like falling with the ground a long way down and plenty of time to look at the view.

* * *

"You're leaving."

Wendell stood at the edge of the camp with a saddle over one shoulder and her horse already bridled — her horse, saddled by his hands, which meant he had known before she did, which meant he had watched her write three letters and pack her kit and had said nothing, because that was Wendell's whole religion: watch the small things, say nothing, be there when the large thing arrives.

"I'm going ahead," she said. "Down the river road. Alone."

"The Regent knows your face."

"It knows everyone's face. That's rather the problem."

"Beatrix." He set the saddle down. He came closer, and up close his eyes were the color of the valley fog she was never going to see again, and she hated him a little for making her notice that now, of all mornings. "Whatever speech you rehearsed on that rock, I've heard better speeches from mule traders. You don't go alone. We go together, we go loud, we go through the east gate with the caravan papers, and I stand between you and whatever comes out of that throne room until—"

"Until the Hearth's words leave my mouth." She held up the third letter — his — before he could see the address. "This is for you. You don't open it until I'm gone. Swear it."

"Don't ask me to swear things. You know what swearing costs people like us."

"Then promise it," she said, "which is cheaper, and which I'll believe anyway, because I have a very good tongue and it says you keep your promises even the cheap ones."

Something moved through his face then — grief and fury and, underneath, the terrible tenderness of a man watching someone walk into the fire he had spent twenty years standing outside of. He took the letter. His hand closed around it carefully, the way he handled everything that mattered, as though it might break, as though he might.

"If you die down there," he said quietly, "I will draw another map. To wherever they keep the dead. And I will come for you, and I will be slow about it, and I will complain the entire way."

"That," said Beatrix Ashgrove, royal food-taster, oath-bearer, daughter of the valley that could not follow her, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and I have tasted wedding cake for the crown."

She swung up into the saddle. The horse turned toward the river road without being asked, sensible animal, already pointed at the smoke on the horizon. Behind her the camp was striking tents; ahead of her the road dropped and dropped, switchbacking down through pine and shadow toward the plain, toward the river, toward the city that remembered everything except mercy.

Two days. Two days to Corvane, and then the throne room, and then the words.

She touched the letters at her ribs once, like a talisman, like a goodbye, like a promise — and rode down into the morning, and the mountain closed behind her like a door.
