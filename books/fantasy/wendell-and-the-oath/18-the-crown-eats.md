## 18. The Crown Eats

*Beatrix*

The throne room smelled of copper and cold stone and something sweeter underneath — honey left too long in the sun, turning. Beatrix catalogued it the way she had catalogued every dangerous thing she'd ever been asked to swallow: name it, place it, do not flinch.

She walked the length of the hall as though she belonged there. That was the first lesson of tasting for kings: a poisoner reads the room, but a survivor *becomes* furniture. Her slippers made no sound on the marble. The guards at the doors did not look at her — silver-eyed, all of them, their irises catching the torchlight like coins at the bottom of a well. She counted them anyway. Fourteen. Two by the eastern doors who shifted their weight when she passed, which meant they were new, which meant they were nervous, which meant they could be frightened.

Fear was an ingredient. She had learned that much from the Regent itself.

At the foot of the dais she stopped and bowed — not deep, not shallow. The bow of someone with something to trade.

King Aldric sat upon his throne like a man nailed to it. He was thinner than he had been in the great hall three days ago, before her identity had burned through the court like spilled wine through linen, before the Regent had stopped pretending she was a guest. His hands gripped the armrests. His eyes — his own eyes, brown, human, exhausted — found hers and held them with a desperation so naked it hurt to look at.

His mouth smiled.

"Beatrix Ashgrove," the mouth said, and the voice that came out of it was not Aldric's. It was older. It was patient the way rot is patient, the way winter is patient. It filled the hall without echoing, as if the stones themselves were speaking. "You've come at last. I wondered how long you would make me wait."

"I came because you asked," she said. "Twice. A third invitation begins to sound like begging."

Something moved behind the king's face — not quite anger. Interest. The silver in his eyes brightened by a degree.

"You are afraid," the Regent said.

"Yes." She let it show. There was no point hiding it; the thing could taste fear the way she tasted arsenic, instantly and completely. "I'm afraid. And I'm here anyway. You should find that more interesting than my fear."

The throne room was warm. Too warm for the season, and the warmth came from below — from the vault beneath the hall, where Wendell was even now, she prayed, working through the last of the wards with salt and iron and twenty years of guilt. She could feel the heat of it against her ankles like a hearth through a floor. Or perhaps that was only her heart.

"Sit," the Regent said, and a chair appeared — servants materializing from side doors, bearing a small table, a single cup, a steaming pot. They set it beside the dais and withdrew without ever lifting their eyes. Silver, all of them. Even the steam from the pot rose straight and unnaturally still.

Beatrix looked at the cup and did not move.

"It is tea," the Regent said. "Chamomile and honey. Nothing else. I give you my word."

"Your word," she said, "is the one thing in this kingdom I know the taste of. It's ash. Everything you touch turns to ash, eventually. Your officials. Your food. Your villages." She thought of the town that had forgotten its name, the mother who had looked at her child with polite blankness, and she let that grief rise into her face where the thing could see it. "I'll pour my own."

She crossed to the table, lifted the pot, and poured — and as she did, she *listened*. Not with her ears. With the calluses of her tongue and the back of her throat and the fine poisoned instrument of her whole body. The steam carried molecules to her, and her body read them like a letter: chamomile, yes. Honey. Water boiled twice. And beneath it, faint as a signature at the bottom of a page —

*Dreamless-sleep root. Half a measure. Enough to slow her, not enough to kill.*

So. The Regent wanted her alive and pliant. That was information. That was a weapon. She poured two cups, drank from hers — the poison and all, because her body would shrug it off the way it shrugged off everything, and because refusing entirely would tell the Regent she knew — and set the pot down with a steady hand.

"Good," the Regent said, watching her drink. "Now we can speak honestly."

"We were speaking honestly before," Beatrix said. "That's what you haven't understood about me yet. I have spent my whole life swallowing things that were meant to harm me. Lying to me is a waste of your considerable time."

The king's mouth curved. "Then let us be honest. You carry something. A map. An inheritance. You have seen the First Hearth — I felt it wake when you entered, felt it like a splinter in my heel. And you have come down from my mountain to take something from me."

"Not take." She sipped again, buying time, tasting the air above the braziers — woodsmoke, tallow, and under it, very faintly now, a thread of ozone and old iron rising through the floor seams. Wendell was close. Wendell was *through*, or nearly. "Give back."

The temperature of the room changed. Not literally — or maybe literally; she couldn't always tell anymore where the Regent ended and the world began. The torches guttered in unison, fourteen flames bowing like courtiers.

"Aldric dies without me," the Regent said, and now the voice dropped its patience and showed the thing beneath, vast and hungry and suddenly, terribly direct. "Twenty years I have kept this body breathing. Twenty years I have held his heart beating when it wanted to stop, kept his mind whole when the crown would have hollowed him. You would pull me out and leave him a corpse on this throne. Is that your mercy? Is that what the Hearth teaches?"

"That's what you teach," Beatrix said quietly. "That there is no way out that doesn't cost a self. But you're lying. You always lie. It's the shape of you."

"And what would you know of my shape?"

"Everything." She set down her cup. "Because I've been tasting you since the day I arrived in Corvane. In the bread. In the water. In the air of your council chambers. You seep, Regent. You leak into everything you rule, and everything you rule tastes of ash — except no one else can taste it. No one but me." She took a step toward the throne, and the guards stirred, and she stopped, palms open, harmless. "Your food-tasters died of 'wasting illness,' one after another, didn't they? Because they kept sampling the king's meals and the king's meals were seasoned with you. I am the first tongue in twenty years that your poison cannot kill. That frightens you. Don't bother denying it — I can smell fear, and yours smells like a struck match."

For a long moment the hall was silent except for the fire.

Then King Aldric laughed.

It was a broken sound, half sob, wrenched out of him sideways — and for one heartbeat the mouth was *his*, the voice was his, cracked and human and disbelieving. "*Oh,*" he gasped, and his brown eyes shone wet. "Oh, you magnificent — " And then the silver flooded back and the mouth smoothed into calm and the moment was gone, sealed away, a prisoner waving through bars.

The Regent let the silence stretch. When it spoke again, its voice had gone soft, intimate, almost kind — and Beatrix understood with a lurch that this was the true weapon, and it had been saving it.

"You want the boy-king free," it said. "Very well. Here is my bargain, taster. Walk away. Take your oath-keeper and your map and go back to your valley, and Aldric lives. I will release him from the throne — he will rule, gray and short-lived but *alive*, and you will have your victory. Refuse, and I will stop holding his heart together before you reach the door. He will die in front of you, and his blood will be on the Hearth's instructions, not mine." It tilted the king's head. "One life against a kingdom's freedom. You've already shown me what you think of such arithmetic. You fled a valley full of people who made the same trade for twenty years."

And there it was. The trap within the trap. Because the Regent was right — that was exactly the arithmetic the elders of the Ashen Valley had done, generation after generation, and called it wisdom, and called it survival, and called it *enough*.

Beatrix felt terror rise in her like floodwater. She let it. She took hold of it the way she had taken hold of every poison she'd ever swallowed — turned it over, examined it, made it hers. Terror was just information about what mattered. And what mattered was standing in front of her wearing a dead man's smile, buying her seconds with every word she traded.

Beneath the floor, faint as a heartbeat, she felt the heat pulse. Once. Twice. Wendell.

*Keep talking,* she told herself. *Talk until the mountain comes down.*

* * *

*Wendell*

The vault beneath the throne room had been built to keep things in, which meant, as with all prisons, it could be turned inside out.

Wendell crouched in the dark among the roots of the dais, blood on his knuckles from the last ward — his own blood; the final seal had demanded it, some old joke of the Hearth's masons — and worked the iron pry-bar into the seam of the anchor-casing. Above him, muffled through six feet of stone, he could hear the voices. Hers. The thing's. He could not make out words, only the rhythm, and the rhythm told him everything: she was stalling. She was trading sentences like a merchant trading coin, giving nothing, taking everything, keeping the creature's attention fixed on her face while he gutted its house from below.

*Don't die up there,* he thought, and then, because lying to himself had never once worked: *I love you, and don't you dare die up there.*

He had meant to say it properly, somewhere with better light. Perhaps on the Salt Road, watching her ration barley into seven identical portions with those precise, scarred fingers. Perhaps at the Hearth, before the oath, when there had still been time for words that weren't instructions. Instead he had said *stay behind me* and *watch the ridge* and *don't drink anything you didn't pour*, and she had said *yes* and *yes* and *I know my work*, and somehow that had been the truest conversation either of them had ever managed.

The casing groaned. Twenty years ago he had helped forge this anchor — he and Master Oren and the others, sweating over the Hearth's coals while young King Maren's binding was hammered into iron and buried beneath the coronation hall, because that was how it was done, because the alternative was unthinkable, because everyone said so. Everyone including him. Especially him. He had drawn the map afterward with shaking hands, the escape hatch none of them had the courage to use, and he had run, and Maren had died alone in a room full of people sworn to protect him.

*Not this time.* The words had become less a vow than a pulse, something his heart did between beats.

The seam split. Cold air breathed out of the gap — old air, air that had waited since before the Regent, since before the crown, since the First Hearth was a hearth and not a ruin. It smelled of coal and rain and the beginning of promises. Wendell pressed his eye to the crack and saw it: the anchor, a fist-sized knot of black iron fused to a vein of living stone, wrapped in chains of script that glowed dull silver. The Regent's tether. Its root. The thing it could not survive losing.

Above him, the voices paused. Then the Regent's voice rolled down through the stone, amused and terrible:

*"Oath-keeper. I know you're there. I have known since the pass."*

Wendell's blood went cold. He looked at the anchor. He looked up through the dark as if he could see through the floor to her face.

*"Come up,"* the Regent said. *"Watch what happens to kings who are loved. Or stay, and dig at your little root while I unmake the woman who came to save you. Choose quickly. I've grown tired of choices."*

Wendell closed his eyes. Breathed once, the way Oren had taught him — down to the bottom, hold, release.

Then he set the bar against the chain-script's weakest link, where the silver writing had thinned to a whisper, and he began to break the oath he had helped forge.

* * *

*Beatrix*

"The answer is no," Beatrix said.

The Regent's smile did not waver. "You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard them. Aldric's life for my compliance. It's the same offer you made in your chamber, dressed in different cloth — my safety for my silence, my home for my surrender. You only have one bargain, Regent. You've been offering it to this kingdom for twenty years, and the kingdom keeps signing." She stepped forward, one step, two, until the guards' spears lowered and she stood in the ring of them like a fly in amber. "But here's what your bargain never accounts for. Compliance isn't mine to give. I already gave myself away — to a map, to a road, to a man who failed a king and has been paying for it ever since. There's nothing left of me to sell. You're negotiating with an empty purse."

The silver eyes flared. For the first time, the mask slipped — really slipped — and what looked out of the king's face was not ancient patience but something rawer. Something *hurried*.

"You are bluffing," the Regent said.

"Am I?" Beatrix lifted her chin, and let the terror do its work — let it sharpen her voice, steady her hands, burn away everything in her that wasn't useful. This was the trick no one had ever taught her, the one she had taught herself across a thousand banquets: fear was not the opposite of courage. Fear was the raw material. Courage was what you forged it into. "Taste the air, Regent. Oh — forgive me. You can't. Only I can." She breathed in, slow and theatrical, reading the currents rising through the marble seams: hot iron, cold rain, coal-smoke, the First Hearth's ghost. "Something is burning below us. Something old. Can you feel your root getting warm?"

The king's hands spasmed on the armrests.

"No," the Regent said — and the word cracked down the middle.

"He's been under your floor for an hour," Beatrix said. "While you monologued. While you offered me tea. While you explained, at length, how inevitable you were." Another step. The guards looked at the Regent for orders and got none, because the Regent was listening — truly listening — to the ground for the first time in twenty years, and what it heard was the sound of its own burial being dug. "You wanted to know what I inherited from my mother? Not the valley. Not the immunity. She taught me the only lesson that matters: *when the powerful offer you a bargain, they are telling you what they fear.*"

The throne room shuddered.

It was small at first — a tremor in the flagstones, dust sighing from the rafters, the fourteen torches guttering as one. Then the floor seams began to glow, thin lines of gold-white light spreading outward from the dais like cracks in porcelain, and the smell of the First Hearth rolled up into the hall, coal and rain and beginnings, and every silver-eyed guard in the room cried out and staggered as the thing that filled them convulsed.

King Aldric screamed.

Both voices at once — the Regent's vast fury and the man's raw human agony, tangled together, indistinguishable, as the light climbed the throne and the crown on his head began, at last, to smoke.

Beatrix ran for the dais through the falling dust, through the shrieking, through the smell of a kingdom's long poisoning finally reaching the air — and beneath her feet, deep beneath, she heard iron begin to break.

"Hold on," she whispered — to Aldric, to Wendell, to herself, to everyone. "Hold on. I'm coming down to finish it."

The crown split along its band like an egg, and the light poured out.
