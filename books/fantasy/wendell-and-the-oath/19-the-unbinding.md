## 19. The Unbinding

The throne room of Corvane had been built to make a person feel small. Beatrix felt it now in the way the vaulted ceiling swallowed her voice before it could echo, in the way the banners of nine generations hung motionless in air that refused to breathe. But she had spent her whole life being small — small enough to slip past notice, small enough that poison passed through her like weather through an open window. Smallness was not the same as weakness. She had learned that on a mountain pass with hunters' blood freezing in the snowmelt.

She walked the length of the hall alone.

The Regent sat upon the throne wearing King Aldric's body the way a hand wears a glove — loosely, carelessly, because it did not intend to keep the glove long. Aldric's face watched her come, and behind the silver sheen of those eyes something human pressed against the surface like a drowning man against ice. His mouth smiled. His eyes begged.

*Hold on*, she thought at him, the way she had once thought flavors into words. *I am coming. I am slow, but I am coming.*

"You came back." The Regent's voice filled the hall without effort, resonant as a struck bell. It had stopped pretending to be one voice; now it was many, layered, ancient and patient and very slightly amused. "I confess I hoped you would. Hope is such a useful thing. It walks people exactly where you want them."

"I've noticed," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded steadier than her hands felt. "You offered me my old life. The valley. Safety. A purse full of gold and a door that locks."

"And you declined so rudely. Through a servant girl, no less. That stung, I'll admit. Mira wept when she told me. Or perhaps she didn't. It's difficult to keep track of which of them are mine anymore." The Regent rose, and Aldric's body moved wrong for a heartbeat — joints bending a half-second out of order, like a puppet remembering its strings. "Here is my last offer, taster. Not gold. Not safety. *Him.* Walk away tonight, take your broken oath-keeper, go plant whatever gardens you like, and the king lives. Old, fat, unremarkable, but alive. Refuse—" the silver eyes glittered, "—and I will wear him until he is dust, and then I will find another head, and then another, and your map and your hearth and your little act of defiance will have purchased nothing but a longer funeral."

Beatrix stopped three steps from the dais. Close enough to smell him. That was the thing no one understood about tasting: distance was everything. At three steps she could read the air off his skin, and what she read made her stomach clench even through twenty years of callus.

Ash. Not metaphor. Actual ash, old ash, the ash of burned oaths and burned names and a village that had forgotten its own children. And beneath it, faint as a vein of metal in stone: *hunger*. Patient, bottomless, the hunger of something that had been eating for so long it no longer remembered what it was eating *for*.

"You're afraid," she said quietly.

The hall went still. Even the torches seemed to lean in.

"Of me," she said. "Not of Wendell, not of armies, not of the map. Of my tongue. Because I'm the only creature in this kingdom who can taste what you are. Everyone else looks at their king and sees their king. I look at you and I taste rot in the wine and ash in the bread and something *feeding*, and I can't unknow it, and neither can anyone I tell. You don't fear my sword. You fear my mouth."

For a moment — one suspended, crystalline moment — the Regent's composure cracked, and what looked out through Aldric's face was vast and cold and *startled*, the way a hunter is startled when the prey turns.

Then it laughed, and the laugh shook dust from the rafters.

"A tongue," it said. "Twenty years of planning, a courier dead in a millrace, an oath-keeper dragged across half a kingdom, and the weapon is a *tongue*. Very well, little taster. Let us see how much truth a tongue can bear."

It snapped its fingers — Aldric's fingers, knuckles cracking like green wood — and the doors at the far end of the hall boomed open. Hunters poured in. Not the ones from the pass; these were worse. Court guards in polished black, moving with that same half-second-late precision, silver glinting at their temples where the Regent's influence sat in their blood like frost. Two dozen of them. Then four. Then more.

Beatrix did not run. Running was for people without a plan.

She reached beneath her cloak and drew out the map.

It had changed again on the road down from the First Hearth — the ink had migrated, reorganizing itself night by night until what remained was not geography at all but *language*: the oath itself, written in the first script, the one older than kingdoms, the one the Hearth had pressed into her mind while the forge-fire sang. She had copied it onto her own skin instead, in the only ink she trusted, tracing the words along her left forearm in the mixture Wendell had taught her — iron-gall and ash and one drop of her own blood, because every oath, the Hearth had shown her, begins in the body.

She unrolled the map anyway. Let them see it. Let the Regent see exactly what it thought it wanted.

"There it is," the Regent breathed, and hunger sharpened the many voices into one. "Bring it to me and the bargain stands. Bring it to me and—"

"No," Beatrix said. "You misunderstand. I didn't bring it to give to you. I brought it so you could watch."

And she began to speak.

* * *

The oath from the First Hearth was not words the way ordinary words were words. They were weights. Each syllable landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, and the well was deeper than she was, and the stones kept falling. She spoke them slowly, deliberately, the way she had once catalogued flavors at a banquet — *bitterness first, then iron, then the long sweet burn underneath* — because slowness was the only armor she had, and she needed every second.

*I, unsworn and unbound,* said the first line, and the torches guttered. *Freely choosing, freely bound.*

The hunters charged.

They never reached her. Above — or below; sound in the palace had always been confused about direction — there came a crack like the world's spine breaking, and the floor of the throne room shuddered. Dust rained from the vaulted ceiling. Somewhere beneath the hall, in the vault where the crown-anchor had slept for twenty years, Wendell was doing what broken men do best: finishing things.

*Another crack.* Louder. Closer. The great banners tore loose from their poles and slid to the floor like shed skins.

The hunters faltered, silver eyes flicking toward the sound, and Beatrix understood suddenly why the Regent had anchored itself to a crown at all — why something so vast had needed a piece of metal buried under a palace. An entity that eats must be *held* somewhere. A binding runs both ways. It had chained itself to the kingdom in order to feed on it, and chains, she thought, speaking the third line and feeling it burn up her arm like swallowed fire, *chains can be broken from either end.*

*I take into myself what was taken unwillingly,* she said. *I hold what he could not put down.*

The Regent screamed.

Not with Aldric's throat — with everything, with the walls themselves, with the torches and the flagstones and the frozen air. The sound went through Beatrix like a blade of cold, and she tasted it fully, helplessly, her tongue cataloguing even this: *gall and grave-dirt and the specific bitterness of a thing being unmade.* The silver light in the hunters' eyes flickered. Some of them simply fell over, unconscious, freed. Others clutched their heads and moaned. The Regent's attention — its vast, distributed, patient attention — was tearing itself in two directions at once: the woman speaking on the dais, and the anchor shattering beneath its house.

It chose her. Of course it chose her. She was the immediate threat; the anchor was already dying.

Aldric's body came down the steps toward her faster than any king should move, and the Regent wore him like a weapon now, all pretense abandoned, and Beatrix kept speaking because stopping was death and speaking was also probably death but at least it was *her* death, chosen, paid for, hers.

*What he inherited, I inherit. What he suffered, I suffer. What was passed to him, I refuse to pass on.*

That was the heart of it. That was the line the Hearth had saved for last, the line that had made her weep in the ruins while Wendell pretended to study the forge. Inheritance is not what we are given. Inheritance is what we refuse to hand forward. Her mother had known it — had carried an oath across a border and set it down forever rather than let it touch her daughter. And Beatrix had hated her for it, and forgiven her, and finally understood: her mother hadn't fled the kingdom. She'd ended a chain. Now Beatrix would end another, with her own body as the breaking place.

The Regent reached her. Silver hands closed around her throat — Aldric's hands, trembling, fighting, *begging* — and the pressure came down like a mountain, and Beatrix looked directly into the king's eyes and finished the oath through a crushed whisper:

*I bind myself, that he may be free.*

* * *

The world inverted.

She had expected pain. What came was worse and stranger: *taste*. Everything at once. The Regent poured into her — or she poured into it; the distinction dissolved — and she tasted all of it, every year, every fed-upon name, the village that forgot itself, the officials with frost in their blood, the courier bleeding out at the millrace with a map against his ribs, twenty years of quiet meals in a quiet valley, her mother's hands kneading bread, the first poison she ever swallowed at fifteen and the prayer she'd made of its flavor. All of it. All at once. She was a tongue and the world was on it, and it was too much, it was infinitely too much—

—and then Wendell broke the anchor.

She felt it happen inside herself, which should have been impossible and was simply true: a sound like a bell dying, a structural grief, the sensation of a load-bearing wall giving way. The binding's architecture collapsed. And the Regent, which had been pouring *into* her, found nothing left to pour into — no crown, no anchor, no structure, only a woman holding an oath with nothing underneath it, and an oath with nothing underneath it does not hold a prisoner.

*It holds a door. And doors close.*

The Regent unraveled.

There was no other word. The silver light that had lived behind Aldric's eyes tore loose from him in ribbons, screaming — actually screaming, a sound like a hundred villages forgetting their names simultaneously — and the ribbons thinned, and frayed, and the frayed ends smoked, and the smoke smelled of ash and then of nothing at all, and then there was no smoke, no ribbon, no scream. Just silence, arriving all at once like a wave.

Aldric collapsed.

Beatrix caught him — or he caught her; they went down together onto the cold flagstones, two bodies too exhausted to stand, and for a long moment neither moved. Then the king began to shake, and the shaking became weeping, ugly and human and loud, the weeping of a man who has been drowning for twenty years and has just felt air.

"It's gone," he gasped into her shoulder. "It's gone, it's gone, I can't hear it, I couldn't hear anything, do you understand, I was in a room with no doors for twenty years and I could hear it *eating*—"

"I know," Beatrix said. Her voice came out ruined, smoke-damaged, a stranger's voice. "I tasted it. Every meal. I'm sorry it took so long."

He pulled back and looked at her, and his eyes were brown. Ordinary, tired, human brown. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been handsome once and intended to be again.

"What did it cost you?" he asked. Kings, she supposed, learned to ask that question eventually. This one had learned it the hard way.

Beatrix tried to answer and discovered she didn't know yet. Something had changed in her mouth — she could feel it, a new sensitivity laid over the old calluses like silk over leather. The air itself had flavor now. The torches tasted of memory. The stones tasted of everyone who had ever walked them. She filed the terror of that away, the way she filed away everything, and said only:

"Ask me tomorrow. If I'm still here tomorrow, the price was survivable."

Footsteps on the stairs — uneven, limping, furious footsteps she knew better than her own heartbeat. Wendell emerged from the vault entrance gray with dust and dark with blood, carrying in both hands, like an offering or an accusation, the shattered halves of a crown.

He looked at the empty throne. He looked at the weeping king. He looked at Beatrix, sitting on the floor of the hall she had just unmade, and his face did something she had never seen it do in all their miles of road: it came apart entirely.

"You took it," he said hoarsely. "I told you — I told you what it required, and you — I was going to find another way, I was going to—"

"There wasn't one," she said gently. "You knew that before I did. You just couldn't say it." She held out her hand. "Come here, oath-keeper. Your king needs picking up, and I appear to be done standing for the moment."

Wendell crossed the hall, dropped the broken crown with a clang that echoed like a verdict, and knelt — not to the king, she noticed, but beside her. Around them, the palace was waking: guards stirring, servants peering through doors, someone shouting for physicians, someone else shouting that the silver was gone, *the silver is gone, look at their eyes, the silver is gone.*

King Aldric of Corvane got one knee under himself, then the other, and stood — swaying, weeping, free — in his own hall for the first time in twenty years.

"The taster," he said, and his voice cracked on the word. "What is your name? Your true name. I will not learn it secondhand."

"Beatrix," she said. "Beatrix Ashgrove. Of the Ashen Valley."

"Beatrix Ashgrove." He said it the way men say prayers they mean. "Corvane owes you a debt no crown can pay. Ask anything."

And Beatrix opened her mouth to say *nothing, ask me nothing, I want to sleep for a year* — but the words caught, because the taste of the hall had shifted again, subtly, and her new tongue, her cursed and consecrated tongue, caught something underneath the dust and relief and woodsmoke.

Faint. Faint as a vein of metal in stone.

Magic. Everywhere. In the walls, the water, the wounded men, the weeping king. Magic in everything, singing, ceaseless, and her immunity was gone, and it was never, ever going to stop.

"Wendell," she whispered, gripping his sleeve as the hall tilted gently sideways. "How long does the hearing last?"

He followed her gaze, read her face, and went pale. "Beatrix. What do you taste?"

She closed her eyes. Opened them. The whole world was on her tongue, and it was beautiful, and it was unbearable, and it was hers now, forever.

"Everything," she said. "I taste everything."
