## 20. Aftertaste

Wendell had counted deaths before. He had stood vigil over kings and kitchen boys, over the old king whose name he could no longer say without flinching, and he knew the arithmetic of a body on the edge — the shallow stutter of breath, the gray creeping up from the fingertips, the way the living seem to lean away from the dying as if death were contagious in reverse. He had done that arithmetic for three days now, kneeling at Beatrix's side in the smuggler's loft above the Salt Road, watching her chest rise and fall with the patience of a man counting coins he did not own.

On the third night, she opened her eyes.

He nearly knocked over the lamp reaching for her. "Beatrix."

Her gaze found him slowly, the way light finds the bottom of a well. Her lips were cracked, gray-tinged. When she spoke, her voice was a rasp worn down to wire. "The crown. Did it—"

"Shattered. All of it. The Regent unraveled screaming. Aldric is free." He swallowed. "You did it."

She closed her eyes again, and for one terrible moment Wendell thought he had watched her leave after all. But her hand moved — found his wrist, gripped it with a strength that surprised them both — and she said, "Then why does everything taste wrong?"

* * *

It took him a day to understand what she meant, and another day to wish he didn't.

The healer they'd paid in the village below — a woman with ink-stained fingers who asked no questions about the burn scar that spiraled up Beatrix's forearm like a brand — said what Wendell already feared: the poison immunity was gone. Burned away entirely, consumed as fuel by the oath, along with twenty years of careful, deliberate self-destruction. The calluses on her tongue, the tolerance built drop by drop since childhood, all of it scoured clean. She was, chemically speaking, a child again. One careless cup of hemlock would kill her now as easily as it would kill any milk-fed farm girl.

But that was not what made her lie awake at night, staring at the rafters.

"It's the magic," she told him on the fifth morning, sitting propped against the wall with a bowl of barley broth she had not touched. "I can taste it. Everywhere. In everything." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Twenty years I trained my tongue to find poison in wine. Now I can't stop finding *this*."

"Show me."

She dipped a finger in the broth, touched it to her tongue, and grimaced. "Iron. Old iron, like a key left in rain. That's the enchantment on this house — someone warded the door generations ago and never renewed it properly, so it's gone sour." She pointed at the window. "The sunlight coming through that glass tastes like honey left too long near a corpse. There's a blessing stitched into the blanket they gave me — someone's grandmother's work, a protection charm, and it's *sweet*, Wendell, cloyingly sweet, like syrup poured over something rotten, because whoever cast it was frightened when they did it and fear ferments." Her voice climbed, fraying. "The water in the well. The road outside. The air itself has an aftertaste, like copper and old smoke, and I think — I think that's the binding. The echo of it. What was in the crown, what I took into myself. It doesn't stop. It never stops."

The room was silent except for the wind worrying the shutters.

"You took the oath," Wendell said quietly. "The Hearth gave you its fire, and fire leaves ash."

"Ash." She looked at her hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "My mother spent my whole life teaching me how to be safe. How to survive. And I traded all of it in one sentence in a ruined forge." She looked up at him then, and her eyes were dry but terribly bright. "And I'd do it again. That's the worst part. I'd do it again, and I hate that I know that about myself."

Wendell knelt beside her, the way he had knelt at the border stones twenty years ago, waiting for a king who never came out alive. He had learned since then that some failures cannot be redeemed, only carried. He reached out and, very carefully, took her hand.

"I know," he said. "I've carried mine long enough to know the weight of it. But listen to me, Beatrix Ashgrove. You are not what was done to you. Not by the valley, not by the crown, not by the oath." His voice roughened. "You are what you refused to pass on."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a real laugh, small and startled out of her. "You've been saving that speech."

"For twenty years," he admitted. "I'm glad someone finally needed it."

* * *

King Aldric came himself, which no one expected and everyone should have.

He arrived at the smuggler's loft with four guards and no heralds, dressed plainly enough that the villagers who bowed to him in the street did so with confusion rather than ceremony. He had wept, they said, when he woke in his own bed for the first time in thirty years — wept like a man released from a prison he had forgotten he inhabited, asking after his wife dead these ten years, asking after children grown without him, asking after every day stolen while something ancient wore his face and praised soup through his mouth.

He climbed the stairs to the loft slowly, and when he saw Beatrix he stopped, and the king of Corvane bowed his head to a food-taster from the Ashen Valley.

"Wendell told me what you paid," he said. "All of it. The immunity. The valley. Your—" He faltered. "Your whole life, the one you built to stay alive. You gave it for a man you'd met once, across a bowl of soup."

"You begged me," Beatrix said simply. "With your eyes. While your mouth praised the soup."

Aldric flinched as if struck. Then he nodded, slowly, accepting the blow as his due. "Yes. I remember all of it. Every banquet. Every cup. Thirty years of watching my own hands do things I could not stop." He drew a breath. "I have offered you anything. I offer it again, formally, before witnesses. Land, title, gold, a place at court, a place far from court — anything within my power to give, and my power is considerable again, thanks to you."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Wendell watched her taste the words before answering, the way she tasted everything now, compulsively, hopelessly — and he realized with a lurch that she was tasting the king's promise itself, weighing it on a tongue that could no longer be fooled.

"Your Majesty," she said at last, "may I tell you what your court tastes like?"

Aldric went still.

"The silver-eyed ones are gone, but their offices still smell of it. The chancellery tastes of iron filings. The treasury tastes of teeth. Whatever the Regent touched for thirty years, it left a residue, and everyone who worked under it absorbed some, and none of them know." She folded her hands in her lap. "I don't want gold or land. I want to be useful in the only way I have left. Let me train tasters — real ones, not poison-swallowers but truth-swallowers. Let me teach them what I can taste, so no entity ever wears a crown in this kingdom again without someone noticing the flavor."

The king stared at her. Then he laughed — a rusty, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a man remembering how. "You were offered a duchy."

"I was offered a return to a life I can't go back to," Beatrix said. "I'd rather build one I can live in."

* * *

That evening, Wendell found her on the loft's narrow balcony, wrapped in the sweet-and-rotten grandmother's blanket, looking east toward mountains he knew she could not cross.

"The valley," he said, sitting beside her. It wasn't a question.

"My mother is meeting me at the border stones. We wrote ahead." A pause. "I keep imagining it. Walking up to the wardens' line and just — stopping. Like hitting glass." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Do you know what the cruelest part is? The oath didn't take my home. The valley will take it. Magic can't follow me there — but I'm made of magic now. I *am* the thing the valley was built to keep out. My mother spent her whole life choosing safety for me, and I chose the kingdom instead, and now neither of us gets what we chose."

Wendell said nothing for a while. Below them, the village lamps came alight one by one, each flame carrying its faint flavor of hearth-blessing and habit.

"When I broke my oath," he said finally, "I thought the worst part would be the shame. It wasn't. The worst part was learning that the world keeps going, and you have to keep going in it, changed, in a direction you never picked." He looked at her profile against the darkening sky. "You get to pick yours, though. That's the difference between us. Aldric offers you anything. So — what does tomorrow taste like, taster?"

Beatrix was silent so long he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she lifted her face to the wind coming down off the White Pass, and Wendell saw her do the thing she could no longer stop doing — reading the air, the road, the wide wounded world — and something in her expression shifted. Not peace. Nothing so kind as peace. But recognition, perhaps. Acceptance's rough cousin.

"Copper and old smoke," she said. "The binding's echo. But underneath it—" she paused, tasting deeper, and her eyes widened slightly, "—underneath it, there's something new. Something green. Coming from the east, from the valley's edge." She turned to look at him, and for the first time since the forge, she smiled. "Wendell. Someone is planting a garden at the border stones."

He frowned. "Who plants gardens at ward-lines?"

"Someone who knows I'm coming," Beatrix said softly, "and wanted the first thing I couldn't reach to smell like home."

She rose, folding the blanket over her arm, and looked back at him with the expression of a woman standing at the beginning of a road rather than the end of one.

"Come on, oath-keeper," she said. "We ride at dawn. My mother hates waiting — and apparently, so do gardens."
