## 21. The Border Stones

The road home took eleven days, and Beatrix counted every one of them the way she had once counted flavors — carefully, reverently, as if each might be the last of its kind.

She traveled lightly. A pack with bread and dried plums, a waterskin, the map rolled no longer in wax but in soft leather against her heart, its purpose spent. Aldric had offered her a guard of twelve knights; she had asked instead for a mule named Turnip, who was stubborn in ways she found honest. Wendell had walked her to the city gates at dawn, his oath-keeper's gray cloak finally brushed clean, and had not said goodbye because they had agreed there was no such thing between them anymore — only *until*, and *again*, and *soon*.

"Eleven days," he'd said, checking her saddle girth for the third time.

"Ten if I push."

"Don't push." He had looked up at her then, and his eyes had done the thing they did when he was trying to be practical about something that was breaking him open. "The valley will still be there. It's been there your whole life. Let it wait one more day for you."

But it was Beatrix who could not wait, and by the ninth evening she had left Turnip grazing in a farmer's field with coin enough to buy him twice over, and walked the last two days on her own feet, faster than sense allowed, because home was a taste on the tongue now and she wanted it *in* her mouth before anything else could happen.

She smelled the valley before she saw it.

That was new. That was one of the things the oath had changed. Where once her nose had been merely excellent — trained, calloused, a taster's instrument honed on twenty years of poisons — now it was something else entirely, something that caught the world's threads and would not let them go. And the Ashen Valley had a smell like nowhere else on the earth: mineral and cold, stone-dust and snowmelt, the particular absence of magic rendered almost as a flavor. Other places tasted loud to her now. Corvane had tasted like a choir singing over a wound. The Salt Road had tasted of old bargains. But the valley — the valley tasted of *quiet*. Of nothing reaching in. Of her mother's kitchen, where the bread rose because of yeast and patience and nothing else.

She crested the last ridge on the morning of the eleventh day and saw it spread below her: the long gray trough of it, terraced fields pale with ash-fed soil, the millrace threading silver through the middle, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless air. The mountains shouldered in around it like old men leaning close over a table. Somewhere down there was the house she'd been born in. The bench where her mother had taught her to distinguish nightshade from horehound by smell alone. The door her mother had locked every night of Beatrix's childhood, and the words she'd said behind that lock, so many times they wore grooves into Beatrix's memory the way water wears stone:

*Nothing follows you here. Nothing can. Stay inside the stones and you will die old and safe and unremarkable, and that is the finest life I know how to give you.*

Beatrix stood on the ridge with the wind pulling at her hair and let herself feel all of it at once — the love and the grief and the small furious tenderness of it — because she had learned, somewhere on a mountain pass with hunters' blood freezing in the snowmelt, that feelings you refuse to taste do not go away. They just ferment.

Then she went down toward the border stones.

* * *

They stood at the valley's narrowest throat, where the road passed between two granite pillars older than any kingdom, older perhaps than oaths themselves. The Ashen Valley's founders had raised them — or found them, the stories disagreed — and set them with runes worn nearly smooth, and everything inside their line was safe, and everything outside was the world.

Beatrix had crossed this line twice in her life. Once at seven, chasing a runaway goat, and her mother had pulled her back over it so fast she'd bruised, and hadn't spoken for a day afterward. Once at nineteen, standing with one foot over the line for a full hour, daring herself, before some deep animal wisdom had made her step back. She remembered how the air had felt different on either side of her ankle. She had never known why until this year.

Now she walked up to the stones with her pack on her shoulder and her heart going hard, and she stepped across.

Or she tried to.

Her foot met the line between the pillars and stopped. Not the way a foot stops against ground — there was no wall, no resistance she could press against. It was more like the moment in a dream when you try to run and your body simply declines. Her boot hovered an inch above the boundary and would go no further, and when she set it down on her own side and tried again, slower, deliberately, breathing the way Wendell had taught her to breathe before tasting something unknown — the same thing happened. The kingdom held her. Gently. The way a hand holds a bird: firm enough that flight becomes impossible, careful enough that nothing breaks.

"Oh," Beatrix said aloud, to the empty road. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

She tried three more times. She tried walking through with her eyes shut. She tried backing through, which felt undignified and worked no better. She pressed her palm flat against the air where the line ran and felt it — faintly, unmistakably — the texture of the oath in her blood answering the texture of the warding in the stones, two old magics recognizing each other like family at a funeral. *You are bound*, the stones said, without malice. *You are ours now. You carry what you carry, and what you carry cannot come in.*

The binding she had taken from Aldric's shoulders was woven through with the kingdom itself — that had been the whole point, the terrible elegant point, of the First Hearth's design. An oath-bearer belonged to the realm she carried. And the valley had never been part of the realm. The valley had made itself a hole in the world precisely so that things like this could not follow.

It had never occurred to her — not in the throne room, not in the burning dark beneath it, not once — that *she* was now one of the things that could not follow.

Beatrix sat down on the cold granite at the base of the western pillar and laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, half sob, and she let it run its course, because there was no one to see and because she had earned the right to be ridiculous for exactly one minute. Then she wiped her face with her sleeve, unpacked her bread, and began, very practically, to wait. If there was one thing twenty years of tasting had taught her, it was what to do after swallowing something irreversible.

You sat still. You paid attention. You waited to learn what you had become.

* * *

She heard the footsteps an hour later — or rather she tasted them, the oath having opinions about everything now — and knew them before she looked up. Flour and lye soap and the sharp green scent of the feverfew her mother grew along the south wall. Underneath, fainter, a thread of something Beatrix had never been able to name in her mother's scent before, and could name now: old magic, banked and buried, the ghost of an oath long since laid down.

Maren Ashgrove came up the road carrying a basket as if she were simply out gathering, as if she had not walked six miles from the village at dawn because a warden's boy had run to her door breathless with news of a woman sitting at the border stones who would not cross them.

Her mother had gone gray in the years Beatrix had been away — truly gray now, not the silver-streaked brown of her letters' descriptions, but white at the temples and thin at the crown. She had grown smaller. Or Beatrix had grown larger. Both things were probably true.

They looked at each other across ten paces of mountain road, and neither of them moved, and the silence stretched until it sang.

"You're sitting on the wrong side of the stones," her mother said at last.

"I noticed."

"The warden's boy said you wouldn't cross. He thought you were being dramatic." Maren's voice was level, dry, familiar as the creak of the kitchen stair. "I told him you were never dramatic in your life. I told him if my daughter sat down at the border stones and didn't get up again, it was because she couldn't."

Beatrix's throat closed. "You knew," she said. "When you read my letter — the one from Corvane, before the end — you knew what I'd done."

"I suspected." Her mother set the basket down with great care, as though the care of the basket were holding something larger together. "Your father's letter came first. The king's own hand, sealed in gold, telling me my daughter had taken the Oath of the Hearth and saved the world's least deserving institution." Something flickered across her face — pride and fury sharing a single expression, crowded together like strangers on a cart. "I read it eleven times. I burned it twice. I dug it out of the fire both times, because I am apparently a sentimental old woman, which will be news to everyone in the valley."

"Mama—"

"I told you never to leave." It came out fast, the way a splinter comes out. "Twenty-six years I told you. I built your whole life around it — the apprenticeship, the taster's post, all of it, everything I pushed you toward was chosen because it would keep you *inside*, do you understand? Every skill I gave you was a wall. And then a dying man put a map in your hands and every wall I ever built came down in a season, and you—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, Beatrix could see her hating it, and Beatrix loved her so much in that moment that it hurt worse than the binding did. "And you did the one thing I fled across half the world to make sure none of my children would ever have to do."

The word landed between them like a dropped plate.

*Fled.*

"I read the letter," Beatrix said quietly. "In the map's lining. I know what you were. Bearer to the last king. I know why you ran."

"Do you?" Her mother came closer, finally, and lowered herself onto the granite beside her with the small grunt of a woman whose knees had opinions. Up close, the buried magic in her scent was stronger, and underneath it, older, Beatrix caught something else — grief, aged twenty years into something like sediment. "Do you know why I ran, Beatrix? Because everyone assumes they know. That I was a coward. That I chose comfort. Even Wendell — especially Wendell — has spent two decades building a monument to my cowardice in his head, and I have let him, because the truth was mine and I owed it to no one."

"Then tell me."

Maren looked out past the stones, down the road Beatrix had come by, toward the kingdom she had once sworn to carry.

"I ran," she said, "because I was pregnant. Because the binding was already eating the last king alive and I could feel it starting to look around for the next mouth, and I knew — the way you know flavors, the way you've always known them — that whatever was in me would be born marked for it. Born *available*. So I broke my oath. I walked away from the worst thing anyone can walk away from, and I came here, to the one place in creation where it couldn't reach me or you, and I built walls out of my daughter and called them a life." She turned and looked at Beatrix then, fully, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "I chose your safety over the kingdom. Everyone since has called it desertion. You are the only person alive whose judgment of that choice means anything to me. So judge it."

Beatrix sat with it. She let it sit on her tongue the way she let everything sit on her tongue now, patiently, completely, refusing to swallow before she understood.

What she tasted was this: not betrayal. Not anymore. What she tasted was a young woman, alone and terrified, doing the arithmetic of two lives against one kingdom and choosing the lives — and then spending twenty-six years paying for it in vigilance, in locked doors, in warnings repeated until they became the walls of her daughter's prison. Her mother had not given her a cage. Her mother had given her the only inheritance she had left after breaking herself to get it: *stay where nothing can follow.* And Beatrix had honored it, right up until the day she understood that the thing following people does not stop at borders. It waits. It wears crowns. It learns patience.

"No," Beatrix said. "I don't judge it. I *am* it." She laughed, wet-eyed. "Mama. I sat down at these stones and I couldn't cross, and do you know what I felt? Not trapped. Not even sad, really. I felt—" She searched for it, and found it, and gave it. "I felt like the walls finally matched the shape of the person living inside them. You built your whole life around keeping me safe from the kingdom. Well. Now I belong to the kingdom, and it turns out the safest place for what I carry is exactly where you spent your life making sure I'd never want to go. You weren't wrong, Mama. You were just early."

Her mother made a sound that was half laugh and half something else entirely, and then Maren Ashgrove, who had not wept at her husband's funeral according to the entire valley's testimony, put her face in her hands and cried — not delicately, but hugely, the way a dam goes, decades of it at once. Beatrix put her arm around her mother's shaking shoulders and breathed in flour and feverfew and old broken oaths, and cried too, and neither of them said anything for a long time, because there was a kind of talking that only works when nobody speaks.

* * *

Later — the sun well past noon, the basket raided, the two of them sitting shoulder to shoulder against the warm granite like girls skipping lessons — her mother asked the question Beatrix had been waiting for.

"What does it taste like? The binding. Now that it's yours."

Beatrix considered. This was the hardest question anyone had asked her, harder than the Regent's offers, harder than the Hearth's demand, because it required her to describe the inside of her own blood.

"Like holding a note," she said finally. "You know how a singer holds a note, and the note isn't work exactly, but it isn't rest either — you're always *doing* it, even asleep, even dreaming. The kingdom hums in me. Roads and rivers and taxes and weddings and grudges. Every oath anyone swears to crown or country passes through what I'm carrying, like water through a filter. I can taste lies in proclamations from forty miles away." She turned a plum over in her fingers. "And the valley tastes like quiet. Like the pause between notes. I stood up there on the ridge this morning and smelled it and I nearly wept before I even saw the stones."

"And you can never come in." Not a question. Her mother already knew; she had known since the warden's boy.

"I can never come in." Beatrix said it aloud and was surprised to find it weighed less than she feared. "Aldric says the scholars are looking for a way. He says it like a promise. Wendell says it like a project — you should see him, Mama, he's got charts. But I think—" She looked at the stones, at the worn runes, at the line of air she could not pass. "I think some prices don't get refunded. And I find I don't need them to. I spent my whole life believing survival was a life's whole work. Then I met a man who failed magnificently and kept going anyway, and a king who praised soup while begging with his eyes, and a village that forgot its own children's names, and I learned that survival is just the *floor*, Mama. It's what you build on top of it that counts. I built something. It cost me the valley. I'd pay it again."

Her mother was quiet a long moment. Then she reached into the basket and brought out a small clay jar, sealed with wax, and pressed it into Beatrix's hands.

"Feverfew honey. From the south wall. For the headaches the binding gives you — don't argue, I raised a taster, I know what oath-echo does to the head." She paused, and her mouth did something complicated. "And I've decided to leave too."

Beatrix went very still. "Leave the valley?"

"There's nothing here for me anymore except a house full of your father's ghosts and a village that thinks I'm a herb-woman with odd hours. My daughter sits on the wrong side of my walls. My—" the hesitation was brief but Beatrix caught it, catalogued it, filed it away to ask about later, "—my former colleagues in the capital are apparently rebuilding an order of oath-bearers under a reformed crown, and I hear the woman training the tasters is insufferably good at it and could use someone who remembers what the old order did *wrong*." She stood, brushing off her skirts, brisk again, the tears packed away somewhere Beatrix was not permitted to see. "Besides. Someone has to teach those children that the finest life is not the one where nothing follows you. It's the one where you become worth following."

Beatrix looked up at her mother silhouetted against the valley sky — the woman who had built a fortress out of warnings, now dismantling it stone by stone to walk out into the loud-tasting world — and felt the last knot inside her come undone.

"Give me a moment," she said. "I want to say goodbye properly."

She turned to the border stones and laid her palm against the western pillar, against the worn runes, against twenty-six years of locked doors and feverfew and bread rising for no reason but yeast and patience. *Thank you*, she told it silently. *Keep them. Keep the quiet. Keep the goat I chased when I was seven and the hour I stood with one foot over the line at nineteen, daring myself. I was happy here. That's not a small thing. I'll carry it the way I carry everything now — forever, and out loud.*

Then she picked up her pack, and her mother's basket, and the two of them walked down the road together, away from the valley, side by side on the same side of the stones at last.

Behind them, far off down the road toward the lowlands, a rider was approaching — a gray-cloaked figure on a tired horse, moving fast, carrying something wrapped in oilcloth that caught the light like a blade, and beside him, riding double and grinning like the sun itself, a king in plain clothes who had insisted on coming to meet his oath-bearer halfway.

Beatrix squinted against the glare, tasted the wind, and started to laugh.
