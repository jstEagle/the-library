## 22. A New Oath

### Beatrix

The training hall smelled of copper and rosemary, and Beatrix had made certain of both.

Copper because her students needed to learn that blood was not the enemy of the tongue but its first teacher; rosemary because it masked almost nothing, and learning what almost nothing meant was the whole of the work. She walked the long table where twelve young tasters sat in rows, each with a battery of small cups before them, and she watched their faces the way her mother had once watched hers across a kitchen in the Ashen Valley — not for correctness, but for honesty.

"You flinched at cup four," she said, stopping behind a boy named Tam whose ears went red when he was caught out. "Tell me why."

"It tasted wrong."

"Wrong is a word for people who haven't learned to describe things." She lifted the cup herself, swirled, sipped, held it on her tongue the way one holds a stranger's name. "Bitter almond under the honey. That's cyanide's cousin — a weak one, grown from oleander root, probably steeped too cool. Someone tried to hide it badly. Now tell me: who would you accuse?"

Tam frowned. "Whoever sent it."

"No." She set the cup down. "You'd accuse whoever *benefited*. The poisoner is rarely the hand; the hand is rarely the mind. Taste tells you the weapon. It never tells you the war." She let that sit in the room like sediment settling. "That's the first lesson, and the last one, and every lesson between: your tongue is a door, not a verdict. What you do past the threshold of knowing — that's the oath part. That's the part no one can teach you except by failing at it in front of you."

At the far end of the hall, Wendell snorted.

He sat on a stool by the window with his bad leg stretched out before him, mending — of all things — a quill, as though the fate of the kingdom depended on the evenness of its barbs. He had taken to teaching the oath-bearers in the afternoons, the ones Aldric's new order called Keepers now, though Wendell refused the title with a consistency Beatrix found almost religious. *I'm not a Keeper,* he'd say. *I'm a man who knows exactly how heavy a kept thing gets.* His lessons were shorter than hers and crueler in the way that surgery is crueler than bandaging. He taught them how oaths broke. He taught them what broken oaths teach best: that the words are easy, that the keeping is a muscle, that a promise made in fear will fail you in fear, and so you must make your promises in the clearest hour you can find and then live inside them when the hours go dark.

"Master Wendell," said a girl in the second row — Sefa, sharp-eyed, the best palate Beatrix had trained — "is it true you failed to save a king?"

The room went very quiet. Beatrix did not intervene. They had agreed on this months ago, standing over the garden beds at the border stones: *let them ask. We spent twenty years letting silence do the crown's work.*

Wendell set down the quill. "It's true," he said. "I swore an oath to King Maren the Third, and I broke it, or it broke me — the distinction stopped mattering around year ten. He died. I lived. And I spent two decades telling myself that living was the punishment, which was a coward's arithmetic, because living is never the punishment. Living is the assignment." He looked at Sefa without flinching. "Ask me the real question."

Sefa swallowed. "Would you swear again?"

"I did," Wendell said. "Last spring. Smaller oath. To a woman with a poisoned tongue and a stubborn heart, that I'd see her work finished whether she saw it finished or not." His eyes found Beatrix's across the hall, dry and level, giving away nothing, which from Wendell was a confession entire. "It's heavier than the first one. I keep it better."

Beatrix turned back to her students before her face could say anything else. "Cups five through nine," she said. "Begin."

### Wendell

In the afternoon he took the Keepers down into the vault beneath the old throne hall, because he believed you could not teach people to guard a thing until they had stood where the thing had bled.

The scar was still there. The masons had offered to pave over it a dozen times — the shattered anchor-stone had left a wound in the floor like a starburst, black glass fused to granite — and Aldric had refused every time. *Let it stay,* the king said. *Kings should have to walk over what their crowns cost.* So the Keepers descended the narrow stair in pairs, torchlight sliding along the walls, and Wendell watched each of them stop at the scar the way he still stopped at it, some mornings, when the memory came up his throat like bile.

"The Regent wasn't a demon," he told them, his voice flat in the stone dark. "I want that understood. Demons are simple. This was older and more patient — it didn't possess Aldric, it *inhabited the office*, and the office let it, because offices are hungry that way. It wore the crown the way rot wears fruit: from the inside, slowly, until the shape looked right and everything beneath was hollow." He crouched, laid his palm near the glass — not touching; he never touched it — and felt the faint cold that still radiated there, the binding's echo, the same echo that lived now in Beatrix's blood and never, ever stopped singing. "It was unbound by a food-taster with no magic, no army, and no title. Remember that when someone tells you power has a shape."

A young Keeper — Corvin, broad-shouldered, earnest as a hound — shifted his weight. "They say she heard it coming. Through the taste of the air."

"They say right." Wendell straightened, his knee complaining. "She tastes everything now. All of it, all the time. Magic doesn't whisper to her anymore; it shouts, day and night, and she stands in that noise and does the work anyway. When you're tempted to think your burden is singular, remember there's a woman upstairs cataloguing the flavor of the kingdom's grief with every breath, and she still gets up before dawn to plant onions."

"Why onions?" asked little Maera, who was fifteen and asked questions like knives.

Wendell smiled despite himself. "Because she says a garden is just an oath you make to dirt. And dirt," he added, limping toward the stair, "keeps its promises better than kings do."

### Beatrix

On the first true morning of spring, Beatrix rode out to the border stones with a cart of seedlings and a letter she had finally decided not to send.

Her mother met her at the stones, as she always did now — walking the mile up from the valley's edge with a basket, because the wardens had long since stopped pretending the border was anything but a line two women had drawn in grief and then redrawn in love. Her mother was grayer. Her hands were still quick. She looked at the seedlings and then at her daughter and said, "You're planting here? At the edge of nowhere?"

"At the exact middle of everywhere I've been," Beatrix said, and knelt, and broke the ground.

The soil at the border stones was strange — valley-earth on one side of the line, kingdom-earth on the other, and where they met, something new, something that belonged to neither and therefore could belong to anyone. She planted in the seam. Rosemary, because her students needed to learn what almost nothing meant. Onions, because of the oath-to-dirt. And along the outermost row, where the kingdom's hunters had once come sniffing for a stolen map, she set out marigolds, bright and bitter-rooted, the flower her mother had grown outside their kitchen window in the Ashen Valley all those years of safe, small, poisoned life.

Her mother knelt beside her without being asked. For a while they worked in the rhythm they'd had since childhood — Beatrix turning earth, her mother setting roots, neither speaking, both saying plenty.

"I got your letter," her mother said at last. "The one about the school."

"And?"

"And I read it eleven times." Her mother pressed a marigold into the seam-soil and firmed the earth around it with fingers that had once fed a daughter spoonfuls of immunity, dose by dose, year by year, a slow salvation that had cost them both more than either had ever said aloud. "Your grandmother would have hated all this. Crowns and colleges and oath-keepers. She'd have said the valley was enough." A pause. The wind came off the pass, carrying — Beatrix tasted it without meaning to, the way she tasted everything now, forever — carrying pine and snowmelt and the faint mineral ghost of the First Hearth, three hundred miles east and still singing in her blood. "But your grandmother also told me once, when I was leaving, that safety is only a virtue if it's a choice. I chose it for you. You chose otherwise. Both choices were love. I've finally made my peace with the arithmetic."

Beatrix sat back on her heels. Her eyes were burning and she let them. "I couldn't cross the line, Mama. Even if I wanted to. The oath holds me to the kingdom."

"I know." Her mother reached over and tucked a loose strand of hair behind her daughter's ear, the gesture unchanged across twenty years and one apocalypse. "And I couldn't leave the valley, even if I wanted to. Isn't it funny? Two women, each bound to the side of a line, growing a garden in the middle of it." She laughed then, wet and bright, and Beatrix laughed with her, and the sound went up over the border stones where it belonged to neither country and both.

Before her mother left, walking back down toward the valley with her empty basket, she paused at the seam of soil and said, without turning around: "The girl who's coming to you next month. From Corvane. The silver-eyed one."

Beatrix went still. "What about her?"

"She wrote to me first. Did you know that? Asked about you. Asked what kind of mother raises a daughter who gives away her own immunity." Her mother glanced back, one eyebrow arching in the old familiar way. "I told her the truth. Nobody raises that. Some people just refuse, all their lives, to pass the poison on." Then she walked home, and Beatrix knelt alone among the seedlings for a long time, tasting the wind, thinking about inheritance — not what we are given, never that, but what we refuse to let die.

### Wendell

He found her at dusk at the border stones, because he always knew where to find her; it was the one talent his broken oath had left him, the finding of people.

She was asleep sitting up against the largest stone, dirt to her elbows, a trowel still in her hand, and the last light was doing something generous with her face. Wendell lowered himself carefully onto the ground beside her, bad leg out straight, and did not wake her. Above the garden the first stars were arriving, and below it the seedlings stood in their rows like a small green congregation, and somewhere in his chest the second oath — the small one, the heavy one, the kept one — turned over contentedly like an old dog in the sun.

"You're staring," she said, without opening her eyes.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring."

"A man's allowed." He pulled his cloak around her shoulders; she let him, which after everything still felt like being permitted past a gate. "Corvin told me today he wants to swear his full oath at midsummer. Maera wants to be assigned to the northern kitchens. Sefa—" he hesitated, and Beatrix opened her eyes at the hesitation, because she knew him well enough now to hear the shape of a sentence before it landed. "Sefa asked me if she could carry the map. The original. She said a school should keep its founding relic where students can see what obedience cost."

Beatrix was quiet a moment. "And what did you tell her?"

"That the map isn't the relic." Wendell looked out at the garden, at the seam-line of soil where two countries' earth grew the same flowers. "I told her the relic is whatever we refuse to pass on. But yes. Show her the map. Let her see the wax seal and the bloodstain and the lining where your mother hid her letter. Let them all see it. That's the whole curriculum, isn't it? Everything else is just cups of rosemary."

Beatrix laughed softly and leaned her head against his shoulder, and they sat like that while the dark came up out of the east, unhurried, ordinary, theirs.

Then she stiffened — a small thing, a catch of breath, but Wendell had spent a year learning her body's language the way she'd learned the language of poisons, and he caught it immediately. "What is it?"

"Taste," she murmured. Her tongue moved behind her lips, reading the air the way she read everything now, forever, the binding's echo translating the world into flavor. "On the wind. From the north." A pause. Her hand found his in the dark and gripped it — not in fear, he realized. In something closer to recognition. "Iron. Old iron, and under it something sweet. Like the Hearth, but colder." She opened her eyes, and in them the starlight reflected twice, and Wendell felt the second oath pull taut in his chest like a rope going to work.

"There's another one," Beatrix said quietly. "Another binding. Somewhere north, something wearing something it shouldn't." She rose, brushing soil from her knees, already reaching for the lantern, already the woman from the millrace with a map burning cold against her skin. "Wake the students, Wendell. Class is about to begin."
