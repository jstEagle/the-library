## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The knife knew the shape before Freya did.

She had learned to let it be that way — hands moving on their own, blade whispering through river-bone while her mind wandered somewhere safer. The bone came from the fallow deer that had died at the valley's edge last winter, cleaned and bleached and left for her by old Marta Hollis, who believed Freya made buttons and hairpins and nothing stranger than a rosary. The bone was pale as milk, dense as promise, and it wanted to become what it always became: a small door.

Freya sat in the back room of her cottage with the shutters latched against the afternoon sun, because sunlight showed every tremor in a carving hand, and hers could not afford to tremble where anyone might see. On the table before her lay the charm taking shape: a disc of bone no wider than a coin, its rim ringed with tiny notches — one for each year the child had lived — and at its center, a hole shaped like a keyhole turned sideways. A door lying flat. A door the dead could look through but never open.

Maren Cooper's daughter. Little Isbet, who had gone under the millrace in the spring flood while her mother's back was turned for the length of three heartbeats. The whole valley had dragged the river for two days before the water gave her back, pale and swollen and impossibly light, like a bird that had flown into glass.

The whispers had started that same night.

They always did. Grief opened doors in the living, and the dead leaned against them from the other side, and only Freya heard the knocking. Isbet's voice came to her now as she worked — thin, watery, confused — *it was cold, it was so cold, why is everyone crying, I'm right here.* The words arrived the way rain arrives through a roof: not all at once, but in slow seeping places she could not entirely patch.

"Quiet," Freya murmured to the empty room. "I know. I know. Hush now."

The voice did not hush. They never did, not truly. But they gentled when she carved, the way a fretful child gentles when sung to, and so she carved, and the knife moved, and outside her window the valley went about its forgetting.

That was the thing about Hollowmere — the thing that had saved her life six years ago, when she'd come over the pass with blistered feet and a name that wasn't hers and a gift she'd sworn to bury deeper than any grave. The valley forgot. Not the people — people remembered births and debts and grudges with perfect fidelity — but the land itself. Magic pooled everywhere else in the world like water finding low ground, soaking into hedgerows and hearthstones and the bloodlines of certain unlucky families. In Hollowmere, it drained away. The soil drank it and gave back nothing. Witches' charms curdled here. Prophets woke from their dreams with nothing but morning in their mouths. Even the dead grew quiet at the borders, their voices thinning to static, to almost-nothing, to the faintest seep that Freya alone, with her stubborn buried talent, could still catch if she pressed her ear to the world hard enough.

The valley was a cup with a hole in the bottom. She had crawled inside it to hide, and for six years it had held.

*It was dark under the water,* Isbet whispered. *There were fish looking at me.*

"I'm making you something," Freya said softly, to the room, to the girl, to no one. "Something so you can rest."

She said such things aloud sometimes, and hated herself for it, and did it anyway. A woman who talked to empty rooms was odd. A woman who talked to empty rooms was not a witch. In Hollowmere, odd was survivable. Witch was a pyre.

She thought of the proclamations that had begun arriving with the caravans from the east, printed on good paper with the black boar seal of King Aldric stamped in wax the color of dried blood. *An edict concerning the corruption of the realm's blood.* She had read it once, standing in the square with the other women while the caravan master read it aloud in his flat merchant's voice, and she had kept her face arranged into the mild confusion of a woman who understood none of it. *All persons practicing or harboring the forbidden arts shall be seized for examination. All who shelter them share their guilt.* Someone behind her had sniffed and said, *Good riddance,* and someone else had laughed, and Freya had studied a pigeon on the well-roof until her hands stopped wanting to shake.

Six years of careful invisibility. Six years of being the quiet carver at the end of Latch Lane, the one who made memorial beads for funerals and buttons for weddings and never joined the gossip, never won the raffle, never stood first in line or last. She had practiced the posture of unremarkable the way other women practiced dance steps. Shoulders soft. Eyes down. A smile that began and ended without involving anything behind it.

And still, when the edict was read, she had felt the valley's peace shift beneath her like a floorboard settling. Borrowed time. It had always been borrowed time; she had simply stopped counting the interest.

The knife finished the last notch. Freya held the charm up to the lamp, turning it, checking the weight of it, the balance. Bone remembered. That was the whole secret of her craft, the truth she had never spoken aloud to another living soul: bone remembered what it had been part of, and if you cut it right — if you gave the memory a shape, a door, a threshold — then the dead could pour their tangle of grief and confusion into the shape and find it *finished*. Held. A word spoken into a cupped hand instead of shouted into wind.

She had made eleven of these since coming to Hollowmere. Eleven deaths the valley had suffered, eleven small doors, each one buried beneath a headstone or sewn into a shroud or dropped into the river that had done the killing, and each time she had told herself the same lie: *this is charity, this is housekeeping, this is only tidying what the dead leave scattered.* Never mind that every charm quieted the voices for weeks afterward. Never mind that she slept better after, and worse before. Never mind that the work was the only hour of the month when she felt like herself — the self she had been before the witch-hunters burned her teacher's harbor-house in the east, before she ran, before she learned that a gift was just a word for something that gets you killed.

The loneliness of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. There was no one to tell. There was no one who would understand that the silence of Hollowmere was not peace but a kind of deafness she had chosen, that she missed the dead the way an exile misses a language, that some nights she stood at the valley border where the land began to remember again and let the far-off murmur wash over her like warm water, just for a minute, just to feel *real* — before walking back to her quiet cottage and her quiet lies and her knife and her bone.

A knock at the front door.

Freya's whole body went still, the way prey goes still. Then she set down the knife, wrapped the half-finished charm in linen, slid it beneath a loose floorboard, and arranged her face into pleasant blankness before opening the door on the late gold light and Marta Hollis's weathered face.

"There you are." Marta thrust a basket at her — bread, a twist of salt, a handful of plums going soft. "For the bead. For Isbet. Maren can't pay you, not this season, but I told her you wouldn't—"

"She doesn't owe me anything," Freya said. "It's a kindness. Tell her the bead will be ready by the burial."

"You're a good woman, Juniper—" Marta caught herself, as she always did, and laughed. "Freya. *Freya.* Six years and my tongue still wants the name you came with. Well. Whatever you were called before Hollowmere, you're ours now."

*Ours now.* Freya smiled the smile that didn't involve anything behind it, took the basket, and closed the door. She stood with her forehead against the wood for a long moment.

Ours now. As if belonging could be decided by other people. As if she hadn't spent six years being a guest in her own life, waiting for the knock that wasn't bread and plums.

She returned to the back room and unwrapped the charm. Isbet's whisper had changed — grown less frantic, more curious, the way the dead grew curious near a finished door. *What is it? What are you making?*

"A way out," Freya whispered. "Or a way in. I've never been sure which."

She reached for the knife to set the final cut — the keyhole's teeth, the last stroke that would close the door forever — and that was when she heard it. Not Isbet. Something else, from far off, from beyond the valley's southern rim where the land began to remember.

Hooves. Many hooves. And beneath them, threading through the ground like smoke under a door, a sound she hadn't heard in six years and had prayed never to hear again:

The hum of magic, waking up.

* * *

Freya stood at her window until the light failed, listening.

The hum faded after a time, or sank, or pretended to sink — she couldn't tell which frightened her more. Magic did not pass through Hollowmere. That was the whole architecture of her safety, the load-bearing wall of her exile. If the land itself had begun to leak, then either the valley was dying, or something was pressing against it from outside with enough force to wear a hole.

She ate Marta's bread without tasting it. She lit no lamp. In the dark, the whispers gathered around her like moths, and for once she did not tell them to hush.

*They're coming,* said a voice that was not Isbet's — older, a man's voice, gravelly with distance. He was one of the far dead, from beyond the border, and his words reached her frayed and broken, like a song heard through a wall. *Riders. Black and red. They stopped at the crossroads and burned a hedge-witch's cart, and they asked questions, and the ones who answered wrong are hanging from the signpost now.*

Freya's hands found the edge of the table and gripped it.

*They ask about a boy,* the dead man said. *A boy traveling alone. A boy carrying stone.*

The floorboard over the hidden charm seemed suddenly very loud in the silence. Freya looked at it, at the linen-wrapped door she had made for a drowned child, and felt the evening tilt beneath her, the way the world tilts in the moment before bad news finishes arriving.

*A boy carrying stone,* the dead repeated, and farther out, past the border, past the hills, something answered — a long, low note like a struck bell, like a shard of grey rock humming in a starving child's fist, though Freya could not have said how she knew that, only that she suddenly, terribly, did.

Outside, down the lane, a dog began to bark at the darkness where the road came down from the pass.

Someone was walking toward Hollowmere. Someone small, and alone, and letting himself be seen, which no one running from Aldric's riders should ever do.

Freya blew out the candle she had never lit, and waited for the knock.
