## 2. Wendell at the Gate

The drowned child's name was Lissbet, and Freya carved it into a sliver of river-bone with the patience of someone who had done this too many times.

She worked at her kitchen table by lamplight, the little house around her smelling of beeswax and juniper smoke. Outside, autumn was closing its fist on the valley — frost silvering the fences, woodsmoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless evening air. Inside, everything was quiet.

That was the point of the valley. That was the whole point.

Freya held the bone charm to the light and turned it slowly. She had scored the name in careful letters, ringed it with the knotwork that meant *rest*, and drilled the hole for the cord with a needle heated in the flame. It was good work. It was always good work. Her hands had learned steadiness before they'd learned anything else, because unsteady hands made charms that leaked, and leaking charms meant voices, and voices meant questions she could not afford to answer.

Lissbet Halloway had gone under the millrace in March, when the thaw came down off the mountains too fast. The village had dragged the pools for two days. They'd found her on the third, tangled in the willow roots downstream, and her mother had not stopped weeping since — which was natural, and right, and would have been so much worse if she knew what Freya knew.

The dead did not always leave cleanly. Especially children. Especially the drowned, who died with water still in their lungs and their last breath caught somewhere between one world and the next. Lissbet had been whispering at the edge of Freya's hearing for eight months now. Not words, exactly. More like a song hummed through a wall. *Mama. Mama. It's cold where I am.*

So Freya carved. A memorial charm, hung over a grave or a hearth, gave the lingering dead a door that was only theirs, a small warm room to settle into instead of drifting. It was the gentlest use of her gift, and the most dangerous, because every charm was also a confession. Bone-carving was a gifted craft. The valley had no gifted. The valley had been scoured of them two generations ago, or so everyone believed, and the peace of Hollowmere depended on that belief holding forever.

She finished the cord, coiled the charm into a scrap of linen, and set it by the door for morning delivery. Then she sat back and let herself feel, for exactly as long as she allowed herself these things — ten breaths, no more — the old familiar grief of it.

Somewhere beyond the mountains there were other carvers, other listeners, people who could say out loud what they were. Here she was Juniper Quintana's quiet daughter, the one who kept to herself, who sold candles and tallow soap and occasionally, if you asked nicely and never wondered why, a little carved trinket for your loved one's grave. *For remembrance*, she called them. The village found it sweet. Sweetness was camouflage. Loneliness was the price of the dye, and she paid it daily, and she had gotten so used to paying that she sometimes forgot she was paying at all.

Until nights like this one, when the silence pressed close enough to have a texture, and she thought: *I could go my whole life this way. Buried alive in all this quiet.*

A knock at the door nearly took a year off her life.

No one knocked at Freya's door after dark. No one knocked at Freya's door much at all. She stood, took the poker from beside the hearth — an old habit, though iron did nothing against men, and less against what else might come knocking — and opened the door on a chain.

A boy stood on her step.

He was perhaps nine, maybe ten, small for either, with the kind of thinness that comes not from a hard week but from a hard season. His hair was matted dark, his coat was a man's coat cut down badly at the wrists, and his shoes had been repaired with twine so many times they were more twine than shoe. He swayed slightly as he stood, the way the starving sway, as if the ground moved under them and they'd simply stopped arguing with it.

"Please," he said. His voice was cracked, rusted. "They said — the woman at the well said you do remembrance things."

"The well woman talks too much," Freya said. "Where are your parents?"

He didn't answer that. His eyes went past her shoulder, into the warm room, and something in his face made her chest hurt in a way she resented immediately.

"I walked a long way," he said. "I'm sorry. I know it's late. I didn't know where else—" He stopped, swallowed. Started again, smaller. "The edge let me through."

Freya's hand tightened on the door.

"The edge," she repeated.

"The valley edge." He said it the way travelers said it, the way everyone outside the mountains said it, with a little hush of superstition. "Everyone knows you can't get into Hollowmere if you're marked. My gran told me. The valley forgets you, she said, it just — turns you around, and you walk out the way you came without knowing it." His chin trembled. "It didn't turn me around. I've been walking toward it for four days and it just... let me in. Like a door opening." He looked up at her, and his eyes were too old for his face, the eyes of someone who had learned what doors opening could mean. "Why did it let me in?"

*Because you're carrying something,* said a voice at the back of Freya's mind, cold and certain, and she hated that voice, because it was usually right.

"You're half-frozen," she said, which was true; he was shivering so hard his teeth clicked. "And you're about to fall down. Come inside before you do it on my step and I have to explain a body."

He came inside the way a stray animal comes inside — low, quick, ready to bolt, astonished by warmth. He stopped three feet past the threshold and stared at the fire as though he'd forgotten fire existed. Freya closed the door, shot the bolt, and stood a moment with her back against it, telling herself she was being a fool, that charity was fine but curiosity was dangerous, that she should feed him, point him at the inn, and wash her hands of whatever had followed him through the valley's ancient wards.

Then she saw what he clutched in his fist, and every sensible thought she owned went out of her head at once.

It was a shard of stone, grey as storm-water, no bigger than her thumb. But it wasn't behaving like stone. Stone didn't hold heat the way a coal holds heat. Stone didn't pulse — faintly, slowly, unmistakably — like something breathing. And stone certainly did not make the dead sit up and pay attention, which is what Freya felt happening in the marrow of her own bones, a sensation like a hundred distant heads turning toward her at once.

"What is that," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

The boy flinched and pulled the shard against his chest. "It's mine."

"I'm not going to take it." She crouched down to his level, slow, hands visible, the way you'd approach a frightened dog. "What's your name?"

"Wendell."

"Wendell. I'm Freya. Now — your gran gave you that? Before you left?"

His jaw set. For a moment he looked ready to lie, and then exhaustion broke him open instead. "She gave it to me the night the riders came. She pushed me out the back and put it in my hand and said *run east, run to the forgetting valley, don't stop, don't look back, and don't let anyone touch this, especially not anyone wearing white.*" His voice wobbled on the last part. "Then the barn burned. With her in it. I heard it." He swallowed. "I've been hearing it ever since. In my head. Is that — am I—"

"Yes," Freya said quietly. "You probably are."

He looked at her with those too-old eyes, waiting for the blow, the recoil, the door closing. Freya had seen that look before, in mirrors, years ago, in the first weeks after her own gift woke and her mother sat her down and explained, gently, what their family was and what it would cost them to stay in Hollowmere. She had sworn she'd never be the one delivering that speech. And yet here she was, kneeling on her own floor, about to tell a starving orphan the truth that would follow him for the rest of his life.

"Give me your hand," she said instead. "Both of us. Let's see what your gran left you."

He hesitated — then, with the terrible trust of the truly desperate, opened his fingers and held the shard out.

Freya touched it.

The hum went through her like a struck bell. Not sound — deeper than sound, a vibration in the blood, in the teeth, in the place behind her sternum where her gift lived. The dead of the valley, the patient murmuring presence she spent her whole life holding at arm's length, went suddenly, utterly silent — hushed, the way a congregation hushes. Every voice she had ever quieted, every whisper she had ever smothered behind her carefully carved walls, seemed to lean toward the little grey stone in the boy's palm.

And beneath the hush, so faint she might have imagined it, something vast and old and broken sang one long, mournful note. A note of severance. Of something whole, once, that was whole no longer.

Freya snatched her hand back. The shard pulsed once, twice, and settled.

Wendell was watching her face. "You felt it," he whispered. "You felt it too."

"I felt it." Her mouth was dry. She got up, mostly because staying crouched near the thing felt suddenly like kneeling at the edge of a well, and busied herself ladling stew into a bowl, because hands needed doing something. "Eat. All of it. Then you'll sleep, and tomorrow we'll talk about what your grandmother was, and how she came to have a piece of stone that makes my skin crawl from here to the churchyard."

"It's warm," Wendell said, almost apologetic, cradling the bowl she gave him. "It's always warm. Gran said it was warm for her too. She said it had been warm since before Mama was born." He ate like a small wolf, guarding the bowl with his elbow, and between bites he watched Freya with an attention that made her uneasy. "You're like me," he said. It wasn't a question either. "That's why the valley let me in. It doesn't forget people like us. It was hiding us. Wasn't it? It's been hiding *you*."

Freya looked at him across the lamplit table — this scrap of a boy with ash surely still under his fingernails and a king's hunters somewhere behind him and a humming fragment of something impossible in his pocket — and felt the first crack run through the careful, load-bearing wall of her solitude.

"Eat your stew, Wendell," she said.

But she did not sleep that night. She sat by the banked fire with her mother's oldest carving knife across her knees, listening to the boy breathe in the loft above, and to the shard's faint pulse coming through the floorboards like a second heartbeat. And sometime past midnight, in the deep hush where the dead ordinarily murmured, she heard — clearly, deliberately, from many throats at once — a single word drift up out of the silence:

*Covenant.*

In the morning, before the frost had melted off the fences, the sound of hoofbeats came down the valley road, and a rider in the white-and-ash livery of King Aldric stopped at the well, unrolling a proclamation sealed in black wax.
