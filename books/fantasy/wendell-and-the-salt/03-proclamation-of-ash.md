## 3. Proclamation of Ash

The morning the riders came, the valley smelled wrong.

Wendell noticed it before he saw anything — a thinness in the air, like the moment before a storm breaks over dry fields. For six days he had lived at the edge of Hollowmere, sleeping in the miller's hayloft, working for bread and a corner by the hearth, and every one of those days the valley had felt the same: soft, drowsy, wrapped in wool. A place where nothing pressed against you. He hadn't realized how much had been pressing until it stopped, and then started again, differently, the way a fever starts after a false recovery.

He was carrying water up from the stream when the first horse crested the southern ridge.

There were five of them, riding in a line that was too neat to be travelers and too slow to be soldiers in any hurry. Their cloaks were grey with road dust, but beneath the dust Wendell could see the color — a deep, official crimson, the red of wax seals and gallows rope. At the lead rider's saddle horn hung a rolled banner, furled tight as a closed fist.

Behind them came a sixth figure on a black mare, and this one made Wendell's skin crawl before he understood why. The rider sat oddly, hunched forward, nose lifted to the wind like a man tasting soup. As the horses picked their way down toward the village green, the sixth rider turned their head — slowly, deliberately — and breathed in.

Wendell set down his buckets very carefully, so they wouldn't clank.

*They can't smell me,* he told himself. *I'm not gifted. I'm just a boy with a rock.* But the shard under his shirt had gone warm against his chest, warm the way it always did when something was near that wanted it, and warmth could be seen. Warmth rose off a body like steam off a kettle. He pulled his collar closed and thought, absurdly, of Freya Quintana's hands, how they'd hovered over the stone without quite touching it, and how her face had gone pale and then very still, the way a pond goes still when something moves beneath the ice.

*You should leave,* she'd said. And then, because she was who she was: *But not tonight. Not hungry.*

* * *

By the time Wendell reached the green, half the village was already gathered there, drawn the way villages are always drawn to trouble — slowly, in twos and threes, pretending they'd been headed somewhere else anyway. The riders had dismounted in front of the meetinghouse. The lead officer, a broad man with a voice built for parade grounds, unrolled the banner across the meetinghouse door and drove iron pins through its corners.

It showed a crown above a broken circle.

Wendell knew that symbol. Every child in the borderlands knew it, though none of them could have said where from — it lived in the way grandmothers went quiet at certain words, in the songs that stopped halfway through. The broken circle. The old covenant, the thing between the living and the dead that everyone agreed existed and no one talked about, the way you don't talk about the foundation your house stands on.

The officer nailed a paper beside the banner and read aloud in a flat, bored voice, the voice of a man reading for the hundredth time:

"By decree of Aldric, rightful King of Veyland and all its territories: Let it be known that the practice of unauthorized communion with the departed is hereby declared treason against the crown. All persons found to harbor such gifts shall be seized for examination. All persons found to shelter, conceal, or aid the gifted shall be judged accomplices. Harbors of the dead — charms, vessels, tokens, bones worked by gifted hands — are to be surrendered or destroyed. Those who hang such harbors about their necks will hang beside them."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat.

Wendell looked around at the faces. Old Marta the weaver, whose hands never stopped moving even now, worrying a thread. The miller, who had let Wendell sleep in his loft, standing with his jaw set and his eyes fixed on the middle distance. Children peering between their mothers' skirts. Nobody spoke. That was the terrible part, Wendell thought. Not fear exactly — fear would have been louder. It was the silence of people doing arithmetic. Counting neighbors. Counting years. Counting what they might have to pretend not to know.

And underneath the arithmetic, Wendell heard something else. Something only he could hear, because of the shard, or maybe because of whatever he was that the shard had chosen. The dead of Hollowmere were gathering. He couldn't see them, but he could feel them the way you feel a change in pressure before rain — a crowding at the edges of things, a leaning-in. They had come to read the proclamation too.

One voice, older than the others, said clearly enough that Wendell almost turned his head: *Count the horses, boy. Six is too many for a paper-reading. Six is a hunting number.*

* * *

The officer finished reading and asked, in that same bored tone, whether anyone wished to surrender harbors now, "for leniency follows honesty." No one moved. The officer shrugged as if he'd expected nothing else, and here Wendell learned something about how cruelty works when it's patient: the man didn't rage, didn't threaten. He simply nodded to the sixth rider.

The bloodhound-mage swung down from the black mare.

Up close, Wendell could see it wasn't a man at all, or not anymore. It wore a man's shape the way a glove wears a hand. Its skin was grey and slightly translucent, like wax held too near a flame, and where its nose should have been there was only smoothness — and yet Wendell could see it breathing, could see the whole face ripple with each inhalation, drinking the air. Chains of small copper bells hung from its belt, and the bells made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than if they'd rung.

It walked the length of the green without hurrying. It paused at the well. It paused at the shrine niche where generations of Hollowmere's little bone charms hung on leather cords — memorial pieces, quiet pieces, the kind every village kept and no one questioned. The mage leaned close to them. Its nostrilless face rippled. Then it moved on, and Wendell exhaled, because the charms were old, so old their whispering had worn down to almost nothing, worn down the way the valley itself wore everything down.

That was the whole trick of Hollowmere, Wendell understood suddenly, watching the creature work. The valley forgot magic. Magic came in and got sleepy and dimmed and eventually dissolved, like sugar in warm water. Whatever gifts people carried here faded to embers, to ash, to nothing. That was why Freya hid here. That was why the dead here were only murmurs instead of voices.

But the mage didn't know the trick. And the mage was walking toward the northern edge of the valley, toward the place where the forgetting was thinnest — toward the gate.

Toward the gap a starving boy had slipped through three weeks ago, when the valley should have turned him away.

* * *

Wendell followed at a distance, along with half the village's children and a few adults who pretended they weren't following. The mage walked the boundary like a surveyor, trailing its fingers through the air, and where it walked the air seemed to curdle faintly, the way milk does when it's turning. At the northern gate — the moss-covered standing stones where the road left the valley — it stopped.

It stood there a long time. The officer waited with the patience of a man being paid by the day.

Then the mage raised one grey hand and pressed it flat against the air between the stones, and the air *dented*. Wendell saw it plainly: a shallow depression, like a thumbprint in dough, ringed by a shimmer that spread outward and frayed at the edges. Frayed — that was the word his mind supplied and refused to give back. The valley's protection, the invisible wool that had wrapped him safe for three weeks, looked from the outside like an old blanket with the hem coming undone.

The mage turned its head. It had no eyes worth the name, but Wendell felt its attention find him anyway, out of the whole crowd, the way a hook finds a fish.

"Here," it said. Its voice was dry leaves and bell-metal. "Something passed. Recently. Weeks." The face rippled, drinking deeper. "Warm. Carried. Carried *stone*."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Wendell became aware of his own heartbeat, of the shard against his chest — warm, warm, betraying him like a coal sewn into his shirt — of the miller, somewhere behind him, shifting his weight. Of the dead pressing closer, dozens of them now, murmuring in voices like wind through reeds: *Run, boy. Run now. While it's only smelling.*

The officer strolled over. He looked at the dent in the air, then at the villagers, and smiled the smile of a man who has all the time in the world and knows exactly how to spend it.

"Fraying," he said pleasantly. "Your valley's protection is fraying. Which means either it was always weaker than the stories say—" he let the pause stretch— "or someone recently brought something through it that magic wants very badly to follow." His eyes moved across the crowd, unhurried, cataloguing. "The crown will return within the month. I suggest that anything carried, hidden, or smuggled be... resolved by then. Leniency follows honesty. So do I."

He remounted. The mage stood a moment longer at the gate, its face rippling, memorizing the scent the way other men memorize faces. Then it mounted the black mare, and the six riders rode south out of the valley, and the banner with the broken circle snapped once in the wind and hung still.

The villagers dispersed in clusters, talking low and fast, already building the story they'd tell each other: *old protections, nothing to do with us, nothing carried, nothing hidden.* Wendell stood alone by the well until they'd gone.

*You have to go tonight,* said the old dead voice, gentle as snowfall. *Not because you're guilty, boy. Because they'll come back with more than six, and next time they'll bring something that doesn't need to smell.*

Wendell looked north, toward the standing stones, toward the road beyond them he'd come down half-dead three weeks ago. Then he looked east, toward the crooked little house at the valley's edge where a woman carved bones by lamplight and pretended she was only making trinkets.

She'd told him to leave. She'd also fed him, and bandaged his feet, and taught him the names of carving tools, and last night she'd laughed — actually laughed — at his terrible attempt at a memorial bead, and something in Wendell's chest had gone warm that had nothing to do with the shard.

The dead murmured around him, urgent now, a rising tide of them, and beneath their urgency he caught one thread repeated like a refrain, passed voice to voice to voice, growing clearer as it came to him:

*Tell the carver. Tell the carver the hunters crossed the border today. Tell the carver they're looking for the stone. Tell her the valley's hem is undone, and the weather is coming in.*

Wendell picked up his water buckets, which he had completely forgotten, and started toward Freya Quintana's house at a pace just short of running.
