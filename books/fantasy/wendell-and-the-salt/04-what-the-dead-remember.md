## 4. What the Dead Remember

The dead came to Freya at dawn, as they always did, sliding through the cracks in her silence like water through a failing dam.

She was in the workshop behind her cottage, hands buried in the ribs of a ewe that had died of age, not grief, when the whispers changed. Most mornings the voices were a murmur, a tide against the seawall of her bone charms — the drowned miller complaining of cold, the old midwife humming a lullaby to no one. But this morning the tide had turned. This morning they were *urgent*.

*The boy. The stone. The boy with the stone.*

Freya set down her knife and wiped her hands on her apron, though there was nothing on them. Through the window she could see Wendell asleep in the chair by her hearth, where he'd been for three days now, eating slowly back to life, his fists still curling around that grey shard even in sleep. Even unconscious, he held it like something might come to take it.

Something was coming to take it.

"Tell me," she said quietly, to the room, to the dead beneath the valley soil and beyond it. "Tell me what I'm not supposed to know."

The valley's magic-dead peace muffled them, made their voices thin and distant, but they pressed closer than they ever had. And one voice rose above the others — an old woman's voice, cracked and warm and stubborn, a voice that did not belong to anyone buried in Quintana Hollow.

*I carried it forty years,* the voice said. *Forty years under my floorboards, and my daughter's floorboards after me, and then the girl ran and the riders came and I—*

The voice broke off. Not faded. *Stopped*, the way a candle stops, all at once.

Freya stood very still. "Whose voice is that?"

*His grandmother,* said the drowned miller, who had been dead sixty years and had lost all patience for gentleness. *The boy's grandmother. She died at the border stone three nights past. She is asking for you.*

---

Wendell woke to find Freya kneeling in front of his chair, and he flinched back so hard the shard nearly fell from his hand. He caught it, cradled it, glared at her with the ferocity of a cat backed into a corner.

"You talk to yourself," he said. "I heard you. In the night."

"I do," Freya said. "And last night, someone talked back."

He went white. She watched him decide whether to run, watched him weigh the door, the window, the winter fields beyond — and watched him stay, because his legs were still too thin for running and because some part of him, the part that had walked into a valley that should have refused him, already knew.

"She followed me," he whispered. "Gran. To the edge. The men on horses came and she told me to run and I ran and—" His jaw worked. "Is she—"

Freya took his hand. It was the first time she'd touched him since that first night, when the shard had hummed against her palm like a trapped bee. She turned his fingers open. The stone lay there, grey and unremarkable, no bigger than a knucklebone.

But the dead were shouting now, all of them, and through Wendell's skin the stone answered them, and Freya heard what she had spent eleven years making sure she would never hear clearly again.

She saw a hall of white pillars. A circle of robed figures, living and dead together, standing around a stone the height of a man — grey, veined with light, singing in a voice that was not sound. She saw the covenant spoken: *the living shall carry the dead, and the dead shall guard the living, and neither realm shall rule alone.* She saw a young man with hungry eyes standing at the circle's edge, a prince passed over, passed aside, passed *through*, and she saw him return at midnight with soldiers and a hammer forged from star-iron and hatred, and she saw the stone—

She saw the stone break.

The scream of it went through her like a blade. Every dead soul in the valley flinched; she felt them flinch, felt the whole unseen country of the dead recoil from that memory as from fire. The stone shattered into shards, and the shards scattered, and the song stopped, and the dead who had guarded the living for a thousand years were suddenly locked outside their own homes, knocking on doors that could no longer hear.

*Aldric,* the old woman's voice said, and now it was close, so close Freya could have sworn she felt breath on her ear. *Aldric broke it. Aldric stole the crown with a broken covenant, and the crown cannot hold unless the last shard is ground to dust beneath the coronation moon. My son's daughter ran with it. My grandson carries it. And I am dead, and I am sorry, boy, I am so sorry—*

"Wendell," Freya said. Her own voice sounded far away. "Your grandmother wants you to know she doesn't blame you."

The boy's face crumpled. He didn't cry — he was past crying, or saving it — but he folded forward until his forehead nearly touched the shard, and the stone glowed faintly warm, and Freya understood that somewhere just past the veil, an old woman was holding her grandson's hands the only way left to her.

Then the warmth in the stone guttered, and the presence withdrew, and Wendell looked up with wet eyes and said, "What was her name?"

"Maren," said the dead. "Maren Ostwick."

"Maren," Wendell repeated, tasting it. Then: "The men on horses. Will they come here?"

Freya looked out the window. The morning was bright and ordinary. Smoke rose from her neighbors' chimneys. Down the lane, Tam Osgood's dog barked at crows.

"The valley keeps magic out," she said. "It has kept it out for four hundred years. That's why people like me come here. Why I came here."

"But?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. Because even as she stood there, she felt it — a wrongness at the edge of her senses, like a tooth with a hairline crack. For eleven years the valley's silence had been total, a wall between her and the roaring country of the dead. Now, for the first time, she could feel the wall *flexing*. Somewhere beyond the hills, something was pressing against it. Something patient. Something with hounds.

*It is fraying,* the midwife whispered. *We can feel the outside leaking in. Little witch-girl, we can feel the outside coming in.*

* * *

By noon, the whole hollow knew something was wrong, though none of them knew what. The sheep wouldn't graze near the western ridge. The well water tasted of iron. Old Tam Osgood, who was not gifted but was old enough to be mistaken for wise, stood in the lane squinting west and said, "Weather's coming. Wrong kind."

Freya packed anyway.

She worked fast and without ceremony, the way she'd always promised herself she would if this day ever came, and hated how rehearsed it felt, how many nights she must have lain awake planning an escape she'd sworn was paranoia. Charms into a leather roll — quieting charms, mostly, her finest work, each one carved from bone that had agreed to be used. Dried food. Her mother's knife. The small tin of coins she'd buried under the hearthstone eleven years ago and never once touched.

Wendell watched from the doorway, the shard on a cord around his neck now. "You knew this would happen," he said. "Before I ever came."

"I hoped it wouldn't."

"That's not what I asked."

Freya tied off the roll. "No," she said. "I didn't know. But I built my whole life like a woman waiting for a flood. That's not knowing. That's just... refusing to be surprised." She looked at him — this scrap of a boy, seven years old maybe, carrying a king's ransom and a dead woman's hope in a pendant. "Eleven years ago I came to this valley because it couldn't hear me. Do you understand? There are people whose gifts are loud, who throw fire, who see tomorrow. My gift is that the dead speak *through* me, and they never stop, and before I learned to carve charms I heard every murdered soul in my city screaming its story into my skull until I wanted to claw my own ears off. The valley gave me silence. I traded my whole life for silence."

"And now you're leaving it."

"Now I'm leaving it." She shouldered her pack. "Because your grandmother died so you could carry that stone to somewhere safe, and because Aldric's hunters don't stop at borders, and because—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Because I've spent eleven years listening to the dead ask for help and pretending I couldn't hear them. I'm done pretending."

Outside, the light had gone strange — yellowed, bruised at the edges, the way it does before hail. Tam Osgood was still in the lane, still squinting west, and now other neighbors had come out of their houses too, drawn by the same wrongness they couldn't name. A child pointed at the ridge. The dog had stopped barking.

That was the worst part, Freya thought later. The silence of the dog.

They were halfway to the eastern path — the long way out, the shepherd's track, the route no rider would think to watch — when the first horn sounded from the western ridge. One note, then another, then a third, and the sound rolled down into the hollow like a stone into a well, and every neighbor in the lane turned toward it at once, and Freya saw old Tam's face go grey as ash.

Through the thinning veil at the valley's edge, her gift reached out despite itself, past the fraying wall, and touched the minds of the riders gathering there. Six of them. Seven. A dozen, with more behind. And among them, leashed on chains of cold iron, three shapes low to the ground, noses to the earth, that were not dogs at all but something older and hungrier — hounds bred from shadow and spell, that hunted not scent but *gift*, that could taste a whisper of magic across ten miles of open country.

And leading them, a woman in grey armor with a lantern that burned black, who lifted her head as Freya's attention brushed hers — and smiled, slow and certain, as if she could feel the touch on her skin.

"There you are," the huntress murmured, across four miles and a dying valley. "There you are, little carver."

Freya grabbed Wendell's hand and ran.
