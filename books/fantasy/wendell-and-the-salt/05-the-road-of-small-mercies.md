## 5. The Road of Small Mercies

*Freya*

They had been walking for four days when the river started talking to Wendell.

Freya noticed it first as a stillness in the boy — a wrongness, because Wendell was never still. He crouched at the bank of the Merle with his head cocked like a heron's, the grey shard hanging against his chest on its leather cord, and his lips moved without sound.

"Wendell." She kept her voice low. On the road, names were currency you didn't spend in the open.

"He says the water's cold this year," Wendell said, not turning around. "He says it comes down from the high snows angry."

Freya's stomach dropped through the soles of her boots. She knew that cadence. She had worn that cadence herself, once, before she'd learned to carve it shut behind bone and silence. *The boy carries a shard of the covenant stone,* the dead had told her, *and the stone does not hide what it touches.*

"Who says?" she asked carefully.

"The river." He finally looked up at her, and there was no fear in his face, only a kind of puzzled wonder. "He's old. Older than the bridge downstream, he says. He wants to know if we're going to cross or go around."

Around them the valley-less country stretched brown and ordinary — hedgerows, fallow fields, a crow arguing with the wind. No magic here. No whispers. Just a boy and a river and Freya's heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out of a locked room.

"We're going around," she said. "Come away from the water."

But they didn't get to go around, because that was when the soldier stepped out from under the alders with his hands raised and his sword belt unbuckled and hanging from his fist like a dead snake.

"I heard him too," the man said. "The river. Thought I was going mad. Been going mad for three days, actually, so it was a relief to be told I wasn't."

He was big — big the way oaks are big, weathered and split in places — with a deserter's beard and a deserter's eyes, red-rimmed and watchful. His uniform had been dyed black to hide the crown blue, but the dye had faded unevenly at the collar, and a ghost of royal azure showed there like a bruise.

Freya put herself between him and Wendell. "Walk past us," she said. "We're nobody."

"Nobody doesn't talk to rivers." He didn't move closer. That was the first thing she liked about Brannoc Hale, though she wouldn't learn his name until later: he kept his distance the way a man keeps distance from a fire he badly needs. "And nobody doesn't look at me like I'm the thing under the bed. You looked. So you know what's on the road behind us, same as I do."

She did know. They had seen the lanterns two nights back, strung along a ridge like a constellation that had fallen and gone wrong.

"There were five of us," the soldier said. "Deserted together out of the garrison at Corren's Ford. The hunters caught the others. I ran." He said it flatly, without shame, which took more courage than any lie would have. "I don't want anything from you. I just — if your boy hears rivers, and you're walking the same road I am, then we're all already in the same grave. It seems stupid to dig it separately."

Wendell tugged Freya's sleeve. "Freya. He's hungry. I can hear his stomach from here."

The soldier's ears went red. "The boy's right," he admitted.

Freya looked at the man's empty hands, at the road behind them, at the shard humming faintly against Wendell's chest where no one else could hear it. Every rule she had built her life on said: *keep walking, keep small, keep alone.* Every rule she had built her life on had failed to save the valley.

"There's bread," she said at last. "Half of it's gone hard. Don't talk with your mouth full."

* * *

*Brannoc*

The hedge-witch found them that evening, or perhaps they found her; afterward none of them could agree on which, and Sefa maintained to the end of her days that the road had simply decided they belonged together, the way roads decide things about people sometimes.

Brannoc saw her first — a small figure standing in the middle of the track ahead, arms crossed, gray braid whipping in the wind, with a mule beside her loaded down with enough dried herbs to stock an apothecary and enough attitude to stock a garrison.

"You're the deserter," she called, before they were even in shouting range. "Corren's Ford colors under that botched dye job. And you—" she jabbed a finger at Freya, "—you've got death all over you. Not sick-death. *Craft*-death. Bone-work, if my nose is any good, and my nose is very good."

Freya went white. Brannoc's hand drifted toward his sword out of pure habit, though the woman was sixty if she was a day and armed with nothing worse than a walking stick.

"And you," Sefa continued, wheeling on Wendell, who had hidden half behind Freya's leg, "are carrying a piece of rock that's been screaming since noon. Honestly, child, how do you sleep?"

"It doesn't scream," Wendell said. "It hums."

"It screams *quietly*. There's a difference, and it matters." She looked the three of them over like a cook assessing scraps for a stew. "There's a waystation half a mile on. Old kings' road shelter, roof mostly whole, hearth that draws if you know its trick. Hunters passed it yesterday heading east, which means they'll circle back west within the week, because hunters are predictable men pretending otherwise. We can risk one night. One."

"Why would you share it?" Freya asked. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone counting exits.

Sefa's face softened by a single degree, which Brannoc would learn was the equivalent of most people weeping openly. "Because forty years ago I healed a girl in a village that burned for it, and I swore never again to be the only one left standing at the end of a story. Come on. The mule bites, so don't pet the mule."

* * *

*Freya*

The waystation had been a shrine once, before the kings' road fell out of use. You could see it in the bones of the place: the hearth laid in the shape of an open hand, the niches in the wall where travelers had set offerings, the lintel carved with words worn smooth to nonsense. Now it was just a stone shell full of pigeon droppings and old rain, and it was the safest place Freya had stood in a month.

Sefa coaxed the hearth alight with a pinch of something green and a word she didn't translate. Tam — for that was the river-speaker's name, old Tam, arrived an hour after dark smelling of mud and singing to himself — fed the fire properly, wood arranged with a craftsman's care.

"Rivers told me you'd come," he announced, settling onto a bench with the groan of a much-abused man. "Not you four specifically. Just *company*. Rivers are vague. Wonderful listeners, terrible correspondents."

"You speak to rivers," Freya said. It wasn't a question. She was cataloguing them now, the way she'd once catalogued the dead: their gifts, their dangers, the shape of what each one carried.

"Rivers, streams, that marshy bit near Hallowford that thinks it's a lake." Tam shrugged. "Never could talk to people proper. Too many words coming at you all at once, faces doing things. Water only ever means what it means." He glanced at Wendell with frank, unembarrassed curiosity. "You hear it too, lad. Raw, though. Untrained. Like a door off its hinges."

"His gift isn't water," Freya said quickly. Too quickly. Three heads turned toward her.

"No," Sefa agreed mildly, stirring the pot she'd insisted on making from everyone's pooled scraps — hard bread, a strip of salt pork from Brannoc's pack, wild onions Tam had pulled from the bank, a twist of dried mushrooms from her own. "It isn't. But it's loud, whatever it is, and it's getting louder, and I'd wager my best mortar that our bone-worker here knows why and isn't saying."

The fire popped. Wendell leaned against Freya's side, heavy with exhaustion, the shard rising and falling with his breath. She should deny it. She should build the wall back up, brick by brick, the way she had for thirty years. *Keep small. Keep alone. Gifts buried alive stay buried.*

Instead she heard herself say: "It's a piece of the covenant stone. The one King Aldric broke."

Silence, except for the fire and the wind worrying the roof.

Tam stopped chewing. Brannoc's hand had gone still on his knee. Sefa set down her spoon with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might explode.

"Well," said Sefa. "That explains the screaming."

"They killed his grandmother for it," Freya went on, and found, to her surprise, that the words kept coming, that thirty years of silence had been a dam and this ragged room was the first crack in it. "The hunters crossed into our valley four days ago. The valley's protection failed. Because of him. Because of this." She touched the shard, gently, above the sleeping boy's heart. "Aldric wants it. He needs it, to finish whatever he began when he shattered the stone. And every mile we walk toward the free cities, we're walking it closer to him."

Brannoc cleared his throat. "Then we walk faster."

"It's not a cart, soldier. You can't whip it onward."

"No. But it's got legs." He nodded at Wendell. "And it's got you, and the witch, and the river-man, and me. Five pairs of legs and one piece of a god-stone. I spent eight years holding a shield line for a king I stopped believing in somewhere around year three. Holding a line for *this*" — he gestured at the boy, the fire, the improbable company of them — "I could do that. If anyone asked me to."

Nobody asked him to. That was the thing Freya remembered afterward, the thing that made her chest ache even years later when everything else had burned away: nobody asked. He just offered, awkwardly, staring into the fire, and Sefa pretended to be very busy with her spoon, and Tam patted the bench beside him as if inviting a man to sit were the same as inviting him to belong.

* * *

*Wendell*

Wendell woke once in the night, the way he always woke, reaching for a grandmother who wasn't there. But the waystation was warm, and the sounds were new and good: Sefa snoring like a small determined saw, Tam murmuring to the rain outside as if taking dictation, Brannoc asleep sitting up with his sword across his knees because some habits don't die, only doze. Freya sat by the embers, carving. Even in the dark he could tell it was bone, and even in the dark he could feel that this charm was different from the ones she wore — open, somehow. Listening.

"You couldn't sleep either?" he whispered.

"I'm watching the road," she said. Then, softer: "Go back to sleep, Wendell. Nothing gets past me."

He believed her. He rolled back into the warmth of the shared blankets and was nearly under when he felt the shard wake against his chest — not screaming, not humming, but *pulling*, gentle and insistent, the way a hand tugs a sleeve.

East, it said, in the wordless way stones say things. East, toward Vaelmoor. *Hurry.*

And outside, far off down the drowned dark of the kings' road, a lantern flickered where no lantern should be — then another, and another, moving west.
