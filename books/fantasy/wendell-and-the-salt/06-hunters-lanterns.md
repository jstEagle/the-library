## 6. Hunters' Lanterns

Brannoc had spent eleven years in the king's army learning to read the night, and the night that came down on the old salt road was wrong in three ways.

First: no frogs. The marsh to the east of the waystation had been singing since dusk, a wet chorus that Sefa said meant rain by morning. It stopped all at once, like a door closing.

Second: the horses. Wendell's pony and Brannoc's own grey mare had gone quiet in their picket, ears flat, staring west with the fixed attention of animals watching something they could not name.

Third — and this was the one that made him reach for his sword belt, the one he'd sworn off wearing, the one he'd buried under a floorboard in a garrison town two years ago before digging it up again because old habits are harder to kill than men — third, there were lanterns moving through the trees where there should have been none. Small ones. Hooded. Carried low, so their light spilled only at the carrier's feet, which was how witch-hunters walked after dark. He'd marched beside them often enough to know. They called it walking humbly, as if the Crown's hounds had ever once been humble about anything.

He found Freya at the waystation's broken hearth, turning a bone charm over in her fingers. "Lanterns," he said quietly. "West treeline. Six, maybe eight. Moving like soldiers."

She didn't startle. He'd noticed that about her early on; she carried her fear somewhere deep, banked like coals, and it never flared where you could see it. She only set down the charm and looked at the boy asleep against Sefa's side by the fire.

"Wake them," she said. "No lights. The back wall has a gap — Tam showed me."

Tam. Old Tam, who talked to rivers and had argued with the millstream outside the waystation for an hour that afternoon until it agreed, he claimed, to run loud enough to cover their voices. Tam was up on the roofless tower keeping what he called the river-watch, which mostly meant sitting with his boots off and his toes in the water.

"I'll get Tam," Brannoc said. "You get the others moving."

"Brannoc." Her voice caught him at the doorway. For the first time since he'd known her, something moved behind her eyes that looked almost like fear. "If they take me, you take Wendell and you don't look back. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to break," he said, and went out into the dark.

* * *

The tower was already empty when he reached it.

For one stupid heartbeat he thought the old man had simply wandered off, the way Tam wandered, following some conversation only he could hear. Then Brannoc saw the lanterns again — closer now, four of them fanned out along the riverbank, and between two of them, small and stooped and walking with his hands bound before him, went Tam.

They hadn't even bothered to gag him. That was the detail that would live in Brannoc's memory afterward, that would wake him sweating for weeks: they hadn't gagged him because Tam wasn't shouting. He was talking. Low and urgent, head bent toward the reeds, and Brannoc would have bet his life — had, in fact, just lost that bet — that the old man was begging the river for help.

The river did not help. Rivers keep their own counsel. Brannoc watched the lanterns swallow the old man whole and made himself do the hardest thing he'd learned in eleven years of soldiering, which was to stay hidden when every muscle in his body screamed to charge.

Because charging was what they wanted. Eight hunters didn't take prisoners in the open unless they wanted the rest of the nest stirred out of hiding. Tam was bait on a hook, and Brannoc knew hooks, had baited enough of them himself along the border marches, dragging deserters' families into the light to make the deserters come running.

He went back for the others with the taste of copper in his mouth.

"They have him," he told Freya. Her face in the firelight — the last of the firelight; Sefa was drowning the coals — went still and terrible.

"How many?"

"Eight visible. Probably more in the trees. They'll expect us to run east or try a rescue. So we do neither. We go north through the marsh, single file, and we're miles away before they work out the hook was empty."

Sefa was binding Wendell's hands to hers with a length of cord — an old hedge-witch trick, Brannoc realized, so the boy couldn't be separated from her in the dark. Her other hand pressed a poultice-wrapped knife against her belt. "And Tam?" she asked.

Freya answered before Brannoc could. "We come back for him. When we know the ground." Her voice was level. Her knuckles, around the bone charm, were white. "That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic."

It sounded like something a soldier would say. Brannoc wondered, not for the first time, what kind of life had taught a bone-carver from a quiet valley to talk like a woman counting casualties.

They went north into the marsh.

* * *

The marsh nearly killed them twice before the hunters did.

The first time was the black pool that looked like solid ground and took Wendell to his chest before Sefa's grip on the cord hauled him back, gasping, mud to his ears. The second time was the willow stand where Brannoc put his boot down on a snare line — old, rotted, mercifully dead — and stood frozen for a full minute listening to his own heart.

Then the lanterns came again.

Not behind them. Ahead. Three points of hooded light sliding through the reeds on their northern line, patient as herons, cutting off the crossing they'd aimed for. And behind — he turned, slow, careful — two more, and beyond those, faint but unmistakable, the sound of dogs. Not real dogs. Mage-hounds made no sound at all until they chose to, and what they chose to sound like was your own mother calling you home.

"They've split," Sefa whispered. "Pushed us onto the shingle bar."

She was right. The ground under their feet had firmed without their noticing, rising out of the mire into a long gravel spine dotted with dead alders — high ground, dry ground, and completely exposed, a stage built for them by someone who understood this country better than they did.

A voice came out of the dark, pleasant and unhurried. "Friends. You're standing on the Wethering Bar. There's water on three sides of you and my people on the fourth. Nobody needs to be hurt tonight."

Brannoc knew that voice. Sergeant-Captain Vess, Crown Inquisition, Third Company. He'd drunk with the man at Fort Marren, back when Brannoc still wore red and believed the red meant something. Vess had a reputation for gentleness that was worse than cruelty, because it was genuine right up until it wasn't.

"You have something the king wants," Vess went on. "Give it over, and the old man walks free. Give it over, and I'll write in my report that we found nothing here but vagrants. I'm told the valley folk are good at being nothing."

Freya stepped forward, out of the shadow of the alders, and Brannoc's blood went cold, because she was holding Wendell's shoulder and pushing the boy half behind her, and that gesture — that exact gesture — told Vess everything.

"There," Vess said softly. Lanterns converged. "There he is."

What happened next happened fast, and Brannoc's memory of it would always come back to him in pieces.

The crossbow bolt came first. He heard it — the flat snap of the cord — and moved without deciding to, shoulder into Sefa, taking them both down behind a fallen alder. The bolt meant for whoever had stepped forward took Sefa through the meat of her upper arm instead, punching clean through, and her scream was short and furious rather than long.

Then Wendell was screaming too, but not in pain — in rage, holding up the shard of grey stone, and the stone was *glowing*, a dull ember-light like a forge at dawn, and every mage-hound in the dark began to howl at once, a sound like grief given teeth.

Then it was steel. Brannoc came up out of cover with his sword finally, shamefully, in his hand, and met a hunter coming over the gravel with a bill-hook, and eleven years of drill did its ugly work. The man went down. Another came and another thing happened that Brannoc would turn over for many nights after: the dead alder beside him *moved*. Bent. A root as thick as his thigh whipped out of the gravel and caught the second hunter across the knees, and old Tam's voice — distant, thin, carried on water — seemed to say *now, lads, now.*

The river rose. Not a flood, nothing so dramatic — just the marsh waters shrugging up their banks, swallowing the lantern lines, turning the hunters' neat positions into scrambling men waist-deep in cold muck. Tam, wherever they were holding him, was spending whatever he had left.

But there were more of them than there was river. Brannoc took a slash across the forearm blocking a hunter's blade away from Wendell, felt the hot rope of it, and then the fight broke apart into darkness and splashing and Sefa, white-faced, pressing her wounded arm shut with a knotwork sigil that smoked faintly, saying, "Go, go, the crossing's open, *go*—"

They went. All except Freya.

* * *

Brannoc found her twenty yards downstream, kneeling in the shallows beside a body.

The hunter lay face-up in six inches of water, and the river had done its work quickly — the man had taken a root-blow to the head, Brannoc saw, or a fall, or both. His lantern floated nearby, guttering, throwing a sick sway of light across the reeds. He was young. Twenty, perhaps. Freya's hands hovered above his chest, not touching, and her lips were moving, and the air around her had gone strange — thick, humming, the way air feels before lightning.

"We have to move," Brannoc said. "Now, Freya. They'll regroup."

"He's not gone yet." Her voice was flat and far away. "There's a little while yet. Long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

She looked up at him, and in the swaying lantern light her face was terrible and calm. "To ask him what Aldric wants with the shard. To ask him where they're taking Tam. To ask him everything his mouth won't say and his blood will."

Brannoc understood then, and his stomach turned. He'd heard rumors, in the army, about bone-speakers — harborers, the decrees called them. Whisperers to corpses. The Church burned them on principle and the Crown paid bounties, and every story agreed on one thing: they didn't raise the dead. They *questioned* them, in that narrow window while the soul's echo still clung to the meat, and what they learned, they learned at a price no living listener wanted to imagine paying.

"You said you'd buried it," he said. "The gift. You told me the valley took it out of you."

"The valley muffled it." She drew a breath. "Out here, with the stone awake—" her hand drifted toward Wendell's shard, glowing faintly in the boy's fist upstream, "—it's not muffled anymore. It hasn't been since the gate let him through. I've been holding the door shut with my teeth for weeks, Brannoc. I can't hold it anymore. And I find I don't want to."

She put her palms flat on the dead man's chest.

Brannoc had seen sieges. He had seen the plague camps at Corren Ford. Nothing in eleven years had prepared him for watching a woman listen to a corpse.

Her spine arched. Her breath went ragged, then stopped altogether — stopped, for four seconds, five, six, while the hunter's drowned face twitched and his half-open eyes rolled as though dreaming. The water around them went glass-still. Every reed in earshot leaned toward her like a congregation.

Then Freya exhaled, a long shuddering sound, and began to speak — but the voice was wrong. Layered. Her own words underneath and something else riding them, a chorus speaking through one throat.

*"The king is afraid,"* she said, in a voice that was hers and a dead man's and something older than both. *"The ritual is half-made. The crown sits crooked on his head and the covenant stone is the keystone — the shard must be brought to the Ash Altar at the turning of the season, or the stolen power unravels and takes him with it. He is not hunting the gifted, soldier. He is hunting one boy. The rest is noise to keep the realm blind."*

"And Tam?" Brannoc managed. "Where do they take prisoners?"

The layered voice turned its head toward him, and the dead man's mouth moved with it, and Brannoc held his ground through sheer stubbornness.

*"North. To the road-fort at Harrowgate. He will be questioned gently, and then he will be questioned otherwise. You have four days before the gentle part ends."*

The corpse sighed — a real sigh, human and final — and went slack, and Freya pitched forward into the shallows. Brannoc caught her before the water did. She was shaking, soaked, gasping, and when she lifted her face to his there were tears cutting tracks through the mud and her eyes were wide with something that was not fear.

"It's true," she whispered. "Everything the dead have been trying to tell me since the valley broke. Aldric shattered the covenant to steal the throne, and the ritual isn't finished — Wendell's shard is the piece that completes it or unmakes him. And they have Tam at Harrowgate, and we have four days."

Behind them, upstream, Sefa's voice cracked across the dark, raw with pain and urgency: "Riders! On the south road — torches, a dozen at least — *they've called in the company!*"

Freya gripped Brannoc's ruined forearm, hard, and got her feet under her.

"Then we stop running north like rabbits," she said, and there was iron in it now, forged new and still hot. "We go to Harrowgate. We get Tam back. And then, Brannoc — then we go to Vaelmoor, and we finish what Aldric started."

The torches were coming. She turned to meet them like a woman reading a sentence she had finally, at last, decided to write herself.
