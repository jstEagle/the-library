## 7. Vaelmoor Under Siege

Sefa felt the city before she saw it — a pressure in her healing-sense, like a bruise the size of a horizon. Vaelmoor sat on its granite spur above the river, walls the color of old iron, and every soul inside it was afraid. Fear had a taste. She'd learned that in plague camps and birthing rooms: fear tasted of copper and cold sweat, and from three miles off, Vaelmoor was drowning in it.

"They're burning the farms," Brannoc said. He stood at the crest of the ridge with his spyglass, and his voice had gone flat in the way it did when he was holding something down. "Smoke for as far as I can see. Aldric's clearing the ground before his siege lines close."

Below them, the river valley that fed the free cities was a smear of black fields. Grain still standing in some places, set alight where it stood. Sefa thought of the farmer's wife two days back who'd given them bread and wouldn't take coin, only pressed a charm against Tam's shoulder and said *for luck*. She wondered if the woman had gotten out.

"Can we go around?" Wendell asked. He was carrying Tam's pack now along with his own; they all were, in turns, because none of them could bear to leave it behind and none of them could say why out loud.

"Around to where?" Freya said quietly.

Nobody answered. The road ran ahead of them, straight as an arrow-shot toward the eastern gate, and on either side of it the world was smoke.

* * *

They came through the gate at dusk, in a river of refugees — carters, shepherds, a whole family of dyers with their hands stained blue to the wrist, a priest of the Quiet Order who kept murmuring blessings over everyone who jostled him. The gate guards weren't checking faces. They were checking hands, throats, eyes. Looking for marks. Looking for gifts.

Sefa watched Freya pull her sleeves down over her wrists and become, in the space of a breath, a tired woman with a boy in tow. It was a skill, that disappearing. Sefa had spent thirty years learning how to be unremarkable herself, and she still couldn't do it half so well.

The guard took Brannoc's chin in a calloused hand and turned his face to the light. "Deserter?"

"Discharged," Brannoc said, and produced papers Sefa had never seen him carry. Forged, surely. Good ones.

The guard grunted and waved them through, and then they were inside, and the smell hit them — woodsmoke and tallow and too many bodies, the smell of a city preparing to be shut like a fist.

Vaelmoor's streets were stacked with timber and sand. Every doorway had bundles of arrows. Children too young to understand were being taught to carry water to the cisterns, and children old enough to understand were being taught to loose crossbows. A woman was nailing boards over a shrine to the Lady of the Threshold, and when Sefa asked her why, the woman said, "Because the king says shrines to the dead are witchcraft now," and hammered another nail, and did not look up.

They found lodging in a tanner's loft near the Wall Street, paying triple what it was worth because everything in Vaelmoor cost triple now. From the little window you could see the plain beyond the walls, and by full dark you could see the fires of the encircling army kindling one by one, west to east, like someone stitching a wound closed around the city.

"There," Wendell whispered. He pointed south, where the fires stopped in a wide gap. "Why's there no camp there?"

"The Saltmarsh," Freya said. "Even Aldric won't bivouac ten thousand men in ground that swallows horses." Her voice was steady but her hands, Sefa noticed, were working at each other in her lap, knuckle over knuckle. "It doesn't matter. They'll seal the marsh road within the week. They're not trying to starve us fast. They're trying to starve us slow, so we break ourselves."

* * *

The council met in the Moot Hall on the second morning, and by some alchemy of desperation — Brannoc's forged papers again, and a healer's satchel, which opened doors because every door in a besieged city needed a healer — Sefa got the four of them into the gallery.

She wished afterward she hadn't.

There were nine seats on the council floor and the men and women in them might as well have been nine different nations. Lord-Protector Cassian of Vaelmoor, gray-bearded and hollow-eyed, wanted to fight. Magister Ovelle of the Charters Guild wanted to negotiate — "Aldric will grant terms if we yield the gifted among us, he has said so publicly—" and the gallery hissed at that, and Ovelle raised her voice: "He has said so *publicly*, which means it is a lever, not a promise, but a lever is more than we have!"

"We have walls," said the harbor-mistress, a broad woman named Ilsette with anchor tattoos gone blurry with age. "We have grain for four months. We have—"

"Four months." A young delegate from the inland towns, still wearing road dust. "And after four months? You've seen the farms burn. There is no harvest coming to relieve us. We fight now or we kneel now; those are the only roads."

"Then let us talk about what fighting means," Ovelle said, and her voice dropped, and the hall went quiet enough that Sefa heard it clearly from the gallery. "It means Aldric's witch-hunters inside our walls the day we lose. It means every man, woman, and child born with a gift dragged to the pyres. And it means this—" she unfolded a paper, "—the decree posted at every gate of ours he's taken. Read it. *Harbors of the gifted shall hang beside them.* Harbors. That is anyone who sheltered us. Anyone who healed us. Anyone who—" her eyes came up, deliberately, to the gallery, "—traveled with them."

Silence. In it, Sefa felt Freya go rigid beside her.

Then an old man rose from the last seat — Sefa hadn't marked him before, a small stooped figure in a Quiet Order robe — and spoke in a voice like dry leaves.

"You argue about levers and walls. I will tell you what you have forgotten." He turned slowly, addressing the whole floor. "This city was founded by the covenant. Your charter, your laws, your very name — Vaelmoor, *voice of the moor* — comes from the old accord between the living and the dead. The ancestors defended these walls once before, in the time of the Sundering. They can defend them again. But the covenant is broken, shattered by the man who now besieges us, and until it is reforged, the dead sleep behind a locked door while their descendants die in the street."

"Counselor Ede," Cassian said heavily, "the covenant is a story."

"So is your charter," said Ede. "Stories are the only things men have ever died for willingly."

The hall erupted. Sefa stopped listening to the words and started listening to the sound — the sound of a city arguing with itself while the noose tightened — and under it, faintly, she heard something else. A hum. Low, rising, like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Freya was staring at Wendell's pack. At the shape inside it.

* * *

That night, in the tanner's loft, Freya laid the shard of grey stone on the floorboards between them and lit one candle, though Sefa knew the light made no difference to her. It was for the rest of them. So they could watch her face while she listened.

"What are you hearing?" Sefa asked.

"More than I've ever heard." Freya's voice was strange — stretched thin, like wire under weight. "In the valley, the dead were whispers. Here—" she pressed her palm flat to the floor, "—under this city there are bones. Generations of them. And the shard is calling to them, or they're calling to it, and it's getting louder every hour the army sits out there. All those people dying on the walls soon. All those ancestors who *remember defending this place.*"

Wendell sat cross-legged by the stone, chin on his fists. "Can they help? Like Counselor Ede said?"

"I don't know." Freya looked up, and in the candlelight her eyes had the exhausted shine of someone who hasn't slept in a week. "Ede is right about one thing. The covenant isn't a story. I've *seen* it — the dead showed me, on the road. A ceremony. A joining. And I know what it demands, and it's—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "It requires a voice. A living voice that carries both realms without any shield at all. No charm. No quieting. Nothing between."

Sefa had been a hedge-witch long enough to know when someone was describing their own death. "Freya."

"It might not be death." Too quick. Much too quick. "It might just be — everything. Forever. Every voice, all at once, always." Freya laughed, and it was the worst sound Sefa had ever heard her make. "I've spent my whole life carving silence for other people. Building walls against it. And the answer was never a wall. The answer was opening the door and standing in it."

"No," Sefa said. "Find another way."

"There is no other way. That's what the dead keep telling me, and they don't lie, they *can't* lie — it's the living who lie." Freya gathered the shard back into its cloth with careful hands, the way she handled everything bone and stone, gently, like it grieved. "Aldric broke the covenant to steal his crown. He'll come for the shard to make certain it stays broken. When the walls fall — and they will fall, Sefa, I've seen the size of his army — he'll come down into this city hunting one orphan boy. Everything ends the same way unless we end it differently first."

From the window, the fires of the encirclement burned steady, patient, thousands upon thousands of them. Somewhere out in that dark, drums were talking to each other across the plain, low and rhythmic, counting down nights.

Wendell reached out and put his small hand over Freya's where it rested on the wrapped stone.

"Then we do it before they come," he said. "Right? Before the walls fall. We find the bone-vault, and we do it first."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Then at Sefa. Then at the candle flame, bending toward the window, toward the enemy, as if even fire knew which way the wind was blowing.

"Yes," she said. "Tomorrow. While we still have a tomorrow."

And far below them, beneath the streets and the cellars and the bones of the founding generations, Sefa felt — just barely, just at the edge of her own small gift — something vast turn over in its sleep, and begin, slowly, to wake.
