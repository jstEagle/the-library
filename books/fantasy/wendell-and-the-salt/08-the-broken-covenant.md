## 8. The Broken Covenant

The bone-vault lay beneath the Hall of Voices like a held breath.

Sefa had heard of it in her grandmother's stories — the free cities kept their dead differently than the crownlands, not under consecrated ground but *with* them, woven into the architecture of their greatest hall. What she hadn't believed was the scale of it. The stair wound down through three levels of pale stone, and every step was set with bones: femurs laid in herringbone patterns, skulls crowned with copper wire, ribs fanned out like the pages of an enormous book someone had abandoned mid-sentence. Lamps burned low and green behind glass, and the light moved across the walls so that the dead seemed to shift, to lean, to watch them pass.

Freya had spent her whole life hiding from rooms like this.

Now she walked down into one carrying a boy whose pocket hummed like a struck bell.

"Careful on the landing," Sefa said, her arm still bound from the ambush, her voice rougher than usual. "The old caretaker said the floor remembers weight."

"The floor can remember whatever it likes," Brannoc muttered behind them. He carried a lantern and a sword he clearly didn't trust himself to use anymore, and he kept glancing at Wendell the way a man watches weather that's about to turn. "I want it on record that I think this is a bad idea. We came here to hide, not to go poking at the oldest bones in the world."

"You wanted to hide," Freya said. "Wendell's stone had other plans."

It was true, and it frightened her how true. Since they'd passed the gates of Vaelmoor three days ago, the shard had grown warmer each night. Wendell slept with it wrapped in wool against his chest, and twice she'd woken to find him sitting up in the dark, staring at nothing, saying quietly, *it's pulling, Freya, it's pulling toward the water.* The council's maps put the Hall of Voices directly above the old cisterns, and the old cisterns above the founding vaults, and somewhere down there, something answered the shard the way a tuning fork answers its twin.

The last stair opened into a round chamber no larger than a granary, and Freya stopped breathing for a moment.

Bones covered every surface here too, but arranged — no, *composed*. They spiraled inward from the walls toward a central plinth of grey stone, and the spiral wasn't decoration. She knew spirals. She'd carved them into charms since she was nine years old, the pattern her grandmother called the listening-line, the shape a voice makes when it has nowhere else to go. Someone had built this room as an ear. An enormous, patient, centuries-old ear, waiting for something worth hearing.

And at the center, on the plinth, sat a socket. A hollow the size of two cupped hands, worn smooth, empty.

Wendell made a small sound. He was already walking, already untying the wool bundle, and Freya reached for him and found she couldn't make herself stop him, because the shard in his hands was glowing faintly now, a grey-gold light like dawn through fog, and the whole chamber had begun to sing — a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet dark of the lungs.

"It fits," Wendell whispered. "Freya. It *fits*."

"Don't—" But her voice died, because the shard left his hands as if the air itself had taken it, drifting up and over the plinth and settling into the socket with a sound like a door closing after twenty years.

* * *

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats. Then the room filled with the dead.

Not whispers. Not the thin, leaking voices Freya had spent her life damming back with bone and willow-bark and careful, careful silence. This was a chorus, layered and vast, rising out of the walls themselves, and it did not come *to* her — it came *through* the shard, through the stone, resonating in the chamber like a bell struck in a cathedral, and for the first time in her life Freya Quintana stood inside her gift without being pierced by it.

She saw everything at once.

A hill above a river, long before Vaelmoor, before any city. People gathered in the green — living and dead both, the dead standing among them translucent as heat-shimmer, and no one afraid. A woman at the center holding a stone the size of a loaf, grey shot through with gold, raising it while the crowd sang a single sustained note. And the woman spoke words Freya understood without knowing the language, because the meaning arrived ahead of the sound:

*We who live will carry those who are gone. We will hear them, grieve them, keep their names. And they who have crossed will guard us, counsel us, hold the memory of what we were. One covenant, two shores, one voice between.*

The vision turned. The woman lowered the stone into a socket in the hillside — this hillside, Freya realized, this very chamber built over the first foundation — and pressed her bare hands to it, and the light that poured through her nearly whited out the vision entirely. When it faded, the woman was kneeling, gray-faced, aged years in moments, and the dead around her bowed their heads to her as to one of their own.

*A voice between,* the chorus said, all of them, gentle and terrible. *This is the price. This has always been the price.*

Then the vision broke apart like ice on a spring river, and showed her the rest: a younger Aldric, not yet king, standing in a vault much like this one in the capital, a hammer of black iron raised over the covenant stone there. His face was not cruel. That was the worst part. It was *hungry*, and behind him stood men in crownguard colors, and when the hammer fell, the scream that came out of the breaking stone was one Freya recognized, because she'd been hearing its echo her whole life in every whisper she'd ever silenced — the sound of ten thousand voices suddenly having nowhere to go.

The dead kings' voices scattered. Some went mad. Some went quiet forever. And Aldric gathered what leaked from the wound — gathered it the way you'd gather spilled wine — and drank power that was never his to drink, and bought a throne with it.

The vision released her.

Freya came back to herself on her knees on the cold floor, Sefa's good hand gripping her shoulder, Wendell's face white and close, Brannoc swearing softly in soldier's cadence. The shard sat in its socket, dimming slowly, and the chamber was silent again — but it was a different silence now. A listening one.

"Tell us," Sefa said.

So Freya told them. The covenant. The ceremony. The woman who had knelt and given herself as the bridge between shores, and what it had cost her — not death, worse than death by some measures, the surrender of every boundary in her soul, every wall between self and other, until the day she died she would carry both realms through her own body with nothing in between. No charm. No filter. No silence, ever again, not for one breath, not for one hour, for the rest of her life.

"And Aldric shattered the stone," Brannoc said flatly, "because a broken covenant means no voice between, which means the dead can't counsel anyone, which means—"

"Which means no one can prove what he did," Freya finished. "No ancestors to testify. No dead witnesses. He didn't just steal a throne. He stole the courtroom."

Wendell hadn't spoken. He stood closest to the plinth, and when Freya looked at him she saw he was crying without any noise at all, the way children cry when they've learned crying gets people hurt.

"My gran knew," he said. "That's why she ran. That's why she hid the shard with me instead of keeping it. She said—" his voice cracked, steadied— "she said someday someone would need to *finish* it. I thought she meant fix a rock."

"Oh, love." Sefa pulled him in against her side.

Freya stayed where she was, on the cold floor, and let the terror move through her the way she'd once let grief move through her over a drowned child's charm. Because she understood now — fully, precisely, with the awful clarity of a carver seeing the finished shape inside the raw bone — what was being asked. Above them the armies were digging siege trenches. The council was fracturing into surrender-factions and defiance-factions, and neither faction could win, because the living alone could not hold these walls and the dead alone could not cross the broken covenant to help. Unless someone stood in between. Unless someone became the door.

She thought of her valley. Of thirty years of carved charms and swallowed words, of flinching from her neighbors' kindness because kindness invited conversation and conversation invited questions. Of the loneliness so total it had become a kind of architecture, load-bearing, structural. She had built her entire life into a shape designed to never be heard.

And the dead were asking her to unbuilt it. All of it. Forever.

"I'll do it."

She said it before she'd decided to, and then discovered she *had* decided, that the decision had been forming in her since the drowned child, since the gate, since the road, since Tam's empty place at their fire and the dying hunter's secrets poured out of his corpse into her unwilling ears. Every moment of her buried life had been a long walk toward this plinth.

"No." Brannoc stepped forward. "Absolutely not. You don't volunteer for a thing like this, you don't just—"

"Brannoc." She got to her feet. Her legs shook. Her voice didn't. "You deserted an army rather than hang gifted children. Sefa heals people the crown calls criminals. Tam talked to rivers and they took him for it. Every one of us has already paid part of this price. I'm only choosing which coin."

"There has to be another way," Sefa said, but her eyes were wet, and Freya knew she was asking because she loved her, not because she believed it.

"There isn't. You saw what I saw." Freya turned to the plinth, to the shard resting in its ancient socket, gold-grey light pulsing now like a slow heartbeat. "When do the walls break?"

Brannoc's jaw worked. "The engineers say three days. Four if it rains."

"Then we have four days to learn a ceremony older than any language still spoken." She reached out and laid her palm flat against the warm stone, and beneath her hand, faint but unmistakable, she felt it answer — not the chorus, not yet, but something smaller. Closer. A single voice, familiar as her own heartbeat, waiting patiently at the edge of hearing.

Her grandmother's voice. Saying, as she had said at every parting since Freya was small:

*Keep your silence, child. Until the day your silence is worth more than your voice.*

Outside, far above, the first siege drums began to roll.
