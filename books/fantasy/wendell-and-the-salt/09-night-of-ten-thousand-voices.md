## 9. Night of Ten Thousand Voices

The wall of Vaelmoor broke at moonrise, and the sound of it was the sound of a world ending: stone shrieking against stone, a groan that rose through the soles of Freya's boots and into her teeth and stayed there, rattling. Dust rolled down the streets like fog. Somewhere in the white haze, men were screaming.

She stood in the bone-vault beneath the old temple with Wendell's hand in hers, and she felt every death above her as a small cold tug, like fingers plucking harp strings.

"They're through," Sefa said from the stair. Her arm was bound to her chest, her face grey. "The east breach. Brannoc's holding the square, but he can't hold it long. There are too many."

Freya looked down at Wendell. He was eleven years old and had not slept properly in nine days, and he held the shard of the covenant stone in both fists the way he'd held it the first night, when it hummed warm against her palm at the valley gate. It was humming now. She could feel it through his skin, through hers, a low note like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

"You don't have to do this," she said. "I want you to hear me say it plainly. You don't have to."

"I know." His voice cracked in the middle. He lifted his chin anyway, the way children do when they have decided to be brave and are daring you to tell them they're too small for it. "But I'm the anchor. The dead said so. The stone knows my grandmother's hands. It doesn't know anyone else's anymore."

*It knows mine,* Freya thought. *And after tonight it will know nothing else of me, ever again.*

That was the price. She had read it in the bones three nights past, in the vault's cold light, while the ancestors of Vaelmoor showed her the first ceremony — two realms, one voice, a living throat willing to carry the whole chorus of the dead without shield, without filter, without mercy. She had spent twenty-two years carving silence into bone charms, building walls inside herself thicker than any city wall, and tonight she was going to take every one of them down and stand in the flood.

"Then we go up," she said.

* * *

The breach was worse than she had imagined. A section of the eastern wall forty feet wide had simply ceased to be, crumbled into a ramp of rubble that Aldric's soldiers were climbing in waves, torchlight bobbing among them like a river of embers flowing uphill. Brannoc's line held at the square's edge — deserters and dockhands and boys with kitchen knives, a ragged hedge of shields and pikes — and the hedge was bending.

Brannoc himself stood at its center, blood sheeting down the left side of his face, roaring orders in the parade-ground voice he swore he'd buried with his old life. When he saw Freya come up out of the smoke with a child at her side, something moved across his ruined features — hope, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"Witch!" he bellowed over the din. "Tell me you've got something!"

"I've got everything," Freya said. "Get your people behind me. All of them. Now."

He didn't ask. That was the thing about Brannoc she would remember all her life: he didn't ask. He turned and drove his people back with the flat of his blade, and the line folded inward like a hand closing, and then Freya Quintana stood alone at the mouth of the breach with the enemy pouring toward her and Wendell kneeling at her left, setting the shard down on a broken paving stone as gently as if it were an egg.

The dead were already there. They had been gathering all night — the defenders who'd fallen on the walls, the sailors drowned in the harbor, the plague-dead in their pits beneath the city, and older things, far older, the ancestors of Vaelmoor who had signed the first covenant in blood and bone before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather was born. They pressed against the edges of her senses like faces against frost-glazed glass.

All her life she had kept them out. Charms of river-bone and crow-bone, carved in secret, burned in secret, a lifetime of whispered silences. Her neighbors in the valley had thought her quiet because she was shy. Only the dead had known the truth: that she was quiet because she was full, and one more voice would have drowned her.

She reached up and pulled the last charm from around her neck — the memorial piece, the one she'd carved for the drowned child in another life, in the valley that forgot — and she did not burn it. She set it beside the shard.

"Wendell," she said. "Whatever happens, whatever you see, whatever I say — keep your hands on the stone. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"If I stop being me — if what comes through isn't me anymore — you pull your hands away and you run. Promise me."

His jaw worked. Tears cut clean lines through the ash on his face. "I promise," he lied, and she loved him for lying.

She knelt. She put one hand flat on the shard, and one hand flat on the cold ground of Vaelmoor, where the bones of ten generations slept, and she opened every door inside herself at once.

* * *

The voices came like the sea.

Not one sea — all seas, every ocean that ever drowned a sailor, every river that ever took a child, every battlefield and birthing bed and fevered night since the first human breath. They came through her and they were agony, they were knives drawn slowly through the soft tissue of her soul, and she screamed, and her scream became their scream, ten thousand throats speaking with one ruined voice.

**WE ARE HERE,** said the dead of Vaelmoor. **WE HAVE ALWAYS BEEN HERE.**

The soldiers at the breach stopped climbing. Torchlight wavered. Men crossed themselves with the wrong hands, the old way, the way their grandmothers had taught them before Aldric's priests burned the old gestures out of the liturgy.

Freya stood. She was crying without knowing it; she was bleeding from the nose without knowing it; she was, in some fashion that had nothing to do with flesh, burning — and she spoke aloud, because the covenant demanded a living voice, and hers was the only one left standing in the door between worlds.

"Hear me!" The words came out layered, harmonized, a chord of the living and the dead. "Hear me, living and dead! The covenant was broken by treachery — by a man who shattered the stone and stole the silence of the ancestors to fuel his crown! We reforge it now! Blood of the living—" she bit through her own thumb and pressed it to the shard— "and bone of the dead, and one voice to carry them both, freely given, forever!"

Wendell's hands pressed down on the stone. The shard flared white.

And the dead rose.

They came up out of the ground like spring coming up out of winter — green shoots of light through black soil. The defenders who had died on the walls stood again among the living, translucent and terrible and grinning. The drowned sailors walked up out of the harbor trailing silver water. And behind them, vast and slow as weather, the ancestors of Vaelmoor — kings and fishwives and bone-carvers, a thousand years of the city's dead — filled the breach from edge to edge, a wall of the risen that no army of the living had ever been asked to climb.

The soldiers broke. Not all at once — first the back ranks, who turned and fled into the dark and carried the terror with them like a contagion — then the front, who dropped weapons and knelt, because some of them were recognizing faces. Grandfathers. Brothers. Sons taken by the levy three winters past and never returned.

Through the chaos, one figure came on. Aldric rode a pale horse through his own routing army, and the crown on his head burned with stolen light, and his face was the face of a man watching his fortune drain out through a hole in the bottom of the world.

"The stone is MINE," he roared, and flung his hand out, and the stolen power leapt from him — a lash of grey fire that should have unmade everything it touched.

It touched the risen dead and unraveled. The ancestors opened like a gate and let it pass through them, diminished, and diminished again, and by the time it reached Freya it was only wind. She felt it break against her like foam.

"You cannot," Aldric said, and for the first time his voice was only a man's voice, thin and mortal. "The stone is broken. I broke it myself. It cannot be reforged."

"It was never yours to break," said the dead through Freya's mouth, ten thousand voices in terrible harmony. "And it was never broken. Only scattered. And we have been waiting, Aldric son of Marren, every hour of every year, for someone willing to carry us home."

The ancestors closed around him like water closing around a sinking man. His horse screamed and fled. He drew a sword that glowed with borrowed years — years drained from the gifted his hunters had seized, from harbors hanged, from children's gifts strangled in their cradles — and the dead took the glow from it strand by strand, gently, the way you take a blanket from a sleeping child, and gave it back to the sky.

He fell to his knees in the breach he had made, in the ruins of the wall he had paid ten thousand lives to crack, and the last thing the living saw of King Aldric was him looking up — not at the armies, not at the ancestors, but at the small boy kneeling beside the blazing stone — and understanding, at the very end, exactly what he had spent a kingdom trying to prevent.

Then the dead took him down into the earth to be judged by those he had silenced, and the crown rolled free across the rubble, and stopped at Freya's feet.

* * *

Afterward there was silence.

No — that wasn't right. Freya lay on the broken stones with Wendell's arms around her and Brannoc's cloak over her and Sefa's healing hands hovering, afraid to touch, and she listened, and there was no silence and there never would be again. The dead of Vaelmoor murmured around her like a tide that had decided, finally, to stay. Not torment. Not drowning. Just... company. Endless, eternal, irrevocable company.

"Well?" Brannoc's voice, rough as gravel. "Did it work? Is she—"

"She's smiling," Wendell said, wonder in it. "Why is she smiling?"

Because somewhere in the chorus, clear as a bell through all the rest, she could hear the drowned child from the valley, singing. Because the covenant was reforged and the gifted of the world would wake tomorrow to a dawn where no hunter's lantern meant death. Because she had given up silence forever and received, in exchange, a family that would never, ever leave her alone.

"Because we won," Freya whispered, in her own voice, mostly her own. Then, softer, to the boy holding her hand: "Wendell. There's something I need to teach you about carving bone. I think... I think it's time you learned."

Above them, the first grey light of morning crept into the breach, and the dead of Vaelmoor began, very quietly, to sing.
