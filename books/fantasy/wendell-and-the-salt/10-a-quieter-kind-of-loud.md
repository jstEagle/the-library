## 10. A Quieter Kind of Loud

The first thing I learned about silence was that Freya had lost hers forever.

I didn't understand it at first. In the weeks after the walls held — after the dead stood up out of the mud and the sea-wind and the old bones beneath Vaelmoor and turned Aldric's army into a running thing — I thought she'd been given something. That was how the songs already had it, and the songs were wrong in every way songs are wrong. The balladeers on the Miren Steps sang about the Lady of Ten Thousand Voices like she'd been blessed, chosen, lifted up. They sang it to her face sometimes, in the market square, and Freya would smile her thin smile and keep carving, and I'd watch her hands and know what the singing cost.

She never heard quiet again. Not in sleep, not in prayer, not in the grey hour before dawn when the rest of us got to be nobody for a little while. The dead spoke through her always now, a low tide of them, grandmothers and soldiers and drowned children and kings who'd been dead so long they'd forgotten their own names. She carried them the way a bridge carries a river. And she did it without flinching, mostly, because flinching was a luxury and she had spent hers at the breach.

But some mornings I'd find her on the roof of the bone-vault with her eyes shut and her jaw tight, and I knew she was listening for the space between voices, the way you press your tongue into the socket of a tooth that isn't there anymore.

That was why I asked her to teach me the carving. Not because I wanted the gift — though the shard in my pocket hummed warmer every month, and everyone said I'd have some measure of it whether I wanted it or not — but because I'd figured out that when her hands were moving, when the knife found the grain of the bone, the voices went easier on her. Like they liked to watch.

* * *

We worked in the vault's upper room, where the light came through windows of thick green glass and fell across the table like pond water. Brannoc had fixed the shutters himself, grumbling the whole time about carpentry being a soldier's punishment in the afterlife. Sefa's herbs hung from the beams in bundles that smelled of bitterleaf and honey-thyme. Old Tam's river-stones lined the sill, one for every waterway he'd made peace with, and he swore the Vaelmoor harbor current had forgiven him for the war but only just.

It was the first room I'd ever lived in that felt like it would notice if I left.

"Thumb here," Freya said. "Not there. There."

I moved my thumb. The awl skated sideways and gouged a white line across the charm blank, ruining an hour of work.

"Sorry," I said.

"Don't apologize to the bone." She turned the piece over in her fingers, studying my mistake with more patience than I deserved. "Apologizing is for people. Bone doesn't care if you're sorry. It cares if you're honest. You rushed because you wanted it finished, and the bone felt you rushing, and it told you no."

"Bone talks?"

"Everything talks." She said it lightly, but I saw her hand drift to her temple, the way it did when the tide of voices rose. "You just have to be quiet enough to hear it. Which is a joke, coming from me."

She set the ruined blank aside — not thrown away; nothing in that room was ever thrown away — and took a fresh one from the basket. Cow femur, sanded smooth, pale as milk.

"Start again. Slower. Tell me what you're carving."

"A memorial," I said. "For Tam's sister."

Freya's knife paused above her own work. "He told you about Marisol?"

"He tells the river about her. I listen to the river." I shrugged, embarrassed. "Old Tam says things to the water he can't say to us. But water repeats itself. Rivers gossip."

Something moved through Freya's face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "Then you're already halfway gifted, Wendell. Half this craft is eavesdropping on things that were never meant for you, and the other half is knowing what to do with it once you've heard."

* * *

The charter came to Vaelmoor by courier in the third week of autumn: seven cities' worth of wax seals, heavy as a paving stone, drafted in the Hall of Concord at Miren with Sefa standing witness as delegate for the healers' guilds and Brannoc looming behind her in his old uniform with the deserter's brand still visible on his wrist, because he refused to cover it. *Let them look,* he said. *Let them see what their last king did to men who wouldn't burn villages.*

They read it aloud in the market square, all of us crowded on the vault steps. *No person gifted in the covenant arts shall be seized, silenced, hanged, or harbored against.* Harbors — that was the old word, the word from the proclamation of ash, for the ones who sheltered us. The word they'd hanged people for. Now it was written into law like a promise.

Wendell, they called me up too — no, that's wrong, I'm writing this myself, I should say it plainly. They called me up. Me, the orphan boy who'd walked through a valley border that shouldn't have let him through, carrying a shard of the broken stone warm in his fist like a coal that loved him. Councilor Vey put the charter copy in my hands in front of half the city and said, "You carried the old covenant. Carry this one too."

My hands shook. Freya's didn't shake when she stepped up beside me, but she stood close enough that our shoulders touched, and I understood that was her version of shaking.

Afterward, on the steps, Brannoc read the whole thing twice more to himself, lips moving, and then cleared his throat roughly and said, "Never thought I'd see my brand worth anything." Sefa squeezed his wrist, right over the scarred letters, and didn't let go.

Tam cried, but he claimed it was the wind off the harbor, and the river backed him up, so we let him have it.

* * *

Evenings, the household gathered in the vault kitchen, which Sefa had transformed from a cold stone box into the warmest room in Vaelmoor through methods she described as "cooking" and Brannoc described as "siegecraft." We ate barley stew and harbor fish and bread that Tam charmed — not truly charmed, he insisted, just *encouraged* — into rising higher than any baker's in the city.

Those nights, Freya was different. Not quieter — she could never be quieter again — but *settled*, the way a harbor is settled with boats in it. The dead murmured through her while she ate, and sometimes she'd relay things without meaning to. *Your mother says you chew with your mouth open,* she told me once, apropos of nothing, and I dropped my spoon, and she looked stricken until I started laughing and couldn't stop, because my mother had been dead eight years and I'd forgotten the sound of being scolded, and here it was, delivered across the divide with a mouthful of stew.

"I'm sorry," Freya said. "Sometimes they slip through."

"Don't be sorry." I meant it so hard it came out fierce. "Don't ever be sorry for that."

She studied me for a long moment. Around her, faint as heat-shimmer, I could almost see them — not faces, not yet, but presences, a crowd standing close in a cold wind. She'd stopped fighting them months ago. She'd learned to make room.

"You know what the ceremony demanded," she said quietly. "A voice willing to carry both realms. I thought it meant sacrifice. Silence traded for service." Her hand turned palm-up on the table, open, the way she held it when she listened. "But a covenant isn't a wall between two houses. It's a door. Doors get walked through from both sides. I spent my whole life building walls, Wendell. The valley taught me that. Hide the gift, bury it alive, be invisible." She shook her head slowly. "The valley was wrong. Or maybe it was right for its time, and its time ended the day a starving boy walked through a border that should have stopped him."

"The border let me through because of the shard," I said. I'd said it a hundred times. It was the story everyone wanted.

"No," Freya said. "The border let you through because it recognized family. Stone knows stone. Covenant knows covenant." She picked up her knife again. "Now eat your stew. Your mother's still complaining about the chewing."

* * *

On the last mild evening of autumn, we climbed to the roof together — me, Freya, and the shard, which I'd taken to wearing on a cord around my neck since the council asked to study it and I'd discovered I couldn't bear to let it leave my skin. Below us Vaelmoor lit its lamps one district at a time, gold spreading through the streets like something poured. Beyond the walls, the siege-works were being dismantled, cart by cart, and farmers were planting winter wheat in ground that two months ago had been trench and corpse-fire.

"It's strange," I said. "Everyone calls it a victory."

"It was a victory."

"It doesn't feel like one. It feels like—" I searched for it. "Like a door opening onto a room we've never been in. Big room. No map."

Freya laughed — really laughed, short and surprised out of her, one of maybe six I'd counted since the breach. "That's exactly what it feels like. Welcome to the rest of your life."

The wind off the harbor stirred her hair, grey-streaked now, though she wasn't old enough for it. Grief did that, Sefa said. So did carrying ten thousand voices. On the roof, with the city glowing below, I could see them if I squinted — the dead around her, or rather the *shape* of them, a vast gentle pressure like weather. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that I knew to listen, the shard humming against my chest in answer, in greeting, in something that felt embarrassingly like love.

"Tell me honestly," I said. "If you could give it back — the silence. Would you?"

She was quiet a long time. Somewhere below, Tam was arguing with the harbor current about the fishing quotas, and losing.

"No," she said finally. "Silence was a country I lived in alone. This is a house full of people." She touched the cord at my neck, and the shard sang one clear note that only the two of us heard. "It's loud, Wendell. It's so loud. But it's a quieter kind of loud than being alone ever was."

She handed me the awl then, and a fresh blank of bone, pale as milk in the lamplight.

"Now. Show me what you'd carve for the child who hasn't drowned yet. Because there will be one, someday, somewhere, and someone will need to make her a charm before the water takes her — not after." Her eyes were bright and terrible and kind. "That's the covenant now. Not memorials. Warnings. Begin."

I set my thumb where she'd taught me, and the bone listened, and far below us the free city burned its lamps against the dark like ten thousand small defiant promises — and under my hands, for the first time, the bone began to speak back.
