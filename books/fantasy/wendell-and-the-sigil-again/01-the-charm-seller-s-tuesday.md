## 1. The Charm Seller's Tuesday

There are three rules to selling charms in Ashmoor, and I wrote all of them myself, which makes them the only laws in the world I've never broken.

Rule one: never sell a charm that works.

Rule two: never let them see you make one.

Rule three — and this is the important one, the one I carved into my own skull sometime around my nineteenth winter — never touch real magic. Not once. Not for anything. Not even a little.

I know what you're thinking. *A charm seller whose charms don't work? That's fraud.* And you'd be right, in the way that most comfortable people are right about things they've never had to survive. But here's what ten years in the night market taught me: nobody who buys a luck-charm wants magic. They want the feeling of having done something. The fisherman with his nets full of holes doesn't need enchantment; he needs to believe tomorrow might be different from today, and a copper coin wrapped in red thread and sold by a woman with kind eyes gives him that for the price of a pint. Magic, real magic, would only ruin it. Real magic has opinions. Real magic keeps accounts.

So on Tuesday nights — Tuesday being the market's slowest night, when the Guild wardens are drowsy and the pickpockets are honest enough to leave a girl alone if she looks poor — I set up my little table between the pie cart and the man who sells secondhand boots, and I sell hope at three coppers apiece, and I go home with my hands clean.

That was the plan for this particular Tuesday, anyway. It was autumn, the kind of damp grey evening where Ashmoor smells like wet stone and frying onions and the river's old regrets, and I'd been at my table since sundown with exactly four sales and one argument. The argument was with a boy of maybe twelve who wanted a love-charm for a girl named Bess, and I told him love-charms were against my religion, which is true, though my religion consists entirely of not doing things.

"Three coppers," he said. "It's got to work. She laughs at me."

"She'll laugh harder if you smell of burnt rosemary," I said. "Trust me. Burnt rosemary is a tragedy, not an aphrodisiac."

"What's an afro—"

"Go home, Tam."

The name came out before I could stop it, the way names do when they've been sitting in your chest too long. The boy blinked at me, unbothered, and wandered off toward the boot-seller, and I stood there behind my table feeling like someone had reached into my ribs and rearranged the furniture.

Tam. Ten years, and I still said it like a prayer I wasn't allowed to finish.

*Focus,* I told myself, in Sef's voice, because his voice was the one that lived in my head rent-free whether I liked it or not. *A witch who broods over her own stall sells nothing.*

Not that I was a witch. Officially. Officially I was Mara Lockridge, purveyor of curiosities, licensed under the city's tinker statutes, taxed quarterly, boring as bread. Unofficially — well. Unofficially there was a version of me that had been apprenticed at fourteen to a marsh-witch with a beard like river moss and a temper like weather, a version of me who had learned things in that crooked little house that would get us both hanged if the Guild ever heard them spoken aloud. That version of me had run away ten years ago and had been running, in small careful circles, ever since.

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing: eventually you arrive somewhere, and then you have to live there, and living turns out to be much harder than leaving. You build walls. You build rules. You become so careful that carefulness becomes the shape of your whole self, the way a shoe becomes the shape of a foot, and one day you look down and realize you can't remember what your foot looked like bare.

I was arranging my charms — *arranging*, as though thread and glass and dried lavender required feng shui — when I felt someone standing at the edge of my stall doing the thing people do when they want to talk but are composing their opening line. I knew who it was before I looked up. I'd known since the bell above the market gate rang at half past seven, because Wendell Fennick was the only person in Ashmoor punctual enough to arrive exactly when he said he would, and earnest enough to think that mattered.

"Mara," he said. "You look — the market looks — hello."

"Hello," I said. "Smooth opening. Very polished. Did you rehearse that on the walk over?"

"I rehearsed several better ones," he admitted, "and then forgot all of them when I saw you." He said it simply, without artifice, which was somehow worse than if he'd been smooth. Wendell Fennick had the terrible superpower of meaning everything he said. "Is that a love-charm?"

He was looking at the little bundle of red thread I'd made for the boy. I swept it into my apron pocket.

"It's a decoration."

"For sale?"

"For no one now. It's retired." I looked him over while I said it, because looking at Wendell was a habit I hadn't yet managed to break and had stopped trying very hard to. He was tall in the way of men who haven't noticed they're tall, all elbows and apology, with ink stains on his fingers — always ink, he was a rare-book appraiser and his hands were permanently the color of midnight — and hair that did whatever it wanted and a face that was, I suppose, handsome, if you liked kindness in a face. I liked kindness in a face. That was the problem.

"You closed early," he said, nodding at my mostly empty table. "Slow night?"

"Every night's slow. That's the business model." I began packing up, more to give my hands something to do than because I was actually closing. "Charm-selling is a volume trade. Three coppers, high margins, low expectations."

"You undersell yourself."

"You have no idea," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it came out too true, with too much weight, and Wendell tilted his head at me the way he did when a book's binding didn't match its pages.

We'd met four months ago, at the pie cart, when he'd ordered the last honeyed pear pie at the exact moment I reached for it and had then, with the gravity of a man defusing a bomb, insisted I take it. I'd refused. He'd bought it and given it to me anyway and then stood there blushing while I ate it, and something in my chest — some long-sealed door, some wall I'd spent a decade mortaring — had developed a hairline crack that I'd been pretending not to notice ever since.

Four months of Tuesdays. Pie, sometimes. Walks along the riverwall where the Guild couldn't hear anything but gulls. He talked about books the way other men talked about war stories, with reverence and grievance, and I talked about nothing, carefully, and every week the nothing got a little warmer, and every week I went home and lay awake cataloguing all the reasons it had to end.

Because here's rule four, the unwritten one: never let anyone close enough to see what you are. People who know what you are can be hurt for it. People who know what you are can be *used*. I had watched it happen. I had, in ways I didn't let myself examine, helped it happen. There was a debt outstanding against my name in a ledger I couldn't read anymore, and until I understood it, everyone near me was collateral.

"So," Wendell said, rocking slightly on his heels. "I found something today. At the shop. And I thought — well, I thought of you. Which is either romantic or concerning, depending on the item."

"Depending on the item," I repeated. "Wendell. What did you find?"

"A book." His eyes had gone bright, that appraiser's gleam, the one that made him beautiful and slightly unbearable. "A very old one. Vellum, pre-Guild, probably two centuries if it's a day. It came in a lot from a house clearance out in the Weepers' Quarter, and everyone else passed it over because the cover's water-damaged, but the spine—" He caught himself. "Sorry. I'm lecturing."

"You're not lecturing. You're glowing. It's different."

"The point is," he said, and now he was nervous again, fiddling with a strap of his satchel, "the point is I thought perhaps you'd like to see it. Books like that, they're — they hold things. Old things. And you always look at my work like you're hungry for it, and I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "Would you walk with me? To the shop? It's on the way. It's barely on the way. It's completely out of your way, actually, I checked."

I should have said no.

I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever cosmic ledger keeps track of such things: I knew, in the marrow-deep way I knew weather coming and lies arriving, that I should have said no. My whole life was built on saying no — no to power, no to risk, no to the part of myself that still remembered how to sing a hedge awake. Saying no had kept me alive through ten years that should have killed me.

But Wendell was standing in the lamplight with ink on his hands and hope all over his face, asking me to walk with him to look at an old book, and the wall with the hairline crack in it just — leaned. A little. The way walls do.

"One look," I said. "Then I'm going home to sleep like a law-abiding citizen."

"You're the least law-abiding citizen I know," he said, delighted. "You sell superstition to fishermen."

"That's called commerce. It's fully licensed."

We walked. The market folded itself up around us, awnings coming down, the pie-cart man banking his coals, the lamplighter making his rounds with his long brass pole and his bored expression. Ashmoor at night was a city holding its breath — the Guild's wards hummed along the major streets, faint as a headache, and everyone with anything to hide learned to walk the minor roads, the ones where the hum faded to nothing. I'd mapped those roads years ago. I took Wendell's arm and steered us down one without thinking about it, and he didn't ask why, which was either trust or obliviousness, and with Wendell it was usually both.

"Can I say something?" he asked, somewhere near the tannery lane.

"You will regardless."

"Probably." He was quiet for a few steps. "You do that. Deflect. With jokes. It's very good, the jokes, I laugh at most of them internally. But sometimes I think there's a whole — a person under there that nobody's met. Including you, maybe."

My step faltered, just slightly. Just enough.

"And I'm not asking you to show me," he hurried on. "I'm not an idiot. Well. I'm partially an idiot. But I'm not asking. I just wanted you to know I suspected there was more, and that I — that it didn't put me off. If anything." He exhaled. "If anything it's the main attraction."

The lamplight caught the ink on his fingers, the earnest ruin of his hair, the absolute sincerity of his stupid wonderful face, and I felt the crack in the wall widen by a hair's breadth, and instead of cementing it shut like any sensible fugitive, I heard myself say:

"There's a pie cart two streets from your shop. Open late. After we look at your book, you're buying me a honeyed pear, and in exchange I'll tell you one true thing about myself. One. Non-negotiable, small, and probably disappointing."

His whole face lit like a struck match. "Deal," he said, too fast, and then, trying to recover dignity: "I mean. Yes. That sounds agreeable."

We turned onto Binder's Row, where his shop sat wedged between a chandler and a shuttered tailor, its windows dark, its sign — *FENNICK & HALE, APPRAISERS* — swinging gently in the river wind. He unlocked the door with the ceremony of a priest, and the smell rolled out over us: dust and leather and old glue, the smell of centuries pressed flat and shelved.

"Mind the step," he said, lighting a lamp. "And Mara? Fair warning. When I opened it this afternoon, I — hm. You'll see."

"Ominous. Wonderful. Lead on."

He led me past the front room, past the locked case of first editions, back to his workbench, where a book lay under a linen cloth like a patient awaiting surgery. He drew the cloth off with both hands, gently, the way you'd uncover something sleeping.

It was ugly. Water-stained boards, a spine re-sewn badly sometime in the last century, edges soft as cloth. But when the lamp-light hit the open page, the vellum seemed to drink it, and the writing that crawled across it was dense and strange and older than the Guild, older than the city's name, and down in the margin, small and precise as a signature—

I stopped breathing.

Because I knew that hand. I had watched it write shopping lists and hexes and one very bad poem about eels. I had fled from it across half a country.

"Sef," I whispered. "This is Sef's hand."

Wendell looked at me, puzzled, ready to be gentle about whatever memory this was. "You know the scribe?"

"No," I said, which was a lie, and we both pretended otherwise. "Who's it about? What does it say?"

"That's the strange part." He turned the page carefully, angling the lamp, and his voice dropped into the hush he saved for genuinely rare things. "It's a prophecy. Or claims to be. Most of it's the usual riddle-slop — mountains, crowns, a stone, souls in lanterns, the Undercourt, all the old bogey-tales. But there's a passage near the end that isn't symbolic at all. It's specific. Dates. Names." He hesitated. "Mara, it names tonight."

Something cold walked down my spine wearing my mother's footsteps.

"Read it," I said.

He read it. And the lamp guttered, though there was no wind, and on the page, in ink two hundred years dry, the letters began — very slowly, the way a sleeper turns over — to move.

* * *

They moved the way ants move when you disturb their hill: purposeful, many, wrong. The words slid apart and regathered, and where they settled, the sentence was different. Shorter. Closer. As though the prophecy had been waiting two centuries to stop speaking in general terms.

Wendell's hand found mine across the workbench. Neither of us pulled away.

"*The Sigil comes to the charm-seller's city,*" he read aloud, unsteadily, "*and the stone remembers what was sundered, and the debt unpaid comes due beneath the mountain.*" He looked up. His face had gone the color of good paper. "Mara. What's a Sigil?"

I couldn't answer. Because the ink was moving again, faster now, gathering at the bottom of the page, and it was forming a word — a name — in a hand I knew better than my own, in letters that glowed faintly like banked coals:

*MARA LOCKRIDGE.*

The last thing I saw before the world folded up like a page turning was Wendell reaching for me, mouth shaping my name, and the shop, the lamp, the safe grey city of Ashmoor dissolving into white.

* * *

We fell out of nowhere onto frozen mud.

Cold hit me like a slap — real cold, deep-winter cold, though it had been mild autumn in Ashmoor not a heartbeat ago. We were standing at a crossroads under a sky with no stars in it, just a flat grey nothing overhead, and the four roads ran away from us into fog that didn't move. No wind. No sound. Even our breathing sounded borrowed.

"Wendell," I said. My voice came out flat and small. "Don't let go of my hand."

"I wasn't planning to," he whispered. "Where are we?"

"In the book." My teeth wanted to chatter; I wouldn't let them. "Or in whatever the book keeps. Hold still. Don't touch anything. Rule one of dealing with old magic is you don't touch anything."

"You have rules for this?"

"I have rules for everything. It's my best quality."

At the center of the crossroads, where no figure had been a moment before, a figure stood. Hooded, robed in a grey so featureless it hurt to look at directly, faceless within the hood — not hidden, just *absent*, the way a word goes absent when you stare at it too long. It raised one hand, or something like a hand, and pointed.

Not at me.

At Wendell.

"*Sigil,*" it said. Its voice was the sound a page makes turning, amplified into speech. "*Bearer. The stone waits. The debt stands. Bring what was sundered to what was broken, and the account closes.*"

"He's not a Sigil," I said. "He's a book appraiser. He gets ink poisoning. He apologizes to furniture."

The figure ignored me, which stung more than it should have. "*The charm-seller knows the road. The charm-seller owes the toll. When the halves seek each other, the Lockridge debt wakes.*" The hood tilted, considering. "*Tell her, little brother. Tell her what the toll bought.*"

"I don't have a brother," I said, and my voice cracked straight down the middle, because I did, I did, I had one, I'd left him behind when I ran and I'd never once gone back to find out why he never wrote—

The figure's sleeve moved, and something swung into view, hanging from what might have been fingers: a lantern. Plain iron, cheap, the kind street-sellers hawked in the Weepers' Quarter. But inside it there was no flame. Inside it there was a light that moved like a person moves in sleep, turning, restless, and against the glass, for less than a heartbeat, pressed something like a face.

The face was sixteen years old. It had our mother's jaw.

"TAM!" 

I lunged forward and Wendell's grip wrenched me back, and the crossroads *tore* — the grey sky splitting like wet paper, the fog rushing inward, the hooded figure receding without walking, raising the lantern in something like farewell or warning — and the last thing I heard, thin as a voice through water, was not words at all but a sound I'd know anywhere, anywhere, after ten years and at the end of the world:

My brother, laughing.

Then: mud. Lamplight. The smell of dust and leather. Binder's Row, the workbench, the book lying open and innocent, its ink settled and still as if it had never danced. Wendell's hand crushing mine hard enough to hurt, both of us on our knees on his shop floor, gasping like drowned things come up for air.

For a long moment neither of us spoke. Outside, a cart rattled past. Somewhere a dog barked. Ashmoor went on being ordinary, bless it, stubbornly, gloriously ordinary.

"Mara," Wendell said finally, hoarsely. "What was that? What — who was that? What's a Lockridge debt?"

I knelt on his floor with my heart slamming and my hands shaking and every rule I owned screaming at once, and I understood, with terrible clarity, that the careful life was over. Whatever came next had already started; it had started two hundred years ago, in ink, and I had just been introduced.

I looked at the book. At the margin, where beneath my name, in fresh wet letters that had not existed a moment ago, a single new line had appeared:

*TUESDAY. THE CHARM-SELLER'S CITY. IT BEGINS.*

"Well," I said, because I am, above all things, professional. "About that true thing I promised you."
