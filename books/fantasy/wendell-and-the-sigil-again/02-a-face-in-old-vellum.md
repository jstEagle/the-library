## 2. A Face in Old Vellum

There are three rules in the rare-book trade, and I learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never trust a seller who says "it's been in the family." Two: never open a book over carpet. Three — and this is the one that matters this morning — never fall in love with a book before you've checked it for teeth.

I hadn't fallen in love with the Voss Codex yet. It had arrived two days earlier in a crate that smelled of church dust and someone else's cigarettes, wrapped in a newspaper from a town I'd have to look up, and it sat on my workbench like a cat pretending not to be interested in me. Calfskin binding, rebacked badly sometime around the last century's middle, water damage along the lower edge, and inside — inside was the kind of thing that made my job worth the poverty. A hand-copied collection of prophetic verse, marginalia in three different hands, one of which had been very angry about something and had expressed it by stabbing the page with a stylus in eleven places.

The Guild would want it catalogued as heretical material and locked in their vaults, which is Guild-speak for *hidden where no one can read it*. My client wanted it appraised for auction. I wanted lunch. These three desires were about to become irreconcilable.

But first, coffee. First, the ordinary rituals of an ordinary Tuesday in Ashmoor, where magic is illegal and rare books are merely expensive, and where I, Wendell Marsh, thirty-one years of age, owner of a shop with a sign so faded it reads *APPRAISAL &C*, had a date tonight.

A date. With a woman who sold luck-charms at the night market and had laughed at exactly four of my jokes last week, which I had counted, because I am the sort of man who counts.

Her name was Mara Lockridge. She had a scar through her left eyebrow like a pen stroke someone had regretted, and she looked at me sometimes as though she were translating me out of a language she'd sworn off speaking. I found this devastating. I had told Nix — no, I hadn't told anyone, actually; I'd told my kettle, which is where I keep most of my confidences — that I intended to be charming this evening, or at minimum present.

So: coffee, then the Codex, then charm. In that order. This is called structure, and structure is what separates an appraiser from a magpie.

* * *

The trouble started on folio forty-seven.

I'd been working through the verse all morning, cross-referencing the marginalia, building the provenance file that would justify whatever absurd number I was going to write down. The prophecy itself was standard century-old doom — the usual business about a stone and a court beneath a mountain and a figure called the Sigil who would come "when the lamps learn to burn backward," which is the sort of line that sounds profound until you realize every prophet who ever lived wrote like a man describing a dream to his lawyer.

Folio forty-seven was different. Not verse. A list. Titled, in a careful hand:

*What the Sigil Shall Carry, and What He Shall Love.*

Well. That got my attention the way a dropped plate gets a cat's attention. I put down my coffee — rule two exists because of incidents like what almost happened next — and leaned in.

The list ran to nine items. Most were gibberish or worse: *a key that opens nothing twice*, *a debt paid in another man's coin*. But item six stopped my pen mid-word.

*A scar worn above the left eye, like a fault in the writing.*

I touched my own eyebrow, which is unscarred, and felt vaguely cheated.

Item seven: *a name spoken twice in error and once in truth.* Item eight was water-damaged beyond recovery, which felt pointed. And then item nine, written smaller than the rest, in ink that had gone brown with age but still held its shape with the stubbornness of a thing that meant it:

*Mara Lockridge.*

I sat back. The shop did its usual morning noises — the radiator knocking like a polite ghost, a cart rattling past on Cobbler's Lane, the clock in the corner lying about the hour. None of it changed. The world remained the world.

Except that I knew a Mara Lockridge. There is exactly one in Ashmoor, as far as I know, and I had spent last Thursday evening watching her wrap a luck-charm in blue paper while explaining, with great seriousness, that the charms were "mostly psychological" and that anyone claiming otherwise was "lying or foreign." She had a scar above her left eye. I had noticed it the way you notice a comet: once, thoroughly, and against your will.

Coincidence. Obviously coincidence. The name wasn't even that uncommon — well, all right, it was uncommon; I'd been an appraiser of documents for nine years and I had never once seen *Lockridge* outside of a land dispute from the 1840s. But names recur. Faces recur. Somewhere in this city there are probably six women with that scar and a hundred named Mara, and the odds that one of them was the woman I'd kissed goodnight on Wexford Bridge —

Kissed. Had we kissed? We had. Briefly. She had done it, actually, quick and decisive, like closing a ledger, and then said "Tuesday?" and walked away, and I had stood on the bridge feeling like a document that had just been appraised at more than expected.

I looked at the page again. Then, because I am a professional, I did the professional thing: I fetched the loupe and the raking lamp and I examined the damn entry like it was trying to sell me a forgery.

It wasn't. The ink was period. The hand matched the rest of the codex's primary scribe — same slant, same cramped descenders, same habit of crossing t's with a little flourish like a man flicking ash. Whoever wrote this list had written it a hundred years ago. Which meant either the scribe had predicted a woman named Mara Lockridge with a scar above her left eye, or —

Or the third option, the one I didn't let myself think until later, standing in front of her stall with the book under my arm like an idiot: or the universe had simply gotten bored and decided to play a joke on the one man in Ashmoor whose entire profession was telling real from fake.

Because here's the thing I haven't mentioned yet. Here's the thing I turned over maybe forty times between noon and dusk, holding it up to the light, checking the watermark, so to speak.

Item five on the list — the item directly above her name, directly above the scar — read:

*A Tuesday, in the market of Ashmoor, when the lamps are lit.*

Tonight was Tuesday. Tonight I was meeting her at the market. Under the lamps.

I closed the Codex very gently, the way you close a door when you're not sure what's behind it, and I went and made tea instead of coffee, because some situations call for the stronger ritual.

* * *

Here is what I decided, walking to the night market with a hundred-year-old heretical text tucked inside my coat like a smuggled ham: I would show her. Of course I would show her. What kind of man discovers a woman's name in a prophecy and says nothing? Not the kind of man Mara Lockridge would go on a second date with, I suspected. She had a look about her — a flinch kept carefully folded away — that suggested she had known men who said nothing, and had not thanked them for it.

Besides, half of me thought it was funny. That's the honest version. Some deep, unexamined part of me looked at a doomsday manuscript naming my date and thought, *well, this will make an excellent anecdote,* and only a smaller, quieter part whispered that anecdotes don't usually come with your own heartbeat attached.

The night market was doing what the night market does: pretending Ashmoor was a city where anything could be bought openly. Lanterns strung on wires between the stalls, frying oil and wet wool and cinnamon, a fiddler murdering a jig near the fountain. The Guild had two watchers at the mouth of the lane tonight, grey coats, bored faces, checking nobody's nothing. Everyone performed innocence in their direction. It was practically a folk dance.

Mara's stall was three rows in, wedged between a candle-seller and a man who claimed his pickles cured melancholy. She'd strung her charms on a line like laundry: knotted cords, bits of river-glass, copper wire twisted into shapes that were almost letters. A hand-lettered card read LUCK, MODESTLY PRICED. I had once asked if she'd considered HONESTLY PRICED and she'd said honesty was a different product line.

She saw me coming and did the thing she does — a smile arriving a half-second after the eyes, like she had to approve it internally first.

"You're early," she said. "Appraisers aren't supposed to be early. You'll depress the market."

"I found something today," I said. "At work."

"That's usually the point of work."

"It's a book. A codex, technically. Hand-copied prophecies, circa a hundred years ago, provenance murky, condition tragic, value — " I heard myself stalling and made myself stop. "Value irrelevant. There's a page in it."

She was wrapping a charm for a customer, blue paper, neat folds. "A page."

"A list. Things the Sigil carries. Things he loves." The customer paid and drifted off toward the pickles. I set the Codex on her counter, between the river-glass and the cash tin, and opened it to folio forty-seven. "And item nine is a name."

She glanced down. I watched her face do something complicated and fast — watched the glance snag, watched the half-second delay arrive, watched the smile get approved and then unapproved and then re-approved at gunpoint.

"Mara Lockridge," she read aloud. Her voice was perfectly level. That was the uncanny part, afterward — not the name on the page, but how still she went, like a pond deciding not to be a pond.

"And item six," I said, gentler than I'd planned, "is a scar above the left eye. Like a fault in the writing."

She didn't touch her eyebrow. I remember noticing that — everyone touches the scar when it's mentioned. She kept both hands flat on the counter, on either side of the open book, the way you'd pin something.

"Old joke," she said. "Scribes used to put real names in these things. Makes the prophecy feel urgent. Sells better at auction." She looked up at me. "You said value irrelevant, but you're an appraiser, Wendell. Everything's relevant. So what's it worth?"

"With your name in it? To the right buyer, quite a lot. Which is why I'm showing you before I catalogue it. Because — " I tapped item five, the Tuesday, the market, the lamps. "Because whoever wrote this knew about tonight. A hundred years ago. And I've spent since noon trying to find the trick in it, and there isn't one. The ink's right. The hand's right. The vellum's right. It's genuine, Mara. It's a genuine hundred-year-old book that knows what you look like."

Around us the market went on — the fiddler, the fry-smoke, a child shrieking with joy over something. The lanterns swung slightly in the evening wind off the mountain, and their light moved across the page, across her name, and I swear — I swear on my professional reputation, such as it is — the light seemed to take longer leaving the letters than arriving at them.

Mara looked at the page for a long time. Longer than a joke deserves. Longer than a coincidence deserves, too, if I'm honest, and I was beginning to be honest, in the way you're honest when the floor tilts a degree and you pretend it hasn't.

"My grandmother used to say," she said finally, "that a name written down is a door left unlocked." She said it lightly. She said everything lightly; that was the whole architecture of her. "Superstition. Hedge nonsense."

"Do you have a grandmother's worth of hedge nonsense in you?"

"None whatsoever." Too fast. Much too fast, and she knew it, and I watched her decide to let it stand anyway, the way you leave a wrong note in a tune because correcting it would admit you're listening.

"All right," I said. "Then it's a funny story. A hundred-year-old book with your name in it, and we laugh, and I buy you the dumplings you pretend not to want, and tomorrow I catalogue it and some collector pays too much for it, and that's the end."

"That's the end," she agreed.

Neither of us believed it. I could tell because she hadn't taken her hands off the counter, and because when she finally did reach out — one finger, hesitating over the brown ink of her own name like a woman testing whether a stove is hot — the lantern-light followed her fingertip down to the page.

And stayed there.

The light pooled in the letters. Just slightly. Just enough that I noticed, and just enough that she noticed me noticing, and the whole market — the fiddler, the fry-smoke, the two grey-coated Guild watchers at the mouth of the lane — seemed suddenly very far away, as though the page had quietly become the nearest thing in the world.

"Wendell," she said, and her voice had lost all its lightness at once, like a coat slipping off a hook. "Don't touch the ink."

I had already touched the ink. That was the problem. That had been the problem since noon, since I'd traced item nine with my finger like any appraiser confirming a reading, and I understood now — with the calm, detached clarity of a man watching a forgery reveal itself — that some things are not entries in a book.

Some things are invitations.

The letters began to move.
