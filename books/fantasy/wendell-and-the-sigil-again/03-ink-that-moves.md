## 3. Ink That Moves

The book sat on my counter like a confession waiting to be heard.

Wendell had brought it back to the night market three days after our date — which, for the record, had gone well enough that I'd allowed myself the dangerous luxury of hoping he'd call, and poorly enough that I'd spent those three days convincing myself he wouldn't. He'd kissed me on the cheek at the corner of Tallow Street, said "I'll see you," in a voice that cracked slightly on *see*, and walked away with his coat collar up against the drizzle looking like a man who'd just been handed something fragile and was terrified of dropping it.

I'd liked him enormously. That was the problem.

Now here he was, standing in my stall among the luck-charms and the fever-knots and the little braided cords I sold to sailors' wives, holding a leather-bound volume wrapped in oilcloth like it might bite him. Which, as it turned out, was not far wrong.

"You said you liked old things," he said.

"I said I liked *old bread*," I said. "Old things are different. Old things have opinions."

He laughed, and I felt that laugh somewhere under my ribs where I keep the parts of myself I don't show customers, and I thought: *careful, Mara.* But I lifted the oilcloth anyway, because curiosity has always been the loose thread on my sweater, the one I can't stop picking even when I know the whole thing will unravel.

The book was vellum-bound, the leather gone soft and dark as old blood. No title. The clasp was silver, tarnished green, worked into the shape of two hands clasping — or maybe strangling each other; antique metalwork is ambiguous that way.

"Where did this come from?" I asked.

"An estate in the Hill Quarter. The Vessing collection." He said the name like I should know it, and I nodded like I did, which is one of the oldest tricks in the charm-seller's book: never admit what you don't know, charge extra for what you do. "It's been in a sealed chest since before the Binding. The appraiser's guild wants me to catalog it. And Mara—" he leaned in, and lowered his voice, and there was something in his face that wasn't the awkward earnest bookseller anymore, something almost frightened, "—there's a page in it. A drawing. It's you."

"It's not me."

"It's *you*. Same face, same scar—" he touched his own eyebrow, mirroring the thin white line I've carried since I was nine, "—and the caption underneath. Your name. *Mara Lockridge.* Spelled exactly."

"Lockridge is a common name in the river towns."

"The drawing is dated. Next Tuesday."

I put the book down. I wiped my hands on my apron, which is what I do instead of screaming. Around us the night market went on jangling and smoking and haggling — a fishmonger singing out the last of her eels, a child crying over a dropped sugar-bird, the lamplighters making their rounds with their long brass poles — all of it ordinary, all of it blessedly, stubbornly real.

"Wendell," I said carefully, "in Ashmoor, a book with moving pictures gets people burned. You know that. You work with old texts. You must know that better than anyone."

"I know," he said. "Believe me, I know. I didn't bring it here to show customers. I brought it because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because when I found the page, I dreamed about you. Both nights since. Not — not anything strange, just you, standing at a crossroads, and it was snowing, and you were calling my name. And I don't believe in dreams. I believe in paper and provenance and marginalia. So I need you to tell me I'm being ridiculous."

And here is the thing about walls: they only work if you stay behind them. I had ten years of walls. I had a rule — *never touch real magic, never let real magic touch you* — worn smooth as a prayer bead from handling. I should have told Wendell to take his haunted book back to his shop, thanked him kindly for the eels we'd shared on our date, and closed the stall early.

Instead I looked at the book. And the book, I swear on every saint the Guild hasn't outlawed yet, looked back.

"Open it," I said.

* * *

The pages were thick as hide and soft as skin. The text was in no alphabet I knew — loops and barbs, like thorns trying to spell something — but the margins were annotated in a later hand, cramped brown ink, and it was the margins that made my stomach drop, though I couldn't have told you why yet. Something about them itched at a memory I'd buried so deep I'd forgotten the shovel.

"There." Wendell turned to the page near the middle. "That's the drawing."

It was me. There was no arguing with it, no comforting ambiguity of likeness. Me, younger by some years or older, hard to say, standing at a crossing of four roads under a sky like scratched tin. Snow falling upward. Behind me, a shape — tall, hooded, its face an absence, a smudge where the artist had given up or been forbidden. My hand in the drawing was raised, palm out, and on the palm was a mark like a burning wheel.

"That's not my hand," I said. My voice came out flat. "I mean — it is my hand, but I've never — there's no mark. I'm not marked. I make sure I'm not marked."

"Mara." Wendell's voice was very quiet. "Read the caption."

*I had read it already.* That was the horror of it. The letters had resolved into words the moment I looked at them properly, words in plain Ashmoor trade-tongue, and they read:

*At the crossroads of the ending year, the Sigil comes, and the Lockridge girl opens the door.*

"I didn't write that," I said stupidly. "I mean — someone wrote that. A hundred years ago. About me."

"Or about a hundred-years-ago Lockridge girl," Wendell offered, with the desperate reasonableness of a man bailing a boat with a teacup. "Names repeat. Faces repeat. There's a whole scholarly literature on—"

The ink moved.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last comfort I had in what followed. It was not a flicker. It was not a trick of candlelight. The black lines of the drawing — the roads, the snow, the hooded figure, my own drawn face — *crawled*. They moved the way ink moves when you spill water on it, except there was no water, and they were moving *against* the grain of the page, gathering, pooling, running toward the edges of the drawing like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"Wendell," I said. "Close the book."

His hands were already moving. Good man. Fast man. His fingers got to the cover and the cover did not close, because the pages would not bend, because the pages were no longer behaving like pages. The drawing was deepening. The crossroads was acquiring distance — actual perspective, actual depth, snow falling upward into a sky that receded like a throat swallowing.

I did the sensible thing. I am, whatever else you may come to believe about me, a fundamentally sensible woman. I grabbed the book to throw it into the brazier.

My hands went through it.

Not through the book — through the *page*. My fingers sank into cold, and then my wrists, and then the cold had me to the elbows and was pulling, patient and enormous, like a tide that had all the time in the world, and Wendell shouted my name and caught my arms, and then the market — the eels, the lamps, the crying child, the whole warm jangling ordinary world — tilted sideways and poured away like water off a table, and we fell.

* * *

We landed in snow.

Falling into a book, for the record, feels nothing like falling. It feels like being *read*. Like every part of you is being taken in, considered, pronounced aloud by something with a vast dry voice. Then the snow broke my fall, which was considerate of it, and I lay there gasping while flakes drifted down past my face — and then drifted back up, unhurried, returning to the tin-colored sky.

"Upward," Wendell said from somewhere beside me. His voice had a fine tremor in it, like a struck glass. "The snow is going up."

"I noticed."

"Is that — is that normal? For books?"

"Wendell, if you say one more word about normalcy I'm going to hit you with this extremely solid piece of ground."

But I took his hand when he offered it, and I noticed I took it without thinking, and I filed that away under *things to panic about later.*

We stood at the crossing of four roads. Of course we did. The roads ran out flat and white to four horizons, and there was nothing else anywhere — no trees, no buildings, no wind, no sound except our breathing and the faint, impossible whisper of pages turning somewhere overhead. The sky was the color of old vellum. When I looked straight up I could almost make out shapes in it — columns of text, blurred, like writing seen through frosted glass.

"We're inside it," Wendell whispered. "Mara. We're inside the book."

"Yes."

"This is impossible."

"Yes."

"I cataloged a Psalter once that was rumored to be haunted, and it turned out the haunting was damp in the binding, and I have been *smug* about that for six years—"

"Wendell."

"—and now I am standing in a manuscript and the manuscript is standing in me, I can feel it, it's like being *marginalia*—"

"*Wendell.*" I squeezed his hand. His knuckles were ice. So were mine. "Breathe. Look around. Find the edges. Every constructed space has edges — doors, seams, the place where the maker got lazy. We find one, we go back through. Probably. Possibly. I'm inventing this as I go, but inventing loudly is half of hedge-craft."

He breathed. He looked. And that was when we both saw the figure.

It stood at the center of the crossroads, where the four roads met, and it had not been there a moment before — I'd have staked my stall on it. Tall. Hooded. Its face was exactly what the drawing promised: an absence, a smudge, a place where the world had declined to commit to details. Its robe was ink-black and moved in a way cloth does not move, flowing and reforming, letters surfacing in it and drowning again.

It raised one hand — long-fingered, precise, a calligrapher's hand — and pointed.

Not at me.

At Wendell.

"*Sigil,*" it said. The word came out like a seal pressed into wax: final, official, done. The snow shivered. Somewhere above us, the great blurred text of the sky seemed to lean closer. "*Bearer of the mark. Keeper of the stone. You are expected. You are written. Come and be completed.*"

"I think you have the wrong person," Wendell said. And gods help me, even knee-deep in a haunted prophecy with his lips going blue, he said it *politely*, with a little apologetic gesture, like a man declining a flyer. "I'm a book appraiser. I have allergies. I'm nobody's sigil."

The hood tilted. Considering. The way a lender considers a debtor.

"*The stone,*" it said. "*Where is the stone?*"

"There's no stone," I said, stepping forward, putting myself between them, because apparently my survival instincts had taken the evening off. "There's no mark, there's no stone, there's just a book and a bad Tuesday, and whoever wrote you clearly needed stronger tea. Now open the door and let us out."

The smudge where its face should be turned to me. And I felt it *read* me — the same feeling as the falling, that vast dry voice taking inventory — and it went still in a new way. A hungrier way.

"*Ah,*" it said softly. "*The Lockridge girl. The door-opener. You are ahead of your page.*"

"What page? What does that mean? Who wrote you?"

"*One who bargained,*" it said, and something in the dry vastness of its voice cracked, just slightly, like ice over deep water. "*One who paid. One who is still paying. Bring the stone, Sigil, and all debts come due. All accounts settle. Even hers.*"

"Whose?" I demanded. "Whose debts? Whose account? *Answer me!*"

The figure raised both hands now, and the snow stopped — hung suspended, thousands of white flecks frozen mid-air like stars — and the four roads began to darken from their ends inward, shadow rolling up them like spilled ink, and I understood with animal clarity that we were about to run out of page.

"Wendell!" I grabbed his arm. "The way we came in — it pulled us through the drawing — think! What pulls things *out*?"

"I don't — Mara, I don't *know*—"

"You're the Sigil! Apparently! It called you something — bearer, keeper — so *keep* something, *bear* something, do it *now*!"

And he — sweet, allergic, impossible Wendell — closed his eyes and held out his hand the way the drawn version of me had held out hers, palm open, and said, in the firm voice of a man correcting a mislabeled shipment: "*No. This entry is in error. Return to sender.*"

The world convulsed.

The suspended snow shattered like a dropped chandelier. The hooded figure lunged — actually lunged, robes streaming into ribbons of running script — and its reaching hand brushed Wendell's shoulder, and where it touched, his coat went gray and blank, colorless as unprinted paper, and he screamed, and I screamed, and I did the last thing left, the reckless thing, the thing my mentor Sef would have flayed me for:

I reached for magic. Real magic. Ten years untouched, ten years locked and barred, and I tore the doors off and seized it with both hands and *poured* it into the ground beneath us, into the snow, into the page itself, shouting words I hadn't spoken since I was nineteen years old and running away —

*Unwrite us. Unwrite us. UNWRITE US.*

* * *

We came out on the floor of my stall, tangled together, knocking over a display of fortune-knots, scattering charms across the boards. The book lay shut beside us, innocuous, its clasp fastened — though I will swear until my dying day that I never saw it close.

For a long moment neither of us moved. The night market roared on outside, oblivious. An eel seller sang. Somewhere a fiddle struck up. The world, bless it, insisting on itself.

"My coat," Wendell said finally, in a small voice. He touched his shoulder. Where the figure had grazed him, the wool was gray — not faded, *blank*, a patch of nothing-color, and when he pinched it between his fingers it made no sound at all. "Mara. My shoulder doesn't hurt. That's worse, isn't it? That it doesn't hurt?"

"Probably," I said. My own hands were shaking so badly I hid them in my apron. Underneath the shaking, deeper in, something enormous and half-asleep was rolling over, opening one eye, tasting the air. My magic. Awake for the first time in ten years, and *hungry*, and I hated how good it felt. "Wendell. Listen to me. We're going to burn that book. Tonight. In salt and rowan ash, proper procedure, none of your archival nonsense—"

"Mara." He was sitting up. He was looking past me, at the counter, and his face had gone the color of the blank patch on his coat.

I turned.

The book lay closed. But across its cover, in fresh wet ink that gleamed like it had been written seconds ago, in a hand I recognized because it matched the margins, new words were drying:

*Three days, Sigil. Bring the stone.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost gentle, which was somehow infinitely worse:

*Ask the Lockridge girl what she buried.*

I looked at that line for a long time. Tam's face rose in my memory — fifteen years old, gap-toothed, laughing at me from the riverbank the summer before everything went wrong — and I pushed it back down where it lived, in the dark, under the shovel.

"Nothing," I said. "I didn't bury anything. It's lying. Books lie."

Wendell looked at me with those earnest, terrible, too-perceptive eyes, and said nothing at all.

Outside, the lamplighters finished their rounds. Three days. I counted charms I didn't see, and made plans I didn't want, and tried very hard not to wonder what, exactly, I'd paid — or someone had paid for me — to keep my life so small and safe for all these years.

Safe things, my mentor used to say, are just debts that haven't been called in yet.

The ink was already drying.
