## 4. What the Guild Knows

There are three things every rare-book appraiser in Ashmoor knows by heart: the smell of foxed vellum, the weight of a coin that's been bitten too many times, and the exact pitch of silence that means someone is standing behind you who shouldn't be.

I learned the third one all over again on Thursday morning, three days after a book tried to eat us.

The shop was quiet in the way I like it — dust motes doing their slow ballet in the window light, the kettle muttering on the back stove, and the Aldous Vane folio lying open on my workbench where I'd sworn I would not look at it again and had looked at it eleven times since dawn. The ink had stopped moving. That was somehow worse. It sat there on the page, smug and still, like a cat that has knocked something off a shelf and now pretends to be asleep.

*And the Sigil shall come bearing nothing, and the Lockridge shall open what was shut.*

Mara's name. In a book bound before her grandmother was born. Along with a description of the scar on her left thumb she'd gotten falling out of a plum tree at nine years old, which she told me about over dumplings while I laughed and then stopped laughing, because I'd read it an hour earlier in ink that had been dry for a century.

I was staring at it for the twelfth time, trying to convince myself that *Lockridge* was a common name — it isn't — when the silence behind me got its pitch exactly right.

"Wendell Marsh," said a voice like a drawer of well-kept knives. "Still appraising other people's treasures. Still not having any of your own."

I turned. Corvane stood in my doorway the way weather stands in a doorway — as if the doorway were a formality he was permitting. He's old the way mountains are old, which is to say you can't imagine him ever having been young, and he wears his Guild grey the way other men wear medals. We go back twenty years, to when he caught me, aged nineteen, selling a forged Vane letter to a collector with more money than sense, and instead of turning me in made me work it off cataloguing confiscated grimoires in the Guildhall basement. Six months among books that whispered. It taught me two things: how to spot a forgery at forty paces, and that the Guild does not confiscate anything. It *keeps* things.

"Corvane." I wiped my hands on my apron, a gesture that fooled neither of us. "You're a long way from the Hall."

"I'm never far from anything that matters." He came into the shop without being asked, which was also very Corvane, and paused at the shelf of travel narratives with the expression of a man checking whether a room has been cleaned properly. "You've had visitors."

"I have customers. Occasionally."

"Three nights ago." He turned. His eyes are pale grey, the color of paper left too long in sun, and they do not blink so much as *file reports*. "Something happened here. Or near here. Something woke up."

My mouth said, "Nothing wakes up in Ashmoor. Everything's asleep. It's civic policy." My stomach said, *oh no,* in the specific register of a man whose date pulled him into a prophetic text and who has spent three days pretending it was a shared hallucination brought on by undercooked lamb.

Corvane smiled. It did not improve his face. "Civic policy," he repeated, tasting it. "Yes. And yet my instruments sang Tuesday night like a choir of strangled birds. Every ward in this district lit at once and then went dark, and in the middle of it all, do you know what my scryers found? A cold spot. A hole in the world shaped exactly like a crossroads." He drifted toward my workbench, and I moved — casually, I hoped — to stand between him and the folio. "And do you know what else? A resonance. Old script. Prophetic register. The kind of thing we haven't logged since—" he stopped, and looked at me with terrible mildness, "—since the last time, Wendell. Which you would know about, if you'd stayed in our employ. If you'd stayed *ours*."

"I was never yours. I was six months of community service."

"You were an investment." He picked up a magnifying loupe from my bench and turned it over, examining it rather than using it, which I understood to be a threat delivered in the grammar of antiques. "We invested in your eye, your discretion, your useful little shop full of other people's secrets. And investments, Wendell, are expected to pay dividends."

The kettle began to whistle. Neither of us moved toward it. It whistled itself out, embarrassed.

"I don't know what you think happened," I said.

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But you know what I *hope* happened. And hope, in my experience, loosens tongues faster than fear. Fear makes people lie well. Hope makes them lie badly, because they're already halfway to believing the lie will come true." He set the loupe down, precisely aligned with the bench edge. "So let me tell you a story, and you tell me if it sounds familiar."

He didn't wait for permission. Corvane has never once waited for permission; waiting implies the possibility of refusal, and refusal is not a thing he keeps in inventory.

"A hundred and six years ago," he said, "there was a woman called the Cartographer, who wrote a prophecy in a hand so fine it could only be read by candlelight. She foretold the coming of one she named the Sigil — a soul marked to carry or break a stone of great consequence. The Guild hunted her for it. Magic was being outlawed, you understand; the city was deciding what it wanted to be, and it decided against wonder. The Cartographer was caught. Her book was seized. Her *self* was—" he paused, choosing, "—processed. But the book survived, because books always survive, they're the cockroaches of civilization, and it passed through forty pairs of hands until last month, when a dealer in Lowbridge sold it to a young appraiser with a good eye and a bad habit of curiosity."

My throat had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the unattended kettle. "That's a lovely story. Very atmospheric. Do you want tea?"

"I want the girl."

The words landed in the shop and just — stayed there. Dust motes swerved around them.

"There's no girl," I said, which was true for approximately four seconds of any given day lately, and false in every way that mattered.

"Mara Lockridge." He said the name gently, the way you'd say the name of a child lost at sea. "Hedge-trained. Unlicensed. Former apprentice of Sef of the Miremarsh, who is himself a matter of ongoing Guild interest. She sells charms in the night market under a false vendor's license — luck-knots, fever-salves, little knotted threads that work *just* well enough to keep customers coming back but never well enough to trip a ward. Clever. Disciplined. Ten years careful." He leaned against my workbench, next to the folio, and I felt the hair on my arms rise. "And three nights ago, Wendell, ten years of careful cracked wide open, because somebody took her to see a book with her own name in it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, and a vendor cried something about eels, and the ordinary city went on being ordinary, and I hated it a little for that.

"If she exists," I said carefully, "and I'm not saying she does—"

"You took her to dinner Tuesday night. The Lamb and Lantern. You ordered the smoked duck. She told you about a plum tree." His voice stayed soft, which was the most frightening thing in the room, softer even than the folio's patient ink. "Wendell. I have known you since you were a forger with ink-stained cuffs. I am offering you the truth, which is worth more than tea: the Guild knows. We have known since Wednesday morning. The only reason she is not already in a cell beneath the Guildhall is that Senior Warden Ostrava wants to know *what activated*, and cells make people stupid. So we watch. We wait. And meanwhile, I am standing in your shop, offering you a choice, because unlike Ostrava I remember that you are a person and not merely an asset."

"And what choice is that?"

Corvane straightened, and for a moment — one strange, flickering moment — the knife-drawer voice warmed, and he looked tired, and old, and almost kind. It was the most dangerous he ever looked.

"The Guild is not what it was," he said. "Ostrava wants to burn everything that glows. But there are those of us who remember why the Guild was founded — not to hunt magic, Wendell. To *manage* it. To keep the world from tearing along its seams. A hundred years ago we put the Cartographer down because her prophecy threatened to do exactly that. Now it's awake anyway, and there will be a reckoning, and the only question is who stands where when it comes." He laid one hand flat on the folio's cover — I flinched; he noticed; he pretended not to — "You found the book. You touched the activation. Whatever the Sigil is, the prophecy has already brushed against you. That makes you either a casualty or a colleague. I would very much prefer colleague."

"And Mara?"

"Mara Lockridge is a hedge-witch who broke the law the day she was born and has been breaking it daily since. She is also, per the prophecy's own text, *the Lockridge who opens what was shut* — which makes her either the key or the fuse, and we do not leave fuses lying around in night markets." He met my eyes. "Surrender her. Bring her in willingly, testify to what happened Tuesday night, and I give you my word — my actual word, Wendell, which I have given perhaps four times in my life and kept every one — that she sees a hearing, not a pyre. That she is processed gently. And that you walk away clean, employed, protected. The Guild shields its colleagues. It has long arms and deep pockets and a memory for its friends."

"And if I don't?"

The warmth folded away like a fan. "Then you're a civilian adjacent to an unregistered magical event, harboring a fugitive, in possession of restricted text. Then Ostrava handles it, and Ostrava does not offer hearings." He buttoned his coat, unhurried. "You have until the Binding Festival. Nine days. The whole city will be celebrating chains and locks and lawful binding, and it would be poetic, wouldn't it, if that were the night the loose ends got tied." He moved to the door, then paused, half-turned, and added — quietly, almost tenderly:

"For what it's worth, I believe you care for her. That's not a weakness. It's simply the lever I'd have used on anyone. Think of surrendering her as the last charm she'll ever need: the one that keeps her alive." 

The door closed behind him with the soft click of a trap that doesn't need to spring because you've already walked into it.

* * *

I sat in the shop for a long time after he left. The kettle had gone cold. The dust resumed its ballet. At some point I realized I was holding the folio without remembering picking it up, and that my hands were shaking, and that the shaking wasn't entirely fear.

Here is the humiliating truth about me: I wanted to say yes.

Not to betraying Mara — God, no, the thought of her in a Guild cell made something in my chest close like a fist. But to the *rest* of it. Colleague. Protected. Long arms, deep pockets. Twenty years I've lived carefully in this city, appraising other people's treasures, keeping my head down, keeping my hands clean-ish, telling myself that caution was wisdom. And here was the Guild — the gray machine I'd dodged my whole adult life — offering to fold me into its warm gray coat, and some small cowardly chamber of my heart whispered, *wouldn't it be nice, Wendell, to stop being afraid?*

That's how they get you. Not with threats. With coats.

Because underneath the offer I'd heard the other thing, the thing Corvane hadn't quite managed to sand smooth: *processed gently.* A hearing, not a pyre. As though Mara were a document with a tear in it, a folio needing restoration. As though a person could be *handled*. And beneath even that, older and colder: the Cartographer, *processed*, a hundred years ago, and her book surviving her, and her prophecy sitting in my shop like a lit fuse wearing a dust jacket.

They didn't want to protect the world from the prophecy. They wanted to own it. And they wanted Wendell Marsh, appraiser, to hand them the key and call it kindness.

I looked down at the open page. The ink lay still. *The Sigil shall come bearing nothing.*

"Bearing nothing," I said aloud, to the empty shop, and my voice sounded steadier than I felt. "Well. That part's accurate. I own nothing worth taking except my word, and apparently I've just been offered a trade for it."

Nine days. The Binding Festival. Chains and locks and lawful binding, celebrated in bunting and fried food while the Guild tied its loose ends.

I closed the folio, wrapped it in oilcloth, and hid it inside the hollowed-out dictionary of Lowbridge dialects where I keep the things I'm ashamed of owning. Then I fetched my coat, because there was one thing Corvane had gotten exactly right, and it was this: whatever was coming, it would come for Mara first, and she didn't even know she was being watched.

She was going to be furious with me for warning her.

She was going to be more furious if I didn't.

I locked the shop, pocketed the key, and set off through Ashmoor's crooked streets toward the night market, rehearsing sentences — *don't panic, but* — and discarding every one of them, because there is no good way to tell a woman who has spent ten years building walls that the people who knock down walls have already measured her house.

Behind me, in the darkened window of my own shop, I thought I saw the lamplight bend — just slightly — as if the glass were reading over my shoulder.

I didn't go back to check.

Some appraisals, I've learned, you're better off not completing.
