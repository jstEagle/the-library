## 5. The Brother She Buried

The book sat on my kitchen table for three days before I could bring myself to open it again.

That's not entirely true. I opened it twice. Once to make sure the ink had stopped moving — it hadn't — and once to throw a dish towel over it, which is what you do when a book is looking at you and you'd rather not be looked at back. The towel did nothing. Of course it did nothing. It was a dish towel.

Wendell had left it with me because he was afraid to take it home. He said this in the tone of a man trying to make cowardice sound like logistics: "You have wards, Mara. You have *experience*. I have a flat above a pie shop and a landlady who complains about my kettle." He said it like a joke, and then he held onto my hand a beat too long when he handed the thing over, and neither of us laughed.

So there it sat, between the salt cellar and the teapot, breathing its slow vellum breath, while I went about the business of being a person who sold luck-charms for six coppers and pretended her life was small on purpose.

Here is what nobody tells you about prophecy: it's mostly bookkeeping.

I know people imagine thunder and ravens and a voice out of a burning bush. In my experience — and I have some, though less than I let certain customers believe — real magic is clerical. Contracts. Margins. Fine print written by someone who wanted very badly for you not to read it. The crossroads figure with its hooded face and its voice like a coin dropped down a well had called Wendell *the Sigil* and demanded *the stone*, and then we had run home through Ashmoor's rain-slick streets like children fleeing a wasp, and once the terror drained out of me, what remained was the part of me that Sef had trained before I ran: the part that reads the fine print.

Because the hooded one hadn't appeared from nowhere. It had come *out of the book*. And books, unlike gods, can be interrogated at leisure.

* * *

On the fourth night I lit every candle I owned, salted the doorways, drew the circle I hadn't drawn in ten years — chalk, not blood; Sef had been poor, not dramatic — and pulled the towel off the book like a bandage off a wound.

*The Sigil and the Sundering*, the spine read, or had read. The gold leaf had rearranged itself since Wendell first catalogued it. Now it said something else, in a script that hurt slightly to look at directly, the way a bright window hurts at dusk. I decided not to translate it. Some things you're better off not knowing until you've eaten.

The text itself was the same as Wendell described: a century-old prophetic fragment, the kind of thing that turns up in estate sales and gets appraised as "curio, decorative." But Wendell had missed the margins. Everyone misses the margins. Margins are where the frightened scribes live — the ones copying the official text by day and scratching their own doubts into the edges by candlelight, hoping no one will look closely enough to burn them for it.

I looked closely. Looking closely is the only spell I never stopped practicing.

The marginalia was in three hands. The first was dry and neat, a scholar noting sources. The second was jagged, urgent, half the letters collapsed into each other — someone writing fast, or shaking. Between them, in a third hand so faint I had to tilt the page into the candlelight to catch it, were the annotations that mattered.

The second hand had written, beside the passage naming the Sigil:

*It wants a vessel. It always wants a vessel. The stone chooses, but the stone must be carried.*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed so hard the nib had torn the vellum:

*Payment precedes the claiming. A soul for a soul. The bargain stands whether the bearer knows or not.*

I read that last line four times. Then I put the book down, walked to the window, opened it, and breathed cold air for a while, because the alternative was being sick into my own circle, which Sef had always said was bad form.

*A soul for a soul.* Payment for what? For whom? The claiming hadn't happened yet — hadn't happened until Wendell touched the book and the ink crawled and the crossroads opened like a mouth. Unless.

Unless the payment had been made ten years ago, by someone else, for a different claiming. A claiming that never came due, because the girl it was meant for packed a bag in the dark and ran south to Ashmoor and built herself a life made of chalk dust and six-copper charms.

"No," I said out loud, to the book, to the room, to whoever was listening. Books like that appreciate being spoken to. It makes them feel included.

The third hand — the faint one — answered.

Not in words, exactly. Not at first. As I bent over the margins again, the faint script began to shift, letters sliding into new arrangements like beads on a string, and I understood two things at once: that this hand was still alive somewhere, still writing, across a century and whatever distance lies between a page and its author; and that it was writing *to me*.

*Mara,* it wrote.

My name. My actual name, in a book bound before my grandmother was born.

I should have closed it. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept: I knew I should close it, and I want credit for even considering it, because what I actually did was lean closer.

*You were promised,* the margin said. *Did he never tell you?*

"Promised," I repeated. My voice came out flat, which is what my voice does instead of shaking. "Promised to whom?"

The letters crawled. Rearranged. Settled.

*To the court beneath. Your apprenticeship was the contract. Your hand the seal. He paid your fee in the only coin they accept.*

I sat down. My knees made the decision for me; the rest of me just followed them to the floor, taking the chair's edge with me, and stayed there with my chin nearly touching the table, reading the same words over and over while the candles guttered and the salt in my doorways did absolutely nothing, because you cannot ward against a sentence.

*He paid your fee,* the margin had said. *In the only coin they accept.*

Sef. Old Sef, with his cracked knuckles and his tea gone bitter from steeping too long because he forgot it arguing with his herbs. Sef, who taught me to read the fine print and never once showed me his own. Ten years I'd carried the guilt of leaving him like a stone in my boot — *ungrateful girl, ungrateful, ungrateful* — and all that time he'd been carrying something heavier, and he'd let me believe the weight was mine.

What coin? I asked the page. What did he pay?

The answer took longer to come, as if the writer on the other end were choosing words carefully, or gathering courage, or both. When it came, it came in letters that trembled.

*A brother. The boy Tam. His soul, sold to the Undercourt as surety — that when the Sigil rose, you would come. That the debt would draw you home.*

The room went very quiet. Candles do that sometimes, when the air changes. Or when something in the air changes.

Tam.

Say a name you haven't said in ten years and see what happens inside your chest. It doesn't ache the way you'd expect. It's more like a door you'd bricked over opening onto a room where everything is exactly as you left it — the chipped mug, the whittled boats, the boy himself, fourteen years old and sunburned and laughing at his own jokes before he got to the punchline, because he never could wait.

Tam, who walked into the marsh fog one evening to check the eel traps and didn't walk out. They found his boots. Just his boots, set neatly side by side on the bank, toes pointing toward the water, like he'd stepped out of them and into another world. The village said drowned. The village needed him drowned. Drowned was a grief you could bury.

Sef had stood at the funeral with his hat in his hands and his face like folded paper, and I had thought: *he blames himself for letting the boy go alone.* I had thought it was grief. It was arithmetic. He was standing over the grave doing sums — one soul paid, one soul protected, the contract sealed, the girl saved — and I had stood next to him holding flowers and hating him a little for surviving us both.

"He told me Tam was dead," I said to the margin. "He buried an empty box and let me mourn—"

*He let you grieve cleanly,* the hand wrote, and there was something in the slant of those letters, something almost gentle. *Grief with hope in it is a fire that never goes out. He chose to smother it.*

"And instead he banked it for ten years and fed it to the Undercourt."

The margin didn't argue. Margins rarely do; they just wait.

I got up. I paced. I drank tea I didn't taste. I stood at the window watching Ashmoor's lamps come on one by one, all those little flames behind glass, and thought about souls kept as lanterns — because of course I knew the stories, everyone raised near the mountain knows the stories, the Undercourt and its bound lights, the pale procession seen through smugglers' grates, the singing that isn't singing. Fairy tales. Warnings to keep children away from cellar doors.

A soul for a soul. The bargain stands whether the bearer knows or not.

Whether the *bearer* knows. Not whether the payer knows. Sef had known every stroke of what he signed. I was the bearer. I was the payment drawn on, ten years deferred, accruing interest in the dark beneath the mountain — and the interest was Tam.

* * *

Wendell arrived at midnight, because of course he did. He'd spent three days pretending to be fine — I'd watched him do it, the careful cheerfulness, the way he catalogued his fear into neat little subjects like he catalogued everything else — and now, apparently, the pretense had expired and he'd come round to sit in my kitchen and be afraid in company.

He knocked the way he does everything, earnestly and slightly too hard, and when I opened the door he was holding a paper bag of honey cakes like a peace offering to a house that hadn't asked for one.

"I couldn't sleep," he said. "Every time I closed my eyes I saw the hood. So I thought — cakes. Cake is a legitimate response to existential dread. People have written poems about it."

"Come in," I said. "We need to talk."

"That's never good."

"It's worse than you think."

He sat. I put the book on the table between us, and he flinched from it the way you flinch from a dog that's bitten you, and then he leaned in anyway, because underneath all the awkwardness Wendell has a spine of something surprisingly stubborn.

"The margins talk," I said. "There's a hand in the margins that's been writing to me. Don't ask how. I don't know how, and I've stopped requiring the universe to explain itself this week."

"Reasonable."

"It says the prophecy was triggered ten years ago. Before tonight. There was a bargain — my apprenticeship, my training, all of it was the contract. Sef made it. And the price—" My voice did the flat thing again. I let it. Flat was load-bearing right now. "The price was my brother. Tam. His soul, sold to the Undercourt as surety that I'd come when the Sigil rose."

Wendell was quiet for a moment. He's better at quiet than I am; I fill silences the way cracks fill with frost.

"Mara," he said gently, "your brother died. You told me. When you were nineteen. You said it was why you—"

"They found his boots." I heard my own voice from far away, describing it like someone else's story. "Neatly on the riverbank. Toes toward the water. You don't leave your boots neatly if you slip. You leave them neatly if you're stepping into something else. I was nineteen and I wanted it to be drowning, because drowning is over. Drowning has a grave."

"And now?"

"Now I think a door opened in the marsh, and something on the other side of it gave Sef a receipt."

I turned the book toward him, to the page where the faint hand had finished its work. The final lines had settled while I waited, patient as a spider finishing a web:

*The debt comes due when the Sigil wakes. The sister's blood bought the delay; the sister's choice will end it. Bring the halves together, and the lanterns go free — or the bearer burns, and the court keeps what it was paid.*

Wendell read it twice. His lips moved on the second pass. Then he looked up at me, and I watched him arrive at the same place I'd been sitting alone for hours, and I watched him refuse to leave me there.

"Then we go get him," he said.

"Just like that."

"Just like that. Well — not just like that. We'll need a plan. And probably a map. And possibly a pickpocket; I've read that under-cities run on pickpockets." He was babbling. I let him. Babbling is what courage sounds like before it finds its footing. "But yes. We go get your brother. Whoever has him, however deep they've got him, we go."

"You understand what it says. *Bring the halves together.* That's what the prophecy wants. That's what the hooded thing wants. Walking into the Undercourt to save Tam might be walking exactly where the whole rotten design wants us."

"Probably," Wendell agreed. "But Mara — the design wants a lot of things. It wanted me to be a weapon. I'm a rare-book appraiser with hay fever. Designs can want. It doesn't mean they get." He reached across the table and put his hand over mine, on top of the terrible book, which I noticed pulsed faintly under our joined fingers like something sulking. "Your brother has been a lantern for ten years because of a bargain made to protect you. You didn't make it. But you can end it. That's not the trap. That's the way out."

I looked at his hand on mine. Ordinary hand. Ink-stained, cake-crumb-dusted, trembling very slightly, staying anyway.

Ten years ago I ran from magic because magic had taken everything — my teacher's trust, my brother, my belief that the world had rules worth following. I built a life with walls so high I'd forgotten there was sky. And now the walls had a hole in them shaped like a boy with sunburned shoulders and a laugh that started too early, and there was no charm in my stock, no ward in my kit, no rule in any book that would keep him behind them.

Guilt is a strange fuel. It burns dirty and it burns long.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we find Nix."

"Nix?"

"The pickpocket. From the market. She deals in stolen names, and if anyone knows the way down the stair that isn't there, it's her." I closed the book. It resisted, briefly, like a child being sent to bed. "And after that, we're going to need to talk about the Guildhall."

"Why the Guildhall?"

"Because the margin had one more line," I said, "and I saved it for last, because I was hoping I'd misread it."

I turned to the final page. The faint hand had left a postscript, small as a secret:

*Half the stone lies beneath the mountain. Half lies beneath the Guild. He split it so that no one hand could hold the whole — and then he buried his half under the hunters' floor, where no witch would ever dare to dig.*

Wendell stared at it. Then at me. Then, absurdly, at the honey cakes, as if they might contain guidance.

"So," he said at last, "the good news is we know where it is."

"Yes."

"And the bad news?"

"The bad news," I said, blowing out the candles one by one, "is that tomorrow we start planning a burglary of the most heavily warded building in Ashmoor — and the man who hid the stone there is the same man who sold my brother's soul, and before any of this ends, I'm going to have to knock on his door and ask him why."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Somewhere under the city, under the mountain, under ten years of careful silence, I imagined — no, I *knew*, the way you know a scar is yours — a light was burning that had my name in it.

"Hold on, Tam," I whispered. "I'm coming. I'm late, but I'm coming."
