## 6. Down the Stair That Isn't There

The smuggler's door was behind a fishmonger's stall, which felt like a joke someone had forgotten to finish.

"Behind the herring," Nix said, in the tone of a person who had said this to a hundred strangers and enjoyed it every time. "Don't touch the barrels with the blue wax. Those are somebody's. Touch the ones with the red wax."

"Whose are the blue ones?" Wendell asked, because Wendell was constitutionally incapable of not asking questions, even when the answer might get us killed.

"Doesn't matter," Nix said. "You'll be dead before you can appreciate the trivia."

I'd met pickpockets before. Ashmoor's night market bred them like damp breeds mold. But Nix was different in one important way: most thieves stole your purse and left you your name. Nix dealt in names themselves — bought them, sold them, traded them across counters I didn't want to imagine. When we'd found her two nights ago, crouched on the roof of my own shop like a cat that had decided the house was now hers, she'd introduced herself with three names, all of them hers, none of them true, and dared me to figure out which one to use.

I'd settled on Nix. She'd seemed pleased. Or possibly insulted. With her it was hard to tell, mostly because she never stopped grinning long enough for anything else to show.

Now she led us through the fish market at the dead hour before dawn, when the stalls were shuttered and the gulls hadn't yet started their arguments, and stopped at a wall that looked exactly like every other wall in Ashmoor: brick, damp, faintly embarrassed by its moss.

"There's no door there," Wendell said.

"There's no stair either," Nix agreed. "That's rather the point." She pressed her palm flat against the bricks, muttered something that sounded less like an incantation than a complaint — *come on, you great lump, open* — and the wall sighed.

That's the only word for it. The mortar loosened, the bricks slid apart like teeth deciding to cooperate, and a staircase unspooled downward into dark, narrow and steep and smelling of cold stone and older secrets.

Wendell made a small sound. I understood it completely. There is a particular kind of vertigo that comes from watching solid things stop being solid, and I had been living inside that vertigo for three days, ever since the ink of a century-old book had crawled off its page and tried to eat us.

"You first," Nix said to me.

"Why me?"

"Because if something grabs him, you'll fight. If something grabs you, he'll freeze, and then I can rob whatever grabbed you while it's busy." She shrugged. "It's a system."

"She's joking," I told Wendell.

"I'm absolutely not," Nix said.

But I went first anyway, because she was right about the fighting part, and because ten years of keeping my head down had taught me exactly one useful skill: knowing when the safest place is also the most dangerous one. Standing still in Ashmoor, after what had happened at my shop — after the Guild's lanterns had swept my street, after Corvane's men had asked their polite questions with their impolite hands — standing still was how you ended up in a Guild cell explaining yourself to people who didn't believe in explanations.

So I put my foot on a stair that hadn't existed a moment ago, and I began to descend into the mountain.

* * *

The stair went down far longer than the city above could reasonably allow. That was the second thing I noticed, after the smell. Ashmoor sits against the mountain's knee like a child leaning on a sleeping giant, and everyone knows the mountain is hollow — everyone says so, the way people say the sea is deep, meaning nothing by it. But walking down, down past where the foundations should have ended, down past where the bedrock should have turned to fire or fable, I began to understand that "hollow" had been doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence.

"How far?" Wendell asked behind me. His voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was cataloguing something valuable and didn't want to breathe on it wrong.

"Till we get there," Nix said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give for free."

Lamps appeared as we needed them — or perhaps they'd always been there, guttering in niches, burning something greenish and slow that cast our shadows up the walls instead of down. The shadows climbed ahead of us like scouts reporting back. I kept my eyes on my feet. I have a rule about magic: don't touch it, don't thank it, don't look at it too long, because looking is the first step of touching, and touching is how you end up bound to a bargain you didn't read closely enough. I'd learned that lesson at nineteen, in a marsh hut, with my brother's name on the bottom of a contract I never saw.

Ten years of rules. And here I was, following a thief down a stair that wasn't there, toward a court that wasn't supposed to exist, over a brother who wasn't supposed to be alive.

The margins of the prophecy had told me that much, in Sef's cramped hand, in ink that had waited a hundred years for someone with a reason to read it. *Payment rendered. One soul, freely given by proxy. The boy Tam, held in surety beneath.* My brother. Sold before I ever fled the marshes, sold to buy my safety, sold by the man I'd called teacher and loved like a father and abandoned without a backward glance.

Guilt makes a very good compass. It points nowhere you want to go, but it points relentlessly.

"Mara." Wendell's hand touched my shoulder, light as a bookmark. "You've stopped walking."

"I'm composing a speech," I said. "For when we find Sef. It has several verses and they're all profane."

"Good," he said. "You should lead with the profanity. It sets expectations."

Nix glanced back at us, and for half a second the grin slipped and something else showed underneath — something quick and assessing and, oddly, almost fond. Then the grin came back like a shutter snapping shut. "If you two are done being disgustingly functional, keep moving. The stair doesn't like being counted."

"Doesn't like—" Wendell started.

"Don't count the stairs," Nix said, with sudden sharpness. "People who count the stairs arrive somewhere else. Don't ask me where. Nobody who counted has come back to describe it."

We stopped counting the stairs.

* * *

The under-city announced itself first by sound.

Above, Ashmoor at dawn is bells and gulls and cart wheels and the particular cough of a city waking up resentfully. Below, the sound was water — water everywhere, running in channels cut into the walls, dripping from ceilings that must have been a mile of stone overhead, pooling in basins where pale things moved. And under the water-sound, voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A murmur like a congregation heard through a cathedral wall.

And light. Not lamplight. Lantern-light — hundreds of lanterns strung along wires and chains between buildings that grew out of the cavern walls like crystal formations, each one glowing a soft, warm gold, each one flickering slightly out of rhythm with its neighbors, so that the whole cavern seemed to breathe.

They were beautiful. That's the thing I couldn't stop noticing, the thing that made my stomach turn even as my eyes refused to look away. They were beautiful the way the prophecy's ink had been beautiful, the way any trap is beautiful right up until it closes.

"What are they?" Wendell whispered.

Nix's grin was gone. In its place was something flat and old. "You know what they are. You saw the book. You saw the crossroads." She didn't say souls. She didn't need to. We'd both seen the hooded figure pull a thread of light out of the air and wind it around its finger like thread off a spool.

"They're people," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the one trick my mother taught me before she died, and possibly the only inheritance I got that wasn't cursed. "Those are people."

"Some of them," Nix said. "Some are animals. Some are ideas. The Undercourt isn't fussy." She jerked her chin toward a side street — a real street, cobbled, lined with crooked houses stacked four and five high, their windows glowing with ordinary tallow candles. Ordinary. After the soul-lanterns, ordinary candlelight nearly made me weep. "This is the Warrens. Smugglers, deserters, debtors, anyone the court wants and the surface wants forgotten. You pay your toll, you keep your head down, you live. Mostly."

"And the toll?" Wendell asked.

"A name," Nix said. "Yours. They hold it. While they hold it, you can't leave without permission." She caught our expressions and laughed — a short, barking sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Oh, don't look like that. I gave mine years ago. That's why I deal in other people's. Supply and demand."

"You gave them your *name*," I said. "Your actual—"

"It's not like a finger, hedge-witch. It grows back. Eventually. If you're stubborn." She said it lightly, the way people say things that cost them everything, and then she was moving again, down the middle of the street, shoulders loose, nodding to the figures in doorways like a priest greeting her congregation.

I watched her go and thought: *she's alone down here. Has been for years. And she walks like the street belongs to her because if she walked like a stranger, she'd be dead.*

Then Wendell's hand found mine, and I thought: *so am I, probably. So is he. Maybe that's what family is — just people walking the same dangerous street, pretending it belongs to them.*

I'm aware that's a low bar for family. You haven't seen my previous attempts.

* * *

Nix stashed us in a room above a tavern called the Drowned Bell, which was either a reference to something tragic or an honest description of the beer. The room had one window, one bed, one chair, and a ceiling low enough that Wendell, who was tall in the way of men who apologize for it, spent the entire first hour ducking.

"We rest till dark," Nix said. "Court's quieter at night — the lanterns burn lower when the Queen holds session, and the guards drift toward the palace like moths. That's when we move on the vault approaches. Tonight I show you the streets. Tomorrow, the door."

"The vault door," I said.

"The vault door," she confirmed, and something in her voice made me look at her properly. She was sitting on the windowsill, knees drawn up, silhouetted against the glow of the distant lanterns, and she wasn't grinning. "You understand what you're asking. You want to walk into the Undercourt's treasury and take a piece of a stone that the Queen herself wears as a crown-jewel. People have been flayed for picking her pocket of a *coin*. This isn't a job. It's a prayer with extra steps."

"Then we'd better be devout," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. "Why do you care this much? About the stone. About the brother. You barely knew him — you said. Ten years gone. You built a whole life on top of the grave." Her voice wasn't cruel. It was curious, the way a surgeon is curious. "Most people would grieve and move on. Again."

I thought about lying to her. I thought about the version of the story where I'm noble and grief-stricken and clean. But Nix had given us her street, her contacts, her knowledge of doors that weren't there, and lies are a poor payment for that.

"Because it's my fault," I said. "Not in the vague way. In the specific way. A bargain was struck to protect me, and the price was him, and I ran from the woman who struck it instead of asking what it cost. Ten years. He's been a lantern for ten years because I didn't ask one question." I heard my voice crack on the last word and hated it and let it stand. "So no, I won't grieve and move on. I'll fix it or die stupidly trying, and honestly the second option is starting to look likely."

The silence stretched. Down in the tavern, someone was singing, badly, a song about a river.

"Okay," Nix said finally. "Okay. Then here's mine." She swung her legs off the sill. "I had a sister. Surface girl. Guild took her for testing when she was nine — they do that, sweep the slums for sparks, and the ones who spark get taken and the ones who don't get billed for the trouble. I got out. She didn't. I've spent six years telling myself she's dead, because dead is better than what the Guild does with the ones they keep." Her chin lifted. "Corvane signed her intake order. I've seen the paper. So when I say I'm in on this job, understand it's not for your coin. Your coin's terrible. It's because your war and my war have the same face, and I'm tired of fighting it alone."

Wendell made a soft sound — the sound he made in his shop when he opened a crate and found a first edition under the packing straw.

"What?" Nix snapped.

"Nothing," he said. "It's just — we're a crew now. That's all. I've never been in a crew."

"You've never been in a fistfight either, I'd wager."

"I was in a debate society."

"Gods help us," Nix said, but she was grinning again, and this time it reached her eyes, and I realized I'd been watching for that all night without admitting it — watching for the moment the mask slipped and stayed slipped.

I sat down on the terrible bed beside Wendell, and Nix stayed on the sill, and none of us spoke for a while, and it was — I want to say it was comfortable, but comfortable is the wrong word. It was *safe*. For five minutes, in a stolen room in a city of stolen names, with soul-lanterns breathing gold outside the window and my brother's hollow face burned into the back of my eyelids, it was safe, and I let it be safe, and I didn't check the exits even once.

Well. Twice.

* * *

Near dusk, Nix went out to "ask some questions," which from her meant something between journalism and burglary, and came back white-faced and moving fast.

"Change of plan," she said, shouldering the door shut behind her. "No tour tonight. No gentle approach. The court knows you're here."

My blood went cold in a way I recognized from the worst moments of the last week. "How?"

"Someone talked. Someone always talks. There's a price on the hedge-witch and the Sigil — a good one. And there's more." She dropped a scrap of paper on the table between us. On it, in a hand I knew — a hand I'd spent ten years trying to forget, cramped and slanted, the handwriting of a man who wrote contracts — was a single line:

*The marsh knows what the mountain took. Come alone, or come not at all.*

"Sef," I said.

"He sent it up through three intermediaries in six hours," Nix said. "Which means he's not in the marshes anymore. Which means your dear old teacher is *here*, in the Warrens, and either he's running from the same people we are—" she paused, and her eyes did that surgeon-thing again, "—or he's bait."

Outside the window, the soul-lanterns flickered, all together, all at once, like a held breath.

Wendell reached over and closed his hand around mine. "Whatever we do," he said quietly, "we do it as a crew."

And God help me, with a liar's note on the table and a traitor's city at the window, that was the safest sentence I'd heard in ten years.
