## 7. The Court Beneath

The stair ended the way all good nightmares do: without warning, and into something beautiful.

I'd expected dark. I'd earned dark, honestly — ten years of careful, charm-sized magic, and here I was three hundred feet under a mountain with a rare-book appraiser who'd never thrown a punch and a pickpocket who'd charged us two coppers and a name for the privilege of leading us to our doom. Dark would have been fair.

Instead there was light. Light everywhere, falling from above in slow golden sheets, and it took me three full seconds to understand what I was looking at, because my mind kept trying to make it be chandeliers.

It was not chandeliers.

* * *

"Keep your eyes down," Nix murmured, not turning around. "And whatever you do, don't look at them like you pity them."

"Look at what like I—"

"Mara." Wendell's hand found mine. His palm was damp. Mine probably was too. "I think she means the lights."

The cavern opened around us like the inside of a cathedral that had been built by something with too much money and no conscience at all. Stalactites hung in glittering ranks, veined with silver ore that caught and threw the light until the whole ceiling shimmered like a night sky someone had gotten greedy with. Below us, terraced down the curve of the mountain's hollow heart, the Undercourt spread itself out in tiers of black stone and colored glass — bridges of spun glass, towers carved from living rock, gardens of pale fungi glowing blue and violet in beds along every walkway. Music drifted up from somewhere below, strings and something percussive, elegant and unhurried. A court at its ease.

And the light — the light came from people.

They hung in the air at intervals throughout the cavern, dozens of them, hundreds, suspended at the height of chandeliers by nothing visible at all. They were dressed in fine clothes, or had been once; they were arranged gracefully, arms relaxed, heads tilted as if listening to the music. And they glowed. Not their skin exactly — something under their skin, something where a soul ought to sit, shining through their eyes and the seams of their mouths and the thin places of their wrists like lamplight through paper.

They weren't dead. That was the worst part. Dead would have been a mercy I could file away. They swayed slightly, all together, in time to the music, and when the music paused between movements, so did they.

"Oh," Wendell said. It wasn't a word so much as the sound a person makes when the floor gives way. "Oh, that's — Mara, those are *people*."

"I know."

"The lanterns are people."

"I know, Wendell."

A century-old prophecy had pulled me through a book last week. My brother had been dead-then-not-dead for ten years. I thought I'd spent my whole allotment of horror for one lifetime. I hadn't. There is always more horror; the world keeps it stocked like a grocer keeps flour.

Nix glanced back at us, and for once the pickpocket's face wasn't smirking. "Told you not to look at them like that."

"Like what?"

"Like they're already gone." Nix adjusted the frayed collar of a coat that had been stolen from someone better dressed. "They're not gone. That's the trick of this place. The Queen doesn't kill souls. She *keeps* them. Burns them slow. A soul burns for centuries if you feed it right." A pause. "My mother's up there somewhere. Third gallery, maybe. I stopped counting which light was hers after the first year. It made walking under them easier."

I didn't have anything to say to that. Some things you just have to let sit in the air between you, ugly and true, until the other person decides whether to pick it back up. Nix didn't pick it back up. Nix started walking.

* * *

We went down through the terraces on smugglers' paths Nix knew — service cuts behind the fungus gardens, stairs worn smooth by generations of people whose names had been traded away. The music got louder. So did everything else. Undercourt nobility passed us on the walkways: tall figures in robes of grey silk shot through with veins of silver, faces elegant and slightly wrong the way a mask is wrong when you can see the strap. Their skin had the same faint luminosity as the lanterns', but banked, controlled, drawn inward instead of poured out. Fed-on souls, wearing their meals.

One of them stopped near us at a railing overlooking the central court, and I felt it like a change in air pressure — attention, vast and bored, sliding over me the way a lighthouse beam slides over a rowboat. I did what any sensible hedge-witch does when confronted with an apex predator: I became furniture. I studied the fungus garden with the deep fascination of a woman who has always dreamed of bioluminescent mushrooms.

The noble moved on.

"They taste intent," Nix breathed once we'd passed. "You're thinking too loud. Both of you. You think like a bakery on fire."

"What am I supposed to think about instead?" Wendell whispered.

"Nothing. Think about bread. Actually — no, don't think about bread, you'll get hungry, hunger has a smell down here—" Nix pinched the bridge of their nose. "Saints' teeth. Just follow me and be boring. Can you be boring?"

"I'm a rare-book appraiser," Wendell said with dignity. "Boring is my native language."

* * *

The heart of the court was a plaza of black glass, and at its center rose the throne — though calling it a throne undersells it. It was a spire of stone grown, not built, spiraling upward like a drill bit, and coiled around it in ascending tiers sat the court itself, hundreds of the grey-robed nobles arranged in concentric curves, all facing inward and downward toward a floor of polished obsidian where matters of state were conducted. Above it all, impossibly high, hung the grandest lanterns of all — great clustered constellations of bound souls arranged in shapes: a wheel, a crown, a tree in bloom.

And above even those, set into the cavern's roof like a second moon, burned a single soul so large and so bright that it cast shadows.

"That one," Nix said quietly, "is the founder. First soul she ever took. They say the prophecy was written by it, back when it still had a mouth." Nix's voice did something complicated on the word *prophecy*, and I remembered — with a lurch — that Nix dealt in stolen names, and that a name had been the price of our passage down. "Don't ask. Later. Maybe never."

I barely heard it. Because I was looking at the wheel.

The wheel of lanterns turned slowly above the court, and as it turned, the light swept across the obsidian floor in long slow rays, and in one of those rays —

I knew him.

Ten years is a long time. Ten years is a boy becoming a man in the dark without anyone to watch it happen. But I knew the set of his shoulders before I knew his face, and I knew his face before I understood what I was seeing, and by then my body had already decided to move and my brain was running behind, shouting objections.

He hung at the rim of the wheel, third spoke from the top, dressed in a fine coat that fit him the way costumes fit mannequins. He was maybe twenty now. He'd been fifteen when he vanished. His hair had been brown. It was still brown, but it was the brown of wood ash, and his eyes —

His eyes were open. They were open and they were full of light, the way all theirs were, but underneath the light there was nothing. No Tam. No brother who used to steal the ends of my bread and blame the cat we didn't own. Just a shape wearing my brother, lit from inside like a wick in a lamp, swaying gently to music he couldn't hear.

"*Tam*," I said.

Out loud. In the middle of the Undercourt.

The word left me like a bird leaving a broken cage, and every head in that plaza — every elegant, hungry, silver-veined head — began, slowly, to turn.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I remember it in pieces, the way you remember being rescued from drowning.

Nix's hand clamped over my mouth from behind — small fingers, shockingly strong, smelling of copper and pipe smoke. Wendell's arm went around my waist and suddenly I was moving, being moved, folded between the two of them into a huddle that looked, to any watching eye, like lovers and a guide admiring the view.

"You do not," Nix hissed into my ear, "say names down here. Names are *currency*. You just rang yourself like a bell."

"He's there," I said against Nix's fingers. "That's him. That's my brother, he's *right there*, he's hanging in the sky like a — like a *lamp*—"

"I know," Wendell said. His voice cracked straight down the middle. "Mara. Mara, look at me. We can't. Not like this. If you go up there they'll take you too, and then there'll be two lights in that wheel and nobody left who knows either of your names."

"And that," said a voice like dry silk, "would be a shame."

* * *

He stood at the edge of the fungus garden we'd been using for cover, and I recognized him from the book — from the frozen crossroads, from the hooded figure who had named Wendell *the Sigil* and demanded the stone. Except here, in his own court, the hood was down, and he was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and he was smiling.

Not at me. At Wendell.

"The Sigil walks in my court," he said, "and brings with him a woman who shouts dead men's names at chandeliers." He inclined his head, courteous as a knife. "You are welcome here, key. Doors open for you whether you wish it or not. That is your nature. Hers—" his gaze slid to me, and it went through me like a fishhook finding flesh, "—hers is debt. She carries an old bargain in her blood. I can smell the interest accruing."

"We're lost tourists," Nix said smoothly, stepping half in front of us. "Festival season. Everyone comes down for the festival."

"The festival is in six weeks."

"Early birds."

Something flickered across the beautiful face — amusement, maybe, or appetite; with that kind of face the two look identical. He raised one hand, and far above us, the great wheel of lanterns slowed. Stopped. And Tam's light — my brother's light — bent, very slightly, toward me. Like a candle flame leaning into a draft. Like something reaching.

Then the wheel resumed its turn, and the moment was gone, and I understood with total clarity that it had not been an accident. It had been a demonstration. *I can move him. I can move him toward you or away. Everything you love down here moves at my pleasure.*

"Go home, little hedge-witch," the courtier said gently. "Or stay. Stay and be interesting. The Queen so enjoys interesting things." He smiled again, wider, and his teeth were very white and very even, like piano keys. "But know that the door you came in by is watched now. All doors are watched. Nothing leaves my Queen's house unless she opens her hand."

And then he simply wasn't there — folded back into the crowd of grey silk as if he'd never stood apart from it, and the music swelled, and the wheel turned, and my brother's light swung away from me at the speed of continents.

* * *

We made it back to the smuggler's cut before my legs quit. I sat down hard on a step worn smooth by other people's disasters, and Wendell sat next to me, and Nix stood at the mouth of the passage with their back to us, keeping watch, giving me the privacy of their spine.

"He looked at me," I said. "Or — the light looked. Whatever's left of him looked."

"Mara—"

"He's in *pain*, Wendell. Or he's in nothing, and I don't know which is worse. Ten years I thought he was dead and I grieved him properly, I did the work, I put flowers on an empty grave, and the whole time he was up there being *furniture* for monsters—"

"Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe."

"Right. Sorry." He took my hand instead, and held it, and didn't say anything else, which was the correct answer, and I hated how much it helped.

Above us, muffled by stone, the music played on. Somewhere in that sound, my brother swayed.

"Nix," I said finally. "The vault. The Queen's vault. The stone — Sef's notes said the payment for the bargain went into the deep treasury. Souls go in. Souls come out. Which means there's a mechanism. A ledger. A *door*."

Nix turned around. In the blue glow of the fungus gardens, the pickpocket's face was unreadable, and then it wasn't — it was tired, and young, and older than any child's face should be.

"There's a vault," Nix said. "Nobody's ever opened it. Nobody's ever come back from trying, except as new lighting." A pause. "But there's a story. Old one. About a key born once a century that can open any lock the Queen ever made." Nix looked at Wendell. "About a Sigil."

Wendell laughed — one short, unhappy note. "I appraise books."

"Not tonight you don't."

I looked up through the cut, toward the distant glow of the plaza, toward the wheel where my brother burned slow and obedient, and something in my chest finished hardening into a shape I recognized from stories about other people. Resolve, the ballads call it. Down here it felt less like steel and more like a debt coming due.

"Hungry things took my brother," I said. "So we're going to bargain with hungry things. And then we're going to take him back."

Nix grinned, sharp as a hook. "Now *that's* thinking loud enough to taste."
