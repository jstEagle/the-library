## 8. Bargaining With Hungry Things

I want it on the record, for whatever record survives this, that I did not want to open the vault.

I am a rare-book appraiser. My professional skills include distinguishing first editions from clever forgeries, negotiating with men who smell of pipe smoke and bad faith, and knowing when a binding has been re-sewn. Nowhere in that skill set does "unlocking the treasury of an underground death-cult monarchy" appear. And yet here I was, standing before a door the size of a cathedral's shame, with every soul in the room watching me like I was the last bottle of wine at a funeral.

The vault sat at the end of a corridor carved from black stone shot through with veins of something that glowed faintly amber — not light exactly, more like the memory of light, preserved in mineral form. The door itself had no handle, no keyhole, no hinges I could see. Just a relief carved deep into its face: a circle bisected by a line, and within the circle, smaller shapes that hurt to look at directly, the way a word repeated too many times stops meaning anything and starts being just noise in your mouth.

The Sigil. Which was me. Apparently.

"You feel it," Nix said beside me. They'd gone quiet since the Court proper — quiet in the way pickpockets go quiet near guards, all their sharpness folded inward. "Don't lie. You've been touching your hand to your chest for the last hour like you're checking for a wallet."

"I'm checking my pulse," I said. "It's been doing interesting things."

"Same thing, down here."

Mara stood on my other side. She hadn't spoken in a while, which frightened me more than the door did. Mara silent was Mara calculating, and Mara calculating usually meant she'd found another thread of guilt to pull, some new way this was all her fault. Ten years of practice, I was learning. She could build a whole cathedral of self-blame out of nothing but a margin note.

"It wants you to touch it," she said finally. Her voice had that flat carefulness she used when she was scared and refusing to admit it. "The door. Don't pretend you haven't noticed."

"I've noticed," I said. "I've also noticed that everything down here that wants me to touch it has teeth. It's a pattern."

Behind us, our escort waited. Three of the Undercourt's honor guard — if honor was the right word for creatures who looked like they'd been assembled from a nightmare's leftovers and then given very good tailoring. Tall, gray-skinned, elegant, with eyes like coins dropped down a well. Their leader had introduced himself as Vessal, Master of Thresholds, in a voice like silk dragged over gravel, and had used the word *guest* four times in a way that made clear we were guests the way a rabbit is a guest of the hawk.

"The Queen awaits," Vessal said now, for the fifth time. He seemed to enjoy saying it. Perhaps it was the only sentence he got.

"And the Queen waits," I murmured, "because the door doesn't open without me. Let's not pretend this is hospitality."

Vessal smiled. It was a good smile, structurally. All the teeth were where teeth should be. That somehow made it worse.

* * *

They took us first to the antechamber, because of course there was an antechamber. Everything beneath the mountain had an antechamber — a waiting room for dread, furnished in velvet the color of dried blood and lit by lanterns that I tried very hard not to look at, and failed, because I am only human.

The lanterns were souls.

I'd heard Mara describe them on the stair down, in a whisper, her hand crushing mine hard enough to grind bone: *living lights, Wendell, they're people, they're people in glass.* Knowing it hadn't prepared me. Each lantern hung from a silver chain, and inside each one something moved — a soft gold radiance that pulsed, and swayed, and sometimes, when you watched long enough, pressed against the glass the way a moth presses against a window. Not randomly. Purposefully. From the inside out.

One of them, hanging nearest the antechamber's entrance, pulsed in a rhythm I almost recognized. Like breathing. Like someone asleep, breathing slow, dreaming of somewhere with sky in it.

I stopped looking. There is a limit to what a man can look at and remain useful, and I'd found it.

"They'll offer you something," Mara said. She'd turned away from the lanterns too, and was studying the mosaic floor instead — interlocking circles, thousands of them, forming a pattern that resolved, from a distance, into one enormous version of the sigil on the vault door. "That's how these things work. Bargains. Contracts. They're legalists, the Undercourt. Everything's terms and conditions."

"How do you know?"

"Because Sef talked about them." Her jaw tightened on the name. "He said you never sign anything down here. He said the Court can't take what isn't given. That's why they bargain. That's why they *need* bargains." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, which I have since learned is simply how her face works under pressure. "Whatever they offer you — and they'll make it good, Wendell, they'll make it so good — remember that the price is always a person. Always. That's the currency down here. People."

"Noted," I said. "No signing. No giving. If anyone hands me a quill, I develop a sudden allergy."

Nix snorted from the corner, where they were doing something furtive to the hem of their coat. "You joke," they said. "Good. Keep doing that. The ones who stop joking are the ones who end up as lamps."

"That's grimly specific."

"I've seen it." And Nix's face closed like a book, and I didn't ask, because some pages you're not ready to read.

A gong sounded somewhere — a deep, resonant note that I felt in my fillings and, I suspect, in my soul, which after tonight felt like a thing I should be more protective of. The far doors opened.

"The Queen," said Vessal, "receives."

* * *

I had expected a monster.

I want to be honest about that. I'd spent the descent building her in my head — something vast and scaled, something with too many joints, a horror in a crown. What I got instead was a woman sitting on a throne of woven shadow, mending a tapestry.

She looked up at us with mild interest, needle poised mid-stitch, and she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful — precise, glittering, and absolutely indifferent to whether you survived it. Her gown was the gray of old spider silk. Her crown was not metal at all; it was a circlet of small floating lights, dozens of them, each one pulsing gently, and I understood with a lurch what I was looking at. A crown of lanterns. A crown of taken souls, worn like jewels.

"There you are," the Underqueen said, and her voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was genuinely warm, the voice of a hostess greeting guests who'd arrived slightly damp from the rain. "The Sigil, and his keeper. Sit. Please. Hospitality is law beneath the mountain, and I keep the law."

There were chairs. Actual chairs, upholstered, positioned before the throne at a conversational distance. This offended me more than chains would have. Chains I understood. Furniture was a trap with cushions.

We sat. Nix remained standing by the wall, melting into shadow with professional ease. Mara perched on the edge of her chair like a bird ready to fly at the first wrong movement. I tried to sit like a man having tea with an aunt, and probably managed *man awaiting execution.*

"You've come for the boy," the Queen said, returning to her tapestry. The needle flashed. "Tam Lockridge. Yes, we know why you've come. News travels strangely down here — faster than it should, slower than you'd like. You want him back." Stitch. Stitch. "And you believe the Sigil can simply... open our vault, and take him, and walk away."

"Can't he?" Mara asked.

The Queen smiled at her tapestry. "The vault holds what the Court has purchased. Purchased things stay purchased. That is the foundation of all order, above and below. But—" and here she set down the needle, and looked at me, and the full weight of her attention landed on me like a falling piano— "the Sigil is not bound by purchase. The Sigil is older than purchase. The Sigil is the lock's first key, and locks do not argue with their keys."

"So I can open it," I said carefully.

"You can open *anything.*" She said it lightly, the way one says *you can have seconds.* "Doors. Vaults. Contracts. Oaths. The binding that holds a soul in glass is, at bottom, a lock, dear Wendell, and you are what locks dream of." She rose, and the crown-lights swayed around her head like a halo that had been arrested mid-orbit. "Which brings me to my offer."

Here it was. Mara's hand found mine across the armrests — cold fingers, gripping hard. *Remember,* the grip said. *The price is always a person.*

"Prophecy is a tedious genre," the Queen said, pacing now, her silk skirts making no sound at all, which struck me as physically rude. "Full of portent and no punctuation. But even tedious prophecies contain truths. Here is one: the stone can be made whole again. And here is another: whosoever bears the whole stone will remake the world according to their nature. Remake it, Wendell. Not rule it. *Remake* it. Every law rewritten. Every debt dissolved or doubled, at the bearer's whim. Death negotiable. Time negotiable." She stopped before me. "I have worn fragments of power for nine hundred years. I know precisely how heavy they are. And I am offering to set one in your hand."

"Why?" Mara's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip. "What do you want?"

"What I have always wanted." The Queen didn't even glance at her. Her coin-well eyes stayed on mine. "The surface. The sun. My people have lived in the dark for a thousand years, paying for a crime no one remembers committing, and I am *tired*, appraiser. Give me the dawn and I will give you everything else. Open my vault. Take your brother-in-law-to-be, take the girl, take the pickpocket, take the whole ragged lot of you, and go free. All I ask—" and here she smiled, warm as hearthlight, and I felt the trap close around my heart before she even finished the sentence— "is the witch."

The chamber went very still. Even the lanterns seemed to pulse more quietly.

"Mara," the Queen continued, sweetly, reasonably, "is the hinge of the prophecy. Her name is written in it. Her bargain started all of this. While she lives unclaimed, the prophecy moves, and while the prophecy moves, the stone remains split, and while the stone remains split, my people rot in the dark. Hand her to me — a simple transfer of claim, one signature, painless, she'll barely notice the change — and the vault opens, the boy goes free, and you two have fifty years together before age does what I cannot." She tilted her head. "Fifty years, Wendell. Most men don't get half that with anyone. And all it costs is one hedge-witch who ruined your life a week ago."

I looked at Mara.

I want to tell you I deliberated. That some part of me weighed it, ran the arithmetic, saw the terrible elegance of the trade. Because that's what the offer was designed for — not to tempt the wicked, but to make the decent feel wicked for hesitating. Fifty years against one life. Certainty against chaos. A man could drown in that math and call it depth.

But I looked at Mara, and she was already letting go of my hand. Already withdrawing. Already composing her face into the expression of someone preparing to be given away, because ten years of running teaches you that everyone leaves eventually and it's better to hand them your coat on the way out. And something in my chest went off like a struck bell.

"No," I said.

The Queen waited.

"No," I said again, and stood, and discovered my legs were steadier than I had any right to expect. "Not because I'm noble. I want to be clear about that. I'm a bookseller. I appraise things for a living, and I know overpriced merchandise when I see it. You're asking me to trade a person for a person, and the exchange rate is obscene, because—" my voice did something embarrassing and I let it— "because she's not *merchandise*. She's the best thing I've found in forty years of cataloguing other people's treasures. And I don't sell the collection. Ever."

Silence. Then the Underqueen laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and almost human. Almost.

"Oh, *good*," she breathed. "Oh, that's the answer. Do you understand? That was the last question. Not mine — the *stone's*. The prophecy needed to know what its Sigil was made of, and now it knows." Her warmth drained away like water through sand, and what remained underneath was old, and cold, and vast. "You may go. Take the fragment of truth I've given you. But hear the rest of the ledger, Sigil: the halves must be joined, and they will be joined, in this court, before the turning of the season. Your refusal bought nothing except the manner of it. You will bring me the stone, or the Guild will bring it *over* me, and either way the world ends and begins. The only choice left to any of us is who stands holding it when it wakes."

"Cheerful," said Nix from the wall.

"Guards," said the Queen, resuming her seat and her needlework as though we were leaving a dinner party rather than fleeing a god's waiting room. "See our guests to the stair. And Wendell—" she didn't look up— "when you hold the whole stone, and you will, remember that I offered you the polite version. What comes next won't ask."

* * *

They walked us back through the corridors in silence, past the lanterns, past the great sealed door that hummed faintly at my passing like a dog recognizing its master. I kept my hands in my pockets. I did not touch anything.

It wasn't until we reached the smuggler's stair — Nix's door, the crooked little hatchway back toward the world of smog and bread and outlawed charms — that Mara finally spoke.

"She lied," she said quietly. "Part of it. Maybe most of it."

"Which part?"

"'Hand her to me and the vault opens.' Souls that are purchased stay purchased — her own words. Tam's soul was *sold*, Wendell. Sold things stay sold. She couldn't free him if I handed her my heart on a plate." Mara's fists were shaking at her sides. "She wanted me to say yes so the prophecy would break, or bend, or whatever it does when its people fail the test. And if I'd said yes—" her voice splintered— "if *you'd* said yes—"

"But I didn't."

"You didn't." She turned and looked at me, really looked, the way she looks at charms she's deciding whether to trust. "Why? Honestly. Not the speech. The truth."

I thought about it. About the lantern pressing against its glass. About her hand going cold in mine. About fifty years.

"Because I've spent my whole life handling rare things," I said. "First editions. Irreplaceable things. And the first rule of the trade is you never trade the irreplaceable for the merely valuable. Power's valuable. Fifty years is valuable. You're—" I shrugged, helpless. "You're the only one of you there is."

For a moment she just stared. Then she laughed — wet, cracked, real — and grabbed the front of my coat and kissed me, hard, there on the smuggler's stair with the dark humming behind us and the mountain's hunger at our backs.

When she pulled away, her eyes had gone sharp again. Working.

"Wendell. What she said at the end. 'The halves must be joined.'" Her voice dropped. "The stone. The sigil-stone. It's *split* — she said it herself, twice. Which means somewhere there's another half, and she thinks we're going to find it for her." She was already moving up the stairs, pulling me along, Nix ghosting behind. "Sef. Sef split it, or knows who did. We need the marshes. We need him. Tonight."

"Tonight?" I said weakly. "I was rather hoping for dinner. Something with pastry."

"Wendell."

"Going. Going."

Above us, far off, a hatch opened onto grey Ashmoor daylight, and I climbed toward it carrying the worst appraisal of my career: the knowledge that everything, everywhere, was now priced — and that sooner or later, something was going to try to collect.
