## 10. Sef's Confession

I knew she was coming before I saw her.

The marsh told me. It always tells me — the reeds go quiet in a particular way when someone crosses the causeway who has business with me rather than with the eels. Ten years I'd lived out here with a heron for company and a kettle that judged me, and in all that time the reeds had gone quiet exactly four times. Three of those were tax collectors. The fourth was a fever dream I don't care to discuss.

This was the fifth.

I put another log on the fire, mostly so my hands would have something to do besides shake, and waited. That's the one advantage of being old and exiled: nobody expects you to come to the door. Doors are for people with futures.

She came up the path through the fog like a memory I'd been trying to drown. Mara Lockridge. Same stubborn set to her shoulders, same way of walking like the ground owed her money. But older now. Harder at the edges, softer somehow at the center, which is the opposite of how most people weather, and it made my chest hurt in a way I decided to ignore on medical grounds.

Behind her came a young man with the look of a librarian who'd wandered into a war. Tall, earnest, clutching his satchel like it might be pried from him by bandits. And behind *him* — gods help me — a small sharp-faced girl picking her teeth with what I hoped was a fishbone and surveying my home the way a magpie surveys a widow's jewelry box.

"Well," I said, before any of them could speak, because if I let them start I'd never get control of the conversation back. "The prodigal apprentice returns. Did you bring an offering, or just the guilt? Guilt's traditional."

"Hello to you too, Sef." Her voice was steady. Good. One of us should be.

"You look terrible," I said, which was a lie and we both knew it. "Marsh air agrees with me, though. Look at this complexion. Years of peat fumes. Doctors hate me."

The girl snorted. The librarian looked alarmed, which suggested he'd been briefed on approximately none of this. Mara just stood there in my doorway with the fog curling around her boots and her eyes doing that thing they used to do when she was twelve and had broken something valuable — waiting to see if I'd yell first so she wouldn't have to feel bad about it.

I stepped aside. "Come in before the bog takes you. It's been looking for an excuse."

* * *

They crowded into my single room, which had not been built for crowds, or rooms, particularly. The librarian — Wendell, apparently, and yes, *that* Wendell, and no, I did not react visibly, I want that noted for posterity — sat carefully on my good stool as though it were made of glass. The pickpocket made straight for my shelf of jars and started reading labels with the frank entitlement of youth.

"Touch the one marked *teeth* and you'll regret it," I said.

"Which teeth?"

"Nobody knows. That's what makes it exciting."

Mara put the book on my table. The prophecy. I recognized the binding before she'd finished unwrapping it — vellum gone the color of old teeth, corners worn round by a century of anxious hands. My stomach dropped through the floor of the marsh, through the water table, and kept going.

"Where," I said, "did you get that."

"Ashmoor night market. A book dealer sold it to—" She glanced at Wendell. "He's cataloguing it. For the Guild."

"For the *Guild*." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "Oh, that's marvelous. That's genuinely marvelous. A hundred years I keep this thing buried, and it surfaces in the hands of a Guild appraiser. The universe has a sense of humor. It's just a very cruel one, and it doesn't do callbacks well."

"Sef." Mara's voice went quiet in the way that meant she'd already read it. All of it. Including the margins. "It names him. The Sigil. It names tonight's date. And there are notes in the margins — old ones, hedge-script — about a bargain. About a soul given as payment." Her hands were flat on the table, steady by force. "About a boy named Tam."

The fire popped. Outside, the heron screamed at something, probably on principle.

"I need you to tell me I'm wrong," she said. "That's all I'm asking. Tell me the margins are a forgery, tell me some other Tam died ten years ago, tell me anything except what your face is doing right now."

My face, I am told, is not subtle. It never has been. Seventy years of expression had carved every failure into it like a ledger, and right now it was open to the worst page.

So I did what any coward does when cornered by the truth. I put the kettle on.

* * *

Tea takes four minutes to brew. We spent them in silence, which is the longest silence I've ever endured, and I've buried two wives and a familiar.

I gave Mara her cup first. Old habit. Then I sat down across from her, and I looked at her — really looked, the way I hadn't let myself since the night she walked out of my cottage with her pack and her fury and her ten-year-old brother's coat still hanging on the hook behind the door — and I understood that there was no version of this where I got to keep lying.

"You were fourteen," I said. "Do you remember what you were?"

"An apprentice."

"You were a *vessel*. You just didn't know it yet." I wrapped my hands around my own cup because they wanted to do something worse. "The prophecy is older than Ashmoor, girl. Older than the Guild, older than the Undercourt, maybe older than the mountain itself. Nobody wrote it. Something wrote *through* somebody, and the somebody didn't survive the experience. And it says — it has always said — that when the Sigil wakes, the stone will choose a hand to carry it. And the stone does not ask permission. It pours itself into whatever fits, and what's left afterward isn't a person anymore. It's a key that walks and talks and opens things until it wears out."

Wendell had gone very pale. "The first claiming," he said. "In the text. There's a line about—"

"The first claiming was a shepherd boy in the hills above Vail, three hundred years ago. The stone wore him for eleven days. When it finished, there wasn't enough left of him to bury." I took a sip of tea I couldn't taste. "The second claiming would have been you, Mara. Fourteen years old. I found the resonance in you by accident — you'll remember the lesson, the scrying exercise, the one where you knocked the bowl off the table—"

"You said I had a knack."

"I said you had a knack because the truth was that every ward within forty miles had started singing your name, and the Guild's diviners would have heard it within the year, and the stone would have heard it sooner." I set the cup down. My hands were not steady anymore; I'd stopped pretending they would be. "So I did what hedge-witches do. What we've always done when something too big comes down the road. I bargained."

"With the Undercourt," Mara said. Flat. Terrible.

"The court beneath the mountain keeps souls the way a miser keeps coins. And they trade. Always trade — it's the only law they honor. They wanted a soul, a young one, unclaimed, with enough light in it to burn bright in their lanterns for a century or more." I made myself say it. I had rehearsed saying it for ten years, alone, to the heron, and it had never once come out clean. "And in exchange they would close the road. Blind every ear that listened for you. Scour your name out of the resonance so completely that even the stone itself would pass you by."

"And the price," Mara whispered, "was Tam."

"He was visiting that week. He was asleep in the loft." The words came out of me like splinters coming out of a wound — each one a relief and each one a fresh agony. "You have to understand, I didn't choose him. The court chose. They take what shines brightest, and that child glowed like a lamp. I tried to substitute myself. Do you hear me? I offered them *me*, seventy years of bad knees and worse temper, and they laughed. Laughter, from under a mountain. I'll hear it until I die."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight. Nix had stopped rummaging. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You could have told me," Mara said finally, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was so much worse than shouting. "Ten years ago. Before I ran. You could have said *your brother is alive, he's below the mountain, I did this* — instead of letting me believe he was dead. Instead of letting me grieve him. Instead of letting me spend a decade hating you for reasons that were too small."

"If I'd told you, you'd have gone after him."

"Of course I would have!"

"And the bargain would have broken!" I was on my feet now, God help me, tea slopping over my knuckles. "The moment you set foot toward that mountain, the blindness lifts, the resonance returns, and the stone starts listening again. Everything I bought — everything *he* paid — for nothing. Don't you see? Keeping you ignorant was the only wall I had left. So I built it high and I lived inside it, and I let you hate me, because hatred keeps people away, and away was the only safe place left for you." I sank back down. "It was the cruelest thing I've ever done and I'd do it again tomorrow. That's what I need you to understand. Not forgiveness. Understanding. Forgiveness is for people who wouldn't repeat the crime."

Mara looked at me for a long time. The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere in my chest, ten years of rehearsed defenses lay in ruins, and it turned out the ruins hurt more than the standing walls ever had.

"No," she said quietly. "I don't forgive you. Maybe I will. Maybe in a year, when Tam is breathing free air, I'll come back and we'll drink something stronger than this swampwater and I'll forgive you properly. But not today." She leaned forward. "Today I need the rest of it. How do I get him back?"

* * *

Here is the thing about confessions: they're like pulling a thread on a knit sweater. You start with one sin and end up naked in front of everyone.

"The bargain had a clause," I said. "There's always a clause. The court doesn't hold a soul forever — they hold it against payment. Tam's freedom was priced against the sigil-stone. Bring them the stone whole, and the debt clears. That was their joke, you see. Their little joke. Because the stone can't be brought whole. It hasn't been whole in three hundred years."

"It was split," Wendell said slowly. "After the shepherd boy. After Vail."

"After Vail, the survivors did the only sensible thing anyone has ever done with that cursed rock. They broke it. Two halves, separated, warded, hidden. One half went down — traded, sealed, eventually worked into the crown of whoever rules the Undercourt. Their queen wears half of the world's worst mistake as a *decoration*." I almost admired it. Almost. "And the other half stayed up here. In Ashmoor. Buried beneath the newest, grandest, most self-righteous building the surface ever raised."

Nix's head came up slowly. "The Guildhall."

"The Guildhall." I spread my hands. "Where else? The mages' hunters sitting on top of the one artifact that could make their cruelty permanent. They don't even know what they've got. Or — no. Worse. Some of them know. There's always one who knows. Corvane, if he's still breathing, Corvane has always struck me as a man who reads the fine print of history."

Mara was staring at the table, and I watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic I'd done a hundred times over ten years, and which had only ever had one answer, and the answer was a door that opened onto a cliff.

"To free Tam, we bring the halves together," she said. "In the Undercourt. Which means stealing half the stone out from under the Guild, carrying it down the mountain, and handing it to the Underqueen. Who wants it. Who has wanted it for a century." She looked up at me. "That's the trap, isn't it? That's what the prophecy wants. It gets its stone whole either way — whether the Guild takes it, or the Queen takes it, or we deliver it gift-wrapped."

"Now you're doing theology," I said. "I'm proud and horrified in equal measure."

"There has to be another way."

"There isn't. There never is. There's only the bad road and the worse road, and the difference between them is usually just who's standing next to you when you walk it." I looked around my miserable little room — at the runaway apprentice who'd come back anyway, at the rare-book appraiser wearing a prophecy like a target, at the pickpocket already eyeing my window latch — and felt something I hadn't felt in a decade, something suspiciously like hope, and hated it immediately.

"So," I said, and reached under the floorboard where I keep the things worth keeping, and drew out a folded sheet of paper covered in thirty years of my own cramped handwriting. "If you're going to rob the Guildhall, you'll want the ward-schematics. I copied them off a drunk acolyte in the year you were born. And you'll want to know that the Binding Festival is in nine days, when half the wards stand down for the processions, and the other half are tended by novices who couldn't ward a henhouse."

Mara took the paper. Her fingers brushed mine, and neither of us flinched, and that was its own kind of truce.

"Why are you helping us?" she asked. "Really."

"Because I sold a child to the dark to save you," I said, "and the interest has come due. And because—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I'm too old and too lame to go down that mountain myself, and it is killing me, and the least I can do is draw you a map."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the marsh for the first time in weeks, and far to the north — so faint it might have been imagination, might have been wind, might have been the mountain clearing its throat — I heard the bells of Ashmoor begin to ring for evening.

Nine days, I thought. Nine days, and then either the world ends or it finally, finally begins.
