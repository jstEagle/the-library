## 11. The Guildhall Job

There are three things I know about planning a job, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

One: never plan a job in a room with a window, because windows invite hope, and hope makes people stupid.

Two: never plan a job with someone who's never stolen anything, because they'll ask questions like "why can't we just ask for it," and you'll have to explain the entire architecture of the world to them while your patience bleeds out on the floor.

Three: never, under any circumstances, fall in love with the people you're stealing with.

I was two for three that morning, and getting worse.

* * *

The room we'd rented above a dye-shop in Tanners' Row had no window — just a vent that breathed the smell of urine and mordant into the space like a roommate who wouldn't leave. Perfect. Mara had drawn the Guildhall's floor plan on the table in chalk dust and candle grease, and it was good work, better than I expected from a hedge-witch who'd spent ten years being careful. Careful people make excellent thieves, actually. They just don't know it yet.

"Here." Her finger tapped the western stair. "Acolytes' corridor. During the Binding Festival, the whole Guild processes through the nave at dawn — every mage, every hunter, everyone with a badge and a grudge. The vaults are supposed to be locked and warded."

"Supposed to be?"

"Corvane's records say the ward-stones get pulled for the ceremony. They use them to anchor the Binding itself." She said Corvane's name flat, the way you'd say the name of a dog that bit you once and got away with it. "The vaults are still guarded. But guarded by men, not magic."

"Men I can handle," I said. "Men are just locks with opinions."

Wendell looked up from the book — the prophecy, which he carried now like other men carry a sick child — and gave me one of his looks. Wendell's looks are a whole language. This one meant *I'm worried about you but I'll express it as mild disapproval because feelings are hard.*

"You're enjoying this too much," he said.

"I'm enjoying this exactly the right amount. Fear is for after. Before, you need appetite." I leaned over the floor plan. "Tell me about the vault door."

Mara told me about the vault door. Iron, three inches, no keyhole — it opened to a spoken word held by the Vault-Warden, who during the Festival would be standing in the nave singing hymns to chains like everybody else. Which meant the door was just iron until someone came back down to open it, and nobody comes back down during the Binding. Nobody wants to miss the Binding. It's the closest thing Ashmoor has to theater.

"So we're not stealing from the vault," Wendell said slowly. "We're stealing from the *hallway outside* the vault."

"Now he's learning," I said. "The stone-half gets brought up for inspection the night before. Tradition. The Warden shows it to the senior guildsmen, they nod at it, it goes back down. For about an hour, the most dangerous object in the city sits in a wooden case on a table in a corridor full of distracted religious fanatics."

"And if we're caught—"

"We won't be caught."

"Nix."

"If we're caught," I said, because he had that stubborn set to his jaw that Mara pretended not to find charming and I found exhausting, "we're caught inside the Guildhall dressed as Guild, holding their holiest relic, on their holiest night. So let's not be caught. Let's be perfect instead. Perfection is mostly just fear wearing nice clothes."

Mara snorted. Then she covered it with her hand, like she hadn't meant to let it out. That's the thing about her — everything true about her tries to escape and she keeps catching it at the door.

* * *

We worked through the details until the candle drowned in its own wax. Disguises: acolyte gray, which Nix could acquire, because Nix could acquire anything short of forgiveness. Timing: the inspection hour, marked by the ninth bell, when the nave choir hits the long note and every ear in the building is pointed at heaven. Exit: the ash-chute behind the reliquary, which dumps into the coal-yard on Vesper Lane, where a cart driven by a man who owed me more than money would be waiting.

"What does he owe you?" Wendell asked.

"A name," I said. "His own, actually. Long story. He didn't used to be called Brannick. He's very grateful about the upgrade."

"That's terrifying."

"That's Tuesday."

Mara rolled the chalk between her fingers and stared at the floor plan like it might confess something. "There's a piece we're missing," she said. "The inspection is real — I found it in Sef's notes, the stone gets shown before the Binding so the old blood in it can 'recognize the season.' But there's always an escort. Two hunters, minimum. And hunters can smell working magic."

"So we don't do any," I said. "That's the beauty of this job. It's honest theft. No spells, no sigils, no witchcraft. Just nerve and costumes and me."

"You're not a spell?"

"I'm a natural phenomenon. Completely different legal category."

But she wasn't laughing. She was doing the thing where her thumb finds the scar on her wrist and worries it like a knot. I'd catalogued her tells by then — it's how I love people, honestly, I collect them, their little leaks. Wendell's tell is adjusting his cuffs when he's frightened. Mara's is the scar. Mine, if you're wondering, is nothing. I'm seamless. Ask anyone I've robbed.

"The escort," she said finally, "will be looking for a hedge-witch. Because Corvane knows I want the stone. He's known since the shop raid — why else offer Wendell the trade? He's not guarding the stone from thieves. He's guarding it from *me*. Which means the disguise has to hold against someone who's seen my face."

"Then you don't wear your face," I said. "Glamour's out, fine. But there are older tricks than glamour. Chalk, charcoal, a change of posture. People see what the uniform tells them to see. An acolyte is furniture. You walk like furniture, you carry a censer, you keep your eyes down and your hands busy, and you will pass through that building like smoke through a keyhole."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then we improvise, and improvisation is just planning with the safety off."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then Wendell said, quietly, "She goes in first. If anything feels wrong, she walks out. We agree to that now or I don't agree to any of it."

"Wendell—"

"No. I watched what the stone did to you for one second, Mara. One second. I'm not walking into the building that keeps it and pretending it's just a rock on a table." His voice didn't rise. That's the frightening thing about him; nothing about Wendell rises, it just deepens, like water finding its level. "First in, first out. Promise me."

Mara promised. People promise each other things in that room like promises were free. First in, first out. Nobody left behind. Everyone walks out or nobody does. I kept my mouth shut, because rule three was already bleeding, and I knew — the way you know rain coming, the way you know a mark's going to cry before he does — that at least one of those promises was going to cost us.

I just didn't know which one yet.

* * *

Festival morning came up gray and smelling of woodsmoke, the way Ashmoor does when it's trying to look holy.

We dressed in the coal-yard. Brannick's cart, Brannick's blankets, Brannick pretending not to exist, which is his best quality. Acolyte gray itches, in case you wondered. Whoever designed the Guild's vestments wanted the wearers to suffer mildly and constantly, on the theory that suffering improves devotion. My theory is the designer just hated laundry.

Wendell couldn't come past the yard. That had been the last fight, three nights ago, and Mara had won it by pointing out that the Sigil walking into the Guildhall was like carrying a lit lantern into a powder store — Corvane would feel him coming from across the city. So Wendell got the cart and the escape route and the worst job of all, which is waiting.

He held Mara's face in both hands before she went, and said something to her too low for me to hear, and she laughed — wet, quick, real — and I turned around and checked the cart wheels for a while because some doors you don't watch people walk through.

"Oi," I said, when I trusted my voice again. "Ninth bell. Inspection hour. We're furniture. Smoke through keyholes. Say it back."

"Furniture," Mara said. "Smoke. Keyholes."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"It won't go wrong."

"That's not the litany."

She grinned at me, gray-hooded and charcoal-shadowed and more alive than anyone has a right to look at dawn in a coal-yard. "If it goes wrong, we improvise."

"Planning with the safety off," I said. "Good. You'll do."

And here's the thing I didn't say, the thing I filed away in the drawer where I keep all my unsaid things, which is getting crowded: the ninth bell rang across the city right on schedule, deep and sweet, and somewhere under that sound the Guild was lifting a wooden case off a table in a corridor, and inside the case was half a stone that had already eaten my friend's brother's soul and nearly eaten hers, and I was walking toward it whistling.

Because that was the morning I noticed the second thing. The small thing. The thing I should have said out loud and didn't.

The acolyte roster I'd forged listed the inspection escort as four names. Four. But when we fell into the procession line in the cloister, hood down, eyes down, censer swinging, I counted the hunters flanking the Warden and his case.

Five.

Somebody extra was walking that stone up from the vaults. Somebody whose gray cloak hung wrong, whose boots were new, whose head turned — just once, just slightly — toward the two pieces of furniture swaying along at the rear of the line.

I didn't know who it was. But I knew the shape of a trap when I was standing in the middle of one, and the ninth bell was still ringing, and there was nowhere left to go but forward.

So I did what I do best.

I smiled, and kept walking.
