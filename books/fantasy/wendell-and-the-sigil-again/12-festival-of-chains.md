## 12. Festival of Chains

The Binding Festival smelled like roasting chestnuts and incense, which was a lie so thorough it deserved its own chapter in a book about lies. Underneath the sweetness — I could taste it even through the acolyte's veil — Ashmoor's Guildhall reeked of iron and old fear. It always had. Ten years ago I'd stood on these same flagstones with my heart hammering and a stolen luck-charm in my pocket, seventeen years old and certain the whole city could smell the magic on me.

Now I was walking back in wearing their uniform.

"Stop fidgeting," Nix murmured beside me. She was dressed as an acolyte too, though she wore it like a costume she intended to steal later. Her hood shadowed her face; her hands were folded in that particular way Guild initiates were taught, fingers laced, thumbs crossed, as if praying to something that might bite. "You're doing the thing where you look guilty."

"I *am* guilty."

"Then be guilty quietly. Guilt is only evidence if someone can see it." She smiled at a passing initiate, a sweet, vacant smile, and the initiate smiled back and walked on, never suspecting that the girl under the hood could pick his teeth out of his skull without breaking stride. "See? Nobody looks at you if you look like them. That's the whole trick of uniforms. That's why I hate them."

We joined the river of white-robed acolytes flowing up the Guildhall steps. Above us, the Binding Chain hung from the tower — a hundred feet of black iron links, each one engraved with a name, each name a mage the Guild had caught and bound over the last century. Festival tradition said you could touch the chain for luck. Festival reality was that touching the chain let the Guild read your skin like a ledger. I kept my hands in my sleeves.

The doors swallowed us.

* * *

Inside, the Guildhall was exactly as I remembered it and nothing like I remembered it, which is a thing memory does to you when it's been sitting on a bad room for ten years. The vaulted ceiling still soared. The banners of the nine guilds still hung in their proud rows. The mosaic floor still depicted the First Binding — a heroic figure driving a sigil-stone into the earth while shadowy figures fled into the dark, rendered by some artist who had clearly been paid by the link.

But when I was seventeen, this hall had been enormous, a cathedral of judgment. Now it was just a big room full of people who thought they were important, and I found that I could breathe in it.

That frightened me more than the guards did.

"Left corridor," Wendell's voice said in my ear, soft through the little speaking-charm Nix had lifted from a smuggler who owed her money. He was somewhere above us, in the gallery with the visiting appraisers, wearing his respectable face and carrying a folio of documents no one would ever ask him to open. His voice was steady. Too steady. The steadiness of a man reciting a shopping list to keep from screaming. "Two guards at the archive stair, rotating every quarter hour. Mara, are you—"

"I'm here," I said.

A pause. Through the charm I heard him exhale. "You're here," he repeated, like a man confirming the sun had come up. "Right. Quarter hour. Nix?"

"On it," Nix breathed, and peeled away from my side like smoke deciding to be somewhere else.

I turned down the right corridor instead, alone, toward the reliquary wing.

Because here was the thing about the plan — the plan Nix had drawn on a tavern table in spilled wine, the plan we'd rehearsed until Wendell could recite it in his sleep — the plan required someone to walk the reliquary corridor twice, once going and once coming back, and time the guard rotations to the second. And the plan required that someone to be me, because I was the only one of us who could pass an inspection if it came to that. Nix couldn't do the catechism. Wendell's hands shook when he lied, bless him, like a man trying to hold water in a sieve.

And me? I'd spent ten years perfecting the art of being nobody. Tonight I got to be somebody specific: Acolyte Third Class Verity Moss, assigned to festival candle duty, bored, tired, invisible.

I lit candles as I went. It was part of the cover, and also — I'll admit it — it settled my hands. Small flames, one after another, each one a tiny ordinary magic that even the Guild permitted, because fire doesn't care whose wick it eats. My fingers remembered the motions from a life before Sef, before Tam, before everything. Wick, breath, flame. Wick, breath, flame.

The reliquary corridor opened ahead of me, long and cold, lined with glass cases holding the Guild's trophies: broken staves, confiscated grimoires behind leaded glass, a dried hand in a jar that I chose not to think about. At the far end, past two guards who were currently absorbed in a whispered argument about festival overtime, stood the door to the deep vault.

Behind that door, beneath three floors of stone and a century of wards, sat half of the sigil-stone.

My palms sweated. I lit another candle.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about infiltrating the people who ruined your life: part of you wants them to notice you. Not to catch you — God, no, not to catch you — but to *see*. To look at the quiet acolyte with the candle-snuffer and realize, with dawning horror, that the girl they hunted across half of Ashmoor ten years ago has been standing in their hallway the whole time, lighting their candles, straightening their banners. To watch their faces do the arithmetic.

I hated that part of myself. I fed it another candle anyway.

The guards finished their argument. One shrugged, shouldered his pike, and started his rotation down the corridor — away from the vault door, thank all the small mercies. The other stayed planted, but turned to watch the procession in the main hall, drawn by the sound of the choir beginning the Binding Hymn, that long grim chant about chains and cleansing that the Guild made everyone sing at festivals. I'd sung it once, at fourteen, before Sef pulled me out of the Guild's outreach program. I'd liked it, then. The melody was genuinely beautiful. That was the trap of it. Everything the Guild built was beautiful on the surface. They understood beauty the way spiders understand silk.

*Ninety seconds,* Nix's voice said in my ear, barely a breath. *Stair clear. Go on my mark.*

I snuffed the candle I'd just lit — waste not — and drifted toward the archive stair, one bored acolyte among dozens, my snuffer swinging at my side like a tiny钝 weapon. The stair wound down into cold stone, torch-lit, the hymn fading above me until the only sound was my own breathing and the tap of my shoes.

Halfway down, I stopped.

There was a niche in the wall. I knew there would be. Ten years ago, running from a Guild searcher who'd nearly caught me in the market — the night I decided, finally and forever, that magic wasn't worth dying for — I'd hidden in that niche while his boots passed close enough to touch. I'd pressed myself into the stone and promised every god I didn't believe in that if I lived, I'd never touch real magic again. And I'd kept that promise. Ten years of charms and trinkets, sleight of hand and superstition, a life built small and safe as a matchbox.

The niche was still there. Of course it was. Stone outlasts promises.

I put my hand flat against it, just for a moment. The rock was cold. It didn't remember me. Why would it?

*Mara.* Wendell's voice, gentle. *Where are you?*

"Taking a moment," I whispered.

Another pause. Then: "Take two. I'll count them for you."

God help me, I almost laughed. Somewhere above me, in a gallery full of Guild dignitaries, a rare-book appraiser with ink on his cuffs was counting seconds for a hedge-witch in a stolen robe, and calling it love, or whatever it was we'd decided not to call it yet because neither of us had time.

*Mark,* Nix said.

I moved.

* * *

The lower corridors were emptier, colder, the architecture shifting from grandeur to function the way a body shifts from face to spine. Here were the ward-rooms, the interrogation chambers with their pleasant names — the Reflection Suite, the Counsel Room — and here, at the end of a hall lined with iron sconces, the vault antechamber.

Nix was already there, crouched in the shadow of a pillar, looking profoundly annoyed.

"Took you long enough," she whispered. "Do you know what I had to do to get past the duty roster? I had to *flirt*. With a *clerk*. I feel unclean."

"You're a professional."

"I'm a *victim*." She produced a strip of black cloth and began winding it around her fingers, cracking each knuckle in turn. "Door's warded. Old work, pre-Guild — they didn't build this, they conquered it. Which means the lock wants a key and the ward wants a name. I have neither. What I have—" she patted her belt, where six thin tools gleamed like fish bones, "—is twenty minutes and a bad attitude."

"The hymn ends in twelve," I said. "When it ends, the processional comes down this corridor. We're out or we're caught."

"Then stop talking and start praying to whatever you hedge-folk pray to."

I knelt beside her at the vault door. Up close, the warding was visible even to my dulled senses — faint silver script crawling over the iron like frost, hungry and patient. It was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Everything the Guild built—

No. Not the Guild's work. This was older. This was *Sef's* generation of craft, maybe Sef's own hand, from the days before his exile, when he'd sold his skill to whoever paid. My mentor's handwriting, written in light, guarding the thing that had cost my brother his soul.

I knew the cadence of it. That was the horrible, wonderful truth: I recognized the rhythm of those wards the way you recognize a lullaby. Sef had taught me ward-craft when I was thirteen, before I understood what he was teaching me, before I understood what he was. *Magic isn't power, little bird,* he used to say. *Power is what other people make of your magic. You just have to decide whose hands it lands in.*

His wards wanted a name. And I knew his naming-cadences the way Nix knew locks.

"Nix," I said slowly. "Give me your knife."

She handed it over without question, which told me how far we'd come, she and I. I pricked my thumb, pressed the blood to the lowest line of script, and whispered the counter-cadence — not a break, a *greeting*, the polite knock of one of Sef's own students. The frost-script shivered. Considered. Parted like a curtain.

Nix stared at me. "Remind me never to make you angry."

"Noted. Work fast."

She worked fast. She worked like water finding cracks, like the dark deciding to be useful, and I watched the door and counted the hymn in my head and tried very hard not to think about the fact that using Sef's craft felt like hearing his voice, and that hearing his voice felt like grief, and that grief was a luxury I'd invoice later.

The lock clicked. The ward sighed shut behind us, seamless.

Inside the vault, on a pedestal of black basalt, under a bell-jar of warded glass, sat half of a stone the size of a fist. Even cut in two, even veiled, it *hummed*. I felt it in my teeth, in the scar on my palm, in the place behind my ribs where my magic had slept for ten years and was now, suddenly, horribly, awake — turning over like something that had heard its name called.

It knew me. The prophecy had known my face before I was born. Of course the stone knew me too.

"Mara." Wendell's voice in my ear, urgent now. "Hymn's ending. Ninety seconds. Take it and *run*."

I reached for the bell-jar.

And the humming changed. Deepened. Became, unmistakably, a voice — not in my ears but in the marrow of me, warm as a hearth, reasonable as a friend:

*You could end the Guild tonight. You could free every soul in the mountain. You could have your brother back whole, and your mentor kneeling, and Ashmoor remade — you've seen it, you know you've seen it. All you have to do is pick me up.*

My hand hovered over the glass. My reflection looked back at me from it, and my reflection was smiling, and I wasn't.

"Mara?" Nix, sharp, real, smelling of pickpocket sweat and clerk-flirting. "Mara, your eyes have gone weird. *Mara.*"

Sixty seconds. The hymn's last verse, floating down through the stone.

I closed my fist around the jar's handle — not touching the stone, not yet, *not yet* — and lifted it free of the pedestal.

Every ward in the Guildhall woke at once.
