## 13. Corvane's Offer

The thing about being hunted through a festival is that everyone smiles at you.

I'd spent the last hour drifting through the Binding Festival with a paper crown some child had pressed on me, a cup of mulled wine I had no intention of drinking, and the particular smile of a man who has a stolen half of a god-stone sewn into his coat lining and would very much like to be anywhere else. Around me, Ashmoor celebrated its chains. Banners of grey and gold hung from every balcony. Children wore little iron cuff charms on ribbons and thought them pretty. Somewhere a band played something jaunty in a minor key, which felt about right for this city.

Mara was somewhere across the square, dressed as a Guild acolyte, walking halls she'd once fled from, wearing another woman's face over her fear. Nix was — wherever Nix went when they said *don't worry about me*, which was always exactly where you should worry about them. And I was supposed to be nobody: Wendell Marsh, rare-book appraiser, tourist, harmless.

I was doing quite well at it until Corvane found me.

He didn't grab my elbow or press a knife to my ribs. That's not how men like Corvane work. He simply appeared beside me at the wine stall, ordered two cups, paid with a coin that gleamed too brightly, and handed me one as if we were old friends who'd arranged to meet.

"Master Marsh." His voice was warm gravel. "Enjoying the festival?"

"Immensely," I said. "The chains are lovely this year."

"They are, aren't they." He sipped his wine and looked out at the crowd with the fond expression of a man watching his own garden grow. Up close, the old guildsman was less a soldier than I remembered from the raid on my shop — more a scholar gone soft around the edges, silver at the temples, ink stains he hadn't bothered to hide on his fingers. A man who read things. That frightened me more than any sword would have. "You strike me as a collector, Master Marsh. A man who appreciates that some objects are wasted in private hands."

"Some objects," I said carefully, "are wasted in anyone's hands."

"Mm." He smiled like I'd passed a test. Or failed one; with Corvane it was hard to tell. "Walk with me."

Every sensible nerve I owned screamed *no*. But Mara was across the square inside a building full of people who wanted her dead, and the last thing she needed was the Guild's second-most-dangerous man making a scene. So I walked. The paper crown sat absurdly on my head. Corvane didn't comment on it, which somehow made it worse.

We drifted along the colonnade at the edge of Founder's Square, past stalls selling candied chestnuts and commemorative shackles — actual souvenirs, tiny cuffs on velvet cushions, because Ashmoor has never met an atrocity it couldn't merchandise. Corvane nodded to guildsmen as we passed. They nodded back and looked at me with mild, professional curiosity, the way dogs look at a stranger carrying meat.

"Do you know what the Binding Festival actually commemorates?" he asked.

"The founding of the Guild," I said. "The sealing of the mountain."

"That's the story we print." He turned a small object over in his fingers — an acolyte's badge, iron and glass. "A hundred years ago, the mages of Ashmoor fought a war against the court beneath the mountain. Souls against sorcery. We won, in the way that matters: we made them afraid to come up. But wars end badly when you don't finish them. The Undercourt never surrendered. It waited. It *collects*." He glanced at me sidelong. "You've seen it. You've been down there."

"I've been to a lot of places," I said. "It's a hazard of owning books. They're full of other places."

"Wendell." He used my first name deliberately, gently, the way you'd steady a spooked horse. "I raided your shop because I had to. Procedure. But I read your appraisals afterward. All of them. Do you know how rare it is, in this city, to find a man who can look at a text of genuine power and describe it honestly? No hysteria. No greed. Just — understanding." He stopped walking. We stood beneath a banner reading GLORY TO THE SEALED GATE, and he gestured up at it as though presenting evidence. "The Guild is full of hammers, Wendell. What it has never had enough of is eyes."

"And you're recruiting eyes."

"I'm offering one a future."

There it was. I took a long drink of wine I didn't want, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "Let me guess the terms."

"You'll enjoy this part less." He resumed walking, and I noticed, with detached horror, that we were heading toward the Guildhall itself — toward the great bronze doors, open today for the festival, spilling golden light and hymn-smoke onto the steps. "Three nights ago, a prophetic text activated in this city for the first time in a century. The Sigil woke. The stone began to sing. My diviners felt it from three districts away — one of them fainted, which I'm told was very undignified." A dry pause. "Since then, half the underworld has been moving, and so have we. We know the stone was split. We know a fragment came out of the mountain. And we know — because we are not fools, whatever you may think of us — that it is currently within fifty feet of me, sewn into the lining of a rather shabby brown coat."

My heart did something acrobatic and unhelpful. I kept my face pleasant, which cost me approximately everything.

"Here's what I'd like you to understand," Corvane continued, conversational as a man discussing weather. "The Guild's plan for the stone is not what you fear. It isn't even what *we* feared, once. For a hundred years the doctrine was destruction — shatter it, bury it, salt the earth. But destruction requires finding all of it, and the other half sits on the brow of the Underqueen herself. So the doctrine changed. Why destroy a key, Wendell, when you can simply decide which doors it opens?" He turned to face me fully, and his eyes were kind, and that was the worst thing about him. "Made whole, the stone doesn't just grant power. It grants *command*. Over souls. Every soul the Undercourt holds — thousands, tens of thousands, taken across a century of quiet bargains — could be called up, bound, and put to work. Healing the sick. Rebuilding the flood-districts. Ending famine. Imagine it. Every lantern in that dark court, lit for *us* instead."

"You're describing slavery," I said.

"I'm describing *stewardship*." His voice didn't rise at all. "They're already enslaved, Wendell. The Underqueen holds them now. She burns them for light and calls it majesty. We would simply... change whose hands held the lamp. And unlike her, we'd let them go home eventually. Amnesties. Terms of service. We've drafted the charters." He actually had charters. I could see it in his face — the sleepless nights over wording, the committees. Evil, in my experience, rarely bothers with grammar. "But none of it works without the Sigil. The stone answers the prophecy's bearer. Not the Guild. Not me. *You.*"

"I'm a book appraiser."

"You're the man the book chose." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to something almost intimate. "And here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so listen. Come inside. Bring the fragment — or don't, tell me where it sleeps, either serves. Stand at my right hand when we make the court whole. You'll have a seat on the Conclave before you're forty. Resources no collector dreams of. The restricted vaults. The sealed libraries — texts older than the Guild, that men have died glimpsing. And protection — real protection — for whoever you name. Your charming hedge-witch friend. The pickpocket. Anyone." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Because the alternative, Wendell, is that we take the stone from you, take the girl, and proceed anyway. I'd rather not. I've read your marginalia. I think you'd be magnificent."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the only luxury I have left after that conversation.

For about four seconds — maybe five — I wanted to say yes.

Not the way you'd expect. Not the vaults, not the Conclave seat, not even the protection, though God knows I thought of Mara first and longest. It was the libraries. It was standing in a room where every book was true, where nothing had been burned or bowdlerized by frightened men, where I could spend a lifetime learning what the world actually was instead of appraising its leftovers for rich idiots. Four seconds of imagining that, and my mouth had already begun composing the yes.

That's the trick of it, I think. Corruption doesn't come for you with claws. It comes dressed as your own longing, holding out the exact shape of your hunger, and says *you were always going to pick this up; I'm merely handing it to you politely.*

Then I thought about whose hands the lamp would be in.

"Charters," I said. "Terms of service. Amnesties." I shook my head slowly. "Corvane, I've catalogued enough confessions to know how this ends. Year one, stewardship. Year five, quotas. Year ten, someone writes a treatise explaining why some souls are more suited to service than others, and it gets assigned reading. You're not wrong that the Underqueen is a monster. You're just a monster with better stationery."

Something flickered behind his kindness — brief, cold, and quickly filed away. "That's a shame."

"It usually is."

"You misunderstand me." He straightened his cuffs. "I meant it's a shame that you believe refusing me is an option you'll get to keep. The offer stands until sundown. After that, the Guild proceeds under the assumption that the Sigil is hostile, and hostile Sigils are handled *firmly*. Think of your friends. Think especially—" and here he smiled again, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which is how I knew he'd done his research, "—of the brother. Tam, isn't it? Still burning in her court. If we hold the stone, we hold him too. Safe. Preserved. Perhaps even restored, in time. Whereas if the witch keeps it..." He spread his hands. "Well. You've seen what she does with power."

"She shattered a curse off a drowning village when she was nineteen," I said. "I've seen exactly what she does with power."

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "So have I. That's rather the problem." He set down his untouched wine on a plinth, precise as punctuation. "Sundown, Wendell. The bronze doors are right there. They're open all festival long. All you have to do is walk through them."

He walked away into the crowd, and the crowd closed around him like water, and I stood alone under the banner with my heart hammering and the stone humming faintly against my ribs — and I realized, with a lurch, that it had been humming the entire time. Listening. Maybe even agreeing.

I tipped the paper crown off my head, dropped it in a gutter, and went to find Mara before sundown — because whatever we decided, we'd have to decide it together, and because somewhere in the back of my mind a new and terrible thought was taking shape:

Corvane hadn't cornered me by accident. He'd let me go.

Which meant either he was confident I'd come back —

or I was already exactly where he wanted me to be.
