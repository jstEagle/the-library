## 14. The Stone in Hand

There's a moment in every bad idea where you can still turn around. I've built a whole life on finding that moment and taking it. Ten years of small, safe Tuesdays, each one a door quietly closed.

I was well past the moment now. I was standing in the Guildhall's reliquary vault, dressed as an acolyte of the very order that would burn me for existing, holding a candle I wasn't allowed to light, and the stone was ten feet away behind glass older than the city.

So. No turning. Only through.

* * *

The Binding Festival roared above us like weather. Through the vault's high slit of a window I could see the lanterns strung across Cathedral Square, thousands of them, each one blessed by a guild mage and each one, if Nix's information held, a tiny anchor in the great chain the Guild was building beneath the mountain. A net for souls. They'd been weaving it for a century and calling it piety.

Down here there were no lanterns. Just me, the smell of cold stone and lamp oil, and the sound of my own heart doing something undignified against my ribs.

The plan had been simple, which should have been my first warning. Simple plans are how the universe sorts the arrogant from the lucky. Wendell would trigger the vault's outer ward by presenting his guild seal — Corvane had given him one, which still made my skin crawl, that man handing out trust like poisoned candy — and while the ward chewed on the seal's authority, I would slip through the maintenance corridor Nix had mapped from a bribed stonemason's memory. Take the stone. Walk out. Be home by midnight.

Nix had said, "Nothing in this building is guarded by fewer than three lies," and then grinned like that was charming instead of terrifying.

Wendell's voice came soft through the earpiece charm — a bead of wax and whispered words pressed behind my ear, warm as a fingertip. "Outer ward's accepting the seal. Mara, say something so I know you're breathing."

"I'm breathing."

"Good. Breathe slower. You sound like a kettle."

"That's love, Wendell."

"It's hyperventilation with good intentions. Go."

* * *

The maintenance corridor was exactly as wide as my shoulders and no wider, which felt less like architecture and more like commentary. I moved by touch, counting paces the way Sef taught me — before he became a name I spat, back when counting paces was just a thing a teacher did with a child in a marsh, both of them believing the world could be navigated if you paid attention.

I hated that the counting still worked. I hated that everything he taught me still worked, that he was stitched into my hands whether I liked it or not.

Forty paces. The wall opened onto the reliquary antechamber, and there it was.

The vault proper was smaller than I'd imagined. All that dread, all those wards, all that festival noise above to drown out what they kept below — and it fit in a room the size of my kitchen. One pedestal. One case of black glass. One stone.

Half a stone, really. A jagged shard the length of my thumb, grey as grave-dirt, sitting on velvet like it was asleep. It didn't glow. It didn't hum. It looked like nothing at all, and that was somehow worse. Things that announce their power you can avoid. Things that look like nothing get into your pockets, your blood, your Tuesday nights.

The case was warded — I could feel it, a flat pressure against my senses like a hand held up, saying *not yet, not yet*. But the ward was keyed to guild authority, and Wendell's borrowed seal had cracked its knees. I pressed two fingers to the glass and spoke the counter-phrase Nix had bought from a disgraced warder, and the black glass sighed open like a door deciding, finally, to stop pretending.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like temptation. It arrives looking like relief.

I picked up the stone.

* * *

The world went white, then went true.

That's the only way I can describe it. Everything I had ever seen dimly — the threads under things, the seams in the air, the way magic moves through the world like water through cloth — snapped into focus so hard it hurt. I could see the wards on the walls, great slow animals of light, sleeping. I could see the festival above, every lantern a knot of gathered warmth, and beneath them, far beneath, the faint gold filaments of bound souls trailing down through rock and dark toward the Undercourt, hundreds of them, thousands, a root system of stolen light.

And I could see the shape of everything. That was the terrible part. Not glimpses. Not hints. The whole architecture of what could be, laid out like a map someone had drawn just for me.

I saw Ashmoor without the Guild. I saw the guilds' towers unmade stone by stone, gently, the way a wave unmakes a sandcastle, and the people who'd lived in fear of mages living instead beside them — wary, then easy, then laughing. I saw the Undercourt's lanterns go out one by one because they weren't needed anymore; the souls inside them walking up the long stair into daylight, blinking, weeping, forgiven.

I saw Tam.

Not hollow-eyed Tam, not the lantern-wick ghost I'd glimpsed in the Underqueen's hall. Tam at fourteen, sunburnt, furious, alive, turning to yell at me across a marsh creek — and then Tam grown, the years returned to him, a man I'd never met wearing my brother's face, and he was smiling at me, and he said my name—

*Mara.*

It wasn't a memory. It was an offer. The stone was showing me the price list, and the price was nothing, the price was just *yes*, just take me, just hold on, and everything broken gets mended, everyone sold gets freed, every wall you ever built becomes a garden—

"You could remake it all," I whispered, and my voice sounded wrong, layered, like the prophecy itself had learned to speak through my mouth. "You could fix everything."

Could I? That was the question, wasn't it. And underneath it, quieter, the question under the question: *who says fixed means what you think it means?*

Because I could see further now too. I saw what remaking cost. To mend the world with this thing I'd have to decide what mended meant — and my decision would be law, written into the grain of everything, forever. Every person I saved, saved *my way*. Every enemy unmade according to *my* judgment. I thought of the Underqueen, ancient and patient and utterly certain, and understood suddenly, horribly, that she hadn't started cruel. She'd started certain. Certainty was the first thing the stone ate. Cruelty was just what it left behind.

I thought of Sef, who'd tried to save me from this exact moment and done it in the worst possible way, selling a boy's soul to buy a girl's future — and hadn't that been the same arithmetic? *One soul weighed against the world.* He'd held the scale. He'd been sure. Look what it made him.

"No," I said.

The stone pulsed, gentle, reasonable. *Yes,* it said, in the voice of every wish I'd ever swallowed. *Yes, but—*

"No." My fingers wouldn't open. That was the part I hadn't planned for, the part no plan survives: the stone wasn't gripping me. I was gripping it. Some traitorous handful of me — the tired, frightened, ten-years-lonely handful — did not want to let go. "Wendell," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle. "Wendell, I need you here *now*."

* * *

He came fast. I heard him before I saw him — boots on stone, the ward-animals above us stirring, waking, growling low frequencies I could suddenly perceive in colors that don't have names. Then he was in the doorway, and whatever was happening to my face stopped him dead.

"Mara?"

"It won't let go." Laughable. Pathetic. True. "It's showing me — Wendell, it's showing me everything fixed. Tam. Everyone. It would be so *easy*—"

"Put it down."

"If I put it down, the Guild—" The vision flared, generous, helpful: the Guild finding the stone, Corvane's smooth face bending over it, the net of lanterns drawn tight over the world. "They'll use it for chains. I can use it for doors. Don't you see? I'd be *careful*—"

Everyone says that. Every tyrant in history has said that, probably, right up until the moment careful stopped being an option and convenient took its place.

Wendell crossed the room and took my face in his hands. His palms were shaking. Good, I thought distantly. Someone should be scared.

"Mara Lockridge." He said it like a spell, and maybe it was one; maybe names are the only magic that never gets outlawed. "Ten years ago you walked away from power because you knew, even then, what it wanted from you. You were right then. Are you going to tell me you're wrong now?"

"The stone showed me you," I said, and hated myself for it. "It showed me a life where nothing hurts you."

"It showed you a lie with your favorite things in it." His thumb traced my cheekbone, over the scar the old vellum had known before he ever met me. "I don't want a life where nothing hurts. I want one where you're in it and *you're* in it. Both of you. The real ones."

The stone surged, furious now, flooding my arm with cold fire, whispering faster, offering more, offering Wendell young and old and safe, offering my mother back, offering the marsh green again, offering, offering —

And in the middle of the flood, one image surfaced that wasn't the stone's at all. It was mine. It was last week, in the shop that didn't exist yet, Nix teaching Wendell to pick a lock badly, both of them laughing, no power anywhere in the room, no destiny, no prophecy — just three people who had chosen each other in a world that hadn't offered them anything worth having except each other.

The stone couldn't show me that. It only dealt in what could be given. It had nothing to sell me about what I'd already chosen.

My fingers opened.

"Wendell — catch—"

He caught it. Of course he did. He's the Sigil; catching is half the job description. The shard hit his palm and the whole vault *rang*, a bell-note through the floor, and every sleeping ward-animal in the walls opened its eyes at once.

But I was empty. Cold and clean and empty, kneeling on the vault floor with my hands open like a beggar's, and the silence where the vision had been was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

For one heartbeat I mourned it. Let me be honest, since honesty is the only payment I've got left: for one full heartbeat I grieved that map of mended things, and I hated Wendell for taking it, and I loved him for taking it, and both of those were true in the same breath, the way most true things are.

Then the alarms started.

* * *

"Up," Wendell said, hauling me to my feet, the stone vanishing into the lead-lined pouch at his belt. "Mara. Up. We are leaving."

We ran. The corridor was exactly as wide as panic required, which is narrower than hope. Behind us the vault's wards woke fully, and I felt them reach — hungry, official, *guild* — and find our scent.

Above, the festival music faltered. Just for a measure. Then it resumed, louder, the way crowds sing when they've decided not to notice something.

"Rendezvous," I gasped. "East cistern. Where's Nix?"

"Nix went out the north gallery five minutes ago. Should already be—" Wendell's earpiece charm crackled. We both stopped. From the little bead of wax, thin and distorted, came Nix's voice, and Nix never sounded like that, Nix sounded like fun even when bleeding:

"—got company, they knew, they *knew*, don't come north, don't—"

Static. Then, faintly, someone else's voice on the line. Smooth. Unhurried. Almost kind.

Corvane.

"Well," he said, to whoever was listening, and I felt the words land in my chest like a hook. "Now we know which of them carries it."

* * *
