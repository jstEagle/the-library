## 15. Nix's Price

Here is the thing nobody tells you about selling people out: it's easy. That's the horror of it, really. You'd think there'd be more ceremony. Some moment where you stand at a crossroads and hear a devil whispering in each ear. But no. It happens in a cellar over bad tea, and the man across from you has kind eyes, and he says *pardon* like it's a small word, like it doesn't weigh anything at all.

Corvane had kind eyes. I want that on the record. Whatever else gets said about me after this — and something tells me things will get said — I want it known that I didn't sell out my friends to a monster with a face like a hatchet. I sold them out to a grandfatherly old man who poured the tea himself and remembered my mother's name.

That last part should have been the warning. Nobody remembers your mother's name for free.

* * *

Let me back up. Three days before the Binding Festival, I sat across from Corvane in a Guild safehouse that smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and I told him where Mara Lockridge would be on festival night, and what she was wearing, and which door she'd come through.

I told myself it wasn't really betrayal. That's the other thing about selling people out: you become a philosopher. I told myself that the pardon covered me only — that Wendell and Mara were grown adults with a whole prophecy's worth of agency, and if they got caught it was because they were doing something illegal, which they were, technically, in fairness. I told myself that Nix of Ashmoor had survived ten years in the under-city by never being the last rat on a sinking ship, and this ship had been taking on water since the night a book ate two people at a crossroads.

Corvane listened to all of it with his hands folded, nodding along like I was telling him about weather. When I finished he said, "And the stone?"

"The stone's the stone," I said. "Half of it's under your floor already. They're going to take it during the festival, when every acolyte in the building is busy chaining up the year."

"And you'll be where?"

"Elsewhere." I met his eyes, because you have to, in negotiations. The moment you look down, the price goes up. "Gone. Out of Ashmoor by morning. That was the arrangement."

"That was the arrangement," Corvane agreed, and he smiled, and I swear to every god whose name I've ever stolen, it reached his eyes. That's what did it. Not the tea, not the pardon papers with their ribbons and seals — though those were lovely, I held them, I read them twice — but the smile that actually reached his eyes. Liars smile with their mouths. I know. I've made a career of it.

He slid the papers across the table. Full pardon. All prior offenses remitted. Signed by the Guildmaster's own hand, or a very good copy of it, and I've seen enough forged signatures to know the difference, and this one was real.

"Welcome back into the light, child," Corvane said.

I walked out of that cellar feeling clean for the first time in years, which should also have been a warning. Clean is not a feeling I'm built for. Clean sits wrong on me, like a rich man's coat.

* * *

The plan was simple, and plans that simple are always someone else's plan, and you're just the part that breaks.

Mara would walk into the Guildhall as an acolyte — she had the face for it, apparently, some history I still hadn't gotten the whole of — while Wendell played a nervous rare-book appraiser delivering festival texts, which required no acting whatsoever. Nix would handle the locks, the guards' rotation, and the little problem of the ward-seal on the vault door, which was keyed to blood and could be fooled with the right borrowed name. I deal in stolen names. It's the family business, if pickpockets can be said to have a family business, and mine can, because I invented it.

We spent the night before the festival in the smuggler's loft above the stair-that-isn't-there, eating stolen pastries and arguing about whether Wendell could pass for a Guild clerk. He could not. He had the posture of a man apologizing to furniture. But Mara vouched for him with that stubborn set to her jaw she gets, and I kept my mouth shut about the cellar and the tea and the papers folded against my ribs, and I laughed at the right moments, and I hated myself in a very efficient, professional way that left plenty of time for pastry.

Wendell caught me looking at him once. Just once. He was mid-argument, flour on his sleeve, gesturing with half a custard bun, and he looked at me the way he looks at everything — like the world was a book he genuinely wanted to finish — and something in my chest did a thing I didn't authorize.

"You're quiet," he said.

"I'm always quiet. Quiet's the job."

"No," he said, "you're quiet like you're counting something."

I gave him my best grin, the one that's cost three city guards their purses and one magistrate his reputation. "Counting the hours till we're rich and infamous."

He laughed. Mara rolled her eyes. And I sat there with a pardon in my coat and a lie in my mouth and thought: *one more day. One more day and then they catch them fair and square, with no help from me beyond the ordinary treachery of knowing them, and I walk away clean, and nobody bleeds.*

You can tell yourself anything at two in the morning over stolen pastry. That's why they invented dawn.

* * *

Dawn came, and with it, four Guild wardens and a net woven with null-silver thread.

They took me in the market, buying apples, which felt pointed. A warden put his hand on my shoulder almost gently, the way you'd steady a child, and said, "Nix? There's a gentleman wants a word," and I knew before my feet left the ground. Kind eyes. Grandfatherly voice. Nobody remembers your mother's name for free.

They didn't drag me. That was the worst part. They walked me, unhurried, through streets full of festival bunting, past stalls hung with paper chains, while children ran by with painted faces and somebody's grandmother pressed a luck-charm into my cuffed hands — a real one, hedge-made, warm as a heartbeat — and said, "For the festival, dearie, everyone needs a bit of luck today." I held that little charm all the way to the Guildhall. I'm holding it now, or I was. Things are slightly on fire around me at the moment; I'll explain.

They put me in a room with good light. That's how you know it's serious. Torture happens in the dark; persuasion happens where the prisoner can see the sky they're about to lose.

Corvane was waiting with tea again. Two cups.

"Nix," he said warmly. "Sit."

"Where's my pardon?" I said. "Because I delivered. Names, faces, doors, the whole inventory. I sang like a choir of bribed canaries."

"You did," he agreed, pouring. "Beautifully. Every word confirmed. Your friends walked into our halls an hour ago, exactly as you described." He handed me a cup. I didn't take it. "Which is precisely why there is no pardon."

I want to say the floor dropped out of my stomach, but that's too gentle. Floors drop once. This was slower. It was the feeling of watching yourself from a great height and thinking, *oh, there I am, being the fool in the story.*

"Explain," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"A pardon requires a crime to pardon," Corvane said, settling back. "But you see, whatever you did before this week — the thefts, the names, the charming little trade of yours — none of that ever happened. Officially. There are no records, Nix. There never were. A person without records cannot be pardoned, because a person without records does not exist." He smiled, and this time it stayed politely above his eyes. "You're not a citizen receiving mercy. You're a tool that has served its purpose. Tools don't need pardons. They need disposal."

"The papers—"

"Were real. Read the name on them again when you get a chance. Whose name did you give me, that first day, when I asked who you were?" He waited. He had all the time in the world and he knew it. "You gave me your mother's name. The name you were born under, the one you haven't used since you were nine years old, because I asked so kindly and you wanted so badly to be forgiven. So the pardon is real, and it belongs to a girl who died in a fire in Mill Row twelve years ago. Legally speaking, I am currently having tea with no one."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, faintly, the festival drums had started.

"So here is what happens now," Corvane continued, pleasant as a sermon. "Your friends will take the stone-half, because I want them to. It's bait, child. The whole prophecy is a fishing line, and the Guild has been holding the rod for a hundred years. Let them assemble the halves. Let them carry it down to the Undercourt. And when the stone is whole, we take it — from them, from the Underqueen, from whoever's hand it's in — and every soul in that mountain becomes Guild property. Lanterns don't get opinions." He leaned forward. "You, however, are a loose thread. And I do apologize, because I genuinely enjoyed our conversations. You remind me of my daughter."

"You don't have a daughter," I said.

"Not anymore," he agreed, and rang a small bell.

* * *

Here's another thing nobody tells you: null-silver burns. Not the skin — the self. They wrapped my wrists in it and marched me down toward the cells, and with every step my own name got quieter in my head. Nix. Nix. Like calling down a well and hearing less echo each time. By the stairs I couldn't remember why I'd stolen my first purse. By the corridor I couldn't remember my mother's face, only that Corvane had known her name, and that he'd used it like a key.

They put me in a cell with no records attached. Of course they did. You can't imprison a person who doesn't exist; you can only store them.

I sat on the cot and I did the arithmetic of my life, which is short and mostly theft, and I found — this surprised me — that I wasn't crying about the pardon. Pardons are paper. Paper's lied to me before; I've forged enough of it. What hurt was the pastry. What hurt was Wendell with flour on his sleeve saying *you're quiet like you're counting something,* because he'd seen me, actually seen me, the way nobody had since I was nine, and I had sold the sight of him for a cup of tea and a smile that reached his eyes.

And then I thought: *bait.* He'd said it out loud, to a dead girl with no records. Which meant Mara and Wendell were walking into a trap built around the exact plan I'd sold, which meant every detail I'd given was a nail in it, which meant —

Which meant I was the only person alive who knew the shape of the trap. Because I'd built it.

Well. Me and the grandfather.

I took out the luck-charm the grandmother had pressed on me. Null-silver on my wrists, but they'd missed the charm tucked in my cuff — sloppy, festival-day sloppy, everyone's eyes on the stone. Hedge-magic. Mara's kind of magic, the small warm kind, the kind the Guild calls worthless. It was still warm. Ten years in the trade teaches you what warmth means in a charm: it means it's *on*. Working.

It was a luck-charm. Luck is just probability leaning your way. And probability, in a Guild cell on festival morning, with every warden in the building staring at the vault, leaned very hard indeed when the lock on my door — old, overworked, maintained by a budget Corvane had clearly redirected into tea services — jammed, then clicked, then swung open on its own, because a hinge pin had rusted through at precisely the moment I needed it to.

I stood in the doorway of my cell and laughed until I cried, which took about four seconds, and then I ran.

* * *

I won't pretend the escape was elegant. I stole a warden's cloak off a hook, which is not a metaphor, it was literally on a hook, unattended, festival-day sloppy again. I stole a ring of keys off a boy who'll hopefully learn from this. I stole a name off a passing clerk — "Bramwell, third floor, records division" — and used it twice to talk my way past checkpoints, and I felt the name fray a little each time, wearing out like cheap coin, and I thought: *this is what Corvane did to me. Wore my name out. Spent it.* Well, Bramwell, wherever you are, I hope you appreciate being useful.

The hard part wasn't getting out. The hard part was the choice in the courtyard.

There's a gate on the east side of the Guildhall, festival-day, half-guarded, opening onto a street that runs downhill to the river, and from the river a body like mine can be anywhere in the world by midnight. Gone. Records-free, name-free, debt-free. The life I'd traded my friends for, handed back to me by a rusted hinge and a dead grandmother's charm. One gate between me and clean.

Below me, through the courtyard arches, I could see the procession forming for the Binding rite. And near the west colonnade, being escorted with courteous iron attention toward the vault stair, two figures: a nervous appraiser with terrible posture, and a woman in acolyte grey walking like she owned the corridor and hated every inch of it. They didn't know. They were walking straight down the throat of the trap, smiling at their guides, carrying the plan I'd sold like it was theirs.

Corvane's voice in my head, pleasant as tea: *let them assemble the halves.*

The gate was thirty steps away. The colonnade was forty.

I counted them anyway. Old habit. Then I stopped counting, turned my back on the river and the clean and the gone, and ran toward the two people I had betrayed, shouting "BRAMWELL" at the top of my lungs because it was the only name in my head and it seemed important to use it while I still had one.

A warden grabbed my cloak. I let him have the cloak. Underneath, I'm faster than I look, and I look fast.

I hit the colonnade at a dead sprint, took Wendell's arm, took Mara's arm, and said, very calmly, considering: "Don't go down those stairs. It's bait. The whole thing's bait. I sold the plan and they rebuilt it around you, and the stone-whole goes to the Guild, and every soul in the mountain ends up in a lantern, and I am — " my voice cracked, which was inconvenient, " — I am so sorry, Mara. I took tea with him. I took *tea.*"

Mara looked at me. Wendell looked at me. Behind us, the shout went up — prisoner loose, seal the hall, bells starting to clang the festival alarm — and ahead of us, the only way out was through the vault corridor itself, past the very door we'd come to rob, into the dark under the mountain where the second half of the stone was waiting with the patience of a hundred years.

Mara's jaw did the stubborn thing.

"Well," she said, gripping my wrist hard enough to bruise, right over the null-silver burn. "Then we'd better steal it before they can spring the trap. Both halves. Tonight."

The bells were ringing. The drums had stopped. And somewhere beneath our feet, deep in the dark, something old heard the word *both* and began, very softly, to sing.
