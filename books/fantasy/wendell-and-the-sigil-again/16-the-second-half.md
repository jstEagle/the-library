## 16. The Second Half

The stone-half woke up three days after the Guildhall job, and it did it the way everything in my life had learned to do things lately: loudly, at an inconvenient hour, and with a strong preference for ruining my sleep.

I'd wrapped it in three layers of wool and one of Wendell's spare shirts — the blue one, because I'd decided that if the thing was going to hum like a hive of angry bees, it could at least do it in a color I liked — and buried it in the bottom of Nix's flour barrel in the safehouse above the tannery. The tannery smell was supposed to mask the stone's signature from Guild seekers. It also masked everything else, including, briefly, my will to live, but nobody said sanctuary came scented.

At the hour when even the rats consider sleeping in, the barrel started to sing.

Not sing, exactly. Resonate. That's the word Sef would have used, back when I still took vocabulary lessons from a man who thought "apology" was a unit of currency he didn't deal in. The sound came up through the floorboards like a note held under water, and I lay in my cot and felt it in my teeth, in the scar over my eyebrow, in the place behind my sternum where my magic used to live before I'd poured every drop of it into breaking a god-stone in half.

Wait. No. That comes later. Forgive me — the order of events has gotten slippery since I started narrating my own catastrophe. Three days after the job, the stone sang, and I hadn't broken anything yet except my own heart's previous record for bad decisions.

I went downstairs. Of course I went downstairs. You can leave a stone in a flour barrel, but you can't leave it alone, and neither, apparently, can it.

* * *

Nix was already there, sitting on the barrel with her boots dangling, looking like a cat who'd found the cream and then discovered the cream could talk.

"It started an hour ago," she said. "I figured if it exploded, you'd want to be asleep for it."

"Considerate."

"I'm a very considerate person. Ask anyone whose pockets I've picked." She slid off the barrel and knocked on its stave twice, the way you'd knock on a door. "It's not just humming. It's humming *toward* something. Feel."

I didn't want to feel. Feeling was how this whole mess had started — feeling the prophecy's ink crawl, feeling Tam's name in the margins of old vellum, feeling the stone-half flood me with a vision of remade worlds when I'd held it for four seconds in Corvane's vault. Four seconds. I'd seen the city rebuilt in light, seen the Guild on its knees, seen myself crowned in something that wasn't quite gold and wasn't quite fire, and I'd wanted it so badly that my hands had shaken when Wendell pried the stone out of my fingers.

Four seconds. I still caught myself thinking about it in the bath. In the dark. When the tannery smell got into everything and I couldn't tell which thoughts were mine.

So no, I didn't want to feel. But I put my palm flat against the barrel anyway, because I was Mara Lockridge, and my defining trait — after cowardice, stubbornness, and a truly regrettable talent for self-justification — was doing the thing I least wanted to do.

The hum climbed through my arm and settled behind my eyes, and there it was: a direction. Not a sound anymore but a pull, faint and patient, like a fisherman's line gone taut somewhere far downstream. It pointed down. Down through the floor, through the smuggler's stair, through the mountain's roots, toward the Undercourt.

Toward the Underqueen's crown.

"Oh," I said.

"Yeah," said Nix.

"We should wake Wendell."

"He's already awake. He's been awake since it started. He's making tea about it." She paused. "He makes tea when he's frightened. Did you know that? He made tea during the Guildhall job. Mid-heist. There was a whole pot cooling on a windowsill while we were hiding from acolytes."

"I know," I said. "It's one of the ten thousand things I love about him."

Nix looked at me sideways, and for once she didn't have a joke loaded. Ten years of picking pockets teaches you to read people, and what she read off me just then must have been plain as a ledger, because she said, quietly, "You're going to go down there, aren't you. Even knowing it's exactly what the book wants."

The book. The prophecy. The century-old vellum that had swallowed us once already, that had named Wendell the Sigil, that had been written — we now knew — by the first soul the Underqueen ever took, a dead woman's hand reaching across a hundred years to arrange her own liberation or her own revenge or both. Every step we'd taken since the night market had landed precisely where the ink said it would. I'd told myself we were choosing. I was running out of ways to believe that.

"Tam's down there," I said. Which wasn't an answer. It was the answer I hid inside when real answers got too heavy to carry.

* * *

Wendell's tea ritual is a liturgy. He warms the pot first. He measures the leaves like a man defusing something. He lets it steep for exactly the time it takes him to say one full sentence about his feelings, which means the tea is always slightly over-steeped, because Wendell's feelings take a while.

He set a cup in front of me without asking what I wanted, because he knew, and sat across the safehouse table with his own, and for a moment we just drank tea in a room that smelled of tanneries and flour and fear, two people pretending the third presence in the room wasn't a shard of a god singing through the floor.

"The resonance strengthened overnight," he said. "Roughly thirty percent, by ear. I've been keeping notes." He had. There was a notebook. Of course there was a notebook; the man appraised rare books for a living, and I had come to understand that Wendell metabolized terror through documentation. "And it's directional. It points—"

"Down," I said.

"Down," he agreed. "Mara. Say it plainly. I need to hear someone say it plainly."

I set down the cup. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd have bet money on shaking.

"The other half of the stone is the jewel in the Underqueen's crown. The halves want each other. They've wanted each other for a hundred years, and now that one half has been carried out of a vault and into the open air, they're calling across the distance like — like Tam's name in the margins, like everything else in this cursed story. And the only way to free my brother is to bring them together. In her court. On her ground. At the exact moment and in the exact manner the prophecy has been steering us toward since the beginning."

There. Plainly. It hung in the air between us, ugly and true.

Wendell turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit he had when assembling courage. "Then we shouldn't go."

"No," I agreed.

"We should find another way. Sef might know one. Or the freed souls — some of them were courtiers once, they might know the vaults, the rituals, some back door—"

"Wendell." I reached across and covered his hand. His knuckles were cold. Tea never fixes anything; it just gives your hands something honest to hold. "Sef doesn't know another way. If he did, he'd have traded it to me instead of his confession. And the souls don't remember their own names half the time, let alone the architecture of hell. There is no other way. There's only the way the book wrote, and the question is whether we walk it as puppets or walk it with our eyes open."

"And if our eyes open is exactly what the book wants too?" His voice cracked on *too*, and I loved him so much in that moment that it physically hurt, like a charm worked too hard. "If the whole point — if the author designed it so that the ones who see the trap walk into it *knowingly*, because that's the flavor of suffering the stone feeds on—"

"Then we'll be the best-informed fools in the history of prophecy," I said. "We'll go in knowing. We'll go in together. And when the moment comes — whatever moment the ink has planned — we'll do the one thing no prophecy can fully script."

"Which is?"

"Refuse beautifully."

He laughed. It was a wet, wrecked laugh, but it was a laugh, and under the table his hand turned over and gripped mine like a man holding a rope over a long fall.

* * *

We spent two days preparing, which is to say we spent two days discovering there was nothing to prepare with.

Nix went out into Ashmoor's gray morning and came back with intelligence the way she came back with everything — effortlessly, illegally, and slightly damp. The Undercourt's gates had closed since the Guildhall job. Corvane's fall had left the surface Guild leaderless and paranoid, and the Underqueen had sealed her stair against raiders from above, which meant our descent would not be a matter of bribing a smuggler this time. We would have to go the way the stone pulled: through the deep door beneath the tannery district, the one the old maps called the Rootway, the one even Nix's contacts crossed themselves about.

"There's a story," Nix said, spreading a stolen chart on the table and weighting its corners with teacups. "About the Rootway. That it isn't a road so much as a throat. That things go down it easy and come up changed, if they come up at all."

"Cheerful," I said.

"I haven't gotten to the good part. The good part is that the story says the throat swallows loudest for those who carry pieces of the First Bargain." She looked at the flour barrel. "Which, hi. Hello. That's us."

Wendell wrote *Rootway — throat? verify* in his notebook. I have never loved a man more than in that moment of doomed bibliography.

But underneath the planning — the routes and rations and Nix's cheerful inventory of everyone who wanted us dead — something else was happening to me, and I want to be honest about it, because honesty is the one charm I have left that actually works.

The stone was talking to me.

Not in words. In wants. All day, wherever I stood in the safehouse, I could feel the direction of it like a compass needle behind my ribs, and the needle didn't just point down — it pointed *home*. That's the filthiest trick of it. The resonance felt like belonging. Like the missing half of a sentence. Like my brother's hand in mine on the road to Sef's cottage ten years ago, before everything. The stone had reached into my grief and put on its shape, and some traitorous part of me leaned toward it the way a cold person leans toward a fire, knowing the fire eats houses.

I caught myself standing at the top of the Rootway stair on the second evening, coat on, no memory of deciding to put it on.

That's when I understood the trap completely. Not the trap the prophecy had built — the trap I'd built, brick by brick, out of guilt. Because here is the arithmetic of my heart: Tam's soul hangs in the Underqueen's lantern-court, hollow-eyed and ten years gone, and the price of him is the joining of the stone, and the joining of the stone is the coronation of whoever holds it, and the prophecy has arranged matters so that the only way I can save my brother is to become the exact thing I swore on the night market's cold cobbles I would never be.

They say the Underqueen was once a hedge-witch. They say she walked into the mountain carrying a bargain and never walked out the same. I used to think that was a warning meant for the powerful.

It's a warning meant for people exactly like me.

* * *

We left before dawn on the third day, because dawn seemed presumptuous.

Nix led, because Nix always leads and simply permits the rest of us to believe otherwise. Wendell carried the stone in its wool wrappings against his chest, in a sling I'd sewn, because we had decided — after debate, after my four-second vision, after Wendell watched me watch the barrel with an expression he later described in his notebook as *"the look of a woman reading a letter she already knows is bad news"* — that I should not touch it again until the moment demanded it. He carried the Sigil's burden literally now. He'd stopped flinching from the title somewhere on the march beneath the mountain the first time, and I hadn't asked what it cost him, because I was afraid the answer would be *nothing*, and then afraid the answer would be *everything*.

The Rootway mouth opened in the foundation of a collapsed granary, a doorway shaped like nothing — no frame, no lintel, just an absence in the stone with cold air breathing out of it. Old ward-marks ringed the threshold, worn to ghosts. Someone had once forbidden passage here with great ceremony, and time had rubbed its thumb across the prohibition until only the wish remained.

The stone sang louder as we stood before it. Through the wool, through Wendell's coat, through everything, it sang, and far below — miles below, I'd have sworn it — something sang back.

"There it is," Nix said softly. "The other half. Crown-jewel of the Queen of Hungry Things." She spat, ceremonially, to the side. "You know what the oldest thieves' saying in Ashmoor is? Never steal what wants to be stolen."

"This isn't stealing," I said. "This is returning stolen goods."

"To the most dangerous fence in creation. Voluntarily. Down a throat." She grinned, and it was her real grin, the one she wore when the odds were worst, which I had learned to read as her bravest face. "Well. I've had worse business plans. I once tried to sell a Guild captain his own signet ring."

I looked at Wendell. He looked at me. Somewhere below us, my brother's soul burned in a lantern, and a dead woman's ink waited with its pen poised, and a queen with a hundred years of hunger sat on a throne wearing half of a god.

"Mara," Wendell said quietly. "Whatever happens down there — whatever the stone shows you, whatever the Queen offers, whatever the prophecy has written in its margins about this exact hour — remember the Tuesday. Remember the night market. Remember that before any of this, you chose me. Nobody wrote that. No vellum, no bargain, no stone. That one was ours."

I kissed him, because some arguments are best settled without words, and because if the Rootway was a throat, I wanted it swallowing the taste of that kiss along with us — evidence, tucked into the mountain's belly, that we had been real before we were characters.

Then I stepped through the absence in the stone, and the dark took us down, and the singing rose around us like water rising in a lock, note upon note upon note, until I could no longer tell whether the song was the stone's, or the mountain's, or mine —

— and far ahead, at the bottom of all that dark, a light kindled that was not a lantern, and was not a star, and was waiting for us with the patience of a hundred years.

The trap did not snap shut.

That's the terrible part. It never snaps. It just closes, gently, the way a book does — page by page by page — and we were walking down into the binding.
