## 17. March Beneath the Mountain

I have never been brave. I want to be clear about that up front, because what follows might read like bravery, and I'd hate for anyone to get the wrong impression of me. What I am is a man who appraises rare books for a living — a man whose entire professional life has been built on the principle that you look closely at dangerous things before you touch them, and then, ideally, you don't.

But there comes a point in every appraisal when the item in question must be handled. And so, on the morning we marched beneath the mountain, I handled it.

* * *

The smuggler's door Nix had shown us months ago — a door that wasn't a door, a stair that wasn't a stair — stood open now. That was the strangest part. For our first descent we had crept, bribed, and lied our way down into the dark. This time the way was lit with lanterns, and people were waiting by them.

"They heard," Nix said. She stood at the threshold with her arms crossed, wearing her good coat, which is to say the coat with only one knife hidden in it. "Word travels fast under the mountain. Faster than it does up top. Somebody freed a lantern-soul in the Underqueen's own court, and now every outcast from here to the deep galleries is asking whether it's true."

"Whether what's true?" Mara asked.

Nix looked at her the way she always did when Mara asked a question that seemed obvious to everyone else — half fondness, half exasperation, like a cat regarding a dog that has failed to catch its own tail. "Whether the Sigil's real. Whether he can open doors." She jerked her chin at me. "Him."

"I'm not a weapon," I said. It came out more petulant than I intended.

"No," Nix agreed. "You're worse. You're a key. Weapons they know how to fight. A key just opens things, and nobody knows what's going to come through until it does."

I thought about that all day, actually. I'm still thinking about it now, if I'm honest, which is a thing about me: I turn problems over like pages, looking for watermarks, looking for the printer's error, looking for the truth underneath the binding. And the truth underneath this particular binding was that Nix was right, and that being a key frightened me more than being a sword ever could. A sword decides. A key only permits. Everything I opened, I would be responsible for whatever walked through.

* * *

The first of them was waiting at the third lantern down.

She was old — old the way stone is old, not the way people are — with a face like a riverbed and hands wrapped in grey bandages. When she saw Wendell — when she saw *me*, I mean, I keep forgetting I'm writing about myself; old habit from cataloguing other people's lives — she knelt. Right there on the smuggler's stair, in the muck, she went down on both knees and pressed her bandaged forehead to the ground.

"Don't," I said, uselessly.

"The lantern-wardens burned my name off forty years ago," she said to the floor. "I've been no-one since. If you're the key, key, open the door and let me be someone again."

Mara crouched beside her. I watched my girlfriend — a word that still startled me, even now, even after everything; somewhere between the prophecy and the Guildhall job I had apparently acquired a girlfriend, a pickpocket, a marsh-witch, and a destiny, none of which I had ever put on an inventory — I watched Mara take the woman's bandaged hand and say, gently, "What's your name? Your real one?"

"Bethel," the woman whispered, as though it might be stolen back for saying aloud.

"Then walk with us, Bethel," Mara said. "You don't kneel to anyone. Not anymore."

Bethel rose. And I swear on every rare book I have ever appraised, she stood straighter than any person I had seen above ground or below. That was the moment I understood how this march was going to go. Not as an army. As a procession of the unnamed, gathering names as they came.

* * *

By the second gallery we were thirty. By the third, eighty.

They came out of side-tunnels and forgotten service stairs, out of cracks in the mountain I hadn't known existed. A lamplighter whose light had been taxed out of him. Twin sisters who had been bound as living ledgers, their skin inked with debts that weren't theirs. A boy of maybe fourteen who had been born below and had never once seen the sky, who walked beside me for an hour without speaking and then finally said, "Is it true it moves? The sky. Is it true it *moves*?"

"It moves," I told him. "It's very unreliable. You'll love it."

His laugh cracked in the middle like a young bird's voice, and something in my chest cracked along with it.

Here is what nobody tells you about being looked at as a symbol: it's unbearable. Every eye that found me on that road expected something. Hope, mostly, which is heavier than hatred, if you want to know. Hatred you can set down. Hope gets handed to you and then you're carrying it, and it multiplies, and soon you're eighty people's hope and then two hundred, and I am a book appraiser from Ashmoor whose greatest prior achievement was correctly identifying a forged Aldermere first edition, and I want it noted that I did not ask for any of this.

And here is what else nobody tells you: it works anyway. Being the symbol, I mean. Because the hope wasn't really about me. I figured that out somewhere around the fourth gallery, watching Bethel teach the ledger-sisters a walking song in a language the Undercourt had tried to burn out of them. The hope was about them. I was just the door they'd chosen to believe in. All I actually had to do was stay open.

A key doesn't have to be strong. It only has to be willing.

* * *

We stopped at dusk — under-dusk, rather, since the mountain kept its own hours — in a vaulted chamber where an underground river ran black and quick along one wall. Sef built a fire out of marsh-reeds he'd brought for exactly this purpose, because of course he had; the man planned for contingencies the way other men plan for breakfast. Mara sat beside him, not quite touching, the way they'd been sitting since the marsh. Forgiveness refused, understanding earned. Some accounts take longer to reconcile than others. I appraise books; I know about slow restorations.

"You're quiet," Mara said, when I sat down next to her. "Quieter."

"I'm doing arithmetic."

"Dangerous hobby."

"There are two hundred and six of us now," I said. "Two hundred and six people following a key down a hole toward a queen who turns souls into lamps. And tomorrow we reach the court, and the stone-half in your pocket starts singing to its other half, and everything the prophecy wants us to do is exactly what we're going to do, willingly, with banners, essentially delivering ourselves." I turned my hands over. "I keep trying to find the flaw in the plan, and the flaw is that there isn't one, and that's the trap."

Mara was quiet a moment. Firelight moved on her face, on the scar the vellum had promised her a lifetime ago.

"When I sold charms in Ashmoor," she said, "I had a rule. Never touch real magic. Never be anything. And I thought the rule kept me safe, but really it just kept me small. Ten years of small." She looked at the fire. "You're not wrong about the trap. But Wendell — some doors you walk through knowing it's a trap, because what's behind it matters more than what happens to you."

"That's either very brave or very foolish."

"It's both. Most important things are both." She leaned her head against my shoulder. "Besides. You're not walking through alone. Two hundred and six, remember. Plus a marsh-witch, plus a pickpocket, plus me."

"Plus Tam," I said quietly.

Her breath caught — just barely, just enough that I felt it. "Plus Tam," she agreed.

That was the arithmetic I hadn't said aloud. Tomorrow we would stand in front of the Underqueen, and somewhere in that splendor was a hollow-eyed man with his sister's stubborn jaw, ten years gone, waiting for a door. I had never met him. I already knew I would burn the whole court down before I let him stay a lantern. Metaphorically. Mostly metaphorically.

* * *

Nix found me near midnight, standing watch at the chamber's edge, listening to the river.

"She's going to offer you something tomorrow," Nix said, without preamble. Nix never used preambles; preambles were for people with something to hide, and Nix hid things openly, which was somehow more honest. "The Queen. She offered you before, in the vault. Power, right? A place. It'll be bigger this time. Sweeter. She'll have learned what you want."

"What do I want?"

Nix snorted. "Please. You want to matter. In the useful way, not the loud way. You want to be the one who opens the right door at the right time and saves everybody, and you want it so bad it embarrasses you." She picked at her coat's single knife-hilt. "So when she offers it — really offers it, no strings visible — remember that wanting it isn't the failure. Taking it from *her* is."

"And if taking it from her saves everyone?"

Nix looked at me for a long moment. Down here her eyes were almost the same color as the river.

"Then you'll know it didn't," she said. "That's the thing about hungry things, bookman. Their gifts always cost more later. You taught me that, actually. Back in the vault, when you told her no." She shrugged, embarrassed by her own sincerity, and turned to go. "Get some sleep. Keys need their hinges oiled."

"Nix."

"Yeah?"

"Thank you. For coming back for us. At the Guildhall. You could have taken the pardon and run."

Something flickered across her face — the ghost of the girl who had traded information for a lie of safety, and escaped, and chosen us instead. "Yeah, well," she said. "Turns out you lot are worth more than a pardon." And she vanished into the dark the way she did, soundless as a turning page.

* * *

I slept badly, which was traditional, and woke before the others, which was also traditional, and stood at the head of the column as dawn-that-wasn't filtered down through vents in the mountain's bones.

Two hundred and six souls. Bethel at the front with her bandaged hands folded. The ledger-sisters singing low. The boy who had never seen the sky walking three rows back, chin up, practicing courage. Sef grim as a root. Nix sharp as a hinge. Mara with the stone-half in her pocket, humming faintly, singing to its distant twin like a lovestruck fool, pulling us all toward the court and the crown-jewel and the choice that would end everything or begin it.

I had spent my whole life believing I was a spectator. The man who reads the adventure, catalogs it, shelves it, keeps his hands clean. Standing there, I finally understood what the Sigil was. Not the hooded figure's weapon. Not the Guild's prize. Not the prophecy's puppet, whatever the prophecy wanted.

A key. Just a key. And a key's whole purpose is this: to stand in a lock, and turn, and trust what comes through the door.

"All right," I said, and my voice carried further than I expected, down the gallery, into the dark, into two hundred and six pairs of ears. "Let's go meet a queen."

And far ahead, deeper than the deepest lantern-light, something vast and cold began — very softly — to sing back.
