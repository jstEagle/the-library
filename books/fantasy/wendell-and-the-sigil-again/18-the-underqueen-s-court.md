## 18. The Underqueen's Court

The last stair let out into a cavern so vast that my mind did what minds do when confronted with the impossible: it pretended the ceiling was sky.

It was a good pretense. Veins of pale luminescence crawled across the stone overhead like slow lightning, and somewhere far above — or what I chose to believe was above — water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental. A heartbeat, maybe. The mountain's, keeping time for everything beneath it.

"Don't stare," Nix murmured at my elbow. "Staring's how you get noticed."

"I'm trying very hard not to notice anything," I said. "Noticing is going badly for me."

Wendell walked on my other side, and he hadn't spoken since the tunnel narrowed and then opened like a throat. He kept flexing his right hand, opening and closing it, the way a man does when his fingers remember holding something enormous. The stone-half rode against my sternum in its wrapping of silk and salt, warm as a sleeping bird, and every few steps it pulsed — once, twice — like it knew we were almost home.

*Home.* The word arrived uninvited and I evicted it immediately.

The road into the Undercourt was paved with something that wasn't quite bone and wasn't quite marble, and it was lined with lanterns.

I had heard about the lanterns. Sef had told me once, drunk on marsh-rot whiskey, in the voice he used for things he was ashamed of: *They don't kill them down there, girl. They use them.* I had filed it away as one more reason to hate him, which was easier than picturing it.

Picturing it was worse.

Each lantern hung from an iron crook along the road, and each held a light that moved. Not flickered — *moved*, pressed against the glass from inside, the way a moth presses against a window. Some of them were small and frantic. Some were large and slow, drifting like jellyfish, and when we passed close to one of the big ones, it turned. Followed us. And though lights have no faces, I would swear on every charm I ever sold that this one looked at Wendell with something like hope.

"Don't," Nix said again, softer. "You can't open them. Believe me. I've seen people try."

"What happens to people who try?" Wendell asked quietly.

"They join them."

We walked on. My hands were shaking, so I put them in my pockets where they could shake privately, and I thought about Tam. Ten years I'd thought him dead, and then ten minutes ago — no, longer now, days, weeks, the whole mad spiral since the book — I'd learned he wasn't dead at all, just *spent*. Sold. A soul as payment, and I'd been the invoice.

Ahead, the road ended at gates.

I want to describe them properly, because they deserve it, but the truth is my eyes kept sliding off. They were tall — taller than the Guildhall, taller than sense — and made of a dark metal worked into shapes that resolved the harder you didn't look: interlocking hands, mostly. Thousands of them, hand over hand over hand, climbing toward a lintel carved with a single word in a script older than Ashmoor's charter.

Nix read it aloud anyway, because Nix has never met a rule she wouldn't test. "It says *Paid In Full*."

"That's cheerful," I said.

"It's honest. Down here, honesty's the only currency that doesn't devalue." She spat, ceremonially, on the bone-marble. "Right. This is where I stop being useful and start being a liability. The Queen knows my face. Also my name, my mother's name, and the name I was born with before I stole a better one."

"You're coming with us," Wendell said.

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't brave, exactly. It was just Wendell stating a fact about the world and expecting the world to comply, which is a quality I used to find naive and have recently come to recognize as load-bearing.

Nix looked at him, then at me, and something crossed her face that she'd deny under torture. "Fine. But if I die, I'm haunting both of you, and I'm *very* creative."

The gates opened before anyone touched them.

* * *

The throne room — court hall, audience chamber, whatever you call a space built to make you feel like an insect that wandered indoors — was lit entirely by souls.

Chandeliers of them. Cascades. They hung in tiers from the impossible height, and the whole hall glowed with a light that had no color, only warmth, and the warmth had no comfort in it at all. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks: some human-shaped, some wearing their shapes loosely, like coats slightly too big or slightly too small. Their skin held a faint translucence, as if the light behind them came from within.

And on the dais, on a throne of fused glass that might once have been sand on some drowned shore, sat the Underqueen.

She was beautiful the way a verdict is beautiful. Final. Her gown was the black of the space between lanterns, and her crown was — I felt my stomach drop before my mind caught up — a circlet of pale stone, cracked down the middle, the halves held together by bands of old gold.

The other half of our stone. Worn openly. Of course. Why hide your half of a god-making engine when everyone below already kneels?

"Mara Lockridge." Her voice filled the hall without effort, the way water fills a bowl. "Hedge-witch. Charm-seller. Daughter of a bargain." She smiled, and the smile was kind, which was the worst available option. "And the Sigil himself. You've brought my property back to me."

"The stone isn't yours," I said.

My voice came out steadier than I deserved. Under my coat, the wrapped half pulsed hard enough that I felt it against my ribs like a second heart arguing with my first.

"Nothing here is owned," the Underqueen said. "Everything here is *owed*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire architecture of my court." She rose. The courtiers bowed in a wave, like wheat, like water, like nothing living. "Come forward, charm-seller. You've walked a long way to ask your questions. Ask them."

I should mention that I had questions. I had a speech prepared, actually — Wendell and I had drafted it in the tunnels, and Nix had edited it down from forty minutes to nine words, all of them sharp. But standing in that light, with those chandeliers turning slowly above us like slow constellations of the damned, the speech evaporated and left only the raw thing underneath.

"My brother," I said. "Tam Lockridge. Where is he?"

Something moved through the court. Not a gasp — courts this old don't gasp — but a shifting, a settling, the way a house settles when it hears a name it knows.

"Ah," the Underqueen said. "Yes. The payment." She descended one step, then another, unhurried. "You want to know how a boy's soul comes to hang in my hall. Very well. But stories have prices down here, and yours has already been paid — by him, by your teacher, and by a woman named Vess who wrote a book." 

I stopped breathing.

"Vess," I repeated.

"The author," Wendell said beside me, very quietly. "Of the prophecy."

"The author," the Underqueen agreed, and now she stood three steps above us, looking down, and her eyes were the gray of deep water. "You've read her work, Sigil. You've *been read by it*. Did you never wonder why a prophecy written a century ago describes a scar on a living woman's arm? Why it names a date?" She tilted her head. "Prophecies are not predictions. They are *invoices*. And Vess wrote hers from the only vantage point that sees all sides of a bargain."

"Which is?" Wendell asked.

"Inside." The word dropped into the hall and the lantern-light shivered. "Vess was the first soul I ever took. Before this court had a name, before the mountain had stairs, I was hungry and she was dying on the wrong side of a door, and I took what she carried. It is the nature of what I am. I do not apologize for hunger; I only apologize for the *table manners*." She said it flatly, without performance, and somehow that was more horrifying than any cackle. "Her soul has hung in my hall for a hundred years. And a soul remembers. A soul, given time enough in the glass, learns to *write*. She wrote the prophecy with the only ink she had left — herself. Every word cost her. She wrote a trap, charm-seller. She wrote it for *you*."

"For me." My mouth had gone dry. "Why me?"

"Because she saw what I see." The Underqueen descended the last steps and stopped close enough that I could smell her — cold stone and snuffed candles. "A soul sold as payment leaves a shape. A debt leaves a *shadow*, and shadows can be walked. Vess wrote a road out of her own cage and aimed it at the one person whose grief was loud enough to walk it. She needed someone who would come down here. Someone who would carry the stone. Someone who would stand exactly where you're standing." She smiled again. "Congratulations. You're on time."

The wrapped stone-half burned against my chest, and for one vertiginous moment I understood — really understood — that every step since the night market had been a step in a sentence someone else was writing. The book. The crossroads. The Guildhall job. Even Sef's confession, even Corvane's fall. Not fate, exactly. Worse than fate: *composition*.

"So the prophecy," Wendell said slowly, "wasn't a warning."

"It was a key cut to fit two locks," the Underqueen said. "The Sigil opens my vault. The witch carries the stone. Bring the halves together, and the door Vess died writing will finally open." She extended one hand, palm up, and the gold-banded crown seemed to lean toward mine. "She never intended for either of you to survive the reading. That part she left out. Writers do."

"And if we refuse?" Nix said, from behind my shoulder, sounding remarkably casual for someone surrounded by translucent aristocracy. "If we just walk back up the stairs?"

"Then the stone stays split, the debts stay owed, and your brother hangs in my hall until the mountain wears down to sand." The Underqueen turned her gaze on Wendell, and gentled it, which frightened me more than any threat. "But you won't refuse. Because there is a third option, and you've already begun choosing it."

"Which is?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since the vault. Since the fragment of truth we'd stolen on the way out.

"Make it whole." She lifted her chin, and the cracked crown caught the soul-light and threw it back fractured. "Two halves, joined. Do you know what the stone *is*, charm-seller? Your mentor buried half beneath your pretty Guildhall and I set the other in gold upon my head, and neither of us understood what we were guarding. It is not power. Power can be spent. The stone is *potential* — every spell never cast, every life never lived, every future folded up small enough to hold. Made whole, it offers its bearer everything. Every road. Every ending rewritten."

"And the price?" Wendell asked.

"There isn't one," the Underqueen said simply. "That's the horror of it. No price, no bargain, no debt. The stone doesn't take. It *offers*. And the bearer chooses, freely, gladly, and cannot afterward claim they were forced." Her eyes came to me. "Made whole, it will make its bearer a god. Or — if the bearer falters, hesitates, doubts — it will hollow them in the space of a heartbeat and add them to my collection. A god or a lantern. There is no third shape. Vess knew. It's why she wrote the trap rather than spring it herself. She had already seen what it does to the ones who reach for it."

The hall was silent except for the chandeliers, turning, turning, each light pressing at its glass.

And then, because I am my mother's daughter and my brother's sister and apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone, I asked the question underneath all the others:

"If it makes whoever holds it a god — why haven't *you* joined your half to ours? A century with half a crown. You must have thought about it."

For the first time, the Underqueen's composure moved. Something flickered across her face — fast, quickly buried, but unmistakable.

Fear.

"Because I took Vess's soul," she said, "and a taker cannot be trusted with wholeness. The stone would know. It would offer, and I would accept, and I would become exactly what I have spent a hundred years making sure I am not." She turned away, ascending her steps again, and her voice came back down to us stripped of all its velvet. "I am the court's keeper, not its prisoner. If the stone goes whole, it goes through *your* hands, witch. That was Vess's design, and I have hated her for it every year of her afterlife."

"Then give us Tam," I said. "Give me my brother, and we'll take the stone and go, and your court can keep its debts."

"No."

The word fell like a gate.

"Your brother is collateral," the Underqueen said. "He walks out of my hall when the stone is made whole — not before, not after. Those are Vess's terms, written in her own substance, and even I am bound by them. Wholeness first. Then the boy." She settled onto her glass throne, and the audience was, visibly, over. "You have until the mountain's next heartbeat to decide. But we both know you've already decided. You've been deciding since a Tuesday night in a market, when a man with ink-stained hands showed you your own face in old vellum."

Above us, the chandeliers turned. Somewhere in the third tier — I was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of your own name — one of the small, frantic lights went still, and turned, and watched me with the particular attention of someone listening for their cue.

Tam.

I put my hand over the wrapped stone-half, and it pulsed against my palm, eager, patient, *offering*.

God or lantern. No third shape.

Except — and the thought arrived quiet, in Sef's gravel voice, in Nix's stolen grin, in Wendell's stubborn fact-stating certainty — nobody down here had ever accounted for a charm-seller who'd spent ten years perfecting the art of refusing magic.

"Wendell," I said, without taking my eyes off the throne. "When I tell you to open the vault — don't."

His hand stilled mid-flex. "Mara—"

"I've just realized something the Underqueen missed." I smiled at the Queen of the Hungry Things, and it was not a nice smile. "Vess wrote a key with two locks. But she wrote it a hundred years ago, and she never met me."

The stone-half pulsed. The mountain's heartbeat tolled somewhere far above, slow as a funeral drum.

The Underqueen leaned forward on her throne, and for the first time in a hundred years, the court beneath the mountain leaned in with her.
