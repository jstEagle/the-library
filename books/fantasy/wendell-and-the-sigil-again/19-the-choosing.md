## 19. The Choosing

I have appraised a great many dangerous objects in my life — cursed psalters, a dagger that whispered its owner's secrets to anyone standing downwind, one truly malevolent set of tax ledgers — and I can tell you that the worst of them never announce themselves. They sit quietly on a shelf and wait for you to pick them up.

The sigil-stone halves sat in Mara's cupped hands like two pieces of a broken heart, humming.

"They want to be together," she said. Her voice had gone strange since the Underqueen's court — scraped thin, the way a bell sounds after it's been struck too hard. "That's the whole trick of it. That's always been the whole trick."

Around us, the Undercourt's outcasts waited in the shadow of the great stair: Nix with her arms crossed and her knives showing, which for Nix was practically a hug; Tam, hollow-eyed but upright, leaning on a freed lantern-soul named Bess who still glowed faintly at the seams; a dozen others who had followed us down from the smuggler's galleries because they had nothing left to lose except, apparently, their faith in a rare-book appraiser.

The Underqueen's throne hall yawned above us, all veined obsidian and slow blue light, empty now. She had withdrawn behind her doors to consider our terms, which in my experience is what powerful beings do right before they stop considering terms altogether.

"Mara," I said carefully, "you're not thinking what I think you're thinking."

"I'm thinking we need time."

"Time is not something this place gives away. Believe me, I've seen the price list."

She turned the stone halves over in her palms. The light they threw wasn't light exactly; it was more like remembering, a glow that made you recall things you'd rather keep buried. I thought of my shop. I thought of the smell of old vellum. I thought, absurdly, of the way Mara laughed when she forgot to guard herself — a snort, undignified, wonderful.

"The prophecy needs them joined," she said. "That's the mechanism. Whole stone, whole power, whole claiming. But joined *wrong* — joined by someone who isn't the prophesied bearer —" She looked up at me, and I saw where her sentence was going, and I hated it with my entire being. "It stalls. It buys hours. Maybe a day."

"And who," I said, though I already knew, "isn't the prophesied bearer?"

"Wendell—"

"No. No, no. I've read enough prophecy to know how this scene goes. The ordinary man holds the terrible thing, the terrible thing gets ideas above its station, and by chapter's end he's wearing a crown made of other people's teeth." I gestured at the throne hall. "There is *literally* a room full of teeth-crowns up there."

"You're not ordinary," Nix said, unhelpfully.

"I am *extremely* ordinary. It's my defining feature. I catalog books. I own one good coat. I once fainted at a dentist's."

"You're the Sigil," Tam said quietly. He hadn't spoken much since waking. His voice sounded like someone relearning an instrument. "It opened the vault for you. It knows your hand."

"It opened the vault because I bribed it with proximity to Mara," I said. "That's not destiny, that's just being in love with the actual chosen one."

Nobody laughed. The stone hummed. Somewhere far above, muffled through miles of mountain, the Guild's festival drums were still beating — Corvane's armies massing at the surface gates, the Underqueen's patience thinning, every clock in the world running down at once.

Mara took my hand. Her fingers were cold. They were always cold, and I had spent ten months finding excuses to warm them, and I would have traded every hour of that for the right to refuse her now.

"If I hold it," she said, "it takes me. You saw what happened in the Guildhall. Three seconds and I was designing cathedrals in my head. I'm the bearer, Wendell. It wants me the way fire wants a wick. But you—" her thumb traced the scar on my palm, the one I'd gotten falling off a ladder over a first-edition, "—you're a key, not a wick. Keys don't burn."

"Keys get *used*," I said. "That's the entire job description of a key."

"Ten minutes," she said. "Maybe fifteen. Just long enough for Bess and the others to get Tam and the freed souls to the deep galleries before the Queen or the Guild or the prophecy itself comes looking. Then you put it down and walk away, and I will personally carry you if I have to."

"That's a lie," I said. "You'd have to be carrying the stone."

"I'd put the stone down."

"You would absolutely not put the stone down."

She didn't smile, but something moved behind her eyes that was the ancestor of a smile, and I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only at moments of catastrophic personal error, that I was going to say yes. Not because of prophecy. Not because of strategy. Because she was asking, and because ten months ago a woman selling luck-charms in a night market had looked at me across a table of cheap wine and decided I was worth the risk of wanting things again, and I had built my whole self around being worth it.

"Fine," I said. "But I want it noted for the historical record that this is a terrible plan."

"Noted," said Nix. "Historically. Terrible."

* * *

The stone halves came together in my hands like magnets, like lungs remembering breath, like — I'll be honest — like a homecoming, and that should have been my first warning.

For about ninety seconds, nothing happened. The joined stone sat warm in my palms, heavy as a sleeping cat, and I stood in the middle of the Undercourt's antechamber feeling profoundly foolish while Mara counted souls toward the deep stairs and Nix argued with a goblin factor about tunnel tolls.

Then it noticed me.

I don't know how else to describe it. An object becoming aware of the person holding it — I know how that sounds. I've read the accounts of mad kings and guild-mages gone wrong, and I always assumed the language was decorative. It isn't. There is a moment when the thing in your hands stops being a thing and starts being an *audience*, and everything you are becomes a performance whether you consented to the stage or not.

*Appraiser,* it said, without sound, in the register of my own thoughts. *You value things. What am I worth?*

"Nothing," I said aloud. Several people nearby looked at me with concern. "You're a rock with delusions."

*Everything,* it corrected gently. *I am the sum of every bargain ever struck beneath this mountain. Every soul weighed. Every name sold. Hold me and hold the ledger of the world.*

And here is the shameful part, the part I will spend whatever remains of my life declining to mention at dinner parties: it felt *good*. Not like fire, not like ice. Like competence. Like the moment an appraisal clicks into place and you know — know beyond argument — what a thing is and what it costs. All my life I had been a man who understood the value of objects and none of the value of himself, and here was a stone offering to do my accounting for me, forever, in both directions.

*I could tell you what you're worth,* it murmured. *Shall I?*

"Don't," I said.

*Wendell the Sigil. Key of keys. Opener of vaults. Do you know what the Guild would give for you? What the Queen would give? What the* world *would give? You have been appraising treasures your whole life, little clerk. Have you never wondered why? Because you were meant to be the treasure. Meant to be held. Meant to be* wanted—

The cold started in my fingertips. Or perhaps it was warmth; the distinction seemed suddenly pedantic. My spine straightened. My thoughts arranged themselves into tidy rows, catalogued, cross-referenced, *valued.* I looked across the antechamber at the goblin factor haggling with Nix and thought, with serene and total certainty: *that creature's life is worth approximately four hundred crowns, and I could name the buyer.*

I found this thought reasonable.

That's the horror of it. Not the cruelty — cruelty I recognized, having met Corvane, having read history. The horror was how *reasonable* it all felt. How the stone didn't make me monstrous so much as it made me *certain*, and certainty is just cruelty wearing a very good coat.

"Wendell?" Mara's voice, from somewhere to my left. Distant. Poorly valued. "Wendell, put it down."

"In a moment," I heard myself say. My voice had changed. Richer. I approved of it. "There's a matter of inventory to settle first."

* * *

Time went strange. I believe it was minutes. It may have been less. The stone showed me things — no, that's passive, and the stone did not permit passivity; the stone let me *see*, which flatters a man into thinking seeing is the same as choosing.

I saw Ashmoor from above, its crooked streets laid out like a ledger awaiting entries. I saw the Guildhall with its half-buried foundations, and Corvane's face, and assigned him a number. I saw the Underqueen's crown and knew precisely which soul powered which jewel. I saw Mara — small, magicless-soon, mortal, breakable — and the stone offered me her valuation too, and I felt my mind reach for the number, and some last unlit corner of me recoiled so hard it cracked something.

Because there was no number. That was the flaw in the ledger. That was the arithmetic error at the heart of the world's oldest bargain. The stone could price a soul-lantern, a crown-jewel, a king, a god. It reached for Mara Lockridge and found — nothing. Not zero. *Nothing.* A blank column. Value beyond denomination, which to a perfect accounting is not treasure but *error*, and errors must be corrected, struck through, erased—

"No," I said.

*She is an unpriced asset,* the stone said, patient as interest accruing. *All unpriced assets destabilize the ledger. You see the problem.*

"I see," I said, and my voice was grand now, resonant off the obsidian, and people were backing away from me, and part of me noted their retreat as prudent and part of me — the part still smelling vellum and cheap wine — was screaming. "I see the problem perfectly. And I have a correction."

Somewhere in the fog of my own magnificence, I raised the stone. I believe I intended something grand. A judgment. A valuation of the whole groaning world, entered at last in a fair hand.

And then Mara walked directly into my line of sight, put herself between me and everything, and did the one thing no prophecy, no guild, no queen, no omniscient rock had thought to account for.

She talked about Tuesday.

* * *

"Do you remember," she said, "the night market? Our first date?"

I remember, some distant shard of me thought. Aloud, I said, "Irrelevant."

"You wore your one good coat. You'd clearly ironed it. There was a crease down the front like a scar." She took a step closer. Nix hissed *Mara, don't* from somewhere behind, and Mara ignored her, which took more courage than anything she'd done with magic, and she'd done plenty. "You asked me what my charms actually did, and I lied, and you knew I lied, and you bought three anyway."

"Sentimental expenditure," I said. "Poorly valued."

"You paid too much on purpose." Another step. Close enough now that I could see her hands shaking, close enough that the stone's cold ran up my arms and I could feel it *wanting* to price her, reaching, grasping, finding nothing, nothing, nothing. "You told me later it was because haggling felt rude. Who does that? Who pays full price in a night market?"

"A fool," I said.

"An earnest idiot," she agreed. "My favorite kind. Wendell. Look at me. Not the mountain. Not the ledger. Me."

*I cannot find her value,* the stone said, and for the first time it sounded uncertain, and uncertainty, in a perfect system, is agony. *She does not resolve. She does not resolve.*

"She doesn't," Mara said, as if she could hear it, and maybe she could; maybe love is a kind of hearing. "Ten months, Wendell. Ten months of Tuesdays. You learned how I take my tea. I learned you talk to books before you open them. You kept the receipt from the night market folded in your wallet — I've seen it, don't pretend. Three charms, overpaid, kept like a love letter."

"Those are," I said, and my grand voice faltered, "*small* things."

"They're the whole point of things!" She was crying now, furious about it, wiping her face with the back of her hand like tears were an inefficiency. "The stone prices kingdoms because kingdoms are easy! Anything big can be counted! But nobody — nothing — can count a man who irons his coat for a charm-seller and keeps a receipt for a year! That's not in any ledger! That's not *worth* anything, and it's worth everything, and that's why you're losing, you stupid beautiful rock, because he was never a key or a Sigil or a treasure — he's *mine*, and mine is not a number!"

The stone screamed.

Not in sound. In arithmetic. I felt it strain against the blank column in its perfect ledger, felt it try and try and try to resolve her and fail, and in its failure I felt — cracking open like ice in spring — myself. The appraiser. The fool. The man with one good coat and a receipt in his wallet.

I looked down at the stone in my hands, whole and blazing and *furious*, and I said, in my own ordinary voice, the best line I have ever delivered:

"Your valuation is rejected."

And I let go.

* * *

I didn't drop it — dropping an artifact of cosmic consequence seemed unwise even mid-epiphany. I opened my hands and stepped back, and the stone fell to the obsidian floor with a sound like a bell and a door slamming, and the grandeur drained out of me so fast I nearly went with it. My knees hit the floor. Someone caught my shoulders. Nix, I think. She was saying words that were mostly profanity arranged around the word "idiot," which from Nix is a lullaby.

Mara knelt in front of me. Her face was a mess. Her eyes were the clearest thing in the world.

"Hey," she said.

"Hello," I managed. "I appear to have briefly become insufferable."

"Briefly."

"There may have been talk of valuing people."

"There was talk of valuing *me* specifically, which is worse."

"I withdraw all of it. Retroactively. With prejudice."

Her forehead came down against mine, and we stayed like that, two idiots on the floor of hell, while around us the outcasts breathed again and Tam watched us with an expression I couldn't read — hope, maybe, worn cautiously, like a coat borrowed from someone bigger.

The stone lay where it had fallen, dimmed, sulking. But not dead. As I watched, its light began to pulse — slow, rhythmic, deliberate. Like knocking. Like something inside it turning over and saying, in the register of my own thoughts, quieter now, almost petulant:

*The Underqueen comes. And she has held me whole before.*

Above us, the great doors of the throne hall began, without any hand touching them, to open.

Blue light spilled down the stair.

"Oh good," said Nix, drawing both knives. "Company."
