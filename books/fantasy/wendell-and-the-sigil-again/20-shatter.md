## 20. Shatter

The stone was whole, and it was singing.

That's the part nobody warns you about — that a thing made to unmake the world would sound beautiful. The two halves had come together in the Underqueen's hand like a book closing, and the note they sang went through my teeth, my ribs, the marrow of me. Around the dais, the court of the mountain knelt. Wendell didn't kneel. Neither did I.

"Behold," the Underqueen said, and her voice was the voice of a landslide deciding to be patient. "The Sigil made whole. One bearer. One will." Her eyes found mine, and there was something almost like pity in them, which frightened me more than the power did. "You understand what happens now."

I understood several things, actually. I understood that the prophecy we'd been running from since a Tuesday in Ashmoor had finally caught up with us. I understood that the stone could make its bearer a god or a lantern, and that the Underqueen had been a lantern once herself, before she learned to eat light instead of burn in it. And I understood — with the terrible clarity that only arrives when it's far too late for clarity to be useful — that every road out of this chamber led through someone's soul.

Most of them were already hanging from the ceiling.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last hour happened fast and I want to remember it properly.

We had come up through the deep stair with an army of outcasts at our backs — Nix's pickpockets, Sef's marsh-witches, lantern-souls who'd slipped their chains when Wendell turned his key. The Undercourt had never seen anything like it: ragged, half-starved people walking openly through halls built for terror, carrying stolen names and borrowed fire.

And at the end of it, the Underqueen had simply held out her crown, and the second half of the stone had risen out of it like a fish rising to bait, and clicked against the half in Mara Lockridge's pocket.

Because yes. I'd carried it since the Guildhall. Against every instinct I owned, I'd carried it, because it wanted to be carried, and because Tam's name was written in it somewhere, in the fine print of a bargain I'd triggered at nineteen years old without knowing I was signing.

"You should give it to me," the Underqueen said now, gentle as a knife going in. "Or the Sigil should take it. Those are the shapes this night can make. A god beneath the mountain, or a god above it. Choose your heaven, hedge-witch."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the stone, in that moment, holding it whole in both hands:

It was hungry. Not evil — hunger isn't evil, hunger is just hunger — but vast, and patient, and utterly without mercy, the way winter is without mercy. It had been made from a soul, the first soul the Underqueen ever took, a prophet who'd tried to warn the world and been pressed into mineral for the trouble. Every word of the prophecy was that soul still trying to speak. *Wendell and the Sigil Again.* A title, not a sentence. A soul screaming itself into grammar.

And it offered. Oh, how it offered. The moment my skin touched the whole of it, I saw everything. I saw Ashmoor with magic legal and bright, guild banners burning in the square. I saw Tam whole. I saw myself old and kind and worshipped, and underneath all of it, like a watermark, I saw the price: everyone else, kneeling. Everyone else, lit from inside. Everyone else, a lamp.

"No," I said, mostly to myself.

"Mara." Wendell's voice. He'd come to stand beside me, and he wasn't looking at the stone; he was looking at me, which took a particular kind of courage, given what the stone was doing to the air. "Whatever you're seeing — it lies by telling the truth. That's the worst kind of lie."

"I know."

"The Guild wants it to enslave the court. She wants it to enslave the city. The prophecy wants—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The prophecy just wants to be finished. Any ending will do."

"I know, Wendell."

"So don't choose a side," he said. "Choose none."

And that was the moment I understood what I'd come down the mountain to do, though I think some part of me had known since the crossroads, since the ink crawled, since the very first night Wendell sat across from me in the night market being awkward and earnest about rare books while I sold him a luck-charm I'd faked with dyed glass.

There was no way to win. There was only a way to end it.

* * *

Magic, for those of you lucky enough never to have any, doesn't feel like power. It feels like water. It feels like something moving through you that was moving before you were born and will move after you die, and you're just the riverbank it happens to be shaping. My whole life I'd dammed it, walled it, sold charms powered by nothing but dye and confidence, because the one time I let it run free, my brother paid for it with his soul.

Now I opened every gate I had.

The stone felt it instantly. Felt me pouring — not taking, *pouring*, which it had never once been asked to do. Gods take. Lanterns are taken. Nobody, in a hundred years of prophecy and a thousand years of court, had ever looked at the sigil-stone and thought: *what if I just gave it everything and asked for nothing back?*

"What are you doing?" The Underqueen's patience cracked, finally, beautifully. "Stop. *Stop—*"

"It's a bargain, isn't it?" I said. My voice came out strange, doubled, like the stone was learning to speak through me even as I unmade it. "Everything under the mountain runs on bargains. So here's mine. You want a vessel? Take one. Take all of me. Every spell I ever learned, every hedge-trick, every hex Sef beat into me and every charm I ever faked. Take the lot."

The light went white.

* * *

I have tried, in the months since, to describe what breaking felt like, and the honest answer is: it felt like the last day of a fever. It felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing. It felt like my mother's kitchen, and rain on the marsh, and the first time Tam and I jumped off the mill-dam together — all the moments magic had soaked into, wrung out at once, wrung *out*, until I was just Mara. Just a woman. Damp, ordinary, free.

But in the moment itself, there was no poetry. There was the stone screaming in my hands as a hundred years of stored souls hit it all at once like a flood hitting a dam — because that's what my magic did, do you see? It wasn't strong. It was *porous*. It opened the stone from the inside, let all the light it had hoarded remember which way was out.

The ceiling of the Undercourt came apart into stars.

Not metaphor. The bound souls — the lanterns, hundreds of them, centuries of them — tore loose as the stone shattered, and rose, and the black vaulted dark above us filled with lights like a sky remembering what it was for. Some shot upward toward the smuggler's stair and the distant smell of rain. Some circled the chamber, dazed, blinking. And one — one came down.

He came down slowly, like a man waking from a long sleep in a bright room, and when his feet touched the marble he staggered, and Wendell caught him, and I —

I couldn't move. Isn't that absurd? Ten years of guilt, five chapters' worth of desperate climbing and bargaining and blood, and when my brother finally stood in front of me, hollow-cheeked and blinking and *alive*, I couldn't do anything but stand there with ash on my hands where the stone had been.

"Mara?" he said. His voice was rust. His eyes were finding focus the way eyes do when they've spent a decade pointed at nothing. "Mara, why do you look — you were *nineteen*. Why do you look..."

"Older," I managed.

"Sad," he said, and then his face crumpled, and he crossed the space between us, and I held my brother for the first time in ten years while the sky full of freed souls wheeled overhead, and I cried the way you cry when you've been holding a door shut for a decade and someone finally tells you the flood's gone out.

* * *

The Underqueen was gone. Later I'd learn she'd fled into the deep places with what remained of her court, diminished, mortal-ish, hunting scraps of the stone's power that no longer existed. In the moment, all I saw was an empty throne and a crown rolling on the marble, spinning down slow, and stopping.

Nix appeared at my elbow, soot-faced, grinning, bleeding a little from a cut over one eyebrow. "Tell me you got souvenirs."

"Got a brother."

"Huh." Nix looked Tam over, professional and quick. "Bit thin. We'll fatten him up." Then, quieter, actually quiet, which for Nix was roughly equivalent to a state funeral: "You did it. You mad, terrifying woman. You actually broke the bloody thing."

"We broke it," I said. "All of us."

"Don't get sentimental, I'm holding a knife."

Sef came hobbling through the wreckage of the dais, marsh-mud still on his boots, and stopped in front of me, and for once in his long acid life had nothing barbed to say. He looked at my hands. He knew. Of course he knew; he'd known what the bargain would cost before I was born, probably.

"Mara," he started.

"Don't," I said. Not cruelly. Just final. "Not tonight. Tonight I'm going to hold my brother and go home."

He nodded, and stepped back, and let me have it. Small mercies. Even from him.

* * *

We surfaced into Ashmoor near dawn, through the smuggler's door, into an alley that smelled of rain and bread and ordinary human morning. Above us, the freed souls were still rising — the city would wake to lights drifting over the rooftops like festival lanterns with nowhere to be tied, and the Guild, leaderless, would spend the coming weeks pretending it had always meant well.

Tam stood in the alley breathing real air, real rain, and laughed — a rusty, broken, wonderful sound. "It's cold," he said, delighted. "Mara, it's *cold*. Do you know how long since I felt cold?"

"Ten years," I said.

"Feels longer." He looked at me, and his expression shifted into something older than his years, something that had seen the inside of a hundred years of dark. "What did it cost you? I can tell. There's a — a shape where something used to be."

I reached inside myself, to the place where the river had always run, and found — quiet. Not emptiness. Quiet. A room with the windows open.

"Nothing that matters," I said, and found, to my enormous surprise, that it was true.

Wendell put his arm around me. Tam watched us both with the dawning, horrified delight of a younger brother realizing his sister has a *person*, and Nix was already complaining about breakfast, and the sun came up over Ashmoor like it hadn't been watching any of it, like the world was just going to keep turning, ordinary and unbewitched.

My magic was dead. My brother was alive. And somewhere behind us, in the dark of the mountain, the last echo of the prophecy faded out — unfinished, unsaid, finally silent.

Tam squeezed my hand. "So," he said. "Tell me everything I missed. Start with the boy."
