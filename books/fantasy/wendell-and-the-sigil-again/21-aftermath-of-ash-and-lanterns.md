## 21. Aftermath of Ash and Lanterns

The first thing I remember is light.

Not lantern-light. Not the cold, honeyed glow of the Undercourt, where the light came from people — from us, from what was left of us, strung up along the vaulted halls like festival decorations that had forgotten they were ever anything else. No. This was messy light. Grey and gold and indifferent, the kind that comes through a dirty window over a shop counter and doesn't care whether you deserve it.

I was sitting in a chair. Someone had put a blanket around my shoulders. I did not remember how to be a person who sat in chairs, but apparently my body did, because it stayed.

"You're doing it again," said a voice. A woman's voice, rough at the edges, like she'd been crying recently and had decided to stop and hadn't quite managed it. "The staring. You do it for ten minutes at a stretch and then you blink like you're surprised the world's still there."

I turned my head. It took effort, the way everything took effort now — not because I was weak, but because I was *heavy*, suddenly, full of weight I'd spent ten years without. Souls are lighter than people. Nobody tells you that. When they take your soul, they take the weight of you: the wanting, the dreading, the remembering. What's left is smooth as river stone and about as talkative.

The woman had my face. That was the strange part. Not exactly my face — hers had more lines around the eyes, and a scar through one eyebrow I didn't recognize, and her hair was shorter than mine had been — but underneath it, the architecture was the same. The Lockridge jaw. The stubborn set of the mouth.

"Mara," she said, when I'd stared long enough. "Your sister. In case the name's gone slippery."

"It hasn't," I said. My voice came out like something dragged over gravel. "I don't remember you. But I remember the name."

"That's more than yesterday." She tried to smile. It mostly worked. "Yesterday you asked me if I was the one who fed you."

"I asked that?"

"You did. Fair question. The answer's no, by the way. Wendell feeds you. Wendell feeds everyone. I think it's how he says things he can't say out loud."

"Wendell," I repeated. The name sat strangely in my mouth, warm, like a coin left in the sun.

"The Sigil himself. Though he hates being called that. He's in the back arguing with Nix about whether we can afford a second kettle." She said it lightly, but her eyes were doing something else — measuring me, the way you measure a crack in a wall to see if it's spreading or healing. "You don't have to talk today. Or tomorrow. The others couldn't, at first. Some of them still can't."

"The others," I said. "How many?"

"Forty-one came up with you. Thirty-eight are still with us. Three wandered off toward the mountain before anyone could stop them." She said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've said to yourself so many times it's worn smooth. "We're working on the rest."

*The rest.* Forty-one souls poured out of a shattered stone into a city that had outlawed the very thing they were made of, blinking in daylight for the first time in years, decades, in one poor man's case nearly a century. I knew this because I'd started to remember — not my own memories first, worse luck, but other people's, fragments the stone had held, leaking into all of us like dye through water. I remembered a harvest festival from 1840-something. I remembered a wedding on the mountain's southern slope. I remembered dying, once, badly, in a snowdrift, though I'd never seen snow in my life.

That's the thing nobody warns you about, coming back. You don't return to yourself all at once. You return in pieces, and some of the pieces aren't yours.

* * *

The shop — because it was a shop, I learned; Mara ran a charm stall that had somehow become a refuge, which apparently was the story of her whole life — filled up slowly over the following days. Freed souls drifted in and out like weather. There was an old woman named Bess who had been a lantern for sixty years and now sat by the window cataloguing birds with the ferocious attention of someone making up for lost time. There was a boy of maybe nineteen who wouldn't give a name and wouldn't stop shaking, until Wendell discovered, by accident, that the boy could draw — really draw, lines that moved on the page if you weren't looking straight at them — and after that the boy drew constantly, filling scrap paper with mountains and doors and a woman made of moths.

And there was me, learning to eat.

You laugh. But eating is a memory before it's an act. Hunger is a wanting, and wantings were the first things they took. For the first week I ate because Wendell put food in front of me and watched with anxious eyes until I finished it. In the second week, I tasted something. It was bread, ordinary bread, and I stopped mid-chew and felt my eyes fill up, and Wendell very kindly pretended to be fascinated by the window.

"Sorry," I managed.

"Don't apologize for that," he said. He was a big, awkward man, the kind whose hands never quite knew where to live, and when he was embarrassed he polished his spectacles, which he did now. "First taste back is — well. Nix cried over an orange. We don't talk about it."

"Nix sounds wise."

"Nix would deny it violently." He hesitated, then sat down across from me, chair creaking under him. "Tam. Can I ask you something? You can refuse."

I nodded. Talking was still expensive. I budgeted my words like a poor man budgets coin.

"When you were — there. In the court. Do you remember anything of the surface? Of before? Anything at all that kept?"

I thought about it. It was the question Mara wanted to ask and wouldn't, and I loved her a little for not asking it, and I hated myself a little for the answer.

"There was a door," I said. "In the dark. Every so often I'd find it, or it'd find me. And behind the door there was a kitchen. And someone was burning something on the stove, and laughing, and calling my name like I was late for something." I looked at my hands. They were still too thin. "I used to press my face against the door. I couldn't open it. But I could smell the smoke. That kept. That's what kept."

Wendell took off his spectacles and polished them for a long time.

"She burned the stew," he said finally. "The night you disappeared. She told me that. She was making your favorite, trying to make peace after a fight, and she burned it, and she went out looking for you to apologize, and by morning you were gone. She's carried that burnt stew for ten years like it was a knife she left lying around."

"I know," I said. "It was my favorite."

And Wendell looked at me, and I looked at him, and neither of us said anything else, because some things you carry together without talking about them.

* * *

Mara came for me every evening at sundown.

That was our ritual, though she'd have bristled at the word. She'd finish with the last of the souls, bank the fire, and walk me around the block — one block, always the same one, past the shuttered chandlery and the wall where the night market set up and the corner where a lamplighter still made his rounds even though half the city had switched to gas.

"Guild's falling apart," she told me on one of these walks, in the tone she used for news she thought might frighten me. "Corvane's dead, and half the wardens have melted away like they were never there. The ones left keep raiding empty warehouses. They raided a bakery last week. Arrested forty pounds of flour."

"Why flour?"

"No idea. I think they've forgotten what they're for." She kicked a bottle cap into the gutter. "A hundred years of hunting magic, and it turns out the whole machine only worked while someone was cranking it. Take the crank away and it just... stands there. Rusting."

I thought about the wardens I'd known — no. I hadn't known any. But the stone had known them, had held their fear like sediment holds fossils. The Guild had fed the Undercourt for generations, quietly, officially, souls traded up the chain to keep the peace below and the power above. Corvane hadn't been a monster. He'd been a clerk. That was somehow worse.

"And the ones who come back?" I asked. "The wardens. When they realize what they were part of."

"Some quit. Some drink. One showed up at the shop last week and asked if we were hiring." She shrugged. "People are people. That's the whole horror of it, Tam. Not one big evil thing. Just people, doing their jobs, for a hundred years."

We walked. The lamplighter lit his lamp, and its light was warm and small and nothing like the Undercourt's, and I stopped and looked at it for longer than I meant to.

"Do you remember me?" Mara asked suddenly. Quietly. She wasn't looking at me. "At all? Before."

I searched. It was like fishing in a frozen lake — you knew there were things under there, moving, alive, but the ice was thick.

"I remember being angry at you," I said. "The night I ran. I remember thinking you'd chosen her — Sef — chosen the magic over me. And I remember knowing, even then, even at fifteen, that it wasn't fair, because you didn't choose anything. You were a kid too." I swallowed. "I remember your handwriting. You used to leave me notes on the table. Terrible spelling. Big loopy letters."

"My spelling is excellent now."

"I'm sure the decade improved it."

She laughed — a wet, cracked sound — and then she was crying, right there on the street corner, and I didn't know what to do, so I did the only thing I remembered: I put my arm around her the way you put your arm around someone when you're twelve and they're eleven and the world has been unfair to both of you. Old muscle memory. Deeper than the hollow.

She leaned into it.

"I'm going to tell you everything," she said into my shoulder. "The bargain, the prophecy, all of it. What it cost. What I did. You deserve the whole ugly ledger."

"I know," I said. "But not tonight."

"Not tonight," she agreed.

* * *

Here is what I will say about grief, since I am newly an expert: it is not the opposite of gratitude. They grow from the same root. Every morning I woke in a narrow bed above a charm shop and grieved ten years — the weddings, the funerals, the seasons, my own face at twenty-five that I would never meet. And every morning I also woke, and the waking itself was a gift so enormous I could hardly hold it. The souls who didn't come back — the three who walked toward the mountain, and the ones too far gone to surface at all — they were the measure of my luck, and I hated that luck needed a measure, and I measured anyway.

Bess caught me at it once, standing at the window watching the sun come up like it was a performance staged for my benefit.

"Enjoying the show?" she asked.

"Something like that."

"Hmph." She settled beside me, bird-list abandoned. "Sixty years I was down there. You know what I missed most? Not my family — they're all dead now, poor dears, and I'd have missed them either way. Rain. I missed rain." She tapped the glass, where grey clouds were gathering over Ashmoor's rooftops. "So when it rains, I stand in it. People think I'm mad. Let them."

It rained that afternoon, the first real rain since I'd surfaced, and I went out and stood in it with Bess, and it fell on my face and ran down my chin, and I laughed and cried at the same time, which I am told is standard.

* * *

On the last evening of that first month, Mara took me down to the night market.

My choice, actually. I'd asked. I wanted to see the place where she'd built her life, the small safe thing she'd made while I was underground — and I use *safe* loosely, because the market was chaos, stalls and smoke and a fiddler playing badly two feet from a man selling knives. But it was surface-chaos. Warm chaos. Chaos with rain in it.

Mara's old stall was gone — folded up, sold off, the space taken by a candle merchant. She stood looking at the empty spot for a moment with an expression I couldn't read.

"Doesn't feel like mine anymore," she said. "Ten years I kept that little life like a jewel box. Now it fits wrong."

"What will you do?"

"Keep the shop. Teach the ones who want teaching. Wendell wants to start appraising books again — proper ones, not cursed ones, he says, though I give that a month." She glanced at me sideways. "There's room for you. If you want it. No obligations. You could walk out that gate tomorrow and I'd help you pack."

I looked at the market — the smoke and the fiddler and the crowd, all of it unbearably alive — and then at my sister, who had a stranger's face and my mother's way of standing, arms crossed, waiting for bad news because bad news had always come eventually.

"I don't remember you," I said. "But I remember the door. The kitchen behind it. The burnt stew." I took a breath. "I'd like to stay and find out if the cook's improved."

Her face did several things at once, none of them orderly.

"The stew is *still* terrible," she managed. "Some things survive anything."

"Then it'll be like coming home twice."

We walked back through the market, and the fiddler struck up something faster, and somewhere behind us a candle merchant shouted about prices, and none of it mattered and all of it mattered, and I thought: *this* is what they took, and *this* is what she gave back, and the ledger between us will take years to balance, and thank every merciful god that we have years.

At the shop door, Wendell was waiting with a lantern — an ordinary lantern, oil and glass, no souls in it anywhere — and Nix perched on the step beside him, eating an orange with tremendous concentration.

"Well?" Nix said, around a mouthful. "How was the big outing?"

"Loud," I said.

"Good. Loud means alive." Nix tossed me the orange. I caught it — barely; my reflexes were still arriving in installments — and the citrus smell hit me like a memory of a memory, bright and impossible.

Inside, the shop was warm. Bess had fallen asleep over her bird list. The moth-boy's latest drawing was pinned above the fire — mountains, doors, and now, new since morning, a small figure standing in the rain with its face turned up.

I looked at that figure for a long time.

Because here is the thing I haven't told anyone yet, the thing I've been carrying for three days like a stone in my shoe: the drawings aren't imagination. I've seen that mountain before. Seen those doors. And this morning, when the boy handed me his sketchbook by accident instead of his bread plate, I saw what was on the next page — a page he'd drawn in his sleep, he says, and doesn't remember.

It was the Undercourt. Empty. Its lanterns dark, its throne cracked down the middle.

And beneath it, in the boy's careful, unknowing hand, four words:

*SHE IS NOT DONE.*

The orange rolled from my fingers onto the floor, and outside, far beneath the mountain, something old turned over in its sleep.
