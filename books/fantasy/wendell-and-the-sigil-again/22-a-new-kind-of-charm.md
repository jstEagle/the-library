## 22. A New Kind of Charm

The sign went up crooked on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays had always been my day — the night market's slowest, safest shift, when the pickpockets were still sleeping off their Mondays and even the Guild patrol walked like they'd rather be anywhere else. I'd built a whole life on Tuesdays once. Small, careful, walled-in. It seemed only fitting that the new one should start the same way.

Except this time the sign was mine, painted by Nix in letters that leaned like drunkards: **LOCKRIDGE & CO. CHARMS, BOOKS, & SECOND CHANCES**.

"The ampersand is crooked," Wendell said, squinting up at it from the street with his hands jammed in his pockets the way he did when he was trying not to criticize.

"The ampersand is *aspirational*," Nix said from the top of the ladder. "Like me."

"It's going to fall off."

"Everything falls off eventually, bookman. That's why we sell glue."

I stood in the doorway of what had been, four months ago, a derelict cobbler's shop with a collapsed roof and a family of very opinionated pigeons, and I watched the two of them argue about typography in the grey morning light, and I thought: *this*. This was the thing I'd been selling luck-charms to buy, all those years, without ever knowing the name of it.

Here's what nobody tells you about losing your magic: it doesn't feel like losing an arm. It feels like putting down a heavy bag you've carried so long you forgot you were carrying it. For three days after the shattering, I kept reaching for it anyway — the little warm current under my skin that had been there since I was six years old, since Sef first put a candle flame in my cupped hands and said *gently, gently, it's a living thing* — and every time I reached, I found nothing, and every time I found nothing, I braced for the panic.

It never came. That was the strange part. Ten years I'd been afraid of my own power — afraid of what it cost, afraid of what it called to me across bargains and blood and old vellum, afraid that the girl who could work charms in a city where magic meant the Guild's iron cuffs would one day be the girl who couldn't stop. And now the fear was just... gone. Evicted. The room it used to live in stood empty and full of morning light, and I kept walking through it wondering when I'd stopped being its tenant.

"You're doing the face," Nix said, climbing down the ladder. "The thinking face. The one where you look like you're auditing your own soul."

"I'm deciding whether we open today or spend another week arranging shelves."

"We've arranged the shelves eleven times."

"Twelve," Wendell said. "You moved the grimoires next to the cookbooks yesterday. Someone could die."

"That's called *merchandising*, Wendell."

It was true that we'd been slow about opening. There was no rush, really. Ashmoor was still finding its feet, same as the rest of us. Corvane had fallen — literally, at the last, into the dark beneath the mountain, which struck me as the universe having a sense of humor after all — and the Guild hadn't dissolved so much as deflated, like a parade balloon three weeks after the festival. The mages-hunters who remained spent most of their time filing paperwork about souls that no longer needed hunting. The Undercourt's freed lanterns had come up the smuggler's stair in ones and twos and then dozens, blinking in daylight they hadn't seen in decades, some of them wearing bodies a hundred years out of fashion, all of them looking at the sky like it was a rumor they'd finally gotten confirmed.

Forty-one of them lived in Ashmoor now. Nine of them slept in our back rooms until we got the upstairs sorted.

Which was, I suppose, the other thing nobody tells you: freedom is loud. Freed souls don't know how to sleep in beds. They don't know what money is for, or weather, or why bread goes stale. They stand in doorways at 3 a.m. watching the rain like it's a miracle, because for them it is, and they wake the whole house asking questions — *what is this fruit called, why does the sun move, who decided Tuesday* — and you answer them, and then they ask again tomorrow because wonder doesn't retain information, wonder just wants to keep wondering.

I wouldn't have traded it for all the magic in the mountain.

* * *

Our first customer, on the first proper day of business, was a dead woman named Hesper who had been a lantern in the Underqueen's court for sixty-three years and had, before that, been a midwife in the marsh villages. She came in at dawn because she still didn't trust the sun to stay up without supervision, and she wanted a charm against nightmares.

"I don't make those anymore," I told her. "Can't, actually. The talent went with the stone."

Hesper looked at me the way the freed ones sometimes did — that long, level look of someone who has watched centuries of people lie to themselves and finds the habit tiresome. "Child. You pulled forty souls out of a god-stone with your bare will. Whatever you've got left, I'll take two of it."

So I made her a charm. No magic in it — a river stone, a knot of blue thread, a scrap of paper on which I'd written, in my own hand, *you are allowed to rest*. I tied it off the way Sef taught me, with the knot-work that was half craft and half prayer, and I handed it over, and Hesper held it in both palms and cried, quietly, the way people cry when they've forgotten how.

"That's not nothing," she said.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

That became the shop's whole thesis, though it took us a while to say it out loud. Wendell catalogued the books — real ones, mostly, rescued from his ruined shop and donated by sympathetic collectors, plus a shelf of the genuinely dangerous texts we kept behind a locked case more for the neighbors' peace of mind than anything else. Nix ran the counter and the rumors and, unofficially, the entire informal economy of Ashmoor's recovering underworld, which turned out to overlap with legitimate commerce about seventy percent, to everyone's mild embarrassment. And I did the charms.

Not magic. Something adjacent. Knots and stones and written words and, above all, *teaching* — because within a month it became clear that what the freed souls needed wasn't trinkets. It was instruction. How to hold a pen when your hands remembered quills made of featherless things. How to haggle. How to read, if they'd been taken before they learned. How to sit in a crowded market without flinching when someone laughed too loudly, because laughter in the Undercourt had always meant something was about to be eaten.

I taught a class on Thursdays. Attendance varied between five and thirty depending on weather and whether Tam was cooking, because Tam's cooking drew a crowd the way nothing else in Ashmoor did.

* * *

My brother. Even now, months on, I turn the words over like a coin I'm checking for counterfeit.

Tam woke on the floor of the shattered court with the whole freed host around him, and the first thing he said — before *where am I*, before *what year is it*, before *who are you* — was my name. He didn't know why he knew it. His memory of me was a child-shaped hole with a few bright fragments stuck to the edges: a girl who whittled him boats, a girl who sang badly on purpose to make him laugh. Everything else had been sold. Ten years of him, gone down a hungry throat, and what climbed back up the mountain was a man of maybe twenty-five years' total living, thin as a held breath, looking at his grown sister like she was a stranger wearing a familiar face.

We're working on it. Slowly. Some evenings he remembers something new — the smell of our mother's kitchen, the name of the dog we never actually had but apparently both invented independently, which we've decided counts as evidence of sibling telepathy. Some evenings he looks at me and I can see him searching, and I let him search, because you can't hurry a soul back into itself any more than you can hurry bread to rise. Sef's voice, in my head, saying *gently, gently, it's a living thing.* Turns out that applies to people too. Applies to everything, probably. Sef knew a lot of things. That was never his problem.

He runs the kitchen now. The shop's back room has a stove and a long table and a standing invitation, and on any given evening you'll find a dozen freed souls and a reformed pickpocket and a rare-book appraiser arguing about soup, and Tam moving among them with a ladle and a look of fierce concentration, rebuilding himself one meal at a time. He makes the flatbread our mother used to make. He makes it slightly wrong. Nobody tells him. We eat it like it's holy, because it is.

Sef came once. Three weeks after the shattering, standing in the doorway dripping marsh water, holding a jar of honey like a peace offering, which — knowing Sef — it absolutely was, and also a bribe, and also possibly a weapon; with him you could never rule out the third option. He looked at the shop, at the freed souls learning letters at the big table, at me with no magic and no fear, and his face did something complicated that I chose, generously, to read as pride.

"You could have come to the wedding," I said. There hadn't been a wedding. Wendell and I had simply, somewhere in the months of rebuilding, stopped saying goodbye at night, and everyone had noticed before we had, and Nix had thrown a party and charged admission.

"Wasn't invited."

"You didn't need an invitation. You're the mentor. Mentors show up uninvited, it's traditional."

"Hm." He set the honey on the counter. "Your knots have improved."

"Ten years of practice."

"Mm. Shame about the power."

"Sef."

"Aye?"

"Thank you." I said it before I could decide not to. "For what you did. Selling Tam. Buying me those ten years. It was monstrous and it was wrong and I understand it, and I'm glad I understand it, and I'm still furious, and I think all of those can be true."

He was quiet a long moment. Old eyes, marsh-flat and patient, taking my measure one last time. "Aye," he said finally. "They can." And then, because he was Sef: "The honey's for the tea. Don't let the thief near it, she'll water it down and resell the difference."

Nix, from across the room, without looking up: "He knows me so well."

He stayed for supper. He left before dawn, the way he always did, and I didn't watch him go, because some doors close better when you're not standing in them. But I kept the honey jar. It's on the shelf behind the counter now, empty, holding paintbrushes. That's the kind of charm I deal in these days.

* * *

The Guild sent a representative in the autumn. A young woman, nervous, clutching a folder of forms, who stood in our shop surrounded by freed souls and looked like she'd rather be swallowed by the mountain.

"We're — the Office is conducting a census," she said. "Of, um. Returned persons. There's a registration process. For public safety."

Nix leaned on the counter and smiled the smile that had gotten her out of more prisons than she'd been in. "Sure. Registration. And what does the registrant get?"

"Legal... recognition. Protections. Access to the resettlement stipend."

"And what does the Office get?"

The young woman flushed. Behind her, Hesper — who had appointed herself the freed community's unofficial ambassador, on the grounds that she'd spent sixty-three years watching politicians and therefore knew exactly how they worked — folded her arms.

"Here's how this goes," Hesper said pleasantly. "You leave your forms. We fill them out or we don't, as we choose. And the Office learns that if it wants cooperation from forty-one people who spent decades as living lamps in a god's basement, it might begin with an apology rather than a clipboard."

There was a silence in which several possible futures balanced on a knife's edge.

Then the young woman closed her folder, took a breath, and said, "I'm sorry. Genuinely. My grandmother was taken in 'forty-one. I grew up thinking she was dead." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Hesper's arms unfolded, and just like that the knife-edge tipped toward mercy.

She comes on Thursdays now. She's learning letters alongside the others. Her name is Bree, and she's terrible at haggling, and Nix is teaching her, and I have high hopes.

Because that's the thing about the light, I've come to believe. It's not a place you arrive. It's a thing you hand each other, person to person, in shops and kitchens and badly-run classes, and it doesn't matter how small your piece is, because it regenerates. It's the one charm that works on the giver.

* * *

Closing time, on the shop's first anniversary. Wendell locks the door while Nix counts the till and lies about the total, and I bank the fire, and outside the window Ashmoor's night market is setting up — the same stalls, the same lantern-light, the same city that outlawed everything I am and then, slowly, clumsily, learned better.

"Inventory," Wendell says, settling beside me on the counter, which is against health regulations and also tradition. "One crooked sign. One haunted cookbook shelf. One ex-thief skimming the till."

"Heard that."

"Meant you to." He bumps his shoulder against mine. "And one hedge-witch with no magic, no fear, and — according to Thursday's class — the best handwriting in Ashmoor."

"Low bar. Half my students are a century out of practice."

"Still." He's quiet a moment, watching the lamplighters work down the street. "Do you ever miss it? The power?"

I consider it honestly, because he deserves honesty and because I promised myself I'd never lie to him, not even comfortably. "Sometimes. Like you miss a language you used to dream in. But Wendell — I spent ten years building walls, and I thought the walls were keeping me safe. They were just keeping me alone. The magic was never the danger. The loneliness was." I look around the shop — the books, the knots, the smell of Tam's bread drifting from the back, Nix humming as she cheats the arithmetic, the empty honey jar on its shelf. "I traded a god-stone for this. Best bargain I ever made, and I wasn't even the one holding the scales."

From the back room, Tam calls that supper's ready, and Nix appears already holding plates, and the evening comes in the way evenings do now — noisy, imperfect, crowded, ours.

Tomorrow there'll be inventory, and Thursday's class, and Bree's haggling lesson, and whatever new question the freed souls bring home about rain or Tuesdays or the sun. Next spring, Hesper says, we should open a second shop in the marsh villages, where the old hedge-craft is fading and somebody ought to teach the children the knot-work before it's lost — and she says it like it's already decided, and knowing Hesper, it is.

But tonight there's just the lamp, and the loaf, and the people who chose to stay.

Gently, gently. It's a living thing.

I put out the light, and it doesn't frighten me at all.
