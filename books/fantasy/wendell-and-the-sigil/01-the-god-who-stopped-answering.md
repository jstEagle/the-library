## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The shrine at Grayfall Pass had been built by hands that expected to be thanked.

Rosalind could see it in the joinery — the careful dovetailing of the roof beams, the way the stone altar had been dressed smooth on its top face and left rough everywhere else, because no one was meant to look at the sides. Someone had loved this work. Someone had climbed three thousand feet with a chisel and a mallet and a head full of certainty that Vael the Keeper would notice, would care, would answer.

She knelt anyway. Her knees cracked in the cold like green wood splitting.

"Vael." Her breath fogged against the altar stone. "It's Rosalind Ishikawa. Sworn blade, ninth year of service. You know this. You knew it every day for nine years, and then you stopped."

The shrine said nothing. Shrines generally didn't, but there was a difference between a shrine's ordinary silence and *this* silence. Ordinary silence was a held breath. This was a room where someone had died.

Fourteen months now. Fourteen months since she'd knelt in a chapel in Corvane with her palm flat on the anvil-stone and felt — nothing. Not anger from her god, not absence-as-punishment, not even the neutral quiet of a father working in another room. Just a hole where a voice had been, clean-edged, as if it had been cut out with a knife.

Every other sworn blade in the company had felt it the same hour. She'd checked. That was the worst part, maybe — standing in the yard while forty men and women compared the shape of their emptinesses, all of them describing the same wound.

"You owe me nothing," she told the altar. That was doctrine; she'd said it a thousand times to frightened converts. The gods owe us nothing. Service is its own architecture. "But I'm asking anyway. Not for a miracle. A word. A nudge. Tell me which road."

Wind moved through the pass. Snow ticked against the shrine's roof. Somewhere below, a crow complained about something.

Rosalind waited until her knees had gone from aching to numb, which was her version of giving a god a fair hearing, and then she stood, brushed the snow from her trousers, and went back down the mountain to be broke in person.

* * *

Brask smelled the way it always smelled in late autumn: woodsmoke, wet wool, tanneries, and the particular sourness of a town that had decided winter was coming and intended to drink about it. Rosalind came down off the pass road at dusk and went straight to the Gilded Antler, because the Antler kept a contract board behind the bar and because Maggit the barkeep still extended her credit, though the extension had grown thin enough to see light through.

The board was a humiliation.

She read it twice to be sure. *Cart driver wanted, Brask to Hallowmere, pay on delivery.* No. *Wanted: man to clear beaver dams, must swim.* Certainly not. *Bounty: deserter from the Third Lances, last seen south.* She'd taken deserter bounties before and hated every hour of them; hunting men who'd done nothing worse than stop wanting to die for someone else sat poorly in a stomach already full of other things. And the fee was eleven marks. Eleven marks wouldn't cover what she owed Maggit, let alone the resupply she needed if she meant to winter anywhere above the snowline.

"You're looking at it wrong," Maggit said, sliding her a bowl of barley soup she hadn't ordered and would be charged for. He was a big soft man with a beard he trimmed into shapes when business was bad, and business had been bad long enough that his beard currently resembled a failing topiary. "You're reading it like a mercenary. Read it like a poor woman."

"I'm a sworn blade of the Order of the Kept Word."

"You're a woman whose order hasn't paid dues in a year because," Maggit dropped his voice with the delicacy of a man handling a live coal, "there's no word being kept. Everyone knows, Roz. Half your brothers and sisters took farm work. Old Denna's running a caravanserai outside Hallowmere. The ones who stayed sworn are the ones too proud to eat."

Rosalind ate her soup. It was good, which annoyed her.

"The escort job," Maggit said. "The one through Thornfell. It's miserable, but it pays thirty, and the client's desperate enough to add food."

"Miserable how?"

"Winter convoy. Two wagons, salt fish and lamp oil, up the Thorn Road to the mining camps at Cerenne's foot. Three weeks, maybe four if the weather turns." He wiped the bar with a rag that had seen better centuries. "Nobody wants it because nobody comes back down until spring, and the camps are grim, and the Thorn Road past Waystation Nine has bandits this year. Real ones, not boys with knives. Deserters from somewhere, well armed."

"And the client?"

"A factor named Ollus Peale. Fat, nervous, talks too much. He's sitting right over there pretending to drink that wine."

Rosalind turned. In the corner booth sat exactly the man described: round, sweating despite the cold, clutching a cup of wine he'd been nursing so long it had probably achieved sentience. He caught her looking and flinched, then rallied, waving her over with the desperate cheer of a drowning man hailing a gull.

She finished her soup first. Let him sweat a little longer. It was petty, but a mercenary's dignity lived in small cruelties these days.

* * *

Ollus Peale talked too much, exactly as advertised. Rosalind learned within ten minutes that he had a wife in Corvane who despised him, a brother-in-law who'd gotten him the factorship, a theory about why lamp oil prices would rise come midwinter ("supply, Mistress Ishikawa, *supply*, it's all in the numbers"), and a chronic fear of heights that made the Thorn Road his personal nightmare.

What she also learned, listening the way she listened — letting words pile up around the few that mattered — was that Peale wasn't just hauling salt fish. The second wagon carried crates marked FISH OIL that weighed wrong. Too dense. Packed too carefully. And Peale watched those crates the way a man watches a debt collector cross a room.

"That wagon stays sealed," he said, for the third time. "Whatever you hear shifting in there, whatever sounds—"

"Sounds?"

"Settling. Cargo settles." He mopped his forehead with a handkerchief already gray with use. "Thirty marks, plus provisions, plus five more on delivery at Camp Ferrow. You keep me alive, keep the wagons rolling, and don't open anything."

Rosalind turned her cup slowly on the table. "I don't haul contraband, factor. If the Choir stops us on the road and finds whatever's in that wagon, they won't ask whether I knew. They'll burn me with you."

Peale's face did something complicated. Fear, calculation, and under both, a strange flicker of shame. "It's not contraband. It's—" He lowered his voice to almost nothing. "It's votive goods. Old ones. From before. There are collectors in the camps, men who pay well for pre-erasure pieces, and the Choir doesn't patrol the Thorn Road in winter because even zealots have sense enough to stay home by the fire. Nobody gets hurt. Things move. That's all trade ever is."

Pre-erasure pieces. Rosalind kept her expression flat, but inside, something old and tired turned over.

A century ago, Vaelmoor had been erased. Not conquered — erased. The armies had come, or the gods had, depending on which drunk you asked, and afterward there were no maps showing Vaelmoor, no songs naming its kings, no graves marking its dead. The kingdom east of the Cerenne range had simply been subtracted from the world, and the world had agreed to forget, the way a body agrees to forget pain once the wound closes. Even now, if you tried to say certain names aloud, your tongue stumbled. Scholars called it the Unmaking. The Hollow Choir called it mercy. The Choir said Vaelmoor had been a cancer on the soul of the world, that its people had worshipped wrongly, hoarded divinity, and that the gods themselves had consented to the amputation.

And then, fourteen months ago, every god in Thornfell had gone silent at once, and Rosalind had privately begun to wonder whether the amputation had cut deeper than anyone admitted.

"I'll take the job," she heard herself say.

Peale blinked. "You will? Just like that?"

"I'm expensive when I'm cheap." She stood. "We leave tomorrow at first light. You'll want to buy better boots than those."

* * *

That night she counted her money on the floor of a rented room above a farrier's shop: two marks and seven coppers, a whetstone worn to a sliver, and her oath-token, which was worth nothing and everything.

The token was a small iron square, stamped with the sigil of the Kept Word — a closed door. Every sworn blade carried one. When you swore, the priest pressed it into your palm and Vael's presence pressed into you behind it, warm as a hand on the back of your neck, and you were never alone again. For nine years she had never been alone again. She'd prayed in ditches and on battlefields and once, memorably, upside down in a ravine with a broken collarbone, and every time, the warmth had been there. Not answers, usually. Just presence. Company.

Then, fourteen months ago, on an ordinary Tuesday, the hand had lifted.

She held the token up to the candlelight. Iron doesn't tarnish much, but hers had dulled, the sigil going soft at the edges, as if the stamp were fading from the metal. Or as if the metal were forgetting.

Other sworn blades had thrown theirs away. Denna had hurled hers into the Whitefen and told everyone it felt like spitting out a rotten tooth. But Rosalind had been raised by a mother who kept a dead husband's coat on its hook for twenty years, dusted weekly, and she understood something the others didn't, or couldn't bear to: that duty outlives its object. That you can serve a thing after it's gone, and the serving changes nothing except you, and sometimes that's enough. Sometimes that's the whole point.

"You'd laugh at me," she told the token. "If you're anywhere. If there's an anywhere."

The candle guttered. Downstairs, the farrier's dog barked twice at nothing and settled.

She put the token away and did what she always did instead of despairing, which was make lists. Rope: had some, needed more. Rations for four weeks: buy at dawn. Arrowheads: adequate. The pass road conditions: ask the quarrymen. Bandits past Waystation Nine: assume yes, plan for six, hope for fewer. It was a good list. Lists were how she kept the silence from getting in — each item a small stone in a wall against the vast cold question of *why*.

Because that was the question she never let herself finish thinking. Why would the gods go silent? Gods didn't die — everyone knew that, it was catechism, it was bedrock. Gods didn't sleep, didn't sulk, didn't abandon. Unless. Unless something had happened to whatever they were anchored to. Unless the erasure a century ago had been like pulling a keystone and everyone had simply been waiting, all this time, for the arch to notice.

She rolled herself in her blanket and stared at the ceiling beams.

Three weeks on the Thorn Road. Salt fish, lamp oil, and whatever wept softly inside crates marked FISH OIL. Thirty-five marks and a winter in the mining camps, where she could think, and pray to a god who wasn't listening, and wait for spring like everyone else who'd been left behind.

It was a miserable job. She'd had worse. She'd had better, too, back when the world had a god in it, but those days were inventory now, archived, done.

Sleep came slow and thin. In her half-dreams, she knelt again at the Grayfall shrine, and this time the silence answered — not with words but with weather, snow falling upward, and somewhere east beyond the mountains, in country that wasn't on any map, something small was calling a name. Hers, maybe. Or maybe just a name, any name, the way lost things call.

* * *

They left at first light, as promised, and the first week went the way miserable jobs go: cold, dull, and punctuated by Peale.

He talked about supply chains. He talked about his wife's opinion of his posture. He talked about the Choir — carefully, glancing around as though white robes might be lurking behind a boulder on the empty Thorn Road — and how their processions through Brask had doubled in the last year, how they'd bought the old granary for a chapterhouse, how they sang at dawn in a harmony that made his teeth ache. "Devout folk," he concluded, in the tone of a man describing a disease he hoped was benign. "Very devout. Very certain. I admire certainty."

"Do you," Rosalind said, from the front of the column, where she'd been walking point since dawn and wanted only for him to shut up.

"They say the silence is temporary. That the gods withdrew to consider us. That if we purify—" He caught her look and subsided into cargo-related muttering.

On the ninth day it began to snow in earnest, the fat slow flakes of a real Thornfell winter settling in for a long argument, and on the eleventh day they reached Waystation Nine, a squat stone barn full of hostile strangers and excellent stew, and on the thirteenth day, past the waystation, they found the first sign of the bandits: a freight sled abandoned in a drift, its team cut loose, its driver nowhere, blood on the snow frozen brown-black and furred with frost.

Peale looked at the blood and was, for the first time all trip, silent.

"We'll double the watch at night," Rosalind said. "You sleep in the wagon with your crates. If I whistle twice, you get under the canvas and stay there no matter what you hear."

"What will I hear?"

"Hopefully nothing."

They camped that night in the lee of a rockfall, wagons circled, the horses steaming in the cold. Rosalind took first watch, as she always did, standing at the perimeter with her bow strung and her breath streaming silver, watching the dark between the pines.

This far north, past the waystations, the world got quiet in a way that had texture. The mountains rose black on either side of the road, and ahead, when the clouds tore open, she could see Mount Cerenne itself, vast and moonlit, wearing its glaciers like a hood. Somewhere beyond it lay the place that wasn't there anymore. She found her eyes drawn to it despite herself — the notch in the eastern ridge that maps showed as blank paper, that chartmakers labeled only *UNSURVEYED* in small apologetic letters.

Her god had never spoken to her about Vaelmoor. Priests didn't either; the subject slid off doctrine like rain off oilcloth. But she remembered, from her first year of service, an old sworn blade named Corvic who drank too much one festival night and told her he'd once patrolled the border ranges, back before the erasure, back when there was a border. He said the valleys beyond Cerenne had been the loudest places in the world. Not with noise — with presence. Every stone humming with godlight. He said it was like standing next to a choir you couldn't hear yet, feeling the note build in your chest.

Then he'd cried into his beer, and in the morning he denied everything.

Rosalind watched the blank notch in the ridge and felt the oath-token against her sternum, dull iron, faintly cold. For a moment — a moment she'd later count among the strangest of her life — she thought she felt it warm. One degree, maybe two. Like a hand on the back of the neck, there and gone.

She turned, scanning the camp. The horses shifted. Peale snored inside the wagon like a man being paid for it. Snow fell.

And then, faint, from somewhere off the road to the north — from the trees, from the drifts, from the direction of everything unsurveyed — she heard a sound that did not belong to any animal she knew.

A voice. Small. High. Saying something over and over in a language she didn't recognize, though the shape of it snagged on her ear strangely, familiar the way a dream is familiar.

Not calling for help. Calling a name.

She stood very still in the falling snow, bow half-raised, and listened.
