## 2. A Knock in the Snow

The storm came in off the western peaks an hour past dusk, the kind of wind that didn't howl so much as *insist*, pressing at the cabin's shutters like a creditor at a door. Rosalind sat by the fire with her boots off and her sword across her knees, doing the thing she did most nights now: maintaining a weapon for a war that had stopped hiring her.

She'd oiled the blade twice already. She did it a third time.

Outside, Thornfell was turning white and mean. The passes would close within the week — earlier than any winter in living memory, the trapper who'd sold her salt had said, spitting into the wind like he could argue with it. Which meant no caravans. No merchants needing escort through the high country. No contracts until spring, if spring bothered to come at all.

No contracts since midsummer, if she was honest, and she tried to be, because lying to herself was the one dishonesty her vows had never forgiven.

She set the sword down on the table beside the shrine.

It was a small thing, the shrine. A shelf of black pine, a bowl of river stones, a candle stub she couldn't afford to burn but burned anyway. At its center, a sliver of iron shaped like a broken arrow — the sign of Vethra, the Unbowed, patron of soldiers and sworn blades and everyone else who made their living standing between other people and harm. For eleven years Rosalind had prayed to it every morning and every night, and for eleven years Vethra had answered: not in words, never in words, but in the warm weight of certainty behind the sternum. In the sureness of a drawn bow. In the knowledge, absolute as arithmetic, that her hand would not fail when it mattered.

For four months now, the certainty had been silence.

She lit the candle. She knelt, because kneeling was habit, and habit was all that remained of faith.

"Vethra." Her breath fogged in the cold room. "I don't know what I did."

Nothing. The flame bent in a draft from under the door and righted itself. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse went about its business with more purpose than the gods had shown all autumn.

"I've kept the oaths," she said. "I haven't taken coin from a widow or a corpse. I buried Halla with full rites even though we were enemies by then, and you *saw* me do it, so don't pretend—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated the kneeling, hated the little iron arrow that sat there gleaming like a tooth pulled from a dead god's jaw.

Four months. And she wasn't the only one. Every sworn blade she'd met on the roads this season wore the same hollow look, the same careful blankness where conviction used to live. Half the mercenary companies in the lowlands had dissolved. The other half had turned bandit, which was just dissolution with better bookkeeping.

"Fine," she told the shrine. She stood. Her knee popped; she was thirty-four and everything popped now. "Fine. Keep your silence. I kept mine for eleven years, so we're even."

She pinched out the candle. That was the whole of prayer, these days: light it, speak to nothing, put it out. A ritual reduced to its skeleton, still walking around out of stubbornness.

She was pulling her blanket up over her shoulders when she heard the knock.

Three strikes. Small ones. Almost apologetic, if a fist in a blizzard could be called apologetic.

Rosalind went very still. Nobody knocked on her door. Nobody knew where her door was — that was the point of a cabin two days' climb above Brask, at the treeline, at the end of a goat track that didn't exist on any map worth trusting. The last visitor had been a tax assessor fourteen months ago, and he'd needed rescuing from a snowdrift before he could assess anything.

Her hand found the sword hilt without her deciding to reach for it. Old reflexes were the only prayers that got answered anymore.

Another knock. Weaker. Then something that wasn't a knock at all — a soft collapse against the wood, the sound of a body giving up the argument with gravity.

*Bandit bait,* said the part of her brain that had kept her alive through two wars and a dozen bad contracts. *A child crying in the snow is the oldest trap there is. You open the door, you catch a crossbow bolt, and some clever bastard takes your boots, your blade, and your salt.*

But the sound had been small. And there hadn't been a second set of footsteps in the snow — she'd have heard them, even through the wind; she'd been listening to snow for half her life. One set of steps, small and dragging, and then the fall against the door.

And underneath the calculation, in a place she didn't like to look at, another voice: *You know what it is to be left out in the cold by something that should have answered.*

She opened the door.

The storm shoved its way in first — a fist of white and screaming cold that snuffed the banked fire down to embers and sent ash swirling. Behind the storm, crumpled against her threshold like a dropped bundle of laundry, was a child.

A boy, maybe seven years old, maybe eight. Dark hair matted with ice. Wearing what might once have been a fine coat — she could see the ghost of embroidery at the cuffs, silver thread gone gray — now soaked through and frozen stiff. His lips were the blue-black of old bruises. In his right hand, clenched so tight his knuckles had gone bloodless, he held a stone.

Not a rock. A stone. Cut, deliberate, the size of a hen's egg, carved with a symbol she didn't recognize: interlocking lines, angular, dense, like writing compressed into geometry. Even frost-crusted, it seemed to hold the dim light differently than stone should.

"Wendell," the boy said.

His voice was a rasp. He said it again — "*Wendell*" — and she understood after a moment that it wasn't a word in any language she knew. It was a name. His own. He was introducing himself to the only person left between him and the dark, and he was doing it in a tongue that sounded like water running over rocks in a cave, all soft consonants and long vowels, nothing like the trade-speech of Thornfell or the clipped border dialects or the church-tongue of the lowland temples.

"Ishikawa," she heard herself say. Stupid. Automatic. Eleven years of identifying yourself to sergeants and clients and dying men. "Rosalind Ishikawa."

He blinked at her. Snow was building on his eyelashes. Then he said something else, urgently, a whole sentence of that flowing language, and pointed back down the mountain — toward the pass, toward whatever he'd walked away from — and his face did something complicated: fear and grief and a desperate, furious hope, all fighting for the same few inches of skin.

Then his eyes rolled back and he fell forward into her arms.

* * *

She worked on him for two hours and spent most of that time telling herself she was being an idiot.

Mercenary doctrine was clear on foundlings. She'd been taught it at fifteen by a scarred veteran named Odo, who'd learned it in a worse school: *Anything that shows up helpless wants something, and anything that wants something is either a liability or a lure.* Odo's solution to foundlings had been unprintable. Rosalind's version was merely cold — carry them to the nearest settlement, collect whatever finder's fee existed, walk away. Sentiment was a debt you paid with interest, and interest was always collected at the worst possible moment.

But the nearest settlement was Brask, a day and a half down through terrain that was currently trying to kill everything on it, and the boy's core temperature was somewhere south of survivable. Moving him tonight was a death sentence. Leaving him outside was a death sentence with extra steps.

So: idiot it was.

She stripped the frozen coat off him — carefully, peeling it where it had welded to his skin — wrapped him in her spare blanket and her own bedroll, and held him in front of the rebuilt fire, feeding the flames until the cabin glowed orange and the shadows danced. She rubbed warmth back into his fingers and toes, working from the extremities inward the way a field medic had once shown her, murmuring curses under her breath the entire time, a steady litany of profanity that was, in its way, also a prayer. To whom, she didn't ask.

The sigil-stone she pried from his grip with effort — the boy's fingers had locked around it like a root around rock — and set on the table next to the shrine. Up close, in good light, it was worse than strange. The carving wasn't decorative. It was *textual*. Lines of script wound around the stone's surface in spirals, and the script matched the sounds the boy had made: those same soft, liquid shapes rendered in stone. Someone had taken a language and cut it into a rock the way you'd cut a key.

And the stone was warm. Not fire-warm. It had been sitting in a child's bare hand in a blizzard, and it was warm as a sleeping cat, and when Rosalind touched it she felt something faint move through her palm — not heat exactly, more like the memory of heat, like standing near a hearth in a house that had burned down years ago.

She put it down and wiped her hand on her trousers and did not touch it again.

The boy woke near midnight.

She knew it before his eyes opened — his breathing changed, the shallow flutter of deep cold giving way to something steadier. When his eyes did open they were dark, nearly black, and they fixed on her with none of a child's usual confusion. He looked at her the way a soldier looks at a map: assessing, placing, deciding what she was.

"You're alive," she said in trade-speech. "Good. Don't do that again."

He tilted his head. He understood tone if not words — she could see it, the way his gaze tracked her face, cataloguing. Then he spoke, a short question, rising inflection, and gestured at himself, at the cabin, at the storm beyond the shuttered window.

"No," she said, and shook her head, and tapped her own chest. "I don't understand you. Do you understand *me*?"

Something flickered across his face — disappointment, quickly mastered, too quickly for a child. Children didn't master things. Children cried and raged and let feelings pour out of them like spilled milk. This boy swallowed his disappointment like a soldier swallowing bad news, and that frightened her more than the storm did.

He looked around the cabin instead. Methodically. The weapons rack. The dried meat hanging from the beam. The shrine with its dead candle and its iron arrow. When his eyes reached the shrine, they stopped.

He stared at it for a long time. Then he looked at the sigil-stone on the table, and then back at the shrine, and his expression did the complicated thing again — grief and recognition tangled together, like a man finding his hometown's name on a list of the dead.

"What?" Rosalind said. "What is it?"

He said a single word. Softly. Almost reverent. It flowed out of him like the rest of his speech, but slower, weighted, and he laid his hand flat over his heart as he said it, and Rosalind felt the hair rise on her arms, because she had seen that gesture before — a thousand times, in a thousand shrines. Hand over heart. Acknowledgment. Homage.

The boy was greeting her god. Or mourning it. With that stone in his lap and that language in his mouth, she couldn't tell the difference, and neither, she suspected, could he.

"Sleep," she said roughly, and pushed him gently down onto the cot, and pulled the blanket to his chin. "We'll figure out the rest tomorrow. If you try to leave in that weather, you'll die, and I'll have wasted two hours of firewood, and I resent wasting firewood."

He watched her settle back into her chair by the fire, sword within reach, boots back on. He watched her the way she'd once watched strangers in camp: waiting to learn whether the night would be safe.

Somewhere past the middle hours, his breathing slowed into true sleep, and Rosalind sat alone with the fire and the wind and the stone on her table, and admitted the thing she'd been refusing to admit since she'd opened the door.

She hadn't opened it because of tactics. She'd run the tactical argument afterward, honestly enough, but it hadn't been the reason. The reason was smaller and worse: a knock in the night, and four months of silence, and the sudden unbearable thought that if she let one more thing freeze to death on her threshold, she might as well lie down in the snow beside it.

Odo would have laughed himself sick.

She picked up the sigil-stone again despite herself. Turned it in the firelight. The spiral script caught the glow and seemed almost to move, letters chasing letters, a sentence going somewhere. She thought about the boy's hand over his heart. She thought about the shrine, and the iron arrow, and the silence where certainty used to be.

*Everything that shows up helpless wants something,* Odo had said.

Fine. Let it want something. Everything alive wanted something. She was so tired of guarding a life that wanted nothing, that had been pared down to firewood and salt and a god who wouldn't speak.

The boy made a small sound in his sleep — a word, half-formed, in that flowing tongue. It sounded, though she knew it couldn't be, like *home*.

Rosalind fed another log to the fire and kept watch until dawn.

* * *

Morning came gray and vicious. The storm hadn't broken; it had settled in, the way storms sometimes did above the treeline, digging in like an army preparing a siege. Through the gap in the shutters, the world was a white roar.

Rosalind stood at the window with a cup of barley tea and did the arithmetic she was good at.

One: the boy couldn't travel. Not today, probably not for days. Two: she had food for one person for three weeks, maybe four if she stretched it, and a mouth that ate like a growing child would cut that in half. Three: Brask was a day and a half in fair weather and a week in this, and this wasn't breaking. Four —

Four was the question she kept circling. *Where did he come from?*

There was nothing above her cabin. Nothing but the pass, and the high fells, and the ruined watchtowers of an old war nobody remembered the reason for. No villages. No homesteads. The nearest anything was Brask, downhill, and no family from Brask sent a seven-year-old alone up a mountain in the first storm of winter wearing a coat with silver thread at the cuffs.

Unless he hadn't come up the mountain. Unless he'd come *down* it — from the far side, from the high passes where the maps went vague and the old surveyors' notes used words like *anomalous* and *survey party lost* and, in one case, in a shaky hand Rosalind had once read in a Brask archive, *the valley that will not be recorded*.

Superstition. Mountain talk. But the coat was real, and the stone was real, and the stone was warm.

Behind her, the boy stirred on the cot. She turned. He was sitting up, hair wild, watching her with those dark assessing eyes, and he had the blanket clutched around his shoulders like a cloak of office.

"Hungry?" she asked, and mimed eating, because apparently she'd become the kind of person who mimed at children.

He nodded. Then, before she could turn to the bread, he raised one hand — *wait* — and pointed at the sigil-stone on the table. He said something urgent and low, several sentences, and pointed from the stone to the window, to the north, to the high country beyond it. His voice cracked on the last word and he pressed his lips together hard, mastering it again, that awful adult control.

"I don't know what you're saying," Rosalind said quietly. "I'm sorry. I don't understand a word of it."

The boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he slid off the cot, crossed the floor barefoot, and stood in front of her — small, frost-burned, unbowed — and held out the sigil-stone in both hands. An offering. Or a plea. Or, given the gravity in his face, a commission.

He said one word. The same soft, weighted word he'd said to the shrine.

Rosalind took the stone. It pulsed its faint impossible warmth against her palm, and outside the wind screamed down off the peaks like something vast and patient calling a name it had lost.

Whatever the boy was running from — or running *to* — it was north. And north, come spring, was the only direction she had left.
