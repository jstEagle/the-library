## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

Brask smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular despair of a town that had once been on a trade route and no longer was. Rosalind led Wendell down the high street with her hand fisted in the back of his coat, not because he ran off — he didn't, he stayed pressed to her side like a burr — but because she needed to hold something while she thought.

Three days since the cabin. Three days of porridge stretched thin, of the boy watching her with those dark unblinking eyes, of words she couldn't parse spilling out of him at night like water from a cracked jug. He'd stopped shivering on the second day. He ate everything she gave him and asked for nothing, which was somehow worse than asking.

She had coin enough for a week. Maybe ten days if she haggled the rooms down and ate standing up. After that, nothing. The escort contract was dead along with the merchant who'd posted it, found frozen two miles from where Rosalind had been paid to see him safely past. She'd collected half up front; the widow wanted it back and Rosalind had given it back, because that was what a woman did when her god went quiet and all she had left was the shape of her own honor.

So: Brask. Lowland, lawless-adjacent, full of people who bought and sold things that shouldn't be bought and sold. If anyone could put a name to the boy's tongue, it would be someone here.

"Stay close," she said.

Wendell looked up at her and answered in that language — soft, liquid, consonants falling away at the ends of words like snow sliding off a roof. He said it gently, as if explaining something to someone slow.

"I don't know what you're telling me," she said. "That's the whole problem."

He touched his own chest. "Wendell." Then hers. She'd taught him *Rosalind* by pointing, over and over, until he'd shaped it around whatever his mouth wanted to do with it. It came out wrong every time, but it was her name, and hearing it made something in her chest pull tight in a way she refused to examine.

The archive occupied a former countinghouse near the river, a squat stone building with a sign so faded it read only **CHIVE**. Inside, dust hung in the light from high windows like something suspended mid-fall. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs informed them that consultation cost four coppers per question, six if the question concerned anything before the Concord.

Rosalind put eight coppers on the counter. "I need scholars. Plural."

The clerk counted the coins twice. "The Fellows meet Thursdays."

"It's Wednesday."

"Then tomorrow—"

"Today." She set down two more coppers. "The boy speaks a language. Nobody I've asked can place it. I want it placed."

The clerk's eyes flicked to Wendell, who was studying a wall map with an intensity that should have warned everyone in the room. His lips moved silently, reading sounds that weren't there.

"Where's he from?" the clerk asked.

"That," said Rosalind, "is the question."

* * *

They brought three men and one woman to a back room smelling of lamp oil and old vellum. The first was a fat philologist named Ostermeyer who listened to Wendell recite — the boy stood on a chair, hands behind his back like a soldier at inspection, and spoke for a full minute, unhurried, precise — and then declared it "a Slavic-inflected pidgin, possibly invented, children do this."

"He didn't invent it," Rosalind said.

"Children invent languages constantly. My nephew had one built entirely around the word *blup*."

"This one has grammar. Consistent grammar. He uses past tenses."

Ostermeyer waved a hand as though grammar were a personal failing. The second scholar, a thin woman from the university at Corvel, lasted longer. She made Wendell repeat phrases, wrote them phonetically, frowned at her own notes, made him say them again. In the end she admitted, with visible reluctance, that the vowel system resembled nothing in any family she knew. "It's not degraded anything," she said. "It's not broken anything. It's whole. Languages don't stay whole unless they're spoken."

"Spoken by whom?"

"That's rather my point, mercenary. No one speaks this."

The third man never got past Wendell's pronunciation of his own name. The woman scholar — Maren Voss, she signed the receipt — stayed after the others left, chewing her pen, and finally said: "There are archives. Restricted collections. If this is real, and not some trick—" she looked hard at Rosalind, weighing whether a sellsword could be running a con involving a child and a dead language, and evidently decided the arithmetic didn't favor it, "—there's a man who would know. Old Halloran. Blind as a stone, but he read more before his eyes went than the rest of us have touched. He works evenings, alone. Tell him Voss sent you, and tell him I'll owe him."

"What will this cost me?"

"Nothing now." She gathered her papers. "But if the boy is what I'm starting to fear he might be, sell him to a museum and not to a private collector. Museums feed you."

Rosalind watched her go and felt the first cold weight settle behind her sternum.

* * *

Halloran's room was above a chandler's shop, reached by stairs that groaned like a ship in weather. He opened the door before she knocked, which meant he'd heard them coming, which meant his ears were doing the work his eyes couldn't.

"You're armed," he said. "And there's a child. And Maren Voss's perfume, or what passes for it. Come in before the draft does."

The room was books. Not shelves of books — walls of them, floor to ceiling, stacked double-deep, with narrow canyons between. A single lamp burned on a table beside a chair worn smooth by decades of sitting. Halloran lowered himself into it with the care of a man handling his own skeleton.

"Now," he said. "Voss sent you, which means she heard something she couldn't name. Sit the boy down. Let him talk."

Wendell climbed onto the stool across from the old man and looked at him — really looked, the way he'd looked at nothing else in Brask, the way he'd looked at the sigil stone clutched in his fist these three days without letting go even to eat.

Then he spoke.

He started low, almost formal, the way he'd addressed the scholars, but halfway through the first sentence something changed. His voice loosened. The words came faster, warmer, and Rosalind — who had spent three days hearing this language as beautiful noise — suddenly heard it differently in this small book-lined room. It sounded less foreign here. It sounded like it belonged to the lamplight and the dust and the old man's listening face.

Wendell talked for two minutes. Three. He wasn't performing anymore. He was telling Halloran something, urgently, leaning forward, and the old man sat absolutely still with his hands flat on his knees like a man in prayer.

When the boy finished, the silence went on so long Rosalind thought Halloran had died in his chair.

Then she saw his face.

The color had gone out of it. All of it. His lips were gray, and his blind eyes — milky, useless, aimed slightly past Wendell's shoulder — were wide open, and his hands had begun, very quietly, to shake.

"Halloran?" Rosalind said. "Old man. What is it?"

He didn't answer her. He leaned toward the boy, slow, like a man approaching an altar he'd only ever read about, and said, in a cracked whisper, in the boy's own language:

One word.

Wendell's breath caught. He answered — a short phrase, soft — and Halloran made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and pressed both trembling hands over his mouth.

"Tell me," Rosalind said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Now."

Halloran lowered his hands. When he spoke Common again, each word cost him visibly. "Do you know what Vaelmoor was?"

"A story. A kingdom that got itself erased. Everyone says it's a cautionary tale they tell children about pride."

"It was real." He said it simply, the way you'd say *the sun rose*. "I spent forty years collecting what remained of it. Fragments. A psalter with the covers burned off. A merchant's ledger, half a page. Grave-rubbings. Enough to know the shape of the grammar, never enough to speak it. There are perhaps six people alive who can read Vaelmoorian, and none who can speak it, because speaking requires hearing it spoken, and it has not been spoken aloud in a hundred years." He turned his ruined eyes toward Wendell. "Until now."

Rosalind's hand drifted to her sword hilt without her permission. "You're certain."

"Certain." He laughed, wet and awful. "Child, I've dreamed in that grammar. I've reconstructed conjugations on scraps and prayed to be proven wrong, because do you know what it means? It means everything I believed was a madman's obsession is true. It means the Erasure happened exactly as the forbidden accounts describe. It means—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It means there are people who will kill for this knowledge. And people who will kill to prevent it."

"The Hollow Choir," Rosalind said.

"You know them."

"I know their work." Everyone whose god had gone silent knew their work, though the connection lived mostly in bitter tavern talk. "White robes. They burn things."

"They burn *words*, mercenary. They burned Vaelmoor — not just the country, the *idea*. Every record, every map, every gravestone with the old script. They call it mercy. A wound cauterized." His shaking had steadied into something harder. "And now a living echo walks into my room wearing a soldier's spare coat."

Wendell had gone still on his stool, watching their faces, understanding none of the Common but all of the tone. His fist closed around the sigil stone.

"What is that?" Halloran asked suddenly. "What's he holding? Describe it to me."

Rosalind hesitated. Then she crossed the canyon of books and pried the boy's fingers open — gently; he let her, which surprised her — and described the stone: palm-sized, black, dense as lead, carved with interlocking lines that might have been script or knotwork, warm to the touch in a way stones shouldn't be.

The old man's breath left him in a long, controlled exhale, the way soldiers breathe out before violence.

"A sigil," he said. "The royal sigils of Vaelmoor were cut from mountain-glass the night before the Erasure. Seven of them. Carriers of the blood and the borders. The Choir hunted them for years afterward. They found five." His voice dropped. "Two were never accounted for."

"And you think—"

"I think a hundred years ago, someone ran. Someone took a sigil and a child's inheritance and hid it in the world outside, and I think the line kept its head down and its mouth shut until the language thinned to nothing — except it didn't thin to nothing, because here he sits." Halloran reached out and, with exquisite care, closed Rosalind's fingers back over the stone, folding the boy's hand around hers. "Where did you find him?"

"Frozen on my doorstep. Alone."

"Alone." He tasted the word. "No. Someone put him somewhere they hoped the Choir wouldn't look, and the somewhere failed, or the someones died. Either way—" he gripped her wrist, sudden strength in the old fingers, "—listen to me. You cannot stay in Brask. You cannot stay anywhere with records, because records get searched. You cannot take the southern roads, because the Choir works south. And you must not, under any circumstances, let anyone official learn what he is, because the moment a magistrate writes it down, it exists, and what exists can be owned."

"Owned," Rosalind repeated.

"Or destroyed. Those are the only two verbs that apply to relics." He released her wrist and sagged back in his chair, suddenly looking every year of his age. "Maren Voss knows. I'll speak to her; she's greedy, not cruel, and greed can be bargained with. But the clerk logged your questions. The scholars talked. This town has ears, and some of those ears are paid by the hour."

Rosalind looked down at Wendell. He was watching her with that bottomless patience, and when he caught her eye he said something in Vaelmoorian — a question, from the rising tone — and waited for an answer she couldn't give.

"He asked something," Halloran murmured. "What was it?"

"How would I know?"

"Describe the sound. The last word especially."

She replayed it. "Soft. Two syllables. Like... *home*, almost. But older."

Halloran was quiet a moment. "In the fragments I have, there's a phrase that recurs in letters and last wills. *Vael-sira.* It translates roughly as 'the road back' or 'the way home.'" His blind eyes seemed, for once, to be looking straight through her. "I believe your boy just asked if he could go."

* * *

They left Halloran's by the chandler's back stair, into the alley, into the early dark. Rosalind bought bread, dried meat, a second blanket, paying in scattered coppers so no single transaction would be remembered. She was halfway back to the rooming house, Wendell's hand in hers, when she saw the white robe.

Just one. Across the square, at the mouth of the street that held the archive, talking quietly to the night clerk of the countinghouse. Not searching. Asking. Taking notes.

The clerk pointed.

Not at her — the square was full of people, and the gesture was vague, sweeping. But it swept in her direction, and the white-robed figure turned its head, unhurried, and began to cross the square.

Rosalind turned the other way and walked. Not fast. Fast drew eyes. She walked the way she'd walked past checkpoints her whole career, bored, local, tired, and Wendell matched her stride without being told, and behind them she heard nothing — no shout, no running feet — which meant either safety or patience, and she knew which one zealots could afford.

Back in the room, she barred the door, wedged a chair under the handle, and sat Wendell on the bed with the bread. He ate. She packed.

"We leave before dawn," she told him, knowing he wouldn't understand the words but maybe the tone. "North. Away from everyone who wants to own you or erase you, which as far as I can tell is everyone."

He swallowed his mouthful and looked at her, and said, clearly, deliberately, in that language a nation had died speaking:

"*Vael-sira.*"

Then he pointed north.

Rosalind stood in the lamplight with a bag half-packed and a child pointing at mountains she had no map for, and understood that the question had never been whether she would run. It was how far, and for how long, and what she would become by the end of it.

Somewhere below, in the street, a boot scraped against cobblestone and went still.

She pinched out the lamp.
