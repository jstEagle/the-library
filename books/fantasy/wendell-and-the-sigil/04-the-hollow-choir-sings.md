## 4. The Hollow Choir Sings

The fire started at the third bell past midnight, and Rosalind knew before she smelled the smoke that it was for them.

She had been sleeping badly since Brask — curled on a pallet in the back room of Master Corvin's archive with one hand near her knife and one ear tuned to Wendell's breathing in the next room. The boy slept like a stone that had learned to dream. He made small sounds in that dead language, syllables soft as river stones, and Rosalind had spent three nights telling herself they meant nothing, that a child's sleep-talk was just a child's sleep-talk, even when it made the candle flames lean toward him like listeners.

So when the shouting began in the street — not drunk shouting, not festival shouting, but the flat, organized cadence of men doing a job — she was already moving.

"Wendell." She shook his shoulder once, hard enough to matter. "Up. Shoes. Now."

He came awake the way he always did: instantly, silently, without the confusion of ordinary children. His eyes found hers in the dark and he understood. That was the worst part, she thought later. A seven-year-old who woke knowing the world was hunting him, and wasn't even surprised.

* * *

Master Corvin had told them, that first afternoon, why he'd gone pale at the sound of Wendell's voice.

"Vaelmoorian," the blind archivist had said, fingers pressed to the table as if steadying himself against a blow. "I have read fragments. Charred marginalia. A psalter the Church of the Quiet Flame burned in my grandfather's time and I was foolish enough to copy from memory before the ashes cooled." His sightless eyes had fixed on some middle distance only he could see. "That tongue belonged to Vaelmoor. And Vaelmoor does not belong to anything anymore, because a hundred years ago it was not conquered or destroyed. It was *erased*. Do you understand me, mercenary? Not razed. Unwritten. There are no ruins because there were never any buildings. There are no graves because there were never any dead. Every map, every record, every memory — scoured."

"And him?" Rosalind had asked, jerking her chin at Wendell, who was watching them both with the stillness of a wild animal deciding whether to bolt.

"Then he is either a miracle," Corvin had said quietly, "or an error. And there are people in this world who kill to correct errors."

Rosalind had wanted to laugh it off. She'd wanted to finish her terrible ale, collect the archivist's coin for translating whatever needed translating, and walk back out into a world where gods went silent but kingdoms stayed put. Instead she had asked, "Who?"

And Corvin had said, "The Hollow Choir," in the voice other people used for plagues.

* * *

Now the Choir had come to Corvin's door, and they had brought fire.

Rosalind looked out through the shutter slats and counted. Six in the street, white robes dulled to gray by smoke and night, hoods back, faces lit orange from below by the growing blaze they'd set against the shop's front wall. Two more at the alley mouth behind the building, standing with the bored patience of men guarding a door they expected no one to use. They carried no torches. The fire was their torch. One of them held a censer that swung slow and steady, filling the street with a sweet resinous smoke that did nothing to mask the burning.

A woman stood apart from the six, robed in white so clean it seemed to drink the firelight rather than reflect it. She was singing. That was the thing that crawled up Rosalind's spine and settled there — the woman was singing, low and wordless, a single sustained note that threaded through the crackle of the flames, and the fire burned *in time* with it, flaring on the phrase, settling on the rest.

"The echo," one of the men said. "Corvin. Where is the echo?"

"Please," Corvin's voice, from the front of the shop, wrecked and old. "There is no child here. There is nothing here but paper and—"

The sound that followed was short and wet, and then the singing stopped, and Rosalind felt something in her chest go cold and permanent.

They had killed him. Or they would. It didn't matter which; what mattered was that Master Corvin, who had gone pale at the sound of a dead language and given them a bed anyway, was about to die for it, and every second she stood at this window doing arithmetic was a second bought with his blood.

"Wendell." She kept her voice level. "Back door. Don't run until I say. When I say, you run for the treeline north of the tannery and you don't stop, no matter what you hear. Can you do that?"

He looked at her. In the light leaking through the shutters his face was very pale and very calm, and he said something in Vaelmoorian — two words, soft.

"I don't know what that means."

He touched his own chest, then hers, then pointed north.

"Yeah," she said, throat tight. "Me too. Go."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way violence always does — too fast for the mind, exactly fast enough for the body.

She went out the back door low and quiet, knife already open, and the two men at the alley mouth heard the hinge or smelled her or simply turned at the wrong moment, and after that it was work. Ugly, close, graceless work. The first one was young under his hood, younger than her, and he got half a syllable of that same sustained note out before her blade opened his throat and the note became a gurgle and then nothing. The second was older and better; he got a hand on her wrist and drove her back into the wall hard enough to rattle her teeth, and for one long breath they grappled in the firelight spilling down the alley, his strength against her desperation, his eyes wide with something that wasn't hate — it was *pity*, which was worse.

"You're sworn," he rasped. "I can see it. You're sworn to one of the silent ones. Sister, we're trying to finish the silence. To make it clean. Why would you stand between us and—"

She headbutted him, stamped his knee, and put the knife in under his ribs twice, and he sat down against the wall and looked at her with that same terrible pity while he died.

Rosalind stood over him, breathing hard, blood on her knuckles, and felt the shape of her life rearrange itself around a simple fact: there was no going back. Not to contracts. Not to the shrine that didn't answer. Not to being a woman who took escort jobs and prayed to a god who had stopped listening. She had killed servants of the Church of the Quiet Flame, and the Choir did not forgive, and neither, she suspected, did the paperwork of heaven.

Somewhere inside the shop, the singing started again.

* * *

She ran.

Through the tannery yards, reeking and dark, over a fence that tore her sleeve and the skin beneath it, into the black teeth of the treeline where a small figure waited exactly where she'd told him to wait, hugging the sigil-stone to his chest, breathing in quick white puffs.

"They killed him," Wendell said. It was the longest sentence she'd ever heard him string together in the trade-tongue, and his accent was terrible and perfect. "The old man. I heard."

"Yes."

"He sang to them. Before. He sang the words wrong and they got angry."

Rosalind crouched to his height, and her knees were shaking now that the work was done, now that the fear arrived late the way it always did. "Listen to me. Those people — the ones in white — they want you. Not me, not anyone else. You. And they will burn down every building between here and wherever you're going until they get you. So we're not staying in Brask, we're not staying anywhere with walls, and we're not stopping until I find someone who knows the mountains better than I do. Understand?"

Wendell nodded. Then, in that language older than kingdoms, he said something else, and pointed north again, and this time she caught the shape of it — the same word he'd said in the cabin, the same word he'd murmured in his sleep. A direction. A name. Maybe both.

"Home," she guessed.

His face did something complicated and heartbreaking, and he nodded once.

Behind them, Brask burned. The glow sat on the southern horizon like a second dawn, and above the crackle of it, faint but unbroken, that single sustained note carried through the winter air — patient, unhurried, certain. They knew the child had fled north. They weren't chasing yet.

They were singing him home to them.

Rosalind took Wendell's hand — his fingers were ice, and closed around hers with a grip far stronger than seven years old should allow — and turned her back on the fire, on the contractless life, on the silent shrine and the silent god, and walked into the dark.

North. Toward Thornfell. Toward whatever waited behind the mountains.

Toward, though she didn't know it yet, a drunk cartographer in a gutter outside a tavern called the Drowned Compass, who was about to become the most important man in a country that didn't exist.
