## 5. The Cartographer of Nowhere

The man they called the Cartographer of Nowhere lived above a tannery in Brask's lowest street, where the smell of rendered fat and lye hung so thick that even the gulls wouldn't land. Rosalind smelled him before she saw him — or rather, smelled the street — and understood why no one came looking for Ivo Renner. The stink was a better lock than any door.

She climbed the outside stairs with Wendell on her hip, because his feet had blistered from three days of walking and he refused, with the mute stubbornness of the very young, to admit it. He was heavier than a child his size should be. He carried the sigil stone everywhere now, slept with it under his cheek like a talisman, and when strangers passed too close on the road he would whisper something in that dead language — soft, urgent, the way other children whispered prayers.

The door at the top of the stairs had been kicked in once and repaired badly. Rosalind knocked anyway. Habit. Courtesy. The last two were wearing thin.

"Go away." The voice inside was gravel soaked in wine. "Whatever you're selling, the answer is no. Whatever you're collecting, I have nothing. Whatever you're hunting—" A pause, wet and bitter. "I'm already dead. Ask anyone."

"I'm not selling," Rosalind said. "I'm buying."

Silence. Then a laugh, short and ugly. "Nobody buys what I have."

"Someone will. Open the door."

She heard furniture scrape, a body negotiating with gravity, and then the door swung wide on a man who had been handsome once and had spent the intervening years drinking his cheekbones into ruin. Ivo Renner was maybe fifty, gray-stubbled, wrapped in a surveyor's coat gone green at the cuffs, and behind him the single room was not what she expected.

She had expected squalor. She got an archive.

Maps covered every wall. Not the crude parish charts sold in market squares — real maps, hand-inked, layered, annotated in a cramped precise script. And pinned among them, overlapping them, dominating them like a tumor of paper: maps of Vaelmoor. Dozens of them. Some ancient, edges burned. Some copied and recopied until the ink had gone ghost-gray. Some clearly drawn from imagination, rivers guessed at, mountains shaded in hopeful hatching. One, near the window where the light fell best, was so dense with corrections it had been worked over so many times the vellum had worn thin as cloth.

Wendell made a sound against her shoulder. A small sound. She felt his head turn, and then his whole body went rigid with attention, the way a compass needle goes still over true north.

"Gods' teeth," Renner said, squinting past her. "Is that a child? I don't buy children."

"He comes free." She stepped inside without invitation. "You're Ivo Renner. Formerly royal surveyor to the crown of Vaelmoor."

That landed. She watched it land — watched the drunkard's slack face tighten around something old and hard. "Formerly," he said. "There's a word doing a lot of work in that sentence. There is no crown of Vaelmoor. There is no Vaelmoor. There hasn't been for a hundred years, whatever the madwoman in my hallway says."

"Then explain your walls."

He looked at them. All of them. For a long moment the drunkenness seemed to drain out of him, leaving only the obsession underneath, which was worse and more alive.

"You want the truth?" He didn't wait for it. "Everyone says I'm mad because I map a country that doesn't exist. But here's what nobody asks: *why doesn't it exist?* Kingdoms fall. They get conquered, partitioned, renamed. Their names survive in treaties and old women's songs. Vaelmoor has *nothing*. No ruins anyone can find. No border stones. No graves. You go into the mountains where the histories say it stood and you find — forest. Unbroken forest, older than any kingdom, trees that were supposedly saplings when Vaelmoor's kings held court there. Either a nation of a million souls left no mark on the world—"

"—or the world was made to forget it," Rosalind said.

Renner stopped. Looked at her properly for the first time. "Yes," he said quietly. "Or that."

Wendell slid off her hip. She let him, watching. The boy crossed the room with his blistered feet careful on the floorboards, went straight to the map by the window — the one worn thin with correction — and stood before it, silent.

Then he reached up and touched a place on it. A valley between two inked ridges. And he said a word.

Rosalind didn't know the word. But she knew what happened next in her chest before it happened in the room: a warmth, a pressure, like a held breath releasing. The candle flames all leaned toward the boy at once, though there was no wind.

Renner sat down. It wasn't voluntary; his legs simply ended their contract with him. He sat on a stool amid his life's work and stared at the child and said, in a voice stripped of everything but awe, "That's Cerrenholt. That's — I've been guessing at that valley for thirty years. I've written *uncertain* across it in four different hands. He just — he named it. He named it like you'd name your own street."

"It's his street," Rosalind said. "Or it was. Before someone unmade it."

* * *

They talked while the candles righted themselves. Renner found a bottle — of course he did — and poured two fingers with a hand that wasn't quite steady, and Rosalind surprised herself by accepting. She hadn't drunk since the god went quiet; drink had felt like another form of prayer, asking the world to blur. Tonight she wanted the world sharp.

"The Choir," Renner said, when she'd told him enough. White robes. Burned archives. A blind archivist's shop reduced to ash, and two men dead in the snow behind it, and a road north that closed behind them like water. "You understand what you're carrying, don't you? That boy isn't a person to them. He's an error. A loose thread from a pattern they cut a century ago. They'll burn half of Thornfell before they let that thread knit back in."

"Then I'll carry him through half of Thornfell."

"And after? Where does he *go*, mercenary? You've seen the maps. South is Choir country. East is sea. West is the Salt Marches, and the Salt Marches would sell him back within the week. There's one direction left." He raised the bottle toward the north wall, toward the largest map, the one where the mountains marched along the top edge in confident black strokes and beyond them the parchment simply — stopped. Blank. The way mapmakers marked the end of the knowable world.

"Behind the mountains," Rosalind said.

"Behind the mountains." Renner drank. "Everyone knows the phrase. *Beyond the Cerenne Range lies nothing.* Cartographers write *here be nothing* and think it's a joke. It's not a joke. It's a scar. Something erased a country so thoroughly that even blank space won't grow back over it. And I have spent thirty years trying to prove the erasure has a seam." He set the bottle down and leaned forward, and for the first time she saw the surveyor under the sot — the man who had once measured kingdoms for a living, whose eye could find a false contour at forty paces. "Every erasure has a seam, Captain Ishikawa. Every cut leaves an edge. Somewhere behind those mountains there is a door that was never locked because no one remembered it existed. And somewhere in this room is everything I know about finding it."

"Why help us?" she asked. "You've hidden up here for years. Safe. Drunk. Why walk back into the thing that ruined you?"

Renner laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, something that might have been weeping if it had been less dry. "Ruined me. Yes. Let me tell you how I was ruined, since we're being honest — it's the fashion among broken people, I find." He wiped his mouth. "I was surveyor royal. I mapped the northern passes for the Crown of Thornfell, twenty years ago, and I found something in the high country. A road. Perfect road, cut stone, running up a valley that appears on no chart, leading to a pass that isn't there. I reported it. Three weeks later my commission was revoked, my findings were seized, and a very polite man in white explained to me that I had suffered a heat-fever in the mountains and seen nothing, and that a man who insists he has seen things is a danger to himself and others. I was given a pension and a choice. I chose the bottle. It was the more honest of the two."

"And now?"

"Now a child walks into my room and names a valley I've spent thirty years failing to prove exists." His eyes went to Wendell, who had not moved from the map, who stood with his palm flat against Cerrenholt as if warming his hand at a fire. "Captain, I have been waiting my entire ruined life for a reason to believe I'm not mad. He is that reason. I'll guide you. On one condition."

"Name it."

"When we find the Threshold Door — and we will find it — I get to draw it. First survey, my instruments, my hand. The map that ends the blank space. I want my name on the edge of the world again."

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. A drunk, a disgraced traitor to his own commission, a man whose obsession had eaten everything else. By every mercenary measure, he was a liability.

But she had a feral child who spoke a dead tongue, a sigil that opened doors, zealots burning the country looking for them, and winter coming early and wrong. Her options had died one by one, and this ruin was what remained.

"One condition back," she said. "You stay sober on the mountain. I don't care what you do in towns. On the pass, I need your eyes, and drunk eyes are dead weight."

Renner winced as if she'd struck an old wound. Then he nodded, slow, and took the bottle, and — she watched him do it — corked it and set it behind the maps, out of reach, like a man burying a weapon he might need later and hoped he wouldn't.

"Wendell," she said.

The boy turned. His eyes were wet, though his face was calm, the terrible calm of children who have lost too much to cry easily.

"This man is going to take us home," she said, and heard herself say *home* and did not correct it, because correcting it would have been a lie and she had decided, somewhere on the road out of Brask, to stop lying to him.

Wendell looked at Renner. Then he said something in Vaelmoorian — a full sentence, unhurried, addressed to the old man directly.

"What did he say?"

Renner's throat worked. "I don't speak it. Nobody speaks it. But I've read enough transcriptions to know the shape of the words." He swallowed. "It's a greeting. An old formal one. *Be welcome in the house of the map.*" He laughed, wet-eyed, disbelieving. "They had a greeting for cartographers. A whole country that honored mapmakers, and I've spent my life mapping its ghost."

"Then you're welcome," Rosalind said. "Both of you. We leave at dawn."

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of snow, though it was barely autumn. They went north out of Brask along the river road, three figures and a secret: a mercenary with a sword she'd stopped praying over, a child riding her pack with the sigil stone pressed to his heart, and a surveyor who walked like a man returning to a battlefield.

By midday the mountains had grown teeth against the sky. Renner stopped at a fork where the map showed one road and the ground showed two, and stood consulting a battered brass instrument and his own memory, and finally pointed northeast. "The old survey road. Mine, once. The Choir watches the pilgrim route. This one they think is washed out."

"Is it?"

"Parts of it." He smiled, and it was almost the smile of a young man with a commission. "I never filed the washouts."

They camped that night in the shadow of the first ridge, and Rosalind took first watch, and the wind coming down off the peaks carried a sound she couldn't name — not wolf, not weather. Almost like singing, very far away, very many voices.

Wendell woke once in the dark. He didn't cry. He crawled to her side, leaned against her arm, and whispered into the wind in his dead language, one word repeated, like a warning passed down a hundred years:

"*Vess.*"

She didn't know the word yet.

She would.
