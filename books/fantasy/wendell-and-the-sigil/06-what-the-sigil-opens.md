## 6. What the Sigil Opens

The woman's name is Rosalind. He knows this because she says it to herself when she thinks he is sleeping, like a prayer she does not believe in anymore. *Rosalind Ishikawa.* Two names, the way people have two names here, in the country that is not his country.

Wendell has one name. That is how it is supposed to be.

He sits on her floor with his back against the wall because walls are honest things — they stay where you put them — and he turns the sigil over in his hands. It is warm. It is always warm now, since the men in white came to the valley of his grandmother's grandmother's stories, since the snow started falling wrong, since he ran until his lungs burned and then kept running because stopping meant becoming a story too.

The sigil is a circle with a door cut into it. Not drawn. Cut. When he runs his thumb along the groove it feels like the edge of a real doorway, the kind you could put your shoulder against and push.

*Hesh,* his mother used to call it. *Door-stone.* She said it the way other mothers said *blanket* or *bread.* Ordinary. His mother had one, and her mother before her, and somewhere back along that line there had been a whole shelf of them in a house with blue shutters, and every child in the family learned the words that made them wake.

Wendell knows four hundred and eleven words. He counted once, in the dark, in the cold, to keep from crying. Four hundred and eleven words of a language that used to have more words than snowflakes.

It is enough for the doors.

* * *

He tries the first one three days after they leave the cabin, down in the low green place where the trees grow fat and the air tastes soft as bread. Rosalind walks ahead of him the way she always does, one hand near the knife at her hip, scanning the road like the road owes her money. She talks to him sometimes. Mostly she says *eat,* and *drink,* and *stay behind me,* and none of those are words he knew before, but he is learning hers the way she will never learn his.

They come to a farmhouse. Or what was a farmhouse. The roof has fallen in on one side and the door hangs by one hinge, and beyond the door there is only black — a room where the floor collapsed into a cellar, dark as a closed eye.

Rosalind curses under her breath. "No way through," she says, not really to him. "We'd have to go around. Around means open ground."

Wendell looks at the wall beside the broken door.

There is a place there. He can feel it the way you feel a tooth that isn't loose yet but wants to be. A seam in the world, thin as a hair, running from knee-height up past his head. The sigil warms against his chest like a small animal waking.

*Rosam heth,* he says. *Open, please.*

Nothing happens, because his mouth is wrong. His mouth has been wrong since the snow, since the fear, since the night he ran. Words come out bent.

He puts his hand flat on the wall instead, on the seam, and presses the sigil against the stone, and says it again slowly, giving each word its full bones: *Ros-am. Heth.*

The wall breathes.

That is the only word for it. The stone swells outward like a lung filling, and the seam widens into an outline, and the outline becomes a door — a real door, oak and iron, older than the farmhouse around it, older maybe than the hill the farmhouse stands on — and it swings inward without a sound.

Beyond it is not the cellar. Beyond it is a hallway with pale walls and a floor of smooth grey wood, lit by lamps that burn without flickering, and the air smells like his grandmother's kitchen, like rosemary and rain on hot stone.

Rosalind has gone very still. Her hand is on her knife but she hasn't drawn it, which Wendell is learning is how she says *I am afraid* without saying it.

"What," she says. Then, slower, in the careful voice she uses when she wants him to understand: "What did you do."

He doesn't have the words to answer in her tongue. So he points at the hallway, and then at himself, and then he says the truest thing he can say with four hundred and eleven words:

"Home-way."

She looks at him for a long time. Her eyes are the color of the sky before snow, and right now they are full of something he cannot name, something between wonder and grief, the way a person looks at a grave that has started growing flowers.

"That's not possible," she says quietly. But she steps through anyway, because she is Rosalind, and being afraid has never once stopped her from walking forward. Wendell follows her, and the door stays open behind them like a held breath.

* * *

Here is what Wendell has never told anyone, because there has been no one to tell:

He is not running away.

Everyone who chases him — the white-robed ones, the ones whose singing sounds like teeth — everyone thinks he is a thing fleeing. But a thing fleeing runs anywhere. Wendell runs one direction. North. Always north, even before he knew north was the direction, even when he was small and the word for north was just a taste in his mouth, like a song half-remembered.

He is running home.

Vaelmoor is still there. He knows it the way you know your own heartbeat. They erased it — he heard the archivist say the word *erased* in Brask, saw the old blind man's hands shake — they took it off the maps and out of the mouths of men, they made it so no one could remember it or find it or grieve it properly. But you cannot erase a country the way you erase a mistake. You can only close the doors. And a closed door is not a gone door. A closed door is a promise.

His mother told him that. The last night. The night the singers came to their village with their white robes and their knives and their terrible harmony, the night she pushed the sigil into his hands and said words he has replayed so many times they are worn smooth as river stones:

*"Vaelmoor is not lost, little door. Vaelmoor is locked. And you are the key that grew legs."*

Then she went out to meet them, and she did not come back, and Wendell ran north.

* * *

The second door opens in the hills above a river, and this time Rosalind watches him do it on purpose.

They are being followed. He can tell because of how she walks — faster, quieter, her head turning at sounds that aren't sounds. Twice she has pulled him off the road into ditches and pressed her hand over his mouth, and her palm smells like leather and steel, and her heart beats fast against his ear, quicker than a heart should.

So when they reach the ruined watchtower with its dead end and its drop on three sides, and Rosalind turns to him with her jaw set the way it gets when she is about to say *we fight,* Wendell steps past her.

The tower wall has a seam. Of course it does. Everything has seams if you know how to listen. The world is full of places where Vaelmoor leans close, where the erased country presses up against the underside of this one like a face against a window. Doors want to be doors. That is their nature. All they need is a voice that remembers their name.

He presses the sigil to the stone. *Ros-am heth.*

The wall opens onto a stairway going down into lamplight, and the stairs are worn in the middle the way stairs get worn when ten thousand feet have loved them, and at the bottom there is a landing with a bench, and on the bench someone has left a clay cup, and the cup is still warm.

Rosalind picks it up. Holds it. Wendell watches her thumb move across the rim, and sees her understand, all at once, what the warmth means: that on the other side of these doors, time is different, or kinder, or simply still going. That somewhere behind the walls of the world, people are still drinking tea.

"They're alive," she says. Her voice cracks on the second word. "The Vaelmoorians. They're not gone. They're—"

*Hidden,* he almost says. But he doesn't have that word in her language, so he touches the warm cup too, and nods, and hopes that nodding is a language she speaks.

She sets the cup down very gently, the way you set down something holy, and then she crouches so they are eye to eye, which she does rarely, and looks at him with her snow-sky eyes.

"Wendell." His name in her mouth sounds strange and good, like a word from his own tongue wearing foreign clothes. "Those men in white robes. In Brask. Are they looking for you?"

He considers lying. He is not good at lying; his face is an open window and everything blows through it. Besides. She killed two of them for him. You do not lie to someone who kills for you.

"Yes," he says.

"Why?"

He reaches for the words. *Echo,* the blind man said. *Relic.* Those are her-language words and they are wrong words, cold words, museum words. He searches his four hundred and eleven for something truer.

"Last," he says finally. "I am... last one outside." He taps his own chest. "Inside—" he gestures at the open door, at the lamplight, at the smell of rosemary and rain on stone, "—many. Outside—" he spreads his hands at the grey air, the ruined tower, the whole wide empty world, "—one."

He watches her do the arithmetic. She is good at arithmetic; he has seen her count coins and arrows and miles with the same tight look. One boy. An entire nation behind locked doors. Men in white robes who burn buildings to keep the count at zero.

"We're going north," she says. It is not a question. It has never been a question; she has been following his lean the whole time, the way a boat follows a current without admitting it. "All the way. Whatever's at the end of you."

"Home," Wendell says.

And then, because the door is open and the lamplight is spilling out and the smell of his grandmother's kitchen is rising up the stairs like a hand reaching for him, he does something foolish. He goes down three steps. Just three. Far enough that the light wraps around him like a blanket, far enough that he can hear — faint, so faint, through some door further down that he cannot see — a sound.

Singing. Not the Choir's singing. Real singing. A woman's voice, high and unhurried, singing a lullaby about a river that carries the moon.

His knees nearly give. The homesickness hits him like cold water, like a wave, like the whole weight of a hundred years pressing down on one small boy standing on three steps of a stairway that shouldn't exist. He grips the wall. He lets the lullaby pour over him and fill up all the cracked places, and for a moment he is not hungry or hunted or alone, he is just a child in a warm country, and his mother is in the next room, and the snow outside is the ordinary kind.

Then Rosalind's hand closes on his shoulder, gentle as anything she has ever done, and pulls him back.

"Not yet," she says softly. "Whatever's down there — not yet. If we go in, we might not come out. And if we don't come out, we can't open the rest." She pauses. "You said home is north. Past the mountains. Right?"

He wipes his face with his sleeve, furious at the tears, and nods.

"Then the long way," she says. "The whole way. We don't stop at the first warm room." Something moves across her face, quick and private, like she is arguing with herself and losing. "God help me. The whole way."

* * *

That night they camp inside the third door.

He found it by accident, walking the perimeter of their sheltered hollow while Rosalind built a fire — a seam in a mossy boulder, breathing faintly, and when he spoke to it, it opened onto a small round chamber with a hearth already laid and dry wood stacked beside it, as if someone had stepped out a moment ago and would be right back. There are blankets on the bed. There is a bowl of apples that are not wrinkled, not rotten, not possible.

Rosalind checks every corner twice, sleeps with her knife in her hand, and calls it madness. But she eats one of the apples, and then a second, and Wendell pretends not to see her shoulders come down from around her ears for the first time since Brask.

He lies awake after she sleeps, holding the sigil against his sternum, feeling it pulse like a second heart.

Four hundred and eleven words. Three doors opened. Somewhere north, past the frozen ford and the high passes and the mountain called Cerenne that hums in his bones at night, there is a door bigger than all the others. The Threshold. The last lock. On the other side of it, his mother's voice, saying his name the correct way, in the language with more words than snowflakes.

Between here and there: the white robes. The singing like teeth. The winter that is falling wrong.

Wendell is not afraid of the Choir. This is the secret under all his other secrets. He pities them, a little, the way you pity a man hammering boards over a window to keep out the sunrise. They think they are protecting the world from something. They do not understand that the world is already dying of the wound they made — that the silent gods, the endless winter, the maps with holes in them, are all symptoms of the same sickness, and that he is not the disease.

He is the medicine. He is the key that grew legs.

But tonight, watching Rosalind sleep with her knife and her frown and her fierce borrowed duty, he feels the first cold thread of a different fear. Because the doors are opening easier now. Faster. The sigil barely needs his voice anymore; it wakes at his touch, at his breath, like a dog that has decided he belongs to it. And the lullaby-woman's song carried further than it should have. And the apples were fresh.

Doors that open easily can be opened from both sides.

Far to the north, so far it is only a feeling, something vast and patient shifts beneath a mountain — and Wendell feels it turn over in its sleep, the way you feel a storm change direction, and he holds the sigil tighter and does not wake Rosalind, because some fears are for morning.

In the morning, there are hoofprints in the frost outside the hollow. Many horses. Headed north.

Someone else knows the way now too.
