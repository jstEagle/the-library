## 7. Smuggler's Debt

The pass above Brask had three names, depending on who you asked. The shepherds called it the Shepherd's Throat. The cartographers called it the Cerenne Approach, though no map agreed on where it began. Sefa Kade called it the only road worth taxing, which was why Rosalind knew they'd find her somewhere along it.

They found her on the second morning out of the lowlands, at a waystation so old its foundation stones predated the kingdom that built the rest of the road. Wendell was asleep against a post, wrapped in Rosalind's spare cloak, his breath fogging in slow even clouds. Ivo Renner sat hunched over his maps with a stub of charcoal, muttering elevations to himself like prayers to a god who'd never once answered him either.

And on the frozen track below the station, walking up through the pines with the unhurried gait of someone who owned every mile she crossed, came a woman in a smuggler's long coat, leading a mule loaded with crates that clinked when they shouldn't have.

Rosalind stood up before her mind caught up with her body. Ten years of instinct did it for her — hand drifting toward the axe, weight shifting off the heels, eyes cataloguing exits. Then the woman looked up, and the instinct died of embarrassment.

"Sefa."

"Villalobos." Sefa Kade stopped ten paces out and spread her arms, palms open, the old gesture between them: *no blades drawn, nothing hidden.* Snow caught in her black hair, which was shot through now with more grey than Rosalind remembered. There was a new scar splitting one eyebrow. "You look terrible."

"You look older."

"I *am* older. It's been four years." Sefa's grin faded by degrees as her gaze moved past Rosalind — to Ivo, to the sleeping shape under the cloak. "Four years, and then you send me a message through a fishmonger in Brask. A fishmonger, Roz. Do you know what he wanted for that? Two jars of pickled herring and a favor I haven't collected yet."

"It was urgent."

"It's always urgent with you. That's your whole personality." But Sefa was already coming closer, already scanning the treeline the way Rosalind was, the two of them falling into the same rhythm without discussing it, the way soldiers do, the way people who've bled together do. She stopped near the fire pit and lowered her voice. "So talk. What's got you moving north in winter with a scholar and a child?"

"Not a scholar. A cartographer."

"Worse."

"*Much* worse," said Ivo, not looking up from his map. "Pleased to meet you."

Sefa crouched by the dead fire, held her hands over the cold ash as if remembering warmth there, and waited. That was the thing about Sefa Kade. Everyone else in Rosalind's life had needed the story dragged out of them or had drowned Rosalind in theirs. Sefa just waited, patient as a snare.

So Rosalind gave it to her, stripped down. The silent god. The contractless winter. The boy who'd walked out of a blizzard speaking a language that made a blind archivist go white as tallow. The men in white robes who'd burned a shop over it. The two she'd killed. The flight north.

She didn't mention the sigil. Some debts were paid in full disclosure; others were paid in trust. She watched Sefa's face while she talked, and Sefa's face did what it always did — nothing, absolutely nothing, until the very end, when her jaw tightened around something she swallowed.

"The Hollow Choir," Sefa said at last.

"You know them."

"Everyone on this mountain knows them now. That's why I'm here, actually. Not for you." She glanced at Wendell, still asleep, snow gathering on the cloak. "I've been running cargo up the pass all month, and every waystation, every tollhouse, every hired blade between here and the Cerenne col has the same new employer. White robes pay triple rates and don't haggle. They're looking for someone coming north."

Rosalind felt the cold move under her skin. "Looking for a child."

"Looking for an echo, is how the contracts phrase it. Doesn't matter. Every mercenary company on Thornfell has taken Choir silver — the Redmantles, the Corbie twins, half of Kell's Irregulars. Your name's on some of those papers, Roz. *Description* of you, anyway. Woman, mid-thirties, sword-and-axe, traveling with a boy. Dead or delivered, either pays."

Ivo's charcoal stopped scratching. In the silence, Wendell stirred but didn't wake.

"How much?" Rosalind asked.

"What?"

"How much are they paying?"

Sefa laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "Of course that's your question. Enough. Enough that Kell himself came down off his mountain to take the job personally. Enough that men who owe *me* money are asking me if I've seen you." She stood, brushed snow from her knees, and looked at Rosalind straight on. "Enough that I should be turning you in right now and retiring to a warm coast for the rest of my life."

The wind came down the pass and moved through the pines, and for a moment neither woman spoke. This was the arithmetic Rosalind understood better than any other kind. She'd done it her whole career: what a person's loyalty weighed against what their survival cost. She'd lost friends to lighter sums than the one Sefa was holding.

"So why aren't you?" Rosalind asked quietly.

Sefa didn't answer right away. She walked to the edge of the waystation's porch and spat over the rail, an old habit, then turned back.

"Nine years ago," she said. "The Saltmarsh job. You remember."

"I remember."

"You carried me two miles with an arrow through my lung. You carried me *after* the contract was voided. After the client ran and everyone else scattered. There was no pay in it. No glory. You carried me because—" She stopped, jaw working. "Because that's what you do. You're the most annoyingly dutiful person I have ever met, and I have met kings' executioners."

"That wasn't duty."

"No?"

"No. Duty's what you owe. That was—" Rosalind found she didn't have a word for it. Her god used to supply her vocabulary for things like this. Lately the shelf where those words lived was empty. "That was just you."

Sefa stared at her for a long moment. Then she laughed again, different this time, rougher, and wiped at her eye with the back of a glove. "Gods help me. Fine. Fine." She whistled low, and the mule's ears swiveled. "Here's how it stands. The Choir's sealed the high route — checkpoints at the Tollhouse Stone and the Widow's Stair. But nobody seals a mountain completely; they just make the cheap routes expensive. There's a way across the col that isn't on any surveyor's map because I cut it myself, eleven years ago, for moving goods that didn't want names. It's ugly. It's exposed in two places. And it puts us on the glacier a day ahead of anyone chasing us, if we're fast."

"And if we're slow?"

"Then we're a story other smugglers tell." Sefa was already unloading the mule, tossing a wax-sealed crate onto the porch. "Dried meat, hardtack, lamp oil, wool. Consider it a down payment."

"On what?"

"On the rest of my debt. Which I'm deciding, right now, is paid in full only when you're somewhere the Choir can't reach." She looked up, and beneath the smuggler's flippancy her eyes were dead serious. "You don't get to argue, Villalobos. Life-debt doesn't negotiate. Ask anyone in my line."

"Life-debts are superstition."

"Life-debts are *accounting*," Sefa snapped, and for a heartbeat Rosalind saw the girl Sefa had been before the Saltmarsh, before the coat and the grey hair — young, terrified, drowning in her own blood on a riverbank while the world decided whether she was worth saving. "Everything in my life since has been interest on what you did. Let me pay it down or I'll never sleep again."

Behind them, small feet crunched on frost. Wendell stood in the doorway of the station, cloak pooled around him, watching Sefa with the flat, ancient wariness he gave all strangers. His hand was closed around something in his pocket. The sigil. He never let Rosalind see it anymore, but she knew the shape of his fist.

"This is Sefa," Rosalind said gently, in trade-tongue. "She's coming with us."

Wendell studied the smuggler for a long time. Then he said something in Vaelmoorian — soft, questioning, the rising lilt of it strange against the wind.

"He wants to know if you can be trusted," Ivo translated from his corner, sounding faintly amazed. "That's... the polite version. The actual phrasing is closer to 'does she keep her oaths when the wolves come.'"

Sefa blinked. Then she crouched slowly, bringing herself level with the boy, and answered in trade-tongue, clearly, so Ivo could carry it across.

"Tell him this. Tell him I owed his friend my life before he was born, and I intend to die owing it, because a debt you've finished paying is a reason to walk away." She paused. "And tell him I've never once broken my word to anyone whose blood I've carried."

Ivo translated. Something in the cadence of it — or maybe just the steadiness of Sefa's voice — reached the boy. Wendell considered her a moment longer, then nodded once, gravely, the way a magistrate accepts testimony, and went back inside to the fire.

Sefa exhaled. "Well. That was more terrifying than the Redmantles."

"He does that," Rosalind said.

They broke camp within the hour. Sefa led, cutting off the surveyed road onto a track that was barely a suggestion — switchbacks through scree, a frozen waterfall they climbed hand over hand, a ridge traverse where the wind tried to peel them off the mountain like bark. Ivo cursed continuously and creatively. Wendell, to Rosalind's quiet astonishment, moved like the mountain was familiar, placing his feet where the stone wanted them, as if some older part of him remembered these heights from a life a century dead.

On the first night of the high route, they sheltered in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Sefa took first watch. Rosalind lay awake beside Wendell's small warm weight and listened to the wind, and after a while Sefa's voice drifted back to her, low, meant for the dark.

"They're not just paying blades, Roz. Word at the Tollhouse is the Choir's got something else coming up the pass behind us. Something they keep hooded. The mercenaries won't talk about it. Men who'll brag about anything won't talk about *that*."

Rosalind stared at the overhang above her, at the frost feathering the stone.

"How far behind?"

"A day. Maybe less. And closing."

Above them, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, the clouds had begun to gather out of a sky that had been clear all evening — thickening, lowering, moving against the wind.

Moving south to north.

Toward them.
