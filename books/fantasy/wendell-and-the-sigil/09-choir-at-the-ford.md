## 9. Choir at the Ford

The ford came at dawn, or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of dirty wool. Rosalind smelled it before she saw it — woodsmoke and wet wool and something underneath, incense maybe, sweet and wrong, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

She held up a fist and the column stopped. Four days out of Ivo Renner's map-room, three days since Sefa Kade had fallen into step beside them on the pass with her crossbow slung casual and her grin too wide, and already the road north had narrowed to this: a frozen river, a shelf of grey ice spanning the water like an old scar, and beyond it the pines climbing black toward Cerenne's shoulders.

"Trouble," Sefa said, coming up beside her. She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her eyes were working the treeline.

"How much?"

"Enough." Sefa spat. "Smoke's been following us since yesterday. Not camp smoke. Moving smoke."

Wendell stood at Rosalind's hip, wrapped in her spare cloak, the sigil hanging against his chest where he kept one small hand pressed over it like a man holding a wound. He was looking at the ford with an expression she'd learned to read now — not fear exactly. Recognition. His lips moved, shaping sounds in that dead language, soft as breath.

"What's he saying?" she asked Ivo.

Ivo Renner pushed his spectacles up his nose and squinted. He'd been transcribing Wendell's speech for days in a battered notebook, filling margins with a hand that shook less when he wrote than when he did anything else. "I don't — wait. *Ford.* He's saying ford. And..." The old cartographer went still. "*They're singing.*"

Rosalind listened. Wind in the pines. Ice creaking somewhere downstream. And beneath it, so faint she might have invented it, voices. Many voices, layered, rising and falling in unison. A hymn without words she knew.

The Hollow Choir.

"They're not hiding," she said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "They want us to know."

* * *

There were eleven of them visible when Rosalind crawled close enough to count through the frost-furred bracken: white robes gone grey at the hems from road-dirt, faces bare to the cold, arranged along the far bank in a loose crescent. They weren't building a fire. They weren't eating. They stood in the snow like they'd been standing there for hours, singing, their breath rising in a single plume.

At their center, on a flat stone like a pulpit, stood a tall figure whose voice carried above all the others — a woman's voice, clear and cold and perfectly controlled, threading through the chorus like a needle through cloth.

"We could go around," Ivo whispered. "Upstream. There's a gorge—"

"The gorge adds two days and the Choir owns both banks by then. Sefa?"

Sefa lay flat beside her, crossbow cradled, chewing on a strip of dried meat she'd have sworn she wasn't eating. "Ice'll hold. Barely. It held for them, didn't it? Question is whether we fight on the bank or the middle. Middle's better. Middle, we only got enemies on two sides instead of three."

"You've thought about this."

"I think about everything, Ros. That's my whole tragedy." She swallowed the meat. "Eleven singers, one cantor. Cantors don't fight. They conduct. Kill enough of the chorus and the song falls apart, and these types fall apart with it. Their faith is a choir loft, you understand? Take out the loft—"

"—and everyone falls."

"I was going to say something more colorful, but yes."

Rosalind looked back down the slope at Wendell, who sat between the roots of a pine with Ivo's coat around him, watching her with those dark, patient eyes. He wasn't afraid. That frightened her more than anything. A child should be afraid of a thing like that hymn floating across the ice.

She touched his shoulder. He looked up.

"We're going to cross," she said slowly, plainly, the way she'd learned he understood best. "Stay behind me. If I say run, you run to Sefa. Say it back."

"*Run to Sefa,*" he repeated, accentless, perfect. Then, in Vaelmoorian, something longer, softer.

Ivo translated in a whisper: "He says... 'the door is behind the song.' He says the river remembers the way."

"He says a lot of things about doors," Sefa muttered.

"Yeah," Rosalind said. She checked her blades — the long knife at her hip, the short one at her back, the axe on her pack strap. "Yeah, he does."

* * *

They hit the ice at a dead run.

It was Sefa's plan dressed in Rosalind's legs: no creeping, no negotiation, just four figures sprinting onto the frozen river with the dawn wind at their backs, because a charging enemy makes archers rush and rushed archers miss. The first bolt cracked off the ice a stride from Rosalind's boot. The second took Ivo's hat clean off his head, and the old man made a sound of pure personal outrage and kept running.

Then they were among the white robes, and the singing stopped, and the ford became a butcher's argument.

Rosalind fought the way she'd been taught twenty years ago at a shrine altar: economically, without flourish, every motion ending either in a wound or a position from which to make one. Her long knife opened the throat of the first chorister before his hands finished rising from his sleeves. The second swung a staff — iron-shod, heavier than it looked — and she let it glance off her shoulder pauldron and put her short blade through the gap below his ribs, twice, because once was never enough with zealots.

They didn't scream. That was the worst part. They fell singing, or trying to, blood bubbling through the hymn until the notes drowned.

"Left!" Sefa barked, and Rosalind turned in time to catch a staff-swing meant for her skull, deflect it, drive her boot into the man's knee until something gave with a sound like green wood splitting. He went down praying. She left him praying and moved.

Three choristers had broken off the crescent and were running — not at her, not at Sefa. At Wendell.

The boy stood at the center of the ice, alone, the crossing churning around him, and as the three white robes closed in he did something Rosalind would remember for the rest of her life.

He spoke.

One word, in that language older than kingdoms, pitched low and clear, and the ice *answered*. A crack ran out from under his feet like lightning frozen mid-strike, splitting the river's skin in a jagged line between him and the runners. Two of them skidded, fell. The third leapt the crack —

— and met Sefa Kade coming the other way.

Later, Rosalind would try to reconstruct those thirty seconds and fail. Memory kept giving her fragments: Sefa's crossbow discarded, her long knife out, the two of them meeting at the center of the ford with the broken ice groaning beneath them. The chorister was bigger. Younger. Armored under the robes where Sefa's coat was just wool and stubbornness. She saw the exchange — parry, parry, the chorister's blade slipping inside Sefa's guard—

"Sefa!"

—Ivo screaming it, Wendell screaming it in two languages, and Sefa doing the thing Sefa would always do, which was step *into* the blade rather than give ground, taking it high in the left side below the collarbone so her own knife could reach the man's throat.

They went down together. The chorister didn't get up. Sefa tried to.

"Don't—" Rosalind reached her in four strides and put her hands on the wound, and blood welled hot between her fingers, shockingly bright against the grey ice. "Don't move. Ivo! The kit, now!"

"Got it, got it—" Ivo sliding down beside them, old knees cracking, hands surprisingly steady unwrapping bandages, needle and gut thread already laid out on a cloth like a surgeon's tray. Twenty years of mapping remote country; of course he knew how to stitch a body.

"It's fine," Sefa said. Her face was grey as the ice. "It's — ha — it's fine, Ros, I've had worse."

"You have not had worse."

"I've had *equally* bad." Her teeth were chattering now, and not from cold. "Just — do it fast, all right? Do it fast and I'll complain about your stitching for the rest of my life, which, I want to be clear, will be very long."

Rosalind sewed. She'd done it before, on other bodies, other rivers, other years, and her hands did not shake, because her hands never shook, because shaking was a luxury for people whose gods still spoke to them. Beneath her knuckles Sefa's heartbeat fluttered quick and stubborn, like a bird refusing to land.

Wendell knelt on Sefa's other side. He put one small hand flat on her forehead, the way you'd quiet a horse, and began to speak — low, rhythmic, that same cadence he'd used on the dark in the cave, on the doors, on the ice. Comfort-words. Or prayer. Or both.

And the bleeding slowed.

Rosalind stared. She told herself it was cold, clotting, coincidence. She told herself a great many things that morning.

* * *

The last chorister standing was the cantor.

She hadn't fought. All through the slaughter she'd remained on her flat stone, watching with an attention so complete it felt physical, and when the last of her chorus fell silent she simply — stopped. Folded her hands. Stepped down onto the ice and walked toward Rosalind across a field of her own dead without looking at any of them.

Rosalind met her ten paces out, blade low, body angled, Wendell behind her and Ivo kneeling over Sefa and every instinct she owned screaming that this woman was more dangerous than the eleven combined.

"Cantor Vess," Ivo breathed behind her. "That's — Rosalind, that's *Vess.*"

"You know her?"

"I know *of* her. Everyone did. Before."

The woman stopped. Up close she was younger than her voice suggested — forty perhaps, sharp-boned, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had then decided other things mattered more. Her eyes were pale grey, nearly colorless, and they went past Rosalind entirely, to the boy.

"So it's true," Vess said. Her voice was the hymn's voice, stripped of chorus. "The echo lives."

"It's a child," Rosalind said.

"It's a *key*." Vess's gaze slid to Rosalind at last, and something moved through her expression — recognition, slow and strange, like ice shifting. "I know you."

"Do you."

"The shrine at Karst Hollow. The mercenary who prayed every morning before she took a contract. You had a discipline the others lacked. You treated the god like a commanding officer." A pause. "I envied that. Once."

The bottom dropped out of Rosalind's stomach.

Because it was true. Because she'd stood at that altar for six years and this woman had stood beside her — some junior acolyte-turned-blade, some sister of the order she'd never bothered to learn the name of, one face among dozens, gone when the god went silent, scattered like all of them.

"You were sworn to the Quiet One," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question.

"I was sworn. I *prayed*. I bled on the altar stones every season for nine years." Vess's composure held, but her voice developed a hairline fracture, a crack in glaze. "And when the silence came, do you know what the elders said? *Wait. Endure. Faith is faithfulness in absence.*" She smiled, and it was the worst thing Rosalind had seen all day, and Rosalind had seen eleven people die singing. "So I waited. I endured. And then I understood that the silence wasn't absence. It was theft. Something was taken from us, mercenary, and someone — *something* — is keeping it."

"And you think it's him." Rosalind shifted, putting her shoulder more squarely between Vess and the boy. "A child."

"I think a child is speaking a language that was erased from the world, and doors open when he talks, and the dead are stirring in valleys that aren't on maps anymore." Vess's pale eyes were terribly calm. "I think the silence has a shape, and the shape is standing behind you, and if we cut it out — burn it, bury it, *unmake* it the way Vaelmoor was unmade — the god comes back. To all of us. Every silenced altar, every empty shrine. One echo, returned, and the whole choir finds its voice again."

"That's not faith," Rosalind said quietly. "That's arithmetic."

"All devotion is arithmetic. We simply disagree about the sum." Vess spread her hands, and Rosalind saw they were scarred — altar scars, vow-scars, the same pattern Rosalind carried on her own palms. Mirror-marks. "Come with us. Bring the boy. You'll see. When it's done, when the silence breaks — you'll hear Him again. Don't you want that? After all those mornings on your knees in the cold?" Her voice softened, and that was the trap inside the trap, the tenderness wrapped around the blade. "I heard you pray once, at Karst Hollow. You prayed like someone who expected an answer. I can give you back the answer."

For one moment — one long, ice-creaking moment — Rosalind let herself feel it. The morning prayers. The warmth of being held by something larger. Six years of kneeling, and the specific loneliness of the last year, praying into a well with no bottom.

Then she heard Wendell breathing behind her. Small. Alive. Saying her name — not the mercenary name, the plain one, *Rosalind*, the way he said it when he wanted reassurance and not orders.

"No," she said.

Vess studied her for a moment. No anger. That was somehow worse — the look of a woman confirming a figure she'd already calculated.

"So be it. We'll meet again before Cerenne. There are more of us than there are of you, and the mountain is ours." She stepped back, and raised her voice, and it carried across the ice with that terrible choir-trained clarity: "Grieve your friend quickly, mercenary. Winter is coming early this year. We intend to finish what the erasure started — and this time, nothing gets out."

She turned and walked away up the far bank, unhurried, and the pines swallowed her white robes one branch at a time.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a rockfall, off the road, fire small and shielded. Sefa slept — actually slept, feverish but alive, the wound stitched and bound, Wendell's hand resting on her brow like he was keeping watch over her dreams. Ivo sat sharpening a pencil he hadn't used, staring at nothing.

Rosalind washed her hands in snow. The blood came off easily. It always did. That was the lie of it — that the work ever really washed away.

"Karst Hollow," Ivo said quietly, beside her. "Was she telling the truth?"

"Probably."

"And she was... one of yours. Sworn to the same god."

"Sworn to the same silence, now." Rosalind wrung out the cloth. "You want to know the difference between us, Ivo? There isn't one. Same vows, same scars, same empty altar. She just did the math differently. She found someone to blame for the quiet, and I found no one, and we both kept walking." She looked at the fire, at the boy asleep against Sefa's good side, one fist curled in the smuggler's shirt even unconscious. "She's what I become if I stop being tired and start being certain."

"You're not her."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "Not yet."

She banked the fire and took first watch, blade across her knees, facing north. Somewhere up there past the black pines, Mount Cerenne waited with its door, and somewhere behind them the Choir was regathering its voices, and somewhere overhead the stars were coming out hard and cold and early, earlier than they should, winter stacking itself against the sky like a debt come due.

In her sleep, Sefa whimpered once. Wendell murmured something in the old tongue without waking, and the fire burned a little brighter, and Rosalind sat alone with her god's silence and understood, finally, that she'd chosen her side of the arithmetic years ago — she just hadn't known the price until now.

By morning, the first flakes were falling. Out of season. Out of reason.

Out for blood.
