## 10. Why the God Went Silent

Ivo Renner had spent thirty years learning how to lie with maps, and the first thing he'd learned was that a map is a confession. Every line you draw admits what you know. Every blank space confesses something worse.

He sat closest to the fire because his hands were the oldest and the coldest, and he watched the boy across the flames and did not draw anything at all.

They had made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder two days out from the village that wasn't there — the one whose streets Wendell had named aloud while lamps lit themselves in empty windows, one by one, like a country clearing its throat. Rosalind hadn't spoken much since. Sefa's wound was dressed and ugly and she joked about it too loudly. And Wendell sat with his knees drawn up, turning the stone sigil over in his small hands, watching Ivo with those eyes that were too old for the face they'd been issued to.

The boy knew. That was the thing that kept Ivo awake on watch. The boy knew Ivo had been circling something for days, sketching in the margins, crossing things out, drinking less so he could think more clearly and then drinking more because clear thinking hurt. Children always knew when an adult was carrying a confession like a full cup.

"Ask me," Ivo said finally, to the fire as much as anyone.

Rosalind looked up from her whetstone. "Ivo."

"No, let him." He rubbed his face. His beard had gone greyer since Brask; he could feel it happening, like weather. "Wendell. You've been waiting. Ask."

Wendell tilted his head. When he spoke it was that language again, the one that made the air feel thinner, and Ivo understood maybe one word in ten now — he'd been studying, filling notebooks, cross-referencing fragments against survey records no sane person kept — but he caught *why* and he caught *silent*, and the boy pointed one small finger at the sky, at the place where gods were supposed to be.

"Yes," Ivo said. "That's exactly it. Why is everything silent?"

* * *

Here is what Ivo Renner confessed to the fire, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten which shoulder it lived on:

Before the erasure, before Vaelmoor was scraped off the maps and out of the marriage records and the trade ledgers and finally out of memory itself, he had been a junior surveyor attached to the Royal Commission of Boundaries. Nineteen years old, ink-stained, ambitious. He had walked the borderlands with a chain and a theodolite and a head full of trigonometry, and he had been the last licensed man to survey the Cerenne massif before the war, before the treaties, before whatever happened in the capital that made an entire kingdom into a word nobody would say.

And here was the thing he had never told another living soul, because the one time he'd tried — drunk, twenty-five years ago, shouting it across a tavern table in Corvane — they had laughed him into the street and taken his license for good measure.

"The mountains were wrong," he said. "That's how I'd put it now. Then I just thought my instruments were broken. Every reading near the heart of Vaelmoor came back impossible. Bearings that shifted between morning and noon. Distances that didn't reconcile. Elevation that changed depending on which side of the valley you stood on." He turned his palms up. "A country can't do that. Land doesn't argue with itself. Unless—"

"Unless," Rosalind said quietly, "the land isn't holding itself up."

Ivo pointed at her, finger trembling slightly, because yes. Yes. That was it. Thirty years alone with it and she'd said it in six words.

"I spent a decade after the erasure trying to prove Vaelmoor had existed at all. Records burn, fine. Memories fade, fine. But I'm a surveyor. Stone remembers. Or it should. And everywhere the kingdom had been, the stone was—" he searched for the word and hated it, "—smoothed. Not destroyed. Smoothed. Like a scar over a wound, except the wound wasn't healed, it was *removed*, and the body keeps reaching for the missing part. Rivers bend where cities used to be. Roads end in fields for no reason. And every shrine, every temple, every holy site within three hundred miles of the old borders went quiet within a single generation."

Sefa had stopped joking. She was sitting very still, her bandaged side propped against her pack, and her face had the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You're saying the gods died," she said.

"No." Ivo shook his head hard. "That's the coward's version. That's the version the theologians sold us so we'd stop asking. Gods don't die. Listen — I've mapped this. Actually mapped it." He dragged his satchel over and pulled out the notebook, the good one, the one with the waxed cover, and opened it to pages dense with his cramped surveyor's hand: circles, radiating lines, distances measured not in miles but in years-since-silence. "Every god that has gone quiet in the last century — and there are dozens, dozens, Rosalind, your own among them — every single one went silent in order of proximity to Vaelmoor. Closest first. It took ninety years for the silence to reach your goddess's range, but it got there, right on schedule, moving outward from the erased center like ripples. Like a bell that stopped ringing and the sound draining away ring by ring."

Rosalind had gone white around the mouth. He watched her hand drift toward her chest, toward whatever private shrine she still carried, and stop halfway, and come back down.

"So they're not dead," she said. Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice. "They're cut off."

"They're *unanchored*." Ivo leaned forward, and the fire threw his shadow huge against the rock behind him, and for once in his life he didn't care how mad he sounded. "Think about what a god actually is. Not a person. A *position*. A fixed point that prayer can find. Every prayer ever answered was a line drawn from a kneeling human to somewhere fixed. And Vaelmoor — I believe Vaelmoor was where the lines converged. All of them. The anchor-point. The peg that held the whole net of heaven stretched taut over the world. Erase the peg and the net doesn't fall — it just goes slack. Slack everywhere. Silent shrines. Empty prayers. A long winter that won't leave, because even the seasons were hung on the same net."

The wind moved through the pass above them, a long low moan through stone, and nobody spoke until it finished.

"That's madness," Sefa said, but gently, the way you say it to someone you love.

"It's a *survey*," Ivo snapped. "It's measurements. It's the only theory in a hundred years that fits the data, and I will die before I apologize for having data." He breathed. Pressed his knuckles to his eyes. "Forgive me. It's been a long time since anyone listened long enough to call it madness."

* * *

Then he looked at Wendell, and made himself say the rest, the part that mattered, the part that had been sitting in his chest since the blind archivist in Brask had gone grey and whispered *Vaelmoorian* like a death sentence.

"But here's what I got wrong, all these years. I thought the erasure was total. Complete. A perfect crime." He turned the notebook around so the firelight fell on the last page, where he'd drawn — badly, lovingly — a single unbroken thread running through a field of severed ones. "You can't erase a whole country perfectly. Something always survives. A coin in a wall. A name in a christening record. A lullaby someone's grandmother sang without knowing why. The erasure took everything big. But it couldn't take everything small."

"And him?" Rosalind said.

"He's not small." Ivo's voice cracked and he let it. "He's what's left of the center. Don't you see? The sigil, the language, the doors that open where walls should be — he's not a relic of Vaelmoor. Relics are dead things we keep. He's a *thread*. A living thread still tied to the anchor. The Choir didn't burn the archivist's shop because the boy knows a dead tongue. They burned it because if the thread is real, the peg can be found. And if the peg can be found—"

"—it can be hammered back in," Rosalind finished.

"Or pulled out forever," Ivo said. "Depends who holds the hammer."

Wendell had stopped turning the sigil. He was looking at Ivo with an expression Ivo had no word for — hope, maybe, but the kind that has been hurt before and checks its pockets first. The boy said something soft in the old tongue, a question by its rising shape.

Ivo spread his empty hands. "I don't understand. I'm sorry. But I'm going to learn. Every word. Because if I'm right, then you're not what they say you are. You're not an echo. An echo fades." He tapped the drawing of the thread. "You're the opposite of fading. You're the last line still drawn true, and everything slack in this world — every silent shrine, every unanswered prayer, every frozen spring — is pulling toward you whether it knows it or not. Including the gods. *Especially* the gods."

Rosalind stood abruptly and walked to the edge of the firelight, and Ivo let her go, because some men need to be alone with a reframe that large. From the dark she said, without turning:

"My goddess went silent eleven years ago. I checked the dates tonight, in my head, while you talked. Eleven years." A pause. "The silence reached her range right on schedule. Your ripples. Which means she isn't gone. She's just—" the word cost her visibly, "—waiting for the net to be strung again."

"Yes," Ivo said softly.

"Then the Threshold Door. If Vaelmoor exists behind the mountains — if it was never destroyed, only *folded away*—"

"Then crossing back through might not be escape," Ivo said. "It might be restoration. For him. Maybe for more than him."

Sefa laughed, short and disbelieving, wincing around her wound. "So we're not smuggling a child anymore. We're re-hanging heaven. Wonderful. Any other apocalypses you'd like to schedule?"

But Wendell had come around the fire. He crouched beside Ivo's notebook, studied the little drawn thread for a long moment, and then reached out and touched it — one fingertip, careful, the way you touch something sleeping. And he said a single word in Vaelmoorian, quiet enough that Ivo almost missed it, and the sigil in his other hand grew briefly, unmistakably warm, glowing faint gold at its carved edges like a coal remembering fire.

Ivo didn't know the word. But he wrote it down anyway, phonetically, in the margin, and under it he wrote: *he agrees.*

It was deep in the night watch, later, when Ivo woke to a sound and lay still in his bedroll listening. Wind, yes. But under the wind, far off to the north, past the black shoulders of the pass — singing. Many voices, thin and harmonized and wrong, carrying impossibly over miles of frozen stone.

The Hollow Choir had entered the mountains.

And they were singing the way people sing when they have heard the same rumor Ivo had just spoken aloud: that somewhere ahead of them, a thread was still tied to the peg, and they meant to cut it.
