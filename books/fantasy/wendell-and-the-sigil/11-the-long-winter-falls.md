## 11. The Long Winter Falls

The storm came out of a sky that had no business holding it.

Rosalind felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, the same wrongness she'd learned to read in the hour before an ambush. She stopped on the trail and turned her face north, toward the high passes, and listened.

Wind. But not mountain wind. Mountain wind gusted, died, gusted again, moved in the grooves carved by ten thousand winters. This was a single sustained note, low and constant, like something enormous drawing breath and refusing to let it go.

"Storm coming," Sefa said behind her. She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but Rosalind heard the edge under it. "We should push for the waystation."

"It's June," Ivo said. He had his map case clutched to his chest like a child. "It's *midsummer*. There is no snow above four thousand feet this time of year. I've surveyed these ranges. I have the *figures*—"

"Ivo." Rosalind didn't turn around. "Look up."

He looked up. They all did.

Above the tree line, where the sky should have been hard blue with heat shimmer, a wall of cloud was building. Not drifting. Building — stacking itself in perfect ranks, gray-white and vast, like an army assembling in silence. And at its leading edge, already falling, already swallowing the highest peaks one by one: snow.

Wendell made a sound.

She'd been carrying him on her back through the worst stretches of scree, but he was walking now, and he stood very still in the middle of the trail with his face tipped up and his small hands open at his sides. The sigil hung against his chest on its cord, and Rosalind could see it — even from three paces she could see it — glowing faintly through his shirt, pulsing in time with that held, endless note of wind.

"What's he saying?" Sefa asked quietly.

Wendell spoke constantly now. On the trail he narrated everything in his dead language, naming trees and rocks and birds as if the world would forget them if he didn't. Rosalind had started to recognize the cadences. Greeting. Question. The particular rising lilt he used when he wanted her attention.

This was none of those. This was flat. Frightened. A word repeated over and over, soft as prayer.

"I don't know," Rosalind said. "Wendell."

He didn't respond to his name. He never fully had; it was a sound she'd given him, not one he owned. She crouched in front of him instead, took his cold little hands in hers, and waited until his eyes found hers. Gray eyes, old eyes, eyes that had seen something a week ago in Brask that no seven-year-old should carry.

"Show me," she said. It was the phrase they'd built between them, half gesture half speech, their private bridge across the language gap. *Show me.*

He pointed up at the storm. Then he pressed both palms flat together, drew them apart slowly, and made a sound like tearing cloth.

"Splitting," Ivo translated hoarsely. "Or closing. In Vaelmoorian the same root covers both — *serran*, to shut, to seal—" He stopped. His face had gone the color of old tallow. "Gods below. He's saying it's sealing."

"The pass?" Sefa asked.

"The *country*," Ivo said. "Everything. The whole wound."

The first flakes reached them then, fat and slow and warm-ish, falling through air that should have been baking. Within a hundred heartbeats the warm ones were gone and the snow turned mean — dry, granular, driven sideways by wind that arrived all at once, as if a door had opened somewhere above the world.

"We move," Rosalind said. "Now. Waystation's two hours. We make it or we dig in."

They made neither.

* * *

The waystation, when they finally found it — or the ruin that had been the waystation, because the roof had gone and the walls were half-swallowed by drifts that hadn't existed yesterday — offered nothing but a memory of shelter. Rosalind stood in the wreckage of what had been, according to Ivo's maps, a solid two-story toll house, and watched the storm erase the world around them.

Visibility had dropped to arm's length. The temperature was falling in a way that felt less like weather and more like intent. Her fingers ached inside her gloves. Wendell shivered against her back where she carried him, and his shivering wasn't slowing, and that frightened her more than the storm did.

"Cave," she shouted into the wind. Sefa leaned close to hear. "There's a cave — half a mile back down-trail, below the ridge line. Granite overhang. I marked it when we passed."

"You marked a *cave*?"

"I mark everything. Move!"

Backtracking through whiteout was the hardest hour of travel Rosalind had ever done, and she had once crossed the Salt Marches blindfolded after a fever took her sight for six days. She navigated by feel — slope of the ground underfoot, the lean of the wind against her left cheek, the memory of her own footsteps. Twice she lost the trail entirely and found it again only because Wendell, pressed against her ear, made a small insistent sound each time she drifted, like a compass needle trembling true.

At last the rock rose out of the whiteness ahead, black and solid, and there was the overhang, and beneath it a mouth of darkness going back into the mountain's bones.

They collapsed inside like dropped puppets.

Ivo got a fire going from their emergency pitch and a handful of scrub pine he'd had the foresight to stuff in his pack — the first competent thing she'd seen him do, and she told him so, and he laughed in a cracked, hysterical way that meant he was closer to breaking than any of them. Sefa checked the wound at her side, grimaced, repacked it. Wendell stopped shivering near the fire and fell asleep sitting up, mid-word, his head lolling against Rosalind's shoulder.

Outside, the storm howled its one long note.

Rosalind sat with the boy's weight warm against her and did the arithmetic she was always doing now. Food: nine days, careful. Firewood: whatever scrub grew near the cave mouth, which wouldn't be much under four feet of new snow. Options: wait the storm out, or—

Except storms ended. That was the thing about storms. They blew themselves out in hours, a day at most, even the killers. This one had been falling for six hours without slackening, without gusting, without variation. One note. One breath, held.

*I've surveyed these ranges,* Ivo kept saying. *I have the figures.*

She looked at the cave mouth, where the snow was building a wall of its own, sealing them in grain by grain, and thought: *the same root covers both.*

* * *

It was deep in the night — or what she guessed was night, since time had gone soft and directionless in the cave's black throat — when Wendell woke and began to speak to the dark.

Rosalind surfaced from a shallow doze to hear his voice, small and clear, aimed at the inner reaches of the cave where the firelight gave out. He wasn't talking to them. He was talking *inward*, the way you'd talk to someone standing just past the edge of what you could see.

Sefa was awake too. Their eyes met across the flames. Neither of them moved.

Wendell asked a question. Rosalind knew it was a question by the lift at the end, the shape she'd learned. And then — she would swear this later, under oath if anyone had ever demanded it, though no one ever would — the dark answered.

Not words. Not quite. A sound came back out of the cave's depths, low and resonant, a vibration more felt than heard, rolling up through the stone floor and into Rosalind's spine like a plucked string the size of the mountain. The fire guttered. The sigil at Wendell's throat flared bright enough to throw shadows.

The boy smiled — a wide, unguarded, *childish* smile, the first she'd seen from him that wasn't borrowed from some adult expression — and answered, and the dark replied again, and the two of them conversed in the oldest language either of them knew, while three adults sat frozen around a dying fire.

"What is it saying?" Ivo whispered. His voice shook. "What is it—"

"Hush." Sefa's hand found her knife.

"No." Rosalind put her hand over Sefa's wrist and pushed it down, gently, firmly. "No knives. Look at him."

They looked. Wendell knelt at the border of the light, rocking slightly, chattering away with the delight of a child describing a dream to a parent, utterly without fear. Whatever was in the dark, it was not frightening him. That was the whole argument, and it was sufficient.

The conversation went on for perhaps a quarter hour. Then the deep sound came one final time — longer, softer, almost tender — and faded, and Wendell yawned enormously, crawled back to Rosalind, burrowed into her side, and was asleep again within a minute.

The cave was silent. The storm's note continued outside, muffled now by the snow wall that had sealed the entrance to a gap barely wide enough to crawl through.

"Well," Sefa said at last. "That's new."

"He said the mountain remembers," Ivo said. His voice was strange, thick. He was staring into the dark with tears running freely down his face, and he didn't seem aware of them. "That's what he told me just now, when I asked. He said — he said the mountain remembers Vaelmoor. All the stone does. The erasure took the maps and the names and the graves, but stone doesn't forget, stone can't be made to forget, and the boy can hear it. He's been hearing it since Brask. Since before Brask. He thought everyone could."

Rosalind looked down at the sleeping child against her ribs. Warm. Breathing. Talking to mountains.

"And the mountain said what? Just now. What did it tell him?"

Ivo wiped his face with the heel of his hand. When he answered, his voice had changed — steadier, harder, the drunk burned off it like fog off a morning field.

"It told him the storm isn't weather. It's the erasure finishing its work. The same power that unwrote Vaelmoor a century ago is trying to unwrite the passes — seal Thornfell shut so nothing crosses, so nothing *leaves*, so the last echo dies in the cold with the rest of the country's memory." He swallowed. "And it told him something else. Something it said the Choir must not learn."

Rosalind's blood went quiet and cold. "Which is?"

"That the Threshold Door opens from both sides." Ivo met her eyes across the fire. "Vaelmoor isn't gone, Rosalind. It's *behind*. Behind the mountains, behind the winter, behind a hundred years of silence. And it's still there. Still waiting. Still *ours* — if anyone can reach the door before the world finishes nailing it shut."

The storm screamed its single note against the mountain. Wendell slept on, his small fist closed around the sigil, and deep beneath them, patient as stone, something in the dark hummed a lullaby in a language older than kingdoms — and waited, with them, for spring.
