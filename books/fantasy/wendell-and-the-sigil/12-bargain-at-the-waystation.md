## 12. Bargain at the Waystation

The wound in Sefa Kade's side had opinions about the weather.

It spoke to her at night, mostly — a low, wet complaint that rose when the wind came off the pass and fell when she pressed her palm flat against the bandage and breathed like Ivo had taught her, slow, through the nose, counting. Four in. Six out. The arrow had gone through muscle and out the other side, which the waystation's keeper said was lucky, and which Sefa knew was arithmetic: an inch deeper and it would have found her liver, and then Rosalind would have buried her under a cairn on the ford's far bank and carried on north with a boy who still flinched when Sefa laughed too loud.

She was not going to die on a cot in a smuggler's waystation with her boots off. She had made that decision somewhere between the blood loss and the fever, and it was the only clean thing left in her head.

The waystation sat in a fold of the mountain like a stone swallowed by a bigger stone — no sign, no road on any map worth trusting, just a door of grey timber set into the hillside below the tree line, known to maybe forty people alive and half of them dead by now. Inside it smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of beer kept too long in a cold cellar. The keeper was a woman named Hesk, broad as a door herself, with forearms that suggested she'd once done work requiring them and eyes that suggested she remembered everyone who'd ever owed her.

Sefa had been there four days. Long enough for the fever to break. Long enough to learn the rhythm of the place: Hesk's husband hauling water from the spring before dawn, the mule complaining in its shed, the wind testing the shutters like a thief working a lock.

Long enough to notice the man in the corner.

He'd arrived the second evening, alone, riding a good horse badly — the posture of a man more used to pews than saddles. He wore a plain brown traveling coat, unremarkable, but Sefa had spent fifteen years reading people for coin and she read him in about ten seconds: the way he sat facing the door, the way his hands stayed visible on the table, the small white scar of a removed brand at the edge of his jaw where ink or fire had once marked something he no longer wanted to be. He paid with new-minted silver. He prayed before eating, briefly, lips moving, thumb tracing a line down his sternum — the gesture of the Choir, the one they called *the plumb line*, measuring the soul against the silence.

A sympathizer. Not white-robe himself — they didn't travel alone, didn't travel soft — but a listener. A giver of alms. A man who believed, and whose belief had bought him a warm coat and a full purse and a role to play: ears on the mountain.

Hesk called him Master Ollen. He tipped well. He asked, casually, over three days, whether much traffic came through the high route this late in the season, and whether any of it was armed, and whether Hesk had heard anything about trouble at the ford.

Sefa lay on her cot behind the partition curtain and listened to all of it with her heart going like a drum in a procession.

* * *

On the fifth morning, Rosalind changed the dressing.

She did it the way she did everything — efficiently, without ceremony, her hands steady even though Sefa could see the exhaustion banked behind her eyes like coals. Four days of watching, four days of Wendell sleeping curled against her side like a burr, four days of Ivo muttering over his maps by the fire and drinking less than he wanted to, which for Ivo was its own kind of heroism.

"It's clean," Rosalind said. "Closing from the inside out. Another week and you can walk."

"Another three days and I can walk."

"Another week."

"You always were a tyrant with the convalescence."

"I had a sergeant who lost two men to reopened wounds on a retreat," Rosalind said, tying off the bandage. "After that I stopped negotiating." She sat back on her heels and looked at Sefa, and for a moment the mercenary mask slipped and what was underneath was just tired, just human, just a woman who had pulled her oldest friend out of a frozen river of a battle and hadn't slept properly since. "You shouldn't have held the crossing."

"Somebody had to."

"I was coming back."

"I know." Sefa grinned, because grinning was cheaper than saying what she meant. "I just got there first. You're slow for someone so smug about being fast."

Rosalind almost smiled. Then Wendell appeared at the curtain's edge with two chipped cups of Hesk's terrible barley tea, offered one to Sefa with both hands, the way he did everything now — carefully, formally, like a small ambassador from a court that no longer existed. He'd taken to tending Sefa with grave attention: refilling her water, fetching the extra blanket, sitting beside her cot in the evenings while she told stories about Rosalind's younger years, most of them exaggerated, all of them true in spirit.

"How is the pain?" he asked. His accent had softened since Brask; he was learning Thornic words the way other children learned songs, greedily, repeating them under his breath.

"Like a dog bit me and I deserved it," Sefa said.

Wendell considered this seriously. "Did you?"

"Absolutely."

He nodded, satisfied, and went to sit by the fire with Ivo, who was teaching him the names of mountain peaks off a map that Ivo had drawn himself and annotated in a hand that shook only slightly anymore. Sefa watched them together and felt the thing in her chest that wasn't the wound — the other thing, the heavier one — settle into place like a stone finding its socket.

That was why she did what she did next. She would tell herself that, later, over and over, until the telling wore grooves in the lie deep enough to walk in.

* * *

Master Ollen took his supper alone at the corner table, as he had every night. Sefa waited until Hesk was in the cellar and her husband was mending harness, until the room held only the fire's crackle and the wind and the man's quiet breathing. Then she got up, moving carefully, keeping her weight off the left side, and crossed the floor and sat down across from him without asking.

Ollen looked up. His face did the polite blankness of a man who has been trained not to react, but his hands went still around his bowl, and stillness, in Sefa's experience, was its own confession.

"You're the one from the ford," he said quietly.

"I'm the one from the ford."

"The woman who held the crossing against six." His eyes went to her side, to the way she held herself. "They say you killed two of ours."

"They say right."

He didn't flinch. That was the thing about the believers — the true ones, the ones who'd given something up for it. They didn't flinch. They looked at you with pity, which was worse. "You're protecting the echo," he said. "The child."

"I'm protecting my friend and her kid. The theology's above my pay grade."

"He isn't anyone's child." Ollen set down his spoon. "He's a wound in the world, mistress Kade. A thread the gods themselves cut loose, and every day he walks free, the silence grows teeth. You've seen it. The winter that won't end. The passes sealing. The land itself trying to close around him like flesh around a splinter."

Sefa had seen it. That was the problem. She'd seen the blizzard come down out of a blue sky, seen the village that wasn't there light its windows at a boy's voice, seen things on the climb north that she'd stopped talking about because talking made them realer. Fear was a lever, and Ollen knew how to find it, and she hated him a little for being competent.

"What do you want?" she said.

"To help you." He leaned forward, voice dropping. "There are blades on this mountain who've taken Choir coin, a dozen at least, and Cantor Vess herself is coming up the western road with thirty. Your party is one wounded smuggler, a drunk surveyor, a woman with a sword, and a child. You will not reach Cerenne. But there is a way through that Vess doesn't know — the old drover's cut, under the Horn, hard going but open. I can draw it for you. I can tell you which waystations are safe and which have been bought. I can buy you three days' lead."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange—" and here his voice went gentle, which was the trap closing, "—you tell me when you leave. Which route you take. Nothing more. No names beyond what I know. Just enough that when the Cantor comes asking, I have something honest to give her, and she doesn't burn this station looking for answers Hesk can't afford to give."

*Just enough.* Sefa turned her cup slowly on the table. It was a small treachery. That was what made it seductive — it came pre-packaged as mercy, wrapped in arithmetic. A departure time and a direction, traded for a route that might save their lives. Nobody dies of it. Maybe everybody lives of it. And if the Choir caught up anyway, well, mountains were big, and information aged fast in snow.

Except she'd stood at the ford and watched men in white walk through arrow-storm like it was weather, singing, and she knew exactly what Vess did with *just enough.*

And she thought of Wendell bringing her tea with both hands. Of Rosalind changing her bandage with those steady, exhausted hands. Of Ivo, who had cried once, drunk, about a country he'd never seen and loved anyway.

"You're hesitating," Ollen observed.

"I'm doing math."

"Then let me add a figure." He reached into his coat and set a small purse on the table, heavy, clinking. "For your recovery. For the road. Alms freely given."

Sefa looked at the purse. She thought about the life-debt — the one that had put her on this frozen idiot pilgrimage in the first place, the arrow in her side, the whole ruinous beautiful mess of it. Rosalind had dragged her out of the Siltmarsh campaign with half her guts hanging out and never once mentioned it, never once collected, and debts like that don't expire, they compound.

If she refused, and the party walked blind into Vess's net, and Wendell died screaming in some white-robed ritual — that debt defaulted too. All of it. Everything.

If she accepted, she'd be selling a little piece of them. A departure time. A direction. *Mercy,* she told herself, and heard how thin it sounded even inside her own skull.

"Three days' lead," Sefa said finally. "Your cut route. Safe stations named. And the purse."

"And in return—"

"A day. I'll give you one day after we move. One day, and then you can send whatever you want wherever you want. That's the bargain. Take it or I'll scream that you tried to buy me, and Hesk will feed you to her mule."

Something moved behind Ollen's eyes — disappointment, maybe, or relief, or the patient satisfaction of a man who knows that one day is a crack and cracks widen. "One day," he agreed.

They shook on it. His hand was dry and warm and steady, and hers, she noticed, was not.

* * *

She drew the route for herself that night, copying Ollen's description onto the back of Ivo's spare map by candlelight, her letters careful, her side throbbing. The drover's cut under the Horn. Two safe stations. A spring that ran warm enough to drink. It was good information. It might genuinely save them.

She hid the map in the lining of her pack, under the false bottom she'd sewn for smuggling years ago, and lay back on her cot and stared at the rafters and did not sleep.

Guilt, it turned out, was not a sharp thing. She'd expected knives. What she got was weight — a slow accumulation, like snow on a roof, like the way the long winter had been pressing down on Thornfell all these weeks, silent, structural, waiting for one beam to give. Every time Wendell laughed at one of Ivo's jokes, the roof settled lower. When Rosalind checked her bandage the next morning and said, "You look better," with actual warmth, Sefa had to turn her face toward the wall and pretend the movement hurt.

It did hurt. Just not where Rosalind was looking.

She caught herself rehearsing confessions. *Ros. I need to tell you something.* And then imagining Rosalind's face — not anger, worse, that quiet recalculation, the mercenary doing threat-assessment on her own oldest friend — and Sefa couldn't finish the sentence even in her head. So she filed it away with all the other things she'd survived by not saying, and told herself the same story on a loop until it almost held: *it's mercy, it's mercy, one day is nothing, one day buys them a clear road, I'm buying them time, I'm buying them time.*

The fourth night after the bargain, the fever came back — low-grade, annoying, the body's invoice for walking on a wound — and Sefa drifted in and out of shallow sleep by the fire while the others talked in low voices. She surfaced once to hear Ivo say, "...Cerenne by the new moon, if the cut holds," and realized with a lurch that she'd given them the cut, that her treachery was already load-bearing in their plans, already part of the architecture. She surfaced again later, near midnight, to hear Wendell singing.

It was the first time she'd heard him sing. Softly, barely voiced, in the old tongue — a wandering melody with no chorus, the kind of song that sounded like it had been sung to children for a thousand years in a country that no longer existed. Rosalind sat listening with her chin on her fist, and the firelight moved on her face, and for a moment she looked young, and unguarded, and utterly defenseless in the specific way of people who love something they cannot protect forever.

*One day,* Sefa told the ceiling. *One day, and then they're ghosts to him. Snow covers tracks. Mountains eat roads. By the time his message reaches Vess, we'll be stone throws from the door.*

The ceiling didn't answer. Ceilings never do. That's why people talk to gods instead, and look how that had gone for everyone.

* * *

On the seventh morning, Sefa walked to the door and back without support, twice, sweating, triumphant. On the eighth, Rosalind stood at the window at dawn with a stillness Sefa recognized from a hundred campaigns — the stillness of a scout reading a landscape — and said, quietly, "Smoke. Western road. A lot of it, moving slow."

Vess. Thirty white robes and a cantor's voice, coming up the mountain like a slow tide.

"We move today," Rosalind said, turning. "Drover's cut, under the Horn, like Ivo planned. Pack everything. Sefa — can you ride?"

"I can ride," Sefa said. Her mouth was dry. "I can ride."

They were on the trail within the hour, five figures and two mules threading up out of the valley into the grey throat of the pass, and Sefa rode at the rear and did not look back at the waystation, and did not let herself calculate how many hours of lead they had, because she knew the number exactly, and the number was one.

Behind them, far down the western road, a rider was already turning his good horse toward the white robes, carrying a departure time and a direction like a lantern cupped against the wind.

And ahead, past the Horn, past the dying storm, something waited at the top of the world that Sefa hadn't bargained for — because that evening, as they made cold camp under an overhang, a figure stepped out of the falling snow alone, unarmed, white-robed, and Rosalind's sword cleared its sheath before the stranger raised both empty hands and said, in a voice like a struck bell:

"I only want to talk to the sworn one. To the woman whose god I used to serve."
