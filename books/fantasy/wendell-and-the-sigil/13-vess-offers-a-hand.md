## 13. Vess Offers a Hand

The waystation at Hollowmere was the kind of place that existed because no one had bothered to tear it down. A long low building of grey timber, a stable with three horses in it, a common room that smelled of tallow and old smoke. The map Ivo carried — his own map, ink still wet at the edges — showed nothing here at all, which by now Rosalind had learned to read as *something*.

They'd been on the mountain four days since the ford. Sefa's wound had gone from angry to merely sullen, which Sefa herself announced as good news twice an hour. Wendell slept against the wall of the common room with the sigil tucked under his chin like a second heart. Ivo drank one cup of wine and then, visibly wrestling with himself, a second.

Rosalind went out to see about the horses.

That was the mistake, or it would have been, if Vess hadn't already made all of hers for her. The Cantor was sitting on the mounting block in the yard, white robes folded neat as laundry, hands resting on her knees, watching the snow come down off Cerenne with the patience of someone who had never once in her life needed to hurry.

"Mercenary," Vess said.

Rosalind's hand found her sword hilt and stopped there, not drawing, not yet. "You're alone."

"I'm alone." Vess turned her head. She was older than Rosalind had guessed at the ford — fifty, maybe more, with a face like a dried riverbed, all channels and no water. "Your smuggler is inside telling your cartographer a story about a man she killed in Kestrel Bay. Your boy is asleep. And my people are a mile down the trail, warming themselves around a fire I told them not to leave." A small shrug. "I keep my word. It's the last currency I have left, so I spend it carefully."

"You spent forty lives at the ford."

"I offered them passage first." Vess said it without heat, without apology, the way you'd note that you'd checked a lock before breaking a door. "They chose badly. That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic."

Rosalind knew arithmetic. She'd lived on it for twenty years — contracts priced in blood, retreats calculated to the stride. She hated hearing it in another woman's mouth, especially this mouth, especially now.

"Why are you here?"

"Because you're the only person on this mountain I can talk to," Vess said, "and we both know it."

* * *

She didn't move from the mounting block, and Rosalind didn't sit, and so they conducted the strangest negotiation of Rosalind's career standing and sitting in falling snow, ten feet apart, like two generals who'd wandered into each other's camps by accident.

"You were sworn," Vess said. "Before the silence. Don't insult us both by denying it. I've seen how you pray — or how you used to. You still make the sign when you think no one's looking. Left shoulder, then the heart, then the breath. That's the Ashen Order's rite. That's *our* rite."

"The Choir isn't mine."

"The Choir is what was left when the god stopped answering and somebody finally asked why." Vess leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and for a moment the cantor fell away and there was just a tired woman in the cold. "I was a hearth-priestess, mercenary. Thirty years. I kept the flame at Vellichor for a congregation of two hundred souls, and every morning I sang the waking verse, and every morning He heard me. Then one day He didn't. No thunder. No omen. Just — quiet. Like walking into a room where someone died an hour ago and hasn't been found yet."

Rosalind's throat tightened. She knew that room. She'd been living in it for six years.

"We tore the world apart looking for the reason," Vess went on. "We burned libraries. We interrogated heretics. We did things I will answer for eventually, if anything is left to answer to. And do you know what we found? Nothing. Because the answer wasn't hidden. It was *removed*. An entire kingdom, scraped off the face of the earth, and every voice of heaven went out like lamps in a wind."

"And your conclusion," Rosalind said, "is to kill the child."

"Our conclusion is to finish the work." Vess's eyes came up, and they were steady, and that was the worst part. Fanatics blinked. Believers wavered. This woman did neither. "What was erased can be made permanent. What remains can be gathered and burned. And when the last thread of Vaelmoor is ash, the wound closes over clean, and maybe — *maybe* — the gods find their voices again in a world that has stopped pretending the past can be undone."

"That's not faith. That's surgery."

"It's both." Vess smiled, thin and genuine, and Rosalind understood with a lurch that she was talking to a mirror with different scars. "But you didn't come out here for my theology. You came out here because your horses needed checking, and I'm here because I have something you want. So let's stop circling."

* * *

Vess stood. Up close she was taller than she'd seemed, and there was a smell under the incense that clung to her robes — snowmelt and iron, the smell of the ford, of men dying in white water.

"Six years," Vess said. "That's how long you've been deaf, isn't it? I know the Order's rolls. Ishikawa, sworn at seventeen, decorated at twenty-two, silent at twenty-five. You took contracts after, but you took them wrong — too cheap, too far north, always within earshot of some shrine or other. You weren't working, mercenary. You were waiting by a door that wasn't going to open."

Every word landed like a coin dropped into a well. Rosalind stood very still and listened to them fall.

"I can open it," Vess said.

"No."

"I haven't made the offer yet."

"You don't have to. I know what it costs."

"Do you?" Vess tilted her head. "Humor me. Say it aloud. Say what I'm asking for."

Rosalind looked past her, up the slope, toward the waystation window where lamplight glowed yellow through frost-furred glass. Somewhere behind that glass a boy was sleeping with a dead kingdom held against his chest.

"The boy."

"The boy," Vess agreed gently, as if Rosalind had answered a catechism correctly. "And in exchange: your god's voice, restored. Not someday. Not metaphorically. We hold rites, mercenary, that the Order abandoned centuries ago — old ones, from before the erasure, when the boundary between His attention and ours was thinner. When Vaelmoor is finished, when the last echo is silenced, the severance completes itself cleanly, and everything that was cut falls back into place. Every oath honored. Every prayer heard. You would kneel at a shrine and He would *answer*, the way He answered when you were young and whole and certain." Vess paused. "I'm told you've forgotten what that's like. I haven't. I remember it every day. That's what keeps me doing this."

The snow came down between them, fat and slow, filling the space where a sword stroke should have been.

"You're lying," Rosalind said. "Or you're wrong. Either way, you're selling something you can't deliver."

"Probably," Vess said, and laughed — short, real, ugly. "Probably! Do you imagine I don't know that? I am betting the souls of everyone who follows me on a theology I cannot prove, built on a crime I helped commit, in the hope that the gods are simple enough to be repaired like a broken bridge." She stepped closer, and Rosalind let her, which she would think about later with something like shame. "But here is what I *can* deliver, today, in this yard. I can call off forty blades. I can give you the high pass unmarked. I can put food in your smuggler and wine in your cartographer and let the boy go home to whatever hole he crawled out of. No more fords. No more burning shops. No more dead friends. You walk away poorer than you are now but with everyone breathing, and you spend the rest of your life wondering — every morning, at every shrine, making that little sign, left shoulder, heart, breath — whether the old man in the sky would have spoken to you again if you'd just handed over one child who was never yours."

The words went in exactly where Vess wanted them. Rosalind felt them settle, felt the truth of the shape of them even as she despised the hand that had carved it. Six years of silence. Six years of contracts taken wrong, of praying into a well, of being a woman defined by a duty that had stopped acknowledging her existence. And here it was, itemized: everything she had lost, priced at one sleeping boy.

It was almost respectable, the honesty of it. That was the trap's finest craftsmanship. Vess hadn't lied to her. Vess had simply shown her the ledger and let her read her own name in the debit column.

"Wendell isn't a thread to burn," Rosalind said quietly. "He's a person. He gets cold. He's afraid of the dark until he talks to it, and then he isn't. He counts the horses before he sleeps. He thinks Sefa's laugh means the weather's turning bad." Her voice stayed level; she was proud of that, later. "You want me to price him. I'm telling you he doesn't have one."

"Everything has a price. You taught me that, mercenary — your whole profession is the proof."

"Then you've mispriced me."

"Have I?" Vess studied her, and for a moment something moved behind the steadiness — not doubt, but its cousin, the thing doubt becomes in people too disciplined to admit it. "I watched you at the ford. You fought like a woman with nothing behind her worth defending, and then the boy shouted, and you changed. I've commanded soldiers for thirty years. I know the difference between duty and love, and I know which one moves faster." She stepped back, retreating into ceremony, folding her hands. "So. I'll ask once, plainly, priestess to whatever you are now. Give me the echo, and be whole."

The yard was very quiet. Snow ticked against the stable roof. Inside, faintly, Sefa laughed, and Wendell must have woken, because a thin thread of Vaelmoorian drifted through the shutter cracks — a question, probably, or a joke, in a language only three living people could speak and one of them wanted it dead.

Rosalind thought about the shrine at Brask. About kneeling until her knees went numb, saying the waking verse into silence, getting up and doing it again the next morning because that was what she was, that was the whole of what she was, a sentry posted at a door no one would ever come through. She thought about how easy it would be. How Vess would even let her believe it afterward. How she could spend fifty years kneeling in front of that restored altar and never once be sure the voice she heard was Him and not her own need, wearing His shape.

And she thought about a knock in the snow, half a winter ago. A small fist on a cabin door, and her own hand — traitor hand, mercenary hand, a hand that had done arithmetic all its life — opening the latch anyway.

"No," Rosalind said.

Vess closed her eyes briefly, like a merchant watching a fortune walk out of her shop. When she opened them, the warmth was gone, packed away, and what remained was the thing that had burned an archivist's shop with a blind man inside it.

"I hoped," Vess said. "Genuinely. It would have been easier for everyone." She turned toward the trail, then paused. "For what it's worth — the offer stands until the Door. After that, it doesn't, and neither do you. My people move at first light. Sleep badly, mercenary. It sharpens the reflexes."

She walked down into the snowfall and was gone between the pines, white robe into white world, and Rosalind stood alone in the yard with her sword still gripped and her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

Inside, she'd have to tell them. First light. Forty blades. She'd have to wake Ivo, wake Sefa, wake the boy, and get them onto a mountain in the dark with a wounded woman and no head start.

Rosalind sheathed her sword, looked once more at the lamplit window, and started running for the door.
