## 14. Refusal

Vess stood in the snow with her hands open, the way you'd stand before a horse you didn't want to spook.

Behind her, half-hidden in the white pines, Rosalind counted shapes. Six that she could see. Probably four she couldn't. The Choir wore no armor — they never did, as if the world itself were supposed to protect them, as if faith were a kind of mail — but the two flanking Vess carried hooked blades, the kind made for taking a man alive.

"You heard me," Vess said. "I know you did. I watched your face when I said his name."

They had come down from the ridge at dusk to a hollow where the stream ran under ice, a place Ivo swore was sheltered, and found it already occupied. That was Sefa's fault, or Sefa's bargain coming home, though nobody had said so yet. Nobody needed to. Rosalind had seen the way Sefa wouldn't meet her eyes since the waystation, and she had filed it away in the place where mercenaries kept things they'd deal with later, if there was a later.

Now Vess stood ten paces off with snow settling on her white robes like ash, and Wendell's small hand was fisted in the back of Rosalind's coat, and the offer hung in the air between them, still warm.

Her god's voice returned. Her oaths honored. The silence lifted after eleven years of praying into a well with no bottom.

All she had to do was give them the boy.

"I'm waiting," Vess said gently. "I can wait. I'm good at waiting. It's what we do, those of us who were left behind when He stopped speaking. We wait, and we keep the forms, and we tell ourselves He's testing us." Her breath fogged. "But He isn't testing us, Ishikawa. He's *gone*. And this child is the thread that leads back to Him. You must feel it. You were His. I can always tell."

Rosalind felt it. That was the obscenity of it. She looked at Wendell — at the sharp little face, the frost-bitten ears, the sigil on its cord against his chest — and something in her chest turned over like a key in a lock that hadn't been oiled in years. She had felt His presence through the boy from almost the first night. A warmth in the dead shrine of her own ribs. She'd told herself it was just the child, just having someone to feed.

Maybe it was. Maybe it wasn't. It didn't matter anymore, and that was the thing she understood standing there in the blue dusk, understanding it the way you understand a wound has gone numb: it didn't matter because she had already chosen, days ago, weeks ago, without ceremony, without noticing, the way water chooses downhill.

"No," she said.

Vess's face didn't change. "Think—"

"I have thought." Her voice came out flat and tired, and she let it be tired. There was no performance left in her. "You want me to say something noble. I don't have anything noble. I'm fifty-one years old, my knees are shot, I haven't been paid for anything in eight months, and the only contract I've got left is walking that boy north. So no. Not for his sake. Not even mostly for his sake. Because he's mine now, and I don't leave contracts, and I don't leave family, and I am so *tired* of losing gods and kings and countries that never gave a damn whether I lived."

The words came out of her like splinters, one after another, and she heard herself and thought: *well. There it is.* Eleven years of silence and the truth finally arrived, and it wasn't holy at all. It was just a tired woman refusing to lose one more thing.

Vess exhaled slowly. Something moved behind her eyes — not anger. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"I prayed beside you once," Vess said. "At Corvane, before the fall. You sang louder than anyone. I remember thinking, there is a woman He will never abandon." She lifted her chin, and the gentleness went out of her voice like a lamp going out. "Take them."

* * *

The first hooked blade came out of the pines on her left, and Rosalind was already moving, because she had spent the entire conversation counting and positioning and doing the arithmetic she was always good at while her mouth said other things.

She pulled Wendell down and behind her, drew steel, and met the man's wrist with the flat, then the point. He folded around it with a sound like wet rope. The second one was faster, coming low from the streambank, and she gave ground instead of holding it — ground meant nothing here, only angles meant something — and put a pine trunk between them. His blade bit wood. She hit him across the temple with the pommel and he sat down in the snow and stayed there.

Six visible. Two down. Four plus the hidden ones.

"IVO!" she shouted. "THE ROCKS!"

Because Ivo Renner, drunk or sober, coward or not, knew every fold of Thornfell better than any living man, and three days ago he'd pointed at this hollow and muttered that the scree slope above it was "rotten as old cheese, one good shout brings the whole hillside down." She'd filed that away too. Mercenaries filed everything away. It was the whole profession.

A crossbow bolt hissed past her ear and buried itself in the pine. On the slope above, Ivo — bless his ruined liver — had understood, and he was shouting, wordless, arms windmilling, hurling rocks himself like a madman, and the hillside answered him. A groan went through the scree field, deep and mineral, and then the white slope began to move.

"SEFA! WENDELL! UP THE TRAIL, NOW!"

Sefa Kade came out of the trees with her axe already red, which meant the hidden ones hadn't all been hidden well enough, and she scooped Wendell up one-armed — he weighed nothing, he weighed *nothing*, when had he last eaten properly — and ran. Rosalind backed after them, blade up, giving the Choir her eyes because somebody had to watch the blades while the mountain fell.

Cantor Vess didn't run. She stood in the rushing white flood of it, robes whipping, and she wasn't looking at the avalanche at all. She was looking at Rosalind with an expression of terrible calm, and she called out over the roar:

"You'll change your mind at the door, Ishikawa! When you see what it costs! Everyone does!"

Then the scree took the hollow, and the pines went down like matchsticks, and Rosalind turned and climbed.

* * *

They gained the ridge in the dark with their lungs full of knives.

Below, the dust of the slide still hung in the air, glittering in the moonlight, burying the hollow and the stream and whatever men the Choir had lost to their own ambush. Not Vess. Rosalind would have bet her remaining coin Vess had walked out of it untouched, because women like that always did; the world protected them, or they protected themselves, and after long enough there was no difference.

Sefa set Wendell down against a boulder and leaned on her axe, breathing in great tearing gulps. Blood sheeted her left forearm — a hook-blade cut, shallow but long. Ivo collapsed onto his back and stared at the stars and laughed, high and cracked, until Rosalind crouched next to him and put a hand on his shoulder and he stopped.

"You shouted," she said. "Thank you."

"I've wanted to bring down a hillside for thirty years," Ivo wheezed. "Surveyors aren't allowed. Very frowned upon." He wiped his mouth. "Was that all of them?"

"No," Rosalind and Sefa said together.

Sefa straightened. She looked at Rosalind, and Rosalind looked back, and the thing between them sat there in the snow between the three of them and the boy, patient as a debt.

"The waystation," Rosalind said quietly. "What did you trade, Sefa?"

Sefa's jaw worked. "Route and timing. Nothing else. I swear it on—" She stopped. Whatever she'd been about to swear on, she'd lost the right to it somewhere along this road. "They were going to take the pass ahead of us anyway. I thought — I thought if I gave them something small, they'd let us buy through the Narrows clean. I thought I was buying us a road."

"You bought us an ambush."

"I bought us an ambush I could see coming," Sefa said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Roz. I've carried you out of two burning buildings and a river. I would carry you out of hell. But I've watched you get slower every year and I couldn't — I couldn't watch you walk into a pass with forty zealots ahead of you and do *nothing*. So I did the wrong thing the wrong way, and I'll wear it. Say the word and I'll go."

Wendell had been silent through all of it, pressed against the boulder, watching with those dark unblinking eyes. Now he got up, crossed the snow, and took Sefa's bleeding arm in both hands. He looked up at her and said something in the old tongue — soft, unhurried, three syllables that sounded like water over stones.

"What did he say?" Sefa asked hoarsely.

"He says you're still his," Rosalind translated. She'd been learning it despite herself, the way you learn a language someone you love dreams in. "That's what it means. Still his."

Sefa put her good hand over her face and cried without any sound at all, the way soldiers cry, and Wendell held her arm like he was holding a door shut against a wind.

Rosalind looked away and looked north.

Above them Mount Cerenne stood black against the stars, wearing its storm like a crown, and somewhere beneath it was a door, and somewhere behind them was a woman in white who would follow them to the end of the world and call it mercy. The long winter had sealed every other pass. This was the last road in a country that no longer existed, and everyone on it was broken, and one of them was a child who spoke to doors.

"All right," Rosalind said. She sheathed her sword, shouldered her pack, and held out her hand to Wendell, who took it without looking, the way you take a thing you've always taken. "We climb tonight, while they're digging out. Nobody sleeps warm again until we're through."

Ivo groaned. Sefa wiped her face and picked up her axe.

And Wendell — Wendell tugged her hand, and when she looked down he was gazing up at her with an expression she couldn't read, ancient and young at once, and he said one word in Vaelmoorian that she didn't know yet.

"What's that mean?" she asked.

He smiled, small and crooked, and shook his head. "Later," he said, in the common tongue, his accent thick as fresh snow. "When we're home."

Then he turned and started up the mountain, and because there was nowhere else in the world to be, they followed him.
