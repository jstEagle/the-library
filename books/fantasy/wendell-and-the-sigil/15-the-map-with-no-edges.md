## 15. The Map With No Edges

Ivo Renner had not drawn a true line in eleven years.

He told himself it was the drink, and for a long time that was a comfortable lie. The drink was convenient. The drink explained the shaking hands, the ruined commissions, the morning he'd been found asleep in the surveyor's office in Cael Harrow with his trousers soaked to the knee and the royal seal inked onto his own forehead. The drink was a door he could point at instead of the truth, which was this: on the last day of his career, standing before the Surveyor-General with a map of the northern ranges spread across the table, Ivo had drawn a kingdom that did not exist, and when they laughed at him, something in him had folded up like a wet chart and never fully opened again.

Vaelmoor. He'd put it there because the triangulations said it should be there. Because the rivers bent wrong. Because three separate travelers' accounts from before the war described a pass through the Cerenne massif that no expedition since had ever found, and because Ivo Renner was the kind of man who could not look at an absence on a map without wanting to fill it.

They had called it his fancy. Then his obsession. Then, quietly, his disgrace. He'd been given a pension and a small room above a tavern in Brask, and he had spent eleven years proving them right one cup at a time.

Now he sat in the waystation's back room with a candle burning down and a sheet of good vellum pinned flat under four river stones, and his hands — still shaking, but differently now, shaking the way a bowstring shakes — were drawing Vaelmoor.

* * *

The boy sat across from him, cross-legged on the floor, wrapped in Rosalind's spare cloak like a small grey boulder. Wendell did not sleep much. None of them slept much anymore; the blizzard outside had settled into a low continuous roar, the sound of a world clearing its throat, and the waystation's walls groaned against it. But the boy watched. He watched everything with those dark, unblinking eyes, and when Ivo drew, Wendell corrected him.

That was the miracle of it. That was the thing Ivo could not stop turning over in his mind like a coin too valuable to spend.

He would draw the river course as the old accounts gave it — the Vessin, bending east below the terraced hills — and Wendell would make a small sound, *tch*, and reach out one finger and drag it west. And Ivo would check his sources, sweating, heart hammering, and find that yes, yes, there was a marginal note in the Aldous fragment, disputed, dismissed by every editor since, saying exactly what the child had just said. The river ran west. It had always run west. A hundred years of scholarship had gotten it wrong and a feral boy in a borrowed cloak got it right on the first try.

"You've seen it," Ivo said, for perhaps the fortieth time. "You've walked these streets."

Wendell considered him. The boy's Vaelmoorian came out of him like water out of stone — effortless, ancient — but the trade-tongue sat badly in his mouth, and he used it sparingly, the way a miser spends coin.

"Before," he said finally. "And after." He touched his own chest, then the floor. "After is longer."

Ivo didn't know what to do with that, so he did what he always did with things he couldn't hold: he put them on the map.

* * *

He worked through the night. Sefa brought him tea twice and whiskey once, and stood over his shoulder breathing whiskey-adjacent commentary until Rosalind appeared in the doorway and said, "Sefa," in the voice she probably used on mutinous hirelings, and Sefa went.

But she'd said one thing first, looking down at the half-formed country taking shape under Ivo's pen, and her voice had been strange: "My grandmother used to sing a song about a river with no name. Said it was from before the maps went blank. We all thought she was mad." She'd tapped the vellum where the Vessin ran west. "She wasn't, was she."

"No," Ivo had said. "She remembered."

"Then finish the damn thing," Sefa said, and left him to it.

So he finished it.

He worked from everything. From the Aldous fragment and the Pellham itinerary and the sad, water-stained pilgrim's guide he'd bought off a Brask rag-picker for two coppers eleven years ago and kept like a relic. From his own disgraced triangulations, which he now laid beside the new work and found — this was the part that made him laugh, actually laugh, alone in a freezing room at three in the morning — had been *correct*. Every bearing. Every elevation. He had drawn a true map of a false country, and the falseness had been the world's, not his.

And from Wendell. Mostly from Wendell.

The boy knew the capital — Ivo had stopped thinking of it as *the legendary city of* anything; it was Carreth Vale now, it had streets and a fish market and a bridge with seventeen arches because Wendell had counted them twice, stubbornly, the way children count things that matter to them. He knew the road north along the Vessin. He knew the shrine towns and the salt road and the high shepherd paths. His knowledge was patchy in places — he was a child, and children do not map countries, they map the distance between their bed and the things they love — but where his knowledge ended, the old accounts began again, and the two fit together like joinery. Like the country had been waiting a century for someone to ask after it in the right order.

It was near dawn, the candle a stub drowning in its own wax, when Wendell went very still and pointed to the northeastern corner of the vellum.

"There," he said.

Ivo looked. The corner held Mount Cerenne itself, drawn from his own surveys — the great white shoulder of it, the glacier field, the passes. He'd drawn mountains his whole life. There was nothing remarkable about the drawing.

"Where?" Ivo asked. "There's nothing there but rock and ice."

Wendell made a frustrated sound and climbed up onto the bench beside him, cloak dragging, and took Ivo's hand in both of his small cold ones. The boy guided the pen himself. Down the southern flank of Cerenne, past the tree line, into a fold of the mountain that Ivo's own contour lines showed as a shallow depression — nothing, a wrinkle, the sort of feature a surveyor draws ten thousand times without a second thought.

Here, Wendell's hand pressed down, hard enough to dimple the vellum.

"What's here?" Ivo whispered.

Wendell looked up at him, and for a moment the boy's face did something terrible and old, the way a locked house looks different when you learn someone died in it.

"The Door," he said, in trade-tongue. Then, softer, in the other language, the dead language, the living language: "*Serevan.*"

And Ivo Renner, drunk for eleven years and sober for eleven days, felt the hair rise on his arms, because he *knew* that word. It was in the Pellham itinerary. The last entry, the one every scholar had dismissed as a pilgrim's fever-dream: *We came at dusk to the serevan beneath Cerenne, a door of black stone, and the doorkeeper asked us our names, and we gave them gladly.*

He set down the pen. His hands were shaking properly now. He picked it back up and wrote, in careful surveyor's lettering, beneath the fold of the mountain: **THE THRESHOLD DOOR**.

The map was done.

* * *

Rosalind came in when the light did, grey and thin through the shutters, and stood looking at the vellum spread across the table the way people look at altars.

"That's it?" she said. "That's Vaelmoor?"

"That's Vaelmoor." Ivo heard his own voice crack on the name and didn't care. Eleven years. Eleven years of being the joke of the Surveyor-General's office, the madman of Brask, the drunk who drew kingdoms that weren't there. He wanted to say something grand. What came out was: "The scale's a bit generous in the southwest. I extrapolated."

Rosalind leaned over the map. Sefa crowded in behind her, and even Sefa went quiet, which for Sefa was practically a religious experience. The candlelight moved across the vellum and the country seemed almost to breathe — the terraced hills, the westward river, the seventeen-arched bridge, the little marks Ivo had made for the shrine towns, and there at the top, beneath the white shoulder of the mountain, the black-stone door.

"It's real," Sefa said. "Gods below, it's actually real. You can see it. It has *edges*."

"No," Ivo said.

They looked at him.

He touched the border of the map, where the vellum ran out and the country simply stopped, cut off by the rectangle of the page. "A map has edges because a country has edges. This—" He shook his head. "This isn't how I'd draw a living land. A living land bleeds past the frame. Roads keep going. Rivers keep going. This map ends because my paper ends, not because Vaelmoor does." He looked at the boy. "Does it?"

Wendell studied the map for a long moment. Then he reached out and placed his palm flat against the empty vellum beyond the northeastern edge, beyond the mountain, beyond everything Ivo had drawn.

"*Serevan*," the boy said, to the blankness, the way you'd call someone home from a long way off.

And Ivo would swear on his ruined career, on his mother's grave, on whatever gods were left listening — he would swear the vellum warmed under the boy's hand. Just slightly. Just the way a stone steps warm when the sun finds it.

"See," Wendell said. "No edges."

* * *

They planned around the table while the storm screamed. Rosalind was different now that the route existed — Ivo had watched her all week move through the waystation like a woman walking through deep water, and now the water was draining away and underneath it was something hard and bright and terrible. She traced the ascent with one finger: up the southern flank, through the fold Wendell had shown them, avoiding the high col where the Choir would surely be watching.

"How far from here?" she asked.

"Four days, if the weather breaks. Six if it doesn't." Ivo tapped the fold in the mountain. "This depression here — it'll shelter us from the wind for the last stretch. If my contours are right."

"And if they're wrong?"

"Then we're lost on Cerenne in a killing storm, and honestly, Rosalind, at this point I've waited eleven years to be wrong in the field again. Let me have it."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and died young. She straightened. "Then we leave when the storm gives us a window. Sefa — provisions. Ivo—"

"I know," he said. "Copies. If we lose the original, the knowledge survives." He was already pulling fresh sheets toward him. It felt extraordinary, this ordinary work. Being useful. Being *necessary*. He had forgotten the specific pleasure of it, the way a man forgets sunlight in a long winter: that his skill could matter to someone's survival, that a line drawn well might be the difference between a door found and a door lost forever.

Wendell stayed at the table while the others moved. The boy bent close over the finished map, lips moving, naming each place in turn — the bridge, the market, the shrine towns, the river running west — a child saying his prayers over the body of his country.

Then he looked up at Ivo, and said something in Vaelmoorian, slow and deliberate, clearly meant to be understood.

"I don't speak it," Ivo admitted. "What does it mean?"

Wendell thought. He was getting better at translating, or maybe just more willing. "'The man who remembers,'" he said at last. "Is your name. In my language." He touched the map, right where the door was drawn. "You remembered us. So we remember you."

Ivo Renner turned away quickly and pretended to check his ink supply, because he was sixty-one years old and a professional, and professionals do not weep over a compliment from a child in a freezing waystation while the world tries to bury itself in snow.

He got the ink. He started the copy. Outside, the storm roared on — but underneath the roar, faint, rhythmic, almost patient, he could hear something else.

Footsteps in the snow. Many of them. Circling the waystation.

And a voice, lifted against the wind, singing in parts.
