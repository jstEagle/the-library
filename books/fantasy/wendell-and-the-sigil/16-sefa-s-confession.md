## 16. Sefa's Confession

Sefa Kade had lied to better people than Rosalind Ishikawa and slept fine afterward. That was the trade. You learned it young on the passes: a lie was a tool like any other, a piton or a knife, and you didn't weep over a knife.

She told herself that again the morning after the waystation, sitting on her bedroll with her wounded leg stretched out in front of her, watching Rosalind break down the camp with the economy of a woman who had done nothing else for twenty years. The lie sat in Sefa's chest like a swallowed stone. She named it a tool. It did not become one.

The bargain had been simple. At the waystation, while Rosalind slept and Ivo drank himself toward sleep and Wendell — Wendell was always somewhere else, listening to walls — Sefa had found the sympathizer. A woman with a Choir pin hidden under her collar and frostbitten fingers and the particular hunger of someone who wanted out of something. Sefa had traded her what she knew: their route through the Cerenne col, their numbers, their condition. In exchange, passage past the Choir's forward pickets. A corridor of mercy, three days wide, before the information reached the wrong ears.

Three days. It had been four.

She'd told herself it was mercy. That was the part she couldn't stop chewing on. Not cowardice, not coin — she hadn't taken coin — but mercy. Because Rosalind would never have agreed, and Rosalind would have been right not to agree, and Sefa had decided that she knew better than the woman who had once dragged her, screaming, out of a burning customs house at Greymouth with two arrows in her back. *I owe you my life,* Sefa had said, all those years ago, meaning it the way you mean things at twenty-two. *So I'll spend it how you say.* And then, twenty years later, when it finally came due, she had spent it behind Rosalind's back, purchasing safety with the only currency she had left: the truth about where they were going.

The boy looked up from the fire. He had a way of looking at her lately, Wendell did — not suspicious, exactly. Attentive. Like he could hear the stone rattling around in her chest every time she moved.

She looked away first.

* * *

It broke open at noon, on the shoulder below the col, where the wind came off the ice fields hard enough to lean against.

They were roped together, the four of them, moving single file across a slope of scree and old snow, when Ivo stopped so suddenly that Sefa walked into his pack. He was holding his map case open against his chest, and his face had gone the color of the glacier above them.

"This is wrong," he said.

"Everything's wrong," Sefa said. "Keep walking."

"No." He turned the map case around. Inside, drawn in his cramped surveyor's hand, was the route ahead — the col, the descent into the hidden valley, the long climb to Cerenne. And pinned to the page, weighted by a stone, a slip of paper that had not been there an hour ago.

Rosalind took it. Sefa watched her read it, watched her face do nothing at all, which was worse than anything.

"What is it?" Sefa asked, and heard her own voice come out flat and careful, the voice of a woman standing very still near a cliff edge.

Rosalind read it aloud. Her voice was quiet and even, the way it went before violence. "*The col is watched. Take the eastern shelf. We will be waiting where the map ends.*" A pause. "Signed with the Choir's mark. And underneath—" She stopped.

"Underneath," Sefa said, "it says *paid in full, S.K.*"

Nobody moved. The wind leaned on them. Somewhere above, ice cracked and settled, the mountain clearing its throat.

"You went back to the waystation." Rosalind didn't turn around. "While I slept."

"I bought us a corridor," Sefa said. "Three days past their pickets. It should have held. Something spooked them early, some order from Vess, I don't know—"

"You sold our route."

"I bought our lives." Now Sefa did move, stepping forward off the rope line, leg dragging, because if this was going to be a fight she was going to be upright for it. "Look at us, Roz. Look at us. One mercenary with a dead god, one drunk with a map, one child, and me on half a leg. Against a choir that hires every blade on the mountain. I found the one crack in them and I paid for it with the only thing I had. I did the arithmetic."

"You did the arithmetic," Rosalind repeated.

"That's your language, isn't it? Duty's arithmetic. One truth against four lives. I made the trade you were too proud to make."

Rosalind turned then, and Sefa had been on the wrong end of that look twice in twenty years and had hoped never to see it a third time. It wasn't rage. Rage would have been survivable. It was the look of a woman re-counting everything she thought she knew.

"Ivo," Rosalind said quietly. "Take Wendell up the slope. Fifty paces. Don't listen."

"Sefa—" Ivo started.

"Now."

He went. She heard him murmuring to the boy as they climbed away, low and useless, the sound of a man trying to cover a wound with words.

Then it was just the two of them on the scree, and the wind, and twenty years.

"I'm not going to apologize," Sefa said. "You'd smell a lie."

"Good. Because I don't want an apology. I want to know why." Rosalind folded the note along its crease, once, precisely. "Not the arithmetic. I know the arithmetic. Why didn't you tell me?"

And there it was. The real question, the one under the stone. Sefa looked north, toward the col, toward the place where the map ended and the Choir waited, and told the truth because there was no longer any percentage in anything else.

"Because you'd have said no. And because you'd have been right to say no, and I couldn't carry both of those things, so I carried neither. I told myself it was mercy. Mercy for the boy, mostly. He's the whole reason any of this — Roz, I've watched you since Greymouth. I've watched you spend yourself on contracts for men who didn't know your name, on oaths to a god that stopped answering, on duty like it was a debt you owed the world. And now there's finally one thing you're spending yourself on that's worth it, and I thought — if the price of getting him through is one dirty secret, one thing done in the dark that you never have to know about — then let it be mine. Let me be the one who carries something ugly. You've carried enough."

The wind filled the silence.

"You don't get to decide what I can carry," Rosalind said. "That's not yours."

"No," Sefa said. "It isn't. I know."

"The pickets. The corridor. Is any of it real?"

"It was real. Three days. It's day five." Sefa jerked her chin at the note. "Whoever wrote that either wants us alive enough to redirect us, or wants us exactly where the map ends. With Vess, those are usually the same thing."

"So we're walking into their hands either way."

"We're walking into their hands either way," Sefa agreed. "Difference is, now you know, and you can hate me while we do it."

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment. Then she clipped herself back onto the rope, tested the anchor, and said, "I don't have time to hate you. I need every blade on this mountain, and you're one of them. Walk."

It was the best sentence Sefa had heard in days, and it felt like being pardoned by a judge who intended to remember the crime forever. She fell into line behind Rosalind and climbed.

* * *

They made the eastern shelf by late afternoon, moving fast and badly, Sefa's leg screaming, the storm building behind them like a tide coming in. Camp was a hollow beneath an overhang, barely deep enough for four, wind screaming past the lip of it. Ivo got a fire going in the lee of a boulder, small and mean and precious. Nobody talked much. The note lay between them like a fourth companion.

Wendell sat closest to the fire, knees drawn up, the sigil in his lap. He had been quiet all afternoon — quieter than usual, which for Wendell was saying something — and Sefa caught him looking at her the way he'd been looking at her for two days now. Attentive. Patient. Like a door waiting.

Finally, as the light died, he uncurled and came and sat beside her. Close. Children had no sense of tactical distance; it was one of the things about them that unnerved her most.

"You're sad," he said. His Common had gotten better, month by month, but it still had the cadence of the old tongue underneath it, syllables placed like stones in a wall.

"I'm tired," Sefa said. "There's a difference."

"You're sad," he said again, with the terrible certainty of children, "and you think nobody saw."

Sefa looked at the fire. "People see plenty. Doesn't fix anything."

"Won't you leave?" he asked. "Because of the paper. Rosalind was angry. Angry people send people away. I've seen it."

And there it was, laid out plain, the fear she'd been carrying since noon without letting herself name it. She'd offered to leave, in her head, three times already. Pack up, take the eastern fork alone, draw the Choir after her, buy them another corridor with the oldest currency a mercenary has. It would have been clean. It would have been arithmetic.

"Maybe I should," Sefa said, because lying to this boy had stopped working somewhere back on the pass, and that frightened her more than Vess did. "Maybe that's what people like me are for. Taking the hit so somebody cleaner walks away."

Wendell frowned at her. It wasn't pity on his face. It was confusion, genuine and total, as if she'd described a custom from a country he'd never heard of.

Then he did something he almost never did. He took the sigil off its cord and held it in both hands, and he spoke.

The words came slow, deliberate, each one shaped with care, the way you speak a language you dream in but rarely get to use out loud. The sound of it went through the hollow like water goes through a dry riverbed — the fire flared, the wind at the lip of the overhang dropped for a moment as if listening, and Sefa felt the hair rise on her arms. She didn't know the tongue. Nobody living was supposed to know the tongue. But she understood it anyway, the way you understand a lullaby in a language you don't speak: not the words, but the thing underneath them.

When he finished, he looked up at her and translated, carefully, the way you hand someone something breakable.

"It means *still mine.*" He touched his own chest, then hers, lightly, with two fingers. "In Vaelmoor, when someone does a bad thing and comes back anyway, you say it. So they know the door isn't closed. My mother said it to my father when he lost our winter stores gambling. He cried." Wendell shrugged, as if crying fathers were simply weather. "You did a bad thing. You came back anyway. You're still mine."

Sefa opened her mouth and nothing came out of it.

Twenty years on the passes. Twenty years of contracts and comrades and the particular loneliness of people who fight for money, where loyalty is a word you use and family is a word you avoid. She had built her whole life on the idea that belonging was something you purchased and maintained, quarter by quarter, favor by favor, and that when you failed the payment, the door closed, and you walked on alone. That was the trade. That was always the trade.

The boy had just told her the door didn't work that way.

"I sold your route," she said hoarsely. "You understand that? People might die because of what I did. Maybe you."

"Yes," Wendell said simply. "And you're still mine."

From across the fire, Rosalind was watching. Sefa met her eyes over the flames, and something passed between them that wasn't forgiveness yet — forgiveness was a longer road than one evening — but was maybe the decision to build it. Rosalind nodded, once, small. Then she looked away, back to the dark, back to the watch she was keeping, because Rosalind Ishikawa processed everything through work and always had.

Ivo cleared his throat. "For what it's worth," he said, "in the old surveys, *still mine* — the phrase — appears in land grants. Boundary stones. It's how Vaelmoor marked what belonged to a household. Not property. Kinship. Legally binding kinship, if you can believe it." He smiled crookedly into the fire. "The boy just made you family in front of a witness. I'll draw up the paperwork."

Sefa laughed — a rough, wet sound that surprised her — and put her arm around the boy's shoulders, and he let her, leaning in like it was the most natural thing in the world, like doors that stayed open were simply how doors worked where he came from.

She slept that night without the stone in her chest. Not gone — she'd carry the weight of the waystation until she died, wherever and whenever that was — but set down, shared, absorbed into something bigger than her guilt. That was what families did, she was beginning to understand. They didn't expel the wound. They carried it together so no one had to carry it whole.

* * *

She woke before dawn to Rosalind's hand on her shoulder, and knew before she fully surfaced that something was wrong.

Rosalind's face was gray in the starlight. She pointed east, down-slope, toward the black tree line below the shelf.

Fires. Dozens of them, strung along the valley floor in a patient, deliberate arc. Not advancing. Waiting. And rising faintly over the wind, threaded through the dark like smoke through a keyhole, came a sound Sefa had heard described by men who'd survived the Choir's purges and never slept right again.

Singing. Hundreds of voices, white-robed and hollow, singing the mountains awake.

"They didn't take the corridor," Rosalind said quietly. "Vess closed it. She wants us to know we're surrounded before she takes us." A pause. "We've got maybe two hours of dark. After that, we climb."

Sefa looked at the fires, then at the boy sleeping against Ivo's pack with the sigil curled in his fist, then at the sheer black face of Mount Cerenne rising above them into the storm.

"Climb," she said. "Right. Give me a minute to wake up my leg."

Above them, the storm began to sing too.
