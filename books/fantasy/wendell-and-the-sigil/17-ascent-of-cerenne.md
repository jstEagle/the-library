## 17. Ascent of Cerenne

The storm began to die at dawn, and that was the worst thing that could have happened.

Rosalind understood it before she understood why. For three days the blizzard had been a wall between them and the Choir — white chaos, screaming wind, a blindness that cut both ways. Now the clouds were tearing open above them like old cloth, and through every gap came hard blue light, and in that light they would be visible for miles.

"Move," she said, and her voice came out as a croak. She hadn't spoken above a shout-over-wind in days, and her throat had forgotten how.

They moved.

The slope of Cerenne rose ahead of them at an angle that mocked the word *path*. Ivo's map showed a pilgrim's stair somewhere on the western face, cut into the rock a century ago by people who had believed the mountain was holy. The map also showed the stair buried under a hundred years of ice, which Ivo had annotated in his cramped surveyor's hand: *probable*. Probable. Rosalind had killed men for less optimism than that.

Wendell climbed in front of her. She had tied a rope around his waist and hers — over his objections, which he'd made in Vaelmoorian, a string of syllables that sounded, from the set of his jaw, like a boy insisting he wasn't a child. He was nine. He was nine and half-starved and he had been walking for eleven days on boots two sizes too big, and he climbed like the mountain owed him something.

Maybe it did. It was his mountain, in whatever way a country erased from the world could still belong to anyone.

Behind them, Sefa brought up the rear with her crossbow slung across her back and her wounded side strapped tight. The ford had been six days ago. The wound had gone from angry red to a dull, worrying purple, and she had stopped joking about it yesterday, which frightened Rosalind more than any number of Choir robes on the horizon.

"How far?" Sefa called up.

"However far it is," Rosalind said, "it's closer than stopping."

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the morning was beautiful, and Rosalind hated it.

The sky cleared in patches, revealing the world below: Thornfell spread out to the south, the lowlands gray-green and distant, the passes behind them choked with snow that would not melt until spring — if spring ever came back. And on the southern shoulder of the mountain, small as ants, dark as ink spilled on paper, figures moving. Not many. A dozen, maybe fifteen. But they moved with purpose, in a line, following the trail Rosalind's party had beaten into the drifts.

"They're not hurrying," Ivo said. He had stopped calling it a hike or a climb; he called it *the ascent*, with a capital letter she could hear in his voice. He was breathing hard, and his hands shook — from cold, from the drink leaving his body, from both. He hadn't touched his flask in four days. She didn't know whether to be proud of him or afraid for him. "If they wanted us dead, they'd have archers picking us off right now."

"They want us alive," Rosalind said. "Vess wants the boy delivered whole."

"And you?"

"She wants me to choose." Rosalind checked Wendell's rope, tightened a knot that didn't need tightening. "She thinks everyone chooses eventually. Her whole theology is built on it."

"What did you build yours on?"

Rosalind looked at him. Ivo Renner, sixty years old, frost in his beard, eyes red-rimmed and clear for the first time since Brask. He asked questions like a man taking measurements.

"I don't have one," she said. "I have a contract."

"And when the contract runs out?"

Ahead of them, Wendell stopped. He had gone still in that way he had — the way of a boy listening to something no one else could hear. Then he raised one mittened hand and pointed up and to the left, where the ice sheet curved away from the rock face in a long pale sweep.

"There," he said. In trade-tongue. He only used their language for important things; everything else stayed in the dead tongue, as if he were rationing it. "The stair. Under. It sings."

"It sings," Ivo repeated, and scrambled forward with the eagerness of a man twenty years younger, chipping at the ice with his axe.

It took an hour. An hour with the Choir closing the distance below, an hour of Sefa standing watch with her crossbow braced against a boulder while her color went from bad to worse, an hour of Rosalind swinging Ivo's spare pick until her shoulders screamed and her palms bled inside her gloves. But beneath the ice, exactly where a drunk cartographer's memory said it would be, there was stone. Cut stone. Steps, descending into the mountain's flank — no, ascending, winding up along the face, sheltered by an overhang that had kept the worst of the storms off it for a hundred years.

Vaelmoor had built stairs up its holy mountain because Vaelmoor had expected its people to come home.

"Probable," Rosalind muttered.

Ivo laughed — a cracked, delighted sound. "I told you. I *told* you."

"You wrote 'probable.'"

"I'm a cartographer. We hedge." He stepped onto the first stair and put his palm flat against the rock, and something in his face broke open. "Gods below. It's real. All of it's real."

"Less singing, more climbing," Sefa said from below. Her voice was thin. Rosalind looked down and saw her leaning on the pick she'd planted in the snow, and saw the way her weight listed to the left, off the wound.

"Sefa."

"I'm coming. Don't fuss."

"We rest here first." Rosalind said it in the voice she'd used for fifteen years to end arguments among hired blades. Nobody argued. That was the trouble with authority: it worked fine right up until the moment it couldn't save anyone.

They rested. They ate the last of the smoked goat and half the last of the bread. Sefa slept forty minutes with her head on Ivo's pack and woke looking ten years older. Wendell sat at the edge of the sheltered stair with his sigil in his bare hands — he'd pulled the mitten off despite the cold, and the stone glowed faintly against his palm, warm-colored, hearth-colored — and sang under his breath. Not words Rosalind knew. Maybe not words at all. Just sound, pitched low, like a lullaby for something enormous.

"What's he doing?" she asked Ivo quietly.

"Telling the mountain we're coming." Ivo watched the boy with an expression Rosalind had never seen on him before — naked, unguarded longing. "Or asking permission. With Vaelmoorians it was always hard to tell the difference."

* * *

The stair climbed all afternoon.

It switchbacked up the western face, sheltered, ancient, and in places impossibly intact — railings of black iron still standing, though rusted to lace; lantern niches cut into the rock, empty, waiting. Twice they passed carved inscriptions, and each time Wendell read them aloud without being asked, his voice small and clear in the thin air, translating haltingly into trade-tongue.

*Here the road remembers you.*

*All who climb are already home.*

*Do not bring your fear past this point. Leave it for the next traveler.*

"That last one," Sefa panted, "is the most useless religious instruction I've ever heard."

"It's not religious," Ivo said. "It's practical. Fear makes people slip."

"Fear is the only reason I'm still alive."

"Fear is the reason you almost weren't, at the ford."

Sefa didn't answer. Rosalind, climbing above them, heard the silence stretch and chose not to look down at either of them. There were things being carried on this mountain that had nothing to do with packs.

At midafternoon the wind returned — not the blizzard, but the ordinary vicious wind of high altitude, knifing along the face and finding every gap in their clothing. Rosalind's fingers had stopped hurting an hour ago, which she knew was bad. Frostbitten flesh didn't hurt; it simply left, the way her god had left, quietly and without explanation. She flexed her hands against the pick haft and made herself count the fingers. Ten. Still reporting for duty.

Below, the dark line of the Choir had reached the base of the ice field.

She saw them stop, saw the tiny gestures of scouts reading the trail, saw the moment they found the stair — a ripple through the line, a gathering at its head. Someone in white stood apart from the others, and even at this distance, even as a speck, Rosalind knew the posture. Cantor Vess did not climb like a woman pursuing. She climbed like a woman attending.

"She's coming herself," Rosalind said.

Sefa spat over the edge, a gesture that cost her visibly. "Of course she is. Why send soldiers to do a zealot's job." She hauled herself up another step, gray-faced. "Ros. Listen. If it comes to it—"

"It won't come to it."

"If it comes to it, you take the boy and go, and I'll hold the stair. It's a good defensible position, narrow, single file, and I've got maybe thirty bolts and nothing left to lose but blood I'm not using anyway."

"No."

"Ros—"

"*No.*" The word cracked out of her louder than she meant, loud enough that Wendell turned around on the stair above. She lowered her voice. "Nobody holds anything. Nobody stays anywhere. We all go through, or none of us do. That's the arithmetic now."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — the same thing that had moved there twenty years ago, on a slave ship off the Corvane coast, when a young mercenary had cut her chains and refused, absolutely refused, to discuss payment.

"You always were terrible at math," Sefa said.

"I'm excellent at math. I just don't like the answers other people get."

* * *

Night caught them at the tree line's ghost — a few blackened, wind-twisted pines, dead a century, still standing guard at the edge of the snowfields. Above them the mountain went bare to the summit, and somewhere up there, according to Ivo's map, according to Wendell's certainty, the Threshold Door waited in the roots of Cerenne.

They made camp in the lee of the largest dead pine. No fire. Fire was visibility, and visibility was death. They huddled together instead, four bodies in a knot, sharing warmth the way the poor share everything: reluctantly, completely.

Wendell slept against Rosalind's side, his head under her chin, his fist closed around the sigil even in sleep. His breathing had a whistle in it. High-altitude cough, or the beginning of something worse. She pulled his hood up and thought, not for the first time, about the sheer absurdity of her situation. Fifteen years a sword-for-hire. Contracts in three countries. A reputation that had once gotten rooms free in Brask and drinks free in Corvane. And here she was, broke, frostbitten, hunted by zealots, on a mountain that officially did not exist, guarding a child who spoke a dead language to doors.

Her god had gone silent four years ago. She had prayed at a hundred shrines since, and received nothing, and had told herself she was done praying.

She caught herself doing it anyway, there in the dark under the dead pine. Not words. Just the old posture of it — the inward turning, the held breath, the listening. *Are you there? Are you anywhere? Is this what you wanted?*

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered in four years.

But Wendell stirred in his sleep and murmured something in Vaelmoorian, one soft phrase, and pressed closer against her, and Rosalind thought: *that's an answer of a kind. Just not from you. Not yet.*

She didn't know yet, lying there in the freezing dark, how close that guess would come.

* * *

They were woken before dawn by Ivo, shaking her shoulder with a hand that trembled worse than usual.

"Torches," he whispered. "On the stair. Below."

Rosalind was up before the sentence finished. She crawled to the edge of the pine's shelter and looked down the mountain, and there they were: a string of lights ascending the ancient steps, patient, unhurried, spaced like beads on a wire. A dozen. More behind them, unlit, invisible. And at the head of the line, a torch that burned a different color — whiter, colder — carried by a figure whose robe caught the light and gave nothing back.

Dawn was perhaps two hours off. The summit, by Ivo's estimate, was six hours of climbing. The math was simple and it was bad.

"We don't beat them," Sefa said flatly. She was already awake, already strapping her quiver. "Not with me at half speed. So we don't try to beat them. We find ground where twelve can't fight four."

"The stair narrows near the top," Ivo said. "There's a section on my map — a cut through a ridge, single file, with a drop on the outside. If we could hold that—"

"We're not holding anything," Rosalind said. "We discussed this."

"We discussed you being sentimental. This is tactics."

"It's the same discussion." She looked down at the torches again. Twelve lights, climbing steadily. Vess's white flame leading. "But you're right that we need ground. Wendell."

The boy was already awake, sitting up, the sigil glowing in his lap. In the darkness his face was very calm. It frightened her, that calm — a nine-year-old should not look like that. Like a soldier before a battle he'd already decided to win.

"The door knows we're coming," he said. "It's been listening since the ford." He said it simply, the way children say impossible things. "But it opens slow. Grandmother told me. It's been shut a hundred years, so it has to remember how. When we get there, I have to talk to it for a long time. Maybe hours." He looked up at her, and in the glow of the sigil his eyes were steady. "So you have to keep them off me for hours."

Hours. Against the Hollow Choir, with one wounded smuggler, one trembling scholar, and a mercenary whose god wouldn't speak to her.

"All right," Rosalind said.

"Ros—" Sefa started.

"I said all right." She began packing the camp with quick, brutal efficiency. "Then we climb like the damned. Ivo, lead. You know the route better than any of us. Sefa, middle, and if you fall behind I will tie you to the rope and drag you, and it will not be gentle. Wendell—" she crouched, took his small frozen face in her raw hands, "—you sing to the door the whole way. Every step. You tell it we're coming fast."

He nodded. Then, in Vaelmoorian, he said something soft and deliberate, watching her face to see if she understood.

"I know that one," she said. It was true; she'd learned it without meaning to, the way you learn a song stuck in your head. "'Still mine.'" She'd heard him say it to Sefa, forgiving her. "What about it?"

"Not you saying it. Me." He tucked the sigil into his coat. "When it gets bad. Remember I said it."

Before she could ask what that meant, he turned and started up the mountain.

* * *

They climbed like the damned.

The last stars went out. Dawn came up the eastern sky the color of a bruise healing, and under that light the summit of Cerenne showed itself for the first time — a black horn of rock against the brightening east, and near its peak, faint as a scar, a rectangular shadow that might have been architecture. Might have been a door.

Behind them, gaining, the torches.

Sefa fell twice on the ridge traverse and got up both times before Rosalind reached her. Ivo climbed with his eyes fixed on the rock and his lips moving — reciting elevations, contour lines, the litany of a life's obsession, prayer by another name. Wendell sang. Low, constant, unbroken, his voice cracking with altitude and cold and never stopping, and Rosalind began to feel it in the stone under her hands: a faint answering vibration, deep below, like a heart remembering how to beat.

An hour from the summit, the wind died all at once, the way a held breath releases.

And in the silence, from below, floating up the mountain clear as a bell, came a voice. A woman's voice, trained, resonant, carrying without effort across the frozen air.

*Rosalind Ishikawa.*

She froze on the stair. Below, the white torch was close now — close enough to see the figure bearing it, close enough to see that Cantor Vess climbed alone, her soldiers hanging back on the switchbacks below like a congregation giving their leader room.

*I know you can hear me,* the voice called, conversational, almost kind. *You always could. Even when He couldn't.*

Rosalind's hands closed on the iron railing, rust flaking under her grip.

*One hour, Ishikawa. One hour, and then I stop asking nicely.*

Above her, Wendell had stopped singing. He looked back down the mountain at the approaching torchlight, then up at the shadow near the summit, and Rosalind saw him square his small shoulders the way she had seen mercenaries square theirs a thousand times.

"Hold on," he said — to her, to the mountain, to the door. "We're almost home."

And far below, the white torch began to climb faster.
