## 19. Duty's Arithmetic

The door was smaller than it had any right to be.

Rosalind had imagined something monumental — a gate cut for giants, a slab of black stone taller than the pines, something that announced itself as the end of a country. Instead, what Ivo's map had led them to, three hundred feet beneath Mount Cerenne through a fissure that barely allowed Sefa sideways passage, was a door like any farmhouse door. Black stone, seamless, no handle, no hinges she could find. It stood alone in a chamber of raw granite, freestanding, absurd, patient.

Wendell touched it and the stone warmed under his palm like skin.

"It knows me," he said in trade-tongue, then said something longer in Vaelmoorian, soft, almost shy, the way you'd greet a house you'd been away from too long.

Rosalind stood back with the torch and did not let herself feel anything yet. Feeling came later. Feeling was for after the work was done. That was the first thing they'd taught her at fourteen, kneeling in the shrine of the Silent Brother with frostbitten fingers: *duty is arithmetic done before dawn, while the heart is still asleep.*

She'd always been good at arithmetic.

* * *

They made camp in the chamber because there was no choice. The storm above had followed them down in spirit if not in fact — Sefa's wound had gone bad again, hot to the touch, and the climb from the waystation had cost them a full day and most of their food. Ivo spread his map on the granite with hands that shook from cold and triumph both.

"Beneath Cerenne," he kept saying, half to himself. "Beneath Cerenne. Forty years I told them it was here. Forty years they laughed me out of every surveyor's hall from Brask to the coast." He looked up at Rosalind, eyes wet. "I'd have drawn this map even if no one ever used it."

"Someone's using it," Rosalind said.

Sefa lay propped against her pack, gray-faced, one hand pressed over the bandage at her side where the Choir's blade had gone in at the ford. She caught Rosalind looking and managed the ghost of her old grin. "Don't make that face. I've been stitched worse by worse."

"You've been stitched by me," Rosalind said. "There is no worse."

That got a real laugh out of her, which turned into a cough, which turned into something neither of them named.

Wendell sat cross-legged before the door, talking to it in his low, careful voice, the way he talked to frightened animals and to Sefa when the fever climbed. The sigil-stone hung at his throat. Torchlight moved on the black stone of the door and Rosalind could have sworn the light bent wrong there, pooled instead of sliding, as though the surface drank what fell on it.

She watched him and did the math.

It wasn't hard. That was the terrible thing. She'd expected the numbers to fight her, to blur, to offer some loophole she could hide inside. They didn't. They came clean and cold as well-water.

One soul must remain outside to hold the door open. Forever. Those were the terms, as far as any of them understood them — terms written in a language none of them read, on a door older than the kingdom it exited, explained only in fragments Wendell coaxed from the stone itself. One stays. The rest pass. The one who stays holds the way open so it cannot close behind them, cannot seal them into a country that has spent a century learning how to be erased.

Four of them. One door. Three can cross.

And of the four, who?

Not Wendell. He was the key; without him the door was just rock. Not Sefa — she couldn't walk ten paces unaided, and besides, Rosalind would sooner carve out her own tongue than leave a wounded comrade behind as a doorstop. Not Ivo. Ivo had spent forty years being laughed at for believing in this place; he deserved to stand in it.

Which left, with the neat inevitability of all true sums: her.

She ran it again anyway, because she was good at arithmetic and good arithmetic gets checked. Who else knew the passes? No one — but they wouldn't need passes once they were through. Who else could fight? Sefa, when she healed. Who else could lead? Ivo, in his way; he'd mapped a country that didn't exist, he could certainly walk through one that did. Who else had a god gone silent, contracts dried to nothing, a cabin in Thornfell that was less a home than a place she stored her gear between jobs?

Who else, when you laid all four lives on the table and weighed them honestly, had the least weight?

No one. That was the answer. No one else. Just her, and the math didn't flinch, and she hated it, and she was grateful for it, because hating it meant she still had a heart left to hate with, and gratitude meant the answer was true.

*Duty is arithmetic done before dawn.* Well. There was no dawn down here. There was only torchlight and black stone and a boy talking softly to a door, and she supposed that would have to do.

* * *

She waited until the others slept.

Sefa went first, fever dragging her under despite herself, muttering once — "*Ros. Whatever you're planning, don't*" — and then nothing, which Rosalind chose to interpret as fever-talk rather than prophecy. Ivo slept sitting up against the wall, map folded under his cheek like a child with a favorite book. His snoring filled the chamber, ridiculous and human, and she found she was memorizing it without meaning to.

Wendell didn't sleep. He sat by the door, and when she crossed the chamber and lowered herself beside him, he leaned into her side the way he'd started doing somewhere on the high pass, without asking, as though it had always been permitted.

"The door is tired," he said. "It's been holding itself shut for a hundred years. Nobody asked it anything."

"What does it say about tomorrow?"

"That it will open for my voice." He traced a shape on the floor with one finger — one of the sigils, she recognized the curve of it now, could nearly read the simplest ones. "And that someone has to stay."

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"I asked it if it could be me." His voice stayed flat, matter-of-fact, which was somehow worse than tears. "It said no. A key can't hold a door. A key goes through or everything stays locked forever." He looked up at her, and his eyes in the torchlight were very old and very young at once. "So it has to be one of you. And Sefa is hurt, and Ivo is old, and—"

"Wendell."

"—and you're the one who always—" 

"*Wendell.*"

He stopped. She took a breath and began the lie, and the worst part — the part she knew she'd carry across whatever threshold existed inside herself, if any such thing survived tonight — was how easily it came. Smooth. Competent. Kind, even.

"Listen to me carefully, because this matters." She turned to face him fully, the way she did when briefing a client, when laying out terms of contract. Her voice found its professional register, level and sure. "The rite isn't what the Choir told people. It isn't what the fragments say. I've been reading Ivo's notes — the surveyor's marginalia, the stuff he copied out of the Brask archives before they burned. The binding doesn't take a life, Wendell. It takes an oath. A standing watch. Like garrison duty."

His brow furrowed. "Forever?"

"Garrison duty is forever until relieved." She shrugged, easy, a soldier's shrug. "Doors open both ways. Once Vaelmoor is whole again — once your country remembers itself, once the gods find their voices — someone comes back and takes the watch. That's how oaths work. You don't die holding a door. You *stand* holding a door, and someday someone taps your shoulder and says *go sleep, I've got it.*"

"That's not what the stone said."

"The stone is a door, not a lawyer." She smiled, and hated how natural the smile felt. "Trust me. I've held a lot of watches. It's boring and it's cold and mostly you count the hours. But it ends. Everything ends. Even this winter — especially this winter. It's already breaking. You'll see it from the other side."

He studied her face for a long time. She let him. She'd been lied to by better liars than herself and had believed them, because belief is mostly wanting; and Wendell wanted, more than anything in the world, to go home, and second-most, she suspected, not to be left. She was offering him both. People believe offers like that.

"Promise me," he said finally. "Say it in your oath-words. The ones you say to your god."

Her stomach turned over, quiet and total, like a boat shipping water.

"My god doesn't answer anymore," she said. "You know that."

"Then say them anyway. To the silence. Sefa says you still do, sometimes, at night. She says you never stopped."

*Damn you, Sefa,* thought Rosalind, with genuine warmth, because it meant Sefa had been listening, all these weeks, to the small private failure Rosalind performed each night — kneeling, folding her hands, addressing a god who gave back nothing but weather. And damn her twice, because now the boy wanted the words, and the words were the one currency she had never counterfeited.

But that was the thing about competence. It worked even when it hurt. Especially when it hurt.

She knelt, right there on the granite, the way she'd knelt at fourteen in the shrine, the way she'd knelt every night since the god went quiet. She folded her hands. She spoke the opening formula of the Oath of the Standing Watch — the oldest one she knew, the garrison-oath, the door-watch, the one they swore on walls that mattered.

"I swear by the silent brother and the listening dark. I swear by the road behind and the road ahead. My post is my word, my word is my post. I hold until relieved, and I am not relieved until I am relieved, and no cold, no hunger, no loneliness shall move me from this stone."

The old words filled her mouth like river water, cold and familiar. Wendell watched, solemn, satisfied. Somewhere in the chamber, Ivo snored on.

"There," she said, rising, brushing off her knees. "Sworn. Now listen — the rite has to happen before the door opens, at first light. While the stone is still waking. After that it's too late and we all stay here and freeze, and I refuse to die somewhere Ivo can put on a map." She ruffled his hair, rougher than affection, gentler than she felt. "Sleep now. Both of us. Tomorrow we finish this."

He curled against her side, and within minutes his breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of a child who trusts the person beside him with his whole small life.

Rosalind sat awake in the torchlight and held him and did not sleep at all.

* * *

In the smallest hours she rose, careful as a thief in her own crime, and prepared.

She wrote two letters, because that was what competent people did. One to Sefa, short, brutal, forgiving her in advance for every curse she'd hurl at the closing door. One to Ivo, longer, with instructions: keep the map, correct it with whatever the true country showed him, publish it under his own name and no one else's, drink less, live long, be insufferable about being right. She tucked the letters into their packs where morning would find them, and she did not cry, because crying smears ink and blurs arithmetic, and she was good at arithmetic.

Then she went to the door.

Up close, the black stone breathed. She could feel it now — a slow pulse, faint as a sleeping heartbeat, warm where Wendell's hand had rested. She put her palm flat against it and spoke the first line of the binding rite, the one she'd pieced together from Ivo's stolen pages and Wendell's unwitting translations and the Choir liturgies she'd heard screamed across a frozen ford.

The stone listened.

She spoke the second line. The warmth spread under her hand, up her wrist, into the old scar tissue of her sword arm, and it did not feel like dying. It felt like a watch beginning. It felt like standing a wall at midnight, alone, competent, needed.

*One life,* she thought, doing the sum one final time, checking her work the way she always checked it, *against a child's whole future.* Against a country reborn. Against a god, maybe, finding its voice again on the far side of a door she was holding open. Against Sefa laughing again, and Ivo vindicated, and Wendell — Wendell going home.

It was the easiest math she'd ever done. That was the joke, she supposed. That was the punchline the Silent Brother had been keeping all these silent years: that the hardest thing she'd ever do would also be the simplest.

She drew breath for the third line —

— and behind her, in the dark of the chamber, a small voice began to speak.

Not trade-tongue. Not the halting, careful Vaelmoorian he used for the door.

Her name.

Her *true* name — the name given at her swearing, the name she had spoken to her god alone, the name that had gone unanswered for six years — rolling out of a child's mouth in the old tongue of a dead kingdom, clear as a bell rung in an empty shrine, and the black stone beneath her hand shuddered like a heart remembering how to beat.
