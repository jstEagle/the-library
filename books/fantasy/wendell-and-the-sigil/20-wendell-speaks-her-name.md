## 20. Wendell Speaks Her Name

The door is awake.

Wendell can feel it the way you feel a dog wake up under your hand — a warmth coming through the black stone, a listening. It has been asleep for a hundred years and it is hungry for words, and he has words, he has all of them now, every word Grandmother Ashen ever sang into his hair before the Choir came to the valley with their white robes and their fire.

*Vaelmoor veth,* he had said, and the mountain opened its throat.

But the door has asked its question. He heard it even though it did not use sound. It spoke into the marrow of him, the way the sigil does when it gets warm in his pocket: *One stays. One goes. The threshold must be held from the outside, forever.*

And Rosalind is already kneeling in the snow with her knife out.

Wendell knows what she is doing before she finishes doing it. He has watched her do sums her whole face, this whole time they have been together — counting arrows, counting coins, counting days of flour, counting how many men were behind them on the ridge and whether the number was too many. Her face does a certain thing when the answer comes out bad. Her mouth goes flat and her eyes go somewhere else, somewhere far away where the bad answer doesn't live.

Her face is doing that thing right now.

"No," Wendell says. His voice cracks in the middle of it, because he is nine years old and cold down to the place where cold stops mattering, but he says it again, louder, in both languages so there can be no mistake: "No. *Veth no.*"

She doesn't look up. She is cutting her palm, and the blood is very red against all that white, and she is saying words under her breath — binding words, old words, the rite Ivo told them about with his hands shaking around his cup of melted snow. *I remain. I hold. Let the door lean on me until the stone forgets it was ever a door.* She says them like she says everything, flat and steady, like carrying something heavy across a river.

She thinks he doesn't understand. Everyone always thinks he doesn't understand, because his words come out crooked and because he is small and because sometimes he goes quiet when the missing of Vaelmoor sits on his chest like a stone.

But he understands this better than anyone alive.

He knows what staying means. He has been the one left behind before. He remembers the village burning and his mother's hands pushing him into the root cellar and the door closing over his head, and he remembers waiting, and waiting, and the waiting turning into knowing, and the knowing turning into a hole shaped exactly like a person. He is not going to stand on the far side of a magic door in a country made of memory while Rosalind Ishikawa kneels in the snow forever holding it open with her stubborn dead-god blood.

He will not do the arithmetic. Sefa says he's too young for arithmetic anyway.

"Wendell." Rosalind's voice is gentle now, which is worse. Gentle is what she uses when she has already decided. "Listen to me. You cross. You find your home. That's the whole job. That was always the whole job."

"Your name," Wendell says.

Something flickers across her face. "What?"

"Your name. Your true one." He digs in his mind for it, the way you dig for a coin dropped between floorboards. He has known it for weeks without knowing he knew it — the way he knows things about people now, little warm truths that drift off them like heat off a stove. The door gave it to him, or the mountain did, or maybe he always carried it and only just now found the pocket. Names are like that in Vaelmoorian. They are not labels. They are doors.

Rosalind Ishikawa is not her true name. Rosalind Ishikawa is the coat she wears over it.

"You don't—" She stops. Her knife-hand stills. For the first time since the ford, since the cave, since the storm that screams like something drowning, she looks afraid. Not of the Choir. Not of the dark. Of him, standing small and snow-crusted with his hand out. "Wendell, whatever you're thinking—"

"*Sera-veth Ishka-lome,*" Wendell says.

The world goes quiet.

Not silent — the storm is still screaming above them, the wind still tearing at the mouth of the cavern — but underneath everything, in the deep place where the door listens, there is a silence like held breath. Like a room full of people hearing a bell they thought was broken.

Rosalind makes a small sound. He watches it hit her — the name, the true one, spoken in the tongue of a kingdom that was erased so thoroughly that even its gods went mute. She sways on her knees. The knife drops from her hand into the snow.

"That's not possible," she whispers. "Nobody knows that. My grandmother knew that. Nobody living knows that."

"I know it," Wendell says. "I know you."

"It's just a name—"

"It isn't." He takes a step toward her, boots crunching, and the sigil in his pocket burns through the cloth like a coal, and he lets it burn, because some things you have to let burn. "In Vaelmoor, names are doors. Grandmother said. You say someone's true name and you are *standing inside them*. You say it with the claiming-words and you are—" His throat closes. He pushes through. In Thornfell speech, the words are clumsy, translated, wearing borrowed coats: "You are family. Made. Not born-made. Chosen-made. The kind that can't be undone."

"Don't," Rosalind says, and her voice breaks on it, and Wendell sees it then, plain as a scar: she wants him to stop and she wants him to never stop, both at once, at war inside her like two soldiers who used to be friends. "Wendell. Listen. If you bind yourself to me, then when the door takes count, it counts us as one soul. One soul can't hold the door and walk through it. One soul has to choose."

"Yes," Wendell says.

"So one of us still stays."

"No." He shakes his head hard enough that snow flies off his hair. "You're doing the sum wrong. Everybody does the sum wrong. One plus one isn't two here. Grandmother said that too. *Veth-ka seren, veth-ka mora.* Two who claim each other are one, and one is never divided. The door doesn't want a person. The door wants a *house*. A house holds its own door. A house opens from inside itself."

Behind him, near the passage, he hears Sefa suck in a breath. He hears Ivo whisper something in surveyor's language — numbers, probably; Ivo hides in numbers the way Rosalind hides in duty and Sefa hides in jokes. But Wendell isn't looking at them. He is looking at Rosalind, on her knees in the snow with her cut hand bleeding, and he walks to her, and he puts his small cold hand on top of her big cold one.

"You stayed for me," he says. "In Brask, when the shop was burning. On the pass, when the men came. At the ford, when Sefa fell and you went back for her with the whole river trying to kill you. You keep staying. You think that's just what you're for. Duty." He says the word carefully, the way you handle something sharp. "But duty is a job. What you do is family. You just didn't have the word yet. So I brought you one."

"Wendell—"

"*Serath vael Ishka-lome,*" he says, and this time he speaks the whole of it, the claiming entire, every syllable Grandmother Ashen stitched into the songs: *I name you mine and myself yours; what is kept from you is kept from me; where you are held, I am held; where you go free, I go free; one hearth, one threshold, one soul wearing two skins.*

The sigil flares white in his pocket.

Light pours out between his fingers and up through the snow, and the black door — the great slab of night-stone that has stood shut beneath Mount Cerenne for a hundred years — begins to breathe. In and out. Slow. The carved lines across its surface, which Ivo swore were just weathering, fill with a glow like dawn coming up under the world, and the warmth of it rolls over them and melts the frost off Wendell's eyelashes, and somewhere very deep, deeper than the mountain, something enormous turns over in its sleep and begins, slowly, to wake.

Rosalind is crying.

She doesn't make any noise about it. She just kneels there and the tears come down her face and freeze in her lashes, and she stares at her own hands like they belong to somebody else. "You shouldn't have done that," she says finally, and her voice is wrecked. "You should have saved it. You should have crossed and been safe and forgotten—"

"I don't forget," Wendell says. "That's my whole problem. Ask anybody."

A sound comes out of her that might be a laugh if laughs could bleed. She reaches out and pulls him in, both arms, crushing, careful, the way she hugged him the first night in the cabin when he was half-frozen and snarling and she didn't know yet that she was going to love him. He puts his face against her shoulder. She smells like woodsmoke and iron and the road, and it is the best smell in any world, including the one behind the door.

"The door has to accept it," Ivo says behind them, hoarse. "It doesn't have to accept it. The old rites are clear, the balance is absolute, a soul is a soul is a—"

The door answers him.

It does not use words. It uses *weight*. The pressure of its attention settles over the four of them like snowfall, patient and vast, and Wendell feels it counting — not him, not Rosalind, but the shape between them, the rope of light that the claiming spun, running from his heart to hers and back again, bright and unbreakable. It counts once. It counts twice. And then, with a sound like a lock the size of a season turning over, it accepts.

*One soul,* the door agrees, in the marrow-place. *Two skins. The house may pass, carrying its own threshold within it.*

"There," Wendell says into Rosalind's shoulder, muffled, triumphant. "Told you. Sum done right."

She holds him tighter. When she speaks, her mouth is against his hair. "You impossible little— you rewrote a thousand-year-old covenant to get out of being left behind."

"Yes," Wendell says.

"And bound yourself to a broke mercenary with a dead god and a warrant in three provinces."

"Yes," Wendell says, grinning now, tears freezing on his own face. "Best deal I ever made. Only deal I ever made."

Sefa laughs — a wet, cracked laugh, because she is leaning on Ivo and leaving red spots in the snow from her bandaged side, but a real one. "Kid negotiates like a smuggler. I'm proud and terrified."

Then the light of the door changes.

Wendell feels it before he understands it — a wrongness threading through the warmth, a cold note struck inside the chord. The breathing of the stone stutters. Far above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock and the screaming storm, something else arrives at the mountain.

Chanting.

White-robed voices, many of them, singing the hollow song that unmakes things, and under it the crunch of many boots, and under that — faint, but getting less faint — the particular measured tread of a woman who once prayed to the same silent god Rosalind did, and stopped believing, and started believing in something crueler instead.

Cantor Vess has found the door.

Rosalind lifts her head. Wendell watches the grief drain out of her face and something older and harder pour in to fill the space, and her hand finds her sword, and she rises, pulling him up with her, keeping him behind her hip the way she always does, the way she will always do, because that is what family is — a door that stands between you and the weather.

"How long?" she asks Ivo.

"To charge the passage fully?" Ivo's eyes flick between the glowing seams in the stone and the tunnel mouth above. "Minutes. Perhaps ten. Perhaps fewer."

"Then we hold for ten minutes," Rosalind says.

"Wendell." She crouches to his eye level, and her grip on his shoulder is fierce, and her eyes are the clearest he has ever seen them. "Whatever happens up there — however loud it gets — you keep speaking to that door. You tell it to open. That's your job now. Mine's the other end of the tunnel."

"But—"

"Family," she says, and almost smiles. "Means nobody carries the heavy part alone. Go."

Above them, the chanting grows louder, and the first torchlight spills orange down the walls of the passage, and Wendell turns to the waking door, fills his lungs with freezing air, and begins to speak it open.
