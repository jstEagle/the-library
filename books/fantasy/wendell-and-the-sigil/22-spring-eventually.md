## 22. Spring, Eventually

The first thing Rosalind learned about Vaelmoor was that it smelled like bread.

She had expected—she didn't know what. Ruin, maybe. A country pickled in the century of its erasure, all black stone and held breath. Or nothing at all: a door that opened onto more mountain, onto snow, onto the long joke of a god who had finally stopped pretending.

Instead, when she came through the Threshold Door with Wendell's hand crushed in hers and Sefa's blood on her sleeve, the air on the far side was warm and it smelled like someone, somewhere down in the valley, was baking.

She stood on a shelf of granite with the door at her back—a slab of black stone set into the mountainside, already going quiet, its sigil-lines dimming from white to grey to nothing—and she breathed in, and her eyes stung, and she said, stupidly, to no one:

"It smells like bread."

"Of course it does," Ivo Renner said behind her. His voice was wrecked. He had his map case clutched to his chest like an infant and he was crying without any effort to hide it, tears cutting clean lines through the frost on his face. "It's a country. Countries smell like bread."

* * *

They came down out of the high valley over two days, because Wendell's legs were short and Sefa could not yet walk far and none of them were willing to hurry anymore. There was no one left to run from. The Choir had stayed on the other side—or most of them had. Rosalind did not let herself think about Cantor Vess standing in the snow beneath the door, listening to a dead god say a living name, except once a day, usually at dusk, when the memory rose in her like water and she let it rise and pass.

The land healed as they descended. That was the only word for it. The scars of the erasure—the places where Ivo's old royal surveys showed villages that had been scooped out of the world like seeds from a fruit—were filling in. Not instantly, not miraculously. But where there had been bare stone there was now thin grass, and where there had been grass there were now stone walls, tumbled but real, and by the second morning there were roofs beyond the walls, and smoke rising from chimneys, and a woman in a brown coat standing in a field looking at them with an expression Rosalind would spend the rest of her life trying to describe.

Hope, maybe. Or the thing hope becomes after a hundred years of being wrong.

Wendell saw the woman and made a sound Rosalind had never heard him make—not a word, not quite, something older than words, a note in the back of his throat like a struck bell—and he let go of her hand and ran.

She watched him go with her heart in her mouth, one hand already finding the knife at her hip out of pure habit, and then the woman caught him up and swung him around and pressed her face into his hair and said something in Vaelmoorian, a whole river of it, and Wendell answered, laughing, and Rosalind took her hand off the knife.

"They know each other," Sefa said beside her. Sefa was leaning on a walking stick Ivo had cut for her, her wound bound under three layers of borrowed wool, her face grey with pain and lit anyway. "Family, maybe."

"He never said he had family."

"He's six. What was he going to say? *I have a great-aunt somewhere past the end of the world?*" Sefa snorted, then winced, then grinned. "Kid kept more secrets than you do, Ros. And that's saying something, because I've known you twenty years and I still couldn't tell you where you're from."

"You've never asked."

"I'm asking now."

Rosalind looked at the valley. Terraced fields climbing the slopes, half-wild, half-tended. A stream running silver with meltwater. Roofs of slate and thatch, some caved in, some whole, and people coming out of doorways now, dozens of them, drawn by the bell-note of a child's homecoming, staring up the slope at three ragged strangers with weapons and wounds and a rolled-up map that had no edges.

"I'm from nowhere in particular," she said. "That's changing."

* * *

Vaelmoor was small.

That surprised her more than anything else. She had carried the name of it for months like a weight of iron—a lost kingdom, an erased nation, the anchor of gods—and it turned out to be a country you could walk across in four days if your knees were good. Nine valleys. One real town, called Serel, which had been a city once and wore its empty quarters like a man wears clothes outgrown. Perhaps eleven thousand people, all told, though nobody had counted in a century and nobody rushed to count now.

Eleven thousand people who remembered everything.

That was the part that undid her, in the early weeks. They had not forgotten. The erasure had taken Vaelmoor off the maps of Thornfell, out of the archives, out of the mouths of merchants—but it had not taken it out of itself. The people here had gone on. They had planted and harvested and buried their dead and sung their songs in a tongue the rest of the world had been made unable to hear, and when the long winter came down on Thornfell they had felt it too, a cold in the bones of the country, a silence pressing closer every year, and they had endured it the way people endure everything: badly, bravely, together.

"They knew," Ivo said, the third night, sitting in a tavern in Serel that had no name because everyone in it knew whose tavern it was. He had a mug of something dark and local in front of him and he was drinking it slowly, savoring it, a man who had spent fifteen years being told he was mad and was now being bought drinks by grateful strangers. "On this side of the door, they knew. It was us—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward Thornfell, toward the world "—who were made to forget. The erasure wasn't a wall around Vaelmoor. It was a blindfold on everyone else."

"And the gods?"

Ivo turned his mug slowly on the table. Around them the tavern hummed—Vaelmoorian mostly, that river-sound language that Rosalind was beginning, syllable by syllable, to understand, because Wendell taught her ten words every night before sleep and corrected her pronunciation with the merciless patience of a six-year-old.

"The archivist in Brask was right about one thing," Ivo said. "A land needs an echo. You erase a country from the memory of the world, and the world stops answering it. The gods weren't silenced, Ros. They were made deaf. Prayers went out and hit nothing." He looked up. "Your god didn't abandon you. Your god has been shouting into a closed door for a hundred years, same as these people."

Rosalind thought about the shrine at the crossroads outside Brask. The cold stone. The years of speaking into silence and calling it faith.

"Well," she said. "Then we're even. I shouted back."

* * *

Sefa's wound turned bad in the second week, and good again in the third, thanks to a Vaelmoorian healer named Maren who used methods involving hot stones and bitter tea and language that would have gotten her excommunicated in three countries Rosalind could name. By the time Sefa could walk without the stick, she had also acquired a job (hauling slate for the roof-menders), a circle of friends (the roof-menders), and a suitor (one of the roof-menders, a broad quiet woman named Talla who brought Sefa apples with the regularity of a siege engine).

"You could stay," Rosalind said to her one evening, watching Talla across the yard pretend very badly not to be watching Sefa. "There's work here. Good work. Nobody asks questions about your past."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment.

"You're staying, then."

Rosalind hadn't decided. She'd told herself she hadn't decided. She was aware, distantly, that she had been unconsciously assessing south-facing slopes for drainage since the second week, and that she had asked Maren where seed was kept over the winter, and that she had not once, not for a single hour, thought about the road back east.

"There's a boy here who speaks a dead language and calls me by a name I don't understand," Rosalind said. "Seems rude to leave before he explains himself."

"That's a yes."

"That's a yes."

Sefa nodded slowly, and then, because she was Sefa, she ruined it: "Twenty years I've owed you my life, and the debt turns out to be paid in apples and roof tiles. You're a terrible creditor, Ros Ishikawa."

"Get back to work."

* * *

The name Wendell called her, it turned out, was not a name at all. Not the way she'd understood names.

Maren explained it, in her blunt way, while stitching a tear in Rosalind's coat that Rosalind had been meaning to fix herself for a month. "In the old tongue, kin is not something you're born. It's something spoken. A person can be claimed—made family by words, witnessed, binding. It's rare. It's supposed to be rare." She bit the thread. "A child claiming an adult is rarer still. It takes—" she searched for the word, gave up, used the Vaelmoorian one, "*sennaveth*. Standing. The speaker has to have standing in the family line. Blood or bond."

"Wendell has neither. He's an orphan. He told me."

Maren raised her eyebrows. "Did he? Ask him what his mother's line was. Ask him why the elders stood up when he walked into the hall at Serel. Ask him why the door knew his voice." She shouldered her basket. "That child isn't just the last echo of Vaelmoor, mercenary. As near as anyone can reckon, he's the heir of it. The old royal line ended a hundred years ago—or was believed to. Lines have a way of continuing quietly when kingdoms are erased." She paused at the gate. "He claimed you with the standing of a king. Whatever you were to each other before, you are kin now, in front of gods and country. There's no unringing that bell."

Rosalind sat with her half-mended coat for a long time after Maren left.

She thought about the door. About the arithmetic—one soul to hold it open, forever, and how she had begun the binding rite with steady hands and smooth lies, and how Wendell had looked at her, six years old and frostbitten and utterly certain, and refused. How he had spoken her name—not her name, her *name*, the one under her name, the one she hadn't known she had—and how the words had gone through her like heat through frozen iron, and how the door had considered, and accepted, a family as one soul.

He had rewritten the sacrifice. He had done it without ceremony, without hesitation, the way children do the impossible: because nobody had told them yet that it couldn't be done.

And now, apparently, she was kin to a king.

She laughed. It startled her, the sound of it, rusty in her own chest. Then she finished mending the coat.

* * *

Spring came to Vaelmoor first, and then—it was hard to believe, at first, and people traveled to the passes to see it—to Thornfell.

Word came up the valley with the first traders through the reopened door: the snow breaking on the southern slopes, rivers rising, the unnatural winter that had gripped the mountains for a generation finally losing its hold. In Brask, Ivo reported gleefully, they were calling it a miracle. In Brask, a blind archivist had stood in his rebuilt shop doorway and wept at the smell of thawing earth. In Brask, the Hollow Choir—

Rosalind tensed at that. But Ivo shook his head.

"Broke apart," he said. "Like ice. Some of them are saying their god spoke and they misheard it. Some are just going home. There's talk of a few holding together under new leadership, doing penance, whatever zealots do when the wind goes out of them." He shrugged. "Vess is gone. Nobody knows where. Some say she walked into the high snow. Some say she's looking for another door." He met Rosalind's eyes. "If she comes, this side will know how to greet her. Eleven thousand people remember what was taken from them, Ros. Zealots need a forgetting to work with. There's none to be found here."

Rosalind nodded and let it go. She was learning to let things go. It was the hardest discipline she had ever practiced, harder than swordwork, harder than the years of prayer into silence, and she practiced it daily the way she'd once drilled forms: letting go of the knife at small sounds, letting go of the escape routes she mapped in every room, letting go of the woman she had been, who was defined by duty to a duty that had abandoned her.

Some nights the letting go failed. Some nights she woke in the dark of the little house they'd been given—stone walls, slate roof, a hearth that drew well—with her hand already on the knife and Vess's white robes swimming in her vision, and she would lie awake listening to the house breathe: Wendell in the next room, the fire settling, wind in the eaves. And the dark, when she listened to it, did not answer her the way it had answered Wendell in the cave. But it didn't threaten her either. It was just dark. Just night. Just a country sleeping around her, alive, unafraid.

She was getting better at it.

* * *

She planted the garden on a morning in late spring, with the meltwater loud in the stream and the terraces above the house turning green.

Maren had given her seed—barley, beans, onions, and a packet of something the Vaelmoorians called *selith*, a blue-flowered herb that smelled faintly of anise and featured in every soup Rosalind had eaten since crossing. "Plant it by the wall," Maren said. "It likes to lean on something strong."

The soil was good. That surprised her; mountain soil usually wasn't. But Vaelmoor had been gardened for a thousand years before its erasure, and soil remembers longer than maps do. She turned it with a spade that had been waiting in the shed for a hundred years and needed only a whetstone's attention, and she worked until her shoulders burned, and it was the best ache she had ever owned.

Wendell came out mid-morning, hair wild, still in his nightshirt, and stood at the edge of the turned earth with his hands on his hips in an attitude of command so perfectly regal that Rosalind understood, suddenly and completely, everything Maren had said about his line.

"You're doing it wrong," he said, in Thornic—he spoke Thornic now almost as easily as Vaelmoorian, switching between them mid-sentence the way other children switched between whispering and shouting.

"I'm doing it exactly how Maren said."

"Maren plants rows. We plant in circles." He crouched at the corner of the plot and began scratching a shape in the dirt with one finger: concentric rings, and lines crossing them, and Rosalind recognized it with a jolt—the sigil, the stone he'd carried out of the snow, the pattern that opened doors. "Grandmother says gardens used to be planted like this. So the growing things would know they were wanted."

"Does it work?"

Wendell looked up at her with the expression of a person gently informing an adult of the obvious. "Rosalind. Everything here works."

So they planted in circles. He directed; she dug. He named each seed aloud in the old tongue as it went into the ground—*selith, selith, bean, bean, grandmother's barley*—and if the words did anything, if they woke anything in the soil, Rosalind couldn't see it. But the blue flowers bloomed earlier than Maren promised they would, leaning against the wall, and she chose not to be surprised.

* * *

On midsummer's eve, the whole valley climbed to the high meadow above Serel, and there were fires, and singing, and Wendell fell asleep against her shoulder halfway through the third song with his fingers still curled in her sleeve.

Rosalind sat with him and watched the fires and did the accounting she was always good at, the old mercenary's ledger, except the entries had changed.

One god: silent, possibly listening, status unknown. She found she could live with that. Faith, she was beginning to think, had never really been about answers.

One country: small, scarred, alive, smelling of bread.

One map with no edges, framed now and hung in the archive at Serel, where scholars came from both sides of the door to argue with it, and Ivo Renner stood beside it every day correcting them, happier than any drunk surveyor had ever been.

One smuggler, debt discharged, staying anyway, with apples.

One child: asleep, breathing slow, heavy and warm against her shoulder, weighing approximately nothing and everything.

No contracts. No oaths. No duty but this—the actual weight of him, the actual warmth, the garden by the wall, the seasons arriving honestly one after another like promises kept.

Below the meadow, the lights of Serel came on one by one in the blue dusk, and somewhere a bell rang the hour, and the song started up again, and Rosalind Ishikawa, kin of Vaelmoor by the word of a king, closed her eyes and belonged.
