## 1. The Charm Seller

There are three rules to surviving in Cinderhollow, and I wrote all of them myself, which is the only kind of law I trust anymore.

One: never be interesting. Two: never be the most talented person in any room, or the second-most, or the third. Three: when someone knocks on your door after dark, you were never home, and if they saw you in the market that morning, they are mistaken, and if they swear it on their mother's grave, then their mother was mistaken too.

I had lived by these rules for ten years. Ten years of selling rain-charms and luck-knots and little braided blessings that were, legally speaking, nothing but string. The Graycloaks could arrest a woman for magic, but no magistrate alive could hang her for knotwork. It was a fine line and I walked it the way a drunk walks a rooftop—carefully, every day, with a private appreciation for how far the fall went down.

My stall sat at the crooked end of Tallow Row, wedged between a candle-dipper who hated me on principle and a fishmonger who hated me on the principle that I once outbid him for a barrel of salt. The awning leaked. The table wobbled. Everything about my little enterprise was designed to look like exactly what it was: a woman selling string to fools, nothing worth a second glance.

I had gotten very good at being nothing worth a second glance. It is a craft, like any other. You learn to keep your sleeves long even in summer. You learn to laugh one note lower than your real laugh. You learn that the scarred wrists stay under the table, always under the table, because brands tell stories and stories get people remembered, and remembered people in Cinderhollow got visits from men in gray wool with kind voices and ledgers full of other people's names.

That morning the fog off the slag-heaps had come down thick enough to chew, and business was slow, which suited me. Slow meant invisible. I sat behind my wobbling table re-braiding a cord that didn't need re-braiding, and I let the town happen around me—the cart wheels, the gull-cries off the ash canal, the candle-dipper telling anyone who'd listen that charm-sellers were why his wife left him, which was news to both of us.

She came just past midmorning, when the fog was thinning into that gray dishwater light Cinderhollow does instead of sunshine.

I knew before she reached the stall that she was dying. Not from any gift—I'd buried mine so deep it barely whimpered—but from the way she walked. Dying people walk like they're carrying something breakable, and the something is themselves. She was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, dressed in a widow's black gone green at the seams, and she held herself very straight, the way people do when they've decided dignity is the last thing they're going to spend.

"Which one's for luck?" she asked.

Her voice was rough as a whetstone. Lungs, probably. Or liver. In Cinderhollow it was usually lungs—the smelter smoke settled in the low streets like an unwanted tenant and never paid rent.

"Depends what you need luck *for*," I said, which was my standard opening, and honest, in its way. "Luck at cards, take the red cord. Luck in love, the blue, though I'll want it back after, since nobody's ever reported success."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile. "Neither."

"Then we're in luck," I said, "because those are the two things I'm worst at anyway." I spread out the tray. Knots of every color, each one tied in patterns I'd learned from a woman in the fen country whose name I also wasn't supposed to remember. "Tell me what you need."

She looked at the tray for a long time. Longer than a customer looks at string.

"I need my daughter to make it through winter," she said finally. "She's seven. Her father's dead, my sister's dead, and I'm—" she said it plainly, without drama, the way you'd report the weather— "I'm not going to see spring. I can feel it. I want to leave her something that works."

And there it was. That was the moment I should have said: *madam, these are knots, pretty ones, and if they work it's because believing works, and there's no shame in that.* That was the safe answer. That was the answer of a woman who had spent a decade shrinking her life down small enough to fit through the eye of the law.

Instead I heard myself say, "Come round the side of the stall."

* * *

Rule four, which I never wrote down because writing it down would have broken rule one: never, ever do this.

But she came round the side, and I took her hands—her fingers were cold, cold as a fishmonger's slab—and I looked at her properly. Up close she was worse than she'd seemed. There was a translucence to her skin, a bluish shadow under the eyes that no amount of straight-backed dignity could dress up. Spring was going to come to Cinderhollow without her, and everyone in that street except her daughter would forget her inside a year.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Maren."

"Maren. I'm going to tie you a knot, and I'm going to charge you three coppers for it, because free charms draw questions and questions draw Graycloaks, and neither of us wants that. And while I tie it, you're going to think about your daughter. Not worrying about her—that's different. Thinking about her. Her name, her face, the sound she makes when she laughs. Can you do that?"

"Her name's Issa," Maren said. "She laughs through her nose. Like a little piglet. My husband used to say—"

"Good," I said. "Keep that. Hold onto that."

And I began to braid.

Here is what I will tell you about the knotwork, since we're alone and the Graycloaks aren't listening: the knots were never just knots. A true charm is a shape for a thing to pour into. The braid is the cup; the feeling is the wine. Any hedge-witch in the fens could teach you the shapes. What made mine different—what made them *mine*, what made them dangerous—was that sometimes, when the customer brought enough feeling to the table, something in me answered. Something old and borrowed and long-supposed-dead leaned up against the walls I'd built around it and said, *may I?*

For ten years I had kept that something on a starvation diet. Little sips. Enough warmth in a luck-knot to ease a gambler's hands, enough stillness in a sailor's cord to quiet a storm-jitter. Never more. Never anything a Graycloak's instruments could taste on the air.

Maren thought about her daughter laughing through her nose, and her love came off her like heat off a forge stone, and my something woke up.

It started in my fingertips. Warmth first—which should have warned me, warmth being the last thing I ever allowed myself—and then the pull, that familiar tidal drag behind the breastbone, the sky's attention turning toward me like a vast lazy eye. Ten years ago I had been the woman the storms answered. Ten years ago I had called lightning down on an army and saved a city and been exiled for it, and the price of my survival had been paid in coin I don't talk about, and the first rule of my new life was *never again.*

The fog over Tallow Row shivered.

Just shivered—a tremor in the gray, like a horse twitching off a fly. Nobody else noticed. The candle-dipper kept complaining. The fishmonger kept shouting prices. But I felt the pressure drop by a hair, felt the moisture in the air lean toward my open palms like iron filings toward a lodestone, and somewhere above the fog, clouds that shouldn't have existed over Cinderhollow in high summer began, very quietly, to consider forming.

*No,* I told it. *No. Sit down.*

I closed my fists. I breathed the way Anselm's order used to teach—four counts in, seven counts out, though I hadn't seen Anselm in ten years and hadn't been inside a chapel since before the Edict, and thinking of that now, of all times—

The warmth in my fingertips wanted out. It wanted to go into the knot, into Maren, into the seven-year-old with the piglet laugh, wanted to wrap that child in weather's own favor clear through till thaw. It would have worked. That was the horror of it. It would have worked beautifully, and some Graycloak's resonance-glass three streets over would have sung like a struck bell, and by nightfall there'd be gray wool on my doorstep asking after the charm-seller of Tallow Row.

So I did what I'd done ten thousand times. I starved it. I gave the knot a sip and no more—a thread of warmth, a whisper of favor, the magical equivalent of a copper where the girl was owed a fortune—and I tied off the ends and pressed it into Maren's cold hands.

"Three coppers," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

* * *

She counted them out one at a time, solemn as a priestess at offering, and I took them because taking payment was part of the disguise, the whole architecture of my invisibility resting on the fiction that I was a merchant and nothing else.

"It will work?" she asked. "You'll not lie to me. Everyone lies to dying women, they think it's kindness. Tell me true."

I looked at her. I thought about lying, and about how the truth was complicated—*it will work a little, and I'm sorry it can't work more, and the reason it can't work more is that I sold the biggest part of myself away ten years ago to a court beneath a mountain, and the rest I keep locked in a box because the world decided my kind were criminals, and I agreed, in a way, because the last time I was fully myself, ten thousand people lived and I didn't.*

"True," I said. "It will work. Keep it near her. Don't untie it—if it frays, bring it back and I'll mend it free."

"And if I don't come back," Maren said, "if winter takes me first—you'll look after her? Not charity. I've seen how this town does charity. Just... look."

The fog was lifting. Somewhere beyond it, in the clean bright air where no one could see, I could feel the cloud I'd almost made dissolving back into nothing, sulking, patient. Storms are patient. That's the thing nobody tells you. They wait for you the way debts wait.

"I'll look," I said.

She nodded once, satisfied, and straightened her green-seamed black, and walked back down Tallow Row the way she'd come, carrying her breakable self, upright as a mast. I watched her go until the fog took her, and then I watched the spot where she'd been a while longer, because there was no one left alive whose watching mattered, and a body gets in the habit of spending its tenderness where it can't be taxed.

* * *

I closed the stall early.

This was itself a small transgression—merchants in Cinderhollow kept hours the way monks keep prayers, and the candle-dipper remarked loudly to the fishmonger that some people had no backbone for trade—but I'd earned the right to one early close per decade, and today was the anniversary whether I marked it aloud or not.

Ten years. Ten years tonight since Harrowgate.

I climbed to my room above the stall—it was one room, and it contained a bed, a stove, a chair, and forty-one years of a life folded down into the size of a shoebox—and I poured the cheap barley spirit I kept for medicinal purposes and other lies, and I drank to the city I'd saved and the name I'd lost.

Because that was the other price. Nobody tells you exile has stages. First they take your standing. Then they take your property. Then, if the crime is magic, the Edict takes your *name*—legally erases it, strikes it from every register, so that officially speaking the storm-caller who broke the siege of Harrowgate never existed, and the woman selling string in Cinderhollow is a legal ghost wearing a borrowed name. Jonah Thornbury. It fit me like a coat cut for someone else. I'd worn it so long I'd stopped noticing the shoulders rode wrong.

Most nights, that was the whole ceremony. Drink, memory, sleep. But that night, sitting in my one room with the fog pressing at the shutters, I found my thoughts wouldn't settle. They kept circling back to Maren's hands around the luck-knot, and the way my power had risen in me like a dog hearing its name after years of being called something else.

*May I?* it had said. After ten years of *no*, it had still asked permission. That was either touching or terrible, and I couldn't decide which, and the not-deciding sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing I never said out loud, the thing I barely admitted in the privacy of my own skull: I missed it. Gods help me, I missed it. Not the glory—glory was a young woman's mistake, and I'd made it thoroughly. I missed the *size*. I missed being a creature to whom the sky paid attention. I missed mattering at a scale larger than one wobbling table on Tallow Row, and I hated myself for missing it, because mattering at scale was exactly what had cost ten thousand lives' worth of gratitude and gotten me branded and erased, and a woman who misses the thing that ruined her is a woman who hasn't learned anything.

A life shrunk small enough to be safe. That was what I had. That was what I'd built, plank by plank, rule by rule. And it fit, mostly. It fit the way a coffin fits.

I was still sitting with that cheerful thought when I heard the bells.

Cinderhollow had two sets of bells. The foundry bell rang the shifts, and everyone ignored it. The shrine bell on Bellows Hill rang for deaths and festivals, and everyone ignored that too, because the shrines had been empty for a generation—since the gods went silent, since the Edict, since prayer became a folk custom and nothing more. The bell rang out of habit, the way my grandmother had set a place for my grandfather ten years after he drowned.

Except this ringing wasn't habit. This was fast, and getting faster, and under it—thin, at first, easy to mistake for wind in the flue—I could hear shouting. A lot of shouting. Coming from the direction of Tanner's Lane, where the cracked old Vessia-shrine stood leaning against the hillside like a drunk against a wall.

I went to the shutter and put my eye to the gap, and what I saw made the swallowed stone in my chest turn over.

Frost.

In high summer, frost was climbing the shutters across the street—climbing them in *patterns*, feathered and deliberate, spreading outward from Tanner's Lane like ink through wet cloth, and the air coming through the gap in the shutters carried a smell I knew better than my own name. Cold and green and ancient. The smell of a goddess waking up.

Every hair on my arms stood. The thing I'd starved for ten years turned over in its sleep and *purred*.

Someone had prayed at the cracked shrine. Someone had prayed, and for the first time in a generation, something had answered—and whatever had answered, it wore the signature of a power I had thought dead, a power older than the Spire, older than the ledger, older than every debt I owed.

And every Graycloak in Cinderhollow had heard the bell too.
