## 2. First Answered Prayer

The trouble with selling rain-charms in a town where rain is free is that you have to sell something else along with it. Hope, mostly. I'd gotten good at that, the way a pickpocket gets good at looking harmless.

Cinderhollow's market woke slow that morning, the way it always did—bakers first, then the fishwives arguing with the river about what it owed them, then everyone else shuffling out to pretend the day was worth having. I set up my little table by the tanner's wall, where the smell covered whatever else needed covering, and I laid out my wares: knots of braided cord, each one worked with a pattern that meant nothing to anyone who didn't know better.

Which was everyone. That was the point.

"Charm for your cough?" I said to old Marta as she passed, and she waved me off the way she had every morning for six years, and I offered anyway because that was the ritual, and rituals were how you stayed human in a town built on forgetting them.

Here is what you need to understand about Cinderhollow: magic is outlawed here, but not the way it's outlawed elsewhere. Elsewhere they burn you for it. Here they erase you for it. The Graycloaks don't take your body; they take your name from every ledger, every memory, every mouth that ever said it. You become a stranger even to yourself. There are people in this town who've been erased twice and walk around smiling at their own children like they're meeting someone new.

I had learned to be small. Ten years of practice. Small enough that the Graycloaks' ledgers skimmed past me like water over a smooth stone. Jonah Thornbury, storm-caller, breaker of sieges, exiled traitor—she didn't exist anymore, legally speaking. What remained was a woman called Jo who sold cord and hope and never made eye contact with anyone wearing gray.

It was a good life, if you liked being a ghost.

* * *

The child came at midday, when the market was at its thickest and my table was down to its last three luck-knots.

She couldn't have been more than seven or eight, all elbows and dirty knees, hair the color of wet straw shoved behind her ears like she'd done it herself and hadn't cared whether it worked. She wasn't shopping. She was walking with the particular determination of a child on a mission, threading between legs and carts with the ease of someone who'd been doing it since she could crawl.

I noticed her the way you notice weather coming—some animal part of me registering a change before my mind caught up.

She stopped at the base of the old shrine wall.

Every town has one. A cracked bit of stonework left over from before, when the old gods still had names people could say out loud. Cinderhollow's was a chunk of granite wedged between a chandler's shop and a drainage ditch, carved once with the face of Vessia—the winter goddess, the quiet one, the one who kept the deep snows and the deeper silences. Her features had worn down to suggestions. One eye remained, half-lidded, patient as stone has to be.

Nobody prayed there anymore. Praying to the old gods was legal, technically—it was *answering* that got you erased—but nobody did it anyway, because praying to something that never answers is just talking to a rock, and Cinderhollow had enough shame in it already without adding that.

The girl knelt in the dirt. She pressed both palms flat against the crack in the shrine's face, the fissure that ran from Vessia's remaining eye down through her ruined mouth, and she leaned in close, the way children do when they believe the world will lean back.

And she prayed.

I couldn't hear the words. That should have been the end of it—a child muttering at a rock, nothing new under the sun. But I felt it. Felt it in my teeth, in the scar tissue on my wrists, in the place behind my sternum where my magic had gone to sleep ten years ago and I'd spent a decade convincing it to stay that way.

Something turned over.

You have to understand—I had felt power move before. I had *made* power move. I knew its signatures the way a mother knows footsteps on stairs. And what stirred in that moment had a signature I would have known blindfolded, deafened, dead: the cold, clean, patient pull of Vessia. The goddess of the long white silence. The god whose temples I'd stripped copper from as a girl because her altars were always the richest and her priests always forgave it.

Dead gods don't answer prayers. That was the whole arrangement. The old ones had gone silent a century ago, and the world had filled up their absence with laws and ledgers and Graycloaks, and everyone agreed not to notice the shape of the hole.

The crack in the shrine began to frost.

Not metaphorically. Frost—actual frost, white and feathering, blooming across the granite like ink dropped in water, spreading outward from the girl's small hands in fractals too perfect to be natural. The temperature dropped so fast my breath came out visible in high summer. The dust on the ground glittered. Somewhere behind me, a fishwife stopped mid-sentence.

The frost reached the edge of the shrine and kept going.

Across the chandler's doorstep. Up the wheel of an abandoned cart. Along the gutter stones, in branching veins of white, racing down the street in both directions like the street itself had remembered something it was supposed to be. The air went sharp and sweet the way it does before snowfall, except it was midsummer and the sky was the flat blue of a hammered plate, and there was not a cloud anywhere to account for what was happening.

People started screaming. People always start screaming. It's the most reliable thing about us.

But I didn't scream. I stood there with three luck-knots in my hand and watched frost bloom across a street in the shape of a goddess's attention, and I thought, with the strange clarity that comes in the moment before everything changes:

*I know you.*

Because I did. Not Vessia herself—I'd never met her, no one had, not in living memory. But I knew the *hand*. When I was young and stupid and full of storm, I'd pulled lightning down through a winter sky over Harrowgate, and the cold had come up to meet me, eager, familiar, like the sky itself leaning in to help. I'd always assumed that was just what storms were. Later, alone in exile, I'd wondered if it was something else. If the old gods hadn't been dead at all, only quiet, and if some part of them had recognized me that night and lent me more than weather.

Now something was answering a child's prayer in a market square, and it moved like that night had moved, and every instinct I had spent ten years sharpening was telling me to run.

The girl lifted her hands from the stone. She looked at them—at the frost melting off her palms—and she laughed. A bright, unguarded sound, the kind of laugh that doesn't know yet what it costs to be special in Cinderhollow.

Then she looked directly at me.

Across forty feet of freezing marketplace, past the running and the shouting and the fishwife clutching her basket like a shield, the child found me as if I were the only fixed point in a spinning room. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they held an expression no eight-year-old should be able to make: recognition. Certainty. The look of someone who has been told a name and has just found its owner.

My wrists burned under their wrappings. Old scars, old brands, the marks they gave you at Harrowgate before the exile—not punishment, exactly. *Registration.* So they could find you again if they needed you. So the magic in you would always have a return address.

The frost was retreating now, pulling back down the street the way tide pulls back from sand, leaving everything wet and gleaming and wrong. The girl stood, brushed off her knees, and started toward me through the crowd with the unhurried confidence of someone who knows exactly where she's going.

I did the smart thing. I did the thing that had kept me alive and small and safe for ten years.

I packed up my table.

Hands shaking, cords tangling, luck-knots spilling into my bag without their prices collected—I broke down my stall in record time while the whole market buzzed around me like a kicked hive. Someone was shouting for the watch. Someone else was shouting that the watch wouldn't come, the Graycloaks would, and weren't those the same thing lately? A man knelt at the shrine touching the last of the melting frost and wiping his fingers on his shirt like he could keep it.

The girl reached my table just as I swung my bag over my shoulder.

"You're her," she said.

Her voice was ordinary. That was the worst part. She sounded like any child asking for sweets, and the words she was saying were the words that had gotten people erased for less.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, "but you need to go home."

"Vessia remembers you." She tilted her head, studying me with those pale eyes. "She says the sky used to answer when you called. She says—" the girl frowned, listening to something I couldn't hear, "—she says you *owe* her a conversation."

"I owe a lot of people conversations," I said. "None of them are gods."

The girl smiled then, quick and gap-toothed, and for just a moment it wasn't a child's smile at all. Something looked out through her face—something vast and cold and patient, something that had waited a very long time inside the silence of a cracked shrine—and it regarded me with what I can only describe as *interest.*

Then it was gone, and she was just a grubby kid again, swaying slightly on her feet like she'd stood up too fast.

"You should eat something," I heard myself say, because apparently my survival instincts had taken the day off. I dug in my bag, found a heel of bread from breakfast, and handed it to her. "And you should never do that again. Whatever you did. Never, never again. Do you understand me?"

She took the bread. She nodded. She clearly understood nothing of the kind.

"What's your name?" I asked, and immediately wished I hadn't, because names were dangerous currency in this town and I had just offered to trade.

"Wren," she said, mouth already full. Then, swallowing: "What's yours?"

"Jo," I said. "Just Jo."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Those pale eyes saw straight through the wrapping on my wrists, straight through ten years of careful smallness, straight down to the brand-scarred truth of me. And she smiled again—that gap-toothed, ordinary, terrible smile—and said:

"That's not what the frost says."

* * *

I walked home the long way, which is to say I walked fast with detours, checking reflections in window glass for gray hems and taking streets I normally avoided because they were too open. My heart didn't slow until I was behind my own door with the bolt thrown, standing in my single room above the cooper's, surrounded by the small accumulated evidence of a carefully unlived life.

One chair. One bed. One window that faced the mountain, because I'd never quite managed to break myself of looking at it.

The Spire. You could see its peak from anywhere in Cinderhollow, thrusting up beneath the shoulder of the mountain like a needle through cloth. Nobody talked about it. Nobody built facing it if they could help it. Children were taught it was a natural formation, a geological curiosity, and the adults let them believe it because the alternative was teaching them what the tithe wagons were for.

Ten years ago I had stood on a wall at Harrowgate and traded something precious to something in that Spire, and I had told myself ever since that the trade was done, closed, paid. That whatever lived beneath the mountain had taken what it was owed and lost interest in me, the way creditors do.

But the frost had known my name. Or known *of* it. And things beneath mountains do not send messages casually.

I unwrapped my wrists. The brands had gone cold—they always ran cold, dead metal in dead skin—but tonight they ached, a deep bone-ache like the ache before a storm. Like the ache I used to feel when the sky was deciding whether to answer me.

Outside, the summer evening was doing something impossible. I watched through my window as a thin mist crept up the street below, silver-white and slow, curling around doorways like a cat deciding where to sleep. The temperature was dropping. In July. The cooper's wife across the way lit her hearth and stood in front of it with her arms wrapped around herself, staring at the mist like it had personally insulted her.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of sense, I felt it again: that cold, clean, patient pull. Attention. Interest. A goddess waking after a hundred years of silence, stretching through cracks in the world, finding the one person in Cinderhollow who knew her touch.

*She says you owe her a conversation.*

I sat down on my one chair and put my head in my hands and laughed, quietly, the way you laugh at funerals. Because here was the joke, the beautiful rotten joke of it: I had spent ten years learning to be nobody, and it had worked, and now the dead were digging up my grave to tell me they remembered where I was buried.

Tomorrow the Graycloaks would come. Frost in high summer draws inquisitors the way blood draws sharks, and when they came they would ask questions, and someone would remember the woman who packed her stall too fast, the woman the child spoke to, the woman whose wrists burned under their wrappings.

I should leave. That was the smart play. Pack the bag, take the south road, be a ghost in some other town by week's end.

I looked at the mountain instead. At the Spire's pale needle against the darkening sky.

I thought about a child with straw-colored hair and a goddess's interest in her eyes, standing in a freezing market, laughing like the world was still worth laughing in.

I thought: *they'll erase her. They'll erase that little girl before the week is out, and no one will even remember to miss her.*

I had survived ten years by staying small. By letting other people be taken. By telling myself that survival was neutral, that hiding was innocent, that what the Graycloaks did to the loud ones wasn't my doing because I had never once raised my hand.

The mist pressed against my window. The brands on my wrists ached like distant thunder.

Somewhere out there, a child named Wren was eating my bread and carrying a goddess in her chest like a splinter, and tomorrow the gray hems would come walking up the road, and I had perhaps one night to decide which kind of coward I was going to be.

I didn't sleep. I sat in my one chair and watched the impossible frost form on the inside of my window glass, feathering outward in patterns too perfect to be natural, spelling nothing, saying everything.

By dawn, I had my answer. Though I didn't yet know it would cost me everything I had left.

Down in the street, the first Graycloak was already knocking on doors.
