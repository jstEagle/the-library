## 3. The Graycloaks Come

The trouble with selling luck is that luck, like everything else in Cinderhollow, eventually gets taxed.

I learned this the hard way on the morning the Graycloaks came to market, though I didn't know it yet. I was doing what I always did on a Thirdday: sitting behind my little table with its faded awning, arranging my wares with the studied carelessness of a woman who wants you to believe her goods simply appeared there, fully formed, without any suspicious human intervention. Luck-knots braided from river grass. Iron nails that had been "blessed" — which is to say, I'd held them and thought warm thoughts, which is legal, because no one can prosecute a woman for thinking warm thoughts at a nail. Yet.

"Charm for your troubles," I said to a farmer who passed my stall looking like a man whose troubles had recently multiplied. "Keeps the blight off root vegetables."

He squinted at me. "You're the rain-woman."

"I'm the knot-tying woman."

"They say you sold rain once. Before the Reckoning."

"They say a lot of things." I smiled the smile I'd practiced for ten years, the one that said *I am nobody, please move along.* "Rain comes free these days. It's the only thing that does."

He bought two knots anyway, because farmers are superstitious people and Cinderhollow had been having an unlucky year even before the frost came down off the mountain in high summer and started killing things. He shuffled off toward the vegetable stalls, and I went back to pretending to be furniture.

That's when I saw Maren.

She was three stalls down, at old Tetch's spice stand, arguing cheerfully about the price of pepperroot the way she argued about everything — loudly, with her whole body, as if the world were a tavern debate she intended to win. She was sixty if she was a day, gray-haired, gap-toothed, with forearms like dock rope from forty years of hauling nets out of the Graywater. We'd known each other since my first winter in Cinderhollow, back when I was still flinching at loud noises and sleeping with a knife under my pillow. She'd fed me stew I couldn't pay for and never asked where I'd come from or why my wrists were scarred. In a town built on not asking questions, Maren was the queen of minding her own business, except when it came to feeding hungry strangers.

She caught me looking and raised her mug of cider in salute. I raised my hand back.

And then the bells started.

* * *

Cinderhollow has three bell codes. One toll for fires. Three for wolves. And five — five slow, deliberate strokes, spaced like a funeral procession — for Graycloaks.

Five strokes rang out over the market, and the whole square changed. It's a strange thing to watch, a town remembering what it is. The vendors didn't run. Running draws attention. Instead they did something worse: they became still. Tetch began covering his spices with cloth, slowly, as if he were just tidying up. The fishwife started packing her unsold herring with the calm of a woman who had all the time in the world. A mother by the well drew her children close and began humming a lullaby, loud enough to be heard, a signal that meant *nothing here but ordinary life.*

Everyone became innocent at once. It would have been beautiful if it weren't so damning.

The Graycloaks came through the north arch in a line of six, and I'll give them this much: they know how to make an entrance. Ash-gray cloaks over slate-gray armor, faces hidden behind smooth porcelain half-masks painted with a single closed eye. They walked in step. Their boots made almost no sound, which somehow was worse than stomping. Silence says *we own the quiet here.*

At their center walked the Reader.

Every Graycloak company has one — a man or woman who can taste magic the way a wine-taster tastes vintage. Where a spell has been cast, they can read its residue, follow it like a hound follows blood. The Reader of the Cinderhollow company was new to me, a thin woman with a mask painted in silver rather than white, which meant seniority, which meant trouble. She carried a long brass instrument like a telescope crossed with a tuning fork, and as she walked she held it up and turned slowly, reading the market.

Reading the street where, four days ago, a child had prayed to a dead goddess and the dead goddess had answered with frost in midsummer.

My hands went cold. Not metaphorically — actually cold, a prickling numbness crawling up from my fingertips, and I recognized it with a sick lurch: it was the same feeling I'd had at the shrine, the feeling of power moving near me, recognizing me, wanting me. My magic knew the Reader's instrument the way a dog knows another dog's bark.

*Down,* I told it. *Lie down.*

I kept my eyes on my knots. I braided. River grass over, under, over. A simple pattern, a pattern with no power in it at all, a pattern I could do blind drunk and dying. Over, under, over. Be furniture. Be nobody. Be the charm-seller, the knot-tying woman, the small safe life I had built plank by careful plank for ten years.

The Reader stopped at the shrine first.

Of course she did. The cracked stone shrine of Vessia stood at the market's edge, officially registered as a "historical marker of pre-Reckoning superstition," which is bureaucratic language for *a god we pretend isn't real.* The frost that had bloomed across the whole street four days ago had melted, but frost leaves traces — salt-bloom patterns in the mortar, a faint glassy sheen on the cobbles that doesn't wear away. The Reader knelt, pressed her brass instrument to the stones, and I watched her shoulders go rigid.

Then she stood and spoke, and her voice came out flat and amplified through the mask, carrying across the suddenly silent square.

"A miracle occurred here. An answered prayer. The old law applies." She turned in a slow circle, the closed eye of her mask sweeping over us all. "The one who prayed will come forward. The penalty for concealment is forfeiture of the concealer's soul-record. You have until I finish walking this market."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. Somewhere a baby started crying and was hastily shushed into whimpers.

And I thought — and I am not proud of this, I want to be honest with you, whoever you are, reading whatever record survives of these days — I thought: *she can't prove it was Tam.*

Tam. The cooper's boy. Eight years old, missing two teeth, with a scab on his knee the size of a coin and a mother who worked herself hollow salting fish. Four days ago he'd knelt at that cracked shrine because his father was coughing blood and the physicians wanted money the family didn't have, and Tam had done what children do — he'd asked the sky for help. And the sky, against every law of nature and crown and common sense, had helped.

I'd seen him do it. I'd been twenty feet away, buying tallow. And afterward I'd found him in the alley behind the shrine, wide-eyed and shivering with joy, frost melting in his hair, and I'd crouched down and told him — carefully, gently, in the language of charms and luck-knots — *never tell anyone, never pray there again, never speak of it.* I'd given him a knot for his pocket and told him it would keep him safe.

It hadn't kept anyone safe. Nothing keeps anyone safe. Safety is a story we sell each other at market stalls.

The Reader began to walk the stalls.

* * *

Here is what hiding looks like, in case you've never done it. It doesn't look like dramatics. There's no diving behind barrels, no holding your breath while boots pass inches away. Hiding looks like nothing. Hiding looks like continuing to braid river grass while your heart tries to hammer its way out through your ribs. Hiding is a performance of boredom so complete that you bore yourself into invisibility.

The Reader moved down the row. At Tetch's stand she paused, held her instrument over the spice jars, waited. Tetch stood with his hands visible and his face arranged into peasant blankness. She moved on. At the fishwife's stall, longer. The fishwife's herring got inspected one by one, which I suspect was less about magic than about reminding everyone that they could be inspected. Then the Reader came to me.

She stopped. Of course she stopped. My luck runs backward; it's a family trait.

"Vendor," she said. "Your name."

"Jonah Marsh," I said. Marsh was the surname I'd bought with my first season's earnings, from a clerk who didn't ask questions. "Charms and knotwork. Licensed."

"Licenses can be checked." But she wasn't looking at my license. Her instrument was rising, turning toward me, and I felt my magic rise to meet it like a cat lifting its head at the sound of a can opener, and I clamped down on it with ten years of practice, ten years of being small, *lie down, lie down* —

"Your wrists," the Reader said.

I looked down. My sleeves had ridden up. Just a finger's width of skin showing above my left cuff, where the brand sat: the storm-sigil, seared into me at my trial, the mark they gave sorcerers instead of graves. Ten years I'd kept it covered. Ten years of long sleeves in summer, of checking cuffs like other women check their hair.

I pulled my sleeve down. Slowly. Boredly. "Old burn," I said. "Kettle accident. I'm clumsy."

The instrument hummed. Under the mask, I couldn't see her eyes, but I felt them. And I felt something else — the frost-feeling again, my power stirring toward her like it remembered her kind, like it *wanted* to be found. Traitor. Even my own magic was tired of hiding.

"The kettle," the Reader repeated.

"You know how kettles are," I said. Wry. Calm. Dying inside. "They hold grudges."

Someone in the crowd made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The Reader's head tilted. And then —

"Maren Salt!" the second Graycloak called out, from three stalls down. "Step forward."

* * *

I don't remember standing up. I remember that my knees were suddenly locked and I was upright, watching, as Maren detached herself from the crowd with the same brisk indignation she brought to arguments about pepperroot.

"What now?" she demanded. "I've paid my market fees, I've paid my—"

"You were witnessed at the shrine of Vessia," the Graycloak said, consulting a slate. "Four days past. At the hour of the miracle."

"I walk past that shrine every day of my life, you porcelain-faced fool, it's on the way to the fish—"

"Maren." I said her name before I knew I was going to say anything at all.

She glanced at me. And here is the thing about Maren Salt, the thing I will carry until I die: she saw my face. She saw what was in it. And she understood, instantly, completely, the way she'd understood ten years ago that I was someone who needed feeding and not questioning. She saw that I was terrified, and she saw *why*, or enough of why, and she made a choice in the space of a heartbeat, the way brave people do, casually, as if bravery were just another chore.

"It was me," she said loudly, to the Graycloak. "If someone prayed at that rotten old shrine, it was me. I'm old. I'm allowed to talk to the sky."

"That's not—" I started.

"Quiet, girl." She didn't look at me. "You'll mind your stall."

The Reader had drifted over. She raised her instrument toward Maren, and I waited — some part of me waited, shamefully, desperately — for the instrument to stay silent, for the truth to protect her, for the world to be the kind of place where the innocent go free.

The instrument hummed.

Low. Faint. But it hummed, because Maren had stood near the shrine for thirty years, because grief leaves residue the way prayer does, because she'd talked to the sky over her drowned husband more times than either of us could count, and the Reader's brass ear couldn't tell the difference between an old woman's sorrow and a child's miracle. Or worse — maybe it could, and didn't care. Maybe the hum was convenient. Miracles need arrests the way storms need weather.

"Resonance confirmed," the Reader said.

"This is nonsense," Maren said, and then the Graycloaks took her arms, and she stopped being funny. She was strong, Maren, net-hauler, dock-rope arms, but there were four of them and they knew their work, and besides, she didn't fight, not really. Fighting would have made it worse for whoever came next. She let herself be bound, and as they turned her toward the arch, she looked back at me one last time.

She winked.

Gods forgive me. She *winked*. Like it was a joke between friends. Like she was telling me: *I know, girl. Go on being small. Somebody has to survive to remember us.*

They took her through the north arch. The five bells tolled the all-clear. The market exhaled and resumed, too fast, too loudly, everyone performing normalcy with the desperation of the damned. Tetch uncovered his spices. The mother by the well stopped humming. And Tam — I found him with my eyes across the square, pressed against his mother's side, watching the arch where Maren had vanished, and he was crying without any sound at all, and he had my luck-knot clenched in his fist so tight his knuckles had gone white.

I sat back down at my stall. I picked up river grass. Over, under, over.

* * *

I finished the market day. That's the part that shames me most, if you want the whole ugly ledger of it. I didn't run after them. I didn't stand up and confess. I sat behind my little table under my faded awning and I sold eleven more luck-knots to people who needed luck far more than I'd ever sold them, and I made change, and I smiled my nobody-smile, and when the sun went down I packed my wares and walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear, and I did not look at the shrine.

In my room, I lit no lamp. I sat in the dark and took off my left sleeve and looked at the brand by moonlight: the storm-sigil, my name written in the crown's fire, the mark that said *this woman was once worth erasing.* Ten years I'd worn it like a secret. Tonight it felt like what it had probably always been — a receipt. Proof of purchase. *Paid in full: one soul, complicit.*

Maren had fed me when I was starving. Maren had winked. And I had braided river grass.

I thought about Tam's silent crying. I thought about the frost on the shrine, and the way my magic had risen toward the Reader's instrument like a tide answering a moon, and how tired I was — bone-tired, decade-tired — of lying down. I thought about the story the refugees had started telling, the ones drifting in from the mountain roads: that the cold followed prayers, that the old gods were waking, that something beneath the Spire was drinking the warmth out of the world.

I had spent ten years believing that staying small was staying safe. Sitting in the dark, wrists bare, I finally understood the arithmetic of it: my safety had a price, and other people had been paying it. Maren was paying it tonight, in whatever cell the Graycloaks kept, smiling forever or not smiling at all.

There was a knock at my door. Three soft raps. Then, after a pause, three more — the rhythm Maren used when she brought stew.

But Maren was in a cell.

I opened the door with a knife in my right hand and my heart somewhere in my throat, and standing in the moonlit stairwell was a stranger: a gaunt man in the travel-stained vestments of an order that no longer existed, clutching a leather folio to his chest like a shield, with the look of a man who has read something in an old book that will not let him sleep.

"Jonah Marsh?" he said. Then, quieter, watching my face the way you watch a fuse burn: "Or should I say — Jonah Thornbury? Because I've found your name in a ledger, and it seems the court beneath the mountain still remembers what everyone else was made to forget."

The knife in my hand suddenly weighed nothing at all, and everything.
