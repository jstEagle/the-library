## 4. What the Ledger Owes

There are three kinds of men who keep records for dead orders. The first are scholars, who love the past the way a moth loves a lamp. The second are cowards, who prefer the company of paper to the company of people. I have been assured by everyone who has ever met me that I am the third kind: a man with nowhere else to go.

I did not argue. Arguing requires a future tense, and I had given mine up some years ago, along with my vestments, my vows, and my right to be addressed as anything warmer than "you there."

The Order of the Quiet Hand had been dissolved—suppressed, the crown's writ said, though suppressed is a generous word for what was done to us—for the crime of keeping accounts the government preferred forgotten. We were bookkeepers of the soul. Literally. Before the Edict of Erasure, every bargain struck between a mortal and something older than mortality passed through our hands to be witnessed, weighed, and written down. We could not stop the bargains. No one has ever stopped the bargains. But we could make sure that when a man sold his grandmother's ghost for a good harvest, someone, somewhere, wrote it down in a fair hand and kept the receipt.

The crown burned our chapter houses. It burned our priests, in several cases quite literally. What it could not burn—and this is the flaw in every tyranny, the crack in every pot—was the habit of copying. I had spent thirty years teaching novices that a record exists only in triplicate, and so, when the soldiers came, the records existed in triplicate. Or in my case, in a cellar beneath a tannery in the town of Marrow's Ford, in forty-one crates disguised as tax documents from a defunct wool concern.

Tax documents. There is no hiding place in the world so deep as bureaucratic boredom.

* * *

I found her name on a Tuesday.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the whole of my vocation, and because Tuesdays have never done me any particular harm before or since. It was raining—the ordinary kind, the kind that falls without being asked—and I was cataloguing Crate Thirty-Seven, which contained fragments recovered from the burning of the Harrowgate scriptorium. Fragments is a charitable term. Most of what came out of those crates was char. Paper remembers fire the way skin does, reluctantly and forever.

But one leaf had survived, tucked inside a copper scroll-case, and the case had been buried under a floor before the flames reached it. Someone in that scriptorium had known what was coming. Someone had knelt on cold stone in the dark and hidden a single page, and died—I assume died; one hopes died—without telling anyone where.

The leaf was a ledger fragment. Not one of ours, properly speaking. The hand was wrong for the Quiet Hand; too ornate, too pleased with itself. Our scribes wrote like men burying bodies: efficiently, without flourish. This hand curled and preened. It was the hand of the other side. Of the court.

I will not pretend my hands were steady. They were not. I am sixty-one years old and I have held dying men and burning books and once, memorably, a relic that screamed, and my hands have shaken at all the appropriate moments. But they shook differently that Tuesday. They shook the way they shake when the body knows something the mind has not yet consented to know.

The fragment recorded a single transaction. Payment received: one soul, unencumbered, freely pledged. Pledged by: Jonah Thornbury, storm-caller of Harrowgate. Payable to: the court beneath the mountain, holdfast of the buried king. Date of pledge: the night the siege broke.

Date due: upon the pledger's natural death, or upon default, whichever comes first.

I sat in my cellar among my forty-one crates of other people's sins and read the name eleven times. Jonah Thornbury. The woman whose storm had shattered the siege lines at Harrowgate and drowned the enemy's engines and saved ten thousand lives and cost her everything she had, including, as it turned out, rather more than she had. The woman the tribunals had exiled for the crime of winning too thoroughly. The woman whose name had been scraped from every monument and struck from every roll by royal writ, so that officially no such person had ever existed, and unofficially everyone remembered her anyway, because erasure is a door that only locks from the outside.

She had sold her soul to buy the storm.

And no one had told her—or if someone had told her, she had paid anyway, which is worse, or better, depending entirely on what you believe people owe the world.

* * *

Let me tell you what I did next, because I am not proud of it, and a record-keeper who hides his own entries is no record-keeper at all.

I did nothing. For six days, I did nothing.

I told myself it was prudence. I told myself that a disgraced priest walking up to an outlawed witch in an outlawed town and saying *good morning, madam, your soul is collateral* would accomplish nothing except getting us both a Graycloak escort to a cell. Prudence. Yes. That was the word I used while I drank more than usual and slept less than usual and took out the fragment each evening to check that the name still said what it said. Names do that, sometimes. They change when you're not looking. Hers never did.

On the seventh day I admitted the truth, which is that I was afraid. Not of the Graycloaks. I have been afraid of Graycloaks so long that the fear has worn smooth, like a stair, and I climb it without thinking. I was afraid of her. Because I knew what I would see in her face when I told her, and I knew it because I had seen it before, in other faces, over other ledgers, in the years when I still wore gray and carried the seal. The look of a person learning the exact price of their own survival. There is no expression in the human repertoire so naked. Grief at least has somewhere to go. That look just stands there, holding its receipt.

And beneath the fear, I confess, something less honorable: hope. Thirty years of recording bargains, and in all that time I had never once seen one unwound. The ledger takes; the ledger keeps. That was the first law of my order, taught to novices before they learned their letters. But here was a debt dated to the night of Harrowgate, held by a court that had been silent for a decade, sealed beneath a mountain that no pilgrim road had touched since the Edict. Debts sleep. Debts can be disputed. And if the court beneath the mountain was stirring again—if the prayers at the cracked shrines were being answered, as the frightened letters in my correspondence claimed, frost in high summer, warmth draining out of the world like blood from a wound—then perhaps the ledger was stirring too. Perhaps it was calling in its accounts.

Perhaps she needed to know before it knocked.

A record-keeper's duty, my old master used to say, is not to the past. Anyone can serve the past; the past is grateful and undemanding. A record-keeper's duty is to the living, who cannot remember everything, and to the dead, who cannot remind anyone. I had failed that duty for ten years, huddled over my crates like a dragon on a hoard of other people's griefs.

Seven days. Then I packed the fragment, three changes of clothes, and a bottle of the tannery owner's brandy for which I intended to leave payment and almost certainly would not, and I bought passage east to Cinderhollow.

* * *

Cinderhollow is the sort of town that exists because the road gave up before the mountains did. It sits in the shadow of the Spire's massif like a dog that has learned to live under a table, hoping not to be kicked. Magic is outlawed there with a thoroughness that borders on devotion. There are notices. There are always notices. NO CHARM, NO CANT, NO CALLING, in letters tall enough to read from a galloping horse, which tells you something about how the notices expect to be encountered.

I arrived on market day, which was either luck or the opposite of luck, depending on how you feel about crowds when you are carrying contraband theology in your coat.

Finding her was not difficult. I had expected it to be difficult. I had prepared, in my fussy way, a plan involving three days of discreet inquiry among the sort of people who know things—tinkers, midwives, the lame beggar outside the shrine who sees everything and is paid in nothing. I abandoned the entire plan within the hour, because Jonah Thornbury was standing at a stall near the well, selling knots.

Luck-knots, the sign said, in a hand that was careful to promise nothing. And that was the tell, you understand. A fraud sells loudly. A fraud promises health, wealth, the return of absent sons. She promised nothing at all, and priced her nothing at a copper, and the queue at her stall was longer than at any other stall in the square, because the desperate can smell the real thing even when it is pretending very hard to be string.

She was smaller than the statues that no longer existed of her. That is a strange sentence to write, but it is true in the way that matters: I had seen her likeness, before the scraping, on the Harrowgate memorial—a tall figure with a raised arm and lightning rendered in bronze—and the woman at the stall shared with the statue only the eyes. Storm-gray, and watchful, and tired in a way that ten years of small safe living had not cured, because it cannot, because the storm is not in the sky, it is in the skull.

I watched her work for a quarter hour. I am not ashamed of that either; observation is my trade. She tied her knots with a deftness that was almost certainly innocent and almost certainly wasn't. Her fingers moved in patterns I recognized—old patterns, liturgical ones, the knot-forms the Quiet Hand used for binding testimony—and every few customers she would pause, flex her hand, and look at her own fingers as if they had said something rude. I saw it happen four times. On the fourth, a flicker went up her wrist, under the sleeve, a faint blue-white shimmer like heat-lightning behind cloud, and her face went carefully, deliberately blank, the face of a woman stepping over a crack in the pavement.

Her magic was waking up. Whatever was happening in the world—the answered prayers, the creeping cold—it was reaching into Cinderhollow and knocking on doors that had been nailed shut for ten years.

That decided me. Not the fragment alone, not the duty alone, but the sight of power stirring unwanted in a woman who had arranged her whole life around its absence. If the ledger was calling in debts, it would find her whether I spoke or not. The only question was whether she would hear it from a friend of the record, or from the thing itself.

Neither option was kind. I chose the one I could live with.

* * *

I waited until the crowd thinned. I bought a luck-knot first—paid my copper, tied it to my walking staff, and received in return a look of mild suspicion, which I earned honestly, since I dress like a defrocked priest, walk like a defrocked priest, and have the general air of a man who knows what you did in the winter of the year you'd rather forget.

"Madam," I said, when the last customer had gone, "my name is Anselm. Formerly brother Anselm, of the Order of the Quiet Hand. You will not have heard of us. That was the point of us."

"I don't want trouble," she said. Her voice was flat and low, a voice kept deliberately in the basement of its range. "Whatever you're selling, the answer's no."

"I'm not selling. I'm delivering." I set my hand on my coat, over the pocket where the fragment lay wrapped in oilcloth. "Ten years ago, on the night the siege of Harrowgate broke, a transaction was recorded. One soul, freely pledged, payable to the court beneath the mountain. The record survived the burning of the scriptorium. I have carried it across half the country to put it in front of the only person whose name is written on it."

I have delivered many hard sentences in my life. Death notices to mothers. Debt notices to fathers. I have a technique: say it plainly, quickly, all at once, like pulling a tooth. Slow extraction only multiplies the pain.

It did not work. She did not collapse or rage or do any of the things people do. She went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and looked at me for a long moment, and then she looked past me—at the mountain, at the great blind shoulder of the Spire's massif filling the southern sky—and something crossed her face that I had no ledger-category for. Not surprise. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing a knock at a door she had always known was there.

"You're wrong," she said quietly.

"Madam—"

"The siege." Her jaw worked. "I didn't sell anything. I *called*, and the storm came, and afterward they took everything else—my name, my home, my—" She stopped. Her hand had drifted to her left wrist, to the sleeve, and I understood suddenly that the scars the Graycloaks branded into convicted callers were not the only marks she carried. "Nobody bought anything. There was no bargain."

"No," I agreed gently. "There was no bargain *made*. Bargains require two willing parties, and the court does not ask. It invoices." I drew out the oilcloth. "The record says *freely pledged*. I have spent thirty years reading that phrase, and I will tell you what it means, because no one told me either until it was too late to matter. It means the court considers that you knew. Some part of you, in the moment of the calling, when you reached for a power large enough to drown an army—you knew what it would cost, and you reached anyway. The court counts that as consent. It has counted it that way since before there were courts."

The market noise went on around us—barter, laughter, a child shrieking happily about something—and we stood in the middle of it like two figures in a painting of a different subject entirely.

"They're answering prayers," she said at last. It was not a question, and it was not to me, exactly. It was to the mountain. "At the shrines. Vessia's shrine, on Tanner's Row—there was frost, the whole street, in summer. I thought—" Her hand closed around her wrist. "I thought it meant they were coming back."

"It may mean," I said, with the care of a man laying down a heavy load on thin ice, "that something beneath the mountain has begun to spend what it holds. And a court that spends begins, sooner or later, to collect."

For a moment neither of us moved. Then she untied the luck-knot from my staff, looked at it as if seeing her own work for the first time, and pressed it back into my palm with a grip that was stronger than it had any right to be.

"Show me the page," she said.

And I did. And as her eyes went down the ornate, preening, curling hand of the court's scribe—as she read her own name written in a script that had no business knowing it—I saw, over her shoulder, at the far end of the market square, two figures in gray cloaks stop at the mouth of Tanner's Row. One of them raised a hand, and frost bloomed in the air where the shrine stood, faint and white and wrong for the season, and both figures turned toward it like hounds catching scent.

They had found the prayer. Which meant, sooner or later, they would find the pray-er. And standing beside her, holding the page that named her, I understood that I had just made myself the second most interesting document in Cinderhollow.

Jonah Thornbury folded the fragment once, twice, and slipped it into her bodice with the calm of a woman hiding a knife.

"Well, brother Anselm," she said, watching the gray cloaks over my shoulder, "you'd better walk with me. And you'd better talk fast."
