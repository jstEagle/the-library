## 5. Ten Years Ago

The rain in Cinderhollow never falls straight. It comes down sideways, mean and gray, the way rain does when it has somewhere better to be. I stood under my awning that morning watching it and thought, not for the first time, that I had made a career out of selling people weather I no longer had any right to touch.

The luck-knots were moving well. They always moved well when the sky was ugly — fear is a better salesman than I ever was. An old woman with a cart of turnips bought three, one for each grandchild, and haggled me down to a price that would have embarrassed a beggar, and I let her, because her hands shook and because ten years ago I would have given them away.

"Storm's coming," she said, squinting up at the clouds as if she could read them.

"They always say that," I said. "It's Cinderhollow. The storm is always coming."

She laughed and went off into the wet, and I turned back to my little table of knotted string and river-stones and pressed wax, and I did not think about Harrowgate at all, which is how I spent most of my days: not thinking about Harrowgate, with tremendous discipline, for hours at a time.

But the rain smelled wrong that morning. That's what I'll say, if anyone ever asks — and no one ever will, because no one in Cinderhollow knows enough to ask. Rain has a smell before it falls, a green iron smell, like a blade wrapped in moss. This rain smelled of nothing. It fell like water from a jug, dead water, poured without intent. And some animal part of me, the part that had once held a storm on a leash of will and blood, sat up inside my chest and took notice.

*Something is answering prayers,* that animal part said. *Something old.*

I told it to lie back down. It had been lying down for ten years. It knew how.

* * *

Here is what they tell you about the siege of Harrowgate, now that anyone is permitted to tell anything at all: nothing. The histories were burned. The songs were unmade by decree, every copy gathered up and fed to fires lit by men in gray cloaks, so that the whole event slid out of the world's memory like a stone dropped into deep water. If you ask a child in the capital who broke the siege of Harrowgate, the child will look at you blankly, because there was no siege of Harrowgate. There has never been a siege anywhere. Sieges are outlawed memories, along with storm-calling, along with the old gods, along with me.

So let me be the record, then, since I'm the only one left who can be. Let me set it down here where no Graycloak will ever think to look: inside the head of a charm-seller who ties knots for coppers in the worst town in the world.

Harrowgate sat in a pass between two mountains, a city of white walls and slate roofs built across the only road north. Ten years ago this autumn, the army of the Reach came down out of the high country to take it, because whoever holds Harrowgate holds the north, and the Reach wanted the north the way a drunk wants one more drink. They brought forty thousand men. Harrowgate had walls, and grain, and eleven thousand souls, most of them not soldiers.

We held for sixty-three days.

I know the number because I counted them on the wall of my own house, scratching marks into the stone with a knife, one for each day, until the marks ran out of wall and I started carving them over the doorframe, and then over the window, and then I stopped counting because the counting had become a prayer and I had stopped believing in anyone to pray to.

The old gods were already illegal by then. That's the part the burning of the histories got right, at least in spirit: the gods had been outlawed for a generation, their shrines cracked and their names struck from records, and the priesthoods scattered or gone quiet. Vessia of the rains. Ondru of the thresholds. The Mother of Small Hours. Names whispered, if at all, behind doors. Magic that came from them was contraband. Magic that came from anywhere was contraband. The crown had decided, in its wisdom, that the world was safer with its wonders amputated, and perhaps it was right; I've had ten years to consider the question and I still don't have an answer I like.

I was born with the rain in me anyway. You can't legislate weather. The storm-calling ran in my mother's line, back and back, women who could stand on a hilltop and ask the sky for help and be answered. It wasn't command, whatever the stories said afterward. It was asking. It was being the kind of person the sky was willing to listen to. My mother called a flood once, to save our village from bandits, and drowned half our fields doing it, and spent the rest of her life planting things in the mud with her hands like she was apologizing.

On the fifty-eighth day of the siege, the granaries burned.

Not by accident. The Reach's sappers got under the walls through the old drainage culverts — the ones the city fathers had been meaning to brick up for thirty years — and they fired the grain stores in the night, and by dawn the heart of Harrowgate was ash and the smoke stood over the city like a second, darker mountain. Eleven thousand people, and food for nine days, and an enemy at the gates who had just learned that we were starving.

I stood in the granary square with ash falling on me like filthy snow, and Cassian's hand on my shoulder, and I remember thinking: *this is the moment. Everything before this was prologue. Everything after this is the price.*

Cassian. I should tell you about Cassian now, while I still have the courage, because he keeps arriving in this story the way he kept arriving in my life — late, beautiful, and carrying something I didn't want.

He was a captain of the gate-watch when I met him, four years before the siege. He had a laugh that started in his chest and worked outward, and he was smarter than soldiers are supposed to be allowed to be, and he loved me with a thoroughness that frightened me, the way a flood frightens you even when you've asked for rain. We had a house on Tanner's Row with a blue door. We had arguments about whether the cat liked him better. He used to fall asleep mid-sentence reading aloud to me, and I used to stay awake listening to him breathe and thinking, with a kind of terror, *this. This is the thing I cannot lose.*

On the fifty-eighth day, standing in the ash, he said: "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," I said.

"Jonah—"

"Look at them." The square was full of people queuing for moldy bread that didn't exist, holding places for neighbors too weak to stand. A boy was licking ash off his fingers because his body had decided hunger was a flavor. "Look at them and find me another way."

He looked. I watched him look. And I watched him arrive, the way I had already arrived, at the same terrible arithmetic.

Because there was a way. There was always a way. It's just that the way led down.

* * *

Under Harrowgate — under every city, if you believe the oldest stories, and I had stopped believing them precisely because I needed to — there are roots. The mountain's roots, the world's roots, and in them, at the bottom of everything, a court. The court of the buried king. The stories said he had been put down there before the gods went quiet, that he kept a ledger, that he traded in souls the way merchants trade in salt, and that his prices were always fair, which is the most frightening thing you can say about any merchant.

The stories also said you should never, ever go looking for him.

But the stories assumed you had time. We had nine days of food and forty thousand enemies.

There was a door. There is always a door; that's practically the definition of a disaster. In the deepest cellar of the citadel, behind a wall the masons had bricked up and plastered over and forgotten on purpose, there was a stair going down, older than the citadel, older than the city, cut into the living rock in a style no mason would own. The citadel's records called it the Underway and marked it with a single word in a dead language that the last priest of Ondru, before they cracked his shrine, had translated for me once, laughing, as if it were a joke:

*Accounting.*

I went down on the sixtieth day. Alone, because I wouldn't let Cassian come, and because everyone else who might have stopped me was either dead, starving, or sensible.

I won't describe all of it. Some of it doesn't translate. But I remember the cold getting older as I descended — not colder, *older*, cold with seniority, cold that had been down there since before warmth was invented — and I remember the sound, which was the worst part. A sound like a quill moving across paper. Endless. Patient. Coming from everywhere.

And I remember the court.

Gilded. Frozen. Beautiful the way a corpse at a viewing is beautiful, arranged and polished and presented. Souls stood in the galleries there — I understood that much even then, though I didn't have the words for it yet — souls that had traded themselves willingly, wearing faces of perfect contentment, smiling smiles that had been smiling for centuries. And at the center, on a throne of black ice shot through with gold veins, the buried king, who looked less like a man than like the idea of a debt given a body.

He didn't rise. Kings who are owed things don't rise.

"You want a storm," he said. His voice was the quill-sound. I realized the quill-sound was him, had always been him, was the sound of the ledger writing itself. "You want it large enough to break an army. You have nothing to pay with but yourself. And you are wondering what I charge."

"I'm wondering if you'll take payment later," I said, because I have always been brave in exactly the stupidest proportions.

A smile, or the memory of one. "Everything here is paid later. That is what a ledger *is*. Name your storm."

I named it. I asked for a storm the size of the war, a storm to drown the Reach's camps and scatter their engines and freeze their supply lines and send forty thousand men home telling stories about the sky. I asked for the kind of storm my mother's line had only ever dreamed of, the kind that gets remembered — except nothing gets remembered anymore, does it? Nothing gets to be remembered.

"And the price," the king said, when I finished, "is your soul. Not your death — your soul. Held here. In trust. You will live, and love, and grow old, and none of it will ever quite belong to you, because the best part of you will be down here, in my keeping, accruing interest." He tilted his head. "Or you can walk back up those stairs and watch eleven thousand people learn what famine is. The ledger is patient. It can wait for your answer."

I gave it. Of course I gave it. You already know I gave it; you've met the woman I became, the one who sells luck-knots in Cinderhollow and flinches from her own shadow. Something in me signed that ledger and stayed signed, and I climbed back up those stairs lighter than I'd come down, hollowed out in a place I couldn't point to, and on the sixty-first day of the siege, I stood on the highest tower of Harrowgate and asked the sky for help.

And the sky *answered*.

* * *

I have tried, over ten years, to describe that storm to myself, in the privacy of my own skull, and I have never managed it honestly, because honest description sounds like boasting. So: imagine the sky standing up. Imagine the air deciding, all at once, that it had opinions. I didn't call the lightning so much as introduce it to the ground. The rain came down in ropes thick as my waist. The wind peeled roofs off houses in the Reach's camp and used them, I am told, as weapons. For three days and three nights the storm sat on the Reach's army like a god's thumb pressing down on an anthill, and on the fourth morning the survivors were twenty miles south and walking faster, and Harrowgate opened its gates, and eleven thousand people walked out into the wreckage of the fields and wept and laughed and began, immediately, the endless human work of rebuilding.

Ten thousand lives, give or take. That was the arithmetic I'd done in the dark. Ten thousand saved for one soul pledged. Any merchant in Cinderhollow would call that a bargain.

I carved a sixty-third mark on the wall of my house and then, because it seemed important, I carved a sixty-fourth, for the day after, the first day of peace, the day I woke up next to Cassian and felt the hollowness in me and thought: *worth it. Whatever comes of it. Worth it.*

What came of it arrived eleven days later, in gray cloaks.

* * *

They came quietly, which was unlike them. Usually the Graycloaks arrived in a town like a verdict — wagons, chains, the crack of the record-burners' tongs. This time it was six men and a woman, plain-cloaked, asking questions door to door. Where were you on the sixty-first day. Did you see anyone on the towers. Was there lightning before the thunder, and if so, how long before.

Asking, in the careful bureaucratic language of the crown, whether anyone in Harrowgate had committed the crime of a miracle.

They didn't need to ask. Of course they didn't. A storm like that leaves a signature, the way a forged coin leaves a file-mark. Somewhere in the capital, instruments I never saw and instruments I can't imagine had tasted that storm and known it for what it was: called. Asked-for. Answered. The last public miracle in the history of the world, performed by someone who must have thought, in her arrogance, that saving ten thousand lives would count for something.

I might even have run. I had warning — three days of it, enough to be over the northern passes and gone. But running meant leaving Cassian to answer their questions alone, and I had seen what happened to the families of the accused, and besides — and this is the shame I carry, the small shameful stone at the bottom of everything — besides, I believed in Harrowgate. I believed eleven thousand people whose lives I had saved would never let them take me. I stood in my house on Tanner's Row with its blue door and I waited for my city to speak for me.

My city spoke. Just not in the voice I expected.

They arrested me in the market square, gently, almost apologetically, the way you arrest someone the outcome has already been decided about. And the trial — they called it an inquiry, inquiries being cheaper than trials — lasted two days, and I will not drag you through all of it. I will give you only the moment that matters, the moment I have replayed ten thousand times at three in the morning with my eyes open in the dark of whatever room I was hiding in that year.

The inquisitor read the charges. Storm-calling. Traffic with proscribed powers. The unlawful preservation of the city of Harrowgate — yes, that was really the phrasing, *unlawful preservation*, as though the crime were not the storm but the surviving — and then he said the words that were the whole reason for the theater: "These acts were not accomplished alone. The accused had accomplices. Confess your confederates, and the court may show mercy."

Mercy. The word hung in the hall like smoke.

And Cassian stood up.

I want to tell you he was forced. I have spent ten years trying to build that version of events, laying bricks of maybe: maybe they had his family, maybe they threatened him, maybe there was something I didn't know. I laid those bricks carefully, year after year, and built out of them a prison to keep the question in. But I was there. I saw his face. And his face was not the face of a man being coerced. His face was the face of a man doing arithmetic — the same terrible arithmetic I had done in the granary square, the same cold summing-up — and arriving at an answer, and hating the answer, and giving it anyway.

"The storm was hers," he said. His voice didn't shake. That's what I can't forgive, sometimes, more than the words themselves — it didn't shake. "She called it. She went down beneath the citadel, to the old court, and she made a bargain there. I heard her plan it. I begged her not to go. She went anyway." He paused, and looked at me, and his eyes were wet, and I have hated those wet eyes for ten years, because wet eyes mean it costs the betrayer something, and if it costs them something then why, why, *why* — "I testify to all of it. Every word true."

Every word true. That was the poison in it. He didn't lie. He couldn't even be convicted of perjury in the court of my own heart. Every word he said was true, and he said them anyway, and the difference between truth and betrayal, I learned that day, is not what you say. It's who you decide the saying belongs to.

They took my name first. That's how it works — the erasure precedes the sentence, so that by the time they lead you away, no one is leading *anyone* anywhere; a stranger is simply being escorted. They struck Jonah Thornbury from the rolls of Harrowgate. They struck her from the garrison lists, from the tax rolls, from the marriage register — from beside *his* name, which they left intact, untouched, clean as new snow. They burned the sixty-four marks off my wall with a torch, all of them, even the sixty-fourth, even the one for the day after, the peaceful one.

And then they gave me my sentence, and here is the joke, the beautiful crown-shaped joke at the center of my life: exile. Not death. Exile. Because killing the storm-caller of Harrowgate would make a martyr, and martyrs get remembered, and remembering was the one thing the crown could not afford. So they walked me to the southern road and pointed me at the horizon and erased me, and the crowd watched in silence, eleven thousand people whose lives I had bought with the best part of myself, and not one of them — not one — said my name, because legally there was no name to say.

Except one.

He was waiting for me at the first milestone past the walls, in the rain, no cloak, standing like a man at a graveside. And I want to tell you I refused to stop. I want to tell you I walked past him with dignity. What I actually did was stop, because ten years is a long time but four years is longer, and love doesn't care what the courts have ruled.

"Don't," I said.

"Jonah—"

"*Don't.*" The rain was coming down around us, ordinary rain, dead rain, rain with no one home. "Give me one sentence. One. Make it good, because it's the last thing you'll ever say to me."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. And what he finally said — what he chose, out of all the sentences in the world, to spend our last moment on — was:

"It was the only way to keep you alive."

Which was a lie. Or a truth. Or both, braided together so tight you couldn't find the seam, which I have since learned is what most terrible things are. And I laughed — I actually laughed, the sound tearing out of me like something ripping — and I said, "You kept alive the part of me that was left over," and I turned and walked south into the rain, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you die twice.

* * *

That was ten years ago. That was yesterday. Both are equally true, and this morning in Cinderhollow, both arrived at once.

Because the luck-knot woman came back. The turnip grandmother, hurrying through the dead rain, wringing her hands, and she grabbed my sleeve with her shaking turnip-cart hands and said, "Girl — girl, you have to come, it's the Merrow boy, the little one, he's praying at the cracked shrine on Bell Lane, he's praying to the *old lady of the rains*, and Jonah — girl, forgive me, I don't know your name, no one knows anyone's name anymore — girl, the frost is *answering him*."

And every hair on my arms stood up, and the hollow place in me — the place shaped like a signature on a ledger, the place ten years of knots and coppers had never filled — turned over like something waking from a long sleep, and I heard myself say, in a voice I hadn't used since a tower in Harrowgate:

"Take me to him."
