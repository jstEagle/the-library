## 6. The God-Touched Girl

The gods talk too much.

That's the first thing you should know about me. Everyone thinks hearing the old gods would be like hearing music, or like a warm hand on your head, or like something out of the temple stories Anselm-types tell when they want your coin. It isn't. It's like living under a bridge where nine hundred crows have all decided you owe them money.

They come through cracks. That's how I think of it anyway. There are cracks in the world — there always were, little ones, a crack behind the shrine on Tanner's Row, a crack in the well at the tannery, a crack in the space between one heartbeat and the next if you're built wrong like me — and through the cracks the old gods leak. Vessia with her frost-and-hunger voice. The Mender, who hums. Something down deep that doesn't have a name anymore because it sold its name, and it only ever says *ow*.

I was born in Cinderhollow, which is a town shaped like an apology. My mother went into the ash-fields one autumn and didn't come back, and after that I lived wherever the listening was quietest: culverts, the gap behind the smokehouse, once for a whole month the bell-tower of the dead chapel, which was a mistake, because bells are just cracks you can hit.

I ate what I could catch and what people left out, not from kindness but because they wanted me to go away, and I learned very young that being fed to be gotten rid of is still being fed.

And then, on the day everything got worse and better at the same time, which is the only way things happen in my experience, I heard a name come up through a crack.

Not a god's name. A person's name. A true name, the kind they used to carve on soul-stones back before carving them became illegal, the kind that fits a person so exactly it hurts your teeth.

*Jonah Thornbury,* said the crack, in Vessia's voice, all frost and hunger. *Jonah Thornbury, storm-caller, debtor, daughter of no one, seller of rain.*

And underneath it, quieter, the deep thing that only says *ow* said something different for the first time in my whole life.

It said: *there she is.*

* * *

Here is what I knew about Jonah Thornbury before that morning: nothing. Here is what I knew about her after: everything, all at once, dumped into my skull like a cart of fish going over. Ten years of her. Harrowgate. The storm. The bargain. The man Cassian, whose name made a taste like copper. The wrists with the brand-scars she kept wrapped. The charm stall at the market, third row, next to the candle-dipper, where a woman with gray coming in at her temples sold luck-knots and rain-charms and never once let her shadow touch anything important.

I'd walked past her stall maybe two hundred times. She'd given me a heel of bread once without looking at me, the way you feed a dog so it won't follow you.

She was the most dangerous person in Cinderhollow and she smelled like knotweed and lamp oil and fear kept very tidy.

I decided, in the way children decide things, which is to say completely and forever, that she was mine now.

* * *

Finding her was easy. Keeping her was the hard part, because keeping people requires them to cooperate, and Jonah Thornbury had spent ten years learning not to be kept by anybody, including herself.

I followed her for three days first. Don't judge me; you'd have done the same. On the first day she bought thread and argued about the price of madder-root and told a lie to a Graycloak with a smile so smooth I nearly applauded. On the second day she went at dusk to the cracked shrine on Tanner's Row — the same crack that had shouted her name at me — and stood there with her fists closed like she was holding something invisible and heavy, and then she swore, quietly, in a language I didn't know but the crack did, because the crack shivered.

On the third day she caught me.

I want it noted that I let her catch me. Mostly. There was a moment involving a fish cart and my foot catching on a mooring ring that was genuinely not part of the plan, and then her hand was around my arm and she was looking down at me with the exact expression of someone who has found a leak in her roof directly over her bed.

"You've been following me," she said.

"No," I said.

"You've been following me badly."

That was fair. "You're Jonah Thornbury," I said, because I had been holding the name in my mouth for three days like a hot coal and I couldn't hold it anymore.

Everything about her stopped. Not dramatically — she didn't drop her basket or gasp or do any of the things people do in stories. She just stopped, the way water stops right before it freezes. Her eyes went to the market lane, quick, counting ears. Then they came back to me and they were terrible and old and frightened, and I thought: *oh, she's been carrying this alone for ten years, and it weighs as much as the mountain, and nobody has ever once helped her carry it.*

"I don't know what you mean," she said, "and neither do you, and if you say that name again in the open street I will—"

"Vessia told me," I said, because apparently I was committed to dying stupid. "Through the crack. At the shrine. She said *storm-caller, debtor, daughter of no one.* And the deep one said *there she is.*"

Her hand tightened on my arm. Not enough to hurt. Enough to anchor us both, maybe, though I don't think she knew she was doing it.

"What deep one," she said. It wasn't a question. It was the kind of sentence people say when they already know the answer and are begging the world to correct them.

"The one under the mountain," I said. "The one that only says *ow*. Except it said *there she is*, and it sounded—" I hunted for the word, and found it, and wished I hadn't. "It sounded like your name did. Like it fit."

For a long moment Jonah Thornbury looked at me the way you look at a door you nailed shut years ago and have just heard open downstairs.

Then she said, "You're coming with me. Walk in front of me where I can see you. And if you ever say that word again where the sky can hear it, I will personally feed you to the candle-dipper."

"She wouldn't eat me. She likes me. I wax her floor."

"*Walk,*" said the storm-caller of Harrowgate, exile, seller of rain, and I walked.

* * *

Her room above the charm shop was small and ferociously neat, the way rooms get when neatness is the only wildness a person can afford. Charms hung from every rafter in careful rows — knots and braids and little woven eyes — and every single one of them was empty. I could hear it. Empty charms sound like seashells held to the wrong ear. She wasn't selling magic. She was selling the *shape* of magic, the memory of it, hope tied in a bow, and people paid because hope in a bow is more than most towns give you.

"You're a fraud," I said, delighted.

"I'm a survivor," she said. "Sit there. Don't touch anything. Why did Vessia say my name?"

"Because you're Jonah Thornbury."

"That is not an answer, that is a restatement."

"It's the only kind I have." I pulled my knees up on the chair, which she watched with the face of a woman watching a cat consider her furniture. "The gods talk through cracks. They always have, to me. Mostly it's noise. Vessia's loud lately. Louder every day, like she's pressing on the crack from the other side. And three days ago she pressed through and said your name like she was reading it off a list. A ledger." I frowned, because the word arrived with weight attached. "A ledger. She said it like a ledger word. *Debtor.*"

Jonah sat down across from me, slowly, and unwrapped the cloth from her left wrist without seeming to decide to. Underneath was the scar. I'd seen brands before — Cinderhollow has laws the way a net has holes — but this one was older than the outlawry, older-looking, a mark like a spiral of frost burned into the skin, and when I listened to it the way I listen to everything, it said *ow* too. Quietly. Patiently. The way a debt says *ow*.

"They took payment," I whispered. "Ten years ago. You paid them something. And it's still down there. Still theirs."

"How," she said, very evenly, "do you know that."

"Because the crack knows it. Because Vessia knows it. Because—" and here I made my mistake, or my truth, which is the same thing with me, "—because you're *loud*, Jonah Thornbury. You walk around this town folded up small as a charm-knot and you are the loudest thing in it. You're a hole in the shape of yourself. Everything missing in you rings. I've heard you ringing for years. I just didn't know what the note was until the gods sang it."

She put her face in her hands.

I should tell you what I felt then, because everyone always wants to know what it's like, being god-touched, hearing what others can't. What it's like is this: it's like standing outside a lit window in winter. You can see everything inside — the warmth, the shape of the life — and none of it is yours, and the glass is cold against your forehead, and you stay anyway, because seeing is better than not seeing.

But Jonah Thornbury sitting there with her face in her hands, letting me see her — that was a window opening. First one ever. And I stood in the cold draft of it and I understood, in a way I couldn't have explained, that being known goes both directions, and that I had just shown her the ugliest room in my house too: the feral girl who eats scraps and hears voices and follows strangers home.

People are supposed to look away from that room. She didn't. She lifted her face after a while and looked straight at me, red-eyed, wrecked, and said:

"How old are you?"

"Nine. Maybe ten. The winters here all feel the same."

"And the gods speak to you. Often."

"When they bother. Lately they bother constantly. Vessia's excited about something. She keeps saying words like *returning* and *opening* and *soon*, and yesterday she said *the warmth rises*, which was strange, because it's high summer and she said it like a threat."

Something moved behind Jonah's eyes — I heard it move, a low note of dread, the sound a bridge makes before it decides about collapsing. But what she said was: "Are you hungry?"

I blinked. "What?"

"Are. You. Hungry. It's a simple question. There's bread. There's cheese that's mostly wax. I'm offering them to you."

"Why?"

"Because you're a child who lives in a culvert and hears dead gods, and because you know my name, and because those two facts together mean you're either the most dangerous creature in Cinderhollow or the only person alive who might understand what's happening to me, and either way you shouldn't plan your future around hunger." She pushed the bread across the table. "Also because ten years ago I fed a stray dog to make it stop following me and it worked, and I've regretted the efficiency of that method ever since."

I took the bread. I want to be clear that I knew it was a trap. Bread is always a trap. Bread is how they get you to belong somewhere. I took it anyway, and ate it, and it was the best bread I ever ate, including every bread since, and I have eaten a great deal of bread since, some of it objectively better, and it has never once tasted the way that bread tasted, and I know why. It's because that bread was the first food anyone gave me without wanting me gone afterward.

Being known is terrifying. Nobody tells you the other half. Being known is also the only meal that actually fills you up.

* * *

I stayed. Of course I stayed. She pretended for four days that this was temporary, that she was finding my people, that a girl couldn't just live in the spare corner of a fraud charm-seller's room, and I let her pretend, because pretending is free and she needed the practice at wanting things.

At night the gods got louder. That's the other thing I should say, the thing I didn't say to her yet, because even I have some sense of timing. Every time somebody in Cinderhollow prayed to the old gods — really prayed, the desperate kind, kneeling, meaning it — the cracks got wider. I could feel prayers like footsteps overhead. And every prayer that got answered, every little frost-miracle, every impossible mercy, Vessia's voice came through stronger and the deep one's *ow* came through deeper, and the air got a degree colder, and I started sleeping in my coat in high summer and telling no one.

On the fifth night I woke up because the crack under the floorboards was singing my name.

Not Jonah's. Mine. Wren, it said, in nine voices at once, Wren, Wren, little crack, little door, and I lay there with my heart going like a trapped bird and listened to the goddess of frost explain, patiently, lovingly, the way you explain things to a tool, that I had been *found useful*, and that there was a woman asleep across the room who owed a debt beneath the mountain, and that soon — soon — the warmth would rise, and the doors would open, and I should keep her close.

Keep her close. Like a key.

I got up in the dark and crossed the cold floor and stood over Jonah Thornbury's bed, this folded-up, frightened, magnificent fraud, this woman whose missing pieces rang louder than her present ones, and I understood that the gods hadn't sent me to her.

They'd sent her to me. Or rather: they were using me to keep their debtor in reach, the way you tether a goat near the wolf's den because you'll need the goat later.

I hated them for it. All nine hundred crow-voices of them. And then, because I am what I am — a crack myself, a door, a girl-shaped gap in the world — I knelt down beside her bed and did the one thing the gods hadn't asked for and wouldn't like.

I woke her up.

"Jonah," I whispered, and her eyes opened instantly, the way soldiers' eyes open, already aimed. "Jonah, we have to talk about what you paid them. Because I think they're coming to collect. And I think they sent *me* to make sure you're home when they arrive."

Outside the window, over the rooftops of Cinderhollow, in the middle of the warmest week of summer, the first snowflake of the long winter drifted past the glass like a signature on a document.

Jonah saw it. I saw her see it. And the note of dread in her went all the way down, past the bottom of her, into the place where her sold soul used to be, and rang there like a bell in an empty tower.
