## 7. Winter's First Teeth

### Jonah

The snow came on the ninth day of Highsummer, which was the sort of joke the world had started telling lately, and nobody was laughing.

I was at my stall, hawking luck-knots to farmers who needed them more than they knew, when the first flakes drifted down through air that should have been thick enough to chew. They landed on the awning. They did not melt.

I stood there with a knot in one hand and a lie half-finished in my mouth—*guaranteed to turn your fortunes, mistress, only two coppers*—and watched the white accumulate in little ridges along the rope of my stall like frost climbing a windowpane. Around me, Cinderhollow's market went quiet in that particular way markets do, all the noise draining out at once, hundreds of people arriving at the same impossible conclusion without a word passing between them.

Then someone screamed, and the market remembered how to be loud.

"Get under cover!" I shouted, because that seemed useful, and because shouting gave me something to do with the fear crawling up my throat. "It won't last—it can't last—"

But it lasted. All afternoon it lasted, fat slow flakes falling out of a blue sky, and by evening Cinderhollow wore six inches of summer snow and every soul in town had crowded into the Wayfarer's Rest to drink too much and say *the old gods* in the careful whisper you'd use for a disease.

I sat in the corner with a cup of small beer and did not say anything at all, because I knew something the room didn't.

I'd felt it happen. The night before, at the cracked shrine where the Vessia-child—Wren, her name was Wren—had prayed and been answered, I'd felt a pull behind my sternum. A tug, faint as a fisherman's line, drawing warmth out of me and down, down, into the earth, toward the mountain. I'd told myself it was indigestion. I've told myself a lot of lies over ten years; it's practically a craft.

But now the whole sky was doing what my chest had done. And the arithmetic was simple enough even for a charm-seller: prayer answered, warmth withdrawn. Miracle given, heat collected. Somewhere beneath the mountain, something was keeping books.

And I knew exactly whose handwriting was in that ledger, because I'd signed it.

### Anselm

I have spent thirty years among records, and I will tell you what records are: they are prayers written by accountants. Every line is somebody asking to be remembered, or asking to be owed, or asking to be forgiven, and every archivist learns eventually that the ledger never forgives. It only balances.

So when the snow fell on Highsummer, I did not go out to gawk like everyone else in the Ashcloister. I went down into the record-vaults, took up my lamp, and began to read.

The Order of the Quiet Flame had kept weather-records for four hundred years. Droughts, floods, the long gray winters of the Interregnum—all catalogued, cross-referenced, annotated in the margins by dead men with excellent penmanship. And there, in the oldest folios, in a hand I recognized because I had spent a decade of my life trying to imitate it, I found the precedent.

*In the year of the Gray Famine,* Brother Aldous had written, *the people of the Vale prayed to Vessia for rain, and rain came, though no cloud was seen. That same winter, the ice on the Thornmere advanced a mile. The elders said: nothing is given. Everything is moved.*

Nothing is given. Everything is moved.

I sat back on my stool and let that settle over me like the snow settling over the town above. The old gods were answering again—I had seen it myself, or near enough; I had seen the girl, Wren, with frost in her hair and a look on her face like someone listening through a wall. And if every answered prayer drew its price from somewhere—

I pulled the ledger fragment from my satchel. The one with her name in it. *Jonah Thornbury, storm-caller, one soul, pledged in payment, received in full.* Received by whom? By the court beneath the mountain, the fragment implied, in language so old and so careful that whoever wrote it had clearly been afraid of being understood.

Payment for Harrowgate. Payment for ten thousand lives saved by one woman's storm.

Everything is moved. Ten thousand lives saved—and the warmth to keep them alive drawn out of the world and banked below, accruing interest for ten years while the creditor waited.

I am an old man and a disgraced one, and I have made a profession of believing that paper cannot hurt anyone. But I looked at that fragment, and then at the snow falling past the vault's high slit of a window, and I understood that I was holding the promissory note for the end of summer itself.

There was only one person alive who could read the fine print. She sold charms two streets from the Rest, and she flinched every time I said the word *ledger*, which in an honest woman is as good as a confession.

I put on my coat. Outside, the snow squeaked underfoot, and it was ninety degrees in the shade of the churchyard yew, and the yew was dying. I hadn't noticed until then. Its needles had gone black at the tips, all around, evenly, as though dipped in ink.

Cold doesn't fall from the sky, I thought. Cold rises from underneath.

* * *

### Wren

The gods got louder when the snow came. Like dogs before a storm, except the storm was them.

I sat on the roof of the tannery where nobody bothers you, and I listened. There were more voices now than there used to be. For years it was just Vessia, mostly, murmuring up through the cracks in the world the way water comes up through a floor when the river's high. But now there were others underneath her, older ones, ones with names like stones rolling downhill, and they were all saying the same thing, over and over, in the tone grown-ups use when they think you're asleep:

*Faster. Faster. Faster.*

"They're scared," I told the crow that lives on the tannery ridgepole. The crow didn't answer. Crows are honest that way.

I'd gone back to the shrine every day since the frost-morning. I don't know why. Yes I do. Because when Vessia spoke through the crack, I wasn't just Wren-the-nobody who slept in the tannery loft and stole bread and heard voices. I was a door. Being a door is a terrible thing to be, but it's better than being nothing, and I had been nothing for a long time.

Today the shrine was buried in snow, which was funny, because it was Highsummer, and I dug it out with my bare hands until my fingers went red and then white. And when I pressed my palm to the crack in the stone, Vessia came up through it like cold water, and she showed me things.

She showed me the mountain. Not the outside of it, the gray shoulder of it we all live under the way lice live under a collar, but the inside. A spire, upside-down, hanging root-down into the dark like a stone chandelier, and wrapped around it, floor after floor after floor, a court full of lights that weren't fire. People-shaped lights. Warmth, all of it, hoarded. Enough warmth to make a summer, kept in a cellar where no crop would ever grow.

And she showed me the ledger. I didn't understand the writing but I understood the shape of it—the shape of a thing that eats. Every page a mouth. Every name a meal.

Then she showed me the woman. The charm-seller. Jonah. Standing in the snow in front of her stall with her hands open at her sides like she was waiting to catch something, and around her, where she stood, the snow was melting. Just a little circle of wet cobbles, right where her feet were, like the world couldn't quite bring itself to be cold to her.

Vessia's voice went through me and out my mouth, the way it does, and I said it out loud to the empty street because that's how doors work:

"She paid already. She paid the most. Why do they take from her twice?"

The crack in the stone didn't answer. The gods never answer the questions that matter. They only ever say *faster*.

I climbed down off the shrine and went to find her. Not because I had a plan. Because when everything is ending, you want to stand next to the person the cold makes room for.

* * *

### Jonah

They started coming on the third day of the snow.

Refugees, though from what, nobody could say—the roads out of the hills brought them in ragged lines, farm families with their animals and their grandmothers and their dead carried on cart-beds because the ground was too hard to dig. Black ground. I saw a man kneel in his own field forty miles from here, according to his wife, and pull up a turnip that had frozen in the earth in Highsummer, and he'd knelt there a long time before he got up.

They filled the Rest, the granary, the undercroft of the ruined chapel. They told stories, and the stories all had the same bones.

"A girl in our village prayed at the old shrine," said a drover with frostbitten ears. "Prayed for her da's fever to break. It broke. Next morning our well was froze solid, forty feet down."

"We prayed for rain in the spring," said a grandmother, "and it rained, and our barley came in gold, and then the frost took it in a single night, every stalk, in June."

"The cold follows the praying," said a boy, maybe twelve, with the flat certainty children use for things adults are too polite to see. "Everyone knows it now. So we stopped praying. We smashed the shrines." He held up his hands, blistered from it. "Doesn't matter. Somebody somewhere's always praying."

Somebody somewhere's always praying. I turned my cup in my hands and thought about the luck-knots I'd sold that morning, each one tied with a whispered wish worked into the cord, and wondered—with a nausea that had nothing to do with the beer—whether a charm-seller's knots counted as prayers. Whether I'd been feeding it myself, all these years, one copper at a time.

Anselm found me at the corner table. He didn't ask to sit; old men who carry ledgers stop asking permission somewhere along the way. He set the fragment down between us, and the room's noise seemed to lean away from it.

"You've seen the fields," he said.

"I've seen them."

"Crops blackening from the roots up. Wells freezing from the bottom. The cold isn't falling, Jonah. It's being *drawn*. Up out of the deep places, out of the soil's own warmth, and—" he tapped the fragment once, lightly, like knocking on a door he hoped wouldn't open, "—down into the Spire. Every miracle since the shrines woke has been paid for out of the world's own heat. And the interest is compounding."

"How long?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"If the prayers continue at this rate? The old records suggest—" He stopped himself, which was a kindness. Old men who carry ledgers know when a number is a cruelty.

Outside the Rest's fogged windows, the snow kept falling out of a cloudless sky. A refugee child pressed her palm against the glass and left a print, and I watched the print frost over from the edges in, whiting out five small fingers one by one.

"It's my debt," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Harrowgate. The storm. Whatever bought those ten thousand lives, the Spire's been holding the warmth of it for ten years, and now the shrines have woken and the gods are drawing on the account like it's theirs to spend." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I broke a siege and I'm still paying for it. Everyone's paying for it. Forever, apparently, unless—"

"Unless," Anselm agreed quietly.

"Unless what? Say it. You've been circling it for three days."

He folded his hands on the table, priest's hands, archive-keeper's hands, and looked at me with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself in mirrors for a decade: the face of a man about to recommend a thing he hates.

"The ledger," he said. "Your name is in it. Which means the terms of it are legible—to you, if to anyone. Debts can be read, Jonah. And what can be read can sometimes be—" he chose the word with visible care, "—unwritten. Beneath the mountain. Where it's kept."

The room went distant. The child's frosted handprint, the refugees' murmur, the impossible snow ticking against the glass—all of it faded, and there was just the road, the pilgrim road, the one that goes up into the mountain's roots and does not come back, lying across my future like a fallen tree.

Ten years I had built a life the size of a coin purse. Small enough to hide. Small enough to survive. And now the whole sky was foreclosing on it.

"I sold my soul once," I said. "It didn't take. What makes you think I'll get the rest of me back either?"

"Because," said Anselm, "this time you'd be going to collect rather than to pay. It changes the negotiation."

Behind him, the tavern door banged open, and the cold came in with the snow, and with the cold came a girl—feral, frost-haired, barefoot on a frozen threshold without so much as a shiver—walking straight toward our table through a crowd that parted for her the way water parts around a keel.

She stopped in front of me. She looked at me with eyes that had weather in them.

"He's coming," she said. "The one who testified. He's coming up the south road with the crown's seal on his saddlebags. I heard it in the cracks." Her head tilted. "You need to decide what you are before he gets here, Jonah Thornbury. Because he remembers what you were."

The snow ticked against the windows. Somewhere under my feet, under the town, under the roots of the mountain, something vast turned a page.
