## 8. Cassian at the Gate

The crown's envoy arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were market days. Market days were the only days I made money, and I resented having to share even the weather with important people.

He came up the Cinderhollow road with six outriders in crown livery—gray and gold, the colors of a government that had never once thought about us except when deciding what to take—and a seventh rider who wore no livery at all, just a plain traveling cloak with the hood back, because he had always been vain enough to want his face remembered.

I knew him from forty yards. That was the humiliating part. Ten years, half a continent, a legal erasure of everything I'd ever done, and my body still recognized Cassian Vane before my brain could issue the order not to.

I was mid-transaction. Widow Serrit was buying her third luck-knot of the season, holding it up to the light like a jeweler, though we both knew luck-knots were string and superstition and the only real magic in them was the two coppers they cost.

"Jonah," she said. "You've gone pale."

"I'm always pale. It's called complexion."

"You've gone *paler*."

She turned to follow my gaze down the street, and I caught her elbow before she could crane like a goose. "Envoy from the capital," I said. "Nothing to do with us. Buy your knot or don't."

She bought the knot. She pressed an extra copper into my hand, which was its own small tragedy—Widow Serrit, who counted her coins twice and apologized to them when she spent them, tipping me because she thought I was frightened. I was. But not of the envoy.

The procession clattered into the square and stopped at the reeve's house, because that was where processions stopped, and for about ten minutes I let myself believe that was all it would be. A crown man come to count heads, assess taxes, remind us we existed at the crown's pleasure. Happened every few years. You fed them, lied to them politely, and swept up their hoofprints after.

Then the envoy dismounted, and handed his reins to no one, and stood in the middle of the market square looking around at the stalls and the smoke-stained eaves and the cracked shrine at the corner—the one with frost still ghosting its stone if you knew where to look—and he said, clearly, in a voice built for courtrooms:

"I'm looking for a charm-seller. They tell me there's one worth the walk."

Every head in the square turned toward my stall. Thank you, Cinderhollow. Loyalty of iron, this town.

I began packing my wares with the unhurried hands of a woman with nothing to hide, which is the fastest way to pack when you have everything to hide. Luck-knots into the basket. Rain-charms—I sold those as "damp-proofing, folk remedy, no refunds"—into the satchel. And then he was standing in front of me, and there was nowhere left to put my eyes except his face.

Ten years had been fair to him in the way that a river is fair to a stone: worn him smoother, taken some edges off, left him recognizable and slightly wrong. Gray at the temples now. A scar through one eyebrow I didn't remember giving him occasion to earn. He looked tired in the deep way, the way you get tired in the bones and stop mentioning it.

"Jonah Thornbury," he said.

"No such person," I said. "I'm Mara. Mara sells charms. Mara has never broken a siege or anything else."

Something moved through his face—pain, maybe, or the effort of not smiling, and I hated that I could still read him, that the alphabet of his expressions hadn't changed in a decade. "Mara, then." He glanced at the stall, at the knots and the little clay beads and the bundles of dried herbs. "Business looks quiet."

"It's Cinderhollow. Quiet is the local industry."

"May I buy something?"

"You may not. We're closed."

"Mara." He said it gently, and gentleness from him was worse than cruelty would have been, because cruelty I'd had ten years' practice hating. "We should talk. Not here."

"There's nothing to talk about. You testified. I was exiled. The paperwork was very thorough. I read it several times during the parts where I was being branded."

The brand. His eyes flicked down to my wrists, where the sleeves covered the scars, and I saw him register the flinch I hadn't quite hidden, and I saw him carry it somewhere behind his face and set it down carefully, like a man pocketing a debt.

"The frost," he said quietly. "At the shrine on Tanner's Row. Three nights ago. The whole street bloomed white, in high summer, and every Graycloak from here to the coast is trying to work out how. And I need to reach the Spire before they do, and I can't do that alone, and neither—" his voice caught, barely, "—neither can you."

"I'm doing fine."

"You're selling string."

"It's very good string."

* * *

He wouldn't say more in the square, and I wouldn't go anywhere with him, so we reached the compromise of the alley behind the tannery, which smelled like a dead god and offered privacy at a reasonable rate. He leaned against the wall. I kept my back to the street and my hands visible, old habits from a life I wasn't supposed to have lived.

"Talk," I said.

"The crown has opened negotiations with the court beneath the mountain."

I laughed. It came out ugly. "There's nothing to negotiate. The Spire doesn't trade. It collects."

"It's trading now." He said it flatly, and I watched him choose each word like a man crossing ice. "Envoys have gone in and come out. Myself among them. The buried king wants—recognition. Legitimacy. Borders. And in exchange..." He looked at the ground. "In exchange, the winter stops at the mountain's shadow. The crown keeps its harvests. Its cities. Its people."

"And gives him what?"

"Souls." He said it the way you'd say a word you'd practiced saying until it stopped tasting like anything. "Voluntary ones. Debtors. Criminals. The desperate. There are forms now, Jonah. Crown forms. I helped draft them."

The alley tilted slightly. Or I did. I put a hand on the wall and pretended I'd meant to.

"You helped draft them."

"Somebody was going to. At least I made the terms survivable. Term limits. Visitation rights for—" He stopped himself. "It doesn't matter. What matters is the Graycloaks. They're not negotiating. They think the answered prayers are heresy, and they're right, and they're going to keep burning shrines and hanging hedge-witches until either the cold kills everyone or they do. And the prayers aren't stopping. Something under the mountain is answering them, and every answer makes the winter worse, and nobody in power will say that sentence out loud because saying it means admitting the gods came back and they're hungry."

"And this is my problem because—"

"Because you know the Spire better than any living soul." He almost smiled at that. Almost. "Poor choice of words. Because you *owe* it. Because whatever's happening, whatever Vessia is doing through that child everyone's whispering about—you're the thread that runs through all of it, and I have spent ten years learning how that court thinks, and I cannot get into its heart without you, and I am asking."

"Asking."

"Asking."

"Not ordering. Not testifying. Not calling the Graycloaks down on me the moment I'm useful."

His jaw tightened. "No."

"Then here's my counteroffer." I stepped closer, close enough to see that his eyes were the same brown they'd always been, warm as hearth-coal, the eyes I'd once trusted more than my own name. "You can go to hell. Take the mountain road; it's shorter."

"Jonah—"

"You testified." My voice did something I didn't authorize. Cracked, or nearly. "They asked you one question, Cassian. One. *Did Jonah Thornbury pledge a soul to the buried king to end the siege.* All you had to do was say yes. Say yes, and explain why, and maybe—maybe—they exile me anyway, but they understand, and I don't spend ten years as a legal ghost with scars on my wrists. Instead you stood in that hall and you said, *she bargained willingly, for power, and I heard her do it,* and you let them believe I sold ten thousand lives for ambition. You let them believe I was a monster who got lucky."

"I know what I said."

"Do you?" I was shaking now, and furious about shaking. "Do you actually? Because I have replayed that day every night for ten years and I still can't make it make sense. I saved them, Cassian. Harrowgate stood. Ten thousand people ate breakfast the next morning because I called a storm that should have killed me, and the price was mine to pay, and I paid it, and you—" 

"I know," he said.

"You *know*. Wonderful. That fixes everything."

"I know," he said again, quieter, "because I was there. Because I held your hands while you shook afterward. Because I carried you out of the storm-circle when your legs gave out." His composure was cracking now, hairline fractures running through the court-polish, and some vicious animal part of me leaned in to watch. "And then three weeks later they put my sister's name in front of me and told me she was already in the ledger—that she'd sold herself years before, for her children's fevers, the way the poor have always sold themselves—and they told me the only thing standing between her and collection was my cooperation. So I cooperated. And I have cooperated every day since, and every day I have hated myself in a new and innovative way, and none of it changes what I said in that hall, and none of it un-brands you, and I did not come here for forgiveness."

"No?"

"I came here because the world is ending and you're the only person alive who might be able to do something about it, and I would rather you hate me and live than forgive me and freeze." He pushed off the wall. Up close, in the tannery stink, he suddenly looked less like an envoy and more like what he'd been at twenty-five: a man in over his head, pretending otherwise. "But I'll tell you one true thing before you send me off. Every soul I could reach, I've bought back. Quietly. For ten years. Forty-one of them. Your sister-in-law's mother. The miller's boy. I keep a list. It's the only prayer I know how to say anymore."

Forty-one. The number went through me like a splinter, too deep to dig out. Because it was exactly the kind of thing he'd do, and that was the trap of Cassian Vane: the betrayal and the decency braided so tight you couldn't cut one without cutting the other.

"My name isn't on your list," I said.

"No," he agreed softly. "Yours isn't the kind you can buy back."

He left me then, walking back toward the square and the reeve's house and his six outriders, and I stood in the alley between the tanning vats and hated him with the specific, exhausted hatred of a woman who has just discovered the wound never closed—it had only scabbed over thin enough to see light through.

Because here is what I didn't say, what I will probably never say, what I shoved down under ten years of scar tissue where it can't hurt anyone: when I saw him ride into the square, before the anger arrived, for one whole unguarded second I had felt *glad.*

That's the unforgivable part. Not what he did. That he could still do that to me.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat up with a lamp and my satchel of harmless string and listened to the wind coming off the mountain, and around the third hour I admitted the obvious: he hadn't come alone, and he wouldn't leave quickly, and a crown envoy in Cinderhollow asking after charm-sellers was a lit fuse whether I touched it or not.

Around the fourth hour, someone scratched at my shutters.

I knew the knock-code before I'd fully decided to answer it—three quick, one slow—which told me things about Anselm the defrocked priest that I found touching and alarming in equal measure. I opened the window. He stood below in the dark with his ledger-fragment wrapped in oilcloth and an expression like a man arriving at a fire he'd predicted.

"He's asked the reeve for you by name," Anselm said. "Not Mara. *Thornbury.*"

My stomach dropped through the floorboards. "He swore—"

"He didn't swear anything. You assumed. Priests notice assumptions; it's most of the job." He held up the oilcloth bundle. "And there's more. I've been reading the fragment again tonight. The payment date on your soul—the night of Harrowgate—it's not marked *paid.* It's marked *held.* And there's a second entry beneath it, newer ink, same hand. A lien."

"A what?"

"A claim of interest. Jonah—the court hasn't just been keeping your soul. It's been *lending it out.*" He glanced over his shoulder at the dark street, at the mountain looming black above the rooftops. "And according to this, the loan was called in last week. By name. Which means somewhere under that mountain, something just decided it wants you back."

Above us, far up the black shoulder of the Spire, a light kindled in a window that had been dark for a thousand years—and every dog in Cinderhollow began, very quietly, to howl.
