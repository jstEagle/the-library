## 9. The Road Beneath

I have spent forty years keeping records, and I will tell you the first thing every archivist learns: the past does not stay where you file it. It seeps. It swells in damp weather. It comes up through the floorboards of your life like water through a cellar wall, and no amount of cataloguing will keep it down.

The second thing every archivist learns is that when the past comes for you, it is best to be walking toward it rather than away. Away, it catches you at a disadvantage. Toward, at least you can see its face.

So it was that I found myself, three days out of Cinderhollow, walking down into the roots of the world with a storm-caller who had forgotten how to call storms, a child who spoke in borrowed voices, and a man I had every intention of hating until he gave me a reason not to.

The pilgrim road to the mountain had not been walked in earnest since before the Edict. In the old days—Anselm, you will say, everything is "the old days" with you—in the old days, ten thousand souls a year came down this road on their knees, or near enough, to leave offerings at the shrines cut into the rock. The Order of the Quiet Flame kept the lamps lit. The Order of the Quiet Flame kept a great many things lit, once, and I was the last of them, and I carried our remaining lamp now, swinging on a staff, because I am an old man and the dark beneath the world deserves better than a candle stub.

"Your light's wrong," Wren said.

She was walking ahead of us as she always did, barefoot on stone that would have blistered any other child, her head cocked like a bird listening for worms. She had refused boots. Jonah had offered her boots twice, then a cloak, then half a honey-bun, and Wren had accepted only the honey-bun, which she ate in the manner of someone who has known hunger intimately and intends never to be surprised by it again.

"The light is a consecrated flame," I said. "It has burned without interruption for two hundred years."

"That's why it's wrong." She stopped and looked back at me with those eyes of hers, gray as slate weather. "It remembers being a temple fire. Down there, things remember too. They'll come to it."

"To the light?"

"To what it remembers." She resumed walking. "You should've brought a stupid flame."

Behind me, Cassian made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't died somewhere in his throat years ago. He walked at the rear, which was where Jonah wanted him, which was where he insisted on being anyway—a rare point of agreement between two people otherwise engaged in a slow war conducted entirely through silence.

"You could have told her," Cassian said quietly, falling into step beside me. "That the flame was blessed against exactly this."

"I did tell her."

"You told her it was consecrated. You didn't tell her what it was consecrated *for*."

I adjusted my grip on the staff. "She knows what we're walking into. Telling her more would only frighten her."

"She's eight."

"She's a crack in the world," I said, "and cracks let through whatever presses hardest. Fear presses hard. So do the dead." I glanced back at him. "You of all people should understand that, Master Vael. You've been pressed by both."

He flinched. Good. I confess I took a small, mean pleasure in it. An archivist's pleasures are small and mean; we take them where we find them.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a waystation, one of the Order's old rest-houses, its roof fallen in and its hearth cold for longer than I had been alive. The walls still bore the marks of pilgrims—names scratched into stone, small prayers, the occasional obscenity, because piety and profanity have always traveled together like an old married couple.

Jonah built no fire. She sat apart, sharpening a knife she had no practical use for, and I watched her watch the road behind us.

"They'll have found the shrine by now," she said, without looking at me. "The drowned one. Where Vessia spoke through the girl. Graycloaks read traces like priests read scripture."

"Then they are three days behind us."

"Graycloaks don't tire."

"No," I agreed. "But they follow rules. And rules can be broken by people willing to pay for the breaking." I settled myself onto a bench worn smooth by centuries of other backsides. "I burned the archive, Jonah. All of it. Forty years of records—the ledgers, the correspondence, the confessions. Every debt the Order ever held."

She looked at me then. "Why?"

"Because a ledger in the hands of the Spire's court is a weapon, and a ledger in the hands of the crown is a leash, and I decided I was done furnishing either." I smiled, and felt how thin it was. "Also because I am a coward, and burning them meant no one could ever make me testify about what was in them."

"What was in them?"

"Names." I looked into the dark beyond the ruined roof, where the stars were coming out, indifferent as ever. "Mostly names."

Wren was asleep—or appeared to be—curled against Cassian's pack with the shameless ease of children and cats. Jonah watched her a long moment.

"If the Graycloaks catch us," she said, "they won't take Wren to a cell. They'll take her to the Spire. A god-touched child is worth more to the court than a hundred souls."

"I know."

"And you—an archivist of a defunct order—they'll want to know what you burned, and why, and who put you up to it."

"No one put me up to it. That's what will infuriate them."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, come to pay respects. "And Cassian? What do they do to envoys who defect?"

"Envoy," I said carefully, "is a word that implies he defected. As far as I know, he told the court he was negotiating a surrender."

Jonah went very still. "A surrender of what?"

"Of you." I said it plainly, because plainness is the last kindness left to old men. "He bought your safety from the Graycloaks by promising the court he'd deliver the storm-caller to the throne room alive. Whether he intends to keep that promise—" I spread my hands. "I keep records, not prophecies."

She was quiet for a long time. The knife kept moving against the whetstone, slow and even, like a heartbeat deciding whether to continue.

"He'd sell me again," she said finally. Not a question. A conclusion, drawn and filed.

"He sold you once to save his sister. People repeat their solutions, Jonah. It's the great tragedy of the species." I rose, joints cracking like green wood. "But I watched him carry the girl four miles today when she pretended to sleep so she wouldn't have to admit her feet hurt. Draw your own conclusions. Just—file them provisionally. That's all any of us deserve. Provisional filing."

* * *

The dreams started on the fourth night.

I dreamed of the archive. Of course I did—I dream of the archive most nights, the shelves stretching away into darkness, the smell of vellum and lamp oil. But in this dream the shelves were empty, every one, and in the middle of the reading room stood a figure in gray robes with its back to me, sorting through ash.

"You burned them," it said. Its voice was the voice of every superior I had ever obeyed. Prior Malden. Prior Ossory. The old woman who taught me my letters and beat the stammer out of me. "Forty years of memory, and you burned them."

"They were being used as chains," I said.

"Memory is always chains. That is what memory is *for*." The figure turned, and it had no face, only a smooth gray blankness, and yet I knew—I knew with absolute certainty—that it was smiling. "Come home, Anselm. The Order forgives. The Order always forgives. That is the trap of it."

I woke with frost on my blanket inside a sealed room, and Wren sitting cross-legged at my feet, watching me.

"It wasn't a dream," she said.

"Wren—"

"It wore faces at you. Old ones. Teachers." She tilted her head. "It couldn't find yours. It tried mine first but I'm mostly holes, so it fell through." She reached out and touched my knee with one small hand. "They're on the road behind us. Three of them. They walk like the dead but they aren't. They're wearing the dead."

I got up and woke the others, and we stood in the ruins of the waystation while the wind came down off the mountain carrying a smell like snow and old coins.

"How far?" Jonah asked.

"A night," Wren said. "Maybe less. They don't stop. Stopping is a thing bodies do."

"Can your flame stop them?" Cassian asked me.

"My flame can make them hesitate. Hesitation is not stopping." I looked at each of them in turn. "There is a side path, half a mile on. The Order used it for... retrievals. When a pilgrim died on the descent and the body needed bringing home. It bypasses the main switchbacks and drops straight through the old offering galleries. Narrow, dark, and consecrated ground entire—the whole passage was walled with votive stones. Nothing wearing a stolen face walks easily through two thousand years of prayer."

"And the cost?" Jonah asked, because she was the sort of person who understood instinctively that everything has one.

"The cost is that the galleries descend through the mountain's grief," I said. "The Order sealed them for a reason. Pilgrims heard things in there. Voices of their dead, real or otherwise. Some came out mad. Some didn't come out at all." I paused. "And the bottom opens onto the outer approaches of the court itself. There is no turning aside once you commit. The path goes down."

"Everything goes down," Wren said, with the flat certainty of a child stating the weather. "That's the whole problem with under."

* * *

We took the side path at dawn, such as dawn was under the mountain's shoulder—a graying of the dark, nothing more. Behind us, on the switchbacks above, I saw them: three figures in gray, moving with the terrible economy of things that have no need to hurry because they have never once been late.

They did not pursue. They simply stood, at the edge of the cliff-path, and watched us go. And though they were too distant to show faces, I felt—archivist's intuition, or something older and less comfortable—that they were smiling.

"They know where we're going," Cassian said.

"Everyone knows where we're going," Jonah said. "That's rather the point of a pilgrimage."

The side path took us down through the bones of the mountain almost immediately. The air changed—cooler, wetter, tasting of minerals and something sweeter underneath, like flowers rotting in a locked room. My lamp-flame leaned steadily toward the depths, as if pulled, and I corrected it with my hand and pretended not to notice.

The votive stones began within the hour. The walls of the gallery were faced with them, thousands upon thousands, each carved with a name or a plea or a simple mark meaning *I was here, and I grieved, and someone saw it.* Two millennia of human sorrow, mortared into place. It should have been suffocating. Instead it was the opposite—it was the quietest place I had ever stood, because grief, given enough company, stops being noise and becomes architecture.

Wren ran her fingers along the stones as she passed, reading them the way other children read shop signs. "This one lost a daughter. This one lost a harvest. This one—" she stopped. "This one's mine."

We all stopped.

She was standing before a stone no different from the others, worn nearly smooth, bearing a single scratched mark: a spiral, the old sign for *returned*. Below it, letters too faint for my old eyes, though Wren traced them without touching: *For Wren, who came back wrong, and whom we loved anyway.*

"When was this carved?" Jonah asked softly.

"Before," Wren said. "Before me." She shrugged, the shrug of a child shouldering something enormous and pretending it was a satchel. "Somebody loved somebody who came back wrong before. And somebody carved it so the next wrong thing would know it wasn't the first." She patted the stone, once, gently, the way you'd pat a dog. "Okay," she told it. And walked on.

None of us spoke for a long while after that. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

* * *

The voices began at the third gallery.

At first I mistook them for echoes—our own footsteps returning distorted from some deeper chamber. But echoes do not say your name. Echoes do not use the voice of a novice you buried thirty years ago, a boy called Tam who died of fever in his first winter of service and whose funeral I had performed myself, badly, because I was young and frightened and had never liked him much.

"*Anselm.*"

I gripped the lamp-staff and kept walking. "Don't answer them," I said. "Whatever you hear. Don't answer, don't look, don't—"

"*Anselm, it's cold here. Why did you burn us? Why did you burn the warm places?*"

"Tam is dead," I said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "Tam died in the winter of the Year of Ash, and I buried him, and this is not him."

"*But you remember my voice,*" the dark said, kindly, horribly. "*That's all we need. Just the remembering. Give us the remembering and you can keep the rest.*"

Beside me, Cassian had gone rigid. Whatever the voices were saying to him, he had clamped his jaw shut over it, and I saw a muscle jumping in his cheek like something trying to get out. Jonah walked with her hand fisted in Wren's collar—not dragging her, anchoring her—and Wren's lips were moving, answering voices only she could hear, in languages that predated the Edict, that predated perhaps language itself.

And then, from the dark ahead, a voice that stopped my heart:

"*Sebastian?*"

No. Forgive me. I misremembered—no, worse, I *invented*, which is the archivist's original sin. Let me set the record straight, as I have spent my life trying to do: the voice from the dark ahead was a woman's voice, young, and it said:

"*Cassian. Cassian, it's Sera. I'm cold, Cassian. You promised you'd buy me back. You promised.*"

Cassian broke.

I will give him this—he broke quietly. No scream, no collapse. He simply turned, all at once, like a door blown open, and started walking back up the gallery toward the voice, and Jonah caught him by the arm and spun him around and hit him across the face with an open hand, hard enough to ring off the stone walls.

"Sera is alive," she said, low and fierce. "Sera is alive because you sold your soul buying hers back piece by piece, and whatever that is, it isn't her, and if you go to it, it will wear her forever and you will be its hands."

Cassian stood there, bleeding a little at the lip where his own teeth had cut it, breathing like a man surfacing. "How do you know?" he whispered. "How does anyone know? How do we know any of us are—"

"We don't," Jonah said. "We choose anyway. That's the whole of it. That's all faith ever was."

The voice called again—*Cassian, please*—and then, finding no purchase, changed tactics, and called in my voice, and in Jonah's, and last of all in Wren's, high and sweet: *Mama? Mama, I'm down here, why did you leave me down here?*

Wren laughed at it. Actually laughed, a child's bright scornful laugh. "You're doing it wrong," she told the dark. "I never had a mama. You have to use true things or they don't stick." And the dark, rebuffed, went quiet around us like a crowd embarrassed at being caught.

We walked on. My hand shook so badly the lamp-light shivered on the votive stones, two thousand years of grief flickering like a living thing, and I thought: *this is what commitment is. Not the vow at the start. This. The middle of the dark, with your dead calling your name, and you keep walking.*

* * *

Near the bottom, the gallery opened without warning into a vast space, and my flame—my faithful, two-hundred-year flame—bent double and went out.

Darkness, total. Then, slowly, a light that was not light: a glow rising from below, gold shot through with blue, beautiful the way frost on a dying crop is beautiful. The frozen radiance of the court beneath the mountain, spilling up the throat of the world.

"There it is," Wren said. She sounded tired, suddenly, terribly tired, an old soul in a young body looking at a long-owed bill. "The Small Mercies."

Jonah stood at the edge of the gallery with the dark behind her and that false gold rising on her face, and I saw her square her shoulders the way people do before doors they cannot unopen.

"Once we go down," I said, "there is no path back up. The galleries seal behind pilgrims. It was designed that way—to keep the grieving from fleeing their own grief halfway through."

"I know," Jonah said. "I helped design it."

I stared at her. "What?"

"Harrowgate's engineers consulted on the deep roads after the siege. Reinforcing the approaches. Sealing the retreats." Her voice was flat, matter-of-fact, and utterly hollow underneath. "I signed the plans, Anselm. Ten years ago. I sealed these galleries myself—with the storm-magic they'd already taken payment for." She laughed, one short bitter note. "Every road into that court, I built the locks on. And now the locks are going to close behind me, and the only key is whatever's waiting at the bottom."

Above us, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong to the mountain: the scrape of gray robes on votive stone. The three watchers had entered the gallery. They were not hurrying. They had never once been late.

"Then we'd best meet them at the bottom," Cassian said, "rather than at our backs."

Jonah looked at each of us—me with my dead lamp, Cassian with his split lip, Wren with her hole-riddled soul and her ancient patience—and whatever she was looking for, she found it, or found enough of it.

"Down, then," she said.

And we went down, the four of us, into the gold-and-blue glow of the Court of Small Mercies, while behind us in the dark, unhurried, patient as arithmetic, the things wearing the faces of the dead followed us down.
