## 10. Vessia's Hunger

The thing about being a crack in the world is that nobody asks the crack how it feels about things leaking through.

I'm Wren. I'm nine years old, or maybe ten — I stopped counting when counting started to feel like something the Graycloaks could use against me. I have hair the color of river mud and eyes that people don't like to look at for too long, because my left one has a frost-flower in it that blooms when the gods are close, and people can tell when a god is looking back at them out of a child's face. It makes them drop things. It makes them pray, which is funny, because praying is what got everyone into this mess in the first place.

Jonah says I talk too much. Anselm says I talk *precisely* too much, which he thinks is different. Cassian doesn't say anything, mostly, which is how you can tell he's thinking about running away.

But this part isn't about them. This part is about the shrine, and the water, and the night I stopped being just a girl who hears voices and became a girl who gets *used*.

* * *

We found the drowned shrine on the third day of walking down the pilgrim road, if you can call it walking when half of it was climbing down over roots the size of houses. The road used to be paved with white stones, Anselm said, back when pilgrims came by the thousand to beg favors from the old gods. Now the stones were black and slick and most of them had slid into the dark, and the forest above us had gone quiet in the wrong way — not peaceful-quiet, but held-breath-quiet, like the whole mountain was listening to us.

"It should be here," Anselm kept saying, checking his little book of records against landmarks that weren't there anymore. "The Shrine of Still Water. There was a spring. Pilgrims would float candles on it."

"There's your spring," Jonah said, and pointed with her chin.

It wasn't a spring anymore. It was a lake — no, that's too nice a word. It was a flooded hollow, black water lying flat as glass between the roots, and rising out of the middle of it was the shrine itself: a ring of standing stones with a dome of carved rock on top, sunk to its shoulders. The carvings were faces. Hundreds of faces, all looking up at the surface, all wearing expressions like they'd been interrupted mid-sentence. Water lapped at their chins. Their mouths were below the waterline, so from where we stood on the bank it looked like they were holding their breath forever.

"I don't love this," I said, because somebody has to say these things or nobody does.

"You don't love anything," Jonah said. "You didn't love the inn. You didn't love the bridge. You didn't love the perfectly good apples."

"The apples had worms."

"They had *one* worm. Between four apples."

"A worm is a covenant," I told her. "You bite an apple with a worm in it, you've made an agreement with the tree that you'll take the bad with the good. That's old law. Vessia's law, probably." I said it lightly, the way you do, and then felt the frost-flower in my eye go cold, and knew I'd said something truer than I meant to.

Jonah went still the way she goes still — everything stopping at once, like a clock someone's hand is pressed against. "Wren," she said carefully. "What did you just hear?"

"Nothing," I said.

"Wren."

"Nothing *yet*," I amended, because lying to Jonah is like lying to weather. She can't always tell what's coming, but she can tell the pressure's changed.

Cassian had his hand on his sword already, which is his answer to everything, including soup. Anselm was reading his book by lantern-light, lips moving. And I stood on the black bank of the drowned shrine with my toes hanging over the water, and the frost-flower bloomed wide open, and I heard her.

Not words first. Never words first. First it's like standing next to a door in winter — you feel the cold coming off the gap before you see the light. Then it's like hearing your own name sung underwater. Then, only then, if she really wants to, Vessia speaks.

*"Little crack,"* she said. *"Come closer. I cannot reach you across the water. The water remembers being holy, and it holds that memory against me."*

"I'm not coming closer," I said out loud, because the others needed to hear my side of it even though they couldn't hear hers. "Last time you borrowed me, I woke up three miles away with snow in my boots and no memory of Tuesday."

*"Tuesday,"* said Vessia, and there was something in her voice I'd never heard before, something that took me a long moment to recognize because I'd never heard a god be afraid. *"You keep such small accounts, little one. Tuesdays. Boots. Do you know what I would give to have a Tuesday again? To be small enough to fit inside one?"*

That scared me more than anger would have. Angry gods are just big wolves — you run, you hide, maybe you get eaten, but the rules are simple. A god who wants to be small again is a drowning woman looking for something to hold onto, and I am very holdable. I'm small. That's practically my whole job description.

"She's here," I said to the others. My voice came out strange, stretched. "Vessia. At the shrine. She's — " I put my hand flat against my own chest, because that's what it feels like, someone else's hand pressed against the inside of you. "She's *leaning* on me."

Jonah crossed the bank fast and grabbed my shoulder. Her hands are rough and warm and they always smell faintly of the oils she uses on charm-knots, cedar and iron, and I hate how much I love that smell, because it means safe, and nothing that means safe stays safe. "Tell her no," Jonah said. "Whatever she wants. Tell her no."

*"Storm-caller,"* Vessia said, and now I could hear her attention slide off me like oil off wet stone, onto Jonah, and the whole lake shivered without any wind. *"You sold yourself cheaply once. Do not sell the girl's mouth the same way. I only want to speak. I have wanted only to speak for a hundred years, and every door in the world has been shut to me except this small broken child, and even she flinches from me, and I do not blame her, I do not blame anyone anymore, I only want—"*

And then the leaning became a pushing, and I was going under.

* * *

Here is what it's like, since everyone always asks and nobody believes the answer: it's like being a coat.

You're still there. You're still warm-ish, still shaped like yourself, still hanging on your own hooks. But someone else is inside you, moving your arms, and their arms are longer than yours, and when they move your mouth the words come out in a voice like ice cracking on a lake in early spring — that deep groaning boom, the sound the lake makes when it's deciding whether to be solid or not.

My body walked into the water.

I want that written down somewhere, in case anyone ever thinks I chose it: **my body walked into the water and I did not tell it to.** The black water climbed my legs, my waist, my chest, cold enough to burn, and my body didn't shiver because it wasn't mine to make shiver. It walked until the water was at my collarbones and the nearest carved face was right in front of me, its drowned mouth an inch below the surface, and then my head tipped back and my mouth opened and Vessia spoke through me to the world.

Except — and this is the part I need everyone to understand — she wasn't speaking *to* the world. She was speaking to Jonah. Only Jonah. Everything she said was aimed at that one woman standing on the bank with her scarred wrists hidden under her sleeves like always, like we don't all know, like hiding them helps.

*"Jonah Thornbury,"* said my mouth, in the lake-cracking voice, and the word *Thornbury* made ripples. Actual ripples, rings spreading out across black water from where I stood, as if my voice had weight. *"They erased your name from every record in the kingdom. They burned the rolls, they unmade the songs, they taught children to say 'the storm came by itself.' But names do not die. Names sink. And I am very old, and I remember where everything sinks."*

"Let her go," Jonah said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't; I could see them shaking at her sides, and I catalogued that, filed it away in the part of me that was still me, floating up near the ceiling of myself like a moth trapped in a chapel. Jonah Thornbury scared. Good. Someone should be writing this down besides me.

*"I am letting her go,"* Vessia said. *"She floats. I hold. There is a difference, storm-caller, and you of all people should know it — you held a storm once. Did it drown you?"*

"What do you want?" Jonah asked. And then, quieter, in the voice she uses when she's asking the real question underneath: "Why now? Why after ten years of silence? What changed?"

And Vessia laughed.

I have heard a lot of laughs. Mean laughs, kind laughs, the fake laugh Anselm does when Cassian makes one of his jokes that aren't jokes. But a god laughing through your own throat is none of those. It comes up from below your ribs like water coming up through a floor, and it hurts, and it sounds almost like crying, and I understood that for Vessia those were the same thing and had been for a hundred years.

*"What changed,"* she said, *"is that the doors are opening. All of them. Everywhere. Every answered prayer is a key turning. Can you not feel it, even you? The world growing colder. The warmth draining south and down, down, down, into the dark beneath this mountain, into the court of the buried king, who drinks heat the way his jailers once drank wine."* My arm rose out of the water and pointed — my finger, my crooked little finger with the scar from the fox trap, pointing past Jonah, past Anselm and Cassian, down the road, down and down toward the roots of the Spire. *"He holds our cage, storm-caller. He has held it for a hundred years. We were not sleeping in our shrines. We were imprisoned in them. And now the locks are failing, and we are getting out, and I will tell you what you do not want to hear, because unlike my siblings I have spent a century learning honesty the way other creatures learn patience:"*

The lake went absolutely still. Even the ripples from my voice stopped, frozen mid-ring, and the frost-flower in my eye bloomed so wide I could see its edges, petals of white creeping into my vision like frost crossing a window.

*"We are not returning to you. Everyone prays for the return of the gods. The priests wrote it in their books: the gods will come back, the gods will restore the balance, the gods will forgive. No. We are escaping. And an escape is not a homecoming, Jonah Thornbury. An escape is a fire. We will come up through the cracks of the world burning everything that binds us — every ledger, every contract, every debt, every shrine-wall, every oath sworn to us by kings who thought we were furniture. And your world, your small warm world of Tuesdays and boots and apples with worms in them —"* and here the voice faltered, cracked, went almost young, almost like mine — *"your world is made entirely of the things that bind us. Do you see? To free ourselves we must unmake the walls. And the walls are everything."*

Nobody said anything. Somewhere behind me I heard Anselm's book close, very softly, the way you close a book when the news in it is worse than the news you were pretending to look up.

"That's not rescue," Jonah said finally. "That's just a bigger siege."

*"Yes,"* said Vessia, simply, horribly. *"Now you understand why I liked you. You were the only mortal in a hundred years who fought a siege instead of starting one."*

"Why are you telling us this?" Jonah's voice dropped low. "If you're going to burn the world anyway, why warn the girl? Why warn *me*?"

And the god was quiet for a long moment, and I hung in my coat-body in the freezing water and listened to her not-answer, and then she answered, and I wished she hadn't.

*"Because I am not my siblings,"* she said. *"Because I have watched this child dream. Because when the fire starts, there will be a few hours — perhaps a day — when the cage stands open and the king's attention turns upward, toward us, toward the breaking, and his court will be unguarded. And in those hours, storm-caller, a soul might be taken back out of a ledger. A soul sold on the night of Harrowgate. Yours."*

The cold went through me then, deeper than the water, deeper than the god. Because I hadn't known that. Nobody had told me that. Jonah's soul, in the ledger, in the Spire, and here was a goddess offering a window to steal it back — and offering it the way you offer meat to a dog you want to follow you somewhere.

"And what does it cost?" Jonah asked. She's smart. She's the smartest person I know and I will fight anyone who says otherwise. "What do you want me to do in those hours, Vessia?"

*"Hold the door open,"* said the goddess through my mouth, gentle as rot. *"When we break through — and we will break through, with or without you — stand at the crack, storm-caller, and call your storm, and keep the king's gaze turned upward while we pour past him into the sky. That is all. That is everything. Be our storm one last time. Be the thing they exiled you for being. And take your soul out of the fire as payment."*

"And the world?"

The pause lasted long enough for me to count my heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

*"The world,"* said Vessia, *"will be what survives us."*

* * *

Then she let go, all at once, like a hand opening, and I went under.

Real under, this time — my own under, my lungs full of shock and my own panic, which is a much better panic than the borrowed kind, believe me. Hands grabbed me. Jonah's hands, hauling me up and back through the black water, and then I was coughing on the bank with mud in my teeth and my whole body shaking so hard my bones buzzed, and Jonah was holding me against her chest and her heart was going like a rabbit's and she was saying my name, my real name, the one nobody knows, over and over like a charm.

"Wren. Wren. Look at me. Are you in there? Are you *you*?"

"I'm me," I croaked. "Mostly. There's some lake in me too. And some goddess residue. It's very crowded in here."

She laughed — a horrible wet laugh, half sob — and squeezed me hard enough to crack a rib, probably, but I didn't say so.

Anselm wrapped me in his cloak, muttering prayers to an order that didn't exist anymore, which is either pointless or the most faithful thing I've ever seen. Cassian stood apart, staring at the drowned shrine, and his face was doing something complicated. Later I'd learn why. Later I'd learn that Cassian Vale knew exactly what lived in ledgers under mountains, better than any of us. But that night he just stared at the water like it owed him money.

"We're not doing it," Jonah said. She said it to the lake, to the faces in the stones, to the whole listening mountain. "You hear me? We're not your door. We're not your diversion. I'm not calling any storm for you to escape through while the world burns behind you."

The lake didn't answer. But the frost-flower in my eye stayed open, and far away, down the road, down in the dark, I felt something vast and patient shift its attention — the buried king, maybe, or the ledger itself, or just the cold, which by then was the same thing.

"She's lying," I said quietly, into Jonah's cloak. "Partly. About the window. About your soul."

Jonah pulled back and looked at me. "How do you know?"

"Because gods can't give anything back," I said. "Only trade. And she talked about your soul like it was already hers to promise." I wiped my nose. "Also because when she said *hold the door open*, she wasn't talking about the king's attention, Jonah. I felt what she meant. She meant *you*. Your body. Your power. A door has hinges, and hinges are made of something that bends."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Jonah stood up, and pulled her sleeves down over her scars, and looked down the road into the dark where the Spire waited with her soul in its teeth.

"Then we go down faster," she said. "Before the gods get there first."

And that's how we ended up walking into the mountain in the middle of the night, four people and one crack-in-the-world, racing a prison break.
