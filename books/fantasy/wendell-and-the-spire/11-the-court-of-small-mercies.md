## 11. The Court of Small Mercies

The last stretch of the pilgrim road was not a road at all.

It was a throat. That was Anselm's word for it, muttered somewhere behind me as the walls narrowed and the dark went from ordinary dark to the kind that has weight. I preferred his word to mine, which had been *coffin*, so we compromised on *throat* and both pretended we felt better about it.

Wren walked ahead of us without a torch. She didn't need one; she said the cracks in the world glowed down here, faint seams of gold light running through the stone like veins in marble. Cassian carried the only lantern, which meant Cassian carried the only thing any of us would have grabbed first if the walls started closing in, and he knew it, and he held it very carefully, like a man who has finally been trusted with something breakable and is terrified of the responsibility.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," I said, mostly to hear my own voice in the dark.

"Because the ledger holds your soul," Anselm said. "And every other soul the court keeps. And because destroying it voids all debts."

"And the fine print?"

"The fine print," Cassian said quietly, "is that nobody knows what happens when you void ten thousand years of debt. The court doesn't keep records of its own collapse. Courts never do."

Comforting. I filed it away with the rest of the comforting things I'd collected since Cinderhollow: *the gods are escaping, not returning; the winter is collection, not punishment; the man who betrayed you has been secretly buying back souls for a decade.* A whole shelf of it. I'd need a bigger shelf.

The throat opened.

I want to describe it properly, because I have spent my life describing things—storms, mostly, and the faces of people watching storms—and this deserves better than the words I reached for first, which were *beautiful* and then, three heartbeats later, *wrong*.

The outer court of the buried king was a cavern the size of Harrowgate's old quarter, and it was lit. Not by fire. By gold—thin threads of it running through every surface, the same seams Wren had seen above, converging here into something like daylight. Pillars rose out of the floor and vanished into a ceiling so high it might have been sky. Between them stood buildings. Actual buildings, with balconies and window-boxes and laundry strung between upper stories, except the laundry hadn't moved in what looked like centuries, and neither had anything else.

Because everything was frozen.

Not ice-frozen. Time-frozen. The gold light came from the seams, and where it touched, things simply *stopped*. A bird hung mid-flight over a fountain, wings spread, caught forever in the act of landing. Water stood in the fountain's basin in perfect sculpted arcs. And people—

People everywhere. Walking the streets, sitting at tables outside a tavern whose sign read THE QUIET HEARTH, leaning from balconies calling down to friends below. All of them mid-gesture, mid-laugh, mid-life. All of them smiling.

All of them warm-looking, which was the worst part. Down here, beneath a mountain, under a world going gray with stolen heat, they glowed. Their skin had color. Their eyes were bright. Whatever the cold was taking from the world above, some of it ended up here, banked like coins in a vault, and these people were wearing it.

"They're not dead," Wren said. She'd stopped at the edge of the street, head tilted, listening to whatever she always listened to. "They're not even asleep. They're *happy*. They're happy right now, all of them, all at once."

"That's worse," I said.

"I know," she said, and she was nine years old, or thereabouts, and she said it with the flat certainty of someone much older, and I hated that she knew it.

Anselm had gone pale in the gold light, which took some doing given how little color he'd started with. He touched one of the frozen—a woman at the tavern table, hand raised around a cup—and pulled his fingers back fast. "Warm," he whispered. "She's warm. Jonah, she's *warm*, she's—" His voice did something complicated and failed. Anselm had spent thirty years keeping records for an order that catalogued souls the way tax men catalogue grain. I watched him understand, all at once and bodily, what the entries in those ledgers had actually been.

"Souls who traded themselves willingly," Cassian said. He'd been here before. I could tell by the way he didn't look at any of it, the way you don't look at a wound you've already decided not to treat. "That's what the court offers. Not torment. Comfort. You come down the pilgrim road of your own feet, and they show you this, and they say: you can stop. Right here. Forever. No more hunger, no more grief, no more winter. Just this afternoon, just this table, just this smile, for eternity."

"And people say yes."

"People *line up*." He finally made himself look at the frozen street, at the golden bird and the golden water and the golden faces. "That's the part nobody believes until they see it. Hell with a queue."

* * *

We walked in. I don't know who decided it; I think we all just started moving, because standing still among them felt like being the only sober person at a funeral. The gold seams brightened as we went deeper, and where they brightened, the frozen grew less frozen—or maybe more finished. Near the edges of the court, people were caught mid-stride, mid-word. Toward the center, they were caught at rest. At peace. As if the closer you came to the heart of the place, the longer you got to enjoy it before the moment closed over you like water.

A child ran past us—frozen, of course, but close enough to touch—with a wooden horse lifted high in one fist and joy all over his small face, and I thought: *someone sold themselves for him.* Someone climbed down here and signed the ledger so this boy could be warm and laughing under a mountain instead of starving in the gray fields above. And the court had taken the payment and given the boy this. An afternoon. Forever.

"Jonah." Cassian's voice, low. "There's something you should see before we go further. Something you specifically should see."

"I've seen quite a lot, thanks."

"It's about your sale." He said it carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "What it purchased. You've spent ten years thinking you know. You don't."

Ten years. Ten years of knowing exactly what I'd bought with the only thing I had left after Harrowgate: ten thousand lives, the siege broken, the city breathing. I'd done the arithmetic every night for a decade. One soul against ten thousand. The only trade I'd ever made that I didn't regret, and I'd built a small gray life on top of that certainty the way you build a house on rock.

So I followed him. Of course I followed him. He led us off the main street, through a colonnade where frozen lovers leaned against pillars with their foreheads touching, into a square I felt before I saw.

It was quieter than the rest. The gold was softer here, pooled rather than threaded, and in the middle of the square stood a monument—not stone. People. Hundreds of them, arranged in tiers like a wedding cake carved by someone who loved humanity and understood it not at all, each figure caught in an attitude of gratitude. Kneeling. Weeping with relief. Arms raised toward a sky that wasn't there. And above them, worked in gold so fine it looked like weather, a storm.

My storm.

I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The spiral of the front, the lightning forked in the pattern I'd used—the pattern Vessia had lent me, though I hadn't known *lent* was the word then. The sculptor had gotten it right down to the rain-shear, the way the wind bent the rain sideways off the walls of Harrowgate. Underneath, in letters that hurt to read because they were beautiful:

**TO HER WHO SOLD THE RAIN, THAT THE CITY MIGHT LIVE.**

"No," I said.

"Every soul in this monument," Cassian said, "traded itself within a year of the siege. Not for themselves. For others. A woman who gave herself so her son wouldn't be conscripted. A soldier who traded his spot in the ledger for his whole company. They heard what you'd done—one soul, ten thousand lives—and they thought, *if she could pay that price, so can I, for less.*" His voice cracked on the last word, and I discovered I had no voice at all. "You didn't buy ten thousand lives, Jonah. You started a fashion."

The tiers went up and up. I counted without wanting to. Four hundred. Four hundred and forty. There were empty spaces near the top, gaps in the sculpture like missing teeth, and I understood those too, eventually, standing there with the gold light pooling warm as bathwater around my boots.

Those were the ones Cassian had bought back.

"You said you'd been purchasing souls," I managed. "Quietly."

"A few dozen. It takes years. Each one requires a favor to the court, a debt of my own, a piece of myself I didn't know I had." He looked at the gaps. "I couldn't reach the ones in the monument. They're display pieces now. The king calls them the Gratitude. He shows them to visitors." A pause, and then, because he was Cassian and cruelty and kindness lived in him side by side like tenants who never spoke: "He'll show them to you. When you meet him. He'll want you to see what your sale became."

"He wants me to feel proud of it."

"He wants you to feel *responsible* for it. Pride and guilt are the same door, opened from different sides. He's had ten thousand years to learn which handle works on which person."

Wren had climbed onto the lowest tier and was standing among the grateful dead—or the grateful paused, or whatever they were—with her face turned up toward the golden storm. "They're singing," she said. "Inside. All of them. The same song. It's the prayer Vessia taught them during the siege. The one you used to call the lightning." She looked down at me, and her eyes were too old and too bright. "They're still saying thank you, Jonah. Every one of them, forever. That's what the court did with their gratitude. It kept it. It *banks* it."

"Banks it for what?"

But I already knew. Everything down here was currency. Warmth was currency, souls were currency, and apparently gratitude was too—ten thousand years of harvested thank-you, stored in four hundred and forty frozen hearts, feeding the seams of gold that lit this underground counterfeit of a summer day.

The long winter above wasn't just collection. It was *investment*. The court wasn't hoarding the world's warmth out of spite. It was spending it on this—on comfort, on beauty, on a frozen festival that never ended—and every answered prayer above sent down more capital, and every soul that lined up below added another candle to the chandelier.

Horror dressed as comfort. Comfort dressed as heaven. And underneath both, the same arithmetic I'd been doing for ten years, except now the numbers went all the way down.

"We should keep moving," Anselm said gently. He'd come to stand beside me, and he didn't touch me, which I appreciated; I was fairly sure that if anyone touched me I was going to come apart like wet paper. "The inner court lies past the Hearth. If we're to reach the Spire's heart, we cross it while the court still thinks we're pilgrims."

"Pilgrims."

"Everyone who walks this road willingly is a pilgrim until they say otherwise. It's the one courtesy the court observes." He hesitated. "There's something else. On the way in—I saw a name. On the tavern's door, chalked in the old order's script. A reservation. The court keeps a guest list, and it's expecting arrivals tonight, and one of the names is yours. It has been for ten years. Room's been kept."

"Of course it has," I said. "I paid a deposit."

Nobody laughed. I hadn't really expected them to. But Wren took my hand, her fingers small and hot as coals—everything down here was warm, that was the trick of it, warmth everywhere like money everywhere, and none of it real—and we went on, through the streets of the smiling, past the fountain where the bird would land forever and never land, toward the heart of the mountain where something that called itself a king had kept a room for me the way you keep a room for family.

The gold brightened ahead. Somewhere beyond the colonnades, I began to hear music—distant, sweet, patient—and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't frozen at all.

The court knew we were coming.

And it had started playing our song.
