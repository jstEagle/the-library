## 12. The Price of the Siege

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a confession you haven't made yet. I'd been living inside it for ten years, so you'd think I'd have grown comfortable there, the way a man grows comfortable in a cell he's decorated with his own drawings. But walking beside Jonah through the frozen gilded halls of the court beneath the mountain, that silence sat on my chest like a debt collector who had learned patience.

The court of small mercies, Wren had called it, and the name was crueler than anything I could have invented. Souls who had sold themselves stood in alcoves along the walls, smiling. Smiling forever. A woman in a merchant's coat smiled at a wall. A soldier with half his face gone to frost-smoothness smiled at nothing at all. They looked like guests at a feast where the food was memory and everyone had eaten their fill.

Jonah walked past them without looking. She was very good at not looking. She'd had a decade of practice.

I had a decade of practice too — at not saying it. And now Anselm was somewhere behind us with his burned records and his ruined hands, Wren was humming a tune no living person knew, and Jonah had just asked me, in a voice flat as a frozen lake, "Why did you do it, Cassian? The testimony. Tell me the truth this time."

So much for the silence.

* * *

Let me tell you how a man becomes a traitor, since apparently I'm the only one qualified to explain.

It doesn't happen the way the ballads say. There's no moment where you choose darkness over light, no cackling figure offering you gold. What happens is smaller. What happens is a Tuesday.

Harrowgate fell under siege in the autumn of the year they now call the Year of the Storm, though the histories — the few that survived the memory-edicts — get even that wrong. It wasn't one storm. It was one woman holding a storm together for nine days while the Vessian army threw itself against the walls like water against glass. Nine days. I counted them because I counted everything then; I was the quartermaster's clerk, and numbers were the only prayers I trusted.

On the ninth day, the storm-caller came down from the wall gray as ash, and she couldn't remember her own mother's face. That's what calling costs when you call that big. It eats backward through you, taking the soft things first.

And on the tenth day, a man in a gray cloak found me in the quartermaster's cellar counting nails, and he said, "Cassian Vale. Your sister Mira owes a debt to the court beneath the mountain. Her soul, specifically. Contracted three years ago, when your father gambled away what he shouldn't have gambled." He smiled the way clerks smile. "But debts can be transferred. With proper testimony."

I want to be clear about what I understood in that moment, because I've spent ten years lying to myself about it, and I'm done. I understood perfectly. I understood that if I stood before the tribunal and swore that Jonah Thornbury had trafficked with the buried king — that her storm was bought, not called — then the ledger would accept my testimony as payment-in-kind, and Mira's name would come off its pages, and mine would go on in a lesser column, a servitor's mark instead of a seizure.

I understood that I would be destroying the woman I loved to save my sister, and I understood that I would do it anyway, and I understood — this is the part that still wakes me at night — that some cold clerk-thing in my chest was already relieved. Because loving Jonah Thornbury was like standing next to a lightning strike waiting to happen, and here was a way out that let me call it duty.

I testified. I wept while I did it, which made it worse, because everyone said afterward: *he wept, he must have been coerced.* No. I wept because it was true and false at once — her storm *had* touched the deep places, everything that big does — and because a lie wrapped around a truth is the only lie that survives a tribunal.

They exiled her. They erased her from the rolls. Mira went home to our mother and lived, and married, and last spring named her daughter after a saint because naming her after me would have been an insult to saints.

And I took a position with the crown's Office of Reconciliation, which is a beautiful phrase meaning: the office that negotiates with the Spire. Ten years of negotiating. Ten years of learning the ledger's grammar, its exceptions, its loopholes — because a ledger, whatever else it is, is a document, and documents are written by clerks, and clerks can be beaten at their own game.

I bought back souls. Quietly. One at a time. Crown funds misallocated, crown favors traded, crown secrets bartered — I embezzled an empire in installments and spent every coin of it on names. Four hundred and eleven souls repurchased from the court of the buried king and returned to their bodies, most of them never knowing why they woke one morning feeling like they'd gotten something back. Four hundred and eleven. I kept the list in my head because paper burns.

It wasn't enough. It was never going to be enough. But it was what I had, and I did it in Jonah Thornbury's name, in secret, knowing she'd never know, knowing I didn't deserve for her to know.

That's the whole architecture of my treachery. Now you know what I built and what it cost, and you may judge it however you like. I've judged it every way there is, and the verdicts disagree with each other, which I've decided is what being human means.

* * *

I told her all of it there in the frozen court, standing between two smiling souls who smiled through my confession like it was music.

Jonah didn't interrupt once. That was the worst part. She listened the way she used to listen to weather reports — completely, professionally, already calculating what the sky intended.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long time. Somewhere far above us, the mountain groaned, ice settling, or the Spire breathing.

"Mira," she said finally. "Your sister. Is she—"

"Alive. Happy. Her daughter has her laugh."

"Good." And then, because she was Jonah, and mercy and cruelty lived in her so close together you couldn't always tell which hand was which: "Four hundred and eleven souls. How many did the siege cost?"

"I don't—"

"How many, Cassian. You're a clerk. You'll have estimated."

"Nine hundred," I said. "Give or take. The storm fed the Spire directly. Big workings always do. Yours was the biggest in a century."

"So I put nine hundred people in these walls," she said, gesturing at the smiling dead-alive, "and you took four hundred out, and you think that arithmetic buys back a testimony."

"No," I said. "I don't think anything buys it back. That's rather the point of what I'm confessing."

She turned to look at me then, and up close her eyes were the color of the sky before hail, and I remembered — God help me, I remembered — that I used to fall asleep counting the freckles on her nose like a man counting coins he couldn't believe were his.

"Do you know what I did with the ten years?" she asked. "I sold rain-charms. Little knots of string. I made sure they worked by luck alone, no craft, because craft leaves a signature and signatures get old friends taken by Graycloaks. I watched them take Tam Voss last month because he prayed at a cracked shrine, and I hid my wrists and said nothing. That's my decade. Small enough to be safe." Her voice cracked on *safe*, just slightly, a hairline fracture in stone. "You got to spend yours buying people back. Even your guilt was useful, Cassian. Mine just hid."

"That's not fair," I said, and immediately hated myself, because what right did I have to invoke fairness, in this place, to this woman?

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Isn't that funny. Nothing down here is fair. That's the whole business model."

Wren appeared at the end of the gallery then, a small feral shape against all that gilded ice, and she looked at Jonah, and then at me, with the terrible directness of children and gods.

"He's telling the truth," Wren announced. "The cracks say so. All of it. Even the part about being relieved." She tilted her head. "He doesn't like that part either."

"Thank you, Wren," I said weakly.

"You're welcome. She's deciding about you." And the child wandered off toward an alcove where a smiling woman in a baker's apron stood, and began, softly, to hum to her.

Jonah watched her go. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something quieter and more dangerous than anger.

"Here's what I keep snagging on," she said. "Not the testimony. I've had ten years to hate the testimony; hatred gets comfortable, like an old boot. What snags me is that you did the right thing after. You spent ten years doing the right thing, Cassian, beautifully, secretly, at real cost. And somehow that's worse. If you'd just been a monster, I could file you away. But you're a good man who did an unforgivable thing for a forgivable reason, and then spent a decade trying to pay it down like—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—like a debt. Like everything down here is a debt."

"Isn't it?"

"No." She stepped closer. This close, I could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the frost-burn on her knuckles from the descent. "That's the lie the ledger tells. That everything balances. That every wound has a price and every price can be paid and the books come out even in the end. My whole life I believed something like that — that power had a cost and you paid it and moved on. Then I came down here and saw what balancing the books actually looks like." She nodded at the smiling souls. "It looks like that. Perfectly balanced. Perfectly empty."

"So what do you want from me, Jonah? Absolution? I won't ask for it. Restitution? I've paid everything I have and it isn't enough. Punishment? Name it and I'll—"

"Stop," she said. "Just — stop performing your guilt at me. I've seen your ledgers, Cassian. I know how you make everything balance." She rubbed her face with both hands, and for a moment she looked less like the storm-caller of Harrowgate and more like a tired woman in a mountain full of ghosts, which is what she was. "I don't know what I want. That's the honest answer. I want the ten years back and I can't have them. I want to hate you cleanly and I can't do that either, because you saved four hundred and eleven people and got my sister's name off a page I didn't even know existed until this week."

"My sister's—"

"Mira's debt transferred to you. Which means when you started buying souls back, hers was the first one you redeemed. Which means every soul since has been purchased with the margin of a debt that was originally *mine* to forgive or not." She shook her head slowly. "You even made your penance arithmetically entangled with me. Only you, Cassian. Only a clerk could manage that."

I had no answer. For once in my life, the numbers failed.

* * *

We found the others in the antechamber beyond the gallery — Anselm sitting with his scorched record-case held on his knees like a sleeping cat, Wren cross-legged in front of the sealed doors to the inner court, listening to them the way other children listen to seashells.

"They're loud in there," Wren reported. "The king knows we're coming. He's setting out chairs."

"Chairs," Anselm repeated faintly.

"For bargaining. Kings always set out chairs." She looked up at Jonah, then at me, and her expression went sly in a way that six-year-olds should not be able to manage. "You two fixed it. Mostly. The crack between you is smaller. Still leaks, but smaller."

"We haven't fixed anything," Jonah said.

"You decided not to hate him today. That's most of fixing." Wren shrugged, profoundly unimpressed by the magnitude of what she'd just described. "The rest takes longer. Like soup."

Anselm cleared his throat. "Before we go in there — and I confess I would rather walk into any fire in the world than through those doors — I should say that I read the Office of Reconciliation's transfer records during my years with the order. Master Vale's purchases. We assumed corruption." He paused. "It was the only file in thirty years of audits that I hoped was corruption."

"Thank you, Anselm," I said. "I think."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment."

"It rarely is, from you."

Jonah hadn't spoken. She stood looking at the great doors — black wood veined with silver frost, tall as a cathedral's hope — and I watched her shoulders rise and fall twice, the way they used to before she called weather. Gathering. Deciding.

"Cassian," she said at last, without turning around. "I'm not forgiving you. I want that on the record, since you love records. Forgiveness is a feeling, and feelings are weather, and I don't trust my weather anymore. But forgiveness is also a choice, Anselm says — he preached about it once, badly — and choices I can make." She turned. Her eyes were dry and steady and terrifying. "So here's my choice: I choose to walk into that room with you at my back instead of in front of me. I choose to let what you did stand next to what you've done, unresolved, on the books, accruing. No absolution. No punishment. Just — forward. Together, if you can bear it."

"If I can bear it," I said. "Jonah, I've been bearing it for ten years. Forward is the first direction that's ever felt lighter."

"Don't make it pretty."

"I would never. I'm a clerk. I'll make it itemized."

Despite everything — despite the smiling dead and the groaning mountain and the winter eating the world above us — the corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. The ghost of one, maybe. The place where a smile had been evicted and might someday reapply.

Wren scrambled up and pressed both small hands against the doors. "They're opening," she said. "Remember the rules. Don't sit in any chair he offers. Don't eat anything. And Jonah—" the girl looked back over her shoulder, and for a heartbeat her eyes weren't a child's eyes at all but something vast and cold and frightened, "—he's going to offer you *her*. Your soul. He keeps it in a pretty box and he's been saving it, because he thinks everyone wants to be whole more than they want to be free."

"And if he's right?" Jonah asked quietly.

"He's not," Wren said. "But he's rich, and patient, and wrong people win arguments all the time."

The doors swung inward without a sound, onto a hall lit by candles that burned blue and gave no heat, and at the far end of it, on a throne of black ice, the buried king rose to greet us — and behind him, on a table of silver, small as a locket, small as a heart, sat a box that made every soul in my body lean toward it like a plant toward sun.

Jonah's hand found mine in the dark. Briefly. Without looking. Then she let go, and we walked in.
