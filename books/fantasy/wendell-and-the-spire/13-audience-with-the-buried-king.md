## 13. Audience with the Buried King

The doors to the inner court were made of ice that had forgotten how to melt, and they opened for us without being touched.

I want to say I walked through them with my chin up. The truth is uglier. My legs were shaking, and Wren's small hand was clamped in mine so hard her knuckles had gone white as the frost on the walls, and Anselm was murmuring something behind me that might have been a prayer or might have been an inventory of everything he'd ever done wrong. Cassian walked at my shoulder like a shadow that had learned to walk upright, and I hated how much steadier his presence made me feel.

Beyond the doors, the court of the buried king spread out like a cathedral designed by someone who had only ever heard love described secondhand.

Gilded pillars rose into a ceiling of black stone veined with frozen light. Chandeliers hung from chains of silvered bone, each candle burning with a flame that gave off cold instead of heat—I could feel it on my face, a soft blue radiance like moonlight you could lean into. And everywhere, everywhere, the courtiers.

They were beautiful. That was the first obscenity of it. Men and women in robes of woven frost, faces smooth and serene, standing in clusters around fountains of liquid glass, conversing in voices so gentle I couldn't make out a single word. They smiled. All of them smiled. Not the rictus grin of the dead—something warmer than that, something that reached their eyes, and that was the second obscenity, because I knew what those smiles cost.

"Souls," Wren whispered. She'd gone very still against my side. "They traded them. They're *happy*."

"I know, little bird."

"They don't remember being sad."

"No." I squeezed her hand. "That's what they paid."

A woman detached herself from one of the clusters and drifted toward us, and up close I could see the flaw in the perfection—a faint translucence to her, as if she'd been dipped in candle wax and allowed to set. Her smile deepened when she saw me, and something about it snagged on a hook ten years old.

"You came back," she said, and her voice was kind, and familiar, and wrong. "We kept your seat warm, storm-caller."

I knew her. Ten years ago she'd been a camp follower at Harrowgate with a laugh like a cart rattling downhill, and she'd sold me a charm against fever once because she felt sorry for the girl who was about to call down a storm. Her name had been Maren. I was almost sure her name had been Maren.

"What did you trade?" I asked her.

Her smile didn't waver. "The winter," she said. "I traded the winter. It was so cold that year, and my hands hurt all the time, and now I'm never cold." She held them out to show me—smooth, unscarred, translucent hands. "You should see what they're offering you. It's better than what I got."

She drifted away before I could ask anything else, back to her cluster of smiling ghosts, and I stood there with my heart hammering and understood that this was the first move of whatever game we'd walked into: not terror. *Hospitality.* They wanted me comfortable. Comfortable people negotiate.

* * *

The throne room was smaller than I expected, which somehow made it worse. Intimate. A chamber of black ice lit by those cold-burning candles, and at its center, on a chair carved from a single block of glacier-glass, sat the king.

He looked tired.

That's what no song about the buried king ever tells you. They give him a crown of thorned iron and eyes like dying stars and a voice that cracks mountains, and what's actually sitting there is a man who looks like he hasn't slept since the world was young, wearing robes that might have been magnificent three thousand years ago and now hang off him like curtains in an abandoned house. His crown was real enough. His exhaustion was more real than that.

Beside the throne, on a slightly lower chair of the same glass, sat an empty seat. Waiting. I tried not to look at it and failed immediately.

"So," the king said. His voice was quiet. It filled the room anyway, the way deep water fills a well. "The storm-caller comes home after ten years. Sit down, Jonah Thornbury. You look like you've been walking for days."

"We have been walking for days."

"Then sit." He gestured, and chairs of ice bloomed up from the floor for the others—Anselm took his stiffly, like a man sitting on a judge's bench; Cassian lowered himself with the careful ease of someone who'd been in this room before and hadn't enjoyed it; Wren refused hers entirely and stood pressed against my side, glaring up at the king with a ferocity that would have been adorable if my mouth weren't so dry.

The king watched her with something that wasn't quite amusement. "The crack speaks through you, little one. Do they tell you what they want?"

"They lie," Wren said.

"Yes," the king agreed, and there was something in it too heavy to be satisfaction. "They do. So do I, sometimes. But not tonight. Tonight I'm going to tell you the truth, Jonah Thornbury, because you're the first person in a century to walk in here with your soul still in your chest, and I find I've missed the sound of it."

"Flatterer," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"It's not flattery. It's arithmetic." He leaned forward, and the candles guttered blue. "You've seen the ledger. Your priest friend has been reading it for weeks. You know what the winter is."

"Collection," I said. "Interest on answered prayers. The gods fled here when the Spire was raised, and every miracle since has been a loan drawn against the warmth of the world."

"And someone must hold the ledger," he said. "Someone must enforce the debts, or the whole edifice collapses—and if it collapses while the gods are still trapped inside it, they will tear their way out through the skin of the world. You've heard them screaming through the child. You know what they intend."

"Burn the world to escape it," Anselm said quietly. "Yes. We know."

"The ledger must be held by a living soul. That is the binding at its heart—it was written by people who believed a soul was the strongest currency there is." The king's mouth twisted. "They were wrong, but the binding doesn't care. For three thousand years I have held it alone. Do you know what that means, storm-caller? Every debt collected passes through the holder. Every soul weighed, every prayer audited, every winter calculated. I have spent three millennia being the cold hand of an arithmetic I did not write, and I am *tired*, Jonah Thornbury. I am tired in a way that has no word in any language still spoken above ground."

"That sounds like a personal problem," I said.

Cassian made a small sound that might have been a swallowed laugh. The king's eyes—gray as slate, gray as the sky before hail—fixed on me, and for a moment I saw the man under the crown, and he almost smiled.

"There she is," he said softly. "The woman who told the Harrowgate council to evacuate themselves if they liked, but she was calling the storm either way. Yes. I have watched you for ten years, Jonah. Selling rain-charms. Shrinking yourself down small enough to fit inside a life where nothing could reach you. Do you know why I never sent for you? Why I let you hide?"

"Because you're patient."

"Because I was *ashamed*." He said it flatly, without performance, and it landed harder than any threat could have. "I took your soul as payment for a storm you called to save ten thousand lives. I have taken many souls for far less. I am the collector, and collectors are not permitted to find their own ledgers obscene. But I found yours obscene, and I let you go, and I have regretted it every winter since, because every winter is partly my doing, and some small part of it was always avoidable."

"Get to the offer," Cassian said. His voice was tight. "Your Majesty is famous for getting to the offer."

The king turned to him, and something passed between them—old acquaintance, old transaction, old shame. Then back to me.

"Here it is. No theater. Your soul sits in my vault. It has sat there for ten years, and it is the finest thing I own—not because of what it is, but because of what it chose. Souls that are taken crumble. Souls that are *sold* keep their shape. Yours has kept its shape beautifully." He rose from the throne, and the movement was slow, joints cracking like river ice, and I realized with a jolt that he was old in a way that had nothing to do with years. "I offer you a trade. Take the empty seat beside mine. Take half the ledger. Hold it with me, and the burden halves, and the collection can be gentler—we can take the interest in small coin instead of whole winters. Your soul returns to you the moment you sit down. Whole. Warm. Yours."

"And the winter?" Anselm asked.

"Eases. Not ends—the debts exist, and they must be paid—but eases within a season. Crops grow again. Children stop freezing. The world keeps most of its warmth, forever, because two holders can be merciful where one holder must be exact." He spread his hands. "This is what I offer, Jonah Thornbury. Not power. *Mercy.* Mercy requires a holder. The alternative is what your goddess-friends propose: burn the ledger, void the debts, and let the gods loose on a world that owes them nothing and will receive them anyway. Or refuse me, walk away, and watch the winter deepen until there is nothing left to save. Those are your choices. I have dressed none of them up."

The room was silent except for the cold flames ticking in their sconces.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn: for one long moment, I wanted it.

Not the throne. Not the power. I wanted the *arithmetic*. I wanted a problem that could be solved by holding on—by sitting down, by enduring, by being the steady hand on the scale for the rest of time. I had spent ten years learning how to endure. Endurance was the one skill I owned outright. And he was offering to make endurance holy—to take the small, shrunken, careful life I'd built and expand it until it held up the sky. My soul back. The winter easing. Children warm. All of it purchased with the only thing I'd ever been good at: staying.

Wren's hand found mine. She was looking up at the empty seat with an expression no child should wear—an expression of someone doing terrible sums.

"If you sit there," she said, "you'll forget how to be angry."

The king went very still.

"I can hear the seat," Wren said. "It talks. It says *sit down, rest, it's so heavy, let someone else carry it awhile*. And the lady next to you"—she pointed at the empty chair—"the last one who sat there, she forgot her brother's name, and then she forgot she'd had a brother, and then she forgot why she was sad about it, and then she smiled." Wren's voice cracked. "It eats the anger first. Because the anger is what remembers."

"Wren," Anselm started.

"She's right," the king said.

We all turned to him. He stood beside his throne with his head bowed, and for the first time I saw the chains—not iron, nothing so honest, but the way his shoulders curved inward, the way his hands hung like things that had set down their burdens and forgotten how to pick up anything else.

"She is right. The seat takes your griefs one at a time, gently, in the order of how much they hurt. Mine went first, then my wife's name, then my children's faces, then the war I built it to end. I have been enforcing debts for three thousand years with the part of myself that remembers why debts are cruel." He looked up at me, and his eyes were the grayest thing in that gray room. "I am not offering you mercy, Jonah Thornbury. I am offering you company in hell, and dressing it as mercy because I am lonely, and because I think you would be good at it, and because if you take the seat perhaps I can finally be permitted to die."

"*Permitted?*" Cassian was on his feet.

"The holder cannot die while the ledger stands unheld. I have tried. The binding won't allow it." He said it the way you'd report the weather. "Two holders, though. Two holders can release each other, if both consent, if both are whole. That is the clause no one has ever reached, because no one has ever come down here whole." His gaze moved over us—the disgraced priest, the traitor-envoy, the god-touched child—and settled back on me. "You brought a whole soul into my court, storm-caller. Do you understand yet why I let you live ten years in Cinderhollow selling rain-charms? I was waiting to see if you'd come back with reasons not to sit down."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the winter continues exactly as it is, and I continue exactly as I am, and in a hundred years or so there will be no world left to argue about." He sat back down on the throne, heavily, like a man resuming a sentence he'd been speaking for millennia. "I will not force you. The binding forbids coercion—that much mercy the writers left in it. You must choose with your soul in your chest, freely, or the seat rejects you. Which means, Jonah Thornbury, that I must let you walk out of here and think about it, and hope, against three thousand years of evidence, that you come back."

The candles burned their cold light. The empty seat waited, patient as arithmetic.

"Why me?" I asked. "Really. There are other souls in your vault. Other sold souls, whole ones, ones who'd jump at—"

"Because yours was sold to *save* people," he said simply. "Every other soul in my vault was sold to escape something. Fear, pain, poverty, grief. Yours was sold forward—into a storm, into responsibility, into the lives of strangers. The binding responds to that. The seat wants a keeper who understands that holding is a form of saving." He paused. "Also, you are the only person in a thousand years who has insulted me to my face within a minute of meeting me, and I find I trust people who do that. Flatterers have sat in that chair before. It digested them."

I laughed. It escaped before I could stop it, short and cracked, and the sound bounced off the black ice and came back sounding like someone I used to be.

"I need to talk to my people," I said.

"They are your people," the king agreed, "and that is precisely the sort of thing the seat will take from you first." He waved one tired hand toward the doors. "Go. The court will give you beds and food that tastes like memory of food. You have until the next answered prayer—somewhere above us, right now, someone is praying to Vessia, and the frost is already climbing. When it arrives, the choice must be made. The ledger does not wait, and neither, anymore, does the winter."

We walked out. At the doors I stopped and turned back, because there was one question scratching at me, the kind of question that keeps you awake in strange beds.

"The last person who sat in that seat," I said. "The one who forgot her brother. What was her name?"

The king looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes—grief, maybe, or its fossil.

"I don't remember," he said. "That's rather the point."

The doors closed behind us, and in the gilded corridor full of smiling souls, Wren slipped her hand back into mine and whispered, so quietly I almost missed it:

"He lied about one thing. The seat doesn't eat the anger first." Her eyes were enormous, god-lit, terrified. "It eats the *love* first. The anger is just what's left over. Jonah—the seat knows your name already. It's been saying it since we walked in. And it says it the way *he* says it. Like it's known you a lot longer than ten years."

Above us, faint through a thousand feet of mountain, I heard the first muffled crack of new frost spreading through the world—and somewhere in the dark below our feet, something that wore the buried king's loneliness began, very softly, to laugh.
