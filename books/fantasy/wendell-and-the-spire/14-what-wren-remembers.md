## 14. What Wren Remembers

The thing about being a crack in the world is that nobody tells you. You have to figure it out yourself, and mostly you figure it out by falling through.

I'd known for a while that I was different from other children, the way you know a tooth is loose before it hurts. Other children had one voice inside them — their own, grumbly and small, saying things like *I'm hungry* and *I hate turnips*. I had mine, plus weather. Plus whispers. Plus, on bad nights, a chorus of old things arguing in languages that tasted like snowmelt and iron. The priests in the village where I was born — before Cinderhollow, before any of this — had called me touched. Then they had called me possessed. Then they had called me nothing at all, because they'd put me out past the fields with a blanket and half a loaf, and what you call someone after that is mostly *gone*.

So when I say I knew I was a crack, I mean it the way Jonah knew she was a storm-caller: by the evidence of everything that had been taken from me for it.

We were camped in the frozen court, in the shadow of a gilded archway where a smiling woman stood perfectly still, her breath long since stopped, her smile still going like it was being paid by the hour. Anselm was asleep sitting up, which he did a lot now, like his body had learned not to trust lying down. Cassian was awake too, because Cassian was always awake lately, doing sums with his eyes the way some people do prayers. And Jonah sat with her back against the archway's base, sharpening a knife she never used, because sharpening a knife is something to do with your hands when your heart is busy screaming.

That's when I told them.

Or — no. That's not honest. That's when it came out of me, the way water comes out of a cracked jug: not poured, leaked.

* * *

It started because Vessia was talking again.

She talks through me most at night, when the court is quietest and the cold has teeth. She doesn't use words so much as she uses *pressures*, like someone leaning their whole weight against the inside of my ribs. That night she leaned hard, and what came out of my mouth wasn't my voice. It was older, and sadder, and it said:

*SHE IS INCOMPLETE AND SHE DOES NOT KNOW HOW MUCH.*

Everyone woke up. Even the smiling woman seemed, for a second, less sure of her smile.

"Vessia." Jonah was on her feet with the knife actually pointed somewhere for once. "What do you mean, incomplete?"

*I MEAN WHAT I SAY. THE LEDGER SPAT HER BACK.*

Cassian made a sound like a man swallowing a stone. He'd been reading ledger fragments for days, copying numbers into a little book he thought I didn't know about. I knew about it. Cracks hear things.

"Explain," Jonah said, and her voice had that flatness it gets, the one that means underneath it there's a fire and she's sitting on it.

Vessia sighed through me, and my whole chest went cold, and then — this is the strange part — she let go. All at once, like setting down something heavy. And what was left standing there was just me. Wren. Small, cold-handed, hungry-again Wren, with four faces looking at me and every one of them asking a question.

I could have run. I've run from smaller dangers than being understood.

I didn't run.

"I died," I said. "A long time ago. Before I was me."

* * *

Here is what I remember, and I want it written down properly, because Anselm writes everything down and he says memory that isn't written gets eaten:

I remember warmth. Not weather-warmth. *Hearth*-warmth. A kitchen with a crooked door and a mother who hummed off-key and a name — my first name, the one before Wren — that I can't say anymore because saying it feels like putting my hand into a fire. I remember being small and sick, the coughing kind of sick, the kind that took whole villages back then. I remember my mother praying. Not to Vessia, even. To anything. To the dark under the floorboards if it would listen.

Something listened.

I don't remember the bargain. That's the part that got spat back wrong. There's a hole in me shaped exactly like the moment of the deal, and everything around the hole is clear as glass: the kitchen, the humming, the fever, the prayer — and then me, alive, seven years old, standing in the snow outside our door with no memory of how I'd gotten there, and my mother looking at me like I was both the best thing and the worst thing she'd ever seen.

"The ledger took your soul," Cassian said quietly. "As payment. But souls are — they're not coins. They're not clean. The court tried to take yours whole and it didn't fit through the accounting. So they kept most of it and returned the remainder."

"Returned it," Anselm repeated. His voice does a thing when he's horrified and trying to be scholarly about it. It goes very precise, like each word is being weighed on tiny scales. "Returned it into a living child."

"I'm the change," I said, because I'd worked that part out myself and I was proud of it. "They couldn't give her all of her back, so they gave her the bit that was left over. Like when you pay with a coin that's clipped and the merchant gives you back the clippings."

Nobody laughed. I've noticed adults don't laugh when you say true things in a cheerful voice. They look at you like you've broken something.

"That's why the gods speak through you," Jonah said slowly. I watched her put it together, watch the pieces click behind her eyes the way they do. "You're not a vessel. You're not chosen. You're a *gap*. Where your soul should be seamless, there's a hole, and things on the other side of the hole can lean through."

"And shout," I agreed. "Vessia shouts a lot. She's very loud for a goddess everyone forgot."

Jonah crouched down in front of me. This is a thing she does that I have decided means she loves me, although she would deny it under torture. She looks at me straight-on, eye level, like I'm a person and not a phenomenon.

"Wren. How much of you is missing?"

And here is where I should tell the truth, all of it, the way Anselm says truth is owed to people who will fight for you.

But the truth was big, and I was tired, and part of me — the clipped-coin part, the leftover part — was afraid that if I showed them the whole shape of the hole, they'd stop seeing the girl around it.

"A lot," I said. "I don't know how much. You can't count a hole from the inside."

* * *

That was only half of it, though. The other half came later, and it's the half that matters, so I'll tell it right.

Later — after Anselm had filled three pages with notes and Cassian had gone very quiet in the way that meant he was doing arithmetic with souls instead of coin — Jonah found me sitting alone near the edge of the court, where the gilded streets start crumbling into raw tunnel. The smiling dead don't come this far. They stay where the gold is. I think the gold is part of what holds them together.

I was practicing.

This is embarrassing, but I'll say it anyway: I was practicing being whole. There's an exercise I do. I list myself. *My name is Wren. I like the smell of rain on hot stone. I am scared of deep water. My favorite color is the green things turn right before spring.* Every item is a stitch across the crack. Some days the stitches hold. Some days I get to *favorite color* and find nothing there, just smooth blank nothing where a preference used to be, and I have to make one up, and I can't always tell afterward which ones are real.

Jonah sat down next to me without asking. She's good at that. Not asking.

"You're counting yourself," she said.

I stared at her. "How did you—"

"Because I do it too." She turned the knife over in her hands, not sharpening it now, just holding it. "Different reasons. Same exercise. When you've sold pieces of yourself, you keep inventory."

"You didn't sell pieces," I said. "You sold the whole thing. Everyone knows. Well — everyone who reads ledgers knows, which is two people in this tunnel, but still."

"I sold it," she agreed, "and I've spent ten years learning which parts went with it. You'd be surprised what a soul carries that you don't notice until it's gone. Certain kinds of laughing. The ability to cry at songs. I used to cry at songs, Wren. Terribly. In front of people." She almost smiled. "Now I just feel a pressure behind my eyes and think, ah, yes, that's where that used to happen."

I laughed, and then I felt guilty for laughing, and then I thought: *that's another thing to check off the list. Guilt after laughing. Still present.* The inventory grows.

"Can I ask you something?" I said. "And you have to answer true, because you always answer true even when it makes everyone mad, which is honestly one of the most annoying things about you."

"Ask."

"If we get to the ledger. If we break it. And breaking it costs something—" My throat did a squeeze. "Anselm thinks it'll cost the breaker something. Cassian keeps looking at me when he says things like *binding* and *price*, and he stops talking when he sees me looking back. What's it going to cost?"

Jonah was quiet for a long time. Down the tunnel, somewhere deep in the mountain, something vast shifted its weight, and the frost on the walls crackled like distant applause.

"I don't know," she said finally. "But I know this. Whatever it costs, it comes out of me. Not out of Anselm, not out of Cassian, and definitely not out of you. That's not negotiable. That's decided. I decided it about ten years ago when I made my first terrible bargain, and I've gotten better at bargains since. Mostly by making all of them badly."

"That's not how it works," I said.

"It's exactly how it works. I'm the adult."

"You're the adult who sold her soul."

"One bad transaction doesn't disqualify you from future negotiations. It gives you experience."

I wanted to laugh again. Instead what happened was that Vessia leaned on me.

I felt it coming — the pressure, the cold spreading up from my stomach into my mouth — and I tried to hold it back, because it was *our* moment, mine and Jonah's, and I didn't want a goddess in the middle of it. But cracks don't get to choose what falls through them. That's the whole definition of a crack.

What came out was my own voice, mostly, but arranged by something older, the way wind arranges leaves:

*LISTEN TO HER. SHE WILL OFFER HERSELF. SHE HAS ALWAYS OFFERED HERSELF. IT IS THE ONLY PRAYER SHE KNOWS.*

Jonah went rigid. "Vessia—"

*THE CHILD KNOWS WHAT THE BREAKING COSTS. THE CHILD HAS SEEN IT. ASK HER.*

And then Vessia let go again, quick and cruel, like shoving someone into a room and locking the door behind them, and it was just me, sitting there, caught.

Because I *had* seen it. That was the thing I hadn't told anyone. That was the hole's second secret.

When the gods speak through me, sometimes I see what they see. It's not like dreaming. It's like being a window someone else looks out of. And weeks ago — the night at the drowned shrine, the night Vessia first spoke plainly through me — for half a heartbeat I had looked out through the goddess's eyes at the Spire's heart, at the ledger itself, and I had seen what it asks of whoever breaks it.

I'd seen a woman standing on a black book the size of a field. I'd seen the book open beneath her like a mouth. And I'd seen it take — not blood, not life, but *people*. The faces of everyone she loved, sliding off her like water off oiled cloth, leaving her standing whole and healthy and utterly alone, unable to remember why she was crying.

I hadn't known whose face the woman wore. Now, sitting next to Jonah, watching her jaw set in that stubborn line, I knew.

"It takes love," I whispered. "The ledger. If you break it, it takes — it unthreads everyone you care about. Out of your memory. Out of you. You survive and you don't know what you survived *for*."

Jonah didn't flinch. That's the maddening, wonderful, awful thing about her. She absorbed it the way stone absorbs rain — no visible damage, and all of it going somewhere deep.

"All right," she said.

"*All right?*" My voice cracked, and not in the metaphysical way, in the ordinary seven-year-old way. "That's the worst price there is! Worse than dying! Dying you're done, but that — you'd forget Anselm, you'd forget Cassian, you'd forget *me*, and we'd all still be here, walking around, loving you, and you wouldn't even know what we were—"

"Wren."

"—and I'd have to introduce myself to you every day like a stranger, and you'd look at me the way you look at strangers, politely, with your hand near your knife—"

"*Wren.*"

She took my face in both hands. Her palms were rough and cold and I hated how much they steadied me.

"Listen to me," she said. "I have already lost everything once. I lost it slowly, in a courtroom, in front of a crowd, and I have spent ten years being nobody on purpose. Do you understand? Being forgotten isn't a threat to me, Wren. It's my native country. I have a house there."

"But—"

"But here's what I won't do." Her grip tightened, gentle and absolute. "I won't trade anyone for you. Not Anselm, not Cassian, not a single stranger in a single village above us. And I am telling you now, in front of whatever goddess is eavesdropping through your ribs, so that you can't unhear it: if it ever comes down to a choice between the world and you, I need you to know that I am *not trustworthy*. I will choose you. So don't put me in the position. Don't offer yourself. Don't volunteer. Promise me."

And that — that was the moment the whole weight of the book landed on me, the moral weight Anselm is always going on about, except it didn't feel like weight. It felt like a hook. Because she'd just handed me the exact thing I'd been afraid to want: proof that I mattered enough to be a temptation.

Which meant I had to refuse it. Didn't I? That's what being good is. Wanting the thing and putting it down.

Except my hands were shaking and I didn't want to put it down. I wanted, more than I had ever wanted anything, to be the person someone breaks the world's rules to keep.

"No," I said.

Jonah blinked.

"No," I said again, louder, and Vessia rose up in me unasked and I shoved her down, *my* mouth, *my* words, for once. "No. I won't promise. And I won't let you trade anyone for me either — not Anselm, not Cassian, not yourself, not anybody. Nobody gets traded. That's the whole point, don't you see? The ledger is a machine for trading people. Every soul in this frozen city smiled because trading themselves was easier than the alternative. If we go down there and do one more trade — even a noble one, even *your* noble one — we're just adding a page. We're just being the ledger with legs."

The tunnel was silent. Far off, the mountain breathed its slow cold breath.

"So what's your plan, then?" Jonah asked, and there was something new in her voice. Not mockery. Wonder, maybe. Or fear wearing wonder's coat.

"I don't have a plan," I admitted. "I'm seven. I have a *rule*. Nobody gets traded. We break the book, and whatever it takes, it takes from all of us, in pieces, so no one is left whole-and-alone, and no one is left sold. That's my rule. You have to follow it. I'm the crack in the world; I outrank you."

For a long moment Jonah looked at me, and I watched her decide something. I'm getting better at reading her. Ten years ago, apparently, she was easy to read — storm on her face, storm in her hands. Now everything's filed away. But that night, something surfaced.

"Deal," she said. "Nobody gets traded."

"And you have to swear it on something you actually care about. Not your soul, you already spent that. Swear it on—" I cast around. "On your knife. You love that knife. You talk to it."

"I do not talk to it."

"You said 'not tonight' to it last week when it wouldn't cut rope."

"That was situational."

"Swear on the knife, Jonah."

She swore on the knife. And if a goddess laughed through me at that moment — high and cold and, for once, almost fond — well. Cracks don't get to choose what falls through them.

* * *

I couldn't sleep after that. I sat up in the gold-lit dark and did my inventory, and for the first time I added items I'd never dared list before:

*My name is Wren. I am the clippings of a coin. I am a door left open. I am loved enough to be a temptation, and I refused it, and refusing it hurt worse than the fever ever did, and I think that hurt might be the biggest part of me that's real.*

And then, because honesty is a discipline like any other, I added the last item, the one I'd been avoiding since the drowned shrine:

*Somewhere below us, the buried king is waiting, and he knows what I am, and I am the only one of us he cannot ledger — because you cannot collect on a debt that was already repaid in the wrong coin. Which means, when we reach him, I am the only weapon we have that he can't buy.*

I didn't tell the others that yet.

I was working up to it when the frost on the far wall began to sing — thin and sweet and wrong — and every smiling dead soul in the court turned its head toward the depths at once, smiles fixed, eyes empty, and Anselm sat bolt upright and said, in a voice stripped of all scholarship:

"He knows we're here."
