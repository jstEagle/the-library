## 15. The Graycloak Verdict

I have spent fifty-one years learning that obedience is a kind of cowardice that has been given a uniform and told it is holy. I learned it slowly, the way one learns a language — a word at a time, each one reasonable, until one day you find you can say whole sentences in it fluently and cannot remember ever having thought in anything else.

The Graycloaks taught me that language. I helped write some of its grammar.

So when their horns sounded above the frozen court of the buried king — three notes, descending, the old signal for *heresy confirmed, proceed to censure* — I recognized them the way you recognize your own handwriting on a letter you wish you'd never sent.

"They're early," Cassian said. He had his ear pressed to a pillar of veined ice, and he looked, as always, like a man doing arithmetic he hated. "A day ahead of my estimate."

"Your estimates have been wrong before," Jonah said.

"My estimates got us into this mountain."

"Yes," she said, "so you'll forgive me if I don't consult them for the weather."

Wren was laughing at something only she could hear — or rather, something only she could be made to hear, which is a different and more terrible thing. She sat cross-legged on the gilded floor with frost crawling up her sleeves in patterns like script, and when the horns sounded again her laughter stopped mid-breath and her head turned toward the sound with the smooth, wrong precision of a weathervane finding wind.

"They brought the censers," she said, in a voice that was partly hers and partly not. "They brought the white fire. They are going to burn the smiling people."

"The smiling people are already dead," Jonah said gently.

"I know." Wren's own voice came back, small and furious. "That's why they shouldn't get burned twice."

* * *

Let me tell you what I carried down the pilgrim road, because it matters to what I did with it.

When the Order of the Ledgered Word fell out of favor — when the crown decided that our records were an embarrassment and our order a relic and dissolved us with a signature rather than a pyre, which was, for the crown, an act of mercy — I was permitted to keep the archive. Not all of it. A fragment. The ledgers of debts incurred between the years of the Harrowgate siege and the year of my disgrace, forty-one volumes, chained and locked in a chest I had carried across three provinces on a handcart while pretending to be a seller of funeral candles.

Forty-one volumes of names. Of souls sold, traded, collateralized, inherited. Of the arithmetic by which the court beneath the mountain had purchased its patience from desperate people, and by which desperate people had purchased their lives from the court.

Jonah Thornbury's name was in volume nine. I found it eleven years after the fact, in a reading room that smelled of tallow and regret, and I remember thinking: *here is a debt that could unmake a hero.* And I remember thinking, a moment later, in the small honest voice I kept locked in the back of my skull where the Order couldn't hear it: *here is a woman who paid everything, and everyone who profited from her payment signed this page but her.*

I did not go to her because I was brave. Let no one — let no god, let no chronicler, let no well-meaning ghost — record that Anselm of the Ledgered Word descended into the dark out of courage. I went because I was old, and tired, and ashamed, and because shame is a debt too, and I had been carrying mine so long it had begun to accrue interest.

The chest was with me now, in the outer court, behind a pillar of ice carved with faces of people who were smiling because they had been made to smile forever. Forty-one volumes. Every name. Every debt.

And the Graycloaks were coming to collect me.

* * *

You should understand what Graycloaks do, since I trained two generations of them.

They do not primarily hunt witches. That is the popular error, encouraged by the Order's own propaganda, because it is more flattering to be a hunter of monsters than what we actually were. What Graycloaks actually do is *audit*. We are the crown's accountants of the forbidden. When magic surfaces somewhere — a charm, a prayer answered, a frost blooming in high summer — we trace it to its source, we assess its value, and we determine whether it can be taxed, licensed, or destroyed. The burning comes last, and honestly, it is the least of it. Fire is quick. The paperwork is eternal.

I know the procedures because I wrote the manual. Chapter four: *On the Containment of Miracles.* Chapter seven: *On the Interrogation of the God-Touched.* I wrote chapter seven. There is a section in it about children. I have not been able to reread that section in twenty years, and I will not describe it here, except to say that Wren was exactly the sort of child chapter seven was written for, and that if they took her, the men following those horns would know precisely what to do, because I had taught them, carefully, in my neat archival hand.

"They'll come through the Pilgrim's Gate," Cassian said. He had a map of the court scratched into the frost on the floor, and he was moving pebbles around on it like a general, which he had never been, though he had the temperament of one — all cold assessment and hidden cost. "Six hours, maybe less. They'll have a soul-reader with them."

"A what?" Jonah asked.

"A reader," Cassian said. "Someone who can look at a person and see whether they're... whole. Whether they've got everything they started with." He didn't look at her when he said it. He hadn't been able to look at her directly since the drowned shrine, since Vessia had spoken through Wren and said things about the two of them that neither of them had wanted said aloud. "If they read you, Jonah, they'll know immediately. You're missing a piece. It shows, to someone trained. Like a window with the light off."

"And Wren?" I asked quietly.

"Wren lights up like a festival," Cassian said. "She's a crack in the world. She's the loudest thing in this mountain."

Wren, to her credit, looked pleased about this.

"We run deeper," Jonah said. "Past the court, toward the Spire's heart. If the ledger's there—"

"It's there," Cassian said. "Everything's there. That's the whole point of the place."

"Then we run deeper, and we break it, and none of this matters."

It was a good plan. It was even, in its way, a true plan. But plans require time, and time requires that no one be standing at the gate with white fire and a warrant, and I did the arithmetic — I am, whatever else has rotted in me, still an accountant — and the arithmetic would not balance.

Six souls running. Two of them slow: myself, and the chest. One of them loud: the child. One of them marked: the storm-caller. Against them, a full censure company, fresh, equipped, and led — I knew it would be led by him, I knew it the way you know rain is coming by the ache in your knee — by Prelate Corvane, my finest student, my successor, the man who had learned my language better than I had and never once stumbled over a word of it.

There was only one asset in the court that could buy six hours against Corvane.

It was not courage. I want that recorded. It was bookkeeping.

* * *

I waited until they slept. That is the coward's privilege and the coward's gift; you may only be noble when no one is awake to stop you.

The court of small mercies glowed faintly, the way deep snow glows under moonlight, and the smiling souls stood in their alcoves and along their colonnades, warm-faced and glass-eyed, forever content with their purchases. I passed among them with my chest on my back and my breath smoking, and I confess I spoke to one of them. A woman in a merchant's coat, sold thirty years ago to pay a gambling debt, smiling her fixed smile.

"You should not have been allowed to sign," I told her. "No one should have been allowed to draw up the contract. I drew up contracts like yours. I thought I was keeping records. I thought a thing written down honestly was a thing done honestly." Her smile did not change. It was, at least, a fair hearing. "I am going to burn the books," I said. "All of them. Yours first, if I can find the volume. You'll be the easiest debt in the whole ledger to void, and I want you voided quickly."

She smiled. Somewhere inside the smile, perhaps, something heard me. I have chosen to believe so. Belief, at my age, is less a conviction than a preference, and I prefer it.

The Pilgrim's Gate stood at the far end of the court, a black arch through which the Graycloaks would come, and beside it — this was the detail that mattered, the detail I had carried in my head since the drowned shrine — stood the court's great brazier, an iron bowl tall as a man, in which burned the pale flame the locals called *the king's candle*: fire fed on warmth drawn up from the world above. Every hearth gone cold in Cinderhollow. Every crop blackened. Every answered prayer, rendered down to heat, piped beneath the mountain to keep the dead comfortable.

It was, I realized, standing before it with my hand on the chest's lock, the largest single concentration of stolen warmth in the court.

It was also, being a brazier, flammable.

Gods forgive me the joke. I needed it. My hands were shaking.

* * *

Corvane arrived early anyway. Of course he did. I taught him that too: *arrive before your estimate, and let the enemy's clock be the one that breaks.*

I heard the boots before I saw the gray. They came through the Pilgrim's Gate in column, torches hooded, censers swinging on chains, and at their head walked a tall man in a prelate's gray trimmed with white, carrying a soul-reader's lens on a cord around his neck like a saint's medal. Fifty of them. I had commanded five hundred once. The Order's decline, like my own, had been gradual enough that neither of us had noticed dying.

"Prelate Corvane," I said, stepping out of the arch's shadow. "You're late. I expected you an hour ago."

He stopped. For a moment — I will treasure this moment, wherever I am kept — the great Corvane simply stared, and his face performed the small catastrophe of a man seeing his teacher where his training said no one should be.

"Brother Anselm." He recovered quickly; I had also taught him that. "You're alive. The reports said dead, in Cinderhollow, of age and drink."

"The reports were half right. I am in Cinderhollow."

"You are beneath a mountain in the Spire's court, in the company of a heretic, an apostate envoy, and a vessel of the old gods." His voice carried the flat music of recitation. He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it in my cadences; I could hear my own rhetoric wearing his voice like a stolen coat. "By the statutes of Censure, article twelve—"

"Article twelve concerns the destruction of proscribed texts," I said. "Yes. I wrote it. Which is why I must inform you, as the ranking authority present, that the proscribed texts in question are about to be destroyed, and that you are late to witness it."

I opened the chest.

Forty-one volumes. Eleven years on a handcart. Every name, every debt, every careful entry in my careful hand — including, in volume nine, the page that had begun all of this, the page with a storm-caller's name and a date and a price, the page I had carried across three provinces not to preserve but, I understood finally, to return.

I threw them into the brazier one at a time.

* * *

Here is what I did not anticipate: the books screamed.

Not the paper — paper burns with a whisper and a smell. The *debts* screamed. Each volume, as the flames took it, released what it held, and what it held was warmth — stolen warmth, contracted warmth, the heat of ten thousand hearths and harvests and living bodies, all of it escrowed in ink. Volume after volume went up, and the brazier roared higher, and the light coming off it was not orange but gold, gold like summer, gold like a field at harvest, and the temperature of that frozen court rose for the first time in centuries.

The smiling souls felt it. I saw it happen. Their smiles did not change — they could not change, they were not theirs anymore — but their eyes did. Something moved behind the glass. Something turned over, like a sleeper hearing a familiar voice.

"Stop him!" Corvane's composure cracked clean through. "Stop him, you idiots, that is crown property, that is *collateral*—"

Graycloaks broke ranks and ran at me, and I will not pretend I fought them, because I did not fight them; fighting was never in my curriculum. I threw books. That was the whole of my strategy and the whole of my life's final act: an old man hurling archives into a fire while young men in gray tried to drag him away from the flames. If any chronicler is reading this, I ask only that you render it accurately. Accuracy was the entire religion I ever had, and I betrayed it for fifty years, and I would like to die inside it.

Volume nine went in fourth from the last. I saved it deliberately. I wanted to watch.

The page with Jonah Thornbury's name caught, curled, went gold, and the scream it gave was long and rising and ended in something that was almost — I swear this on what remains of my faith — almost a *laugh*. And far above us, far above the court and the mountain and the winter, I felt rather than heard a storm begin to gather over a world that had forgotten how to make them.

Somewhere up there, a woman who had sold her soul to save ten thousand strangers was running deeper into the dark with a child and a traitor, and she did not yet know that her debt had just been voided, that the window with the light off had been paid off, that she was — for the first time in ten years — *whole*, and owed nothing to anyone.

I hope she finds out soon. I hope she is insufferable about it.

* * *

They took the chest from me at the end, when there was nothing left in it but ash and the smell of scorched vellum, and they took the lens-reading of my soul while they were at it, because Corvane is thorough. The reader — a young woman who would not meet my eyes, who had perhaps been in my lecture hall once, whose name I mercifully did not know — looked at me through the lens and frowned.

"He's clean," she said. "Mostly. There's..." She adjusted the lens. "Prelate, there's almost nothing left to read. It's like a house after the furniture's been sold. Just rooms."

"Rooms will suffice," Corvane said. "Bind him. He'll testify."

"About what?" I asked, pleasantly, as they put the irons on. My wrists remembered the shape of shackles from the other side of the table; it is remarkable what the body keeps. "You have no texts, Prelate. No records. No ledger. Whatever happens in this mountain now happens unwritten, unrecorded, unowed. You may burn me, and it will change nothing, because there is nothing left in the world for my testimony to attach to." I smiled at him, and it was the first genuinely honest expression of my life. "You should have come sooner. I taught you that, too."

Corvane looked at the brazier, still roaring with forty-one volumes' worth of stolen summer, and then at me, and I saw him arrive at the understanding that would define the rest of his career: that the audit had failed, that the accounts were destroyed, that the court beneath the mountain had just lost its claim on every soul it held — and that the only man who fully understood what that meant was standing in shackles, grinning at him like a drunk at a wedding.

"Take him to the holding cells by the Gate," he said finally. "And send runners to the Spire's heart. The heretic is heading for the ledger. If it burns like these did—" He stopped. He could not finish the sentence. Nobody can finish that sentence. A world without the ledger is a world where nothing is owed, and no one in power, living or dead, knows how to govern such a place.

They marched me past the alcoves of the smiling souls, and I looked at each of them as I went. The merchant woman was last. As the Graycloaks pulled me by, her glass eye rolled — barely, barely — and fixed on me, and though her smile never moved, I heard, or dreamed I heard, or choose to spend my remaining years believing I heard, a voice like a page turning:

*Thank you.*

"You're welcome," I said aloud, to the horror of my escort. "Tell the others the books are balanced."

* * *

The holding cells by the Pilgrim's Gate are cut from the mountain's root, and they are cold, and I am old, and I do not expect to leave them. I have made my peace with the arithmetic. Every life is a ledger; mine closes in credit, which is more than I ever expected, and more than I deserve, and exactly what I chose.

But I can hear, through the stone, the sounds of the company moving deeper — the child's laughter echoing up the shafts like a bell rung underground, the storm-wind gathering somewhere above that has no caller and needs none, and underneath it all, growing louder, a sound I recognize from the oldest pages of the oldest books, a sound the Order spent five hundred years insisting was metaphor:

The buried king has noticed that his books are burning.

And he is coming up the mountain to find out who is holding the matches — and the only person standing between him and the Spire's heart is a woman who does not yet know she is free.
