## 16. Storm Without a Caller

I have fled from exactly three things in my life, and I can tell you the exact flavor of fear each one left in my mouth.

The first was the Graycloaks, when I was nineteen and stupid enough to call lightning over a festival crowd because a boy I liked asked me to. That fear tasted like copper and stolen plums.

The second was the court beneath the mountain, ten years later, when I signed my name into a book that weighed more than I did. That fear tasted like nothing at all, which is worse.

The third was now — running up the pilgrim road with Wren's hand crushed in mine and Anselm's breath sawing behind us and Cassian somewhere back in the dark buying us seconds with whatever coin he had left, which was never money but always cost him anyway. This fear tasted like ash and old snow, and underneath it, faintly, like the air before lightning.

That last part is what made me stop running.

Because I knew that taste. I had spent a decade pretending not to know it, the way you pretend not to know your own face in a window at night, but I knew it. The storm-sense. The pressure behind the eyes, the itch along the scarred wrists where the brand met skin, the feeling of the sky leaning down to ask what it should do with itself.

The sky was asking. For the first time in ten years, the sky was *asking*.

"Jonah." Anselm caught up to me, gray-robed and gray-faced, his ledger-fragment clutched to his chest like a talisman that had stopped working. "Don't stop. Whatever you're thinking of doing, don't stop."

"I'm going to seal the passage."

"With what?"

"Rain," I said. "Ice. A hundred tons of mountain brought down on the road behind us so that nothing wearing a dead man's face can follow us out of here." I turned to look back down the way we'd come, into the throat of the world, where the frozen court glittered like a lie told beautifully. Somewhere down there, Anselm's sacrifice was still holding — he'd burned forty years of records and let them take him for it, and every hour we gained was an hour bought with his freedom. I wasn't going to waste it. "So stand clear."

Wren tugged my sleeve. She had frost in her eyelashes and gods in her mouth, and she looked at me with an expression no child should have to wear: pity.

"You can't," she said.

"Wren—"

"They took it back," she said simply. "When they started screaming through me. They said—" her face went distant, the way it did when Vessia spoke through the cracks in her, and then came back, and she finished in her own voice, small and terrible: "They said the storm was never yours to begin with."

* * *

Here is what I believed about myself for thirty-one years, and what I am about to describe dismantling in the space of four breaths:

I believed the storm lived in me.

Not metaphorically. Not in some poet's sense of *the storm within*. I mean I believed there was weather in my blood, that when I raised my hands the clouds answered because they were mine, called by mine, bound to me the way a hound is bound to the whistle. When I broke the siege of Harrowgate I believed I had done it. Me. Jonah Thornbury, storm-caller, the woman whose name got legally erased from every record in the kingdom because she was too dangerous to remember.

And when they exiled me, when they branded my wrists and scrubbed my deeds from the archives and left me selling luck-knots in Cinderhollow, I believed I had chosen to be quiet. I believed the silence was discipline. Ten years of small charms and small rain and never once letting the sky lean down — I thought that was restraint. I thought I was a dammed river.

I was not a dammed river. I was a dry creek bed, and someone upstream had been kind enough, or cruel enough, to keep the water coming.

"Stand clear," I said again, because I am stubborn, and stubbornness is the last thing you lose.

Anselm stepped back. Wren didn't. She just watched me with those cracked-mirror eyes while I turned to face the dark of the passage below and did the thing I had not done since Harrowgate.

I reached for the storm.

It's hard to describe what reaching feels like when it works. It's like opening a door you've kept locked so long you've forgotten what's behind it, except the door opens onto weather. Onto pressure and charge and the vast slow attention of the sky turning its face toward you. At Harrowgate it had felt like being *known* by something enormous and deciding, in the same instant, to be worthy of it. I had built my whole self on that feeling. Every year of exile, every charm sold, every night I lay awake missing a power I refused to use — all of it rested on the certainty that the door was still there, still locked, still mine.

I opened it.

There was nothing behind it.

* * *

Not silence. Silence would have been bearable. Silence would have meant the door was stuck, or the storm was sulking, or exile had finally rusted the hinges. What I found instead was worse than silence: I found the shape of where something used to be. Like pressing your tongue into the socket of a lost tooth. Like walking into your childhood home and finding the rooms empty and the walls still bearing the pale rectangles where pictures hung.

Something had been lent to me. And the loan had been called in.

"No," I said, out loud, like a madwoman talking to a doorway. Because that's what I was. "No, you don't get to do this. You don't get to lend a girl a storm, let her spend it saving ten thousand lives, then take it back and leave her the bill."

The bill. Oh, there was a word for it now, wasn't there. I'd seen the ledger. I'd stood in the gilded cold of the court of small mercies and read names in a book that traded souls like coin, and I had understood, intellectually, that the whole economy ran on debt. But I had never once put myself in the arithmetic. I had sold one soul — mine — and considered the transaction closed.

But storms aren't souls. Storms are *permission*. And permission, it turns out, has a lender.

Wren touched my hand. Her fingers were cold as shrine-stone. "They're scared," she said. "That's why. They need the ledger whole to get out, and they know what you are, even if you don't. So they took the leash off early. They took everything back."

"The storm was a leash?"

"Everything given is," Wren said, with the flat wisdom of children and goddesses, who are often the same thing. "You just liked the collar because it was warm."

I want to tell you I laughed. I want to tell you I raged, hurled curses at the sky, did something dramatic and narratively satisfying. What I actually did was sit down. Right there on the frozen pilgrim road, in the dark under the mountain's roots, with pursuit behind us and winter above us, I sat down on the ice like an old woman at a market stall and put my head in my hands.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about losing the thing that made you *you*: you don't just lose it. You lose every story you told about yourself that depended on it. And I had been telling one story for ten years, over and over, in the dark, to keep myself company:

*I could have escaped any time. I stayed small because I chose to.*

Take away the storm and what's left? A woman who sold rain-charms in Cinderhollow. A woman whose great deed was done by borrowed weather. A woman exiled not because she was dangerous but because she was *convenient* — because the crown needed a name to erase and the gods needed a debtor to keep, and I fit both ledgers nicely.

"Jonah." Anselm crouched beside me. He smelled of smoke and burnt vellum; he would smell of that for the rest of his life, I think, the way men do after they burn their own histories. "I have spent my whole career reading accounts of people like you. Storm-callers, tide-speakers, flame-hearths. The order's doctrine was very clear: the gift dwells in the vessel." He paused. "The order was wrong about most things. I burned the records partly for that reason."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying that if the power was always borrowed, then the sin was never in the power." He looked at my wrists, at the brands. "And neither was the virtue. Which means everything else you did — the choosing, the staying, the ten years of small mercies in Cinderhollow — that was all you. Unborrowed."

"That's very kind, Anselm."

"It wasn't kindness. It was accounting." He almost smiled. "I'm a ledger-man. It's the only prayer I know."

* * *

We heard them before we saw them — the things wearing the faces of the dead, coming up the passage behind us. Not fast. They never hurry; the dead have excellent credit and all the time in the world. But the sound of them, that soft many-footed slush of footsteps, carried up the dark like water finding its level.

Cassian's voice came echoing up from below, hoarse and furious: "*Run*, Jonah!"

And I did something I hadn't planned. I stood up, faced the dark, and raised my hands anyway.

Not because I expected lightning. Not because I believed anymore. I raised my hands because if the storm was gone — if it had never been mine — then whatever I did next had to be done by the only thing left in me that was. And I wanted, just once, to act from that unborrowed place before I died or was taken or forgot everyone I loved. I wanted to know what Jonah Thornbury could do with nothing.

Nothing, it turns out, includes a voice.

"There is no storm coming," I shouted down the passage, and my voice cracked against the ice and came back doubled, trebled, thrown by the acoustics of the mountain's throat until it sounded like thunder anyway. "There is no caller. There's just me, and I have nothing left to take, and I am *still standing in your way.*"

The footsteps slowed.

Maybe it was the echo. Maybe it was the sheer audacity of a disarmed woman threatening a legion of the dead with her good posture. Or maybe — and this is the thought I've turned over every night since — maybe the things wearing the faces of the dead recognized something. A soul with nothing left to collect. A zero balance walking upright and refusing to move.

Whatever it was, they stopped. And in the ringing silence, Wren slipped her cold little hand into mine and said, wonderingly:

"They're afraid of you."

"Don't be absurd."

"They are. Vessia says—" and then her face went distant and the goddess spoke through her, and the goddess's voice out of a child's mouth will never stop being the worst sound in the world: "*The uncollared dog bites hardest. We should not have shown her the empty room.*"

Then the goddess was gone and Wren was Wren again, blinking, shivering, and far below us Cassian was shouting my name, and above us the first flakes of high-summer snow were sifting down through a crack in the mountain like ash from a fire we couldn't see yet.

Anselm put his hand on my shoulder. "Up," he said. "We go up. Whatever you are now — and I suspect, Mrs. Thornbury, that you are considerably more than you were this morning — the world above needs to meet you before the winter finishes arriving."

I looked at my hands one last time. Ordinary hands. Charm-seller's hands. Hands that had held a dying woman's luck-knot and a child's fingers and, once, long ago, the face of a man I loved while he testified me into erasure.

No storm in them. No borrowed lightning. Just me, whatever that was worth.

"Up," I agreed.

And we climbed toward the winter, carrying the only weapon I had left, which was the truth about myself, still warm, still unborrowed, and — though none of us knew it yet — heavy enough to break a book older than the world.
