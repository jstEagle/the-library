## 17. The Ledger's Arithmetic

I have spent ten years learning to read the language of debts.

It is not a skill they teach you in the capital, though perhaps it should be. The crown's envoys are expected to master treaties and tariffs, the polite fictions of borders and grain quotas. But the true work of my office—the work I was sent beneath the mountain to do, the work I have done with hands I sometimes no longer recognize as my own—is arithmetic. Souls in, souls owed, interest accrued. The court of the buried king keeps its books the way a miser keeps his coin: close, and cold, and always balanced.

So when Jonah thrust the ledger fragment into my hands in the frozen antechamber where we'd made our camp—such as camp can be made in a place where fire gives up its heat to the walls before it warms your fingers—I understood, for the first time since Harrowgate, what I was looking at.

"You've been staring at this for an hour," she said. "Either you're reading it or you're praying over it."

"Both," I said. "Though I'm not sure which frightens me more."

She almost smiled. It was a thin thing, that smile, worn down by the road and the cold and whatever she'd seen in the outer court—the smiling souls, the gilded halls, the terrible comfort of people who had traded everything and called it fair. But it was there, and I clung to it like a drowning man clings to driftwood, because I had not earned her smiles in ten years and did not expect to earn them now.

"Talk," she said.

* * *

The ledger is written in a script older than the crown, older than the order Anselm served, older than the names of most of the gods. But scripts are only languages wearing formal clothes, and I have spent a decade dressing down. The buried king's clerks—souls, all of them, bound to their work by debts of their own—had taught me the grammar of obligation. Every entry follows the same structure: *name, debt, term, interest.*

Name. Debt. Term. Interest.

It was the interest column that had kept me staring.

Every soul in the ledger carried an interest notation, but not the kind you'd expect. Not coin. Not years. The entries recorded warmth—not metaphorically, not poetically, but as a quantity, measured in some unit I had no name for until I saw the pattern. And beside each entry, a date: the day the prayer was answered.

"Jonah," I said, "when did the gods leave?"

She frowned. "Leave? They didn't leave. They went quiet. After the Spire was built, after the old worship was outlawed—they just... stopped answering. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows wrong." I turned the fragment toward her. "Look at this entry. A prayer answered at a shrine in the Weald, sixty years ago. Look at the interest column."

She leaned close. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron, and I hated myself for noticing, for the way ten years collapsed into nothing when she was near enough to touch. Focus, Cassian. You owe her that much focus.

"That's not currency," she said slowly.

"No. It's heat. Actual heat. Degrees of it, drawn from—" I traced the line across the page. "From somewhere. From everywhere. Each answered prayer draws warmth out of the world and into the Spire's reserves. That's how the court pays for itself. That's how the king keeps his halls golden and his souls preserved. He isn't hoarding souls, Jonah. He's hoarding summer."

"But the gods haven't answered prayers in decades—"

"They fled, Jonah. They didn't go quiet. They ran." I heard my own voice crack and didn't bother to smooth it. "And every prayer that gets answered now—every miracle, every frost-blooming shrine, every bit of wonder Wren carries around like a lantern—they're not gifts. They're withdrawals. The gods are escaping through the cracks, and each escape costs warmth, and the ledger records the debt, and the debt gets collected."

"The winter," she said.

"The winter isn't punishment. It's not the king being cruel, or the gods being angry. It's collection." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, brittle as ice on a pond. "All those refugees saying the cold follows the miracles? They're right. It does. Every answered prayer is a little hole in the world's coat, and the warmth runs out of it, down here, into his coffers. The world is freezing because the gods are running up a tab."

Jonah sat back. In the gloom of the antechamber, lit by the pale gold glow that seeped from the walls themselves—the stolen warmth of sixty years of prayers, radiating out of the masonry like a hearth built from other people's summers—her face was very still.

"Wren," she said. "The prayers at the shrines. Vessia answering the child at the cracked altar. All of it."

"All of it. Every miracle since the first one has been drawing the world colder." I closed my eyes. "And here's the part I couldn't make myself say until now. Look at the oldest entries. The founding entries."

She took the fragment from me. I watched her read, watched her lips move slightly, counting, the way she used to count when we were young and poor and she was tallying charm-sales against rent. Some habits survive even exile.

"These go back further than the Spire," she said. "Further than the mountain. These are..." She looked up. "Cassian, these are creation entries."

"Yes."

"The warmth of the world. The seasons. The sun itself. It was all—" She stopped. Started again. "It was all borrowed?"

"The whole world is a loan," I said. "That's what the Spire is. That's what the buried king is. He's not a tyrant who seized power. He's a creditor. The gods borrowed the warmth of creation to make the world, and the ledger records the debt, and someone has to hold the book and call it due. The winter isn't the end of the world, Jonah. It's the end of the *term*. The loan is coming due, and there's nothing left to pay it with except everything."

* * *

Wren found us there, later—or maybe she'd been there the whole time, curled in the shadow of a pillar, listening with that unnerving stillness she could summon when she chose. She was eating something she'd scavenged; I didn't ask what. The child had a way of making questions feel obscene.

"It voids," Wren said, mouth full.

Jonah looked at her. "What voids?"

"The book. If you break it." Wren swallowed and wiped her chin with her sleeve, a gesture so ordinary, so childlike, that it seemed impossible she was also a crack in the world through which goddesses screamed. "Anselm said it too, before. Debts need a record. No record, no debt. That's why the king guards it. That's why he wants you to hold it instead. If the book breaks, everyone walks free."

"Everyone," I said carefully. "Including—"

"Including me," Jonah finished.

And there it was. The thing I had come down the mountain to say and had been circling for hours like a moth around a lamp it both wanted and feared.

Her soul. The one she'd sold on the night of Harrowgate, the payment that bought the storm that saved ten thousand lives. It sat in the ledger like any other entry: *name, debt, term, interest.* Ten years of interest, drawn from the world's warmth, from her own small life in Cinderhollow, from every rain-charm she'd sold while her real self rotted in a vault beneath a mountain.

Break the ledger, and the entry voids. She walks free. Whole. Herself, for the first time since the siege.

I should have led with that. Any decent man would have led with that. Instead I'd buried it under the arithmetic, because I am a coward in the ways that matter most, and because I knew—I knew—there would be fine print.

"There's a cost," I said.

Jonah's eyes came up to mine. Green-grey, storm-colored, the same eyes that had watched me testify against her while I died inside. "Of course there is. There's always a cost. Say it."

"The ledger doesn't just record debts. It *enforces* them. It's the mechanism. Break the enforcement mechanism and every debt becomes uncollectible—but the breaking has to be paid for. The book demands a binding from whoever breaks it. Something held in trust, transferred at the moment of shattering." I made myself keep talking, made myself walk into it. "I don't know what form it takes. The clerks' notes mention 'the breaker's portion.' Nothing more specific."

"So I break it, and it takes... something."

"Something. Yes."

"And you're telling me this now because?"

"Because you deserve to choose with open eyes." I spread my hands. "Because I spent ten years choosing for you, or rather, letting others choose for you while I stood by and called it necessity, and I will not do it again. Whatever it takes from you—whatever the breaker's portion is—you should know it exists before you reach for that book."

Wren had gone quiet in the way she did when the gods pressed close. Her eyes had that faraway shine, frost gathering at her lashes though the air was warm-ish by Spire standards. When she spoke, her voice had harmonics in it, two voices braided, and neither of them was entirely a child's.

"*The breaker pays,*" she said. "*The breaker pays in what the breaker holds dearest.*"

"Wren—" Jonah started.

"*Not a riddle,*" the girl-goddess-thing said, shaking her head hard, and just like that she was Wren again, small and scared and furious. "It's not a riddle, it's just what they know. What you hold dearest. That's the price. That's the fine print." She looked at Jonah, and then—at me. And I felt the look like a blade sliding between ribs, because the child had spent weeks with us, had listened to us, had learned to read us the way I'd learned to read ledgers.

She knew what Jonah held dearest.

I knew what Jonah held dearest.

And I knew, with the certainty of a man who has spent a decade doing the court's arithmetic, that it wasn't me. How could it be? I was the betrayal. I was the testimony. I was the wound she carried. What she held dearest was the life she'd built in spite of me—the small safe life, the charms and the market stall, the neighbors whose names she knew, the dying woman she'd sold a luck-knot to. Her memories of the world she'd made when the world had taken everything else.

Or maybe—maybe—it was the people. Anselm, who burned his order's records rather than let her be taken. Wren, who followed her like a duckling imprints on the first warm thing it sees. Maybe it was them. Maybe it was all of them, every soul she'd let past her walls in ten careful years.

"Whatever it takes," Jonah said quietly, "it's less than what the winter takes if we do nothing."

"That's not—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't you dare do the thing where you decide my sacrifice is too precious to allow. You don't get to spend ten years selling me to save your sister and then discover scruples now, Cassian. I know what you did. I know about Mira. I know about the souls you've been buying back, one by one, for a decade, like a man bailing a flood with a thimble." Her voice softened, which was worse than anger. "You did what you could with the choices you were given. So did I. This is mine."

I looked at her—this woman I had loved and ruined and loved anyway, who had walked into exile and out of legend and down into hell itself to take back a soul she'd sold to save strangers—and I understood that I had never once deserved her, and that deserving had never once been the point.

"The clerks' notes," I said, because my voice needed somewhere to go that wasn't begging. "There's one more thing. About the binding."

"Say it."

"The notes say the breaker's portion is *proportional*. The more the breaker holds dear, the more it takes. The court calls it mercy." I smiled, and felt my face crack around it. "They designed it so that only the empty-handed can break the book cheaply. Only someone who loves nothing walks away whole."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off in the gilded dark, the smiling souls sang their endless contented songs, and the walls gave off their stolen warmth like a hearth built from other people's summers.

"So the price of freeing the world," Jonah said at last, "is proportional to how much of it you love."

"Yes."

"And I love—" She stopped. Looked at Wren. At the passage where Anselm had gone ahead to scout, alone, because that was the kind of man he'd decided to become too late in life to enjoy it. At me, last, longest, and I could not read what passed behind her eyes, and that frightened me more than the king, more than the winter, more than anything waiting below.

"I love enough," she said simply. "Let's go break a book."

* * *

We caught up with Anselm at the threshold of the inner court, where the gold light thickened into something almost solid, and the temperature climbed in a way that should have been comforting and was instead the worst thing yet—warmth, real warmth, the kind that meant the walls around us were dense with harvested summers, with the heat of harvest festivals and hearth-fires and mothers' hands on feverish brows, all of it banked here like grain in a granary while the world above starved for it.

Anselm had his back to us. He was looking at something on the floor.

"Cassian," he said, without turning. His voice was strange. Flat. "Your clerks' training. Come look at this."

I went to him. Jonah and Wren followed.

Laid into the stone of the threshold, worn nearly smooth by centuries of feet, was an inscription. Ledger-script. The grammar of obligation. I knelt and read it, and as I read, the hope I had been carrying like a coal in my chest—the hope of the voided debt, of Jonah walking free, of the world's warmth spilling back into spring—began, very quietly, to die.

*What is broken must be replaced. What is voided must be rebound. The book cannot end; it can only change hands.*

"It's a warning," Anselm said. "Carved by the builders. Before the king. Before any of it."

I read it again. Then a third time, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something kinder. They didn't. Words never do.

"It means the ledger can't be destroyed," I said. "Only transferred. Breaking it doesn't void the debts—it forces a rebinding. A new ledger. A new holder." I looked up at Jonah, and the storm-colored eyes were already understanding, already three steps ahead of me the way she always was, and I hated the world for making her pay for being clever.

"Someone has to hold it," she said.

"Someone has to hold it," I agreed. "Forever. Bound to it, the way the king is bound. The throne isn't a reward, Jonah. It's the job posting. The king isn't a monster—he's the previous fool who broke the book."

Behind us, Wren began to scream.

Not the child's scream and not the goddess's scream but both together, harmonics tearing out of her small body as frost exploded across the golden floor, and through her mouth came a voice like an avalanche given language:

"*SHE MUST NOT HOLD IT. SHE MUST NOT BREAK IT. SHE MUST NOT—*"

And beneath the screaming, faint but rising, from the deep places of the Spire where the heart of the book waited on its pedestal of frozen light—we heard footsteps. Many. Patient. Coming up to meet us.

The king had known we were coming all along.
