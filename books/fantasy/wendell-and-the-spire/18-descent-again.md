## 18. Descent Again

There is a particular kind of madness that arrives quietly, dressed as common sense. You don't recognize it at first. It looks like resolve. It sounds like a plan. It stands at the mouth of a frozen tunnel with its hands on its hips and says, *obviously we go back down*, and you nod along like it's the most reasonable thing anyone's said all winter.

"We are not going back down," I said.

"Jonah." Cassian had that voice he used to use when I was about to do something spectacular and stupid — the voice of a man who had learned, over ten years of loving me and then testifying against me, that both were equally inevitable. "The ledger's in the heart of the Spire. The arithmetic doesn't work unless—"

"I heard the arithmetic. I was standing right here for the arithmetic." I looked at Wren instead, because looking at Cassian still cost something, even now, even after the confession, even after I'd decided — provisionally, grudgingly, the way you decide to trust a rope bridge — that his betrayal came with an asterisk long enough to fill a page. "Wren. Tell him."

Wren was sitting on a shelf of black ice with her knees pulled up to her chin, shivering in a way that had nothing to do with cold. Her eyes were doing the thing they did lately: flickering between brown and a pale glacial blue, like a lamp behind frosted glass. When she spoke, her voice came out doubled, hers and something vast underneath it, two singers a half-beat apart.

*Turn back,* said the god in her mouth. *Little storm-caller. Turn back and live.*

"See?" I said. "Even the apocalypse agrees with me."

"That's exactly why we can't listen to it," Cassian said.

And there it was. The quiet madness. He was right, and I knew he was right, and every step of logic between where we stood and the Spire's heart was sound, load-bearing, inspected and approved. That was the horror of it. Good reasoning had brought us here. Good reasoning would take us back down through the court of smiling dead people, past the gilded alcoves where souls sat in comfortable forever, down to the one book in the world that had to break.

It didn't make it less mad. It just made it *ours*.

"Say it plainly," I told him. "I want to hear how insane it sounds out loud."

Cassian took a breath. Ten years ago he'd been beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful. Now he was beautiful the way a worn tool is — dulled, dented, still true. "We go back into the mountain. Voluntarily. Through the court, because there's no other road. We find the ledger before the Graycloaks regroup, before the king's things find us again, before—" he glanced at Wren, gently, the way you'd touch a wound, "—before they stop being able to hold her. And Jonah breaks it."

"And if she breaks it wrong?"

"Then we're no worse off than if the world freezes."

"That's not true," I said. "If she breaks it wrong, the ledger takes a binding from the breaker. It told us that much itself. Books always tell you their teeth before they bite; it's how they get you close enough." I flexed my hands. My wrists ached under the wrappings, the old brand-scarred skin remembering fires I'd spent a decade pretending hadn't happened. "Something gets taken. Maybe more than something."

Wren lifted her head. For a moment she was only a girl — nine years old, maybe ten, feral as a fox kit, with frost in her hair that never melted anymore. "You'd lose us," she said. Simply. The way children say the worst things. "That's what it would take. It already showed me. It shows me everything, because I'm a crack, and cracks see what's on the other side." She hugged her knees tighter. "You'd walk out of the mountain and look at Cassian like he was a stranger. Look at me like I was nobody's girl. And the world would be warm, and spring would come, and you wouldn't remember why you cried about it."

The tunnel was very quiet. Somewhere far above, wind moved over the mountain's shoulders, and somewhere far below, the buried king sat on his throne of debts and waited, patient as interest.

"Yes," I said.

Wren flinched.

"Yes, that's probably the price. Yes, I'm going to pay it anyway." I crouched down to her level, which put my face in the path of that flickering blue light, and I made myself hold it. "Listen to me, crack-girl. The gods want the ledger intact. You've heard them screaming it since the drowned shrine. They need it whole to tear their way out of this world, and they'll burn everything down to the roots getting free — Vessia said so herself, and Vessia has never once lied to you, which makes her the most dangerous creature we've met yet. If we turn back, if we run, if we let them keep their book, then everyone who ever prayed and got an answer keeps paying. Every answered prayer since the gods fled, drawing warmth out of the world like blood from a vein. High summer snow. Blackened fields. Children starving in a famine caused by *gratitude*." I heard my own voice go low and rough, the old storm-caller rising in my throat where no storm would answer it anymore. "I sold my soul once to save ten thousand lives. I know how these bargains work. And I am telling you: this one, we break."

"You won't remember breaking it," Wren whispered.

"No," I agreed. "But you will. And Cassian will. And Anselm, wherever they're keeping him." My chest did something complicated around Anselm's name — the old priest who'd burned a lifetime of obedient records to buy our escape, who'd walked back into the Graycloaks' chains with his chin up like a man settling accounts. If the ledger broke, his debts voided too. His chains with them. That was worth carrying, even if I couldn't carry the memory of carrying it. "Someone remembers. That's enough. That's the whole trick of it, Wren — somebody always has to remember, or nothing anyone sacrifices means anything."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — a small, cracked, entirely human laugh, and the blue light guttered out of her eyes like a candle pinched between fingers.

"The gods hate you," she said fondly. "They called you *the stubborn weather*."

"They're not wrong."

* * *

Going down was worse than coming up. Coming up, we'd been fleeing, and fear is a fast horse. Going down, we were choosing, and choice walks.

The pilgrim road descended in switchbacks through the mountain's roots, past walls veined with ice that wasn't ice — it caught the light wrong, held it too long, gave it back the color of old gold. Cassian went first with a lantern whose flame burned blue and gave no heat. I followed. Wren came last, roped to my belt, because the gods kept trying to wear her like a coat and I wanted to feel it when they reached for the strings.

"Tell me again why the court won't stop us," Cassian said over his shoulder. His breath fogged and hung in the air too long, reluctant to disperse, as if even his breathing was being recorded somewhere for later collection.

"Because the court doesn't stop anyone," I said. "That's the whole obscenity of it. The court *invites*. Everyone down here traded themselves willingly — that's what makes it currency instead of theft. Souls given freely spend better than souls taken." I remembered the outer court from our first pass through: the gilded alcoves, the frozen revelers, the woman who had smiled at us with perfect contentment while frost grew in her open eyes. "They'll smile at us. They'll offer. That's all they'll do. The king wants me on the throne beside him more than he wants me stopped, and the gods want the ledger intact more than they want me alive. Nobody with power in this mountain actually benefits from killing us."

"How comforting," Cassian said dryly. "We're protected by everyone's greed."

"It's the only protection that's never failed me."

He almost smiled. Then the road opened into the court, and neither of us smiled at anything for a while.

* * *

The Court of Small Mercies had not changed, except that it was colder, and the cold had teeth now. The gilded alcoves blazed along both walls like a street of lit windows, and behind each one a soul sat in its purchased paradise. A merchant counted coins that never diminished. A mother rocked a child who never woke and never needed feeding. A young man stood at a window that looked out on a summer that had never existed anywhere, watching a sea that was painted on the glass.

They turned as we passed. They always turned. And they smiled — that was the part I'd never get used to, the *sincerity* of it. These weren't prisoners performing contentment. These were people who had gotten exactly what they'd asked for, and the asking had hollowed them smooth as river stones.

A woman stepped out of an alcove as we passed. She wore a wedding dress gone gray with frost, and she looked at Cassian with an expression of gentle recognition.

"You came back," she said. Her voice was warm. Warmth, down here, was the most horrifying thing available. "Everyone comes back eventually. The ledger keeps what's owed, and everyone owes something. Isn't it a relief? To stop running? To just... settle?"

"Move along, Mara," Cassian said quietly.

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "Mara," I repeated. "Your sister's name is Mara."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The woman in the wedding dress tilted her head at him, and her smile deepened by one degree, and it was the happiest expression I had ever seen on anything, and I wanted to burn the whole court down and understood, finally, why I couldn't: there was nothing here to free. Freedom would be the cruelest thing we could give her.

"She testified for you," I said. Not a question. "Ten years ago. Your sister stood up in front of the tribunal and swore that you'd sold your soul to save Harrowgate, and the price of keeping her name out of the ledger was your testimony against mine."

"He bought me a wedding," Mara said dreamily, to no one, to the painted sea. "Such a good brother. Such a good trade. I'm so happy here. Aren't I happy? Come see how happy I am."

"*Walk*," Cassian said, and his voice cracked down the middle, and he walked, and I walked with him, and Wren's small hand found mine in the dark without either of us arranging it.

Only when the alcoves had fallen behind us did he speak again, to the tunnel ahead rather than to me. "Every soul I could buy back, I bought. Quietly. Over ten years. Hers was the first debt and it's the only one I couldn't touch — the court won't sell what was paid willingly by the buyer himself. She signed. She chose it, Jonah. She was dying anyway, and she chose the warm room over the grave, and I have hated myself every day since for building the room." His jaw worked. "I'm telling you this now because if we die down here, someone should know her name was Mara, and she liked honey wine, and she laughed at terrible jokes, and she is not suffering. That's the trap of this place. It works because it's kind."

"I know," I said. "That's why the book has to break. Kindness shouldn't come with a collector."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he used to across a campfire at Harrowgate, before the siege, before the storm, before everything we built came down on top of us. "When it takes your memories," he said, "it'll take her too. You'll forget I ever told you."

"Then tell me again after," I said. "Every day, if you have to. Start with the honey wine."

* * *

The gods made their final argument three levels from the Spire's heart, and they made it through Wren, and it nearly worked.

She went down between one step and the next, like a puppet with cut strings, and when she rose she was not rising — she was being *lifted*, joints moving in an order that wasn't hers, and the blue light poured out of her eyes and mouth and the seams of her, and the tunnel filled with a sound like a choir singing inside an avalanche.

*STORM-CALLER.* Every icicle on the ceiling rang with it. *WE OFFERED YOU EVERYTHING. THE THRONE REFUSED, VERY WELL. BUT TURN BACK NOW AND WE GIVE YOU THIS: YOUR SOUL, RETURNED WHOLE. NO LEDGER REQUIRED. NO PRICE. WALK AWAY AND BE WHAT YOU WERE. THE STORM WILL KNOW YOUR NAME AGAIN.*

And oh, it was a beautiful offer. That's the thing I need written down somewhere, in whatever survives of me: it was *beautiful*. I felt it land in the hollow place where my magic used to live, that borrowed place, that empty room where the storm had always knocked before entering. Ten years I'd mourned it. Ten years selling rain-charms, feeling the weather move through me like a language I'd forgotten, and here was the sky itself offering to hand me back my own name.

*YOU COULD CALL THE RAIN ON CINDERHOLLOW AGAIN. THE DROUGHT ENDS. THE CHARMS BECOME TRUE. ALL OF IT, IF YOU ONLY LET US KEEP OUR DOOR.*

"My door," I said aloud, and my voice shook, and I let it shake, because a lie told steadily is still a lie. "Your door. Your cage door. You fled into the Spire when the world got too heavy for you, and you've been hiding behind a book of debts ever since, and now you want to punch your way out through the walls of everything that's grown up around your hiding place." I knelt in front of Wren — in front of the god wearing her — and I took her small frozen hands in mine. "You want to escape. I understand escaping. I wrote the book on it. But you'll do it over the bodies of everyone who ever believed in you, and I have spent ten years learning exactly what that costs, and I decline."

*WE MADE YOU,* the avalanche-choir screamed. *EVERY STORM YOU EVER CALLED WAS OURS LENT TO YOU. WITHOUT US YOU ARE NOTHING. YOU ARE A WOMAN SELLING KNOTS OF STRING IN A TOWN THAT FORGOT HER NAME.*

"Yeah," I said, and I was crying, and I didn't care, and I held onto those little blue-lit hands like they were the railing at the edge of the world. "Without you I'm a charm seller in Cinderhollow. Without you I'm a woman who got exiled and got small and got lonely and survived all three. And that nothing — that small, forgotten, rain-charm nothing — is walking down this tunnel to break your book. Because somebody has to remember that love isn't a debt. That mercy isn't a contract. That a prayer answered should cost the *answerer*, not the asker." I leaned closer, forehead nearly touching hers, close enough to see the girl fighting underneath the god, close enough to whisper the part meant for Wren alone. "Hold on, crack-girl. Almost done."

The light flared. The choir screamed words in a language older than mountains. And then — because gods, like kings, like ledgers, only understand one language and it isn't love — the pressure vanished all at once, and Wren collapsed into my arms, human-sized, human-warm, sobbing.

"They're so scared," she gasped into my shoulder. "Jonah, they're so *scared*. That's why they're like this. They think if the book breaks they'll just... thin out. Become weather. Become nothing that knows itself."

"Maybe they will," I said, standing, lifting her onto my hip the way you carry a child who has forgotten she's allowed to be carried. "Maybe becoming the rain that falls on Cinderhollow without asking anything back is the best thing that could happen to them." I looked at Cassian. He had the lantern raised, and beyond him the tunnel bent downward toward the heart of the Spire, toward the throne, toward the book, toward the king who had been waiting ten years for me to return and finish our transaction.

"Last chance to run," I said.

"We passed it," Cassian said. "About nine levels ago."

"Good." I shifted Wren's weight, felt her heartbeat against my collarbone, small and stubborn and mortal. "Then let's go commit the largest act of debt forgiveness in the history of the world."

The tunnel breathed us in. Far below, something vast turned a page.
