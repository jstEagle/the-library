## 20. Thaw

**JONAH**

The first thing I noticed when we came out of the mountain was the smell.

You don't notice a smell until it's gone, and for three weeks underground everything had smelled of cold stone and old gold and the particular sweetness of souls pretending to be comfortable. But at the mouth of the pilgrim road, where the mountain finally let go of us like a fist unclenching finger by finger, the air smelled of wet earth. Of mud. Of things rotting the way they're supposed to rot, in service of something growing.

I sat down in the mud. I'm not ashamed of it. My legs made the decision without consulting me, and I have learned over a long and inconvenient life that my body is often wiser than my head.

"Is she dead?" Wren asked, crouching beside me with the grave concern of an eight-year-old who has seen too much to be frightened by a woman sitting down.

"No," I said.

"She's crying."

"I said no."

Cassian stood a little apart from us, the way he'd stood for the whole climb out—close enough to help, far enough to be refused. He looked up at the sky, and I watched his face do something complicated. The clouds were breaking. Not the gray wool-pack of winter clouds, but tearing, actually tearing, along seams of blue so bright it looked like the sky had been mended with new cloth.

It was the twenty-third day of Highsummer. The snow on the lower slopes was sliding off the pines in wet slabs. Somewhere below us, I knew, Cinderhollow's frozen wells were groaning open, and the blackened barley fields were doing the impossible arithmetic of seeds that had been dead for a month deciding to live again.

The ledger was broken. The debts were voided. The world's warmth was flowing back where it belonged, interest unpaid, principal forgiven, the whole monstrous account book of answered prayers burned down to its brass fittings and scattered.

And I couldn't remember why I was crying.

That's not quite true. I knew *that* I had paid something. My hands remembered it—they still curled sometimes around the shape of a book that wasn't there anymore, the way a missing tooth makes your tongue keep visiting the gap. And I knew the shape of what I'd lost, even if I couldn't see inside it: there was a Jonah-shaped hole in me with people-shaped impressions in it, like bodies that had lain in snow and melted away. I could feel the outlines. Cassian. Wren. Someone named Anselm whom I apparently loved like a brother and could not picture above the shoulders.

I just couldn't remember the color of any of it.

"You should eat," Cassian said, and offered me bread, and I took it because my hands trusted him even though nothing else did yet.

* * *

**WREN**

The gods got quieter every day, like a kettle going off the boil.

I didn't mind. People thought I would mind—that I'd cry about losing them the way you cry about losing a friend—but they weren't friends exactly. They were more like weather that talked. And now they were becoming actual weather, which everyone said was a mercy, and which I thought was probably right, because I had heard them up close, through my own cracks, and I knew what they wanted when they wanted things. They wanted out. They wanted *through*. They wanted the world to be a door instead of a home.

Now they were rain. Now they were the long warm wind off the southern fields that made the barley stand back up. Now they were the first thunder of the thaw, rolling over the mountains like something enormous turning over in its sleep and deciding to keep sleeping.

Vessia came to me one last time on the third night after we came out, when we were camped in the ruins of a pilgrim hospice with a fire going. She didn't speak through my mouth this time. She just sat across the fire wearing my face the way she always did, but older, or maybe younger, it was hard to say, and she looked at Jonah sleeping against her pack with one hand curled near the fire like a child.

*She gave up everything she loved,* Vessia said, inside my head, where she lived. *And she will never know what it bought.*

*I'll know,* I said. *I'll remember for her.*

*Yes.* Something moved behind my face that might have been a smile. *That is what cracks are for.*

Then she went out of me like breath off a window, and I was only Wren again, all the way through, and I sat by the fire until morning because I wanted to feel how it was to be just one person in one skin. It was smaller than being a goddess. It was also mine.

In the morning Jonah woke up and looked at me and frowned, the way she did every morning, like she was trying to fit me into a hole in herself that had my exact shape.

"Do I know you?" she asked. She asked every morning. It wasn't cruel. It was just true.

"I'm Wren," I said. "You found me in Cinderhollow. You feed me first at meals even though you pretend you don't."

She considered this. "That sounds like something I'd do."

"It is," I said. "You do it today, too. Watch."

**ANSELM**

I walked out of the Court of Small Mercies on the fourth day after the breaking, and I want it recorded—I am a record-keeper to my bones, even with no order left to keep records for—I want it recorded that the doors were unlocked.

Not broken. Not forced. Unlocked, and standing open, and beyond them the long gallery of the willing smiled their eternal smiles as the frost went out of their gilded frames, and some of them, I think, were beginning to be able to stop smiling. That is a horror I will carry to my own grave: watching a soul spend eleven hours unlearning a smile it had held for two hundred years. The muscles do not know how to let go. The face comes apart in stages, grief first, then confusion, then—on some faces, the lucky ones—a plain human restlessness, the look of someone waking late and wondering what the day requires of them.

What the day required of most of them, of course, was to die properly, at last, and go wherever souls go when no one is holding the deed. Some few drifted toward the open doors like leaves on a slow current. I stepped aside for each of them. It seemed the least courtesy owed.

My own freedom was simpler. When the ledger shattered, every debt it recorded became void—including, as it happened, the debt of obedience that the Order of the Quiet Flame had sold on my behalf when I was nine years old and too pious to know what piety cost. I had served the Graycloaks' precursor for forty years on the strength of a signature I never saw. The ledger held that signature. The ledger was ash. I felt the voiding go through me like a bone setting—painful, then suddenly, astonishingly, straight.

So I walked out. Past the empty throne where the buried king had sat for an age, wearing his crown of debts, and which was now merely a chair in a very large cold room. Past the heart of the Spire itself, where the shards of the ledger lay scattered like the wreckage of a mirror, each fragment showing whoever looked into it a face they had forgotten. I did not look. I had spent enough of my life reading other people's accounts.

The climb took four days. On the second day I found tracks—boot prints, small bare feet, a drag mark that told me someone had carried someone—and I followed them up and out, because I am an old man and old men are stubborn, and because I had heard, before I left the court, that a woman had broken the book and paid for it with the people she loved, and I had thought: *she will need someone who remembers them properly. Someone tedious and thorough. Someone who kept records.*

I caught up with them at the ruined hospice on the fifth night, and I stood at the edge of the firelight with my heart going like a boy's, and Jonah looked up at me with a stranger's polite blankness, and Cassian half-rose with his hand already out, saying my name like a man greeting a ghost he'd prayed for.

"There you are," I said, because forty years of composure has to be good for something, and my voice only broke a little. "Anselm Vey, formerly of the Order of the Quiet Flame, formerly keeper of records, currently unemployed." I sat down heavily by the fire. "Madam, I am given to understand you and I were friends. I regret to inform you that I remember everything and you remember nothing, and I intend to exploit this mercilessly."

She stared at me. Then—this is the part I will keep, whatever else the years take—she laughed. She didn't know why she was laughing. I watched her not know. It was the laugh of a person hearing a song from childhood: no words left, only the tune, and the tune was enough.

"All right," she said. "All right. Tell me the embarrassing parts first."

* * *

**JONAH**

He told me the embarrassing parts first. There were, apparently, many.

I learned that I once set his sleeve on fire demonstrating a rain-charm and lied about it for six months. That I called him "the Archivist" as an insult and he wore it like a title. That during the descent I had—his words—"complained about the dark with such sustained artistry that it constituted a performance." That Wren had ridden on my shoulders through the flooded galleries of the drowned shrine and sung a song about eels that I had, according to both witnesses, encouraged.

None of it was memory. It was testimony. But I found that testimony, repeated daily, begins to put down roots. By the time we reached the lowlands—the green returning to them like ink spreading through wet cloth, the roads opening, refugees walking the wrong way, homeward, carrying children who pointed at puddles as if puddles were a miracle, which after this winter I suppose they were—I had begun to build myself out of their accounts the way you build a fire: one dry fact at a time, patiently, trusting the shape of it.

Cassian told me less than the others. I noticed that. He hovered at the edge of his own story like a man at the edge of a property line, telling me about the siege in careful public language, about Harrowgate and the storm and the trial, and never once saying the word that Anselm's silences kept circling. I caught him watching me sleep twice. I caught him stopping himself from touching my shoulder three times. Whatever we had been to each other, he had decided I would hear it from anyone but him, and I was too tired, in those first weeks, to press.

The world, meanwhile, was busy forgiving itself. In the valley towns the Graycloaks had simply stopped—woken one morning to find their warrants voided, their authority resting on a ledger that no longer existed, and had done what authorities do when the paperwork dies: changed clothes and become tax collectors. The shrines stayed cracked. Nobody rushed to mend them. People left offerings anyway—bread, buttons, a child's boot in one case—but casually now, the way you leave a saucer of milk out for a cat that may or may not come. The old gods weren't answering anymore. They were raining. They were spring. It was, Anselm said, the ending the theologians had never dared hope for: not dominion, not escape, but retirement.

"They're in the seasons now," Wren told me solemnly, walking beside me with her hand hooked in my belt because she had decided, with the absolute sovereignty of children, that I was hers. "Vessia's in the thaw-water. You can tell because it tastes like iron and being forgiven."

"That's very poetic."

"I stole it from her," Wren said. "She doesn't need it anymore."

We came down into the hill country above Cinderhollow on a morning so mild it felt like a bribe. The town lay in its bowl with smoke rising straight from the chimneys, and the market square was visible even from here, stalls unfolding, awnings going up, the whole shabby machinery of ordinary commerce grinding back to life. My chest did something complicated at the sight of it. Homesickness, I decided, for a place I could prove I'd lived, even if I couldn't remember living there.

"You could come to the capital," Cassian said quietly, beside me. "There are people there who owe you—the whole world owes you—"

"The whole world owes me nothing," I said. "I voided the accounts. All of them. Including mine." I looked at him. His face did the thing it did whenever I looked directly at him, the flinch-and-hold, the man braced for a blow that had already landed somewhere he couldn't reach. "I'm going home. I'm going to sell charms. Apparently I'm good at it."

"You're terrible at it," Wren said. "You give half of them away."

"Then I'm terrible at it in a way that works," I said, and started down the hill, and heard them follow.

* * *

**ANSELM**

We reached Cinderhollow at dusk, and I confess I hung back at the gate a moment to watch her enter her own life.

The town had heard rumors—of course it had; rumor travels faster than armies—but the law that had exiled her died with the ledger's authority, and besides, half of Cinderhollow had spent the winter praying to cracked stones and getting answers, so the town's relationship with outlawed magic had grown philosophically flexible. A crowd gathered anyway. It kept a respectful distance, the way crowds do around people they've decided are stories. Jonah walked through it with her chin up and her jaw set and her hands in her pockets, and I saw her glance sideways at Wren, and I saw Wren take her hand, and I saw the crowd watch that happen and soften, all of them at once, like frost going off a window.

She rented the same stall. The same stall! The market clerk, a woman with a ledger of her own—paper, mundane, honest—found the entry from ten years ago and charged her the accumulated fees with an apology so elaborate it bordered on liturgy. Jonah paid it. Then she unpacked her charms onto the boards: luck-knots, rain-braids, fever-strings, all of them harmless now, all of them just knots and braids and strings, and if a little something extra lived in the tying of them, well. The town had decided that was between her and the weather.

I took rooms above the chandlery. I am writing this by candlelight, and tomorrow I begin the work I have chosen for what remains of me: a true account of the Spire, the court, the ledger, and the woman who broke it, compiled from testimony, beginning with hers. She has agreed to sit for it, one evening at a time, mostly because I bribed her with supper. She remembers almost nothing and asks excellent questions. Between the questions and my records and the girl's impossible recollections, I believe we can rebuild her history like a wall rebuilt stone by stone—not the same wall, but sound, and hers.

Tonight, though, one thing more, and then I will close this page.

At dusk, before the lamps were lit, I passed the market square and saw Cassian Vale standing at the edge of it, watching her pack up her stall. He had a traveling bag at his feet. He had clearly been standing there some time. He had, I judged from the wear on his boots and the set of his shoulders, been standing at edges for ten years, and I recognized the posture, because I had worn it myself for forty: the posture of a man waiting for permission he does not believe he deserves.

She looked up. She saw him. And I saw her frown—not the stranger's blankness she gives the rest of us, but something sharper, something troubled, the look of a person with a splinter too deep to find.

"Why do you keep leaving?" she called across the square. "Every time I turn around, you're at the edge of things. Sit down or go home, Cassian. Pick one."

He picked up his bag.

Tomorrow I will ask her to tell me about the siege, and she will tell me what Cassian has permitted himself to tell her, and at some point—soon, I think, sooner than he plans—I will open my records to the pages marked *Harrowgate, the trial, the testimony of C. Vale*, and I will read aloud what love looks like when it testifies against itself. She asked for the truth in installments. It is time for the next payment.

But tonight the thaw-wind came down off the mountain smelling of iron and being forgiven, and the lamps of Cinderhollow came on one by one, and the charm seller walked home through her own small life with a god-touched girl swinging on her arm, and if she paused once, at her door, looking back at the square with an expression I could not catalogue—

Well. Even archivists are allowed one mystery per volume.
