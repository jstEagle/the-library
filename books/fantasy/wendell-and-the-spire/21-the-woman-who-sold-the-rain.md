## 21. The Woman Who Sold the Rain

The first thing you should know about tending a woman who has forgotten you is that she is very polite about it.

That's the part that breaks your heart, if you're me. Not the blankness. The courtesy. Jonah Thornbury looks at me across the breakfast table in the little house above Cinderhollow's charm-row — *my* little house, *her* little house now too, though neither of us says so — and she thanks me for the tea. She thanks me for the bread. She asks, with the careful gentleness people use on strangers and madmen, whether I'd like her to leave.

"I live here," I tell her, most mornings.

"And I'm grateful for the hospitality," she says, "but I don't recall inviting you."

"You didn't. You married me."

She sets down her cup. She studies my face the way she used to study weather — patiently, professionally, waiting for it to turn. "That's a strange thing to say to someone who doesn't remember it."

"It's not strange," I say. "It's just true. Those are different things."

* * *

I had a whole speech prepared, back when we came down out of the mountain. Anselm helped me draft it — he's good at words, being a priest, even a defrocked one; he's good at them the way soldiers are good at knives, which is to say he knows where they go. The speech had structure. It began with Harrowgate and ended with forgiveness, and somewhere in the middle there was a passage about the ledger's arithmetic that I thought was genuinely moving.

Jonah heard exactly none of it. She was sitting up in Wren's bed — we gave her Wren's bed, because Wren insisted, and because nobody argues with Wren — and she looked at me with eyes that held nothing at all, and she said:

"Who are you?"

And every word I'd written turned to ash in my mouth, because you cannot open a story with the ending. You cannot hand a woman her own history like a bill of sale. She'd spent ten years learning to be small, and then she'd unmade herself to save everyone, and what was left was a stranger in her own body who flinched at thunder.

So I threw away the speech. And I started over.

"My name is Cassian," I said. "I'm going to tell you a story. It'll take a while. You don't have to believe any of it."

She nodded slowly, the way you nod at a charm-seller in a market you can't afford. "How long is a while?"

"However long it takes," I said. "One day at a time."

* * *

Here is how the days go now.

Mornings, I make breakfast, because Jonah's hands remember knots and storms but have apparently filed cooking under *unnecessary*. She sits by the window with her tea and watches the street below, where spring is doing its slow, suspicious work on Cinderhollow — snowmelt running in the gutters, the first green pushing up through ash-grey mud, people walking with their faces tipped toward a sun they no longer trust.

She doesn't know why the town nods to her. That's the strangest thing. Old Marta from the dye-works leaves bread on our step. The cooper's boy swept our walk all winter without being asked. When Jonah goes out — and she goes out more now, drawn to the market like a tide to the moon — people touch their foreheads or press small gifts into her hands, and she comes home frowning at a ribbon or an apple or a carved wooden bird, saying, "They keep giving me things. Do I owe them money?"

"No," I say.

"Then why?"

"Because of what you did." And then, because I've learned: "But that's chapter nineteen. We're only on chapter six."

"Chapter," she repeats. She almost smiles. "You've made my life into a book."

"You were always a book," I say. "I'm just reading you aloud."

* * *

Wren stays near. That's the other true thing I promised to tell you.

The girl is half-goddess now — or perhaps she always was, and the mountain simply made it official. Vessia speaks through her still, but gently these days, the way an old woman speaks through a granddaughter: a word here, a warning there, mostly about weather. Wren stands in the garden with her arms out and tells us it will hail on Thursday, and it hails on Thursday, and Jonah watches this with an expression I can't read, because I no longer have ten years of her expressions memorized. I'm having to learn them again. It's slow work. It's the best work of my life.

"She looks at me strangely," Jonah told me last week.

"You're her favorite person."

"I've known her three weeks."

"You've known her eleven years," I said. "You just haven't read that far yet."

Wren handles it better than I do, if I'm honest. She doesn't try to be remembered. She just *is near*, the way weather is near — sitting at Jonah's feet while I talk, braiding and unbraiding a length of blue thread, occasionally correcting me.

"You're telling it wrong," she said yesterday, when I got to the part about the siege.

"I was there."

"You were there wrong." She frowned at her thread. "You make yourself smaller in the story than you were. Vessia says that's a kind of lying."

And the terrible thing — the humbling, grace-giving thing — is that she's right. I testified against Jonah Thornbury before a tribunal, and I have spent ten years telling myself it was to save my sister, and both of those things are true, and neither of them excuses the other, and when I tell the story aloud I catch myself sanding down my own guilt until it fits neatly in a sentence. Wren won't let me. A child who is also a crack in the world will not let you lie small.

So I tell it right. "I stood up in front of the tribunal," I say, "and I told them the storm was hers, and that she'd called it knowingly, and that magic was outlawed, and I let them take her. And then I went home and ate dinner."

Jonah listens to this with her tea going cold. She doesn't look horrified. She looks — thoughtful. The way she looks at a knot that won't hold.

"Why would I love you after that?" she asks.

It's the first time she's said the word *love*. My hands shook so badly I had to put down my cup.

"Because I spent ten years buying souls back from the ledger," I said. "One at a time. With everything I had. Because I came to Cinderhollow knowing you'd hate me, hoping you'd let me help anyway. Because—" and here I had to stop, because the truth was too large for my mouth, "—because some things don't stop being true when you forget them. I loved you before you knew my name. I'll love you after, however long the after takes."

She was quiet a long moment. Then: "That's a lot to ask a stranger to carry."

"I know."

"All right," she said. "Keep telling it. But Cassian—" and she said my name carefully, like a word in a language she was relearning, "—don't skip the parts where you're wrong. I want the whole arithmetic."

*The whole arithmetic.* She doesn't remember the ledger. She doesn't remember shattering it. But something in her kept the shape of the lesson: that debts must be counted honestly, all of them, especially your own.

I could weep. Some days I do.

* * *

Anselm comes by on rest-days. He walks out of the mountain's shadow thinner than he went in, freed by the voided debts along with every other soul the court held, and he carries his order's burned records as ash in a jar, which he keeps on his mantel like a relic. He is relearning how to be a man instead of an archive.

He and Jonah get along splendidly, which I find deeply unfair.

"You sold rain-charms," he said to her recently, delighted. "In an outlawed town. For ten years."

"They were luck-knots," Jonah said. "Legally distinct."

"Legally distinct," Anselm repeated, savoring it. "Cassian, did you hear this? Ten years of crime, and her defense is prepared."

"It's not a defense," Jonah said, "if there's no law against it anymore. Is there? There isn't. I checked."

She checked. Of course she checked. The woman in my kitchen has no memories and a complete command of the current legal code, because she walked to the records hall in her second week and read statutes for two days straight, and came home and announced, "You weren't lying about the exile. It was rescinded posthumously, except I'm not dead, so there's a clerical problem."

"There's always a clerical problem," Anselm said fondly. "Welcome to bureaucracy."

But sometimes — sometimes — she goes quiet in the middle of a joke, and her hand drifts to her wrist, to the old brand-scars she doesn't remember earning, and her face does something complicated. Those are the days I worry about. The days when the missing years aren't an absence but a pressure, like weather behind a sealed window.

Last night she sat across from me after supper and said, without preamble: "Tell me about the price."

"The price of what?"

"Whatever I paid. Nobody shatters a— you said I broke a book. A ledger. Nobody does that for free. What did it cost me?"

I looked at her. She looked back, steady, patient, professional. Waiting for the weather to turn.

"You," I said. "It cost you us. Everyone you loved. Your mother's face. The siege. Ten years of rain-charms and hiding. Me."

She absorbed this. Then she said, "Was it worth it?"

"Yes," I said, without hesitating, because I will not lie to her, not even kindly. "The winter ended. The world thawed. Thousands of souls went free. Yes."

"Good," she said. And then, softer, looking into her empty cup: "Good. Then I'd do it again. Whoever I was, I'd do it again."

And I understood, sitting there, that I wasn't giving her back her past at all. I was introducing her to someone. A woman she'd never meet — brave and bitter and careful and fierce — and she was deciding, day by day, whether she approved of her.

Yesterday she decided. She said, "I like her. The one in your story. I hope I turn out like her."

"You already have," I said. "You check the statutes. You thank people for bread. You took Wren in before I finished explaining who she was."

"That's just decency."

"That's *her*," I said. "That's exactly her."

* * *

This morning, spring proper arrived in Cinderhollow. The plum tree outside the window — planted, I told her, the year after her exile, by a woman who refused to stop hoping — opened its first blossoms overnight, and Jonah stood in the doorway looking at them for a long time.

"Cassian," she said.

"Yes?"

"Today's chapter. Tell me the one where we meet."

I smiled. "We meet in chapter five. But you won't like it."

"I don't have to like it," she said, coming to sit across from me, folding her hands, ready. "I want all of it. Start at the beginning."

So I did. I started at the beginning — the walls of Harrowgate, the storm gathering in my hands' memory, the woman I loved standing in the rain she'd called down — and I watched her listen, and I thought: this is what love is, in the end. Not the having. The telling. The daily work of handing someone back to themselves, one true sentence at a time, and trusting that whatever they build from it will be theirs.

Outside, Wren laughed at something in the garden — a bright, crack-in-the-world laugh, wholly loved, wholly loving — and the plum petals came down like a small, local, entirely lawful snow.

Jonah leaned forward. "Go on," she said. "What happened next?"
