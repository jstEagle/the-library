## 22. Charm Seller, Twice Made

The trouble with selling luck is that everyone who buys it believes you.

I learned that the first time around, back when my charms were real and my name was worth more than a hangman's rope. People believed in me so hard they built a siege around it. Now I sell to people who believe a little less, and it turns out that's the healthier market.

Spring came late to Cinderhollow this year — later than any spring in living memory, which is saying something, since most of living memory got legally erased and had to be rebuilt from gossip. The thaw came up out of the ground before it came down from the sky, so for two weeks the town smelled like wet stone and green things insisting on themselves, and every window in the low quarter stood open to let the winter out like it was airing out a sickroom.

My stall sits where it has always sat: third from the corner on Tallow Row, under the crooked awning that leaks only when it rains, which is rarely, because I keep it that way without meaning to. Old habits. A knot here, a twist there, and the weather over my little square of market stays polite.

Small magics. Legal-adjacent magics. The kind of magic that, if a Graycloak squinted at it, would look exactly like good knotwork and a seller's instinct for weather. Ten years I kept it that small because I was afraid. This year I keep it that small because I have measured my life against the alternative and found the small version better.

That's the part nobody warns you about, coming back from a mountain with holes where your people used to be. They warn you about the grief. Nobody warns you about the *choosing*.

* * *

The first customer of the morning was Marta Ostrow, who buys a luck-knot every month whether she needs one or not, which she doesn't, which is why she needs one.

"Rain-charm too?" she said, squinting up at a sky that was doing nothing in particular. "For the barley."

"Marta. It's been raining twice a week since the thaw. Your barley is going to drown."

"Then a *sun*-charm."

"A sun-charm costs double."

"It always does." She counted out the coppers anyway, sliding them across the boards with the grim satisfaction of a woman who has negotiated with me before and lost. "You know what my mother used to say? She said there was a storm-caller once, broke a siege with weather, saved ten thousand souls. And they erased her. Wiped her name off every record in the kingdom. You couldn't find her in a book if your life depended on it."

"That sounds like an exaggeration," I said, tying off her knot.

"My mother swore it was true. Said she saw it herself, when she was a girl. A woman standing on the walls of Harrowgate with lightning in her fists." Marta took the charm, turned it over, tucked it away. "Funny thing to erase, isn't it? Something that big. Like burning down a house and expecting nobody to remember where the street was."

"People forget streets all the time," I said. "Streets aren't much to look at."

"No," she agreed. "But everybody knows how to find them."

She left. I stood there a while with my hands flat on the stall boards, feeling something I couldn't name move through me like water through a cracked jar — not sadness exactly, not quite. An echo of a feeling whose original had been spent paying a debt. Somewhere in me was a shape where a memory should be, and the shape ached the way an amputated limb aches, and I have stopped asking it questions because it never answers.

Most days that's fine. Most days the shape stays quiet.

Some days a woman buys a rain-charm and tells me a story about a storm-caller, and the shape hums.

* * *

Midmorning brought the boy with the cracked flute.

He comes by every few weeks. Nine or ten years old, gap-toothed, plays badly with tremendous conviction outside the shrine on Tallow Row — the cracked one, the old god's shrine, Vessia's, the one that split down the middle during the long winter and never got fixed because nobody could remember which god it belonged to. He plays to it anyway. Says it likes music. Says sometimes, when he hits the high notes wrong, the crack *sings back*.

Today he wasn't playing. Today he was standing at my stall turning a river-stone over in his hands like it owed him money.

"You're the charm lady," he said.

"I'm the knot lady. Charms are a state of mind."

"Wren says you're not."

The stone went very still in my hands. So did I.

"Wren," I said carefully, "is a girl who says a lot of things."

"She says you sold the rain. She says you bought it back with remembering. She says—" he screwed up his face, reciting, the way children do when they've been made to memorize something and are proud of it anyway— "she says, *tell her the knots are good but the hands are better, and tell her I'm keeping the rest safe until she wants it.*"

He set the river-stone on my stall. It was nothing. Wet slate, river-worn, the kind of thing that litters every bank in the world. Except that when I touched it, the shape inside me — the amputated place, the humming hollow — went quiet. Not empty. Quiet. Like a room where someone you love is sleeping.

"Where is she?" I asked. My voice came out strange. Smaller than I meant it.

"The temple road. With the priest. She won't come into town, she says the bells are too loud now that they mean it." He shrugged, having delivered his message and exhausted his interest in it. "Can I have a luck-knot? I'll pay you Thursday."

"You'll pay me now, or you'll pay me never."

"I'll pay you Thursday."

I gave him the knot. He grinned with all nine of his teeth and ran, and I sat back down behind my stall holding a river-stone that weighed nothing and everything, and let the echo-hum wash through me until the market noise came back.

Keeping the rest safe. Until I want it.

Here is what I know, assembled from other people's stories the way you assemble a lamp from its scattered pieces: there was a ledger, and I broke it, and breaking it cost me every memory of everyone I loved. There was a priest named Anselm who walked out of a frozen court freed by voided debts, and a man named Cassian who — according to Marta, according to the tavern-talk, according to the shape that hums — stayed. Stayed near me. Told me our story from the beginning, one day at a time, for months, until the day I looked at him kindly and blankly and asked him why a stranger would do that, and he finally understood that some prices don't get negotiated down.

They say he left in the autumn. They say he writes letters he doesn't send. I wouldn't know. To me he is a name attached to an ache, a door in my chest that opens onto a room I've never seen.

And Wren — Wren, who was a returned soul, a crack in the world the gods spoke through — Wren keeps the rest of me somewhere safe, and sends it to me in pieces, through children with flutes and river-stones and messages I'm not sure I want.

Because here's the thing about wanting it. If I take the memories back, I get the man and the love and the ten thousand lives and the betrayal and the storm. All of it, braided together, the way it actually happened. And I would become her again — the woman on the walls, the woman with lightning in her fists, the woman whose life was so large it required erasure.

Or I keep the knots. And the ache. And the choice.

* * *

Afternoon. A stranger stopped at the stall.

You can always tell the ones who aren't buying. They stand a half-step too far back, and their eyes go over the merchandise instead of at it, and there's a particular way they hold their shoulders — like men carrying something heavy who haven't decided yet whether to put it down.

This one was tall, gray at the temples, with a traveler's cloak worn soft at the seams and a face that did something complicated the moment he saw my hands working the knotwork. Just my hands. He watched them like a man watching a fire he'd thought was cold.

"Help you?" I asked.

He didn't answer right away. He was busy being stricken, quietly, in the way of people who've had years to practice it.

"Do you—" His voice caught. He started over. "Do you sell rain?"

"When it's wanted. Sun when it isn't. Luck mostly. Love-charms are a scam and I'll say so."

A breath of laughter escaped him, ragged, like it had been held a long time. "You used to say that. You—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Forgive me. You remind me of someone."

"There's a lot of that going around."

"Yes," he said softly. "There is."

He bought a luck-knot. Paid full price without haggling, which marked him as either wealthy or heartbroken, and the way his fingers brushed mine when he took it marked him as both. He stood there a moment longer than a transaction requires, and the shape inside me sang so loud I nearly put my hand over my own sternum to hush it.

"Fair roads," I told him.

"Fair roads," he said. And then, at the edge of hearing, almost to himself: "*Jonah.*"

"What was that?"

"Nothing." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen all year, and I have seen the thaw come to Cinderhollow after a winter that ate the world. "Nothing. Good luck with your stall."

He walked off down Tallow Row, and I watched him go the way you watch a door close on a warm room — knowing, without knowing how I knew, that I would see him again tomorrow. And the next day. And the day after that. Some things keep returning like weather.

* * *

Evening. I closed the stall early and walked out the temple road with the river-stone in my pocket.

Wren was sitting on the wall where the road bends above the valley, legs swinging over the drop, hair full of wind. She's fourteen now, or looks it; time moves strangely on girls who were once spat back out of a ledger incomplete. Anselm was nearby, fussing with a cookfire, a gray-bearded man with ink-stained fingers and the permanent expression of someone reading a document he disapproves of. He nodded to me the way you nod to a colleague.

"You got the stone," Wren said.

"I got the stone."

"Do you want the rest?"

I sat down on the wall beside her. Below us, Cinderhollow was lighting its lamps one by one, a handful of embers scattered on the darkening hills, small and stubborn and entirely sufficient. Somewhere in that scatter of light was a stall with a leaking awning and a stock of knots, and a man in a soft-worn cloak who would probably come back tomorrow and buy another charm he didn't need, because that's what love does when it can't do anything else. It shops.

"I don't know," I said. Honest, because she'd hear a lie anyway. "What's in the rest?"

"Cassian. Mostly." She said it gently. "The siege. Harrowgate. The night you sold your soul, and the night you took it back. Him telling you the story every day for a season. Him leaving when you asked him to stop, because you asked him to stop." She swung her feet. "It hurts. That's the honest answer. It's the best thing you ever had and it hurts, and you'd be choosing both at once."

"And if I don't choose it?"

"Then you keep choosing this." She gestured at the valley, the lamps, the road, the whole small architecture of my days. "And that's allowed. That's the whole point of what you did. You made it allowed."

Anselm cleared his throat from the fire. "For what it's worth — and as a former keeper of records, I am professionally aware of what things are worth — the ledger recorded debts. It never recorded what anyone *owed themselves*. That column was always blank." He poked the fire. "I mention it only because no one ever reads the appendices."

"He means you don't owe yourself your suffering," Wren translated.

"I mean read the appendices," Anselm said.

I turned the river-stone over in my fingers. The shape inside me hummed, and I let it, and I found — this is the true thing, the thing I came up the mountain to learn and only understood down here among the knots and the lamps — that the ache was not a wound. It was a *room*. A room with a warm hearth in it and a man's laugh in it and ten thousand lives saved in it, and I could stand outside the door and feel the warmth through the wood, and that was not nothing. That was not even small.

But warmth through a door is not the same as fire in your hands.

"Ask me again tomorrow," I said.

Wren grinned, gap-toothed like the flute boy, like maybe the same boy, like the world keeps sending me itself in pieces. "I'll ask you every day," she said. "One day at a time. It worked for him."

* * *

I walked back into town in the blue hour, past the cracked shrine, where someone had left wildflowers in the fissure — the crack sings back now, sometimes, softly, when the wind is right, and the town has decided this is charming rather than heretical, which tells you what surviving a winter that ate the world does to a town's theology.

Marta was locking up her own stall. She nodded at me across the row.

"Storm-caller," she said, testing it, casual as commenting on weather. "My mother's story. You ever wonder if she was happy? After? Whatever became of her?"

I thought about the man in the soft-worn cloak who would come back tomorrow. About the stone in my pocket humming like a sleeping room. About a wall above the valley and a girl who asks me every day, and will ask me tomorrow, and the day after.

"Yeah," I said. "I think she got to choose. In the end. Small life, freely taken. There are worse endings."

"There's only one better," Marta said, and locked her till, and went home.

I stood a moment under my crooked awning in the lamplight, hands steady, knots stacked and waiting for morning, the ache singing its low warm note in the room behind the door.

Tomorrow he'll come back. Tomorrow she'll ask. And someday — not today, but someday, when the choosing feels less like losing and more like opening — I think I'm going to open that door.

Tonight, though, I have knots to tie, and a stall to mind, and a spring that came back from the dead just to watch me work.

Survival's price is paid. It was steep. It was worth it.

And the best part — the part the ledger never had a column for —

is that tomorrow, I get to choose it all again.
