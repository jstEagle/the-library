## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The echoes arrived on a Tuesday, which Ansel Winterbourne considered a good omen. Tuesdays were ledger days, and ledger days were the only days the Guildhall felt like it remembered what it was for.

He counted the crates twice before signing for them—once by eye, once by hand—and found forty-one bottles where the manifest promised forty. The discrepancy was noted in his own neat script in the margin: *One short. Carrier claims miscount at Vell's Crossing. Recommend inquiry.* He did not expect an inquiry to come. Inquiries required someone to authorize them, and authorization required someone to care, and the Ledgerkeepers' Guild had been running out of people who cared the way a candle runs out of wick: slowly, invisibly, and then all at once.

He signed anyway. That was the point of him.

"Forty bottles," said the carrier, a gray woman with dust in the seams of her coat and the look of someone who had walked a long way through country that didn't want her. "Manifest says forty."

"The manifest says forty-one." Ansel turned it so she could read his finger under the line. "You'll want to initial the correction. It protects you as much as us."

She stared at him as if he had spoken a dialect from a drowned country—which, he supposed, in a way he had. Nobody initialed things anymore. Nobody believed that a small, precise record could stand between a person and ruin. But she took the stylus when he offered it, because there was something in the way he held out the manifest—flat, patient, unarguable—that made refusal feel like the stranger act.

"You're the last one here?" she asked, glancing up at the Guildhall's façade as she wrote. The stone above the door read *EVERYTHING HAS A PRICE; EVERYTHING HAS A PLACE*, though the second clause had been worn nearly smooth by weather and neglect, so that the building now seemed to promise only half its creed.

"I'm the last apprentice," Ansel said. "There are masters. Somewhere."

"How many?"

"That depends," he said honestly, "on whether you count the ones who answer their doors."

She almost smiled. Then she looked at the crates—at the bottles inside them, each one the size of a fist, each one holding a curl of pale light that moved like smoke remembering itself—and her face closed again, the way faces did around impossible goods. She left without another word, her footsteps fading down the colonnade, and Ansel was alone with forty-one—or forty—bottled echoes and the great hush of the hall.

* * *

The receiving room had once been the loudest place in the kingdom. Three hundred years ago, when the Guild was young and flush, merchants had queued out the doors to trade in things no ordinary market would touch: borrowed courage, secondhand griefs, the last words of the drowned sold back to their widowers by the ounce. The floor tiles still bore the grooves of a thousand queueing feet. Now the grooves collected dust, and the only sound was the tick of the great regulator clock and the faint, wet shifting of the echoes in their glass.

Ansel worked. This was the whole of his religion, and he performed it devoutly.

Each bottle came out of its straw packing and onto the scale—a brass balance older than the kingdom, its pans worn silver by use. Each echo was weighed, not for gold but for *weight itself*, because the first law of the Guild was that nothing traded could be lighter or heavier than its price, and the second law was that you never, ever guessed. Each bottle was then examined against the light: color, density, movement. A grief moved slow and sank. A joy rose and scattered. An echo of a voice—the most common trade, these days, parents buying back the sound of children grown and gone—hung level and trembled when spoken near.

This shipment was catalogued as *miscellaneous ambient*, which meant the seller hadn't known what they had, which meant Ansel would have to know for them. He uncorked the first bottle with a glass stopper and a practiced wrist, held it to the window, and listened.

A kitchen. Rain on a roof. Someone humming off-key, badly, happily. A dog's nails on flagstone.

*Domestic contentment,* he wrote. *Approximately one hour's duration. Provenance unknown. Condition: excellent.* And beneath it, because the form required it: *Assessed weight: 2 drams.*

Two drams of a happy afternoon. It fit in his palm. He thought, briefly—not for the first time, but always with the same dull surprise—about how much two drams would go for, and to whom, and what kind of person needed to buy an hour of someone else's kitchen. Then he corked it, labeled it, shelved it, and reached for the next.

This was the part of himself he understood best: the reaching for the next. There was a comfort in the work that he had never tried to explain to anyone, partly because there was no one to explain it to, and partly because he suspected the explanation would sound like an accusation. The ledgers asked nothing of him. They did not ask him to be clever or charming or brave. They asked him to be *accurate*, and accuracy was a thing he could do alone, in silence, forever. Every line balanced. Every entry witnessed. Every debt, however strange, however sad, written down so that it existed somewhere outside the hearts of the people who owed it—because debts kept in the heart grew teeth, but debts kept in ink stayed honest.

His grandmother had taught him that. *Ink is kinder than memory, little wren. Memory argues. Ink agrees.*

He was on the nineteenth bottle when Master Ellery-Vance appeared in the doorway, looked at him for a long moment as though trying to place him, and said, "You're the Winterbourne boy."

"Yes, Master."

"There's a leak in the west archive. Roof, I think. See to it."

"The cataloguing—"

"Will keep." And he was gone, his slippers whispering away down the corridor, leaving behind the faint smell of snuffed candles and old decisions.

Ansel sat with the twentieth bottle in his hands and let the injustice of it pass through him like weather. *The Winterbourne boy.* Eleven years in the Guild, and he was still *the boy*. He had catalogued four thousand entries. He had caught the Marrowfield discrepancy that had saved the Guild a lawsuit over a traded shadow. He had re-inked the entire eastern registry by hand during the damp winter when the clerks' copies blurred, working nights, asking nothing. None of it had accumulated into anything. Invisibility, he had learned, was not something the Guild granted; it was something it *maintained*, like a temperature, like a policy. The masters preferred apprentices who could be forgotten, because forgotten apprentices made no claims.

And the truth—the truth he kept in the locked drawer of himself, next to the things he'd never say aloud—was that he had *chosen* it. He had arrived at fifteen, orphaned and odd, carrying his grandmother's ledgers in a satchel too big for him, and he had looked at this vast crumbling house of rules and thought: *here is a place where I will never be asked to want anything.* Wanting was dangerous. Wanting was how people ended up across the counter, trading seven years of laughter for one perfect memory, and calling it a bargain.

He set the bottle down, fetched the ladder and the pitch pot, and went to mend the roof.

* * *

The west archive was the oldest part of the Guildhall, and the roof leaked the way old things leak: everywhere at once, apologetically, as if embarrassed to still exist. Ansel climbed into the rafters with a lantern and found the gap above the third aisle, where a slate had slipped and rain had been dripping, drip by patient drip, onto the shelving below.

Below. His stomach dropped before his eyes confirmed it. Directly beneath the gap, fanned open on a reading desk where some master had abandoned it mid-consultation, lay a volume of the master registry—one of the *master* registries, the deep ledgers, the ones recording trades so old and large that their pages were chained to their desks. Water had been falling onto it for days. The ink at the top corner of the open page had begun to bloom, softening, spreading like a bruise.

Ansel came down the ladder faster than dignity allowed.

He blotted the page first—clean linen, pressed flat, never rubbed—and then he read the entry to assess the damage, because you couldn't repair a record you hadn't read. It was a sale, centuries old. *One border ward, maintained in perpetuity, against consideration received.* The consideration column was smudged beyond recovery, the letters drowned into gray ghosts of themselves. But the item was legible, and the item stopped his breath for a moment longer than he cared to admit.

Border wards. The Guild had brokered those? He'd assumed the wards were crown works, king's magic, the kind of thing sung about in taverns. But here it was in the deep ledger: bought, sold, *maintained in perpetuity*—and perpetuity, in the Guild's arithmetic, was not a word but a schedule. Something was paid, regularly, forever, to keep the borders standing. He scanned the entry for the payment terms and found them redacted—not torn, not damaged, but *ruled out* with a single clean line of black ink, deliberate as a scar.

Someone had struck through the terms. On a chained master page. Which was, as far as Ansel knew, impossible. You could not amend a master entry. It was the third law, older than the building: *what is written in the deep books is written.*

He stood there a long time, blotter in hand, listening to the drip find a new rhythm on the bare desk. Then he did what he was made for. He fetched the repair kit, sleeved the damaged page in treated vellum, noted the water damage in the margin in his own hand with date and initials, and logged the incident in the maintenance register: *West archive, roof breach, slate replaced. One master volume exposed. Damage contained. Terms column of entry [number illegible] previously obscured—cause unknown, predating incident.*

*Previously obscured.* He stared at those two words after he wrote them. They were accurate. Accuracy was all he had to offer, and accuracy said: this was hidden before I ever found it, and hiding it took a hand steadier than rain.

That night he worked late, finishing the echo catalogue by lamplight while the Guildhall settled around him with the creaks of an old animal dreaming. Bottle thirty-three held something unusual—an echo with a golden thread running through it, laughter braided with something brighter underneath, and he weighed it three times because the scale kept settling at a weight he'd never recorded: heavy for its size, heavier than sound should be. *Content uncertain,* he wrote. *Possible composite. Recommend senior review.*

He knew, writing it, that no senior review would come. The recommendation would sit in the register like a seed in frozen ground. Still, he wrote it. That was the discipline. You did the thing correctly even when correctness went unwitnessed—*especially* then, his grandmother's voice said in his skull—because the ledger was not written for the people watching. It was written for the person who would come after, someday, needing the truth.

He capped the ink. He banked the lamp. And as he gathered his coat, he noticed the day's accumulation of small wrongnesses lining up in his mind like figures refusing to sum: a missing bottle on a manifest; a master entry with murdered terms; a shipment of echoes sold by someone who hadn't known what they had; thirty-three bottles of other people's lives shelved in a hall where nobody remembered his name.

Tomorrow, the register said, there would be clients. The first appointment in weeks: a private consultation, requested by name, for a woman who wished to make a trade. The slot had been entered in the book in Master Ellery-Vance's hand, with a note in the margin that Ansel had read three times already and would read again before sleep:

*Winterbourne to attend. Client insists on the boy.*

Nobody insisted on the boy. In eleven years, no one had ever insisted on the boy.

He blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the bottles breathe on their shelves—forty small hours of other people's kitchens and rain and off-key humming, glowing faintly in the black like a sky seen through the wrong end of a spyglass.

Then he locked the receiving room, checked the lock, checked it again, and went home through the empty colonnade, where the worn stone above the door kept its half promise to no one:

*Everything has a price.*
