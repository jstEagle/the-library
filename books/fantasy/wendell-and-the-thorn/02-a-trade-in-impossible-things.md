## 2. A Trade in Impossible Things

The bell above the counting-room door rang at half past nine, and Ansel Winterbourne knew before he looked up that the morning would go badly.

He knew it the way he knew most things—by the ledgers. The *Register of Intentions* had shown a smudge of ink on Tuesday's page, a blot no larger than a lentil, sitting exactly where a client's name should have gone. Ink did not blot by accident in the Guildhall. Paper here was old enough to have opinions, and when it resisted an entry, it was usually because the entry was going to cost someone something they hadn't yet agreed to pay.

So when the woman came in—grey traveling cloak, mud to the knee, hands folded like a prayer she'd already given up on—Ansel set down his pen, squared the blot against the morning light, and said, "You'll want the third chair. The first one wobbles."

She looked at him as though he'd spoken in a language she almost remembered. Then she sat.

"I was told—" She stopped. Started again. "I was told the Guild still takes clients. Even now. Even with the wards going soft."

"The Guild takes clients," Ansel said. "That's most of what's left of us."

There were four Ledgerkeepers remaining in the Hall at Vellum's Reach: Master Ondrey, who slept more each winter; Mistress Callow, who had not descended from the archive in eleven days; old Fenwick, who answered only to questions phrased as arithmetic; and Ansel, who was twenty-six years old and had been the youngest apprentice for nine years running, because no one else had come. He catalogued the shipments. He fed the binding-moths. He kept the small trades moving through the counting-room like blood through a thin vein, because if the vein closed, the whole body forgot it had ever been alive.

"Name?" he asked, pen ready.

"Maren Ostler." Her voice caught on her own name, oddly, as if it were heavy to lift. "From Harrow's Bend."

Ansel wrote it down. The ink took cleanly this time, which surprised him, and then did not surprise him at all—the ledger had simply been waiting to see how the trade would shape itself before deciding whether to accept her.

"And what do you bring," he recited, "and what do you ask?"

Maren Ostler looked at her hands. Outside the tall windows, October was doing its slow work on the courtyard elms, stripping them leaf by leaf with a patience Ansel had always found comforting. Things lost in autumn could be counted on to return in spring. That was the order of it. That was why he loved this place, he sometimes thought—not the magic, which frightened him, but the bookkeeping, which never lied and never asked him to be anything other than precise.

"I want to remember my daughter's wedding," Maren said. "Properly. All of it."

Ansel's pen paused. "We don't deal in memories made. Only in memories kept or traded. If you've lost yours—"

"I haven't lost it." She laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it. "I've *spent* it. Piece by piece. My husband's fever took everything we had, and then it took the house, and then there was a man in Ashford who told me about the Guild, and I sold..." She counted on her fingers, and Ansel watched her lose track halfway through and start again. "I sold the smell of bread baking. I sold the color of our kitchen door. I sold the sound of her voice at seven years old, and the way she looked when she was sleeping, and—" The fingers curled shut. "I sold so much of her that when I try to picture the wedding, all I get is holes. A groom with no face. A dress with no bride inside it. I stood there, Master Ledgerkeeper. I stood at the front of the church and I watched my own daughter marry, and I cannot tell you what she wore, because I sold the knowing of it two winters ago to buy medicine."

The counting room was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, a binding-moth turned over in its cedar case with a sound like a page being licked.

Ansel had heard grief before. The Guild heard grief the way a mill hears water—constantly, structurally, without comment. But there was something in the specific shape of this woman's loss that snagged on him like a burr. He consulted the *Index of Holdings* out of habit, running his finger down the column of assets held in trust from the Ashford district, and there they were: *Ostler, M.—kitchen door (color of), 1 unit. Bread, smell of (baking), 1 unit. Voice, child's, aged seven, 1 unit.* Neat entries. Fair prices. His own hand had recorded at least two of them, three years ago, when a broker from Ashford had brought a crate of bottled goods south and Ansel had logged each bottle without once asking what was inside.

"You want to buy it back," he said. "The wedding memory."

"I want to *have* it," she said. "I'll never buy back the pieces. I know that. The interest alone—" She waved at the arithmetic of her ruin. "But I'm told the Guild can do something else. That if a client brings something of sufficient weight, it can be exchanged not for coin, not for goods, but for a single memory, held whole. One perfect memory, chosen by the client, restored entire. Is that true?"

Ansel knew it was true. It was in the *Codex of Exchange*, Article Nine, and he had copied it out in his apprentice year as punishment for sloppy margins, and even then the words had seemed to him less like law than like a riddle someone had left slightly open on purpose:

*A memory held whole may be granted in exchange for consideration of equal felt-weight, the measure thereof to be determined by the ledger.*

Equal felt-weight. Not equal value. Not equal price. The Codex was very careful about that distinction, and Ansel had spent nine years not thinking about why.

"It's true," he said. "But the exchange runs both directions, and the ledger weighs what you bring. You don't choose what's sufficient. The book does."

"I brought what I have left that's worth anything." Maren reached into her cloak and drew out a small glass bottle, stoppered with wax, wrapped in wool. Inside, something moved—a warmth, a gold flicker, like lamplight seen through honey. "Seven years," she said. "My laughter. From the day she was born until the day I first sold something. Seven years of it, every time, all of it, kept separate from me since. I never opened it. I never let myself. Because I knew—" Her jaw worked. "I knew that if I ever needed it, it would have to be *whole*."

Ansel took the bottle and the weight of it went through his palm and up his arm and settled somewhere behind his sternum, which was not how bottles behaved. He had catalogued thousands of them. Griefs, joys, the taste of a particular summer, the courage of a boy before his first battle—each one inert in the hand until the ledger touched it. But this one was warm before the ledger ever saw it, and he understood, with a lurch, that it was warm because she had carried it close to her heart for seven years, guarding it, starving herself of her own laughter so that it would stay intact.

*Consideration of equal felt-weight.*

He set the bottle on the scales—the great brass balance that dominated the counting-room, older than the building around it, its pans hung from chains that had no visible ceiling attachment—and waited for the ledger's verdict.

The right pan sank. Slowly. Enormously. As though the bottle weighed as much as a child.

"That's—" Maren leaned forward. "Is that good? Is that enough?"

"It's heavy," Ansel said carefully. "It's very heavy. Which means the ledger will grant a large return." He pulled the *Codex* toward him, Article Nine, and read the rest of the clause aloud because the rules required it and because reading aloud gave his voice something to do besides shake. "*Upon exchange, the client shall receive one memory held whole, restored in full fidelity, and shall surrender the consideration permanently, together with all derivative feeling arising from the same.*"

"Together with all—" Maren frowned. "What does that mean?"

Ansel had asked Master Ondrey the same question, nine years ago, and Ondrey had looked at him with those milky eyes and said, *It means the ledger is thorough, boy. Thoroughness is mercy. Half-trades are cruelty.* He had believed it then. He believed it the way he believed in the load-bearing walls of the Guildhall.

"It means," Ansel said, "that when you trade your laughter, you won't merely lose the laughter you've stored. You'll lose your capacity for it. Going forward. All of it. The ledger doesn't take the bottle—it takes the well."

The silence that followed had a texture. Maren Ostler stared at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic—he watched her, a woman who had spent six years selling pieces of her life at retail, suddenly confronted with the wholesale price.

"But I'd have the wedding back," she said at last. "I'd have *her*. Whole. The whole day. Every minute."

"Yes."

"And I'd never laugh again."

"Yes."

She sat with that. Ansel sat with her. Outside, the elms went on surrendering their leaves, and he found himself hating them a little for making loss look so orderly.

The rules required a cooling period of one hour for any trade involving a permanent diminishment, and Ansel used the hour the way he used all hours: cataloguing. There was a shipment from the northern houses to log—fourteen crates of bottled echoes, mostly market-day sounds and harvest songs, the ordinary currency of a kingdom that had begun quietly selling itself off piece by piece. He worked with his sleeves pinned back, entering each item in his small, exact hand, and all the while Maren Ostler sat in the third chair with her hands folded, watching him, and the warmth of her bottle radiated off the scale-pans into the room like a small captive sun.

"Why do you do this work?" she asked, somewhere in the second hour's middle.

Ansel considered lying, decided the ledger would notice, and said, "Because it asks nothing of me."

"That's a strange reason."

"It's the best one I've ever had." He blotted an entry. "Everything else asks. My grandmother asked—I was supposed to inherit her curiosity, her restlessness, her terrible handwriting. The kingdom asks—taxes, borders, whose son dies where. People ask." He glanced up. "The ledgers only require that I be accurate. Accuracy is possible. Most things aren't."

Maren studied him for a long moment. "You talk like a man reading from a book."

"I am a man reading from a book. It's the safest place there is."

"And yet." She nodded toward the scales, where her laughter sat gleaming. "Your books are about to take the last of mine."

Ansel's pen stopped.

He wanted to say: *the ledger is fair*. He wanted to say: *the prices are published, the terms are plain, you came here freely, no one forced you*. He wanted to say all the things the Codex said, in the order the Codex said them, and feel the clean cold comfort of procedure closing over the moment like water over a stone.

Instead he heard himself say: "No. They're not. *I* am. The ledger doesn't lift the bottle onto the pan. I do. The ledger doesn't speak the terms. I do. I've been telling myself for nine years that I'm just the hand that holds the pen, but the pen is attached to somebody, Mistress Ostler, and that somebody is about to watch you trade away every laugh you'll ever have, and write it down neatly, and go to lunch."

The words came out of him with the force of surprise, as though someone else had borrowed his mouth. Maren blinked. Ansel stared at the middle distance, appalled at himself, and then—because accuracy was possible, even about this—said, "I apologize. That was unprofessional."

"Don't you dare apologize," Maren said, and her voice was different now—harder, drier, almost amused. "Do you know what I did before I started selling? I was a midwife. Thirty years. I caught babies. And do you know what I learned? That pain isn't the cruel part. Pain is just pain. The cruel part is when someone stands by and tells you it's *necessary*, in a voice that means they've stopped seeing you." She pointed one work-roughened finger at the Codex. "That book of yours isn't cruel, boy. Books can't be. But whoever taught you that reading it counts as kindness—they taught you wrong."

The clock in the corner ticked through the accusation, indifferent, accurate.

"The hour's ended," Ansel said quietly. "You're free to withdraw. No penalty. Article Twelve."

"I know." Maren looked at the bottle on the scale for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone soft and level, the voice of a woman who had delivered a hundred screaming mothers past the point where screaming stops mattering. "I caught a baby once, in the flood year. The mother didn't survive. And that child grew up and married and had children of her own, and last spring her granddaughter came to me with a fever I couldn't break, and I thought—there it is. There's the trade. Nobody signed anything. Nobody weighed anything. Life does this to everyone, constantly, and calls it nothing." She lifted her chin. "At least your ledger makes me say it out loud. At least it makes me *choose*. That's more honesty than God gives."

She stood. "Do it."

*

The exchange itself took four minutes and Ansel would remember every second of them for the rest of his life, which was, he would later understand, a kind of irony the universe enjoyed.

He unstopped the bottle. The laughter rose out of it like heat-shimmer, gold and grainy, seven years of a woman's joy hanging in the counting-room air—and for one moment, before the scales took it, Ansel heard it. All of it. A baby's first startled giggle. A toddler's hiccuping shriek. A girl of six laughing at her own shadow. A woman laughing alone in a kitchen at some private joke, the door painted whatever color it had been before she sold the color. It layered and looped and braided itself into a single chord, and the chord was so *specific*, so irreducibly hers, that Ansel understood in one vertiginous instant exactly what the phrase *equal felt-weight* had been protecting all along.

Then the ledger drank it.

There was no other word. The pages of the open Register turned themselves, the ink moved, the scales balanced with a chime like a struck fingernail, and the shimmer funneled down into the paper and was gone, and in its place the ledger wrote, in the impersonal copperplate that all official entries took, the terms of restoration.

And Maren Ostler changed.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the worst part. She didn't cry out or clutch her chest. She simply... settled. Like a house after a cart passes. Her face smoothed. Her shoulders dropped into a new geometry. And her eyes—which had been wet and fierce and alive with six years of grief and hunger—went calm. Deeply, terribly calm. She pressed her fingertips to her temples as the memory poured in, and tears ran down her face, and she smiled, and the smile was radiant and complete and utterly silent, and Ansel realized with a horror that would keep him company for years that she was trying to laugh at something her daughter had said at the reception, and there was nothing there. No hitch in the breath. No catch. Just the motion of laughing, empty, like a bell with the clapper removed, ringing shape without sound forever.

"Oh," Maren said, when it was done. Her voice was wondering. "Oh, she wore blue. She wore *blue*, and her father cried into his hat, and the cake leaned to the left, and I remember all of it, I remember every minute—" She turned to Ansel, glowing, ruined, whole. "Thank you. Thank you. It was worth it. Do you hear me? It was worth every—"

And then she stopped. Frowned faintly, as if reaching for a word on a high shelf. And said, "Was there something else? I feel as though we were finishing something."

The ledger had taken the derivative feeling too. Including, apparently, the feeling of the trade itself—including, perhaps, the memory of having made it. Ansel checked the Register with numb fingers. The entry was there, complete and correct: *Ostler, M. — 7 yrs. laughter (whole) → memory, wedding day (held whole). Derivatives surrendered per Art. IX.* And beneath it, in smaller script, the ledger's own annotation, which he had never seen it make before:

*Balance achieved. Client departed satisfied.*

*Satisfied.* Ansel read the word four times. The ledger wasn't lying—that was the thing. By its own measures, by the cold beautiful arithmetic of the Codex, the trade was perfect. Equal weight, willing parties, full disclosure. Every rule observed. Every line accurate. And a woman had walked out of his counting-room carrying her daughter's wedding day like a lamp, and would never again laugh at anything, and did not know what she had paid, and would never grieve it, because he had also sold her the grief.

He sat alone in the counting-room for a long time after the door closed. The binding-moths turned in their cases. The elms dropped their leaves. Eventually he dipped his pen and entered the trade in the daily summary, in his small exact hand, and his hand did not shake, because accuracy was possible, and he hated it, suddenly and completely, how possible it was—how the rules held him steady precisely when he most wanted to fall apart, how the order he loved asked nothing of him and therefore let him do *anything*.

*Thoroughness is mercy,* Ondrey had said. *Half-trades are cruelty.*

But this had been a whole trade. Whole as a grave.

Ansel blew out the lamps and climbed the stairs to the archive to shelve the day's summaries, and it was there, in the green dark between the shelves, that he noticed it: a discrepancy. One line in the master ledger's index, on a page he had passed ten thousand times, where the foliation jumped—from folio 411 to folio 413—as neat as you please, as though no one had ever thought to wonder what happened to 412.

Nine years he had kept these books. Nine years, and he had never once wondered.

He stood in the dark with his lamp raised, looking at the gap where a page should be, and felt the first cold thread of something that was not yet dread, but was learning to become it.

Someone had torn out a page of the master ledger.

And someone had been very careful that he should never think to miss it.
