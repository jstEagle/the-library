## 6. The Night the Stars Went Out

The audit had entered its third hour when Isolde Vane stopped being polite.

"Again," she said, tapping the star-chart with one finger. "The same discrepancy. The constellation you call the Shepherd's Crook is drawn with six stars in your master chart. Every survey I've ever taken records seven."

Ansel leaned over her shoulder, close enough to smell the ink on her hands and something beneath it—cold air, high places, the particular cleanliness of a woman who slept outdoors by choice. "Charts drift," he said. "Ink fades unevenly. A scribe copying a copy of a copy—"

"I've accounted for scribal error since I was twelve years old, Master Winterbourne." She didn't look at him. Her finger stayed pinned to the sixth star, the missing one, and he noticed her hand was very steady and very white. "This isn't drift. This is subtraction. Someone removed a star from your records, and they did it cleanly, which means they knew what they were doing, which means it wasn't an accident, which means—"

She stopped.

Ansel felt it too. Not a sound. The absence of one. The Guildhall at night was never silent—it settled and ticked like an old ship, pipes knocking, the bottled echoes on their shelves murmuring to themselves in their sleep. All of that went out at once, the way a candle goes out, and what remained was a silence so complete he could hear his own pulse in his ears.

Isolde rose slowly from the table. "Do you feel that?"

"Yes."

"What *is* that?"

"I don't know." And the terrible thing, the thing he would remember afterward, was that part of him did know—or suspected—and had known for days, since the rewritten pages in the master ledger, since the entry in his grandmother's hand: *one kingdom, whole.* He had told himself it was an old debt, a settled matter, a curiosity. He had told himself a great many things.

They moved to the window together, not touching, though he was aware of her shoulder a hand's width from his own.

The night sky over the Guildhall was clear. It was always clear here; the guild's founders had chosen the site for its sightlines, back when the order still mattered enough to choose anything. Ansel knew the sky above Thornhollow the way Isolde knew it better—the Shepherd's Crook, the Lantern, the Long Road, the Queen's Ring, all of it as familiar as the lines of his own palm.

He looked up and saw nothing.

Not cloud. Not fog. Clouds had texture, depth, the grey-on-grey suggestion of shapes behind shapes. This was a flat, total blackness, as if someone had hung a sheet of painted velvet where the sky should be. No stars. No moon. No horizon-glow from the distant towns. Just black, absolute and near, so near it seemed he could raise a hand and touch it.

"The stars," Isolde said. Her voice came out small and stripped of all its audit-room authority. "Ansel. Where are the stars."

He had no answer. He watched her instead, because watching her was easier than watching the sky, and saw her do the thing she must have done a thousand times on survey nights: she raised her arm, sighted along her fingers toward where the Lantern ought to hang, and held there, unmoving, for the length of ten heartbeats. Twenty. Thirty.

Her arm lowered. "I've mapped that sky every season for nine years," she said. "Through storms. Through fever. Through a winter I spent snowed into a bothy eating boiled pine bark. I could draw it blind. I could name every star in it and tell you which ones the old charts get wrong." She turned to face him, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet but furious, fury being the shape her fear took. "And now there is no sky. There is no *up*. Do you understand? The whole architecture of navigation is gone. Every road, every coastline, every fix I have ever taken—"

"Isolde."

"—is meaningless. I am a surveyor of a country that no longer has a ceiling, and if the ceiling can go, then what else—"

"*Isolde.*" He caught her hands before he thought about whether he should. They were freezing. She let him hold them, staring at him with an expression he'd never seen on her—not anger, not the bright combative delight she took in catching his errors, but something rawer. Something that looked, unbearably, like trust.

"The ledger," he said. "Come away from the window. Come *look* at the ledger."

* * *

They ran through corridors that had gone strange. Ansel had walked these halls his entire adult life and knew them the way he knew his own handwriting, but tonight the lamps guttered as they passed, and twice he turned down a passage and found it longer than it should be, the far door receding as they approached, and once the corridor they were in was not the corridor he had meant to take at all. Beside him Isolde counted under her breath—steps, turns, landings—a surveyor's habit, pinning the world down with numbers.

"Third landing," she muttered. "Eleven steps. Eleven steps, Ansel, this stair had fourteen this morning."

"Don't count."

"If I don't count, who will?"

The map room occupied the guild's eastern wing, a vaulted chamber where three centuries of cartographers had worked by daylight, because starlight and lamplight both lied. Ansel lit every lamp anyway. He wanted light the way a drowning man wants rope.

Isolde went straight to her work-table, where her audit maps lay weighted with brass corners, and stood over them without moving.

"Ansel."

"What is it?"

"Come look at your kingdom."

He came. He looked. And the floor seemed to tilt beneath him, because the map was wrong in a way no map should be able to be wrong.

Isolde's survey of the realm was a masterpiece—he'd resented its precision for two weeks now, the way one resents anything that threatens to be right. The borders were drawn in her own hand, verified against boundary stones and ward-markers, the coast traced tide by tide. But the ink was moving. Slowly, gently, the way frost creeps across a window, the western border was drawing itself inward. Villages she had plotted were fading—not crossed out, not smudged, simply *lightening*, as if the paper were deciding they had never been worth the ink. The town of Marrow's End, forty leagues west, was already a ghost of itself, its name barely legible, its little church-spire mark dissolving into blank parchment.

"It's shrinking," Isolde whispered. "The kingdom is shrinking on my map."

"That's impossible. Maps don't—"

"No. Maps don't." She pressed both palms flat on the table as if holding it down. "Maps are arguments. Maps are claims. This—" her voice cracked and steadied itself with visible effort, "—this is a record. And records, Master Winterbourne, are your guild's entire business. So you are going to tell me what is written in your ledgers that could do *this*, or I am going to start screaming, and I warn you I have a great deal of breath and nowhere left to spend it."

He thought of the rewritten pages. His grandmother's hand wearing his grandmother's authority like a stolen coat. *Received in partial payment: the northern marches, unremembered. Received: the village of Greywater, its dead and its unborn. Owed remaining: one kingdom, whole.*

He thought of the refugees who would come. He did not yet know they would come, but some animal part of him, the part that had spent twenty years reading other people's debts, already understood what a payment schedule looked like.

"There's a debt," he said.

Isolde stared at him.

"A debt owed by the crown. Centuries old. It's in the master ledger—I found it four nights ago, pages rewritten, and I told myself it was history, bookkeeping, a closed account." He heard his own voice and hated how small it sounded, how much it sounded like a clerk explaining a discrepancy. "I think it isn't closed. I think it's being collected."

"Collected." She said the word the way one handles a dead bird. "The stars going out is a *collection notice*."

"The wards fell at the same moment. You heard it—you must have heard it."

She had. They both had: a sound like tearing silk, enormous and intimate at once, rolling across the sky from every direction, the sound of something woven coming apart. The border wards. Ansel had never seen them—they predated the guild, predates everything—but every child in the kingdom grew up knowing they were there, the way children know the ground will hold. A hum at the edge of hearing. A certainty. And tonight that certainty had ripped, and the dark beyond the wards had poured in through the tear like water through a holed hull.

Outside, faintly, carried on a wind that shouldn't reach this far inland, came bells. Church bells, alarm bells, dozens of towers ringing at once from villages whose names Ansel had catalogued in shipment manifests. And beneath the bells, another sound, farther off, that he couldn't name and didn't want to: a low, vast exhalation, like the country itself breathing out.

Isolde pulled a fresh chart toward her and began, with shaking hands, to plot. "If the wards are down, the edges go first. That's how wards work—perimeter failure, then progressive collapse inward. Give me the border towns' coordinates. All of them. We'll mark them and check the map every hour, and we'll see the rate of loss, and a rate means a schedule, and a schedule means—"

"A due date," Ansel finished quietly.

She looked up at him. Lamplight moved across her face. "You've known for four days."

"I hoped for four days."

"Hoping is not a discipline, Ansel. Counting is." But there was no cruelty in it. She slid the chart toward him and put a pen in his hand, closing his fingers around it the way one closes the fingers of the dead around something precious. "So we count. Together. Whatever's coming, we'll meet it with numbers, because numbers are the only prayer either of us knows."

They worked. It was the strangest hour of either of their lives, and later each would privately think it was also, in its way, the truest: two people who had spent weeks sparring across a table now bent over the same page in a lamp-lit room while the sky outside stood empty, marking the deaths of towns in careful ink. Marrow's End: gone. The twin lights of Cape Sorrel: gone. The ferry-crossing at Lowmere, where Ansel had once bought a bottle containing the echo of a wedding: gone, gone, gone. Each time the map faded a little further, Isolde's pen followed it, recording, and Ansel understood suddenly why she did it—that a thing recorded, even in retreat, was not wholly lost. It was the only mercy their trades allowed.

Somewhere past midnight, her hand stopped moving.

"Ansel." Her voice was very quiet. "Look at the rate."

He looked. The losses weren't linear. They were accelerating, each hour taking more than the last, the curve bending downward like a hill falling away toward a cliff.

"At this rate," she said, and made herself finish, because finishing was who she was, "the capital goes dark inside a month. The center of the kingdom—inside two."

"And after the center?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. They both understood what remained after the center of a thing was taken. Nothing. *One kingdom, whole.* Whole meant all of it. Whole meant them.

Isolde set down her pen. For a long moment she stood with her head bowed over the dying map, and Ansel saw her shoulders shake once, twice, and steady. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and blazing.

"Then we have a month," she said, "to find out who holds this debt, what it wants, and how a crown pays for a kingdom it borrowed. Because I refuse—" her voice rang off the vaulted ceiling, refusing the empty sky outside along with everything else, "—I absolutely refuse to stand in the dark counting down to my own erasure like a good little line item. Somebody signed for this, Ansel. Somebody kept these books. And somebody is going to tell us how to unmake the trade."

He looked at her—at this woman who had arrived three weeks ago to audit his life and had instead, without either of them noticing the exact entry, become the fixed point he navigated by—and felt the terror in his chest change shape. Not smaller. Sharper. Aimed.

"You'll want the master ledger," he said. "The original contract. It's in the deep archive, behind the sealed wing."

"Then lead the way."

He turned toward the door—and stopped, because through the map room's high windows, far off to the west, the darkness was no longer uniform. At the horizon's edge, where the border towns had been, a faint grey shimmer was rising, like heat-haze in reverse, like the world seen through water running backward. The edge of the country, dissolving into whatever the country had been before it agreed to exist.

And moving within that shimmer, dim as drowned lanterns, he could see lights. Tiny, scattered, wavering points of gold and orange, dozens of them, hundreds, drifting slowly eastward through the unwritten dark.

Lamps. Torches. Candles in windows that no longer had walls.

"They're coming," Ansel said. "Isolde—the people from the edges. They're walking out of places that don't exist anymore, and they're coming *here*."

* * *

By dawn, the road below the Guildhall gates was full of them.

They came in families and alone, in carts and on foot, carrying the impossible inventory of the erased: loaves of bread baked in ovens that had never existed, letters addressed to villages no map could find, a spinning wheel still warm, a child's shoe, a caged bird singing a song from a town that was now a rumor. They spoke names that slid off the memory like water off glass—ansel heard a woman say hers three times and lost it between the second and third—and they all asked the same question, over and over, in a dozen accents:

*Where is our home? What was it called? Please. Does anyone remember?*

Ansel stood on the guild steps beside Isolde and looked out at the river of the unrecorded, and felt the full weight of his guild's silence settle onto him like a stone. Somewhere in the master ledger, in his grandmother's hand, the payments were listed. Village by village. Star by star. And he had read those entries and called them history.

Isolde's hand found his. He didn't know which of them reached first, and it didn't matter. Her fingers were cold and did not let go.

"We need the original contract," she said. "Tonight. Before the next payment comes due."

Below them, the crowd parted briefly, and Ansel saw, at the rear of the refugees, a figure on horseback who was not fleeing. The rider sat motionless at the edge of the torchlight, watching the Guildhall with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, and even at that distance Ansel felt the gaze pass over him like a finger running down a column of figures, pausing, circling one entry.

Circling *him*.

The rider turned and was gone into the dark, and Ansel's blood went cold, because he understood, with a clerk's certainty, that the collection had just acquired a face—and that it knew his name.
