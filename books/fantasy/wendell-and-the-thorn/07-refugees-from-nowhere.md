## 7. Refugees from Nowhere

The first of them arrived before dawn, and Ansel knew something was wrong with them before he knew anything else about them.

He had come down to the Guildhall's great doors to bar them against the wind—the hinges had been groaning for a week, and the sound scraped against his nerves like a quill nib gone dry—and he found the fog on the threshold thick and moving against itself, folding inward in slow gray spirals. Out of that fog came people.

They came up the causeway in a strung-out line, maybe forty of them at first, then more behind, their shapes resolving out of the mist one by one like figures surfacing through deep water. Farmers by their hands. A smith with his leather apron still buckled on, though he carried no tools. Children asleep across shoulders, swaying with each step. They moved the way people move when they have been walking for days—not stumbling, not desperate, just *spent*, emptied down to the simple mechanics of putting one foot after another.

Ansel stood in the doorway and did not move to help them, because he was looking at what they carried.

A woman near the front held a wooden cradle, empty, its rockers worn smooth. Behind her an old man clutched a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and even from twenty paces Ansel could see the shape of it: a weathervane, a rooster worked in hammered copper, green with age. A boy of perhaps ten carried a door key the size of his forearm, iron, ornate, the kind made for houses that expected to stand for centuries.

These were not things you grab when your village burns. These were not things you carry when you flee. These were *household gods*, the objects a person takes when they believe—when they know—that everything else will be there tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that.

Nobody flees carrying a door key. Unless the house is already gone.

"Please," the woman said, and her voice was hoarse as if she'd been talking to no one for a long time. "Is this the Ledgerkeepers' place? The old man said—" She stopped. Started again. "We need someone to write us down."

Ansel felt those words go into him like a splinter.

*Write us down.*

Behind him, the Guildhall creaked in the wind, three floors of shelving and ledgers and the accumulated weight of ten thousand impossible trades, and Ansel Winterbourne—who had spent six years learning to be invisible inside it—stepped aside and held the door.

* * *

By midmorning there were two hundred of them in the entry hall and the counting room beyond, sitting on the floor among the ledger carts because there was nowhere else, and the guild masters had locked themselves upstairs behind the bronze door of the Convocation Chamber, which told Ansel everything he needed to know about where the surviving authority of the Ledgerkeepers' Guild intended to be when history came asking questions.

He did what he could do, which was organize. Blankets from the storeroom. Water from the cistern. Bread from the kitchen stores that had been meant for the autumn audit feast. He moved through the hall with his sleeves rolled and his hair falling in his eyes, and the refugees watched him with a patience that frightened him more than panic would have. They weren't demanding. They were waiting to be *processed*. They had understood, somehow, faster than he had, what kind of place this was and what kind of help it gave.

The woman with the cradle was called Maren. She sat on the steps of the dais where the master assessors received clients, her hands folded on her knees, and she answered his questions in a flat, careful voice, the way people speak when they have rehearsed a thing so many times it has worn smooth.

"Harlow Fen," she said. "That's where we're from. Was." She corrected herself without any change in expression, and that correction was worse than weeping would have been. "On the Vell. The river road. You'd know it if—"

"I don't," Ansel said quietly. "I'm sorry. I don't know it."

"No," Maren said. "Nobody does now. That's the thing I keep trying to explain to people. It isn't that it burned or flooded or got sacked. It's that nobody *knows* it. My husband's mother lives—I mean lived—in Callowmere, two days east, and we walked there first, and she looked at me like I was mad. She said, *who's Harlow Fen?* She'd traded wool there every season for thirty years. Her own daughter-in-law's village, and she looked at me like I'd named a place off the moon."

She reached out and touched the cradle beside her, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to check it was real.

"The baby died in winter," she said. "Two years back. The cradle was going to go to my sister when she wed. That's why I took it. Everything else—" Her voice caught, not with grief but with the effort of precision. "Everything else was still in the house. I could see the house. I stood in the road and I could see it, right where it always was, and then I blinked, and there was just the field. Just grass, grown up like it had been fifty years. And I couldn't remember the color of our shutters. I painted them myself, seven years ago, blue, I know they were blue, but I can't—"

She stopped. Looked at her own hands.

"You keep books here," she said. "That's what the old man told us, walking. He said the Ledgerkeepers write down trades in things that can't be traded, and that if a thing is written, it stays written. So I'm asking you. Write us down. Write Harlow Fen down. The names, the streets, the well on Tanner's Row with the crooked lip. Write it before I lose the rest of it too, because it's going, sir. It goes a little more every night. Last night I lost my neighbor's face. I've known Odile since we were girls and I woke up this morning and her face was just—smooth. Like an egg. And she was standing right next to me."

Ansel knelt in front of her. He was aware, distantly, of the hall around them—the murmur of two hundred displaced lives, the smell of wet wool and road dust, the great chandelier above the dais swaying faintly on its chain—but mostly he was aware of the ledger cart at the edge of the dais, and the current-day register lying open on top of it, and the pen in his breast pocket, and the terrible arithmetic assembling itself in his mind.

"Maren," he said. "When did it happen? When did the village—when did the field close over?"

"Nine nights ago." She didn't hesitate. "The night the stars went out. We counted. Everyone counts now."

Nine nights ago. The wards fell. The stars went dark. And Ansel had spent nine days in the archive with Isolde Vane's star-charts spread across three tables, tracing the discrepancy she'd found, telling himself it was an accounting error, a clerical fault, something with a tidy explanation that a tidy man could find.

He opened the register to the payment schedule.

He had seen it before, of course. Every Ledgerkeeper saw it; it was the spine of the whole operation, the rhythm the guild lived by. Debts entered against the crown were collected on a schedule, and the schedule was not arbitrary—it followed the old calendar of settlements, quarter-days and cross-quarter days, the dates when the kingdom's founders had signed away pieces of what they didn't understand. Ansel had copied that schedule a hundred times in his apprenticeship. He had inked it into fair copies. He had *maintained* it.

He put his finger on the column of dates and read downward, and his mouth went dry.

The entries were small. That was the horror of it—he had always thought of the crown's debts as vast, abstract things, kingdoms' worth of obligation, and so he had never actually read the line items. But the line items were villages. *One mill settlement, northern Vell, with appurtenances.* *One fishing hamlet, coast road.* *One parish, population ninety-one.* Each dated. Each spaced along the calendar like beads on a string, marching inward from the borders toward the capital, toward the heart, on a schedule measured in weeks.

And the most recent date—the one marked *collected*, in the old clerk's shorthand, a small neat tick in the margin—was nine days ago.

"Harlow Fen," Ansel said aloud, though he hadn't meant to.

Maren went very still. "You know it?"

"No," Ansel said. "But the book does."

* * *

He left Isolde minding the hall—she had arrived at midday with her map cases under her arm, taken one look at the crowd, and started organizing the children into a corner with the brisk tenderness of someone who had grown up in a big family—and went down alone into the vault levels, taking lamps, taking keys, taking the register and the master index both.

The archive at midnight was a country of its own. Ten thousand ledgers in ranked shelves descending into the cold, each one indexed, cross-referenced, chained to its lectern when consulted. Ansel had spent six years learning this country's roads. Tonight he walked them fast, lamp held high, breath smoking, following the index numbers for crown obligations down and down until he reached the sealed cases—the black-spined volumes that apprentices were taught to shelve and never open.

He opened one anyway. His grandmother's handwriting was in it, that same hand he'd found rewriting the master ledger, and he forced himself past it, past the dread coiling in his stomach, to the thing he needed: the collection record. The actual tally of what had been taken, and when.

He laid it open on the lectern beside the payment schedule and began, with the cold mechanical part of his mind that loved order even as the rest of him shook, to check the arithmetic.

Village by village. Date by date.

It matched. Of course it matched. Every erasure the refugees described—every vanished town, every blank space on Isolde's maps, every smooth-egg face in Maren's memory—fell exactly on the schedule's dates, in exactly the schedule's order, working inward from the borders like frost creeping across a windowpane. The debt was being paid not all at once but *in installments*, and the installments were places, and the places were people, and the people were—

Ansel sat down on the vault floor, quite suddenly, his back against the shelf, and put his head in his hands.

Because here was the thing he could not stop seeing, the thing that had been waiting for him since Maren said *write us down*: he had kept these books. Not metaphorically. Literally. For six years he had trimmed the wicks and oiled the hinges and copied the schedules and balanced the columns of this very ledger, and he had done it gladly, gratefully, because the work asked nothing of him and the order asked nothing of him and the rules asked nothing of him. He had thought himself a caretaker. A humble servant of a quiet institution.

He had been a clerk for the end of the world, and he had been *good* at it.

Every tick in the margin of the collection record was in a hand he recognized—old Master Ellery-Voss, dead eleven years, who had taught Ansel his letters and his sums and how to hold a pen so the ink wouldn't blot. Ellery-Voss had ticked off a fishing hamlet on the coast road and gone to supper. Had ticked off a parish of ninety-one souls and complained about the damp. The guild had known. Some part of the guild had always known, the way a body knows what its hands are doing, and it had filed the knowledge in black-spined books in a basement and called it stewardship.

And Ansel had never asked. Because asking would have made him visible, and visibility was the one thing he had sworn off, the one trade he'd refused to make.

He heard footsteps on the stairs—quick ones, boot-heels on stone—and a moment later Isolde came down into the vault with a lamp of her own and her coat still buttoned wrong from hurrying. She found him on the floor among the shelves and didn't ask why. She set her lamp on the lectern, looked at the open ledgers, and read. He watched her do it: the schedule, the collection record, the matching dates. He watched the understanding arrive in her face the way weather arrives over hills.

"It's a payment plan," she said. Her voice was steady, which he was beginning to learn was how she handled fear—by refusing it room. "The whole kingdom. They're paying it off in tranches. Border first, then the provinces, then—" She traced the last dates with her finger, and her finger stopped. "Then the capital. Then everything. Ansel, how much time does the schedule give?"

He made himself look at the final entry. The last date on the page, the one marked in the old shorthand not as *collected* but as *due in full*. He did the sum twice because the first time he hoped he'd miscounted.

"Sixty-one days," he said. "Give or take."

Isolde closed her eyes briefly. Then she opened them, and pulled off her coat, and sat down on the cold stone beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and picked up the collection record herself.

"Then we'd better start reading," she said.

They read until the lamps burned low. Village names, parish rolls, the cold bookkeeping of disappearance. And somewhere past the third hour, Ansel's finger stopped on an entry near the end of the schedule—a date sixty days out, the second-to-last installment, flagged in the margin with a symbol he had never seen in any guild text: a small circled star, drawn in his grandmother's hand.

Beneath it, in the same writing, four words:

*Not this one. Me.*

He stared at it for a long time. Then he turned the page, looking for whatever she had meant to pay it with—and found the rest of the entry torn away, ragged at the spine, cut out with a blade long ago.

Above him, far up through the floors of the Guildhall, the bronze doors of the Convocation Chamber boomed open for the first time in nine days, and voices came cascading down the stairwells—urgent, frightened, arguing—and Ansel knew, with the certainty of a man who has finally learned to read the signs in his own house, that the guild masters had felt the schedule turn its next page too.

And that someone was coming to collect.
