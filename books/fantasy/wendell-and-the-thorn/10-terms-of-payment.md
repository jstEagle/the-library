## 10. Terms of Payment

The original contract was not kept in the Guildhall.

That was the first thing I understood, standing in the ash-smelling dark of the master archive with Corvane's summons folded in my pocket like a knife I was afraid to look at directly. The Ledgerkeepers' Guild had rules for everything—where a ledger slept, how it was warded, what oil fed its lamps—and the oldest rule of all was the one no one spoke aloud: *the first debt is never stored with the other debts.* You do not keep the wolf in the sheepfold. You keep it somewhere the sheep will never think to look.

My grandmother had thought to look.

I found her handwriting again three nights later, in a marginal note inside a water-damaged codex on the *Etymology of Obligations*, a book so dull that three centuries of apprentices had used it as a doorstop and no one had ever read it through. She had written in the gutter of page 214, in that small, slanted, impatient script I knew better than my own:

*The crown's instrument is held at Vellum & Ash, under the name of a bookseller who never sold a book. Ask for nothing. Bring your own light.*

* * *

Vellum & Ash stood in a street that had been half-erased.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. The street still existed—the cobbles were there, the doorways, the iron sign creaking on its bracket—but it existed the way a word exists after you've repeated it too many times: correctly shaped and utterly empty of meaning. Two doors down, a house was simply *less* than a house. Not ruined. Ruin implies something happened to it. This house had been *subtracted*. Where its windows should have been there were rectangles of grey that my eyes slid off like water off oiled cloth. A cat sat on its step, washing one paw, and when it looked at me it had no face—not wounds, not absence, just a smooth furred blank where a face should be—and it went back to washing its paw as if this were the most ordinary thing in the world.

Isolde walked beside me with her surveyor's case strapped across her back and her chin up, the way she did everything, as though the world were an audit she intended to pass. She had insisted on coming. I had insisted she stay. We had both lost those arguments, which is perhaps the truest definition of partnership I know.

"Your maps," I said quietly, because speaking loudly felt like knocking over dishes in a sickroom. "The eastern march. How much is left?"

She didn't pretend not to understand. "As of last night? Forty-one percent." Her voice was level. Her knuckles, wrapped around the strap of her case, were white. "The coastline's gone. Saltmere, Greyharbour, the whole fen country. My charts show open sea where my own mother was born, and the sea is *accurate*, Ansel. That's the part I can't—" She stopped. Started again. "The paper agrees with the erasure. Paper doesn't lie. That used to be a comfort."

We reached the shop. The door was black oak gone silver with age, and above it, in letters that had been re-gilded so many times they stood out from the wood like scar tissue: **VELLUM & ASH — BOOKS BOUGHT, BOOKS BOUND**. No mention of selling. My grandmother had been exact even in her warnings.

I knocked. Nothing. I tried the handle; it turned.

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air in a tomb that has been politely furnished. Shelves rose into darkness on every wall, laden with books whose spines were blank—not worn blank, but *unwritten*, smooth leather the color of nothing much. A single candle burned on the counter, though I saw no one to have lit it, and beside the candle lay a ledger closed with a clasp of black iron.

And behind the counter, in a chair that must have been magnificent two hundred years ago, sat a skeleton in a bookseller's apron.

I did not scream. I am proud of that, or I was, briefly, before I understood that screaming would have been beside the point entirely. The skeleton's jaw was wired shut with copper thread. Its finger-bones rested on the counter's edge with terrible patience, as if it had only stepped out for a moment, two centuries ago, and meant to return. Pinned to its apron was a note in a hand I recognized with a lurch that went through me like cold water finding a crack in a boot.

*He kept his post until the end,* my grandmother had written. *The end came slowly. Be quicker than he was.*

"Ansel." Isolde's hand found mine in the dark. Her fingers were freezing. "Tell me you knew this was here."

"I knew a bookseller was here." My voice sounded very far away. "I didn't know he'd stayed at his desk."

She squeezed once and let go, and we crossed the shop together, past shelves of blank books—I touched one, and its cover was warm, faintly, like the flank of a sleeping animal—and up to the counter, where the iron-clasped ledger waited beneath the candle's small stubborn flame.

The clasp opened at my touch. Of course it did. It had been waiting for a Winterbourne.

* * *

The contract was written on vellum so fine it was translucent, and it was written in ink that moved.

Not writhed—that would have been almost comforting, almost monstrous, something you could fight. It *moved* the way slow water moves, settling and resettling, so that each time I read a clause the letters had shifted by a hair's width, rearranging themselves into slightly different truths. Isolde leaned close beside me, her breath quick against my ear, and together we read the founding document of our kingdom's damnation.

*In the Year of the Long Frost, being the four hundredth and eleventh year of the reckoning of the Ledgerkeepers' Guild, let it be entered that Aldric Winterbourne, First Keeper, has extended to Osric, second of that name, King of the realm called [here the ink pooled and darkened, refusing to hold its shape], the following accommodation—*

"The founder," Isolde whispered. "Your guild's founder lent the money himself?"

"Not money." I turned the page with a care I usually reserved for things already dead. "Look."

*—namely, existence entire. The said kingdom, having wagered its continuance upon a war it could not fund, does hereby acknowledge itself borrowed into being, principal and interest, and does further acknowledge that continuance is a thing which may be recalled.*

Below that, in a different hand—a king's hand, fat and self-assured, the handwriting of a man who had never once doubted that tomorrow would arrive to serve him—was the royal assent, complete with a seal I had seen a thousand times stamped on tax writs and proclamations. Osric II had signed away his kingdom's existence to win a war, and then, presumably, had won the war, and gone home, and feasted, and died comfortably in his bed, leaving the bill to everyone who came after him.

Four hundred years of everyone who came after him.

"There's a schedule," Isolde said. Her surveyor's mind had gone to work; I could hear it clicking behind her words like a loom. "Payment terms. Look here—*interest accruing at the rate of one village per season, commencing at the edges.*" She laughed, a horrible sound with no humor in it at all. "It's not random. It was never random. The erasure follows the contract's amortization table. Someone has been paying it down for four centuries in installments and calling it bad weather."

"And deferring the rest," I said. The fury from the king's court was back in my throat, tasting of copper. "Every time a crown renegotiated. Every 'extension.' They weren't reducing the debt. They were moving it." Onto lifespans. Onto memories. Onto children, in the guild's later ledgers, entered in columns labeled with euphemisms I had copied out myself in my neat apprentice's hand. I had kept those books. I had *been proud* of keeping them well.

Then Isolde went very still beside me, and said, "Ansel. Read the discharge clause."

I read it.

*Provided always that the whole of the debt may be discharged before final collection by a voluntary trade of equal weight, made willingly, at a door unrecorded.*

"A trade of equal weight," I repeated. My mouth was dry. "Made willingly. At a door unrecorded." The Thorn. My grandmother's briar, growing through the floor of a sealed garden, absent from every ledger she ever kept. She hadn't planted an escape hatch for herself. She'd planted the *mechanism of discharge*, and she had died trying to use it, and the clause had sat here in the dark for four hundred years waiting for someone to find it.

For a moment—one whole, golden, idiotic moment—I felt relief. A trade! Trades were my native language. I had spent my life weighing impossible things: seven years of laughter against one perfect memory, a shadow against a promise, a name against a favor. Give the Thorn something heavy enough and the kingdom stands. Gold. Land. The crown jewels. The king could pay; the court could pay; there were vaults under the capital stuffed with four centuries of plunder that would weigh down any scale in creation.

Isolde read my face, and said gently, "Keep reading."

*Let it be noted,* said the next line, in Aldric Winterbourne's severe founding script, *that weight, in this instrument, shall not be measured in gold, nor land, nor goods, nor years, nor any commodity whatsoever. The Warden of Accounts weighs only what cannot be replaced. Let the parties understand: equal weight is measured in love.*

The candle flickered. Somewhere in the dark of the shop, one of the blank books sighed, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.

"That's not—" I stopped. Started again. "That's not a standard clause. That's not *any* clause. Debts are discharged with consideration. Value. You can't put love on a balance sheet, there's no unit, there's no—"

"There's no unit," Isolde agreed quietly, "because it isn't measured in units. It's measured in *cost*. Ansel, think about what Corvane is. He told us. He was a man once, traded away piece by piece until only the function remained. He knows exactly what things cost, because he was paid for in them." She turned to face me fully, and the candlelight made her eyes enormous. "The Thorn doesn't want your gold. Gold can be stolen back. It wants the thing you'd refuse to give. It wants the payment to *hurt proportionally to the size of the debt*. That's the whole design. That's why nobody's paid it in four hundred years. Because the price of a kingdom, weighed properly, is—"

She didn't finish. Neither of us finished it. The sentence hung there between us, unfinished, enormous, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I was arriving at, and I watched her flinch from it, and I loved her so much in that moment that I understood—with a horror that began in my chest and spread outward like frost across a window—that my love was itself a weight on the scale. That the more we meant to each other, the more the door would ask. That the Thorn had been measuring us since the day we met, patiently, the way a ledger measures everything.

"It asked my grandmother," I heard myself say. My voice was steady. Some detached clerk deep inside me was taking dictation while the rest of me screamed. "She planted the door. She knew the clause. She meant to pay. And she failed—which means either she couldn't find anything heavy enough, or..." I swallowed. "Or she found exactly what was needed, and she couldn't make herself give it. And she was the strongest person I ever knew."

Outside, far off toward the east, came a sound I had learned to dread in the last weeks: a long, low exhalation, like the world sighing out a breath it would not draw again. Another village. Another line item. Isolde's hand found mine again and gripped hard, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the contract hadn't already said better, in moving ink, four hundred years ago.

We copied the clause. We took the copy, and we left the skeleton to his post—he had earned it, God help him, he had kept the wolf's leash longer than anyone—and we walked back out into the half-erased street, where the faceless cat still washed its paw on the step of the house that was less than a house.

Halfway down the street, Isolde stopped.

"I'll do it," she said.

"No."

"You haven't let me finish a sentence about this in three days, Ansel, and I'm finishing this one. I'm a surveyor. I map what exists. Do you understand what I *am*? I am a woman whose entire life is knowing precisely where things are and how much of them there are. I have spent twenty years learning to measure the world, and now I finally know what the measurement is *for*." Her voice cracked and she drove through the crack like a cart through a ford. "If the door weighs love, then let it weigh mine. I have no family. No guild. No one depends on me except you, and you'd survive me—you're the most survivable man I've ever met, you survive *everything*, it's infuriating—"

"*No.*" It came out of me like a slammed door. Louder than I'd spoken in the shop, loud enough to startle birds out of a roof that might not exist tomorrow. "You don't get to spend yourself to save me. That's not sacrifice, Isolde, that's just another way of leaving, and I have buried enough people who thought they were helping—"

"Then what?" she demanded, and her eyes were blazing and wet. "What's your plan, bookkeeper? Because I've read the clause too. Equal weight, measured in love, given willingly. There are exactly two people in this kingdom who qualify, and one of them is standing in front of me refusing to do arithmetic."

I had no answer. That was the truth of it, the clean, terrible, audited truth: I had no answer, because there was only one entry that balanced, and I had known it since the moment I read the clause, and every hour I spent not saying it aloud was an hour I was stealing from the villages at the edges, from the faceless cat, from her.

So I lied to her. It was the first lie I had ever told her, and I told it fluently, in my best ledger-hand.

"We'll find another way," I said. "There's always another way. The guild taught me that."

And she believed me—or wanted to believe me, which amounts to the same thing on a balance sheet—and she took my arm, and we walked home through the unwriting streets to plan a rescue that I already knew had only one possible ending.

That night, alone in the Guildhall, by the light of a lamp I would remember forever, I took out a fresh sheet of paper and practiced, eleven times, writing the words *paid in full* in my own hand.

It has to be my hand. That's the last clause. The discharge must be *entered by the debtor*.

I have beautiful handwriting. Everyone at the guild always said so.
