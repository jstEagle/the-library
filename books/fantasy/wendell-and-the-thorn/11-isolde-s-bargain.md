## 11. Isolde's Bargain

Isolde had learned to read the sky the way other people read faces, and for three nights running the sky had been lying to her.

She sat in the Guildhall's chart room with her instruments spread across a table scarred by two centuries of careful hands—her astrolabe, her brass theodolite, her father's old plumb-line that had never once pointed at anything but truth. Before her lay the master star-chart of the kingdom, forty sheets of vellum stitched together into a single map of heaven, and she was correcting it again. Not because it was wrong. Because it was *shrinking*.

The constellation called the Shepherd's Crook had lost its handle. The River of Night now ended three degrees short of where every survey since her grandmother's time had placed its mouth. And last night, working by candlelight while Ansel slept badly in the next room, she had measured the gap between the stars Warden's Eye and the Miller's Wheel and found it smaller than it had been when she was nine years old and her father first put an astrolabe in her hands.

Stars did not move closer together. That was not how anything worked. Stars were the one fixed thing, the one honest arithmetic, the points against which all other points were set. If the stars were drifting, then it wasn't the stars that were moving.

It was everything else.

"You haven't slept."

Ansel stood in the doorway with two cups of chicory, and Isolde felt the familiar tightening in her chest that she had stopped pretending was irritation about a week ago. He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had found his grandmother's handwriting in a book that should never have been opened, which was precisely what he was. His hair stood up on the left side where he'd been pulling at it, a habit he probably didn't know he had, and his eyes had the particular hollow shine of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"I slept," she said.

"You fell asleep at the table and woke up arguing with the vellum. I heard you."

"The vellum started it." She took the cup because refusing it would have required more energy than she had. The chicory was bitter and over-steeped and exactly what she needed. "Ansel. Come look at this."

He came. He always came—that was the thing about him, the thing she had catalogued without meaning to, the way she catalogued everything: Ansel Winterbourne came when called, checked the locks twice, apologized to furniture he bumped into, and touched ledgers as if they were sleeping animals that might startle. She had made a study of him the way she made a study of constellations, patiently, from a distance, until one night she realized she had memorized him entirely and there was no longer any distance to keep.

He bent over the chart, and she watched his face do the thing it did when numbers agreed with his dread.

"That's the third correction this week," he said quietly.

"Third correction in the same region." She tapped the northeastern quadrant, where the border town of Harrowmere had been before Harrowmere stopped being anywhere. "It's not random. It's systematic. Whatever is eating the kingdom, it eats from the edges inward, and it eats along lines I can measure." She pulled a second sheet toward her, covered in her own cramped notation. "I've been plotting the rate of contraction. It's accelerating, but it isn't chaotic. It follows a schedule."

"A schedule." He said the word the way a man says the name of a disease.

"Someone is keeping books on the sky, Ansel. And the books are being settled."

* * *

They heard the horses at midmorning.

Not many horses—the Guildhall had gone quiet enough over the past weeks that Isolde could count sounds the way she counted stars—and not fast ones. These came at a walk, deliberate, unhurried, the pace of something that had never once needed to hurry because everything eventually came to it. Six riders by the sound, shod heavy, and beneath the hoofbeats a creak of leather and chain that Isolde's ear assigned, without evidence, to harness meant less for horses than for whatever followed them.

Ansel went white. Not pale—white, the color draining out of him like water out of a cracked cup, and he gripped the edge of the chart table hard enough that she heard the wood complain.

"That's him," he said. "That's Corvane."

"You've met him?"

"No. Nobody's met him. That's rather the point of him." Ansel was already moving, gathering loose pages, squaring them, aligning them—a man tidying a room before a flood. "He doesn't come to visit. He comes to collect. Masters Vellum and Osgood fled through the south door yesterday; you knew that. Master Quill took the guild seal and whatever gold was left and—" He stopped himself, jaw tight. "It doesn't matter. What matters is that when Corvane walks through that door, there will be exactly one Ledgerkeeper left in this building, and it will be me."

"And me," Isolde said.

"You're a royal surveyor. You should go back to the capital. You should be nowhere near—"

"Ansel." She let him hear it in her voice, the flat surveyor's tone she used for correcting men who thought enthusiasm substituted for measurement. "Sit down. We have perhaps ten minutes before he reaches the door, and I am not going to spend them watching you tidy."

He sat. His hands wouldn't stay still, so she took one of them and held it between both of hers, and he stared at their joined hands as though they were another discrepancy he couldn't explain.

"I need you to listen to me," she said, "because I've been thinking about this since the night the stars went out, and I've done the sums, and you are too close to them to see them clearly. So you're going to listen, and you're going to be a Ledgerkeeper about it, and you're going to admit the numbers are right."

His throat moved. "Isolde—"

"The debt is a kingdom, whole. Yes? That's what your grandmother's entry says. One kingdom, whole, owed to whoever holds the paper. Corvane holds the paper. He is collecting from the edges inward because a collection that size has to be taken in installments—you told me that yourself, that's how the guild works, nothing collected all at once because nothing can bear it all at once." She was talking fast now, the words coming the way star-fixes come when the horizon finally steadies. "But here's what you haven't considered, because you're a bookkeeper and I'm a surveyor: debts can be restructured. Collateral can be posted. Any account, however large, can be stayed—if something of sufficient value is offered against it."

"Nothing we have is worth a kingdom."

"*I* might be."

The silence after that was very long, and in it Isolde heard the hoofbeats stop outside, and the creak of someone dismounting with great care, and still neither of them moved.

"Explain," Ansel said at last, and his voice had gone very quiet and very controlled, the voice of a man holding a ledger shut with both hands.

"Corvane collects impossible things. That's his function—it's what the guild does, it's what the whole economy runs on. Years of laughter. Names. Memories. Lifespans. You showed me the entries yourself." She kept hold of his hand because letting go would have been a tactical error and possibly an unbearable one. "And what does the crown owe? Existence. The fact of being. Which means the currency this debt trades in is *being*—and I am the only person either of us knows who has spent thirty years making herself valuable in exactly that currency."

"I don't understand."

"Maps, Ansel. Charts. Fixtures." She leaned forward. "Every surveyor learns early that a map is a way of making a place *exist* twice—once in the world and once on paper, so that if the world loses it, the paper remembers. My family has surveyed this kingdom for four generations. I hold in my head the position of every star-fixture, every boundary stone, every triangulation point from the Salt Coast to the Thornwood. I am, quite literally, a secondary copy of the kingdom's existence. If Corvane takes the original—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she talked through it, "—he would be a fool not to take the copy too. Or better: to take the copy *instead*, and call the account restructured. A kingdom's existence, held in trust, in one surveyor's memory, until such time as the crown can pay in full. That's not payment. That's collateral. That's a stay of execution. And bureaucracies love nothing more than a reason not to finish a job."

She watched him work through it, watched the hope and horror arrive in his face in the wrong order, horror first.

"You'd be giving yourself to him," he said.

"I'd be *posting bond*."

"He erased Harrowmere, Isolde. He erased eleven thousand people and the road to them and the name of the road. You think he'll keep you in trust like a deposit? You think a thing like that keeps *anything*?" Ansel was on his feet now, and his voice cracked on the last word. "You want to hand the only person I—" He stopped. Closed his eyes. Started again, quieter, worse. "You want to hand yourself to the creature that is unmaking the world, on the theory that your memory is well-organized."

"It's extremely well-organized."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny." But her hands had gone cold around his, and she suspected he could feel it. "Ansel. Look at me. There is no version of the next week in which both of us walk away clean. Your grandmother tried to pay this debt and failed. The crown deferred it onto farmers' lifespans and children's names—we read those entries, we know what they did. Every path forward runs through Corvane, and Corvane takes payment in pieces of people. The only question left is whose pieces." She made herself say the rest steadily. "Mine are worth more. That's all. It's arithmetic. You taught me to respect the arithmetic."

"I never taught you anything. You arrived already knowing everything and corrected my filing system in an afternoon."

"Then respect it anyway."

* * *

The knock, when it came, was courteous. Three soft raps, evenly spaced, the knock of someone who intended to be answered but would never presume.

Ansel crossed the hall and opened the doors, and Magister Corvane entered the Guildhall of the Ledgerkeepers like winter entering a house—not rushing, not menacing, simply arriving, with the certainty that the building had been waiting for him.

Isolde had prepared herself for a monster. What stood in the doorway shaking rain from a grey traveling cloak was a tall, spare figure with a face like a page written on too many times—features worn smooth by erasure and rewriting, eyes the color of ledger-ink, movements precise as a pen-stroke. He was terrible the way an empty ledger is terrible. He was terrible the way a balanced column is terrible. Everything about him had been reduced to function, and the function fit him like a glove fits a hand that used to be something else.

"Master Winterbourne." His voice was dry and pleasant, paper turning. "And the surveyor. Isolde Vane. Of the Vane surveys." Something flickered across the worn-smooth face—interest, or the ghost where interest used to live. "How remarkable. I had wondered when you would enter the accounts."

"You know me," Isolde said.

"I know of you. Four generations of Vanes, each one drawing the kingdom's existence onto paper, each one adding a second copy of the world to the world's own records." He folded his hands. "Do you know what that makes you, in the strict sense of the term?"

"Collateral," Isolde said, before Ansel could speak. "Yes. That's why I'm here. I'd like to discuss terms."

"Isolde." Ansel's voice was low and shaking. "Don't."

"She's already discussing," Corvane observed mildly. "Which puts her ahead of most debtors, who prefer to discuss weather."

Isolde stepped forward, putting herself squarely between the Warden and Ansel, and made her voice do what it did in courtrooms and audit chambers: level, clear, unhurried. "Magister Corvane. You hold a debt of one kingdom, whole, against the crown of Aldermere. Collection is in progress. I propose a restructuring. In exchange for suspending further collection—suspending it, not forgiving it—I offer myself as security. My knowledge comprises a complete positional record of the kingdom: every boundary, every fixture, every star-charted point from four generations of continuous survey. Destroy the kingdom and you destroy the original—but I remain, and I remember. A debt secured against a living copy of the thing itself. Even your masters would call that sound practice."

For a moment the worn face did something extraordinary: it almost smiled. Not cruelty. Recognition. The smile of a clerk encountering, at long last, a properly drafted document.

"It is sound practice," Corvane said. "It is, in fact, elegant. You have spent some hours preparing this."

"Some weeks."

"And you believe your value exceeds the remaining balance."

"I believe my value is *unusual*, which in an illiquid market is worth more than mere size. Anyone can offer gold. Almost no one can offer the kingdom twice."

"Isolde, *stop*—" Ansel grabbed her arm, and she felt him trembling, felt the whole argument he'd been losing to her for an hour come apart into pure refusal. "You don't know what he does with what he takes. I found the old entries. The Warden before him—there were Wardens before him, that's the point, that's the *horror* of it—they gave and gave until nothing was left but the collecting. He was a man once. He told me. He was a man, and now he's a *function*, and you're standing there offering to become a line item—"

"Yes," Isolde said. "I am."

"Because you'd rather be spent than watch me be erased. That's what this is. Don't dress it up as bookkeeping."

And there it was, laid open between them in front of the oldest creditor in the world, and Isolde discovered she did not have the energy left to deny it, and no longer the wish.

"Yes," she said again, softer, only for him. "That's what it is. I would rather be spent, Ansel. I would rather be a line in his ledger than watch the last line about you get written by anyone else. You've spent your whole life invisible, keeping everyone else's accounts, asking nothing—and the world is about to erase you *again*, permanently, and call it justice. I can't allow it. I'm a surveyor. I fix positions. And my position is here, between you and that door, and I will not move."

Corvane watched them with the patience of a monument. When he spoke, it was almost gently.

"The offer is noted. It will be entered. But I regret to inform the surveyor that her arithmetic contains an error—one I suspect she made deliberately." He unfolded his hands. "Collateral stays collection only while it exceeds the risk of loss. Your memory, madam, is a copy of the kingdom *as it was*. The debt is against the kingdom *as it is becoming*. They diverge daily. By the terms of the original instrument, your value depreciates with every village erased—which means your posting buys days, not years. Perhaps not even days." The ink-dark eyes shifted to Ansel. "No. The instrument names an executor. The debt runs through the blood that wrote it. Master Winterbourne, the account is yours to settle, and there remains exactly one trade of equal weight outstanding."

Isolde felt the floor tilt under the words, felt the trap she had walked into close around the wrong person. She had offered herself as a wall, and Corvane had simply measured it, found it insufficient, and stepped past.

"What trade," Ansel said. His voice was very calm now, the calm of a man stepping off a ledge.

"The Thorn," said Corvane. "Your grandmother's door. It opens for a trade of equal weight, made willingly. And Master Winterbourne—I have weighed you, as I weigh all accounts." For the first time, something almost like sorrow moved across the ruined face. "You carry nearly enough."

"Nearly," Isolde whispered.

"Nearly," Corvane agreed. "Which is why her offer interests me after all. Equal weight is a strange measure. It weighs love, not gold. And love freely given adds mass to whatever carries it." He turned toward the doors, cloak settling, and paused with his hand on the frame. "I will await you both at the garden. Take whatever time you require. I have taken centuries; I can take an afternoon."

The doors closed behind him, and the Guildhall was silent, and Isolde turned to Ansel and saw that he was looking at her with an expression she had never seen on him before—resolved, and grieving, and already saying goodbye.

"Don't," she said. "Whatever you're deciding in there. *Don't.*"

But he was already reaching for the ledger, and outside, far to the north, another star went out.
