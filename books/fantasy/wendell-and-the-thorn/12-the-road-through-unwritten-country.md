## 12. The Road Through Unwritten Country

*Ansel*

The road to the capital had been three days' ride when Ansel was a boy. Now it was a rumor.

They discovered this on the second morning, when the milestone they passed at dawn—*Harrowmere, 40 leagues*—appeared again at noon, identical down to the chip in its corner and the birdlime streaking the numeral four. Isolde dismounted, pressed her palm flat against the stone, and said nothing for a long moment. She had been quiet since the wards fell. Not frightened-quiet; surveyor-quiet. The silence of someone recalculating everything she knew.

"It's not that we're walking in circles," she said finally. "I've been marking our bearing by the sun. We're moving west the whole time." She looked up at him, and there was something almost apologetic in her face, as though the error were hers to own. "The road is moving too."

"Roads don't move."

"This one is being unwritten, Ansel. It doesn't have to obey anything anymore."

He wanted to argue. He was, by training and temperament, a creature of argument-by-ledger: show me the entry, show me the correction, show me the hand that made it. But he had watched Harrowmere's eastern fields go gray two nights ago—not burned, not flooded, simply *unspecified*, like a page left blank where a landscape used to be—and argument felt obscene.

So instead he did what the Guild had taught him to do with any discrepancy. He recorded it.

He sat on the milestone with his travel ledger open on his knee and wrote: *Milestone observed twice. Same stone. Same chipping. Same lime. Distance between observations: six hours on foot.* And then, because his grandmother's rewritten pages had taught him that the Guild's books could lie but his own need not, he added: *Therefore the road is lying, or the country is, and I no longer know which would be worse.*

Isolde read over his shoulder. He felt her breath move the hair at his ear and pretended not to notice, which was itself becoming a full-time occupation.

"You write the way you talk," she said. "Like you're afraid of being overheard."

"I'm an accountant."

"You're a terrible accountant. You keep writing things that are true."

He closed the book before she could see what that did to his face.

They walked on. Behind them, when Ansel glanced back—which he tried not to do, and did constantly—the road they had already traveled was fading at its edges, hedgerows going soft as charcoal rubbed with a thumb. Ahead, the road to the capital held, because the capital still existed, because the debt had not yet reached that far inward. They were traveling down the narrowing neck of a country, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it, and somehow that shared unsaying was the closest thing to conversation they'd managed since the stars went out.

* * *

*Isolde*

Isolde had mapped the kingdom twice in her life: once as an apprentice, copying the great charts of the Survey under a tutor who smelled of ink and disappointment, and once five years later, correcting those same charts from horseback, because the tutor's generation had drawn rivers where rivers had never been and omitted an entire valley out of what she could only assume was spite. She knew the shape of Varethane the way other people knew their mother's face.

Which was why the third map—the one she was making now, in a battered notebook, in pencil, in the margins of her own sanity—hurt her to look at.

She drew it each evening by whatever light remained. Each evening the coastline of it had changed. Not the sea's coastline; the *country's*. The borders of Varethane were contracting on paper, pulling inward like a wound closing, except nothing was healing. Villages she had personally triangulated were gone from the space they'd occupied, and worse—they were gone from the *idea* of the space. When she tried to recall the name of the ferry town at Aldenford Crossing, her memory produced a smooth, featureless absence, the way a tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth.

But the people were still there.

That was the thing she couldn't make anyone understand, because there was no one left to tell. The towns went dark, the names dissolved, the roads looped—but the people remained, standing in the gaps like furniture in a house whose deed has burned. On the fourth day they found a woman selling apples from a cart in the middle of a field that appeared on none of Isolde's maps, in a place that had probably never been a market square until the erasure needed somewhere to put her.

"How much?" Isolde asked, because the alternative was asking *who are you*, and she was afraid of the answer.

"Three coppers," the woman said. "Same as ever."

"Ever since when?"

The woman smiled at her with real warmth and total emptiness. "Since always, dear. Since the square was built." She gestured behind her, and Isolde looked, and there was no square. There was wheat going wild and a horizon with a soft gray seam across it where something had been subtracted. But the woman's hands were sure around the apples, red and cold-shiny, and her cart had a wheel that squeaked in exactly the way carts have squeaked for ten thousand years.

Ansel bought six apples with a coin the woman examined for a long moment, turning it over, before nodding as if remembering that money was a thing she accepted. As they walked away, Isolde heard her call out to no one, cheerfully: "Mind the gap in the road! It bites!"

There was no gap in the road. Then there was. Isolde stepped over it without looking down.

That night, camped in the lee of a hill that her notebook insisted was a valley, she caught Ansel watching her draw. He had a way of watching that was almost apologetic, as if attention were a debt he hated to incur.

"The borders are pulling in," she said, because he was going to ask anyway. "About a league a day now. Faster than the schedule in your ledger predicted."

"You've read my ledger?"

"You leave it open. You're hoping someone will audit you." She tapped the pencil against her teeth. "Your grandmother, maybe."

Something crossed his face—grief wearing the mask of irritation. "She's dead eleven years."

"And she rewrote the master ledger in a hand identical to yours, hid a door in your family garden, and died without telling anyone. Forgive me if I think death slowed her down less than it should have."

He laughed. It startled them both. It was a small laugh, rusty, the laugh of a man who had not used the mechanism in years, and Isolde found herself absurdly proud of it, as if she'd coaxed a fire out of wet wood.

"There," she said. "Record that. *Subject laughed. Cause unknown. Recommend further observation.*"

"I don't sound like that."

"You sound exactly like that. I've read your marginalia." She turned back to her map before he could see her smiling, and drew the border one league smaller, and hated every line of it.

* * *

*Ansel*

On the sixth day they met the ferryman who had forgotten his river.

He stood beside a boat on dry land—a good boat, tarred and rope-handled, resting on grass that had never been a bank—and he was poling it anyway, gently, rhythmically, pushing the pole through soil and lifting it, pushing and lifting, with the patient expression of a man doing honest work. When Ansel greeted him, the ferryman tipped his hat.

"Crossing?" he asked.

"There's no water," Ansel said.

"Not today," the ferryman agreed pleasantly. "Some days there is. You wait long enough, the river remembers itself. That's how rivers work." He resumed poling the dirt.

Isolde pulled Ansel aside. Her fingers were cold when they closed on his sleeve, and he noticed—he noticed everything about her touch lately, catalogued it with a precision he found humiliating—that she only grabbed him when she was frightened and didn't want the world to know.

"He's not mad," she whispered. "That's what I can't stop thinking about. He's *accurate*. The river was there. The erasure took the water and left the man, and his mind is just... lagging behind the facts. Like a ledger that hasn't been balanced."

"Or like a man balancing a ledger against a world that's stopped cooperating," Ansel said. "I know the type."

"Do you?"

He looked at her. She was watching him with that surveyor's directness that made him feel charted, triangulated, fixed in position—and made him, against all sense, want to stay fixed.

"My whole life," he said, "I thought the ledgers kept me honest. Rules, entries, balances. A place for everything." He turned the apple in his hands—one of the apples from the woman in the nonexistent square; they'd been eating them for days, and they never seemed to run fewer, which neither of them mentioned. "Out here I keep thinking: what if it was the other way? What if I kept the ledgers honest? What if it was always me, holding the line, and I mistook the cage for the keel?"

"And now?"

"Now the cage is open and the country is dissolving, and I'm walking toward the capital with the most extraordinary woman I've ever audited, eating apples that may not exist, and I have never—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I have never felt more awake."

The ferryman called out behind them: "River'll be back by morning! Tell your friends!"

There was no morning river. There was never a morning river again. But Ansel found, walking on, that he believed the man completely, and that this belief—this willingness to stand in a dry field and pole a boat through dirt because the river might remember—was the only sane response to the country they were crossing.

* * *

*Isolde*

The forgotten people lived in the gaps, and the gaps had their own weather.

Isolde began to map them on the eighth day, because mapping was the only prayer she knew. There was the village of Bellharbor, or what answered to the name: forty souls living in houses arranged around a well that had never been dug, tending gardens that grew in soil the color of unprimed canvas. They remembered their own names perfectly. They remembered each other. What they had lost was everything outside the circle of their arms—the king's name came out as a warm blur, the war of eighty years ago as a rumor of weather, the very concept of *Varethane* as a word that slid off the mind like water off oil.

"They're not sad," she told Ansel that night, bewildered. "I expected grief. It's not grief. It's—it's like they've been excused. From history. From consequence. They live inside the erasure like fish in clear water."

"Are they happy?"

"That's the worst part. Some of them seem happier than anyone in the capital ever was." She hugged her knees, staring into the small fire. "A woman gave me bread today and told me her granddaughter was born last spring, and the girl has no father listed anywhere, no lineage, no entry in any parish roll, because the parish rolled away. And the woman said, *isn't it wonderful, she gets to be whoever she decides.*"

Ansel was silent a long time. Firelight moved on his face, and Isolde studied the geography of it—the sharpness at the cheekbone, the mouth that lived in a permanent state of almost-speaking—and committed each feature to memory with the discipline she'd once spent on coastlines. If the country was going to forget itself, someone had to keep the charts.

"What happens to them," he said at last, "when the payment reaches the center? When the whole kingdom goes?"

"Maybe nothing. Maybe they're already outside the account." She looked at him. "Maybe being forgotten is the only shelter left."

"Then we should all be so lucky."

"No," Isolde said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she intended. "No, I don't accept that. Being forgotten isn't shelter, Ansel, it's just a smaller cell. That woman's granddaughter gets to be whoever she decides—but decided *how*? With what? You can't choose a self out of nothing. Someone has to remember enough of you to argue with." She poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "My whole profession is built on the idea that things should be *written down*. That a river unmapped is a river that can be lied about. That a person unrecorded can be disappeared without anyone even noticing the disappearance." She stopped. Laughed, short and bitter. "Listen to me. Lecturing a Ledgerkeeper on the sanctity of records."

"You're not lecturing a Ledgerkeeper." His voice was low. "You're lecturing the last apprentice of a guild that sold seven years of a woman's laughter for one memory, and helped a crown hide a debt for two centuries, and taught me that invisibility was a virtue." He looked up, and the firelight did something complicated in his eyes. "Keep lecturing. I'm taking notes."

And because the country was fraying and the night was cold and tomorrow the road might loop them back to the same milestone forever, Isolde reached across the fire and took his hand, and he let her, and neither of them entered it in any book.

* * *

*Ansel*

The road broke on the tenth day.

Not ended—broke, the way a sentence breaks, mid-clause, into something else. One moment they were walking the King's Road through country Ansel half-remembered from a childhood journey; the next, the paving stones ran out into a meadow that had no business being there, and beyond the meadow stood a city wall that was not the capital's wall. It was older. It was built of black stone. And above its gate, in letters that hurt to read directly, was carved a single word:

*UNPAID.*

"We shouldn't be here," Isolde said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used for readings off instruments that were telling her something catastrophic. "This isn't on any chart. This isn't on *anyone's* chart. Ansel, cities don't just—"

"It's not a city." He was reading the gate the way he read ledgers, top to bottom, looking for the hand behind the words. "It's a balance. Look—the walls aren't walls, they're margins. Everything past that gate is the part of the account nobody's settled." His mouth had gone dry. "Corvane's territory. Or Corvane's *nature*. The part of the Warden that used to be a man, maybe, walking around out here where the erasure hasn't finished tidying up."

From inside the gate came a sound like a quill scratching parchment, vast and slow, as if the city itself were writing.

And then, from the road behind them—*their* road, the King's Road, the sane road—hoofbeats. A rider in the livery of the crown, mud to the shoulders, horse lathered near to death. He hauled the animal to a stop and stared at them with the wild eyes of a man who had ridden through the end of the world to deliver one sentence.

"Thank the stars—there are stars again, did you see, they came back for an hour last night—an hour, and then they started going out from the *center* this time—" He fumbled a sealed letter from his coat, wax stamped with the royal arms. "For whoever holds guild authority. Any authority. Please. The king is dying, and the court says there's no such thing as the Ledgerkeepers anymore, and the Warden is already *inside the palace*—"

Ansel took the letter. The seal crumbled at his touch, old before its time, and inside, in a shaking hand he recognized from the crown's hidden accounts, was a single line:

*Come at once. Bring the original contract. We wish to negotiate terms before the last page turns.*

Isolde read it over his arm. Behind them, the black city scratched its endless sentence. Ahead, the road to the capital lay open and ordinary and doomed.

"Well," Isolde said quietly, lacing her fingers through his. "Shall we go meet a king?"

Ansel folded the letter into his ledger, between the milestone entry and the record of a laugh he intended to cause again, and turned toward the capital—and the last of the light.
