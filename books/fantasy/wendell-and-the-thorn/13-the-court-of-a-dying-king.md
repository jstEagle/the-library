## 13. The Court of a Dying King

The capital still believed in itself. That was the first thing Ansel noticed, and the thing that frightened him most.

They came through the eastern gate at dusk, past wards that flickered like guttering candles and guards who checked their papers with hands that shook only slightly. Inside the walls, the lamps were lit. The markets were open. A fiddler played on the corner of Gildersleeve Street, and a woman argued cheerfully about the price of eels, and children chased a dog through the fountain square with the particular shrieking joy of children who had never once considered that the world might end.

It was obscene. It was beautiful. Ansel could not stop looking.

"They don't know," Isolde said quietly beside him. She had her surveyor's satchel slung across her chest, and she kept touching it the way other people touched amulets. "Or they've decided not to."

"Which is worse?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions of measurement. "Deciding," she said finally. "Not knowing can be fixed."

The palace rose ahead of them, white stone gone honey-colored in the last light, its spires needling a sky that—Ansel checked, the way he had checked every night since the stars went out—held nothing where nothing should be. No constellations. No wandering planets. Just a smooth black ceiling, like the lid of a ledger closed over the world.

He had sent word ahead from Thornhollow, three days back: *Ansel Winterbourne, acting executor of the Ledgerkeepers' Guild, bearing matters of crown debt.* He had used the guild's formal seal, the one Corvane's arrival had left him by default when the senior masters scattered like startled birds. The reply had come within hours, which told him everything. Kings did not answer letters quickly unless they were afraid.

* * *

They were admitted through a side entrance, which was itself an answer. Not the great hall with its vaulted ceiling and its throne of antlered oak. A receiving room, long and low, hung with tapestries of hunts that had happened two hundred years ago, when the kingdom's borders were things drawn in ink rather than defended in blood.

The king was young.

Ansel had known this—he had read the genealogies, he had entered the succession in the guild's records himself two winters past, a neat line of ink: *Aldric IV, crowned at nineteen upon the death of his grandmother*—but knowing a fact and standing in front of it were different trades entirely. King Aldric looked twenty-two. He looked like a boy who had been handed a kingdom the way one is handed a hot coal, and had spent three years learning to hold it without gripping.

He dismissed his chamberlain with a flick of two fingers and waited until the door closed before he spoke.

"You're the apprentice." His voice was careful. Rehearsed. "Winterbourne. The last one."

"The last one who stayed, Your Majesty."

"And you're telling me the kingdom is being repossessed."

"I'm telling you it already has begun. The borders are going first. Then the outlying provinces. The erasure follows a schedule, Your Majesty—a payment schedule, set down in a contract your ancestors signed. I've read it. I have copies." He patted the satchel at his hip, where the pages lay wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. "I thought you would want to see them."

Something moved behind the young king's eyes. Ansel had spent his life reading faces across counting tables—the flinch of a client about to haggle over something irreplaceable, the greed, the grief—and what he saw now was neither surprise nor denial.

It was recognition.

"You knew," Ansel said slowly.

The king did not answer. He walked to the window instead, where the lightless sky pressed against the glass like a hand, and stood there for a long moment with his shoulders very straight, the way boys stand when they are pretending to be their fathers.

"There is a room beneath this one," Aldric said at last. "You should see it before we speak further. It seems dishonest to do otherwise."

* * *

The room beneath was reached by a stair so narrow they went down single file, the king first, holding a lamp whose flame bent toward the darkness below as if eager to arrive. The air grew cold and mineral. And then the stair opened out, and Ansel stopped on the last step because his legs simply declined to carry him further.

Shelves. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, receding into shadow deeper than the lamplight could follow. And on the shelves, ledgers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Bound in calfskin and vellum and things he did not want to name, their spines stamped with the royal seal, dating back—

He stepped forward and pulled the nearest one down, and his hands were steady, because his hands always were; it was the rest of him that had come apart somewhere around the third shelf.

*Year 214 of the Concord. Payment deferred by arrangement. Consideration: the lifespans of the household of Fenwick Mill, forty-one souls, transferred to the general account.*

*Year 231. Payment deferred. Consideration: the memory of the flood, held in common by the villages of the Aldwater, struck from the record.*

*Year 268. Payment deferred. Consideration: the firstborn daughters of the line of Marsh, seven generations, unrecorded.*

Page after page after page. Century after century. Every deferral itemized with a bookkeeper's loving precision, every horror given its own tidy column, and at the bottom of each entry, in different hands but always the same confident flourish, a signature:

*Aldric. Aldric. Aldric. Aldric.*

The same name, over and over, because kings named their sons for themselves, and the cowardice was inherited along with the crown.

"They knew," Ansel said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were speaking through him. "Every generation knew. They sat up there in the light with their fiddlers and their eel-markets and they paid the debt in other people's lives."

"Yes," the king said.

"For four hundred years."

"Yes."

"And when the payments got too large to hide—" Ansel turned a page, and another, and found the entries growing shorter, the considerations smaller and more desperate, the handwriting beginning to shake somewhere around Year 380 — "they started paying in things no one would miss until they were gone. Laughter. Names. Stars." He looked up. "Your Majesty, the stars didn't go out. They were *collected*. Someone in this room's history signed them away, and the bill has finally come due in full."

The young king sat down on the step beside the shelf. He did not look like a king anymore. He looked like a boy in a cellar full of his family's sins, holding a lamp.

"My grandmother knew," he said. "She told me the night before she died. She said, *there is a debt, and there is a room, and someday a man will come from the Guildhall, and when he does, tell him the truth, because I never had the courage to write it down myself.*" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old paper. "That was my inheritance, Master Winterbourne. Not the crown. The confession. Handed down like a recipe, generation to generation: how to know, and how to do nothing."

"Why?" Ansel heard his own voice crack with something he rarely permitted himself. "Why keep paying? Why not come to the guild, come clean, renegotiate—"

"Because payment in full meant admitting the crown could not exist on its own terms." The king's eyes were wet and furious. "Because every king who sat on that throne told himself what mine told herself: *not my reign. Let the next one be brave.* And the next one thought the same, and the next, and meanwhile the interest compounded and the payments came due and it was so much easier to sign away a village than a dynasty." He looked up at Ansel, and there was no excuse left in his face, only the naked arithmetic underneath. "Comfort, Master Winterbourne. That was the consideration. Four hundred years of comfort, purchased at retail, in the coin of people who never knew they were spending it."

Ansel closed the ledger in his hands. Very carefully, the way he closed every book, because books deserved care even when they made you sick.

He thought of the bottled echoes in the Guildhall, catalogued in his neat hand. He thought of the woman who had traded seven years of laughter for one perfect memory, and how he had watched the rules work and called it order. He thought of Isolde's maps, the kingdom shrinking on paper while they stood in it, and the refugees from nowhere arriving with belongings from places that no longer existed, and the Warden of Accounts saying *the debt must be collected* in a voice like closing a drawer.

All of it traced back here. To this room. To these shelves. To a long line of men and women who had looked at the same arithmetic he was looking at now and chosen, each time, to let someone else pay.

Fury rose in him—not the hot kind, but the cold kind, the kind that keeps books. It settled into him like a new column in a ledger, and he understood that it would never again fully leave.

"Here is what happens now," Ansel said. "I take the original contract. All of it. The deferrals, the amendments, the whole rotten archive. Corvane will demand a reckoning, and when he does, the crown will stand before him and confess—in writing, in your own hand, under your own seal—that the debt was knowingly concealed and knowingly deferred. You will not hide behind your ancestors. You will not hide behind me."

"And if I refuse?"

Ansel looked at the boy-king sitting among the bones of four centuries of quiet evil, and felt something almost like pity, and refused to feel more than that.

"Then I will enter your refusal in the guild's final record," he said, "and the Warden will collect from the source. From the throne. From you. And I promise you, Your Majesty, having read what he collects—I would rather be erased than collected."

The king held his gaze for a long moment. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the fiddler on Gildersleeve Street had changed tunes, and the sound drifted down the stair like a message from a country that still existed.

"Where will you go?" Aldric asked quietly.

"To the garden," Ansel said. "To the Thorn. There may be a way to pay this honestly—one trade, made willingly, of equal weight. But I'll need to know exactly what the contract weighs." He lifted the oldest ledger, the one whose first page bore a signature in faded brown ink, four hundred years old: *Aldric I, who borrowed.* "And I'll need to move fast. Because according to the payment schedule, Your Majesty—"

He opened the ledger to the final page, where the schedule ran out, and showed the king the last line, written in a hand none of the dead kings had dared to use:

*Final collection: the capital, and all contained therein. Date of settlement: three days hence.*

Above them, faintly, the fiddler stopped playing mid-phrase.
