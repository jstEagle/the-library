## 15. What Corvane Wants

The Warden of Accounts did not sleep.

This was not a boast, nor a complaint. It was simply an entry in the long inventory of what Magister Corvane was: a thing that did not sleep, did not hunger in any way food could answer, did not dream except in the technical sense that he occasionally replayed old entries behind his eyes when the halls grew quiet enough. He had done so for three hundred and eleven years, and he had stopped counting the nights somewhere in the second century, because counting was for creatures who feared the total.

He sat now in the Guildhall's upper scriptorium, where the moon came through windows older than most kingdoms, and he reviewed the collection schedule by candlelight he did not need.

The candle was habit. Habit was all he had left of himself, and he hoarded it the way misers hoarded coin — small rituals performed with perfect precision because they were the last things he did *without being required to*. He lit the candle at dusk. He trimmed its wick at midnight. He watched it gutter at dawn. No ledger demanded this of him. That was precisely why it mattered, though he could no longer have explained *why* if asked, in the way a man with no tongue can no longer explain the taste of honey.

Below him, the kingdom was being unmade on schedule.

He could feel it the way a clerk feels ink drying — a faint tightening across the wards, a softening at the edges of things. The northern marches were gone now. Three days ago, the salt coast. Tonight, per the schedule, the hill country around Vell's Crossing would quietly stop having ever been, and some four thousand souls would become a rounding error in a contract signed before their grandparents' grandparents drew breath.

Corvane felt nothing about this.

That was the entry he made each night, in the private ledger he kept locked behind his sternum: *Felt nothing. Confirmed.* And each night the entry took slightly longer to write, because the nothing had begun to develop texture, the way a silence develops texture when you stand in it long enough. It was not grief. Grief required a self to grieve, and he had traded his away in installments — a memory here, a preference there, his mother's face in year forty, his own name in year sixty-one, the color blue in year ninety, which had seemed a small price at the time and which he still, sometimes, reached for like a man reaching for a stair that isn't there.

The candle flickered.

Someone was coming up the stairs. He knew the tread already — light, deliberate, the walk of a man who had spent his whole life trying not to be heard and had recently, horribly, failed. Ansel Winterbourne. Last apprentice. Executor of the debt, whether the boy understood yet what that word cost or not.

Corvane did not turn. He let the footsteps come.

* *

Ansel had rehearsed the confrontation eleven times on the way up, and every version collapsed the moment he saw the Warden's back.

He had expected something monstrous. The stories said Corvane had been terrible since before the guild had walls — a figure of black lacquer and brass fittings, moving through the halls like a debt given legs. What Ansel found instead was a man sitting very straight at a clerk's desk, hands folded, gray-robed, ordinary in every line except the ones that mattered. His skin had the smoothness of paper that has been handled too long. His eyes, when he finally turned them, were the exact gray of a ledger page with nothing written on it yet.

"You climb stairs like a man who expects to be stopped," Corvane said. His voice was quiet and perfectly level, the voice of someone reading terms aloud. "No one will stop you. I have dismissed the masters. Most fled south. Two tried to bargain." A pause, precise as a semicolon. "Their bargains are entered. You may read them, if it helps."

"It doesn't."

"No," Corvane agreed. "It rarely does."

Ansel stayed by the door. Every instinct he owned told him to keep stone between himself and this creature, but he had come up those stairs with a question burning a hole through him, and questions, he had learned in the last week, were debts of their own — they compounded until paid.

"The villages," he said. "Vell's Crossing. The salt coast. The people who came to our gates carrying furniture from houses that don't exist anymore. Did you do that?"

"I executed a schedule." Corvane turned back to the desk. On it lay the master ledger, open to the page Ansel had found — his grandmother's hand, the crown's ancient signature, the terrible arithmetic of *one kingdom, whole*. "The doing was done centuries ago, by men who wanted a kingdom and could not afford one. I am only the collection. Blaming me for the erasure is like blaming the scale for your weight."

"Then you're just a tool."

Something moved behind the Warden's face. It was very small and very fast, and Ansel might have missed it if he hadn't spent his life reading micro-expressions over ledger lines — the flinch of a client asked to name a price they'd been avoiding. But he saw it. For half a heartbeat, the gray eyes looked *wounded*.

"A tool," Corvane repeated. "Yes. That is one entry for it." He rose, unhurried, and crossed to the window, and the moonlight did strange things to him — it went through him slightly wrong, as if he were a drawing of a man rendered on translucent vellum. "You want to understand me, Winterbourne. Good. Understanding is the first stage of every negotiation, and we are approaching yours. Sit."

"I'll stand."

"Sit anyway. It is a long story, and standing men leave early." Corvane waited, and Ansel, hating himself a little, pulled out the chair opposite the desk and did not sit in it so much as perch on its edge, ready to run.

Corvane nodded once, as if a term had been accepted, and began.

* *

"I was born in a village called Harrowmere," he said. "You will not find it. It is not on any map, and after tonight it will not be anywhere at all. It stood where the salt coast used to be. I mention it first because it is the last thing I remember wanting — I wanted to leave it, specifically, and I did, and everything after that was payment."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "How long ago?"

"Three hundred and eleven years, as I count. Longer, as the ledgers count; time is one of the things they weigh differently." Corvane's hands settled on the back of the chair across from Ansel, and Ansel noticed they were beautiful hands — a scribe's hands, fine-boned, unscarred. Too unscarred. Hands that had never been allowed to touch anything that could mark them. "I was a clerk. Like you. Talented, poor, ambitious in the small ways clerks are ambitious — a better desk, a warmer room, my name in the colophon of a finished audit. There was famine the winter I turned nineteen. My mother was dying of it. And a trader came through Harrowmere's ruin offering trades in impossible things, and I sat down at his table because I had nothing else left to sit down at."

"The guild," Ansel said slowly. "The Ledgerkeepers' Guild did this to you?"

"The guild *is* this," Corvane said. "Or it was. Or it is what remains when this process finishes. You have read the founding charter. Have you read the annexes? The schedules of consideration?" He watched Ansel shake his head, and something almost gentle entered the flat gray voice. "No. Of course not. They keep those in the sealed vault, and the sealed vault is kept by whoever has survived longest without asking questions. You survived longer than anyone in a century, Winterbourne. Do you know why?"

"Because I was invisible."

"Because you were *cheap*." The word landed like a stamp on wax. "Every guildsman pays, continuously, in small increments — attention, warmth, years, the capacity to be surprised. The masters pay more because they hold more. You paid least because you held least, and the guild valued that economy, and so it protected you the way a miser protects his smallest coin. Your invisibility was not humility, boy. It was bookkeeping."

Ansel gripped the chair's edge until the wood bit his palms. "You're lying."

"I never lie. Lying requires a self with something to protect." Corvane came around the desk, slowly, and Ansel forced himself not to retreat. Up close, the Warden smelled of cold ash and old paper. "Here is what I traded, in order, and I remember the order because the order is the only thing they could not take. First, my fear of heights — trivial, a taste, a demonstration. Then my fear of death, which I thought was a bargain and was actually a down payment. Then my grief for my mother, so that her dying stopped hurting, and I learned that a pain removed leaves a hole shaped exactly like itself. Then my ambition, which I had come to hate. Then my loves, one by one, each priced fairly, each sold willingly, because the trader was scrupulous and I was desperate and neither of us ever once broke a rule."

His voice did not change register. That was the horror of it. He recited the destruction of a human soul in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest.

"Year forty, my mother's face. Year fifty-three, music — I had played the fiddle badly and loved it completely. Year sixty-one, my name; after that I was designated by function, and function is a very comfortable thing to be, Winterbourne, I recommend it to no one. Year ninety, the color blue. Year one hundred and twelve, hope, which I sold at a premium because by then I had learned what it cost to carry. And in year two hundred and four, when there was almost nothing left of me but the debts I collected, the guild offered me tenure."

"Tenure," Ansel whispered.

"Eternity of function. Continuity of office. The Warden of Accounts does not die, does not retire, does not feel. It was presented as an honor." Corvane looked out the dark window, toward the north, where Vell's Crossing was even now becoming a rumor. "I accepted it gladly. That is the entry that shames me most, and I no longer have the equipment to feel shame, so I merely note it. Note it nightly. In the ledger behind my sternum, which is the last property I own."

The candle between them had burned down to a stub. Neither of them had touched it.

"Why are you telling me this?" Ansel asked.

"Because you are going to stand at the Thorn within the fortnight, and the Thorn will ask you for equal weight, and you will need to know what full payment looks like." Corvane turned, and for the first time his composure cracked — not into menace, which Ansel had braced for, but into something far worse. Into *pity*. "Look at me, executor. Really look. I am what the guild makes when a man pays everything. I am the end of the road you are walking. I execute the collection flawlessly, forever, and I feel nothing, and the nothing gets heavier every year, and I cannot even tell you whether that is cruelty or mercy because I sold the faculty that would have known."

* *

Later — much later, when Ansel had gone back down the stairs with his question answered and a worse one planted in its place — Corvane remained at the window and permitted himself the nightly audit.

*Felt something,* he wrote, behind his sternum, in the private hand that no longer quite belonged to him. *During interview with executor. Classification uncertain. Proximate to pity. Proximate to envy. Possibly both: I pitied him his future and envied him his present, which is the precise geometry of damnation.*

He examined the entry for a long time.

Three hundred years of perfect emptiness, and now this — texture in the nothing, growing daily since the boy had walked into the Guildhall with star-charts and a surveyor's sharp eyes and a grandmother's handwriting waiting in the master ledger. Corvane was old enough, and honest enough in his accounting, to suspect the truth: that he was not feeling *in spite of* the endgame approaching, but *because of* it. Contracts, at their conclusion, called in everything. Even the parts of the collector that had been collateral all along.

Perhaps that was why the guild had made him Warden. Not as honor. As escrow.

He trimmed the candle wick, though the candle was nearly gone, because the ritual was his and no ledger could take it, and he thought about Harrowmere, or rather about the hole where Harrowmere had been, which was the same shape as the hole where his mother's face had been, which was the same shape as the hole where he had been.

Tomorrow the schedule reached the river country. After that, the heartlands. After that, the capital, and the young king who had known and chosen comfort, and whose cowardice Corvane would collect with the same flawless neutrality he brought to everything.

Unless the executor found the door.

Corvane had read the grandmother's file. All of it, including the pages the masters didn't know existed. Maren Winterbourne had been the finest Keeper of her generation, and she had seen exactly what he had become and built a way out of it — a briar, an unrecorded door, a trade of equal weight measured in love. She had meant to pay the debt herself. She had failed. Her failure was the only reason Corvane still had a schedule to enforce, and some traitorous fragment deep in whatever remained of him had been rooting for her grandson ever since the boy first climbed these stairs.

He despised that fragment. He preserved it carefully. It was, he suspected, the last piece of Harrowmere he had left.

At dawn the candle guttered out, right on time, and Corvane sat in the gray light and did not sleep, and waited to see whether the boy would find the garden.
