## 21. A Name Given Freely

*Isolde*

The man in the garden did not know his own hands.

She watched him turn them over in the morning light, palms up, as though they belonged to a stranger who had wandered into his body and left no forwarding address. There was ink in the creases of his right thumb that would not wash out—the stain of thirty years of ledger work—and he studied it the way she had once studied an unfamiliar constellation, patient, methodical, waiting for it to resolve into meaning.

"It doesn't come off," he said.

"No," Isolde said. "It wouldn't."

He looked at her then, and her chest did the thing it had been doing since the stars came back—that terrible lurch, that reaching for a face and finding it almost but not quite empty. His eyes were the same. Whatever the Thorn had taken, it had not taken the way he looked at her: like she was a problem worth solving, a chart worth reading to the last decimal.

"You knew me," he said. Not a question. He had learned, over these first days, that she flinched when he asked questions about himself, so now he asked them gently, the way one approaches a wounded animal.

"I knew you," she said. "I'm going to tell you everything. But not all at once. Memory isn't a shipment to be catalogued. It's more like—" she searched for the word, "—like starlight. You can't take it in all at once or you'll be blinded. You take it a point at a time until the pattern emerges."

He nodded slowly. "And you're the surveyor."

"I'm the surveyor," she agreed, and something in him almost smiled.

* * *

*Ansel*—though he did not know that he was Ansel, though the name sat in Isolde's mouth like a coin she refused to spend—

*Ansel* woke on the fourth morning to find a small book on the blanket beside him.

It was not a ledger. He knew ledgers now; Isolde had shown him the Guildhall's surviving volumes, the great chained masters with their columns of impossible trades, and something in his blood had recognized them the way a compass recognizes north—aching, involuntary. This book was different. It was cheap paper, badly bound, the kind sold in market stalls for a copper. On the cover, in Isolde's precise hand: *For You. Volume One.*

He opened it.

*Entry the First,* it read. *You were born on the coldest night of a cold year, in the Guildhall itself, because your mother came there fleeing a debt your father owed and died before she could pay it. The guild took you in rather than take the debt. Your grandmother named you herself.*

Below it, in smaller script: *I don't know if this is how memory works. I don't know if telling is the same as having. But you deserve the record, even if the record is only me.*

He read it four times. Then he went to find her.

She was on the garden wall where the black briar had grown, cataloguing its new growth—the Thorn had gone dormant after the trade, its thorns softening to something like bark, like sleep—and she heard his footsteps and turned, and saw him holding the book against his chest the way a man holds a shield.

"Is it true?" he asked. "The mother. The debt."

"Every word."

"Then write the next one tonight." He held the book out to her. "And the next. And however many it takes."

Isolde took the book, and found that her eyes had gone hot, and discovered that this too was a kind of astronomy: some things you could only see clearly when you let yourself burn a little.

* * *

*Isolde*

She taught him in entries, because entries were the language he still spoke even when he'd forgotten he spoke anything at all.

*Entry the Third: You are left-handed, but the guild trained it out of you for the sake of uniform ledgers. You write with your right hand and do everything else—eat, throw, hold a cup—with your left. Watch for it. It will betray you constantly.*

He tested it that evening, eating soup with his left hand while writing practice lines with his right, and laughed—actually laughed, startled by his own body's testimony—and the sound went through her like a blade wrapped in silk, because she knew that laugh, she had missed that laugh, and he was hearing it for the first time.

*Entry the Seventh: You hate the smell of cloves because Magister Hobb used to chew them during audits, and audits meant someone was about to lose something they loved.*

*Entry the Eleventh: You once spent an entire winter secretly re-balancing the accounts of widows the guild had overcharged, forging the masters' correction stamps so well that no one ever caught it. You thought no one knew. I know. I audited you. It's why I fell in love with you, though I didn't say so for another four months, because I am a coward about exactly one thing.*

He read that entry standing very still in the doorway of the cottage she'd taken in the village below the Guildhall. "Four months," he said. "What happened at the end of the four months?"

"The stars went out," she said. "And I stopped being a coward, because it seemed suddenly very stupid to die with things unsaid."

"And what did you say?"

She closed the book. "That's not an entry. That's a demonstration." And she kissed him, and he kissed her back with the desperate thoroughness of a man drinking after a long thirst—not remembering her, not yet, but recognizing her, which might have been better, which might have been the truest thing the Thorn had left him.

Afterward, lying in the dark with her head on his chest, he said, "Tell me the part where I decided. Before the Thorn. Tell me what I chose."

"You chose the kingdom," she said. "Over yourself. Over us. You stood in that garden and Corvane told you the arithmetic—you weighed less than a country, barely, only barely, and only because—" Her voice caught. She mastered it. Surveyors did not weep over their instruments. "Only because I loved you. That was the weight. My love was the counterweight that made you heavy enough to pay for everything."

He was quiet a long time. Then: "That seems unfair to you. To spend my whole self and leave you holding the bill."

"It was enormously unfair," she said. "I intend to complain about it for the rest of my life."

"Good," he said, and she felt him smile against her hair. "Complaints should be entered in a proper ledger. Start a volume."

* * *

*Corvane*

The Warden of Accounts came on a Tuesday, because Corvane was the sort of creature who observed formalities even now, especially now, when formality was nearly all that remained of him.

Isolde met him at the gate of the Guildhall with her arms crossed and her spine straight, and behind her, deliberately, the nameless man stood in the doorway of the cottage, visible, unhidden. Let the Warden look at what remained. Let him carry it.

"Lady Vane," Corvane said. His voice was dry paper and old ink. "You have been busy."

"I've been keeping records."

"So I understand." He produced, from nowhere and everywhere, a document sealed in grey wax. "Which is precisely why I've come. What you are doing—this reconstruction, this... oral history—is legally precarious. A man without an entry in any ledger has no standing, no claim, no name in law. But a claim *held* by another party may be registered. Formalized. You could enter him as yours, Lady Vane. Debtor to your creditor, property to your keeping. The guild's old forms allow it. I have brought the paperwork myself."

Behind her, she heard the nameless man go very still.

"And in exchange?" she said.

"In exchange, nothing. A courtesy." Corvane's face did not move, but something behind it did—the faintest tremor, like a reflection in deep water. "You misunderstand me if you think I offer this from cruelty. I was entered once, Lady Vane. Piece by piece, trade by trade, until the ledger held all of me and nothing was left over. I know what it is to exist only as an entry in someone else's book." The grey eyes fixed on hers. "Register your claim. Protect him. It is the only shelter the law affords."

Isolde took the document. She read every line of it, slowly, because Corvane deserved the respect of a careful audit. Then she tore it in half, and in half again, and let the pieces fall into the mud of the courtyard.

"No," she said.

"Lady Vane—"

"You were entered piece by piece," she said, "and look what's left. You stand there offering me the same poison that emptied you, and you call it shelter." She stepped closer, unafraid, because she had watched stars die and reignite and there was very little left in the world that frightened her. "Some things should never be entered in any book, Magister. Not debts. Not people. Not love. The moment you write a thing down as owned, you've already begun spending it. I won't own him. I won't owe him. I will *know* him—which is not the same thing, and which no ledger in existence has a column for."

Corvane regarded her for a long moment. Wind moved through the courtyard and stirred the torn paper.

"You are aware," he said at last, "that without legal registration, if anything happens to you—if you die, if you are erased, if you simply change your mind—he becomes nothing again. No one will remember for you. No institution will hold what you refuse to formalize."

"Then I'll have to stay alive," Isolde said. "And stay certain. And stay." She smiled, and it was not a gentle smile. "You of all creatures should appreciate a long-term obligation freely undertaken, Warden. It's the only kind that never comes due."

Something happened to Corvane's face then—something so small and so human that she would doubt it later. It might have been envy. It might have been grief. It might have been the ghost of a man who had once been loved outside of any book, and remembered it the way an amputee remembers a hand.

"Very well," he said. "Keep your unwritten things." He turned to go, then paused. "For what it is worth—and it is worth little—I have audited ten thousand trades in my tenure. Yours is the first I have ever regretted being unable to collect."

He was gone between one wind and the next.

* * *

*Ansel*

He began writing entries himself in the second month.

They were bad entries—crooked lines, uncertain dates, the handwriting of a man learning his own letters—but Isolde declared them canon anyway, and read them aloud each evening as though reciting scripture.

*Entry the Nineteenth: I do not remember her, but I know the sound her boots make on stairs, and my heart does something it has no reason to do when I hear it.*

*Entry the Twenty-Second: Today I repaired the Guildhall's eastern shutters without being asked, and my hands knew the work. Whoever I was, he was a mender of small things. I would like to be him again.*

*Entry the Thirtieth: I dreamed of a briar door and woke weeping, and I don't know whose tears those were, but I think they were a gift from a dead woman who loved me enough to plant an exit in the floor of the world.*

On the thirtieth night, after she finished reading, Isolde set down the book and looked at him across the candle flame.

"There's a gap," she said. "In my telling. I've been circling it for weeks and you've been too polite to notice, but you're going to be a proper auditor eventually, so I may as well confess. There's one entry I haven't written."

"Which one?"

"Your name." Her hands were steady; her voice was not, quite. "I've called you *you*, and *love*, and once, memorably, *you absolute disaster*, but I haven't given you your name back. Because a name isn't like the rest. The rest is evidence. A name is a verdict. And I keep thinking—it shouldn't come from a record. It shouldn't come from me at all, like something I'm restoring to my possession. It should come from somewhere free."

He reached across the table and took her ink-stained fingers in his ink-stained ones.

"Then ask me the question," he said, "the way you'd ask a stranger. And I'll answer the way strangers do—by choosing."

Isolde looked at him for a long time. Outside, the stars were out, all of them, every one accounted for; she checked nightly, out of habit, out of grief, out of love, and they burned in their appointed places like a ledger finally balanced.

"All right," she said softly. "Stranger. Who are you?"

And he opened his mouth to answer—to choose, freely, for the first time in two lives—and stopped, because a memory had surfaced that was not a memory at all but something older, planted deeper than the Thorn, deeper than the debt: a dead woman's voice, warm with laughter, calling a small boy in from the garden by a name that was never written anywhere, a joke-name, a garden-name, a thing given freely and never once entered in any book.

He smiled.

"Wendell," he said.
