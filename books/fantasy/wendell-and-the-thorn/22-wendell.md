## 22. Wendell

The garden came back first.

I don't mean that it had gone anywhere. The black briar still stood in the corner of the ruined conservatory where it had always stood, its thorns long and patient, its door-shaped hollow now just a tangle of branches with nothing to be a door of. But when I woke on the flagstones beneath it with dew in my hair and no name in my mouth, the garden was the only thing I recognized—and even that recognition felt borrowed, like a coat left behind by someone else.

I lay there for a while and listened to the world.

It was a loud world, or louder than I somehow knew it should have been. Birds. Wind in the broken glass of the conservatory roof, making a sound like distant applause. And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, a hum from the sky itself—the sound, though I had no word for it yet, of stars burning again.

I sat up. My hands were an old man's hands and a young man's hands at once, ink-stained along the right fingers in a way that suggested years of ledger work, and there was a small white scar on my left palm shaped like a thorn's kiss. I looked at that scar for a long time. It felt like the most important thing about me, and I could not have said why.

That was the shape of everything, in those first days. Important things, weightless. A whole life, poured out and refilled with someone new.

* * *

She found me on the third day.

I had wandered as far as the Guildhall by then—not because I remembered it, but because my feet did. The great doors stood open. Inside, dust hung in shafts of morning light, and the ledgers lay where they had fallen when their keepers fled: thousands of volumes, spines cracked, pages fluttering like startled birds. I stood among them feeling something I had no history to attach the feeling to. Grief, maybe. Or homesickness for a house I'd never lived in.

"You're real," she said from the doorway.

She was tall, sun-browned, with ink on her fingers too—surveyor's ink, mapmaker's ink—and she was looking at me the way people look at a lighthouse they'd given up expecting to see again. Her eyes were wet and furious and relieved all at once, which I would learn was simply how her eyes were.

"I'm sorry," I said. "Do I know you?"

"No." She crossed the floor toward me, and her voice steadied itself the way a hand steadies on a rail. "Not anymore. That's rather the point."

"Then I'm sorry twice," I said. "Once for not knowing you, and once for whatever made you look at me like that."

Something in her face broke and reassembled. "You even apologize like him," she said, mostly to herself. Then, clearly, to me: "My name is Isolde Vane. I'm going to tell you who you were. Not today—all of it takes time—but starting now, because I promised myself I wouldn't wait for a convenient moment, and there is never a convenient moment for anything that matters."

"That sounds like a rule," I said.

"It's the only rule I have."

We sat down among the fallen ledgers, and she began.

* * *

She told me in entries. That was her idea, and it was a good one, because I turned out to be a creature of entries whether or not I remembered being one. Each evening she gave me one—a day of my old life, read aloud from her memory, since no book held them anymore. She told me about bottled echoes and a woman who sold seven years of laughter. She told me about star-charts and a discrepancy I shouldn't have seen and an argument we'd had across a table of brass instruments, an argument she described with such relish that I suspected it had been the beginning of something.

She told me about the debt. About the crown that had borrowed its own existence and let the interest fall on farmers' lifespans and children's names. About Magister Corvane, who had once been a man and had been traded away until only the collecting remained. About the night the stars went out, and the borders unspooling like thread from a dropped spool, and villages full of people who woke up belonging nowhere.

"And then," she said, on some evening in the second week, her voice very quiet, "there was a garden, and a door grown from a briar, and a man who understood what equal weight meant, and went through it anyway."

I looked at my scarred palm. "Was he brave?"

"He was terrified," she said. "He was the most frightened person I've ever known. He went anyway. Those aren't the same thing, and I'll thank you not to confuse them."

"I wasn't confusing them," I said. "I was asking because I want to be the kind of person who goes anyway. If I'm going to inherit his hands, I'd like to inherit that too."

Isolde looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the first stars were coming up over the ruined conservatory, and they came up the way water rises in a well—steadily, from somewhere deep, as if the sky had been waiting a long time to be full again.

"You already are," she said. "You've been here three weeks and you've already started repairing the guild's roof. Nobody asked you to. You saw a thing that needed doing and you did it, and then you wrote down what you'd done, in a book you found yourself, in handwriting I would know anywhere." She laughed—a short, wet laugh. "You are exactly the kind of person who goes anyway. You just haven't had anything worth going toward yet."

"I have you," I said, before I could think better of it.

The words surprised us both. But they didn't feel wrong. They felt like an entry that had been waiting years to be recorded, and had finally found its page.

* * *

In the fourth week, she told me about the name.

"Not your name," she said quickly. "Your name is yours to make or leave alone, and I won't push it on you. But there's another one. Your grandmother—" and here she smiled in a way I was coming to know, a smile aimed slightly past me, at someone standing just over my shoulder—"used to call you Wendell. When you were small. When you'd tracked mud into the archive or fallen asleep facedown in a ledger. She'd say, *up you get, Wendell, books are heavier than boys.*"

"Wendell," I said, trying it out.

It fit strangely—not like a key in a lock, but like a seed in soil. Something that would grow into me if I let it. It sounded like mud tracked into archives. It sounded like being scolded by someone who loved me. It sounded, more than anything else I owned, like mine.

"Why that name?" I asked. "Why not give me back the old one? Ansel. You've said it enough times that I almost feel its shape."

"Because the old name was spent," Isolde said. "It went into the Thorn with everything else, and it bought a kingdom, and I will not cheapen it by handing it back out like change from a purse. You paid for the right to choose. So choose."

I thought about it for six days. I walked the Guildhall's corridors, and the garden, and the lanes of a city busy remembering itself—streets repopulating, shop signs reappearing, children asking parents questions the parents couldn't answer about years that had come back to them like a dream on waking. On the sixth day I climbed to the top of the hall, where the master ledger lay open on its lectern, and I read the final entry one more time.

It was written in my hand. Of course it was; Isolde had told me it would be, and still seeing it stopped my breath. The letters marched down the page calm and certain, the script of a man doing the last piece of work of his life with the same care he'd given the first:

*Received in full settlement of the crown's debt: one kingdom, whole. Paid against: one apprentice, entire. Let it be entered that the payment was made gladly, and that the account between this guild and the world is closed.*

*Closed,* I thought. Except that accounts are never really closed. They're only balanced. And balance, I was learning, is not an ending—it's a place to stand while you decide what to build next.

"Wendell," I said aloud, to the empty hall, to the ledger, to the grandmother whose joke-name it had been and whose unfinished act of love had carried us all here. "Up I get."

* * *

We reopened the guild in the spring.

It took most of the winter to make the building ready. We mended the roof together, Isolde and I, and argued magnificently about where the shelves should go, and discovered that arguing was a language we were both fluent in and that fluency, in her case, looked suspiciously like delight. Word spread, the way word does. People came. First the curious, then the desperate, then the ones in between—because most troubles live in between, and those were always the guild's true clients.

On the first morning, I stood at the counter where my predecessors had weighed impossible things, and Isolde stood beside me with a fresh ledger open, and I spoke the terms before anyone could ask them.

"The Ledgerkeepers' Guild is open," I said, to the small crowd in the hall—maybe thirty people, holding their griefs and hopes in bundles and boxes and cupped hands. "But hear the terms, because they are new, and they are not negotiable. Nothing is traded here that isn't given gladly. We will weigh what you bring. We will tell you honestly what it costs and what it buys. And if any part of you is unwilling—if any part of you is selling because you think you have no choice—then we will help you find a choice instead. That is the trade we offer. Take it or leave it."

An old woman near the front raised her hand. "And if we can't pay?"

"Then you owe us nothing," I said. "Some entries are gifts. The guild has forgotten that for two hundred years. We intend to remember."

There was a silence, and then the old woman set a small jar on the counter—an echo, bottled, the label faded. "My husband's voice," she said. "I kept it after he died. I thought I couldn't live without it. But I've listened to it every night for a year and I'm starting to forget what he sounded like when he was *laughing*, and I'd like to trade it—for nothing. For the room to stop listening. Can the guild do that? Can it take a thing off my hands without giving me anything back?"

I looked at Isolde. Isolde looked at me. And we both started laughing, because of course it could. That was the oldest trade there was, older than ledgers, older than debts: setting a burden down, and having someone witness it.

"Gladly," I said, and meant it in every direction the word pointed.

* * *

Corvane came at midsummer.

I heard the doors before I saw him—that particular hush a crowd makes when something old walks in. He crossed the hall the way winter crosses a field, unhurried, inevitable, and stopped at the counter, and regarded me with eyes like settled accounts.

"You chose a name," he said. His voice was dry paper. "Curious. I had expected you to remain a blank."

"I'm told blanks are your department," I said. "Wendell. And you're standing in a guild with new terms, Magister, so if you've come to collect something, you'll need to find someone glad to pay it."

For a moment—one long, strange moment—I thought the Warden of Accounts might smile. Something moved behind his face, deep down, the way a fish moves under ice: the ghost of the man he'd been, surfacing toward warmth and finding the ice too thick.

"I came," he said, "to audit."

"Then audit," I said, and slid the new ledger across the counter. "Every entry since spring. Every trade witnessed, every price disclosed, every gift recorded as a gift. We have nothing to hide. That's rather the innovation."

He read for a long time. The hall held its breath around him. At last he closed the book with a click like a lock turning—or unlocking; with Corvane it was hard to say which.

"The accounts balance," he said.

"They do."

"There is one entry outstanding." He laid something on the counter between us: a single sheet, old, heavy, sealed with a sigil I knew from Isolde's stories—the mark of the original contract, the one that had begun everything. "A codicil. Discovered in the crown's archives during the restoration. It names the executor of the debt"—his gaze rested on me—"and grants the executor one petition, redeemable at any time, against the creditor. Any petition within the creditor's power."

I stared at the paper. Isolde's hand found mine under the counter and gripped it hard.

"You're offering me a wish," I said slowly. "The Warden of Accounts is offering me a wish."

"I am offering you a form," Corvane corrected. "What you write in it is your affair. You have until the turn of the year." He turned to go, then paused, and for just a moment his voice was not winter but something older and wearier underneath it—the voice of whatever was left of the man. "Choose carefully, Wendell. I never had the option. It is... instructive, watching someone who does."

And he was gone, and the hall breathed again, and Isolde and I stood holding hands over a blank form that could remake anything—including, I understood perfectly well, the Warden himself. Including the man he used to be. Including, possibly, a certain apprentice who had once walked through a door of thorns and left his name on the other side.

That night we sat in the garden under the black briar, which had put out its first blossom in living memory—small, white, absurdly ordinary. Isolde leaned her head on my shoulder. Above us the stars burned in their courses, every one accounted for, every one glad of it, if stars can be glad, and I have decided to believe they can.

"What will you ask for?" she murmured.

I watched the briar flower move in the wind, and thought of a grandmother who planted doors, and a guild that had learned to accept gifts, and a name that fit like a seed in soil.

"I don't know yet," I said truthfully. "But I have until the turn of the year. And for the first time in two hundred years—" I smiled, and felt the smile land, and knew it was entirely, gladly mine— "the guild is keeping an entry open on purpose."
