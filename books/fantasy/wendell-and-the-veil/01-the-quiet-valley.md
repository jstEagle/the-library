## 1. The Quiet Valley

The dead did not speak in Hollowmere. That was the whole point of the place, though most of its people had forgotten it, the way you forget the reason for a wall once the war is over.

Mirabel Quintana remembered. She remembered every morning, when she woke in her workshop above the bone-carver's shop and listened — really listened — to the silence that pressed against the valley like a hand laid flat over a mouth. Three hundred years ago, a dying god had spent its last breath on a single spell, and that spell had settled over Hollowmere and a hundred valleys like it: the Veil, a muteness laid across the world. Magic withered inside it. Gifts went dark. And the dead, who everywhere else still murmured and pleaded and sang their grievances into the ears of the gifted, here said nothing at all.

The valley called it protection. The priests called it peace. Mirabel, who was twenty-six years old and had never told a living soul what she could do, called it the only reason she was not burned.

She came down the narrow stair before dawn, as she always did, past the drying racks where cow bones and deer bones hung like pale laundry, and lit the lamp at her workbench. The shop below belonged to old Master Ferren, who had taught her the trade and who believed — as the whole village believed — that Mirabel Quintana was simply the best pair of hands in Hollowmere. Buttons from antler. Knife-handles from femur. Combs, dice, fish-hooks, the little carved saints that farmers nailed above their doors against ill luck. Nothing strange in it. Bone was honest work. Bone was what the valley gave.

What none of them knew was that even here, even under the Veil, the bones were not entirely quiet.

It came to her faintly, the way sound comes through a closed door. Not voices, exactly. More like the memory of voices — an echo of an echo, a pressure behind the eyes when she held certain bones too long. A cow's knuckle might carry a flicker of the field it died in: rain, the smell of crushed grass, a dog barking far off. A grandmother's finger-bone, brought to her once by a family who wanted it made into a ring, had hummed with something almost like a lullaby, and Mirabel had worked through the night with tears running down her face, carving, because if she stopped she would have to think about what she was hearing.

The Veil muffled everything. But muffled was not silent. And Mirabel had learned, over fifteen years of careful practice, to carve so precisely, so attentively, that whatever faint song lived in the bone passed into the finished piece — softened, gentled, made bearable. She told herself it was kindness. She told herself a great many things.

* * *

The child's name was Tam Wren, and he had drowned in the mere nine days ago, and his mother had come to the shop on the tenth day with a small bundle wrapped in linen and a face like wet paper.

"He was only six," Sera Wren said, standing in the doorway as though the threshold were a river she couldn't cross. "They say — they say a bell. For children who go into the water. So they can find their way back to be born again."

"That's the custom," Mirabel said gently. "A memorial bell, carved from one of his own bones. Hung in the chapel yew until spring."

"Master Ferren used to do them." Her voice cracked. "Before his hands went bad. They say yours are better anyway."

"They say a lot of things." Mirabel took the bundle. Inside lay a single small bone — a finger joint, pale as milk, no longer than her thumbnail. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

Sera Wren left. Mirabel sat a long moment with the bone in her palm before she set it down.

Here was the thing about Hollowmere, she thought, lighting her lamps properly now, laying out her finest blades and her goose-fat polish and the little brass calipers Master Ferren had given her at fourteen: the thing about Hollowmere was that everyone in it believed the same story, and the story kept them safe from each other. Magic was gone, the priests said. The Veil saw to it. Anyone who claimed otherwise was either mad or lying, and both kinds ended up on the pyre eventually — not often, not lately, but the memory lived in the valley like a stone in a shoe. There had been burnings within living memory. There was a black scar on the chapel floor that nobody swept.

So Mirabel Quintana, orphaned at eleven, raised by a carver who never asked why she flinched at certain pieces of bone, had grown up fluent in one language above all others: concealment. She carved beautifully and explained nothing. She went to chapel and mouthed the hymns. When a farmer joked about the old days, when witches walked and the dead whispered, she laughed at the right moments, a half-beat late, the way a person does when they're paying attention to something else.

And underneath all of it, alone in her workshop with the lamp turned low, she listened.

* * *

Carving a memorial bell was three days' work done slowly, and Mirabel gave it five.

The first day she only held the bone. This was the part no other carver did, or needed to. She cupped the little finger-joint in both hands and closed her eyes and waited for the echo — that far-off pressure, that door-with-a-voice-behind-it. With ordinary bone there was almost nothing: weather, hunger, the dumb warmth of being alive. But children were different. Children died loud, even under the Veil. Their deaths rang.

Tam Wren's bone rang.

Not words. Never words, here — the Veil ground language to dust before it could reach her. What came through was shape and sensation: cold, first, a terrible spreading cold, and then green water closing overhead like a lid, and then — this was the part that made Mirabel's chest ache — *light*. Sunlight through the surface of the mere, wobbling gold, impossibly beautiful, seen from beneath. The boy had not been afraid at the end. Or rather, he had been afraid, and then he had looked up, and the light had been so lovely that fear had lost the argument.

Mirabel opened her eyes. Her cheeks were wet. She wiped them with her wrist and got to work.

The second day she shaped the blank. A memorial bell began as a cylinder of bone, turned and hollowed with drills fine as needles, and Tam's finger-joint was barely enough material; she would have to laminate it over a sliver of deer antler, invisible seams, a trick of hers that Master Ferren had called showing off and the village called craftsmanship. She worked with the window open to the grey morning, chips curling away from her blade like petals, and outside the valley went about its business — carts on the causeway, geese arguing, the mere lying flat and silver between its reed beds, keeping what it had taken.

On the third day, a neighbor nearly caught her listening.

His name was Doss Marle, a big red-faced man whose wife had ordered buttons, and he arrived while Mirabel had the boy's bone resting against her cheek — a habit she despised in herself and could not break, pressing the work to her skin as if the bone might tell her what shape it wanted to be. He knocked; she started; the bone dropped; she caught it against her breastbone with a motion that must have looked, to anyone watching, like a woman startled into clutching her work.

"Jumpy today, Mirabel?" Doss grinned through the open door. He was harmless — mostly. Harmless men had built the pyre too.

"Deep in the detail work." She smiled her shop smile. "Buttons for your wife are next week, I promised the Wren family first."

"Aye, the drowned lad." He shook his head, and his grin faded into something more careful. "You know what Father Aldous says about bells. Says the old custom's near to superstition. Says bone-work on the dead should stop altogether." He said it lightly, testing the air, the way men do when they want a thing repeated more than they want it true.

"The custom's older than Father Aldous," Mirabel said, "and I'll hang the bell in the yew myself if the family asks me to."

"Mm." Doss collected his wife's buttons after all — Mirabel found them without looking, her hands knowing the drawers better than her head — and paused at the door. "Strange trade you have. All these dead things, and you handling them day in, day out." A laugh, but not quite a whole one. "Wouldn't catch me at it."

"Lucky for you someone will," Mirabel said, and waved him out, and stood in the doorway watching him go down the lane, whistling, glancing back once.

That glance stayed with her through the afternoon. It always did. Fifteen years of those glances, filed away in the part of her mind that kept accounts: who watched her, who asked twice, who laughed a half-beat wrong. She was very good at it now. She sometimes thought she had become nothing else — a woman made entirely of careful hands and a shop smile, with the actual person folded up small somewhere underneath, like a letter never sent.

* * *

On the fifth evening the bell was finished.

It was the finest thing she had ever made, and she knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, without false modesty. Small enough to sit in a cupped palm, white as frost, its walls thin as eggshell, carved overall with a pattern of reeds and running water — and hidden among the reeds, so fine you'd need her eyes to find them, six tiny figures: a mother, a father, a sister, and the boy himself, three times over, standing, wading, rising toward the light. The clapper was a grain of polished antler. When she struck it with her fingernail, it made a sound like a word spoken underwater.

She struck it once, alone, at lamplight. And because she was alone, and because the Veil was thinnest at night — or so she had always privately believed, though she had no proof and could tell no one — she held the bell close and let herself listen one last time.

Cold. Green light. Then something new, something that had not been in the bone before: the feeling of being *hung*. Of swaying gently in the branches of the yew among all the other small bells, the generations of drowned children, and of hearing — distantly, distantly — the valley going on around it. Carts. Geese. Rain on the chapel roof. Life, arriving secondhand, through a dead boy's finger-bone, to the only person in Hollowmere who could feel it.

*Thank you,* said the echo, which was not words, and was.

"You'll be born again in spring," Mirabel whispered to it. "That's what they believe. Maybe it's true. Maybe you'll get the light from above the water this time instead of below."

The bell said nothing. Bones never answered; they only remembered. That was the whole of her gift, the whole of her curse: she was not a speaker with the dead. She was a listener at a door that never opened, in a house where everyone else insisted the house was empty.

She hung the bell in the yew herself, at dusk, on a hook of copper wire, because Sera Wren had asked her to and because the priest was at supper. The chapel yard was empty. The other bells stirred around it in the wind — dozens of them, small and pale, a tree full of whispers — and for a moment Mirabel let herself imagine that they were all speaking at once, that the Veil lifted just slightly in this one place out of respect for the dead, and that if she stood very still she might hear—

Nothing. Wind. The mere, flat and silver, keeping what it took.

She walked home in the blue dusk past the black scar on the chapel floor, past Doss Marle's lit windows, past the shrine where the valley's founders had raised their stone to the god who died to make them safe. *Rest eternal,* the carving read. *Rest silent.* She had read it ten thousand times and it had always comforted her, the way walls comfort.

At her door she paused, because the evening felt wrong — some animal attention pricking along her neck, the sense of a valley holding its breath. She told herself it was the season turning. She told herself a great many things.

But later, banked in her bed above the workshop, she heard hooves on the causeway road. Many horses, moving fast, coming down out of the north pass where no one ever came from — riders in a hurry, riders armed, the drum of them carrying through the still air long before any lantern showed.

Mirabel lay in the dark and counted the hoofbeats, and did not sleep.
