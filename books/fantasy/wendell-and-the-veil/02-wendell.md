## 2. Wendell

The bell was nearly finished when the dog began to scream.

Mirabel's knife paused above the bronze-green curve of bone — river-cattle femur, cured for two years, dense enough to hold a note. She had been shaping the lip of the bell for the drowned child, Tam Voss, whose mother had come to her door three days ago with red eyes and no coin. *Make it so I can hear him once more,* the woman had begged, not knowing what she asked. Not knowing that Mirabel heard him already, faintly, the way one hears rain through a roof: a small wet voice inside the bone saying *cold, cold, mama, the water was so cold.*

She had learned long ago that grief was easier to carve than to speak aloud.

Now the dog — old Bramble from the Hollis farm, half-deaf himself — was throwing his head back and howling in a register she had never heard from him, high and broken, like something tearing.

Mirabel set down her knife. Through the single window of her workshop she could see the lane below, the low stone houses of Hollowmere with their smoke rising straight in the windless evening, everything arranged and ordinary as a table set for supper. Nothing moved. That was what frightened her. In a valley where everyone worked until dark, nothing should be still.

The screaming stopped. The silence after it was worse.

She wiped her hands on her apron, left the bell half-formed on its stand, and went out into the blue dusk. The air smelled of woodsmoke and turned earth and, beneath it, something else — a smell she knew only from her grandmother's stories, from the old women who muttered charms against things that no longer existed: iron. Cold iron, and under the iron, a sweetness like meat beginning to turn.

*The Veil keeps them out,* she told herself. *The Veil keeps everything out.* Three hundred years since the god died — or slept, or was murdered; the stories disagreed — and the spell it spent its last breath weaving had settled over Hollowmere like snowfall, muffling every gift, every curse, every whisper of the uncanny. Magic starved here. Witches' charms curdled in their makers' hands. The dead lay quiet as stones. It was why her family had come, generations back. It was why she had never left.

It was why, when she heard the crash at the gate of her own yard, she did not at first believe it was real.

He came through the fence rather than the gate — a man in the grey-and-silver of the crown army, or what had been the crown army's colors before King Aldric re-cut them, and he came through it the way a sack of grain comes off a cart, all weight and no will. He hit the ground among her winter cabbages and did not get up.

Mirabel stood very still. Every story her grandmother ever told stood up inside her at once. *A soldier means a war. A war means the Veil has failed, or been ignored, which is worse.*

Then she saw the blood. It had soaked through his coat at the shoulder and again at the side, black in the dusk, and it was spreading in the dirt of her garden in a way that made her hands move before her mind agreed to anything.

She knelt beside him. Up close he was younger than his bearing suggested — mid-twenties, maybe, with a jaw that hadn't decided yet whether to be soft, and a face grey as ash under road dust. His eyes found hers and focused with an effort that seemed physical, like a man hauling a rope hand over hand.

"Quintana," he said.

Her own name in a stranger's mouth, spoken with the flat precision of a man reading it off a list. The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the evening.

"Who sent you?"

"Nobody." He coughed, and the cough had a wet sound at the bottom of it that she liked very little. "I'm looking for you. Mirabel Quintana. Bone-carver." He fumbled at his coat with a bloody hand. "They'll ask. In the village. They'll go house to house. You carve bones — they have your name."

"Who will?"

"Witch-hunters." He said it the way other men said *plague*. "Purifiers. Aldric's own. They're behind me. Half a day, maybe less." His hand closed around something inside his coat and held on, protective even now, even half-conscious. "I need — I'm sorry — I need you to hide me."

"I don't hide soldiers. I carve bells and buttons and the occasional saint's finger for pilgrims who can't tell the difference. There's nothing here for you."

"There's everything here for me." His grip caught her wrist. His fingers were freezing. "You're gifted. Don't insult us both by denying it — I've watched you talk to that memorial yard since I've been lying in the treeline watching this house, and nobody hums to gravestones the way you do unless the stones are humming back."

Her mouth went dry. Three hundred years of the Veil, thirty years of careful, silent, meticulous living, and this bleeding stranger had undone it in an afternoon from a ditch.

"You've been watching my house."

"Since Vasse ford." His eyes were losing focus. "I'm Cassian. Cassian Wendell. And I am about to be very much your problem, so I'd suggest deciding quickly whether you're going to drag me inside or let me bleed out in your cabbages, because I can hear horses."

She couldn't hear horses. But she believed him.

* * *

Getting him inside was a study in small failures. He was heavier than dying men should be, or perhaps dying men are always heavier, full of the weight of everything they haven't said yet. She got him onto the workbench after sweeping her tools aside, cut the coat away from the shoulder wound, and found what she expected: a crossbow bolt, snapped off short, the head still buried. The side wound was a blade's work, shallower but filthy.

He passed out twice while she worked, and both times she caught herself leaning close to his mouth to make sure of his breath, and both times she hated herself for the tenderness of the gesture. This was a crown soldier. Crown soldiers had burned the temple at Corvane. Crown soldiers collected the gifted in wagons, according to the peddlers' rumors, and the peddlers' rumors were the only news Hollowmere allowed itself.

But when she pressed a poultice of yarrow and spiderweb into the shoulder wound, his hand found hers again without waking, and held on, and something in her chest that had been locked for thirty years rattled against its bars.

On his breast pocket, stitched flat against the lining, was a packet wrapped in oilcloth. She didn't open it. She wanted to. Her fingers actually hovered over it, and she made herself pull them back, because whatever it was, it had cost this man blood to carry, and some debts you shouldn't peek at before they're presented.

Instead she lit a lamp, sat down across from him, and finally let herself listen.

Not with her ears. With the part of her she kept folded small and hidden, the way one carries a knife in a boot. Even muffled by the Veil, even thinned to almost nothing, the dead of Hollowmere murmured in their yard across the lane, and tonight they were restless. She felt them the way you feel weather coming in an old scar.

*They're coming,* said old Marta Hollis, dead nine years, who had never in life or death learned to lower her voice. *Iron and dogs and fire, girl. Iron and dogs and fire.*

"How many?" Mirabel whispered, feeling foolish, hearing nothing.

*More than the village has doors,* said Marta. *And they carry something that hurts. Something that bites at us even through the Veil. Like a tooth. Like a great white tooth walking.*

Mirabel looked at the unconscious soldier, then at the oilcloth packet, then at the dark window, and understood — not fully, not yet, but in the way one understands a fall has begun before the landing — that the world outside her valley had found a crack in the wall her whole life had been built against.

* * *

They came at moonrise, but not first to her door.

First there was light in the east, over toward the Ferrow farm, and Mirabel was awake to see it because she had not slept at all. At first she thought it was dawn, misjudged the hour. Then she smelled the smoke, and heard the bells — the real bells, the church bells of Hollowmere ringing the alarm that hadn't rung in living memory — and understood.

The Ferrow farm burned fast and loud, thatch catching like a struck match. From her doorway, Mirabel watched figures move through the yard with the unhurried economy of men doing a job they had done many times. There were perhaps twenty of them, mounted and on foot, in armor of blackened steel, and at their center rode a woman in a white cloak who did not watch the fire at all. She sat her horse facing the village, face tilted upward, as if listening to something above the smoke.

Behind the farmhouse, silhouetted against the flames, the Ferrows were made to kneel. All six of them. Even the children. Mirabel's stomach turned over slowly, like something rolling in its sleep.

The white-cloaked woman dismounted. She walked to the burning barn, reached into it — into the fire, unflinching, her gauntlet going into flame as if into water — and drew something out.

Even at this distance, even across the dark fields, Mirabel felt it.

It was like a hook set behind her eyes. Like the moment of silence between lightning and thunder, except the silence was the thing striking. Across the lane, in the memorial yard, every dead soul she had ever known screamed at once — not with mouths, not with sound, but in the raw channel where she heard them, a single vast note of agony and terror that buckled her to her knees on her own doorstep.

Through the pain, through the roaring, she understood what old Marta had meant. *A great white tooth walking.* The woman in white carried a shard of bone. Old bone. Vast bone. Bone that did not belong to any creature of this earth, and that bit into the dead like a brand into flesh.

The Veil kept out magic. But this was not magic. This was a piece of the thing the Veil had been woven from — a splinter of the god itself, carried like a key into the lock it had been cut from.

That was how they had crossed. That was how they would always cross. No valley was deep enough, no spell old enough, to keep out a piece of the corpse the walls were made of.

In her workshop, Cassian Wendell stirred on the workbench and groaned her name.

The white-cloaked woman dropped the shard back into its case at her belt. The pressure vanished. The dead fell silent — not calm, but silenced, cowering, and Mirabel realized with a new and colder horror that they were hiding from it. The dead of Hollowmere, who had nowhere else to go, were hiding.

The woman remounted. The fire roared behind her. And she raised one arm, unhurried, and pointed down the lane — past the smithy, past the chapel, past the houses of people Mirabel had known all her life —

— at Mirabel's door.

Mirabel stepped backward into her workshop, closed the door, and put her back against it. Her heart was slamming. On the bench, Cassian had dragged himself half upright, grey-faced, one hand pressed to his bandaged shoulder, the other already reaching for the oilcloth packet at his ruined coat.

"They'll ask you questions first," he rasped. "About me. About gifts. Answer slow. Confuse them. And Mirabel—" His eyes, fever-bright, fixed on hers. "Whatever they offer you, whatever they promise — the moment they say your name, the asking is over. After that there's only one question left, and it isn't a question."

Outside, hooves on the lane. Many hooves, coming closer, unhurried as men doing a job they had done many times.

Mirabel looked at the sealed orders, at the soldier, at the door — and reached for her carving knife.
