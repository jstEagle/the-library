## 3. What the Bones Said

The fever broke at moonrise, and that was when Mirabel knew she would have to listen.

Cassian Wendell lay on her workbench pallet, sweat-soaked, his breath a wet rattle in the dark. The arrowhead had come out of his shoulder ugly, tearing more than it cut, and though she had cleaned the wound with boiled willow bark and packed it with honey and moss, the flesh around it had gone hot and angry, streaked red to the collarbone. He muttered now — fragments, names, a word repeated like a prayer worn smooth: *the cities, the cities, deliver it to the cities.*

She had done everything a bone-carver could do without doing *that*.

Mirabel sat on her stool with her hands folded in her lap, looking at them. A carver's hands: knuckles broad from mallet work, fingertips calloused smooth as river stone. For thirty years those hands had made bells and whistles and memorial flutes for the dead of Hollowmere, and for thirty years they had whispered to her, faintly, through the Veil — like voices heard through a wall, like a song played in another room. She had learned to live with the murmur. She had learned, mostly, to pretend it wasn't there.

But Cassian was going to die before dawn if she didn't stop pretending.

*They'll know,* said the voice of old Sera Voss, dead eleven years, whose finger-bones hung in Mirabel's workshop on a cord of braided hemp. Sera had been the last person in Hollowmere to speak openly of gifts, before the neighbors' superstition curdled into something colder. *They always know, girl. The valley doesn't hide you. It only makes you quiet enough to forget you're hiding.*

"I'm not hiding," Mirabel said aloud, and heard how thin it sounded. "I'm *living*."

The bones didn't answer. They never argued. They only waited, patient as sediment, for her to pick them up.

She rose and went to the shelf where she kept the remains no one had claimed — the pauper's box from the flood two winters past, the stranger washed down from the high passes whom no village had come looking for. And there, wrapped in oilcloth at the back, the thing she had told herself she kept out of respect: the hand bones of the witch-hunter who had died at the valley's edge three autumns ago. He'd been one of Aldric's men even then, scouting, asking questions about gifted children. Fever had taken him before he could report whatever he'd found. The village had buried him shallow and unmarked, and Mirabel, unable to help herself, had taken the hand.

She had never listened to it. Not once in three years. It sat in its cloth like a snake she'd convinced herself was a stick.

Now she unwrapped it.

The metacarpals were long and fine, a fencer's hand or a torturer's. Even through the Veil, even untouched, she felt it wake against her palm — a low hum, a held breath. Dead men's bones remembered what their owners had thought important. That was the whole of her gift, the small crooked miracle of it: memory lived in marrow longer than it lived in minds.

"If I do this," she said to the empty room, to the fevered man behind her, to no one, "there's no taking it back."

Cassian coughed. Blood flecked his lips, bright as poppy petals.

Mirabel closed her fist around the hunter's hand and opened her gift all the way.

* * *

It was not like the murmurs. It was not like anything.

The Veil pressed down on Hollowmere like deep water, and beneath that pressure sound became pressure too — the dead's voices came to her not as words but as weight, as cold, as the sensation of standing in a doorway between winter and fire. She gasped. Her knees hit the floorboards. The hand in her grip burned with someone else's dying.

And then the memories came, and she understood why the Church called the gifted abominations, because no living mind should hold this.

A map unfolded behind her eyes. Not paper — *knowledge*, the kind a man carries in his body, drawn by years of riding. She saw the valleys of the eastern ranges laid out like fingers of a hand, and on each finger, marks. Red wax seals. She saw ledgers, hundreds of pages, columns of names in a clerk's careful script: *Hollowmere — 14 suspected. Vasse ferry district — 31. The chalk villages — 60, 60, 22.* Names under names under names, generations of them, grandmothers and infants, midwives and millers, anyone who had ever been whispered about over a fence. She saw the orders they fed into: not arrest. *Collection.* The gifted were to be taken alive to the capital, to the Crown's own bone-workers, who —

— who did things she recoiled from seeing, things done to children in rooms without windows, and she wrenched her mind away and the memory showed her something else instead, something worse in its way:

She saw herself. Or the idea of herself. *The carver of Hollowmere*, written in a margin in fresh ink, circled twice. *Confirmed resonance in local bone-work. Priority upon next survey.*

Three years the page had existed. Three years she'd been a line in a ledger while she whistled and carved and told herself the valley loved her and the valley hid her.

The hunter's dying knowledge gave her one more thing: the schedule. A survey company, forty riders, due to sweep the eastern valleys — *next week*. And a signal protocol, three fires on the ridge, that would bring them early if any scout reported contact with a crown soldier.

Cassian. A crown soldier, bleeding in her house, carrying sealed documents worth killing for.

They weren't coming next week. They were coming as soon as a neighbor's courage failed.

Mirabel came back to herself on the floor, nose bleeding freely onto the boards, the hunter's hand bones still clenched in her fist so hard her own knuckles ached. The room swam. Somewhere far away, Sera Voss's finger-bones were ringing softly on their cord, the closest the dead could come, through the Veil, to applause.

Or mourning. With the dead it was hard to tell the difference.

* * *

She worked until gray light. Boiled wine, willow, the green mold from her cheese cave pressed into the wound — everything her grandmother's grandmother had known, everything the bones had shown her of army surgery in the hunter's memory, field-craft learned watching men survive what should have killed them. Near dawn the fever's edge receded, just barely, just enough, and Cassian's eyes found hers and focused.

"You used it." His voice was gravel. "Your gift. To find the medicine."

"To find out how much trouble we're in." She wrung out a cloth, kept her hands busy so they wouldn't shake. "There are lists, Cassian. Books of names. Every valley, every village. Hollowmere is on them — I'm on them, by trade. And there's a company of forty riders due within days, sooner if anyone reports you."

He tried to sit up and failed, jaw tight with pain. "Then I leave tonight."

"You can't stand."

"Then I crawl." His eyes were fierce and fever-bright. "Those documents can't be here when they come. If they find them —"

"They won't find *you* either, if you're here. You know what they do with witnesses." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "You know what they do with people who help witnesses. They burn farms, Cassian. I've seen it in a dead man's head — I know exactly how they do it, which door they break first, what they say to the children. There's a family two fields over, the Ostrows, with three girls. If I'm harboring you when the riders come, the Ostrows burn too, because neighbors talk, and talk is all it takes."

Silence. Outside, the first birds were trying the morning, tentative.

"So we run," Cassian said finally. "Both of us. Tonight, before —"

"And leave them?" The words tore out of her before she could dress them up. "My whole life is here. Tam with his bad hip who can't split his own wood. Old Maren who thinks my bells keep the floods back — let her keep thinking it, it's kinder than the truth. The graves I tend. The *bells*, Cassian, half-finished, for people who trusted me with their dead." Her vision blurred and she hated it. "If I run, I choose strangers over everyone I ever —"

"No." He said it quietly, and it stopped her. "You don't choose strangers over them. You choose them *for* them. Those lists don't stop because you stay to be found on them. You run north, you get the truth to the free cities, and maybe — *maybe* — the war comes here as armies instead of as witch-hunters, and armies have rules. Sometimes." He grimaced. "I've been a soldier too long to promise it prettily."

Mirabel looked at her hands. Carver's hands. They had spent thirty years giving the dead a voice, and she had told herself that was enough, that beauty was enough, that a quiet life was a life.

The hunter's hand bones lay on the table where she'd dropped them, curled slightly, as if still reaching for something.

"The dead showed me the anchor roads," she said. "The passes out of the valley. There's a shepherd track north of the ridge the surveys missed — the man who mapped them died before he finished, and his notes died with him. Except they didn't." She touched the old bones, gently, an apology. "Nothing dies with them. Not really. Not while I can hear."

* * *

They left at full dark, under a sky scoured clean by wind.

Mirabel carried one pack: tools, dried fish, the sealed documents sewn into the lining of her coat because Cassian's shoulder couldn't bear a strap. At the last moment she stood in her workshop and looked at the shelves — the bells, the flutes, the little bone birds she made for children's graves — and she took nothing but Sera Voss's finger-bones, strung at her throat where they lay cold as a promise.

At the edge of the village she stopped at the Ostrows' fence and looked at the dark windows, the smokehouse, the rope swing. *Forgive me,* she thought, not knowing to whom. Then, because the dead deserved better than a coward's whisper, she did the one thing she could: she pressed her palm flat to the gatepost and sent her hearing out through the wood, through the roots, into the sleeping ground of Hollowmere, and spoke to every bone she had ever buried.

*I'm sorry. I have to go. If I make it — if the truth lands where it needs to land — maybe no one ever comes for you. Sleep well. Ring loud.*

The ground stayed silent, the way ground does. But as she turned away, Sera's bones stirred at her throat, one faint chime, and Mirabel chose — deliberately, desperately — to believe it meant *go*.

They climbed through the birch scrub toward the shepherd track, Cassian leaning on her shoulder, both of them staggering, neither saying the thing about how slow he was, how loud the night felt, how every owl's cry sounded like a horn. Behind them Hollowmere lay black and unknowing, fourteen names on a clerk's list, believing itself safe inside the Veil.

They had just reached the ridgeline when Mirabel froze.

South, down the valley road: torchlight. Not one torch. A column of them, moving fast, unhurried in the way of men who know no one is ahead of them to warn.

Forty riders, arriving four days early. And at the column's head, a lantern raised high, its light catching something the rider wore at his throat — a shard of pale bone, glowing faintly gold even at this distance, burning like a coal that refused the Veil's drowning dark.

Cassian saw it too. She felt him go rigid against her side.

"That's godsbone," he breathed. "That's how they walked through. Mirabel — that's how they'll *always* walk through."

Below them, the lead rider halted. Raised his lantern. Turned its light slowly across the ridgeline, sweeping the darkness where two fugitives stood very still, like rabbits who had mistaken stillness for safety.

The light passed over them. Paused. Began to come back.

"Run," Cassian said.
