## 4. The Road Out of Silence

The Veil ended at a line only Mirabel could see.

Cassian watched her stop at the crest of the ridge, three days out of Hollowmere, and go rigid as a snared hare. Her hands flew to her ears — useless, he knew, because what was coming for her wasn't sound. It was everything else.

"Mirabel." He kept his voice low, though low meant nothing now. "Breathe."

She didn't hear him. Her knees buckled and he caught her, one arm around her ribs, careful of the bandage beneath her cloak where the hunter's blade had kissed her. She was shaking. Not the small shiver of cold — this was the tremor of a woman standing too close to a collapsing wall.

"What is it?" he asked, though he already knew. He'd grown up outside the Veil. He'd never known anything else. The world's magic soaked through everything here like water through wool: in the way the pines leaned toward each other and murmured, in the way the road ahead seemed to remember every foot that had ever pressed it. He had simply learned not to listen.

But she'd spent thirty years behind a dead god's last mercy. And now the dam had broken.

"They're everywhere." Her voice came out shredded. "Cassian — they're *everywhere*. I can't — there are so many—"

"The dead?"

"Everything. Everyone. Every grave along this road for a thousand years." Her eyes were open but unfocused, fixed on some middle distance where he could see nothing at all. "They're not quiet like at home. They're not whispers in bone. They're *screaming*, they're singing, they're asking me things, hundreds of them, thousands—" She gagged. "Make it stop."

"I can't make it stop," he said. "But I can teach you to close the door."

* * *

He made camp early that night, in the hollow beneath an overhang where the wind couldn't find them, and built a fire small enough to hide. Mirabel sat with her back against stone, knees drawn up, hands pressed flat over her ears as if she could hold her skull together by force. She hadn't eaten. He set the dried meat beside her anyway.

"When did it start?" she whispered. "For you. When did you learn to ignore it?"

"I don't have your gift." He fed a twig to the flames. "I just grew up in the noise. Everyone does, outside the Veil. You learn to let it wash past you. Like rain."

"It's not rain." A broken laugh. "It's a flood."

"Then we build you a levee." He shifted so he faced her fully. "My mother was gifted. Faintly — she read weather in bird-bones, nothing more. But she taught me how the gifted survive out here, before the purges made such lessons dangerous." Something tightened in his chest at the word *purges*, and he kept talking to outrun it. "It starts with something of yours. Something you've carried long enough that it knows you."

She pulled the little carved bell from her pocket — the memorial bell for the drowned child, the first thing she'd grabbed when they fled. Of course it was that. He should have guessed.

"Hold it," he said. "Not your ears. Your hands. The gift lives in your hands, doesn't it? In the carving?"

Her fingers closed around the bell. "Yes."

"Now imagine a room. Small. Stone walls. One door." He kept his voice even, rhythmic, the way his mother's had been on the nights the sky itself seemed to whisper. "The dead are outside that door. All of them. But the door has a bolt, and the bolt is yours. When it gets too loud, you don't fight them. You just... walk to the door. And you close it."

"I can't choose which voices—"

"You're not choosing yet. You're just closing the door. Everything. Even the ones you want to hear." He hated saying it. He saw her flinch at it — a woman who'd lived her whole life half-deaf to her own gift being told to deafen herself further. "Later, when you're stronger, you learn to open it a crack. To let one voice through. But tonight, you close it. All the way."

She squeezed her eyes shut. Her lips moved. For a long moment nothing happened, and then her whole body sagged, like a rope cut from its anchor, and she made a sound that was half sob and half relief.

"Gone," she breathed. "It's gone. It's so quiet I can't feel my own hands."

"That's the trick of it. Quiet enough to live in." He passed her the meat and this time she took it. "You'll have to rebuild the room every day. Maybe every hour, at first. It'll get easier."

She ate in silence. Then, without looking at him: "Your brother. Tomas. You said his name in your sleep last night."

Cassian's hand stilled on the fire.

"He was gifted," she went on, still not looking at him, her voice very soft. "Wasn't he. That's why you took the orders instead of burning them. Why you ran instead of reporting. Because you know exactly what Aldric does to people like me."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, an owl called, and Cassian thought: *even the owl sounds like it's asking permission.*

"Tomas could hear lies," he said finally. "That was all. Someone would speak, and he'd know if it was false. Useless gift, my father called it. Dangerous gift, everyone else called it, once Aldric's edicts came down." He turned the spit though there was nothing on it. "He hid it for two years. Then a neighbor sold him for a purse of silver and a pardon. They didn't even give the man the full purse. Half. Aldric's hunters drove a hard bargain even then."

"Cassian—"

"I delivered the warrant." The words came out flat, mechanical, rehearsed from a hundred sleepless tellings. "I was a crown soldier. It was my duty. I stood in the square and I watched, because deserting my post would have endangered my unit, because the law was the law, because I told myself a hundred things that let me stand there and watch my brother die for hearing a lie." He looked at her then, and let her see all of it. "So understand what I am, Mirabel. I'm not your rescuer. I'm a man carrying sealed orders to men who will use them however suits them, running from the army I swore to, protecting the kind of person I helped kill. If you're wise, you'll take the documents off my body while I sleep and finish this without me."

She was quiet a long time. The fire burned down to coals.

"No," she said at last.

"No?"

"You heard me." There was iron under the exhaustion now, and he realized he was seeing the spine of her for the first time — the thing that had let her carve bones alone in a superstitious valley for thirty years without going mad. "You think you're using me. Fine. I'm using you too — you know the roads, you know how to fight, you know how to build this..." she lifted the bell, "...this room in my head. We're using each other. Good. That's honest." She met his eyes. "But you don't get to decide for me what I'm wise enough to do. My whole life, other people decided what Mirabel Quintana could bear. The valley decided I should be hidden. Now you decide I should be abandoned. No."

Something in his chest cracked — painfully, like ice giving way — and he found himself almost laughing. "You argue like a soldier."

"I argue like a carver. Bone teaches you patience. And that it always breaks along its own lines, no matter how you plan."

* * *

They walked north for six days, and each day the room in her head held a little longer.

He taught her between villages — they skirted every settlement, keeping to shepherd tracks and old drover roads — how to open the door a crack and listen for danger the way other scouts listened for hoofbeats. She taught him, without meaning to, how to be still. How to sit with grief instead of marching away from it. On the fourth night she carved a small figure from a deer bone he'd found — a soldier, faceless — and when he asked who it was, she said, "Whoever you need it to be," and he had to walk away from the fire for a while.

On the fifth day, she stopped mid-stride on a hillside overlooking the river valley, and her face went white.

"Cassian." Her hand found his arm. "There's a battle buried under this field. Old one. Centuries old." Her eyes had the faraway look again, but controlled now, deliberate — a door opened a crack, on purpose. "They're... they're trying to tell me something. About the road ahead. About Vasse."

"Vasse is our crossing. What do they say?"

Her jaw worked. When she spoke, her voice had that strange doubled quality he was learning to dread — her words, but older, carried up through the dirt like water through roots.

"*The ferryman remembers the gifted. The ferryman fears the gifted. The ferryman will ask a price no coin can pay.*"

The wind moved through the grass below, and for a moment Cassian could almost believe he heard it too — a thousand throats whispering through the living green.

Above them, far to the south, a smudge of smoke rose against the sky. Too steady, too deliberate, to be a wildfire.

"They're following us," he said quietly.

Mirabel closed the door in her head, opened her eyes, and gripped her carving knife.

"Then we'd better reach the river first."
