## 5. The Ferryman's Toll

Vasse announced itself by smell before it ever showed its walls.

They had walked three days out of the hills, following the river's gray braid through country that grew steadily more inhabited and steadily less alive. Mirabel had thought she understood what it meant for magic to return — she had wept at the first taste of it, kneeling in a meadow with her hands pressed to her ears while ten thousand dead voices roared through her like a flood through a broken dam. Cassian had held her until the roar became a river, and the river became something she could stand in. He taught her to build walls in her mind, to narrow her hearing to a single voice at a time, to close herself like a fist around a candle flame.

But nothing had taught her how to live beside a city.

The stench reached them first — rot and river-mud and smoke, and beneath it all something sweeter and worse, the smell of sickness baked into stone. Then the walls, black with damp, crowned with gulls that screamed like the souls of the drowned. Then the docks, spilling down to the water in crooked tiers, where the dead were so thick in the air that even Cassian could feel it, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the light.

"They're everywhere," Mirabel whispered. She kept her hood up, her hands hidden in her sleeves. In the valley, hiding her gift had been habit. Here it was survival. "Cassian, they're *everywhere*. Under the piers. In the warehouses. Drowned men, plague-dead, fevered children—" Her breath went thin. "I can't sort them. They're all talking at once."

"Then don't listen." He took her elbow, steadying her, and his touch was the one warm thing in the gray morning. "Walls up. We find the ferry, we cross, we keep moving. Ardenne is four days beyond the river."

"The ferry costs money."

"I have money." He said it lightly, but she saw him weigh the purse in his pocket, saw the arithmetic behind his eyes. Three days of road food, and after Vasse there would be no more villages willing to sell bread to strangers who asked too few questions.

* * *

The ferryman's office was a shack of tarred planks wedged between a fishmonger and a chandler, and the ferryman himself was a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, with a beard the color of old rope and hands scarred white in patches — burn scars, or the marks of some pox. His name, painted on a board above the door, was Odo Brask.

He named his price before they'd finished asking.

"Coin I have," he said, when Cassian laid out their coppers and the single silver piece that was meant to feed them to Ardenne. He pushed the money back across the counter with one thick finger. "Keep it. Coin won't do what needs doing."

Mirabel's stomach dropped. She knew — she *knew* — before he said it.

"My docks are dying," Brask said. "Plague came up the river on a grain barge two months back. It's in the pilings now, in the rope, in the tar. My ferrymen sicken and die, my passengers sicken and die, and the Council sends physicians who burn a little rosemary and call it done." He leaned forward, and his eyes — small, shrewd, desperate — went from Cassian's soldier's bearing to Mirabel's hidden face. "There's a thing the old boatmen knew. A bone, carved by gifted hands, set at the prow of each boat. The dead don't cross where the dead are honored. My grandfather had one. Lost it when the Veil went quiet and everyone decided the gift was a fairy story." His jaw worked. "Then the Veil broke, didn't it. And word travels on the river faster than boats. Word of a woman walking north with a soldier, a woman who flinches at every crowd because she can hear what's in them."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed, and beneath it — faint, insistent — Mirabel heard the dock-dead murmuring, a tide of voices like wind through reeds.

"You have me mistaken," Cassian began.

"I have you exactly right," Brask said, not unkindly. "And I'm not threatening you. Threats are for men with soldiers. I've got two ferrymen left and both of them are coughing blood." He spread his scarred hands. "I'm begging. Cross my river for free, ride my best boat, eat at my own table tonight. All I ask is one carved bone. One. And nobody knows but us."

*Cassian*, Mirabel thought. *Tell him no. Tell him we can't be seen, that the hunters are behind us, that every hour we linger is an hour they gain.*

But she was already looking past him, out the fogged window toward the docks, where a woman stood at the edge of a pier staring down into black water as if listening for someone. And Mirabel could hear what the woman couldn't: her son, drowned off pier six, calling and calling and calling, *Mama, it's cold, Mama, why won't anyone answer—*

Three hundred years of silence, and the dead of Vasse had so much to say.

"We'll do it," Mirabel said.

Cassian turned sharply. "Mirabel—"

"One bone. Tonight, before we cross." She met Brask's eyes. "But I choose which bones. And I need somewhere quiet, and I need your grandfather's tools if you have them, and I need you to understand that this isn't a charm. It's testimony. The dead will ward your boats because I'll ask them to, and they'll agree or they won't, and if they don't, no carving in the world will help you."

Brask stared at her. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. "Grandfather said the gifted talked plain. Said they never dressed a thing up to sound bigger than it was." He stood, joints cracking. "Come. Quiet's hard to find in Vasse, but I've got a boathouse the plague emptied. Nobody'll trouble you there."

* * *

The boathouse smelled of pitch and old water. Brask brought her bones — a box of them, river-finds and market-bought, cattle and pig and one long human femur he handled with visible reverence. "Grandfather's," he said. "He always said a ferryman should carry a piece of the folk he ferries."

Mirabel sat on an overturned skiff with the femur across her knees and let her walls down — carefully, a crack at a time, the way Cassian had taught her, letting one voice through instead of a thousand.

The dock-dead came like moths to a lamp.

They crowded her — the drowned of pier six, the fevered children of the rope-walk, an old ferryman who'd died at his oar forty years ago and had been waiting, waiting, for someone to ask him anything at all. Their voices overlapped, tangled, and Mirabel closed her eyes and did what she had done her whole life in Hollowmere, only now without shame, without hiding: she listened.

*It hurts,* said a child. *The sickness is in the wood. It sleeps in the wood.*

*We don't want to frighten her,* said the old ferryman, gruff and gentle. *Girl. Carver. You want our dead hands on these boats?*

*I want your living care,* Mirabel said aloud, her carving knife whispering along the femur. *I can't give you rest. That's beyond any gift I have. But I can make your names known, and I can ask you to stand between the sick and the whole. Will you?*

A rustle through the crowded air, like wind deciding. Then the old ferryman again, closer now, almost warm: *Ask Brask if he remembers the song. The rowing song. If he sings it at the prow-launching, we'll know he honors us proper.*

She opened her eyes. Brask stood in the doorway with a lantern, watching her carve with an expression she recognized from her own mirror — the look of a man seeing a miracle and grieving everything he'd done without it.

"She says — they say — do you remember the rowing song?"

Brask went still. Then, in a cracked baritone, half-swallowed by tears he'd clearly not shed since boyhood, he sang:

*Over the water, over the foam,*
*Carry the quick to the quiet home...*

The lantern flame bent though there was no draft. On the workbench, the box of river-bones rattled once, softly, like applause.

By dawn the carving was done: a bell-shape within a ring of waves, and inside the bell, tiny as a grain of rice, the names of nine dead ferrymen. Brask held it in both palms like a newborn. "It's warm," he whispered. "Saints' teeth, it's *warm*."

"It's listening," Mirabel said. "Keep it that way."

* * *

They crossed at midmorning on Brask's best boat, the carved bone lashed to the prow, and Mirabel felt the dock-dead fall in around the hull like an honor guard, their murmur smoothing to something almost like contentment. For one hour on the brown water, with Cassian's shoulder against hers and the far bank hazy green ahead, she let herself believe kindness might still be simple.

She learned otherwise before they reached the landing.

A man had shared the crossing — a grain merchant, soft-spoken, with ink-stained fingers and a traveler's easy smile, who'd asked no questions until the prow-bone caught his eye. Then just one, tossed off lightly, a coin flipped to a beggar: "Fine work, that. There was a carver in Hollowmere did bells like that, years back. You wouldn't know the valley?"

Mirabel's blood went to ice water. She felt Cassian go rigid beside her, felt him begin to shape a denial—

"Hollowmere's bells are plain," she said, keeping her voice bored, a craftswoman's pride and nothing more. "No one gifted works bone anymore. Everyone knows that." She looked at the merchant's ink-stained fingers, and then — deliberately, letting her walls crack open just wide enough — she listened.

Underneath his heartbeat, underneath the surface calm, another voice clung to him like a burr. Not his. A dead man's voice, thin and resentful, the voice of someone recently and badly dead, muttering the same words over and over: *the list, the list, the woman on the list, tell them at the toll-house, tell them at the toll-house—*

The merchant smiled at her with his mouth. His eyes were already writing letters.

The boat touched the far bank. The merchant stepped off first, unhurried, and turned north along the towpath toward the toll-house at a walker's pace — a man with nothing to hide, covering ground.

"Cassian," Mirabel said quietly. "The toll-house. There's a rider being sent for. We have hours, maybe less. He's carrying word of us to the hunt."

Cassian was already counting their coins, already calculating, and she watched the war move behind his eyes — run east and lose time, or go north through the toll-house and cut the message off at the root.

"How many hours?" he asked.

"Fewer than we need."

He pulled his cloak tight and looked up the towpath, toward the toll-house chimney smoking against the sky. "Then we'd better be faster than we've ever been."
