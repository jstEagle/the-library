## 6. Court of Ash

Ardenne smelled of smoke and lilies.

Cassian had forgotten that — the way the free cities masked their rot with perfume, pumping scented oil through the aqueducts so the poor quarters downwind would never quite catch the stink of the tanneries and the plague-pits and the things the Council preferred not to name. He had grown up two streets from a rendering yard in Vasse. He knew what cities did with their dead. He had simply spent six years in the king's army learning to forget.

He stood now in the antechamber of the Council Spire, in a uniform he no longer had any right to wear, watching dust turn gold in the light of a hundred candles. Mirabel sat beside him on a bench built for petitioners, her hands folded around the strap of her satchel like a woman holding shut a door against wind. She hadn't spoken since they passed the gate. The city was loud for her — he could see it in the way she flinched at nothing, at everything, the way she'd pressed her palms over her ears on the ferry dock until he'd shown her again how to build the wall in her mind, stone by stone, breath by breath.

"They know we're here," she said quietly. "The dead in the walls. They've been whispering your name since we came through the door."

"Then I'm famous."

"It isn't funny."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

The doors opened. A herald in ash-grey silk spoke his name wrong — *Ser Cassian Vael, late of His Majesty's Third* — and led them up a stair that spiraled through the heart of the spire, past murals of the Concord: nine robed figures raising the Veil over a kneeling world, three hundred years ago, when magic had been judged too dangerous to leave loose among the living. Cassian had studied those murals as a boy and thought them beautiful. Now he looked at the painted god beneath the painted Veil — a vast figure of bone and patience, bound not in chains but in *silence*, its mouth a seam of gold — and felt something cold walk down his spine.

Mirabel stopped walking. Her hand found his sleeve.

"Cassian." Her voice was very small. "It's real. The thing under the painting. It's *here*. Under the city. I can hear it breathing."

"Later," he said. "Council first. Then we dig."

She looked at him the way she sometimes did — as if she were reading him the way she read bones, all the way down to the marrow — and then she nodded, and they went in.

* * *

The Council chamber was a half-circle of nine seats above a floor of black marble, and only five of the seats were filled. Cassian recognized Lord-Protector Ameline Duras by her silver jaw-clasp and her reputation; Magister Oren Hale by the ledger chained to his wrist; the others by their indifference. None of them rose. None of them offered chairs. Petitioners stood, and it took Cassian a moment, standing there with sealed orders burning a hole in his coat, to understand that this was what he was now. Not an officer delivering intelligence that could save three cities. A petitioner.

"Ser Wendell," Duras said. "You were reported dead at Redford Bridge."

"I was reported *missing*, my lady. There's a difference. My whole company died at Redford Bridge. I carried what they died for out of the valley."

"You deserted your post."

"My post was a mass grave."

Hale cleared his throat, a dry sound like a page turning. "The documents, if you have them."

Cassian drew the oilskin packet from inside his coat — the sealed orders he'd taken from a dying colonel's body, the supply manifests, the route maps, the letter in Aldric's own hand authorizing the *pacification* of the gifted populations of the northern valleys. He held it out. A clerk descended to take it, and for a moment the room was silent except for the scratch of the seal breaking.

He watched them read. He had imagined this moment on every mile of the road from Hollowmere — imagined the alarm, the mobilization, the great machine of the free cities grinding awake at last. He watched Duras's eyes move down Aldric's letter and saw nothing change in her face at all. That was when he understood.

"You already knew," he said.

No one answered, which was answer enough.

"The harvest schedules," Hale said mildly, to Duras rather than to Cassian. "Aldric moves on the grain valleys first. If we concede the northern passes—"

"You *knew*." Cassian's voice cracked across the marble. "Three cities. Forty thousand people in the valleys alone. You knew he was marching, you knew about the lists, you knew what happens to the gifted when his witch-hunters reach them, and you sat here reading ledgers?"

"We knew," Duras said, "what everyone with functioning eyes knows, Ser Wendell. Which is why the Council has been negotiating."

"Negotiating."

"Aldric does not want war with the free cities. He wants legitimacy. Recognition of his crown. Trade concessions. And—" she paused, delicately, "—certain individuals whose presence in our territories is... provocative to him."

The silence stretched. Beside him, he heard Mirabel's breath go shallow.

"Individuals," Cassian repeated.

"Gifted refugees," Hale said, with the tone of a man discussing drainage. "The treaty draft includes a schedule of surrender. Names, primarily. Aldric believes — correctly, I'm told — that the gifted are the source of the... irregularities... in his territory. Remove the irritant, and the north settles."

"You're going to give him people." Cassian turned to look at each of the five faces in turn, looking for one flicker of shame. "You're going to hand him human beings to burn, and call it peace."

"I'm going to keep forty thousand people *alive*," Duras said, and for the first time something moved behind her eyes — not shame, but the flat exhaustion of a woman who had done this arithmetic before and would do it again. "You want a war, soldier? Have you seen a war? I have. I stood at Cael Ford when I was nineteen years old. Do you know what Aldric's crown does on a battlefield? Do you know what he *is*? No. Of course not. You've been hiding in a valley." She leaned forward. "We cannot fight him. So we buy him. That is not cowardice. That is arithmetic."

"And the arithmetic says my brother was worth less than a trade concession."

He hadn't meant to say it. Tomas's name stayed behind his teeth where it always lived, but the shape of it was in the words anyway, and Duras — who signed the condolence letters, who must have signed thousands — had the grace to look away.

"The documents will be entered into the record," Hale said. "You'll be compensated for your journey. And Ser Wendell — regarding your status. You left your command without orders. You removed Crown property from a battlefield. You traveled in company with—" a pause, a glance at Mirabel, cool and cataloguing, "—an unregistered gifted, which is itself a crime under the Concord Accords. The Council is prepared to be lenient. Desertion carries a maximum penalty of hanging. In light of your service, we commute it to dishonorable discharge and permanent exclusion from Council territory."

"Exclusion," Cassian said. "You're banishing me for bringing you the truth."

"We're *thanking* you," Duras said, "for the documents. The rest is theater, Ser Wendell. It has always been theater. Go home. Be grateful the play ended with you still breathing."

* * *

They were escorted out through a side corridor by guards who wouldn't meet their eyes, and the doors closed behind them with a sound like a verdict.

Mirabel didn't speak until they reached the bottom of the stair, into the noise and candle-smoke of the public hall. Then she caught his arm and pulled him into the shadow of a pillar, and when she looked up at him her eyes were wet and furious.

"They're going to put my name on a list," she said. "My name, and every gifted person who ever ran to these walls thinking they were safe. I heard them, Cassian. The dead in this building — clerks, archivists, people who died copying ledgers — they've seen the drafts. They're already writing the schedules. And the dead here *know*, they're trying to warn me, they keep saying *beneath, beneath, beneath*—"

"Mira—"

"—and you stood there and let them call you a deserter! You let them take your name! Six years, Cassian, you gave them six years and your brother, and they handed you a piece of paper and showed you the door!"

"Yes," he said.

She stared at him. "Yes?"

"Yes." He took her hands. They were cold, and they trembled, and he held them the way you hold something you have already decided you will not lose. "I spent the whole road telling myself that if I could just get the documents to Ardenne, it would mean something. That duty was a real thing and I'd failed it and this would fix it. And they read Aldric's own handwriting and started talking about *grain schedules*." He laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged. "There's no duty left, Mira. There's just people. There's you, and there's the dead under this city begging someone to listen, and there's a god in a cage wearing a king like a puppet. That's the whole list."

"Your discharge—"

"Is the first honest thing they've given me." He touched her face, thumb at her cheekbone, and watched her lean into it despite everything, despite the roar of the dead she was holding back behind stone walls in her mind. "I don't regret it. Any of it. Not Redford Bridge, not the valley, not carrying you out of the dark. Let them hang me in effigy. I know what I am."

"What are you?" she whispered.

"Yours," he said, "if you'll have a man with no country and no rank and a price on both your heads."

For a moment — one breath, two — the noise of the hall fell away, and she smiled at him like dawn coming up over Hollowmere, and he thought: *this. This is the thing worth the treason.*

Then her smile died. Her head snapped toward the far wall, toward the base of the spire, and her grip crushed his fingers white.

"Cassian." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The dead are screaming. All of them at once. Something's coming up from underneath — and there are riders at the gates. Grey cloaks. Bone shards at their belts."

Outside, across the city, a bell began to ring — not the hour, not the alarm, but a broken, stuttering clangor, as though the ringer's hands were shaking.

The hunters had reached Ardenne.
