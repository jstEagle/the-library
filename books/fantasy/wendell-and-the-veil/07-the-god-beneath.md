## 7. The God Beneath

The dead did not sleep in Ardenne.

Mirabel had learned that much in the three weeks since the Veil's edge had fallen behind them and the world had roared open like a wound. In Hollowmere, the voices through the bones had been whispers — a drowned child humming, an old woman counting her grandchildren, faint as breath on glass. Here, the dead were everywhere, pressed into every stone of the free city like damp into old plaster, and they were not quiet. They murmured in the walls of the tenements where plague had taken whole families. They muttered under the flagstones of the market square where debtors had been hanged. They sang, off-key and endless, in the crypt beneath the Council spire, where the founders of Ardenne lay in marble and lies.

Cassian had taught her to shield herself — a trick of focus he'd learned from his brother, who'd been gifted too, before Aldric's purges took him. *Build a door in your mind,* Cassian had said, his hands warm over hers on the ferry across the Vasse. *Not a wall. A door you can open when you choose.* She was better at it now. Most days she could walk the city streets hearing only the living: fishwives haggling, children throwing stones at gulls, the criers calling news no one believed.

But tonight the dead would not be shut out.

They had been rising in her since dusk, a tide against the door of her mind, and by the time the bells rang midnight they were not knocking anymore. They were speaking all at once, in one voice braided from ten thousand throats:

*Come down.*

She sat up in the narrow bed of the safehouse — a tanner's loft above the reek of the curing yards, chosen because the stench kept even the Council's informants away. Cassian slept on the pallet by the door, one arm bandaged from the knife-fight at the docks, his sword within reach even in sleep. The moon through the smoke-hazed window was the color of bone.

*Come down. Come down to us. We have waited three hundred years.*

"Where?" she whispered, and the answer came not as words but as a pull, a thread drawn tight behind her sternum, leading down.

She dressed in the dark. She told herself she would only look — stand at the top of whatever stair or well the dead wanted her to find, mark its location, come back for Cassian. She was lying and she knew it. When the dead pulled, she followed. She always had. It was the shape of her gift, the shape of her whole life: she was a hinge between what remained and what went on.

The thread led her out of the tannery district, down through streets that grew older as she descended — cobbles giving way to fitted stone, fitted stone to blocks so vast no human hand could have moved them. Ardenne was built on something older than itself; everyone said so, the way people say the sea is deep, without ever meaning to sound the bottom. The dead thickened around her until shielding was like holding a door shut against floodwater. She stopped trying. Their voice resolved into clarity as she walked, and what it said made her blood run cold and hot by turns.

*We saw him take it. We felt him take it. He reached into our god with a soldier's hand and pulled out fire, and we could not stop him because we were already dead, and the living would not listen—*

At the end of a street that ended in a wall — a wall of those vast, ancient blocks — the stones themselves were carved. Not ornamented. *Written.* Rows upon rows of characters worn nearly smooth, in a script that predated the free cities, the kingdom, the Veil itself. And beneath the writing, half-hidden by three centuries of soot and ivy, a doorway. No door in it. Just darkness, and cold air breathing out of it like the exhale of something enormous and patient.

The dead pressed close. For the first time since the Veil, Mirabel felt them not as a crowd but as a congregation — kneeling, waiting, terrified and hopeful in equal measure.

"He's down there," she said aloud. "Ossuar. The god."

*What is left of him,* they answered. *Aldric wears what is left outside. What remains below remembers everything.*

Her hand found the cold of the threshold stone. Somewhere behind her, far above, a bell began to ring — not midnight now, not any hour. Alarm bells. She ignored them. Later she would understand that this was the moment the city began to die, and that she had walked into its dying with her eyes open, following the dead the way water follows its fall.

She went down.

* * *

The stairs were cut for giants, each step taller than her knee, and she climbed down them the way one climbs down into a well, backwards, hands on stone slick with condensation. The dark was total, but she did not need light. The dead lit her way — not with glow, nothing so kind, but with attention. Where they gathered thickest, she knew the passage turned. Where they fell silent, she knew to stop and listen with more than ears.

It took an hour. Perhaps two. Time went strange below the city, stretched thin as tallow.

And then the stairs ended, and she stood in a cavern so vast her mind refused its dimensions, and she understood why the ancients had built their wall and their warning-script above. This place had not been made. It had been *found*, and then it had been used.

Chains hung from the unseen ceiling. Chains of black iron thick as ancient oaks, each link tall as a house, descending into a pool of stillness at the cavern's heart — a pool that was not water. It was darker than water. It was the absence of reflection, the absence of echo, and it moved, very slowly, the way a sleeping animal moves when it dreams.

The Veil. She knew it instantly, the way you know your own name. Every story in Hollowmere, every prayer her grandmother had mumbled over the carving bench — *the Veil holds the magic down, the Veil keeps us safe* — and here it was, not a spell spread across the sky but an anchor driven into a prisoner. The chains ran up through miles of rock, she realized, fanning out across the world, and everywhere they surfaced, magic went quiet. Hollowmere. A hundred valleys like it. Three centuries of silence, purchased with one imprisonment.

"You're the anchor," she whispered. "You're not behind the Veil. You *are* the Veil."

The pool stirred.

When Ossuar spoke, it was not with sound. It was with memory — her own memories, dragged up and rearranged, so that she experienced his voice as her mother's lullaby, as the bell she'd carved for the drowned child ringing once, as Cassian saying her name on the ferry. It was the most intimate violation she had ever suffered, and the most sorrowful.

*MIRABEL QUINTANA,* the god said, in the voice of everyone she had ever loved. *CARVER OF BONES. LISTENER AT THE KEYHOLE OF DEATH. I KNOW YOUR HANDS. I HAVE FELT THEM THROUGH THE VEIL FOR YEARS, EVERY TIME YOU CARVE, EVERY TIME YOU LET ONE SMALL VOICE THROUGH MY SILENCE. YOU ARE A LEAK IN MY PRISON, LITTLE GIFTED ONE. AND I HAVE BEEN SO GRATEFUL FOR THE DRIPS.*

She should have run. Some animal part of her screamed to run. But the dead were all around her now, visible at last — not ghosts as the stories painted them, but presences, a multitude standing at the edge of the dark pool like mourners at a graveside that had lasted three hundred years. An old man in a plague-mask. A girl with riverweed in her hair. Soldiers by the thousand, rank on rank, the purged and the war-dead and the vanished, all of them looking at her with an expression she recognized because she wore it every day of her life in Hollowmere.

Hope. Terrible, unreasonable hope.

"They want you to break the Veil," she said. Her voice sounded obscene in the vastness, small and human. "That's why they brought me here. That's why they've been pulling me for weeks."

*YES.*

"If I break it—" She made herself say it. "If I break it, you're free. And everything you are — all that power Aldric stole, all that magic the world buried — comes back. All at once. Everywhere."

*YES.*

"The dead could speak again. Testify. Against him." She thought of the documents sewn into Cassian's coat, the lists of names, the valleys mapped. "At a trial. Before the world."

*THAT IS THEIR PRAYER. IT HAS BEEN THEIR PRAYER SINCE ALDRIC'S GRANDFATHER'S GRANDFATHER CHAINED ME. THEY DO NOT WANT VENGEANCE, MIRABEL QUINTANA. THEY WANT TO BE HEARD. THERE IS NO CRUELTY IN THEM LEFT. THREE HUNDRED YEARS BURNS THAT OUT OF YOU.* A pause, and the god's voice — her grandmother's voice now, cracked and gentle — added: *THE CRUELTY IS ALL MINE, AND I HAVE HOARDED IT LIKE A MISER. ASK ME WHAT BREAKING THE VEIL COSTS. I WILL NOT LIE TO YOU. I AM TOO TIRED TO LIE.*

Above her, impossibly far above, the alarm bells had multiplied. She felt it through the stone now — boots, thousands of boots, the city shaking like a struck drum. The hunters. They had found Ardenne. They had found *her*.

"What does it cost?" she asked.

The pool went utterly still. When Ossuar answered, he used no borrowed voice at all, only the raw pressure of truth:

*EVERY GIFTED EAR THAT EVER HEARD THE DEAD WILL GO DEAF FOREVER. THE VEIL AND THE LISTENING ARE TWO ENDS OF ONE THREAD. CUT IT, AND THE DEAD ARE LOOSE TO SPEAK TO THE WORLD — BUT YOU, WHO HAVE SPENT YOUR LIFE BEING THEIR VOICE, WILL NEVER HEAR ANOTHER WORD FROM ANY OF THEM. NOT THEIR TESTIMONY. NOT THEIR THANKS. NOT CASSIAN WENDELL, WHEN HIS HOUR COMES, AND IT WILL COME; I CAN SEE IT STANDING BEHIND YOU EVEN NOW, PATIENT AS ALL MY CHAINS.*

The horror arrived slowly, the way cold water rises. She thought of the drowned child's hum. Of her grandmother's counting. Of Cassian's brother, whose voice she had let Cassian hear once, on the road, a mercy she had never mentioned since. Silence. Not the valley's soft silence, which was really a murmur — true silence, absolute, forever.

"And if I don't?" she whispered. "If I walk away?"

*THEN ALDRIC KEEPS WHAT HE STOLE, THE HUNTERS TAKE ARDENNE TONIGHT, AND THEY TAKE YOU WITH IT — OR THE COUNCIL SELLS YOU FIRST, WHICH WILL BE GENTLER. I OFFER YOU THIS, BECAUSE SOMEONE SHOULD: THERE IS A THIRD PATH. I CAN HOLD THE VEIL SHUT AROUND YOU ALONE. KEEP YOUR GIFT. KEEP YOUR LIFE. LET THE WORLD BURN SLOWLY, ON A TIMELINE YOU WILL NOT LIVE TO SEE FINISH. THE DEAD WILL HATE YOU. YOU WILL DESERVE IT. BUT YOU WILL HEAR THEM HATING YOU, WHICH IS MORE THAN NOTHING.*

Every path forward demanded an unbearable price. That was the whole architecture of the world, she understood suddenly — it had always been built this way, on someone's unbearable price, paid quietly, underground, unthanked. Aldric had simply decided to be the one who collected instead of the one who paid.

The stone above her shuddered. Dust sifted down through the dark. Somewhere up there, Cassian was awake by now, and furious, and coming for her, and the hunters were between him and her, and there was no version of tonight in which everyone lived.

*CHOOSE,* said the god, in the voice of the drowned child, in the voice of every dead soul in Ardenne, in a voice like a door beginning to open. *THEY ARE COMING DOWN THE GIANT'S STAIRS, AND I HAVE ONLY THIS ONE SMALL LEAK IN MY PRISON, AND SHE IS STANDING AT THE BOTTOM OF THE WORLD ASKING QUESTIONS.*

Mirabel Quintana looked at the multitude of the dead, and at the chains, and at the dark.

She reached out her hands.
