## 8. Hunters in the Dark

### Cassian

The bells of Ardenne rang wrong.

Cassian had heard them a hundred times in his soldier's life — festival bells, funeral bells, the deep noon toll that marked the changing of the Council guard. He knew their voices the way he knew his own heartbeat. Tonight they rang ragged, out of rhythm, and he understood before any messenger reached him: someone had killed the bell-ringers, or driven them from the towers, and the sound that remained was the city screaming in the only language it had left.

He found Mirabel on the steps of the Council hall, her satchel of carved bones clutched to her chest like a child. Torchlight turned her face copper and shadow. Behind her, through the great doors, he could hear the Council still arguing — seven voices, seven cities, none of them agreeing on anything except that they did not want to die.

"They're coming," she said. Not a question. She'd been hearing it all day, she told him — the dead of Ardenne whispering up through the flagstones, restless as water about to boil. *They are coming, they are coming, they carry the god's bone and they are not afraid of anything.*

"How long?"

"Before dawn. Maybe sooner." Her eyes were too calm. He'd learned to fear that calm; it was what she wore when the dead were loudest. "The Council won't fight. They'll give me to him if he asks."

"He already asked."

She went very still. "You've seen the envoy's terms?"

"I've seen them." Cassian kept his voice level, the way you did when delivering orders you hated. Old habit. Dead habit, maybe, after tonight. "Aldric offers amnesty for the free cities. Full trade, open borders, no witch-hunts within their walls. In exchange—" He made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness, because everything else in this war was dressed in silk and lies. "In exchange, the last gifted is delivered to the crown. Alive. The envoy used your name, Mirabel. Your village. Your mother's name. They know exactly who you are."

The torches guttered. Somewhere across the city, a building began to burn, and the smoke rolled down the boulevard like a slow gray tide.

"And the Council?" she asked.

"Four votes to accept. Two abstaining." He paused. "One against. Mine doesn't count. I'm a deserter, remember."

"You're not—"

"I am." He said it without bitterness now; the word had lost its sting somewhere on the road from Vasse, worn smooth like a river stone. "I took those documents north instead of reporting them quietly, and that makes me a traitor by their law and Aldric's both. So I'll tell you what a traitor thinks." He stepped closer, close enough that she could not look anywhere but at him. "The Council will hand you over within the hour unless we're gone. There's an anchor beneath this city — your dead have been pulling you toward it for days, don't deny it — and whatever you mean to do there, you have until dawn to do it. After that, there is no Mirabel Quintana. There's only a name on a treaty."

Her hand found his wrist. Her fingers were cold as the bones she carved.

"Cassian," she said, "if I go down there, if I do what they're asking me to do, I don't know what happens to me. To my gift. To—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "To us."

*Us.* The word landed in his chest like an arrow he'd been carrying unstrung for weeks, finally loosed. He thought of his brother Tomas, who had died face-down in a beet field with a hunter's arrow between his shoulders, and how he had sworn on that body never to let another gifted person be taken while he drew breath. He thought of the sealed orders in his pack, the ones that had started all of this, and how duty had once meant obedience and now meant nothing of the kind.

"Then we'd better make sure there's something left to come back to," he said.

It wasn't a vow. It wasn't a promise. It was the only honest thing he could offer: a door held open, no guarantees behind it.

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment, and then she nodded, and together they turned their backs on the Council of Ardenne and walked into the burning dark.

* * *

### Mirabel

The dead came with her underground.

That was the only way she could describe it — not voices anymore but a current, a tide pressing against the walls of her skull as she descended the stair beneath the Temple of Ashes, Cassian's lantern swinging ahead of her. Three hundred years of Ardenne's dead crowded the dark: plague victims stacked in ossuary niches, builders entombed in the foundations, and older things, far older, whose names had worn away like letters on a riverbed stone.

*Down,* they said. *Down, down. He waits below. Hurry, little carver. Hurry.*

"Hurry," she whispered aloud, and Cassian glanced back at her, and she saw him decide not to ask.

The stairs ended at a door of black iron older than any kingdom, and set into it, at the height of her heart, a socket shaped like nothing human. Her hands knew it before her mind did. She drew out the memorial bell she had carved for the drowned child of Hollowmere — the first true thing her gift had ever made — and pressed it home.

The door opened on darkness that breathed.

And Ossuar spoke to her.

Not in words. In weight. The imprisoned god filled the cavern beyond like water fills a drowned bell, vast and half-mad and *hurting*, three centuries of stolen power bleeding out of it through the Veil to feed a usurper's crown. Mirabel stood at the threshold with her hand pressed flat against the cold air and felt every dead soul she had ever heard singing at once, and understood, finally, the shape of the choice.

*Break it,* the dead begged. *Break the Veil and we will testify. Break it and the thief falls. Break it and you fall with us, little carver, break it and you will never hear us again.*

"There you are."

The voice came from behind them, human and pleasant, and Mirabel turned to see the envoy standing at the top of the stairs with six hunters fanned out around him, their bone-shards gleaming white in the lantern light. He looked like a tax collector. That was the horror of him — soft-handed, patient, smiling.

"The Council dithered," he said, descending. "So I decided to collect in person. Lady Quintana. Or should I say *carver*? His Majesty has such admiration for your craft. He asks only that you lend your gift to the crown instead of wasting it on corpses. A generous offer, given the alternative."

Cassian moved.

Later, Mirabel would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail — the lantern dropping, the ring of steel, Cassian's shoulder driving the nearest hunter back into his fellows, the black door booming shut behind her as though the god itself had sealed her in. What she retained was sound: the crack of a crossbow, Cassian's grunt, the envoy shouting *alive, take her alive*, and above it all the dead roaring in her ears like a storm-driven sea.

*Cassian—*

*Go down,* his voice came through the iron, ragged and fierce. *Whatever you're going to do, do it. I'll hold the stairs.*

"You can't hold six men with a sword and a dying lantern!"

"I held a bridge at Redford for two hours with less." A laugh, breathless, terrible. "Mirabel. Listen to me. Everything I did since Hollowmere — the road, Vasse, the Council, all of it — it was to get you here. Don't you dare waste it standing at a door arguing with a dead man walking."

*You're not going to die.*

"Everyone dies." Steel rang. Someone screamed. "The question is only ever what for. Go."

She went.

The darkness took her like deep water. And behind her, muffled through ancient iron, she heard Cassian Wendell begin to fight — not like a soldier following orders, but like a man defending the only country he had left, which was a girl from a quiet valley and the truth she carried in her two steady hands.

* * *

### Cassian

Three of them down. The envoy had withdrawn up the stairs, clever coward, and sent the remaining hunters in pairs.

Cassian's left arm hung wrong where the crossbow bolt had gone through above the elbow, and blood filled his boot with every step, warm as bathwater. He braced himself across the narrow stair with his back to the iron door and let them come, because geometry was on his side and time was on hers, and a man could spend both freely when he'd finally found something worth the price.

The fourth hunter was good. The fifth was better. They came together, shields high, and Cassian gave ground step by step, giving them nothing cheap, making every foot of stair cost a wound. His sword arm was numb to the wrist. His vision kept graying at the edges, and each time it cleared he saw the same thing: Mirabel's face in the doorway at Hollowmere, the night she'd taken in a stranger and doomed herself to save him.

*Treason,* the Council had called it. Let them. He'd been a traitor since the day he chose her over his orders; tonight he was only signing his name to it.

The fifth hunter's blade opened his thigh to the bone. Cassian went down on one knee, caught the follow-up stroke on his crossguard, and drove his dagger up under the man's jaw with the last of his strength. The sixth hunter hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and in that heartbeat the world changed.

Every candle in Ardenne went out.

Every torch, every lantern, every ember in every hearth, snuffed at once, and in the absolute dark Cassian heard a sound that was not a sound: a great cracking, as if the sky itself had split along an old seam, and through the seam poured voices — thousands upon thousands of the dead, suddenly, everywhere, *loose* — and beneath them a deeper note, vast and waking, the god's first free breath in three hundred years.

The last hunter fled screaming up the stairs.

Cassian slid down against the iron door and sat in the dark, laughing quietly, because it had worked, because she'd done it, because somewhere above him Aldric's stolen crown was crumbling to ash mid-battle and the dead were rising to speak and it had *worked*. His blood spread warm beneath him. His hands were very cold.

The door at his back trembled. Opened a hand's width. Light spilled through — not candlelight, something older, silver-white, alive.

"Cassian." Her voice, broken wide open. "Oh gods, Cassian—"

"Don't waste it kneeling here." He got the words out past the blood in his throat. "The trial. They'll need you at the trial. Every word the dead say, they'll need you to—" A cough. He tasted iron. "Tell Tomas I said the bridge held. Tell him his brother finally—"

She was holding him before he finished, her hands pressed to his wounds as though love alone could stitch what steel had cut, and the silver light of a freed god fell over them both.

Above, across the whole waking world, the dead began to speak.
