## 9. Breaking the Veil

The anchor was not what she had imagined.

She had pictured a throne of bone, a cage of iron and scripture, something built by hands that hated what they imprisoned. Instead the tunnel opened into a hollow like the inside of a bell — smooth stone worn to a dark polish, walls ribbed with veins of pale light that pulsed in slow rhythm, three beats to a breath. And at its center, half-sunk in the floor as though the earth had grown around him, sat Ossuar.

He was small. That was the first cruelty of it. A figure no larger than a kneeling man, made of the same ivory as the bones Mirabel had carved all her life, his spine fused into the rock behind him, his head bowed so far forward she could not see his face. Chains of light ran from his shoulders and wrists up through the ceiling, up through three hundred feet of city stone, up into the Veil itself. Where each chain met his body, the ivory had gone black, like frostbite.

The dead pressed in around her, hundreds of them, thousands, crowding the tunnel mouth and spilling down the shaft above, and for once they were not roaring. They were waiting. The silence of them was worse than any noise.

"You came." His voice did not come from his bowed head. It came from everywhere, from the ribs of the hollow itself, from her own sternum. It sounded like a man who had been talking to himself for three hundred years. "The little listener. The carver. I know your hands. I have felt your hands on my children's bones since you were nine years old."

Mirabel's throat closed. She thought of every bell, every whistle, every memorial dove she had ever shaped — the way the dead had murmured through them, faint as voices through water. *He had felt it. All of it.*

"They told me you'd come," Ossuar said. "The ones Aldric silenced first. The ones he buried under his own castle so their testimony would rot where no one gifted could reach it. They've been whispering your name down the roots of the world for eleven years."

Above her, muffled by stone, the battle for Ardenne went on. She could hear it even here — not the dead now but the living: boots, screams, the wet crackle of witch-fire. Cassian was up there. Cassian, who had kissed her once in the dark of a stairwell and said *go, go, I'll hold the door* as though holding doors were the only prayer he knew. Every part of her wanted to run back up into the smoke and find him. Her feet would not move. The dead would not let them.

"Listen," Ossuar said, and the chains dimmed, and the world narrowed to his voice. "I will offer you what I offered no one else, because you are the only one who ever asked me anything."

"I never asked you anything."

"You asked the drowned girl's mother why she wept when the bell rang. You asked the soldier's ghost if it hurt. You have been asking the dead questions your whole life, child. No one else does. They beg us. They use us. You *ask*." The bowed head lifted at last, and his face was a ruin of beauty — cracked ivory, eyes like wells with no bottom, black spreading from the chain-marks across his cheekbones. "So here is my answer, freely given. Walk away."

The word landed in the hollow like a dropped stone.

"The Veil holds another hundred years in me. Perhaps two. Aldric will drain me slower than he means to; greed is patient even when men are not. Take Cassian — yes, I know, I see everything the light of my own power touches — take him north past the war, take ship, take the silence you were promised. Keep your gift. Keep your hearing. Keep each other. Let the world burn slowly, the way it has always burned. You owe it nothing. It gave you nothing but fear."

And oh, the terrible thing was how much she wanted it. She stood in the warm pulsing dark and let herself want it — a house somewhere green, Cassian's laugh without gunfire under it, her hands carving bells for people who would never burn her for the hearing of them. She let herself want it for ten full heartbeats, and the dead around her waited, because the dead were very good at waiting.

Then she saw, in the well-dark of Ossuar's eyes, the drowned girl from Hollowmere. Tamsin Reeve, six years old, whose bell Mirabel had carved last spring. Tamsin, who had been dead eleven years and still could not tell her mother where she'd hidden before the ice took her. Tamsin, and behind her the purged villages of the borderlands, and behind them the mass graves of Aldric's first year, and behind them three centuries of the silenced stacked like cordwood beneath the whole weight of the world.

"No," Mirabel said.

Ossuar's shoulders sagged. Relief or grief; she could not tell which.

"Aldric offered me survival too," she said. "Silence, safety, a life. Everyone keeps offering me the same bargain wearing different clothes. But the dead aren't asking me to save them, are they? They're asking me to *witness* them. There's a difference." Her voice shook. She let it shake. "You're asking me to break the Veil. Not to free you. To free *them*. So they can speak at his trial. So every grave in this kingdom becomes a witness stand."

"Yes," said the god. "And you should know the price before you pay it. When the Veil breaks, everything it held breaks with it. My power returns to me — and returns to the world. Every gift sleeps in every bloodline wakes at once. The hunters' fire. The council's wards. Madness, miracle, all of it, everywhere, forever. And you—" the wells of his eyes fixed on her, gentle and merciless, "—you have heard the dead through bone and veil and stone your whole life. When the Veil falls, the full voice of the dead comes through unfiltered. Your gift cannot carry it. No mortal gift can. You will hear them one last time, all together, and then you will hear nothing again. Not them. Not anything they say. Silence, Mirabel Quintana. Real silence. For the rest of your life."

*Silence.* The thing she had feared since she was nine years old. The thing the valley had promised her and failed to give.

Cassian had told her once, on the road out of Hollowmere, that courage wasn't the absence of fear — that it was doing the arithmetic anyway, counting what you'd lose, and choosing. She hadn't understood him then. She understood him now, standing in a god's prison with her whole life balanced on the scale.

"How do I do it?" she asked.

Ossuar extended one chained hand. In the palm lay a splinter of ivory, black-veined, humming. "My bone. The shard the hunters carry is stolen from me; yours must be given. Strike the anchor-stone at my heart. One blow, truly meant. The Veil will do the rest — it is already dying, child. It has been dying since the day they chained me. You are only the mercy."

She took the splinter. It was warm, and it whispered against her skin in a language older than names.

Above, the sounds of battle changed pitch — closer, descending, boots on the stair. Someone screamed Cassian's name in a voice that was not Cassian's. Mirabel closed her hand around the shard until it bit.

"Tell them," she said to the dead crowding the tunnel mouth, "that I'm sorry I can't stay to listen."

* * *

She climbed toward the sound of fighting because the anchor-stone faced the stair, and because some stubborn animal part of her refused to die — or live — without seeing him once more.

She found the tunnel mouth a charnel house. Three hunters down, their armor split by a sword worked far past its breaking point. And Cassian against the wall at the turn of the stair, holding it the way he'd promised, one arm useless, the other still gripping a blade, blood sheeting his side where a hunter's hook had gone in and — she saw it with a clarity that would live in her forever — had not come cleanly out.

"Mira." He smiled. Blood in his teeth, and he smiled. "Told you. Door's held."

"Cassian—"

"Don't." He said it softly. "Whatever it is. Don't stop. I can hear them coming down the shaft. You have maybe a minute, and I intend to spend mine looking at you." His eyes were bright and steady and unbearably kind. "Do the arithmetic, remember? Count it. Then choose."

She counted. Him. Her hearing. The drowned girl's unfinished sentence. Three hundred years of graves. She counted all of it, and found that the sum had never actually been in question — that some part of her had chosen back in chapterless Hollowmere, the night she'd first let a dead man's bones speak through her hands.

"I love you," she said. It came out steady. She was glad of that. He deserved steady.

"I know," Cassian Wendell said. "Go be loud, Mira. Go make the whole world hear them."

Boots thundered above. He pushed off the wall to meet them.

Mirabel turned and walked into the bell-hollow, and knelt before the god, and set the black-veined splinter against the anchor-stone over his heart.

"For Tamsin Reeve," she said. "For all of them. *Truly meant.*"

She struck.

The world rang.

Not sound — she would learn later that no ear recorded it — but a tolling that passed through stone and flesh and time, and the chains of light shattered upward like glass thrown at heaven, and the Veil — three hundred years of mute sky — tore open from Ardenne to the sea. She felt the dead go through it, felt them pour past her like a river released from a dam, millions strong, streaming out across the land to the mouths of the living, to courtrooms and kitchens and the ears of kings. She felt Ossuar rise, unfurling, vast, his madness burning away like morning fog, his power flooding home into him and out into the world where every sleeping gift woke screaming and singing at once.

Far above, she felt — everyone felt — the crown's stolen fire gutter and die in Aldric's hands mid-battle, felt the witch-hunters' blades go cold, felt the tyrant stagger on his own battlements like a puppet with cut strings.

And she felt the dead's full voice come through her one last time, all of them together, a chord of gratitude and grief and naming — *Tamsin, Tamsin, thank you, thank you, thank you* —

—and then, between one heartbeat and the next, it was gone.

The hollow was silent. Truly silent. She could hear her own ragged breathing. Her own heartbeat. Nothing else. Nothing else, forever.

Mirabel knelt in the ringing dark with her hands over her ears, and wept — for what she had lost, and what she had freed, and for the man on the stairs who had bought her minute with his last one.

Somewhere above, the fighting stopped. Somewhere above, a kingdom woke to the sound of its own dead saying *we were here, we were here, we were here.*

And somewhere below, in the silence that would be hers for the rest of her life, Mirabel Quintana got to her feet, wiped her face, and began to climb — toward the stair, toward the bodies, toward whatever was left of the world and of Cassian Wendell, carrying the only thing she had left to give anyone:

her hands, and the truth they had witnessed.
