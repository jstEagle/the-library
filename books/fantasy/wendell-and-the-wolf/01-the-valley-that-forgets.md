## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink was the first thing I learned to love in this valley, and it will probably be the last thing I love when I leave — if I ever leave.

It's a strange thing to be fond of, ink. It stains. It ruins cuffs and fingernails and, if you're careless, whole pages of a temple ledger that took a scribe three days to rule and line. But ink is honest in a way nothing else in Ashvale is permitted to be. Ink says what it says and then dries and then stays said. You cannot take back ink. You can only write over it, and even then, if you hold the page up to the light, the old words ghost through like bones under skin.

I have been writing over my life for nine years now, and some mornings I can still see the bones.

"Halloran." Elder Vess stood at the end of my table with her hands folded into her sleeves, which was how she stood when she had bad news she intended to deliver as though it were weather. "The harvest tallies need copying before the equinox. All of them."

"All of them," I repeated. The harvest tallies ran to eleven volumes. The equinox was four days away.

"You wanted work," she said. "You asked for more hours against your debt."

I had asked for more hours eight months ago, when the winter had been long and the debt ledger had shown a number that made my chest hurt to look at. I had forgotten asking. Or perhaps I hadn't forgotten; perhaps I had simply filed it away in the part of myself where I kept all the small surrenders, the ones that added up to a life.

"I'll have them done," I said.

Vess nodded once, satisfied, and moved on down the scriptorium aisle, her shadow sliding over the other desks, the other bowed heads. Twelve of us worked the morning shift. Eleven of us were bound. The twelfth was old Brother Anselm, who had chosen Ashvale freely out of piety or cowardice — no one could agree which — and who therefore looked at the rest of us with a pity he thought we couldn't see.

We could see it. We saw everything here. That was the trouble with a valley that kept you: there was nowhere for your attention to go except inward, or sideways at each other.

I dipped my pen and began.

*Barley, north terraces, forty-one sacks.* My hand moved and my mind did what it always did during tally-copying, which was to leave. Not far. Just to the window, where the light came in slanted and gold through glass so old it had gone faintly green, and beyond the glass to the hills, and beyond the hills —

No. Not beyond the hills. There was no *beyond*. That was the whole point of Ashvale.

Every child raised here learns the lesson before they learn their letters. Stand at the border stones and try to walk out, and your legs will carry you exactly as far as the last stone and no farther. Not because anything stops you — there is no wall, no fence, no guard posted on the ridgeline. You simply find, somewhere around the stones, that you have turned around. You decide you've forgotten something at home. Your bootlace has come undone. The road looks longer than you remembered. And you are walking back into the valley before you have noticed that a decision was ever made.

The elders call it the Quiet. They say the valley is blessed, that the world outside is full of dangerous wonders from which our founders mercifully shielded us. They say magic cannot follow us here, and they say it the way you'd say *the well water is clean*, as a comfort.

They never explain why a blessing needs to keep people in.

*Wheat, mill fields, sixty-two sacks, less tithe.* I wrote. Outside, October was doing its slow gold work on the birches along the temple wall, and somewhere past those trees, Wendell was doing whatever Wendell did with his wolves, which was mostly nothing, mostly standing very still in a way that made the nothing look deliberate.

I did not think about Wendell. I want that on the record, in ink, unerasable: I did not think about him. He simply arrived in my thoughts the way weather arrives, without permission and without any particular meaning, and I had stopped, years ago, trying to bar the door against him.

*Forty-one sacks,* I corrected, because I had written forty-seven, because my mind had been elsewhere, because it was always elsewhere, and the page knew it even if Elder Vess didn't.

That was the other thing about ink. It caught you in your wandering. It made a map of everywhere you'd drifted.

* * *

At midday I carried the finished volumes to the archive, which was my favorite errand and the closest thing I had to a vice.

The archive occupied the temple's oldest wing, a low stone hall where the air tasted of dust and tallow and time. The shelves went back further than the temple's own records. Nobody knew how far back. The deepest racks held ledgers bound in leather gone stiff and dark as old blood, their spines lettered in a hand older than any living elder, and when I shelved books among them I always had the feeling of placing my small neat life inside something enormous and sleeping.

Debt was the reason I was here, and debt was the reason most of us were here, and the archive was where debt lived when it wasn't living in us.

My father had owed the temple. That was the plain shape of it. A bad harvest, then a worse one, then a loan against the house, then a winter when my mother coughed blood and the temple's physician came with medicines that worked and a bill that didn't care whether anyone lived or died. When my parents died within a year of each other — her first, him following like a man walking after a lantern down a dark road — the debt passed to me, as debts do, as surely as eye color. Nine years of service. I had six remaining.

Six years. Two thousand one hundred and ninety days, give or take the temple's generous policy of counting feast days twice.

I knew the number the way you know your own heartbeat. Some days it felt like a sentence. Other days — and these were the days I distrusted myself — it felt like a spine. Something to hang a life on. Here is your desk. Here is your lamp. Here is your supper at sixth bell and your bed at ninth and your Sunday walk along the river path, the same walk, the same river, the same path, until the repetition wears grooves in you deep enough to pour a person into.

Contentment, Elder Vess called it. She said it approvingly, watching us at our work, watching the valley keep its people soft and settled and quiet.

I copied tallies and told myself she was right.

* * *

The afternoon light had gone long and amber when Brother Anselm found me in the archive and asked me to run a message down to the wolf-keeper's cottage.

"He won't come to bell," Anselm said, apologetic. "He never comes to bell. But the equinox stores need his count of hides and tallow, and you've the quickest feet."

I had the quickest feet. I also suspected — though I had never once said it aloud, not even to myself in the dark — that Anselm sent me on purpose sometimes, the way you might toss a scrap to a dog you liked, and I resented him for it and went anyway, every time, at nearly a run.

The path to the wolf-keeper's cottage left the temple grounds through the orchard gate and climbed along the river before bending toward the high pasture where the border stones stood. Everyone in Ashvale knew where Wendell lived, and everyone gave the place a respectful berth, partly because of the wolves and partly because of Wendell, who was the kind of quiet that people mistake for emptiness. He had come to the valley — so the story went — some twelve years ago, a boy of fifteen or sixteen, half-starved, with no name anyone recognized and no family anyone could find. Old Keeper Bram had taken him in, taught him the wolves, and died and left him the cottage and the pack and the valley's collective shrug. No debt ledger held him. He was the only adult in Ashvale who belonged to no one, and I think the rest of us found that vaguely obscene, the way a bird finds an open cage obscene.

The wolves saw me before I saw them. That was always how it went. One moment the hillside was empty gold grass and the next there were five grey shapes arranged along the slope above the cottage, watching me with the patient attention of creditors.

"Hallo, Fen," I said to the largest, because Fen always came nearest, and because talking to wolves was either madness or perfectly reasonable depending on how many years you'd lived in a valley that talked to nothing else. Fen's ears tipped forward. Her tail moved once, a metronome's worth of welcome.

And there was Wendell, down by the woodpile with his sleeves rolled and his dark hair pushed back off his forehead, splitting logs with the unhurried economy of a man who had done ten thousand of them and expected to do ten thousand more.

"Mara," he said, before I'd said anything. He always knew it was me. I had tested this once, coming silently along the river path with the wind in my favor, and he had still said my name to the trees before I rounded the bend. I had decided not to wonder about it. Wondering about Wendell was a door I kept painted shut.

"Anselm wants your counts for the equinox stores. Hides, tallow, the smoked fish if you've any left after feeding that lot." I gestured at the wolves, who received the reference with dignity.

"They'll have fish." He set the axe head-down against the block and wiped his palms on his thighs, and I looked at the woodpile instead of at his forearms, because I was twenty-five years old and a temple scribe and I had my grooves, my ruts, my careful small life, and none of it had room for the way the low sun came through his hair.

"The counts are on the shelf," he said. "Come in while I fetch them, unless—" and here he paused, and the pause had a strange quality, like a man testing ice, "—unless you're needed back."

"I'm never needed back," I said, and heard how it sounded, and laughed, and hated the laugh a little. "I'm needed exactly here, forever, copying numbers until I die and someone copies my number into a ledger."

Something crossed his face when I said it. Quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. If I'd had to name it I'd have said it looked like pain, but that made no sense, so I named it nothing.

Inside, the cottage smelled of smoke and pine and wolf and, beneath it, something I had never been able to identify — a faint cold smell, like the air off a cellar, like winter kept in a jar. I used to think it was the stone floor. I no longer bothered thinking about it at all.

While he gathered the counts from the shelf, I drifted, as I always drifted, to the window. From here you could see clear up the valley's throat to the ridge where the border stones stood in their ancient row, pale against the darkening pines, each one taller than a man and carved all over with letters in no alphabet the temple taught.

Wendell was looking at them too. I realized it gradually, the way you realize a room has gone silent. He had stopped moving entirely. The counts hung loose in his hand. And he was watching the stones — no, not watching. *Listening.* His head was tilted, just slightly, the way Fen tilted hers at sounds I couldn't hear, and his eyes had gone somewhere I couldn't follow, up and over and *beyond*, to wherever the stones stood guard against.

"Wendell?"

He came back the way a man surfaces from deep water. Blinking. Breathing differently than before.

"The stones," I said. "What are they? Really. Everyone says blessing, everyone says protection, but nobody will say who carved them or what for, and you look at them like—"

"Like what?" His voice was even. His hands, setting the counts on the table between us, were not quite.

"Like you're waiting for them to answer."

For a moment I thought he would tell me something. There was a whole conversation standing behind his eyes, pressing at the door. I watched him weigh it. I watched him — and this is the part I have thought about every day since, though I didn't know yet that I would — I watched him choose the valley's silence over whatever truth he carried, and fold it away, and smile at me, a real smile, warm and tired and closed.

"They're just old rocks, Mara," he said. "Older than the temple. Older than the debt. Everything here is older than it lets on."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Fen pushed open the door — she had a genius for latches — and leaned her whole grey weight against my leg, and the moment broke the way moments do in Ashvale, quietly, without witnesses, into the ordinary. Wendell walked me as far as the orchard gate because he always did, and the wolves escorted us because they always did, and at the gate he said, "Mind the frost tonight," because he always said something practical instead of whatever else was in him, and I said, "Bring the counts to bell tomorrow yourself, for once," because I always tried, and he said, "Maybe," because he never would.

I walked back down through the orchard with the counts under my arm and the last of the light going copper in the grass, and I did the arithmetic I always did, the one that wasn't about barley.

Six years left on my debt. Six years of this valley, this desk, this river path, this contentment that fit me like a coat cut for someone else. And on the hill behind me, a man who listened to border stones, keeping wolves that watched the ridgeline the way sailors watch weather, saying *maybe* in a voice that meant *never*.

I told myself it was enough. I told myself I was lucky — fed, housed, useful, safe inside the Quiet where nothing dangerous could reach me.

I believed it right up until the night the royal proclamation came, riding under seal, into a valley that was supposed to be beyond the crown's reach.

But that was two days off yet, and the evening was soft, and the first stars were coming out over the stones, and I went in to supper and ate it and slept, carefully, in my careful small life, while somewhere on the high pasture a wolf lifted her head and stared for a long time at the pass where the world ended — and whined, once, low, like a warning nobody was awake to hear.
