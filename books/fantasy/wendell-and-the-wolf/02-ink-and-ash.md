## 2. Ink and Ash

The proclamation came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Catastrophe, in Mara's experience, should at least have the courtesy to arrive on a day with some weight to it — a solstice, a funeral, the first frost. Instead it arrived on a gray Tuesday in late autumn, carried by a courier whose horse had sweated through its lather on the last mile of the mountain road, and it was delivered not to the temple elders with any ceremony but to Mara herself, because she was the one who answered the gate.

"From the Crown," the courier said, and held out a scroll heavy enough that her wrist dipped under it.

The seal was black wax pressed with a mountain split by a sword. Mara had seen that seal before, once, years ago, on the document that bound her father's debts to the temple after he died — the paper that had turned her from a daughter into an asset. She took the scroll without a word. The courier looked at her expectantly, and she realized he wanted a receipt.

"I'll have the archivist sign for it," she said.

"He's to sign *and* mark his office." The courier produced a second, smaller paper. "The proclamation is to be read aloud within three days, posted at every gate, and a copy of the acknowledgment returned to the provincial seat at Grayhollow. There's a fine attached if it isn't."

"A fine against whom?"

"The temple." He shrugged, already turning his horse. "Everything's a fine against somebody."

She watched him go down the valley road until the border stones swallowed him, those pale standing rocks that marked the edge of the only world she'd ever known. Beyond them the land rose toward the pass, and beyond the pass was everything else: Grayhollow, the provincial courts, the crown, the war she only knew from ledger entries about requisitioned grain. She stood in the gate a moment longer than she needed to, the scroll cold against her palms, and then she went inside to do what she always did with dangerous paper.

She copied it.

* * *

The scriptorium occupied the temple's east wing, a long room of high narrow windows where the light came down slanted and gold in the mornings and went gray by noon. Forty desks, most of them empty now; the temple's copying business had shrunk along with everything else. Mara had the desk nearest the stove, a privilege of seniority she'd earned over nine years of flawless work, and she sat down at it with the proclamation and broke the seal with a bone folder, the way she'd been taught — carefully, so the wax could be re-melted and the seal reused if the elders wished to send it on.

The hand was a court hand, formal and cramped:

*By order of His Majesty's Regency Council, be it known throughout the provinces and vales: that all persons possessed of the forbidden gift, howsoever named or disguised, being sorcerers, hedge-witches, charm-bearers, soul-speakers, and others of that abominable kind, are hereby declared outlaw. Any such person found within the King's dominions shall be taken, tried, or destroyed. Further: any person knowing of a gifted one and concealing them shall share their outlawry. Further still: any community, abbey, temple, or household surrendering such persons to the Crown's hunters shall have remission of debts owed to the Crown, at a rate of fifty crowns per head surrendered, one hundred for a soul-speaker.*

Mara read it twice. The first time her eyes moved over the words the way they moved over any text, smooth and unattached, a scribe's reading. The second time the words caught on something.

*Remission of debts.*

She set down the folder. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Around her the scriptorium was quiet except for the scratch of old Brother Anselm two desks away, copying funerary verses for a farmer's wife, and the tick of the stove. Outside the window, far down the slope, she could see the wolf pens, and a figure moving among them with the unhurried gait she could have picked out of a crowd of thousands.

Wendell was mucking the winter pen. He did not know yet. Nobody knew yet. The ink was still wet on the only copy in the valley, and it was sitting in front of her, and she was the one holding it.

There was a rule in the scriptorium, older than her: *the scribe is the first reader and must be the last to act.* Whatever a text said, however it burned, you copied it true and passed it up the chain and let your betters decide what it meant. She had lived by that rule for nine years. It was a good rule. It kept small people safe.

She pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began the fair copy the elders would require, and her hand did not shake, and that frightened her more than anything else.

* * *

The elders convened that evening in the chapter house, and because the proclamation concerned temple administration, Mara was summoned to take minutes. This was normal. This was her whole life, arranged into rows: minutes, ledgers, fair copies, receipts. She sat at the little side desk with her ink pot and her quill and looked at the seven faces around the table, lit by lamps, and waited.

Elder Cassian had the scroll open in front of him. He was the temple's provost of accounts, a dry man with dry hands, and he read the relevant passage aloud in a voice like someone reading a price list, which, Mara thought, was exactly what it was.

"*Fifty crowns per head surrendered, one hundred for a soul-speaker.*"

He let it hang there. Around the table, the elders exchanged looks — not horror, Mara noticed. Arithmetic.

"The valley's debt to the Crown stands at four thousand crowns," Cassian said. "It has stood there since the flood year, when we borrowed to rebuild the mill and the west terraces. We have paid interest on it for twenty-two years. Interest alone consumes a third of our tithes." He tapped the scroll. "This is a door out."

"Through what?" Elder Serafina asked. She was the eldest, nearly blind, her voice thin. "Through people?"

"Through *outlaws*, Serafina. Through persons the Crown has already condemned. We would not be judging anyone. We would be complying with the law of the land, which we are obliged to do regardless, and being compensated for the trouble of compliance." Cassian folded his dry hands. "I put it to the chapter: do we know of any gifted persons in this valley?"

Silence. Mara's quill hovered over the page. In the silence she heard the stove tick, and a dog barking somewhere down in the village, and her own heart, which had gone strange and loud.

"There was the Hedley girl," Elder Tomas offered at last, cautiously. "Years back. Fits, they said. She left."

"She left," Serafina agreed, "and good riddance to the question. Are we to go door to door now, asking our neighbors if they whisper to animals? If their children float crockery? This valley has been magic-dead since before my grandmother. Nothing gifted can live here. That is the mercy of this place, and the reason our founders chose it."

"That was true when the law was indifferent," Cassian said. "The law is no longer indifferent. Hunters are being dispatched to every province. Do you imagine they will skip us? A debt-bound temple, deep in arrears, harboring — as far as any auditor will know — who knows what?" He spread his hands. "Better we make our own list. Better we present it ourselves, with the acknowledgment, and claim the remission before some hunter claims it for us and leaves us the debt besides."

"And who would be on this list?" Serafina asked.

Another silence, longer. Mara wrote the date at the top of her minutes and the word *Present:* and the seven names, and her hand was very steady, and she hated it.

"No one, presumably," said Elder Ivo, the youngest, uncomfortable. "No one here is gifted. That's rather the point of the valley."

"Then the list will be short," Cassian said, "and the remission will be nothing, and the hunters will come anyway, because the Crown does not distinguish between a temple with no gifted persons and a temple that failed to look." He looked around the table. "We will compile a register. Every soul in the valley, village and temple alike. Names, households, histories. Anything irregular. Anything whispered. We submit it to Grayhollow with our acknowledgment, and we let the Crown's own assessors decide who is worth a hundred crowns and who is worth nothing. That is not betrayal. That is diligence."

Nobody spoke against it. Serafina bowed her head, which might have been assent or surrender. Ivo stared at the table. And Mara wrote, in her beautiful, trained, worthless hand: *The chapter resolves to compile a full register of the valley's inhabitants, to be submitted with the acknowledgment of the proclamation.*

Complicity, she thought, has very tidy handwriting.

* * *

They gave the register to her, of course. Who else? She was the best scribe; the register had to be beautiful, because it was going to Grayhollow, because it was going to be *judged*. For six days she worked on it while the elders collected material — household by household, the village reeve answering questions in the square while neighbors listened from doorways and stopped visiting each other. She watched the valley change in a week. People who had lived shoulder to shoulder for generations began to weigh each other. She saw it in the market: a pause before an answer, a laugh that came half a beat too late. Fifty crowns a head. Everyone now had a price, and everyone knew everyone else's.

On the sixth day, Cassian brought her the final pages of source notes — the irregulars, the whispers, the things the reeve had been too polite to say aloud in the square.

"Copy these into the annex," he said. "Cleanly. No editorializing."

Mara untied the string and looked down the list. Most of it was nothing — a woman who cured warts with spit and scripture, a boy who claimed his dreams came true and was almost certainly a liar, an old man who talked to bees. Entries like that made her almost fond of her neighbors. Almost.

Then she reached the third page, and the room went very quiet, though of course it had been quiet all along.

*Wendell, keeper of the temple dogs and wolves. Household: none. History: foundling, raised at the temple, no kin of record. Irregularities: keeps wolves beyond need or custom; the beasts obey him as no beast obeys a man. Was seen at the border stones on the night of the autumn equinox, standing among them past midnight, and the dogs would not come when called. The reeve's wife says the wolves watch him sleep. Note: foundlings without kin cannot prove their blood. Recommend assessment.*

*Recommend assessment.* Four words that would bring a hunter up the mountain road with his hounds and his instruments, and at the end of it either a hundred crowns of remission or a pyre, depending on what the assessment found, and everyone involved understood that the hundred crowns were the likelier finding, because assessments found what paid.

Mara read it three times. Then she looked up at Cassian, and her voice, when it came, was the voice she used for confirming deliveries of parchment.

"This entry is hearsay."

"It's testimony."

"It's the reeve's wife's opinion of wolves. Wolves watch everyone. It's what wolves do."

Cassian regarded her with mild, dry patience. "Scribe Halloran. You copied the proclamation yourself. You know what concealment carries now. Are you telling me you wish your name noted as objecting to the annex?"

The threat was gentle and complete. Object, and be listed. Be listed, and be assessed. She thought of saying it anyway — the sentence was right there, fully formed, *yes, note my objection* — and she thought of her father's debt, of the paper in the archive with her name bound to this valley until it was paid, of the fact that a listed woman could not pay anything from a pyre.

"No," she said. "I'll copy it cleanly."

"Good." He gathered his papers, then paused at the door, and for a moment something almost human crossed the dry landscape of his face. "For what it's worth, I hope the fellow's dull as a post. But hope isn't a method, Mara. Diligence is. See it done by Friday."

She saw it done by Friday.

That was the part she would carry afterward, through everything that came — the flight, the mountain, all of it. Not the fear. The ease. On Friday morning she sat at her desk by the stove and made the fair copy of the annex in her finest hand, the hand she'd spent nine years perfecting, and Wendell's name came off her nib in black and gold illumination, *Wendell, keeper of wolves*, with a small painted vine curling around the initial letter because the register was going to Grayhollow and Grayhollow expected beauty. She illuminated his name the way you illuminate a saint's. She could not stop her hands. They were good hands. They did what they were told.

When it was finished she blotted it, rolled it, sealed it with the temple's stamp, and set it in the dispatch basket for the Tuesday courier.

Then she sat in the empty scriptorium for a long time, listening to the stove tick, doing the arithmetic Cassian hadn't said aloud. One hundred crowns for a soul-speaker. Four thousand crowns of debt. Forty names' worth of forgiveness, if the valley had forty gifted souls hidden in it, and if it didn't, then one name would have to be stretched very far indeed.

Her name was on the register too. Of course it was. Everyone's was. *Mara Halloran, scribe, bound by debt, household: none, irregularities: none.* None. She had spent nine years making sure of it. She had filed herself into this valley like a document into an archive, and now the archive was being read by the Crown, and there was nowhere in it that wasn't written down.

Except — she thought, staring at the dispatch basket — except that registers could burn. Ledgers could burn. She had seen the archive fire of '09 in the old records; she knew exactly how fast vellum goes, how a single lamp knocked from a desk could erase forty years of debt in an afternoon. She knew, better than anyone alive, how much of a person's life was just paper, and how paper was only ever waiting for a flame.

The basket sat by the door. The courier came Tuesdays.

Wendell was down in the pens, and the light was going gold, and one of the grey wolves was standing at the fence line looking up at the scriptorium window — not at the window, she realized, with a small cold shock, but at *her*, ears forward, utterly still, as if it had been sent.

As if something beyond the border stones had finally started listening back.

Mara rose from her desk. She did not let herself think past the next hour; thinking further was a luxury for people whose names weren't drying in gold leaf on a list bound for Grayhollow. She took the register from the basket, and the acknowledgment, and the elder's signed copy of the proclamation, and she put them inside her coat, against her heart, where paper goes when it has become either a weapon or a wound.

Down in the valley, the first snow of the season was beginning to fall — early, too early, weeks before it had ever come in her lifetime — and it fell without wind, straight down, patient as ash.
