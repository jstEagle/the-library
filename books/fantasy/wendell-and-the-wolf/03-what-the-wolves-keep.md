## 3. What the Wolves Keep

The wolves told him first.

Not in words — wolves don't waste breath on what a man can smell for himself. It was the way they stood that morning, all nine of them along the eastern ridge, facing the border stones with their ears pricked and their tails low and still. Not hunting-still. Waiting-still. The stillness of animals who have heard a door open somewhere far away and are counting the footsteps.

Wendell set down the milk pail and looked at the ridge, and his stomach went cold in the warm morning light.

"Bracken," he said.

The oldest of them, a grey-muzzled female with one clouded eye, broke from the line and came down the slope to him at an easy trot. She sat at his feet the way she had sat at his feet for eleven years, since she was a pup he'd pulled half-drowned out of the flooded quarry, and she looked up at him with her good eye as if to say: *You felt it too.*

"I felt it," he said.

That was the trouble with this valley. That was the whole cursed mercy of it. Magic could not follow a man past the border stones — couldn't find him, couldn't touch him, couldn't read the gift burning quiet in his chest like a banked fire. For six years the valley's dead air had been the only cloak in the world that fit him. But a cloak works both ways. In here, Wendell couldn't feel the gift either. He was a man standing in a room where his own heart made no sound, trusting it beat only because he woke each morning.

And so the valley kept its secrets badly. He had learned to listen through other things. Through the wolves, whose blood remembered wildness even born inside these stones. Through the way frost formed on the north fence — straight or crooked, depending on what moved beyond the borders. Through the ache in his own scarred hands when weather turned wrong somewhere out in the wide gifted world.

This morning, everything said the same thing: *they're coming.*

* * *

He went about the chores because chores were the shape his hiding took. Feed the wolves. Muck the pens. Mend the fence line along the temple's western field where the sheep got out every spring no matter how many knots he tied. The temple scribes called him the wolf-keeper the way you'd call a man a well-keeper or a wall-keeper — something useful, something that belonged to the place, something nobody looked at twice. He'd spent six years teaching himself to be furniture. It was the hardest work he'd ever done, harder than the theft, harder than the running, harder than the night he'd crossed the border stones with his sister's soul wrapped in glass against his heart and the mountain's hounds singing behind him.

Six years of being no one. And it had almost worked.

Almost.

Because there was one person in the valley who looked at him like he was someone, and that was its own kind of danger.

Mara Halloran came down to the pens on market days, ostensibly to record the temple's livestock counts, actually because she liked the wolves and pretended not to. She'd stand at the fence with her ledger and her ink-stained fingers and ask questions she already knew the answers to, and Bracken would lean against the rails to be scratched, betraying eleven years of dignity, and Wendell would answer in short sentences and try not to watch the way sunlight caught the loose hair at her temple.

She thought he was a quiet man. She thought quiet meant simple. Everyone in the valley did, and he had built that misunderstanding plank by plank, year by year, the way you build anything worth having.

But last month she had caught him watching the border stones. He'd felt her eyes on his back before he heard her boots on the path, and he'd turned, and she'd been looking at him with that sharp scribe's attention — the look of a woman reading a page everyone else had skimmed.

"You watch the stones," she'd said. "Every week. Like you're listening."

"Fence lines need walking," he'd said.

"That's not a fence line."

"No," he'd agreed, because lying to her outright had never once worked, "it isn't."

She hadn't pushed. That was the thing about Mara — she collected facts the way he collected wolf-trust, patiently, without forcing, and she kept them. He'd seen her ledger once, by accident. Her handwriting was small and exact, and in the margins, in the same careful hand, she noted things that didn't belong in any official count. Which elder lied about the grain stores. Which pilgrim paid his debt in stories instead of coin. What the wind smelled like the day the frost came crooked.

If anyone in this valley was going to see through him, it would be her. If anyone in this valley would burn for seeing through him, it would also be her.

He should have run years ago. He knew that. He'd known it every single day, and every single day the knowing had lost to the looking — to the sound of her voice reading accounts aloud in the temple yard, to the bread she left on the pen gate post some mornings without a word, to the fact that when she laughed at something Bracken did, something in his chest that had been silent since the mountain unclenched, just slightly, just enough to hurt.

Love, in a magic-dead valley, is the closest thing to magic a man can carry. Maybe that's why he'd stayed. Maybe that's why he'd been fool enough to believe the valley could keep two secrets instead of one.

* * *

That night he went down into the cellar under the pens, the one the temple believed held turnips, and knelt before the false floor.

The vial came up out of the straw-lined hollow the way it always did: heavier than glass had any right to be, cold as a winter river, humming against his palms with a frequency he felt in his teeth rather than heard. Six years in dead air and it still sang. Whatever was in it didn't care about border stones. Souls were older than the valley's silence. Older than the court that had stolen it. Older, maybe, than the mountain itself.

He held it up to the lantern light.

Inside, the glow shifted — blue-white, restless, alive. His sister's soul. Sefryn's soul. Taken by the court beneath the mountain as payment for his own worthless life, the night they came for the gifted children of his village and his mother traded everything she had and it wasn't enough, it was never going to be enough, so Wendell — seventeen, furious, gifted in ways the crown's law named treason — had done the one thing no one had ever done.

He'd stolen it back.

Four years planning. Three nights in the under-mountain dark. One vial. And then four more years of running, until the valley swallowed him and the running stopped and the guilt started, because a stolen soul is not a saved soul. Sefryn was still in the court's keeping — the rest of her, the body of her, hollowed and gentle and wearing whatever borrowed memories the Regent dressed her in. He'd gotten the flame away from their hearth and left the house standing empty. Some rescues are just thefts with better intentions.

He'd told himself, all these years, that he was waiting. Building strength. Learning the roads. Biding. All the words men use for being afraid.

The truth was simpler and worse: the road to the mountain ran through the gifted world, where his gift burned like a beacon and the court's hunters read beacons at a hundred miles. Alone, he'd have maybe three days before they caught his light. Alone, he'd die in some ditch with his sister's soul in his fist, and the vial would go back to the Regent, and everything — the theft, the running, the six gray years of being furniture — would have bought nothing at all.

So he'd stayed. Guarding a fire he couldn't let go out and couldn't afford to light.

He wrapped the vial back in wool, lowered it into the hollow, and replaced the floorboards, and knelt there a moment in the dark with his forehead nearly touching the boards, the way he'd seen pilgrims kneel at the temple rail. He wasn't praying. He'd stopped believing anyone was listening around the time he learned what the court paid for souls. But kneeling was a habit of grief, and grief was the one companion that had made the whole journey with him.

* * *

Three days later, the frost came down crooked on the north fence, and Wendell started packing.

He traveled light. He'd learned light. A knife, flint, dried meat, the wool-wrapped weight that rode against his sternum where another man might wear a locket. He wrote no letter, because letters are evidence, and because there was no one to write to — that was the price of being furniture, and he paid it now in full, standing in his empty room above the pens, understanding that he had spent six years making himself a man whom no one would miss, and he had succeeded completely, and it tasted like ash.

Except.

There was bread on the gate post that morning. Still warm. Mara's doing; hers was the only hand in the valley that baked with caraway, and the smell of it hit him like a fist and he stood there in the cold with his pack over his shoulder and let himself, for ten full seconds, imagine a different life. A life where he was just a wolf-keeper. Where the worst secret he kept was that he liked her laugh more than he liked his own solitude. Where he walked up to the temple scriptorium and asked her, plainly, the question he'd been not-asking for six years.

Then Bracken howled from the ridge — the long, flat note that meant *strangers* — and the ten seconds ended.

They were coming faster than the frost had promised. Riders, several, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been refused entry anywhere. And with them, faint even across the dead air of the valley, something that made the wolves' hackles rise and made Wendell's scarred hands ache down to the bone.

A hound. A soul-hound. Dead air or no dead air, the crown had finally bred something patient enough to walk up to the border stones and wait for the scent to drift out — and souls, unlike gifts, leaked. His had been leaking for six years, a slow bright thread under the temple's nose, and somewhere out there a beast bred in the mountain's kennels had caught the end of it and begun, patiently, to reel.

They hadn't come for a name on a list. Lists were for ordinary fugitives. They'd come for the leak.

He should go now. Tonight. Over the western wall where the stones were old and cracked, out into the gifted dark, alone, three days of beacon-bright running, and may the roads be kinder than his odds.

He stood at the gate with the bread in his hand and the pack on his shoulder and the wolves coming down off the ridge one by one to sit in a silent crescent around him, nine pairs of eyes reflecting the last of the daylight, waiting to see what he would do.

Bracken leaned against his leg.

"Don't," he told her quietly. "Whatever happens — don't follow me. You're valley-born. Past those stones, the world will eat you."

She looked up at him with her one good eye, and he could have sworn she was laughing at him.

He broke the bread in half and ate his share standing up, and left the other half on the post for a woman who deserved better than a lie six years long, and turned toward the western wall —

— and saw, far across the fields, dust rising on the eastern road.

Riders. Already through the outer markers. Coming on at a canter, unhurried, certain, with something pale and lean loping beside the lead horse that was no dog born of any valley kennel.

They weren't coming tomorrow. They were coming now.

And down the path from the temple, ink-stained and oblivious, ledger under her arm, Mara Halloran was walking straight toward the pens — toward him, toward the hounds, toward the whole unraveling — with the evening sun behind her and no idea at all that the life she'd been so carefully copying out was about to be blotted from the page.
