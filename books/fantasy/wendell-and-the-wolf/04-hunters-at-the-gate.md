## 4. Hunters at the Gate

They came on a morning so ordinary it felt like an insult.

Mara was at her desk in the scriptorium, grinding ink, when the bells began — not the temple's soft bronze calling to prayer, but the iron bell above the gatehouse, rung only for officials and funerals. She set down the pestle. Around her, the other scribes lifted their heads like startled birds.

"Royal party," said Brother Anselm from the doorway, and there was something in his voice she had never heard before. Something eager.

She followed the others down into the courtyard, where the elders were already assembling in their best robes, arranging their faces into welcome. Beyond them, through the open gate, she saw the riders.

Six of them, in grey-and-crimson livery, and between two of the horses walked a hound on a silver chain.

It was not a large animal. That was the first wrong thing about it. It moved like water poured uphill, smooth and silent, and its coat was the color of old snow. It wore no collar but the chain, which ran not to its neck but to a ring around its own foreleg, as if it had been fitted with its leash the way one fits a shackle. Its head swung slowly across the courtyard as it came, nostrils working, and every person it passed flinched without knowing why.

Mara understood why a heartbeat later. The hound wasn't smelling for anything. It was smelling *in* everything — sorting the crowd the way she sorted ledgers, line by line, looking for the entry that didn't balance.

*Looking for magic,* she thought, and the thought arrived cold and complete, though she couldn't have said where she'd learned it. The valley had no magic. That was the whole point of the valley. The border stones drank it out of the air like lime drawing poison from a wound. Nothing gifted could live here; nothing gifted could even *find* here.

So why bring a soul-hound through the gate?

The answer came standing beside her in the crowd, quiet as breath: because something in this valley had hidden from that hound before. Because someone believed the valley's emptiness was not emptiness at all, but cover.

Her eyes went, before she could stop them, to the wolf pens at the eastern edge of the temple grounds.

Wendell stood among his wolves the way he always did, still as a fencepost, and if he felt the hound's attention sweeping toward him he gave no sign. But one of the wolves — the big grey female he called Sorrel — had gone rigid, ears flat, staring at the gate with her lips peeled back from her teeth in a silent snarl.

The lead hunter dismounted. He was younger than Mara expected, with a courtier's neat beard and a soldier's way of planting his boots, and when he spoke to Elder Maren his voice carried the particular courtesy of a man who has never once needed to raise it.

"Honored mothers and fathers of the valley. By decree of the Crown, all persons gifted are to be surrendered for assessment." He unrolled a scroll with a snap. "You will have received the proclamation."

"We received it," Elder Maren said smoothly. "And we have prepared accordingly."

*Prepared.* The word landed in Mara's stomach like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of the list she had copied three days ago — the roster of temple residents, names and duties and years of service, compiled at the elders' request for what Brother Anselm had called *a routine accounting*. She had written every name herself in her careful, even hand. Her own name at the bottom, under *scribe*.

She hadn't asked what the accounting was for. She hadn't wanted to know. That was the shape of her whole life in the valley: don't ask, keep your head down, pay your debt page by page until the ledger released you. Contentment as a discipline. Longing folded up small enough to fit in a drawer.

Now the drawer was being opened in front of everyone.

"The temple keeps meticulous records," Elder Maren was saying. "We hold nothing back from the Crown. Indeed, we consider cooperation our sacred duty."

"I'm sure you do," the hunter said, and smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Bring out your people. The hound will confirm your records."

They formed the residents into lines in the courtyard, and the hound walked the rows.

It took a long time. Mara stood in her place, hands folded, watching the animal work. It paused before no one. A baker's boy. Two elderly sisters who kept the candle stores. Brother Anselm, who bowed to it, absurdly, and got nothing but a long yellow stare. The hound's handler watched its ears, its tail, some private grammar of its body, and after each pass shook his head almost imperceptibly.

Halfway down the third row, the hound stopped.

It didn't growl. It didn't bark. It simply sat, all at once, like a verdict delivered, and turned its head toward the eastern wall — toward the wolf pens, beyond the courtyard, beyond the line of residents, toward a man who wasn't even present.

Every head in the courtyard turned the same direction.

"No," Mara breathed. It came out loud enough that the woman next to her glanced over. *No, no, he's not there, he's never there, the valley doesn't allow it—*

But the hound was already rising, already pulling its handler toward the wall, and Elder Maren's face had done something terrible and small: a flicker of satisfaction, quickly buried, like a coin slipped into a sleeve.

*They knew.* The certainty arrived whole. *They put his name on the list. They sent the accounting to the Crown. They've been waiting weeks for this.*

Because the temple owed debts too. Everyone in the valley owed debts — that was how the valley worked, a place where the desperate came to be forgotten, and forgetting was never free. And what better payment than a name? What better trade than handing the Crown exactly what it hungered for, in exchange for forgiveness of a few years' obligation?

Elder Maren caught Mara looking at her and smoothed her expression into benevolence. "Child," she said gently, "you're trembling. Go inside. This is temple business now."

*Temple business.* Wendell was temple business. He had been for eleven years — the quiet man who'd come up from the lowlands with no history and no debt anyone would name aloud, who asked only for the wolf pens and leave to be left alone. Who fixed the scriptorium roof without being asked. Who once, three winters ago, had stood beside her in the snow at the border stones and said, out of nowhere, *Do you ever wonder what color is like, past them?* and looked at her as if her answer mattered more than any scripture he'd never read.

She did not go inside.

* * *

They brought him out at midday.

Mara watched from the arcade above the courtyard, pressed against a pillar, her heart going like a fist against a door. Four hunters had gone to the pens and come back with Wendell walking between them, wrists bound in iron-wire that hummed faintly — she could hear it from thirty feet away, a thin mosquito whine that made her teeth ache. His wolves had not fought. Someone had locked them in the far pen; Sorrel was throwing herself against the gate hard enough to rattle the hinges, and the sound was the only noise in the courtyard besides the wind.

Wendell's face was calm. That was the worst part. He scanned the crowd once, unhurried, the way he checked the pens at dusk — counting, confirming, letting go — and when his eyes found Mara in the arcade they stopped.

He didn't look afraid for himself. He looked afraid *for her*, and then he looked away deliberately, and she understood: *don't. Don't do anything. Be small. Survive.*

Eleven years of being small rose up in her throat like bile.

The lead hunter circled Wendell slowly. "No name in our rolls," he said. "No birth record. No debt registered with the temple — which is itself remarkable, given that everyone in this valley owes somebody something." He smiled at Elder Maren. "Your records are very thorough, honored mother. Except where they need to be."

"He has been a faithful servant," Elder Maren said. "His gift was unknown to us until your arrival made examination necessary."

*Liar.* Mara's nails bit into her palm. The elders had known. Had always known — you didn't hide a wolf-keeper's silence for a decade without choosing to. They had simply decided, sometime in the last month, that his name was worth more than his labor.

The hunter produced a small glass vial from his coat, empty, stoppered with wax. "Standard procedure," he said. "The hound confirms the gift. The vial carries a sample of the soul to the assessors. If you're innocent, you'll be returned within the season." He said it the way men say things they have never seen happen. "Hold out your hand."

Wendell held out his hand.

And Mara moved.

She didn't decide to. Later she would search that moment for a decision and find none — just her body already in motion, down the arcade stairs, across the courtyard, the crowd parting around her because she was moving with such purpose that no one thought to stop her. She was a scribe. She had spent nine years making herself invisible in plain sight, and invisibility, it turned out, worked in both directions: no one saw her coming.

The record table stood at the courtyard's edge, where the elders had laid out the temple's documents for the hunters' inspection — the resident rolls, the debt ledgers, the accounting. Her accounting. The list in her own handwriting, with his name on it, the name that had traveled to the capital and come back wearing grey and crimson.

There was a brazier beside the table. The elders lit it for sealing wax.

Mara picked up the ledger with both hands and fed it into the fire.

For one long second nothing happened — the leather cover smoking, curling — and then the pages caught all at once with a sound like a gasp, and flame went up orange against the pale sky, and every person in the courtyard turned to look at her.

"What—" Elder Maren's voice cracked like a whip. "*Scribe!* What are you—"

"The record is destroyed," Mara said. Her voice sounded strange to her, level and clear, a stranger's voice. "No record, no confirmation. Your proclamation requires surrendered *persons identified by record*, honored mother. I've read it. I copy everything. You should know that by now."

"You foolish girl." Elder Maren had gone white around the mouth. "Do you know what you've done? Your debt—"

"My debt is void," Mara said. "I believe that's the term. Property of the temple, destroyed by the temple's negligence. Ask your own lawyers." She had no idea if it was true. She said it like it was scripture, and half the courtyard nodded along, because everyone in the valley hated the ledgers, and everyone in the valley understood, in their bones, a blow struck against the book that owned them.

The hunter was watching her with narrowed eyes. "That document was Crown property the moment we requested it."

"Then the Crown should have collected it faster."

Someone in the crowd laughed — one short, shocked bark — and smothered it.

And in that second of laughter, in that crack in the world's attention, the wolf pen gate burst.

Sorrel came over the wall rather than through the gate — a grey blur and forty pounds of fury — and hit the nearest hunter's arm, not biting, *shoving*, and the formation broke, and the handler shouted, and the hound spun toward the chaos with its chain snapping taut —

— and Wendell's bound hands closed over the vial in the lead hunter's grip and crushed it, wax and glass, to powder.

Then he was running. Not toward the gate — toward her, straight through the middle of everything, iron wire singing on his wrists, and Mara found she was running too, both of them converging on the same point the way rivers converge, the way they had been converging for eleven years without either of them saying so.

"This way," she gasped. "The north path. The stones."

"They'll ride us down—"

"Not before dark, and the stones are warded against their kind — the temple built them that way, the old wards still hold, I've *read* the foundation charters, Wendell, I read everything—" She was babbling and didn't care. Behind them the courtyard dissolved into shouting; ahead the north path climbed through the pines toward the border, the line of leaning grey stones that marked the edge of the valley's silence, the edge of the world as she had known it for nine years.

He caught her wrist at the tree line and pulled her behind him, and for a moment they stood in the shadow of the pines, both breathing hard, the temple bells ringing alarm below.

"Mara." Her name in his mouth, the first time in years. "What you just did—"

"Don't." Her voice shook. "Don't tell me what I just did. I know what I just did. There's no un-burning a ledger." She laughed, high and unsteady. "Nine years keeping my head down. Nine years. And it takes me one afternoon."

"Why?" he asked. Simply. As if the answer weren't the largest thing in either of their lives.

She looked at him — at the soot on his jaw, the broken wire still humming at his wrists, the man who watched border stones as if listening to something beyond them — and told the truth because there was no time left for anything else.

"Because you listen at those stones," she said. "Because you wondered about color. Because I've loved you since the winter of the roof, and I was never going to say it, and now I don't have to, because apparently I'd rather burn down my whole careful life than watch them put your soul in a bottle." Her breath came out ragged. "That's why. Are we going, or are we standing here while I die of embarrassment?"

Something moved through his face — wonder, grief, and underneath them a fierce, banked relief, like a fire finally allowed air. "The wire," he said hoarsely. "I can't cross the stones in this. It burns gifted flesh. You'll have to take it off."

"Then hold still."

She worked the wire with her scribe's knife, fingers steadier than they had any right to be, and when the last coil fell away his wrists showed two lines of angry red, and he flexed his hands like a man remembering them, and looked up at the ridge where the border stones leaned against the sky like old teeth.

Beyond them: the world. Color, magic, hunters, winter. Everything the valley existed to keep out.

"If we cross," he said quietly, "we can't come back. The valley forgets, Mara. That's its mercy and its curse. Whatever waits out there — we carry it with us forever."

"Good," she said. "I'm tired of being forgotten. I want to be somewhere that remembers."

He took her hand. His palm was warm and callused and shaking slightly, and she held on.

They ran.

The pines whipped past. Below and behind, the alarm bells gave way to hoofbeats — they'd gotten horses moving faster than she'd guessed — but the path bent and doubled as it climbed, switchbacking through rock too narrow for riders, and the light was failing, and the stones were close, close, close—

At the top of the ridge, the border stones stood in their ancient row, frost-rimed, humming with a silence deeper than quiet. Mara had walked past them a thousand times and never touched one. Now she put her palm flat against the nearest, feeling the strange dead coolness of it, the absence like a held breath.

Beyond the last stone, the evening sky burned with colors the valley had never shown her — gold going violet going a green she had no name for, and beneath it the land rolled away in shadows that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, it's *real*."

"It's real," Wendell said. "And it knows we're coming." He stepped over the line first — and the moment his boot crossed, the air around him *changed*, a shimmer like heat off summer stone, like something long starved suddenly fed. He gasped. Then he turned and held out his hand to her across the border, half in the valley and half out of it, a man made of two worlds.

Behind them, far below, torchlight spilled onto the north path. Minutes. Maybe less.

Mara Halloran, debtor, scribe, arsonist, took his hand and stepped out of the only life she had ever known.

The world beyond the stones rushed in like a tide — light, sound, a pressure of presence so vast she staggered — and the last thing she heard from the valley was Elder Maren's voice, thin with rage, ringing off the rocks:

"She's forfeit! Both of them — *forfeit!*"

The stones went dark behind them. And somewhere in the deepening violet sky, something ancient turned its attention toward two small figures walking into the wild, and began, very slowly, to follow.
