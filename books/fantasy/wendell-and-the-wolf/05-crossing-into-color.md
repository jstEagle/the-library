## 5. Crossing Into Color

**Mara**

The border stones were supposed to be the end of the world.

That was what the temple taught. Beyond them lay nothing worth naming — wild country, godless country, a place where the ledgers ran out and no debt could be collected. For eleven years I had copied that teaching into neat columns, and for eleven years I had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold: without thinking, without gratitude.

Then Wendell took my hand, and we stepped between the stones, and the world ended.

Or began. I still don't know which word is honest.

The first thing was the light. In the valley, light falls flat, like ink on vellum — even noon has a certain gray patience to it. But past the border, the late sun came through the trees in long gold blades, and each blade *moved*, bending toward us as we passed, the way wheat bends toward wind. Toward warmth. I stopped dead on the path and watched a shaft of sunlight lean its whole bright body toward my shoulder.

"It's following me," I said. My voice sounded small and stupid against all that gold.

"Light's curious," Wendell said. "It doesn't get many people out here." He tugged my hand gently. "Don't stare at it too long. Curious things get attached."

The second thing was the river.

We heard it before we saw it — a sound like many voices under one voice, rising and falling, almost words. When we came down through the ferns to its bank, I understood why. The water was singing. Not metaphorically; not the way temple poets say a river sings when they mean it makes noise. It sang. Notes rose off the surface like steam, visible in the cool air, little ribbons of sound that curled and dissolved. The melody changed when we approached. It turned, unmistakably, into something glad.

I sat down on a stone because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

"That's the Liss," Wendell said, crouching to cup water in his hands. "She remembers every traveler who ever drank from her. She's greeting us."

"Rivers don't remember."

"This one does." He offered me his cupped hands, and I drank, and the water tasted cold and clean and — there was no other word — *recognized*. Like drinking in a house where someone had set a place for you. Somewhere behind my ribs, something that had been clenched for eleven years let go by a single degree.

"The valley," I said slowly, working it through the way I worked through a corrupted ledger line. "The valley isn't just magic-dead. It's magic-*proofed*. Walled. Everything out here is—" I gestured helplessly at the singing, the leaning light, the ferns that rustled though there was no wind. "Everything out here is awake."

"Now you understand why I stood at the border stones," he said quietly. "All those years."

I did understand, suddenly and completely. He hadn't been restless. He'd been listening — to this, to all of it, through a wall of silence, the way you press your ear to a door of a room full of music.

We walked until dusk. The rules of the world unspooled around us as we went, and Wendell gave them to me one at a time, patient as a scribe teaching letterforms.

*Don't answer if something calls your name twice.* *Never trade with anything that has more than two shadows.* *If the path you're walking starts to feel kind, leave it.* Each rule arrived with a story, and each story made the forest both more wonderful and less safe, until wonder and fear stopped being separate things in my chest and became one warm, frightened, luminous animal.

At full dark we made camp in the hollow of an old yew, and that was the third thing: the dark itself. There were stars overhead, ordinary stars, but between them moved lights that were not stars — slow, deliberate sparks, drifting like lanterns carried by unseen hands.

"What are they?" I whispered.

"Souls that got loose," Wendell said. "Or never got sold. Depends which stories you believe." He lay back against the roots, and one of the drifting lights descended, cautiously, to hover near his face. He didn't flinch. After a moment it drifted up again, satisfied, and rejoined the others.

"They know you," I said.

"They know what I carry." His hand went briefly to his chest, to the shape beneath his shirt that I had felt once, accidentally, years ago, and been told was a locket. "Loose souls are polite about it. They keep their distance from the vial. Like moths avoiding a lit window."

I wanted to ask. I had wanted to ask for three days, since the burning ledger, since the hound's scream, since the elder's betrayal. But asking felt like pressing on a wound to see how deep it went, and I wasn't sure either of us would survive knowing.

So instead I asked, "Are you tired?"

And he laughed — really laughed, short and surprised, the first true laugh I'd heard from him since before the hunters came. "Mara Halloran. We are fugitives in a haunted wood, hunted by the crown, and you ask if I'm tired."

"You look tired."

"I'm free," he said. "It looks similar."

**Wendell**

She fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, her head tipping against the yew's root like a book closing. I shifted her carefully until she lay against my shoulder, and then I stayed very still and watched the loose souls drift and thought about how much trouble we were in.

Because here was the truth I hadn't told her: the valley had been hiding her too.

Magic-dead ground doesn't just blind the hunters' hounds. It blinds everything. Out here, past the stones, a person is legible. Their history shows. Their affections show. And Mara — Mara burned a royal ledger to save a man she barely knew she loved, and acts like that write themselves into you out here, glowing, the way ink glows wet. Any hunter within twenty miles would be able to read her like a proclamation.

I should have left her in the valley. That had been the plan. Run alone, draw the hunt away, keep the vial moving until I found a buyer or a grave. Clean. Simple. No one else's blood in the arithmetic.

But she'd come through the stones holding my hand, and the light had bent toward her, and I had understood that some things are not decisions. Some things are just gravity, and you find out you've been falling for years and only notice when the ground gets close.

She stirred against my shoulder. "Wendell?"

"Go back to sleep."

"Can't. Too loud." She gestured vaguely at the forest, where the night had filled with sound — insects, owls, the river's distant song, and underneath it all a low hum I knew well: the sound of living land, land that hadn't been silenced. "How do you sleep in this?"

"Practice." I hesitated. "There's a trick, if you want it. Gifted children learn it before they learn letters. You give the dark one true thing about yourself, and it lets you rest."

"That sounds like a bargain."

"A small one. The smallest kind." I kept my voice level. "The dark keeps the thing you tell it. That's all. It doesn't use it, doesn't sell it. It just... holds it, so you don't have to carry it while you sleep."

She was quiet a long moment. In the drifting soul-light I could see her face, sharp and tired and stubborn, the face I had memorized across eleven years of temple courtyards and borrowed glances.

"One true thing," she said finally. "About myself."

"Only if you want."

"I burned a ledger today," she said to the darkness, plainly, the way she read aloud figures. "Not for justice. Not for principle. I burned it because his name was on it, and I found I couldn't live in a world where that page existed." She paused. "That's the true thing. Not the burning. The reason."

The dark seemed to lean in, take the words, settle. The hum of the land softened around our hollow like a blanket drawn up.

Mara exhaled. "Oh," she said. "Oh, that's what quiet feels like."

And then, before I could stop myself — because apparently I was also a fool, and had always been one — I said, "My turn. One true thing for the dark."

"Wendell—"

"There's a soul in a vial against my heart," I said. "It's my sister's. I stole it six years ago, and I've run every day since, and I would run six hundred more, except now there's a woman asleep on my shoulder who burned a crown's paper for me, and I find I'm not running *away* anymore. I'm running *toward* something. The mountain court. Where they make winter. And I mean to walk in the front door and take her back."

The dark took that too. The nearest soul-light drifted closer, hovered over Mara's sleeping face for a long moment, then rose again.

When I looked down, Mara's eyes were open, watching me.

"You heard all of that," I said.

"Scribes hear everything. Occupational hazard." Her voice was rough with sleep and something else. "Your sister. Six years."

"Yes."

"And you never told me. Eleven years of watching you stand at those stones, and you never once—"

"How could I?" It came out harder than I meant. "You were safe. You were *boring*, Mara, gloriously boring, copying your ledgers and paying your debt, and the safest thing I could do for you was be a quiet man who liked wolves. Every year I stayed silent was a year the hunt couldn't follow you home."

"And instead," she said, "it followed me anyway. Because of you." She sat up, and I braced for anger, and it came — but banked, controlled, aimed past me at the world. "Good. Then we're already ruined together, and you can stop protecting me from the truth. From now on, I get all of it. Every rule, every danger, every name. I copy ledgers, Wendell. I can hold terrible information. It's the one thing I'm trained for."

I looked at her — ink-stained, exhausted, furious, magnificent — and I understood that the plan had died somewhere between the border stones and this hollow tree, and a new one had been born without my permission.

"All right," I said. "All of it. Starting tomorrow. Sleep now."

"Sleep," she agreed. And then, quieter, settling back against my shoulder: "For the record. The true thing I'd have given the dark, if you hadn't taken it first."

"Don't."

"Eleven years," she murmured, already half gone, "I told myself I watched the border stones because I longed for somewhere else. Lie. I watched wherever *you* were standing. Goodnight, Wendell."

I sat awake a long time after that, her breath evening out against me, the loose souls wheeling overhead like patient lanterns, and I did not sleep at all, and I did not mind.

* * *

**Mara**

Dawn out here was not dawn. Dawn was an event.

Light came up through the mist in bands of green and rose, and the birdsong had harmonies in it that no bird should know, and when I washed my face in a stream-fed pool, my reflection looked back at me with an expression I didn't recognize — hope, maybe, unguarded for the first time in years, before I could arrange it into something more prudent.

Wendell was already up, talking low to the grey wolf that had followed us out of the valley. I still didn't fully understand the wolves — sentries, he called them, friends, he wouldn't say — but this one had refused the border crossing twice and then crossed anyway, and now sat at the edge of our camp like a piece of the old world that had decided to see this through.

"We walk north today," Wendell said, shouldering the pack. "Two days to the market roads. There's a fair — fugitives, traders, people like me. If anyone knows a way into the mountain, they'll be there."

"And if the hunters find the fair?"

"Then we'll wish they hadn't." He said it lightly, but his eyes went north, and I followed his gaze, and for a moment neither of us spoke.

Because far off, past the singing hills, a mountain stood against the sky that had no business existing. Mountains end. They have peaks, and above peaks is sky. This one simply went up and up until the blue gave out, its summit lost in a crown of cloud that did not move — did not drift, did not thin, held perfectly still like a held breath.

Snow slid down its flanks in high white streams. In summer.

"That's it," I said. "That's the court."

"That's the court." Wendell adjusted the pack strap. "Every winter for six years has started earlier and lasted longer. Now you know where it comes from. Someone up there is hungry, and winter is what hunger looks like from down here."

I looked a long time. The unmoving cloud. The summer snow. Somewhere inside that whiteness was a girl in a vial, and a hall full of bought memories, and whatever machinery turned love into cold.

"Mara." Wendell's voice, gentle. "We can still turn east. Find a port, a ship, a life. No one would blame—"

"I'm a scribe," I said. "I finish what I start copying."

He smiled — that rare, real smile, the one I had spent eleven years pretending not to collect — and we turned north together, the wolf loping ahead, the singing river falling behind.

We had walked perhaps an hour when the wolf stopped.

Not paused. *Stopped* — every muscle locked, nose lifted, ears forward, staring at something in the deep grass beside the road. Wendell froze beside me, and his hand found mine, and I felt his pulse jump once, hard.

In the grass lay a strip of cloth, bright and new, snagged on a bramble. Royal blue. Hunter's weave.

And pinned beneath it, driven into the earth as neatly as a signature, a single arrow fletched in silver.

Wendell crouched, touched the arrow's shaft, and went very still.

"They're not tracking us," he said quietly. "They're *herding* us. This is a marker. A boundary." He looked up, north, toward the fair roads, and I watched him arrive at the same arithmetic I was arriving at, and I watched him hate it.

"Mara," he said. "Someone's reached the fair before us. And they wanted us to know."
