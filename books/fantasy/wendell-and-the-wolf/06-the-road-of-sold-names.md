## 6. The Road of Sold Names

The valley's border stones were behind us by the time I understood that I had never once looked back at anything in my life until that morning.

I had looked *at* the temple, at the ledgers, at Wendell across the refectory — but looking is not the same as turning your whole body toward a thing and letting it take you. That morning, on the ridge above the pass, I turned, and the valley lay below like a bowl of grey milk: the temple roofs, the terraced fields, the wolf pens with their small moving shadows. A place where magic could not follow. A place where nothing followed, really. Not change, not risk, not the crown.

"You can still go back," Wendell said beside me. He said it without looking at me, which was his way of saying it gently. "They'll punish you for the ledger. But they'll punish you worse for me. If you tell them I took you—"

"I burned the ledger," I said. "Nobody took me anywhere."

He was quiet after that. He was quiet most of the time, and I had spent years mistaking that quietness for emptiness, the way you might mistake a well for a hole in the ground. Now I knew better. Wells have water at the bottom. You just have to be willing to be lowered into the dark to reach it.

We walked north.

* * *

The world beyond the border stones was not what the temple taught. The temple taught that magic was a sickness the valley had been mercifully spared, that beyond our borders the land was corrupted, wild, dangerous. What I found instead was color. Not brighter color — deeper. The greens came up out of the earth like something remembered rather than grown. Rivers ran loud and opinionated. On the second day I saw light move through a wheat field against the wind, deliberately, like a hand smoothing a bed, and I stopped walking and stared until Wendell stopped too.

"Gifted land," he said. "Old bargains. The fields are paid for."

"Paid for how?"

"Every year. Something small." He didn't elaborate, and I learned quickly that Wendell's silences had architecture. There were doors in them, and some doors he opened, and some he walked past without slowing, and I was learning to tell the difference.

That night we slept in the open because there were no villages. Or rather — there were villages, and then there were places where villages had been.

* * *

The first empty village had no name left on its signpost; someone had pried the board off, maybe to burn it, maybe to carry it away as the last piece of home worth taking. The houses stood with their doors open. Not broken open — open, the way you leave a door when you expect to come back before dark. Cooking pots sat on hearths. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in the dust of the lane, one wheel still turning faintly in the wind, or perhaps I imagined that, or perhaps in this colored world outside my grey valley even abandoned toys kept a little motion in them, unwilling to stop.

Wendell went through the houses ahead of me. I watched him do it and understood he had done it before, many times, in other years, other roads. He checked each room the way a man checks a room when he is afraid of finding someone in it — and also afraid of finding no one.

"They took everyone?" I asked.

"The gifted ones. And sometimes just the ones who argued." He came out of the last house holding a clay jar. He set it down in the middle of the lane, upright, deliberate, like a marker. "Sometimes the ones who were simply standing near a gifted person when the hunters came."

"And the rest? The ungifted families?"

"Gone too. Nobody stays in a place the crown has bled. Word travels. They walk to the cities, or to the coast, or—" he gestured vaguely south, toward everything, "—they scatter."

I thought of the proclamation I had copied. *All persons harboring the gift shall surrender themselves or their harborers to the crown's appointed hunters.* I had written those words in my careful scribe's hand, forty-one times, one for every district office. My handwriting had gone out into the world and emptied villages. It had put doors open and cooking pots cold and wooden horses on their sides in lanes.

I hadn't known. That was true, and it was also worthless. A scribe who doesn't know what she's writing is still writing it.

"Mara." Wendell was watching me. "Whatever you're doing to yourself right now — stop. You copied words. I've done worse than copy words."

"What could you possibly have done worse?"

It came out sharper than I meant it, sharp with guilt looking for somewhere to live, and I saw him flinch — actually flinch, this man who moved through the world like a shadow through grass. He turned away and busied himself with the fire, though the fire needed no busy-ing.

"We should sleep," he said. "Long road tomorrow."

* * *

The rumors found us the way rumors do — sideways, in fragments, from people who materialized at the edges of fires and vanished before full light. An old woman sharing our shelter under a bridge told us about a fair. "A fair of small magics," she called it, and laughed at her own joke with no teeth in it. "Three days' walk past the burnt mill, follow the river where it sings wrong. Everyone who's running goes there eventually. Everyone who's running needs something, and the fair sells it."

"Sells what?"

"Passage. Names. Forgiveness, if you're rich. Souls, if you're buying." She looked at Wendell then, a long look, the kind old people give when they've already decided what you are and are waiting to see if you know it yourself. "And if you're carrying something precious, dearie, keep it next to your heart and don't let anyone see you check on it."

Wendell's hand moved — barely, barely — toward his chest. Toward the inside of his coat. Then it went still, and he thanked the woman for the fire, and she cackled and slept.

I didn't ask him that night. I had learned that much about him: questions asked too early got doors closed in their faces. But I lay awake under the bridge listening to the river sing wrong — a sweet, sliding note underneath the water-sound, like a voice humming behind a wall — and I made a list of everything I knew about Wendell Ash.

He had lived in the valley nine years. He kept wolves, and the wolves obeyed him like soldiers obey a general they love. He watched the border stones as if listening. He was quiet in the way of men carrying weight, not men carrying nothing. And he carried something against his heart that an old woman under a bridge had known to warn him about without ever seeing it.

In the morning I said, "Tell me about your sister."

* * *

He stopped walking so abruptly that I nearly went past him.

We stood on a road cut through low hills, brown and gold in the autumn light, a road that had clearly once been busy — cart ruts worn deep, milestone stubs — and now held only us and the wind. He didn't turn around. His shoulders did something I had never seen them do: they came down, all at once, like a structure deciding to admit gravity.

"How do you know about a sister?"

"I don't. I guessed. You talk in your sleep."

"That's not true."

"It's absolutely true. You say a name. Liss. And you say *I'm sorry* in a voice that—" I stopped, because describing his sleeping voice felt like theft. "Nine years in the valley. Nine years of sorry. Tell me, or don't. But we're walking toward whatever comes for you, and I'd like to know what I'm walking toward too."

For a long moment the wind moved the dry grass along the roadside, and the hills did their patient golden nothing, and I thought he would build the silence up again, brick by brick, the way he always did.

Then he sat down. Right there, on the milestone stub, in the middle of the road, like a man whose legs had finally sent formal notice of resignation. And he told me.

* * *

"There was a court beneath a mountain," he said. "There still is. You won't have heard of it in your valley — your elders made sure of that. They pay the crown, or the crown pays them, depending on the year, but either way the arrangement holds: the mountain takes souls, and the valley stays magic-dead, and everyone pretends the trade is weather."

"Souls," I said. "Actual souls."

"Actual souls. Memories too — memories burn brighter, the Regent likes them best, wears them, I suppose, the way a miser wears gold. The court runs on it. Winter, mostly. Endless winter takes fuel, and the fuel is people. Not their bodies. Their bodies walk away. It's the inside of them that gets poured out and banked like coin."

I sat down on the road across from him. My legs wanted to anyway.

"My family lived on the mountain's southern slope. Poor, gifted — my mother could coax warmth out of stone, which sounds like nothing until you've frozen. The collectors came every few years and took a tithe. One soul per household, chosen by lot, and everyone pretended the lots were fair." His voice stayed level. That was the worst part. He told it like a ledger entry, and I recognized the register, because it was mine. "The year I was nineteen, the lot fell on Liss. My sister. Sixteen. She could make flowers open out of season — that was her whole gift, that was everything, flowers in February, and she'd put them in the windows of the sickhouse—"

He stopped. Not because he couldn't go on. Because he had reached a door he'd never opened, and even now he paused with his hand on it.

"The collectors gave a choice, that year. New policy. One soul per household, but the household could substitute a payment in kind — ten years of service in the deep mines, or..." He looked at his hands. "Or a substitution of persons. One soul for another. Mine for hers. My gift was stronger. Warmth, like my mother, but more. The collectors weighed us like livestock and agreed."

"You sold yourself."

"I signed myself over. And then I ran. Because a signature isn't a soul until they collect it, and I ran with the only thing in that hall worth stealing." His hand went to his coat again, and this time he drew it out: a vial, no longer than a finger, glass gone cloudy with age, and inside it something that wasn't quite liquid and wasn't quite light. It moved when you looked at it. It moved the way grief moves — slow, and always toward you.

"When they take a soul," Wendell said, "it doesn't stay in the person. They decant it. Bank it. The body keeps walking, hollowed, gentle, useful. Liss has been walking around that court for nine years without herself inside her. And this—" he closed his fist around the vial, and I swear the light inside pressed against his fingers like a bird, "—this is her. I stole her back the night I ran. Nine years I've carried her. Nine years I've been trying to find a way down the mountain to pour her home."

The wind came down the road and moved the grass and moved nothing else.

"The hunt," I said slowly. "The proclamation. Gifteds surrendered to the crown."

"Sold to the court. The crown brokers for the mountain. Every gifted they catch is a payment, and every payment buys the crown another season of the mountain's favor." He almost smiled, and it was terrible. "I'm not fleeing the hunt, Mara. I'm cargo the hunt has been looking for since the day I ran. The only fugitive in the kingdom carrying stolen property worth a winter."

* * *

We didn't walk for a while after that. We sat in the road like children, two adults sitting in an empty road in the middle of a kingdom being quietly eaten, and I tried to fit the new shape of him over the old one and found they matched perfectly, which was somehow the most devastating part. All those years in the valley — the quietness, the watching of the border stones, the wolves posted like sentries, the way he never joined anything, never signed anything, never let the temple write his name down twice — it had all been a man hiding a stolen soul against his heart and waiting for the world to finish finding him.

I had loved him for years, in the small dishonest way you love someone when loving them would cost you something. I had told myself it was nothing. Habit. Proximity. The way you're fond of a chair you always sit in.

Sitting in that road, I understood that I had loved him precisely *because* he was carrying something. Because the quietness was load-bearing. Because every day in that grey valley he chose, again, to guard a thing that mattered more than his own safety, and never once asked anyone to admire him for it. I had loved the weight in him without knowing there was weight. And now I knew the weight had a name, and the name was Liss, and she was sixteen forever in a vial, and the man I loved had spent nine years being sorry in his sleep.

"Wendell." He looked up. His eyes were dry, but they had the particular shine of eyes that have done their crying in private, in advance, over years. "When we get her back — when you pour her home — what happens to you? Your debt. Your signature. They'll still hold it."

"I know."

"You'll have given them back their property and they'll still own you."

"I know."

"So what's the plan? After?"

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "There's a fair. Three days past the burnt mill, where the river sings wrong. People who run go there. People who need things." He tucked the vial away, next to his heart, and stood, and offered me his hand, and I took it, and his hand was warm — of course it was warm, warmth was his gift, warmth was his inheritance, warmth was the thing the mountain wanted to bank. "After," he said, "there's no after. There's just getting her back. Everything after that is extra."

"That's not a plan."

"No," he agreed. "It's a direction."

* * *

We walked three more days, and the villages kept coming, and I stopped counting them because counting felt like a form of complicity — as if by numbering the emptied places I was filing them, cataloguing them, doing what scribes do, making the horror orderly. Instead I started learning their names from Wendell, who knew them, because he had spent nine years listening at border stones and wolves carry news the way rivers carry silt.

"Brayford. The mill town. They took the cooper's daughter first, then the whole street stood up for her, so they took the street." And later: "Harrowmere. Nothing taken there at all. They just posted the proclamation and by spring the village had walked itself empty. Fear does that. Fear empties faster than hunters do."

At Harrowmere we found bread, remarkably, in a root cellar — left, I think, by someone who meant to come back, or left deliberately for whoever came next, a kindness addressed to strangers. We ate it walking. And at the edge of Harrowmere, where the road bent toward the mountains, we met the first living soul we'd seen in two days: a girl of maybe twelve, sitting on a fence post, swinging her feet, watching the road as if she'd been waiting for us specifically.

She wasn't afraid of us. That was the first strange thing. In the emptied villages everything had the fear soaked out of it, dried stiff like laundry left out in weather. This girl had none of that. She looked at Wendell the way you look at weather you've decided to enjoy.

"You're going to the fair," she said. Not a question.

Wendell went very still. "Why would you think that?"

"Because everyone going this way is going to the fair, and you're going this way, and you're carrying something that hums." She hopped off the fence. "My gran says the fair's past the burnt mill, where the river sings wrong. Gran says a lot of things. Gran's dead. I'm going to the fair too. You can walk with me or not."

She set off down the road without checking whether we followed, and after a moment Wendell glanced at me, and I glanced at him, and neither of us said what we were both thinking — that a child alone on an empty road was either bait or a miracle, and we had no way to know which, and that we were going to follow her anyway, because the alternative was to stop believing in kindness, and neither of us could afford that yet.

Her name was Pip. She talked the entire way. And on the evening of the third day, past the burnt mill with its black ribs against a red sky, we heard it: the river, singing wrong. A sweet sliding note under the water-sound, like a voice humming behind a wall.

Around the bend, lanterns. Hundreds of them, strung through the trees like fruit. Music. Smoke. Voices — dozens, scores, voices of people who had names the crown wanted.

Pip grinned at us, gap-toothed. "Told you," she said.

And somewhere in the crowd, I felt rather than saw, a pair of eyes settle on Wendell's coat — on the place over his heart — and stay there, patient as winter.
