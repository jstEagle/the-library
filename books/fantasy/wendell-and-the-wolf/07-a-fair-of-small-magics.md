## 7. A Fair of Small Magics

The wolves found the fair before we did.

I should have known they would. They had been pulling east for two days, away from the river road, ears pricked toward some sound I couldn't hear — and then, at dusk on the third day, the grey bitch stopped at the lip of a ravine and looked back at me with an expression I can only call *finally*.

Below us, tucked into a fold of hills like a coin hidden in a fist, lights were burning. Not torchlight. Nothing so honest. The glow came up out of the ground itself, a soft amber breathing through the grass, and around it moved shapes: carts, awnings, people. Music reached us a moment later, thin and strange — a fiddle played by someone whose gift lived in their hands, so that every note came out slightly wrong and slightly better than right.

"Is that it?" Mara whispered beside me.

She had learned to whisper in the last week. In the valley she'd had a scribe's voice, clear and carrying, made for reading proclamations aloud. Out here her voice had gone small and careful, the way you learn to speak when the world is listening.

"That's it," I said. "Or something like it."

"What's it called?"

"It doesn't have a name. Names get written down." I hesitated, because the next part was harder to say. "It moves. Every few weeks. If the hunters find where it was, it's already somewhere else."

She looked at me sideways. "And you know this how?"

"I've been running from the crown since before you could hold a pen, Mara." It came out sharper than I meant. Below us the amber light breathed, indifferent to what it cost the people huddled around it. "I know the places people go when they can't go anywhere."

She didn't flinch. She never flinched anymore, which frightened me more than if she had. A week ago she had been a temple scribe with ink-stained fingers and a life measured out in ledgers. Now there was ash under her fingernails from the ledger she'd burned, and a hunted man's name in her mouth, and she walked beside me like she'd been doing it all her life.

"Rules," I said, because if I didn't say them now I never would. "Three rules. One: don't tell anyone your name. Two: don't tell anyone what you carry." Her hand went, without thinking, to the satchel where the vial rode against my ribs — no, against hers; she carried it now, had insisted, and I still hadn't decided whether that was trust or strategy or both. "Three: don't look too long at anything. Looking is asking, and asking is buying."

"And if someone asks me a question?"

"Lie beautifully. You're a scribe. You're good with words."

A ghost of a smile. "I'm good with true words. Lies come out crooked."

"Then say nothing crooked," I said, "and let me do the talking."

I should have remembered, later, that I am not actually good at talking. Wolves talk with their teeth and their silence, and I had been one of them so long I'd forgotten how to be anything else.

* * *

The fair received us the way a tide receives driftwood.

There was no gate. There couldn't be — gates imply walls, walls imply a perimeter worth defending, and this place survived precisely by having none. We simply walked down into the ravine and the crowd folded around us, and within ten steps I felt the attention settle over us like weather: dozens of gifted senses taking our measure, tasting the air behind us, checking for the iron-and-hound smell of the hunt.

An old woman selling candles that burned memories instead of wax glanced at Mara twice. A boy with moss growing between his knuckles watched the wolves and made a sign against evil, or maybe against envy. Nobody spoke to us. That was the courtesy of the fair: everyone was nobody until proven otherwise.

"Mara," I murmured. "Remember—"

"I remember the rules." She was staring at a stall where a man sold bottled birdsong, each corked jar fluttering faintly. "Wendell. Look at them. They're just—" She stopped, started again. "In the valley we were told the gifted were monsters. Stories to frighten children. And they're *people*. They're just people selling things."

"They're people hiding," I said. "That's different. Hiding makes people dangerous."

"You hide."

"Yes," I said. "And haven't you noticed how dangerous I am?"

She laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. I held onto it. I was learning to hold onto her laughter the way a drowning man holds driftwood, without examining whether it would bear his weight.

We moved through the fair's spine, a lane of canvas and cart-tongues and cook-smoke, and I asked my questions carefully, obliquely, the way you ask in a place where every question is a transaction. *Looking for passage south. Looking for someone who knows the old roads.* I didn't say *under-mountain*. I didn't say *the court*. But the fair heard anyway. Fairs like this one are single organisms, and word moved through it faster than we moved through it, rippling ahead of us from stall to stall, and I felt the ripple change character somewhere around the third question. Curiosity curdling into calculation.

The guide we needed found us before we found him.

He was waiting at the edge of the firelight when we finally stopped to eat — a bowl of stew bought from a woman whose gift was making any food taste like whatever you missed most. Mine had tasted of venison and pine smoke, of winters alone in the high passes, and I ate it without telling Mara why my eyes stung. Hers, she said quietly, had tasted of her mother's kitchen, and she ate it very slowly, and neither of us spoke about that either.

The man by the fire was lean and neat, with the oiled pleasantness of a merchant and eyes that did all his real work. He wore grey, unremarkable grey, the color of a man who has decided to be forgettable, and only his hands betrayed him: long, precise fingers, kept immaculate, the hands of someone who handled valuable things and wanted you to know he knew their value.

"Wendell," he said.

My spine went cold. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"Sef," he said, as if that settled everything. "I deal in passages. Roads, tunnels, safe houses. Also in other commodities, when the opportunity arises." He smiled at Mara, and I watched the smile land on her and hated how reasonable it looked. "You want the under-mountain road. Everyone who wants it wants it quietly, and everyone who asks about it asks me, because I'm the only one who sells it."

"How much?" I said.

"For the road? Reasonable. For the road plus my discretion about certain cargo—" his gaze dropped, delicately, to the satchel at Mara's side, "—we can discuss terms."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind us the fiddle stumbled and recovered.

"I don't know what you mean," Mara said.

"Oh, I think you do." Sef turned his hands palm-up, a showman's gesture, and for a moment — just a moment — I saw the gift move in him, some low cunning thing that let him taste value the way a moneylender tastes gold. His pupils widened. His breath caught. When he looked up again, the pleasantness had gone thin over something hungry. "That vial isn't empty. I'd stake my own soul on it, which, given my profession, is saying something. A soul in a glass, carried by a debt-scribe and a wolf-keeper, heading for the mountain. Do you know what the court pays for a full soul these days? Enough to retire. Enough to *vanish*."

"We're not selling," I said.

"Everyone says that first." He said it gently, almost kindly, which was the worst thing about it. "The road costs three silver. My silence costs more. Think of it as insurance — you pay me, and no one at this fair learns what you're carrying. Because Wendell—" he savored the name, "—if I can smell it, so can others here. Brokers. Worse than brokers. I'm honestly the best friend you have in this ravine, and friendship, in my experience, is best kept paid current."

Mara's hand had closed over the satchel strap. I stepped half in front of her, slow, unhurried, letting the wolf come up into my stance where he could see it. Sef saw. To his credit, he didn't step back. He just smiled wider, like a man watching a beast pace the length of its chain.

"Three days," he said. "The road leaves when the fair leaves. Decide before then." He rose, brushed off his immaculate grey knees, and paused. "For what it's worth — and it's worth little — I hope you choose to pay. I'd hate to see something that rare end up in a vat."

He melted into the fair the way oil into water, and was gone.

* * *

We didn't speak until we'd walked to the ravine's edge, past the last awning, where the amber light gave out and the ordinary dark began. The wolves came with us and lay in a ring around us, facing outward, and I took a poor comfort in that.

"He recognized it," Mara said finally. "How?"

"The vial hums. To anyone with the least sensitivity, it sings." I sat down on a cold rock and put my head in my hands. "I've kept it quiet for nine years. Wrapped in wool, warded with everything I know, buried in a valley where magic can't hear it. And the moment it crosses the border it starts shouting its existence to every ear in the world."

"So we give it to him. Pay him."

"No."

"No?"

"Sef doesn't sell silence, Mara. Sef sells silence *once*, and then sells the secret to whoever pays next. He told us himself — he deals in commodities. The moment a bigger buyer appears, we're a ledger entry." I looked up at her. The fair glowed behind her, all those fragile lives pretending to be safe. "But we may need his road anyway. Which means we need leverage, or speed, or luck. Preferably all three."

She was quiet a while. Down in the ravine the fiddle played its wrong-right notes, and someone laughed, and the laughter sounded almost like home.

"These people," she said. "They built all this. Candles and birdsong and stew that tastes of what you miss. A whole town that exists for one night and then runs." Her voice had gone rough. "And it survives because everyone keeps everyone else's secrets, and one man in grey can undo it all for coin."

"Now you understand the world you've walked into."

"I understood it in the valley," she said. "I just didn't feel it before." She turned to look at me, and the light caught the ash still under her fingernails. "Wendell. If it comes to it — if the choice is the vial or us—"

"Don't."

"—promise me you'll choose us."

I couldn't answer. The vial rode against her ribs, warm as a sleeping bird, and inside it was my sister — the last living piece of her, the debt I'd been paying since I was nineteen years old. Nine years I had carried that promise. I knew exactly what I was, and what I was, was a man who did not break promises.

So I said nothing, and Mara read my silence the way she read everything, fluently and without mercy, and after a moment she nodded slowly, as if I'd answered.

"All right," she said. "Then we find another way. We have three days."

Behind us, down in the breathing light of the fair, a wolf lifted its head and began, very softly, to growl.

I turned. At the far edge of the firelight, half-shadows stood among the stalls — four men who did not belong to the fair, wearing the grey of no guild and the stillness of professionals, and even at this distance I could see the hound at their feet lift its head and taste the wind.

Sef hadn't waited three days.

The hunt had found the fair.
