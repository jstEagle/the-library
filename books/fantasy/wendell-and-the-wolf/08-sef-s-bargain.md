## 8. Sef's Bargain

Sef's tent smelled of cedar smoke and old coins.

That was the first thing Mara noticed — the smell of money, actual money, the kind the temple never handled because the temple dealt only in debt, and debt had no scent. The second thing she noticed was that Sef had arranged his small kingdom with the precision of a man who counted everything twice. Rugs laid flat against the uneven ground. A brazier burning low, so the shadows stayed where he wanted them. Three stools, no more, positioned so that any guest would have to sit lower than him.

Mara remained standing. It was a scribe's instinct: never sign anything until you've read every line, and never sit in a room where the furniture has been weaponized.

"You're the one who copies things," Sef said. He was smaller than she'd expected, narrow as a weasel, with ink-stained fingers that told her he'd once been a clerk too — or still was, in the way that a knife is still a knife no matter what handle you put on it. "Word travels. A temple scribe who burned her own ledger. There are people who'd pay to hear that story."

"There are people who'd pay for a lot of things," Mara said. "We're told you know the under-mountain road."

"I know roads." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, which is exactly what made her distrust it. "Roads, passes, tolls, and what lives in the dark between them. But knowledge isn't free, scribe. Nothing here is free. That's the whole point of this place."

The fair of small magics had taken them two days to find, hidden in a fold of hills where the mapmakers had given up. From the outside it looked like nothing — a scatter of tents, cookfires, mules. Inside, it was the last bright room of a burning house. Mara had seen a woman mend a shattered bowl by singing to it. She'd seen a boy whose shadow moved a half-second ahead of him, always anticipating. She'd seen an old man selling bottled laughter, three drops for a copper, and watched a grieving mother buy a whole vial and drink it straight.

And everywhere, threaded through all of it, the same quiet desperation. These people were running. The fair wasn't a home; it was a held breath.

Wendell stood near the tent flap, arms crossed, and Mara could feel him not wanting to be there. He'd argued against coming to Sef specifically. *He smells like the mountain,* Wendell had said. She hadn't understood what he meant until now, standing in the cedar-and-coin smell, watching the broker's eyes keep sliding — again and again, casual as breathing — toward the leather case at Wendell's hip.

Toward the vial.

"Passage to the court," Sef said, leaning back. "Through the mines, past the checkpoints, down the old shaft-roads the Regent's people don't watch anymore because they think nobody's fool enough to use them. I can take you there. Food, shelter, the right words to say at each gate. I can put you at the mountain's front door in nine days."

"And the price?" Mara asked.

Sef spread his hands. "Now we're talking."

* * *

He named it over tea that tasted of pine resin, poured by a silent woman with silver rings on every finger who never blinked quite enough.

"Not coin," Sef said. "Coin I can get anywhere. What I want — what my *buyers* want — is rarer." He set down his cup. "Memories, scribe. Specific ones. The court beneath the mountain has an appetite, and the finest thing on its menu is a memory of love. First love, deepest love, love that survived something. Those burn brightest in the Regent's hall. They say he wears them like jewels. They say a strong one can light a whole corridor for a year."

Mara kept her face still. Ten years of copying ledgers had taught her that much — the face is just another page, and you learn to write what needs writing on it.

"You're asking me to sell a memory," she said.

"I'm asking you to *choose* which one. That's the generous part." Sef's eyes glittered. "Most people who go down the mountain lose everything eventually. Every memory, sold piecemeal to buy passage, warmth, another day. They arrive hollow and the Regent finishes them. You'd be doing it *deliberately*. One memory. Your pick. In exchange, safe passage for two, and I'll throw in a guide who actually knows which shafts collapse."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then you can walk the surface road like everyone else." He shrugged. "Surface road takes six weeks, crosses two checkpoints, and the hunt's parties are moving north. By the time you reach the mountain, they'll know your faces better than your mothers do. Honestly?" He leaned forward, voice dropping to something almost kind. "You won't make it. Not with a soul-vial that sings loud enough for a hound to hear across a valley."

Mara felt Wendell go rigid beside her.

"You knew about the vial," Wendell said quietly.

"Sef knows about everything," Sef said pleasantly. "It's why people come to Sef."

* * *

They walked back through the fair in silence, past the singing mender and the shadow-boy, past a stall where a woman read fortunes in the patterns of frost on a copper plate — real frost, out of season, beading up from nowhere at her touch. Mara watched the fortune-teller's face as a customer left disappointed. The woman wasn't performing sadness. She was rationing herself. Every little miracle cost her something, and she was deciding, cup by cup, how much of herself she could afford to pour out today.

*This is what the road does,* Mara thought. *It doesn't kill you. It makes you price yourself.*

They stopped at the edge of the camp, where the wolves waited — three of them now, grey shapes in the treeline that had followed them from the valley without ever quite being invited. Wendell crouched and let the largest press its head against his chest, and Mara saw him draw something from the contact, some steadiness, the way she drew steadiness from ink and routine.

"No," Wendell said.

She hadn't said anything yet. That was the thing about him — after years of watching him across the temple yard, she should have known he'd hear the question before she asked it.

"We find another way," he said. "There's always another way."

"Is there? Because I've been counting, Wendell. I'm good at counting. It's the one thing I'm actually for." She heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "Six weeks on the surface road through hunt territory, or nine days underground with a man who smells like the mountain. One memory. Out of everything I have, one."

"One you don't get to choose the shape of afterward." He stood, and the wolf sat back on its haunches beside him like a second opinion. "I've seen what the court leaves behind. My sister—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They don't just take the memory, Mara. They take the *weight* of it. You'll remember that a day happened, maybe. A name, a place. Like reading someone else's letter. All the meaning drained out and sold separately."

"So you'd rather I die on the surface road with all my memories intact?"

"I'd rather—"

"What? What would you rather?" She stepped closer. Around them, the fair went on — a fiddler tuning, a child chasing something invisible, the ordinary noise of extraordinary people pretending at ordinary life. "Because here's what I know, Wendell. I spent ten years being careful. Ten years of small ledgers and small days and telling myself contentment was a choice instead of a cage. And then I burned a ledger for you, and it was the first true thing I've ever done, and I am not going back to being careful. Not even for you. *Especially* not for you."

The words came out harder than she meant them, and she saw them land, saw him flinch the way a man flinches when a blow is accurate.

"That's exactly why I'm saying no," he said finally, low. "Because you mean it. Everything you feel, you mean it completely. Do you understand what they'd pay for that? A person who loves without holding anything back?" He shook his head. "Sef wouldn't take one memory, Mara. He'd take the first one, and then he'd know what you were, and then he'd take another, and another, and you'd let him, because you'd decide each time that the next piece of yourself was worth the road. That's how the mountain feeds. Not by force. By *reasonableness*."

Mara opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because the worst part — the part she'd never say aloud — was that she'd already done the arithmetic. Standing in Sef's tent, smelling cedar and coin, she had already run the ledger in her head. Which memories weighed most. Which ones she could bear to lose. The day her mother taught her letters, pressing her small hand around a reed pen — heavy, but survivable. The winter her father died — no, grief like that might be worth more than love, but she needed her grief; it was load-bearing. And then, unbidden, unwanted, the thought had surfaced anyway:

*The day I met Wendell. The day he carried the lame wolf pup into the temple yard and didn't apologize for tracking mud across the scribes' clean stones, and looked at me like I was a person and not a function.*

She could live without that day. Technically. She would still know him. She'd just know him the way you know a word you can no longer define.

She could survive it. That was the horror of it. She'd checked.

"Mara." Wendell's voice pulled her back. He was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — not anger. Fear. Fear *of* her, or for her, she couldn't tell, and maybe there wasn't a difference. "Whatever you're calculating right now. Stop."

"I'm a scribe," she said. "Calculating is what I do."

"Then calculate this." He took her hands, and his were rough and cold and certain. "If you sell a memory of me to buy passage to save my sister, I will spend the rest of my life owing a debt to a version of you who doesn't know why she bothered. Is that the ledger you want?"

* * *

That night they slept — or didn't — in the loft of a farrier's barn at the fair's edge, among the smell of hot iron and hay. The wolves lay below in the yard like sentries who'd appointed themselves. Through the gap in the boards, Mara could see the fair's cookfires guttering one by one, and beyond them, far to the north, a smudge on the horizon that might have been cloud and might have been the mountain, waiting.

Wendell slept badly, murmuring, one hand curled around the vial-case even in dreams. Mara lay awake and did the thing she was best at.

She wrote the contract in her head.

*One memory, seller's choice, in exchange for passage for two persons through the under-mountain road, duration nine days, guide included.* Clean terms. Almost fair, as these things went. She'd copied enough predatory contracts at the temple to recognize one that had been drafted by someone who wanted the signature more than the trap.

And underneath the contract, in the margin where scribes put their doubts, a single line:

*What is a memory worth, if you'd agree to lose it?*

She turned the question over and over. If she chose the day they met, and sold it, she'd wake tomorrow knowing Wendell as a fact and not a feeling. He'd be a wolf-keeper she'd fled with, a debt she'd taken on, a responsibility. And maybe — this was the seductive part, and she hated how seductive it was — maybe that would be *cleaner*. Love without history. A fresh page. She could choose him again every morning with no weight of ten years of watching and wanting dragging at her.

Or she'd look at him across a campfire and feel nothing where everything used to be, and spend the rest of the road grieving a loss she couldn't even remember making.

Beside her, Wendell stirred. "You're awake," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"You're going to do it."

"No." She said it fast, and heard how fast it was. "No. I said I wouldn't."

He was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone soft and tired. "My sister used to bargain like that. With herself. *Just this once. Just this piece.* The mountain doesn't take pieces, Mara. It takes the hand that offers them, and then it waits to see what else that hand will do."

"I know."

"Do you?" He turned toward her in the dark. "Because when you stood in that tent, you weren't afraid. I was watching. Everyone else who hears Sef's price goes pale. You started *working*. I could see it. Columns. Figures." A breath. "That terrifies me more than the hunters do."

Mara stared at the ceiling boards, at the star-pricks of light between them, and admitted it to the dark where he couldn't quite see her face:

"Then be terrified. But also hear this — I will get us to that mountain, and I will get your sister's soul out of it, and I will pay whatever it costs, and I will not apologize for the arithmetic. That's who you ran away with, Wendell. A woman who burns ledgers and reads the fine print. If you wanted someone gentle, you chose wrong."

His hand found hers in the dark. Held it.

"I didn't choose wrong," he said. "That's what scares me."

* * *

In the morning, the fair woke to shouting.

Not panic — not yet — but the particular raised voices of people who have spotted something wrong at the edge of things. Mara came down the loft ladder to find the farrier standing in his yard, staring north, his hammer forgotten in his hand.

Snow.

Snow in the high passes, weeks early, falling in long grey sheets out of a sky the color of unpolished pewter. And the wind carrying it toward the fair had a sound in it that Mara felt in her teeth more than heard — a low, vast, patient hum, like a note held on the world's largest instrument.

The wolves were on their feet, hackles up, facing north.

Wendell appeared beside her, the vial-case strapped tight. He looked at the snow, and then at Sef's tent across the fairground, and Mara watched him arrive at the same arithmetic she'd already finished.

"He'll raise the price now," Wendell said grimly. "Weather like that, refugees on every road — memories of love will spike. We're worth more desperate."

"Then we negotiate today, while he still thinks we have options." Mara was already rolling her sleeves, already the scribe, already reaching for ink. "And Wendell — whatever happens in that tent, whatever I offer — remember that I know what I'm doing."

"That's exactly what worries me," he said again.

But he followed her toward the cedar-smoke smell, and behind them the snow came down from the early winter like something the mountain had sent ahead of itself — an appetite, arriving on schedule.

And in the margin of her mind, in ink only she could see, Mara added one more line to the contract she was drafting:

*Seller reserves the right to choose which page is torn out.*

She just hadn't decided yet which page it would be.
