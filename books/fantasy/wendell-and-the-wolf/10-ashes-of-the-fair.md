## 10. Ashes of the Fair

The fair died on a morning that smelled wrong.

I woke in the wagon Wendell had rented from a tinker with kind eyes and no questions, and the first thing I noticed was the absence — the market's usual dawn chorus of kettle-smoke and haggling and someone's enchanted fiddle tuning itself out of spite. Instead there was only wind, and under the wind, something sharper. Burning pitch. Burning wool.

Burning people.

"Wendell." I shook his shoulder. He was already awake, already dressed, already listening, his head tilted the way he did when the wolves spoke to him through distances I couldn't measure. His face in the grey light was terrible and calm.

"How long?" I asked.

"Since before dawn. They came down the river road." He was lacing his boots with steady hands, and I hated how steady they were, because steadiness meant he had done this arithmetic before — the arithmetic of running. "Sef's tent is gone. Sef is gone."

"Gone where?"

"He walked out an hour ago. Alone. Toward the hunters' line." Wendell stood and slung the vial's carrying case across his chest, settling it against his heart like a second heartbeat. "He went to buy himself back."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to say that even Sef wouldn't do it, that the broker who'd smiled at us over spiced wine and offered passage for a memory wouldn't sell two fugitives to the men with the soul-hound. But I had watched Sef's eyes when he'd recognized the vial. I had seen the hunger there, patient as frost. Some men don't betray you in anger or fear. They betray you the way a merchant sets a price — carefully, and without malice, because the ledger balances better that way.

"We have minutes," Wendell said. "Maybe less."

"Then we don't waste them arguing."

We came out of the wagon into chaos.

The fair had been built in a hollow between two hills, hidden by charms of unnoticing that had held for thirty years — generations of fugitives weaving their small magics into the same protective lie. The charms were failing now. I could see them failing, which frightened me more than the fire did: the air above the market shimmered like heat-haze in winter, peeling back in strips, and through the gaps the world looked flat and ordinary and full of soldiers.

They were burning the stalls first. That was the cruelty of it — methodical, unhurried. A line of royal hunters in grey moved stall to stall with torches, while others herded the fairfolk into the open ground at the center, where a woman in a hunter's cloak stood consulting a list. Not a fight. A harvest. The soul-hound paced beside her on a chain of white iron, its nose lifting again and again toward the hills, tasting the wind.

Tasting us.

"Back way," Wendell said. "Behind the tinker's row there's a goat track. It climbs to the pass."

"You know this place?"

"I asked questions last night while you slept. I always ask questions." He caught my hand. "Stay behind me. If anyone uses magic near you, drop flat. Gifted fire doesn't care what it burns."

We ran.

The fair I had begun, foolishly, to love was coming apart around us as we fled it. I saw the candlemaker whose wares sang when lit, dragging her daughter by the hand toward the tree line, both of them leaving trails of melted wax that glowed and guttered. I saw old Marta the hedge-healer standing in front of her wagon with her arms spread, doing nothing but standing there, and three hunters simply walking past her as if she weren't worth the rope. I saw a boy — no older than twelve — lift his hand and make the rain start falling upward, just once, just to show them, before a hunter's net of cold iron took him at the knees.

I didn't stop. God forgive me, I didn't stop. I was a scribe; I knew what happened to names that got written onto lists, and every instinct I owned screamed *live, live, live*. But my legs have never forgiven me for that boy, and I don't think they should.

"Mara." Wendell pulled me left, off the market lane, into the tinker's row. Behind us the soul-hound began to bay — a sound like a bell made of grief — and the whole hunt turned toward the sound like iron filings toward a lodestone.

It had our scent now. Or the vial's. Perhaps to that dog they were the same thing.

* * *

The goat track was barely a track at all, a scar of loose stone switchbacking up through gorse and scree. We climbed it with the hound's voice rising below us and the smoke of the fair thickening the sky, until the hollow was a brown smear far beneath and I could see the hunters spreading out along its rim like ink dropped in water.

"They'll fan out," Wendell said between breaths. "Three lines. One takes the pass, one takes the river valley, one holds the burn site. Standard pattern."

"How do you know the standard pattern?"

"Because I've been hunted since I was nineteen years old." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd say *the sun rises*, and something in me cracked a little at that — at how much of him had been shaped by flight, how many years of his quiet life in our valley had been nothing but a held breath.

We reached the first shoulder of the hill and the trees closed around us, stunted pines bent sideways by years of wind. Below, the fair burned. From this height it was almost beautiful — a scatter of orange blooms in the grey morning — and then the wind shifted and brought me the smell again, and it wasn't beautiful at all.

A scream rose from the hollow. Then another. Then a sound I will not describe, because I have spent four years trying to forget it and I am close.

Wendell stopped. I felt him stop — felt the whole weight of him turn back toward the screams like a compass needle finding north.

"No," I said.

"There are children down there."

"If you go down there, we die, the vial dies, your sister dies, and those children still die, because two people cannot stop forty hunters." My voice came out hard and strange, a voice I didn't recognize as mine. "Wendell. *No.*"

He stood there with the wind pulling at his hair and the screams climbing the hill to meet him, and I watched him weigh every life in that hollow against the one soul in the case against his chest, and I understood that whatever he chose, part of him would be buried in that burning fair forever.

A wolf howled from the ridge above us — long, urgent, commanding. Then another, from the east. Then a third, closer, and out of the treeline came the big grey male I knew, flanked by two strangers, all three standing with their hackles up, facing *down* the slope. Facing the hunt.

"The pack found us," Wendell said softly. "Or we found them. They're telling us to run."

"Are they going to—"

"No. They can't fight iron. But they can be seen. Forty hunters chasing wolves is forty hunters not climbing this track." He looked at the grey wolf, and some wordless thing passed between them, some old contract written in years of shared silence, and the wolf's ears flattened in what I could only call acceptance.

"What happens to them?" I whispered.

"Whatever has always happened to wolves near men with chains." He took my hand again, harder this time. "Run."

* * *

We ran the rest of the morning and most of the afternoon, up through the pines and into the bare granite world above them, where the pass cut between two shoulders of mountain like a wound. The hunters didn't follow directly — the wolves saw to that, drawing at least one line of the search away eastward with a game of appearances I could sometimes glimpse far below, grey shapes flickering through the trees while horns bayed after them.

But the third line, the holding line, had put riders on the high trail. Twice we dove into rock shadows and let them pass, close enough that I could hear the jingle of their white-iron manacles. Once, a riderless horse grazed near our path, and Wendell gentled it with a low word in a language that wasn't language, and we nearly took it — nearly — before he shook his head.

"Shod for the king's stables. They'll trace the shoes."

So we kept walking, and the fair fell away behind us, and by late afternoon the only sounds were our breathing and the wind and, faintly, the vial humming against Wendell's chest — a note I hadn't heard before, thin and grieving, as if the soul inside knew what was happening to the world outside its glass.

We stopped at dusk in the lee of a boulder the size of a temple, where centuries of wind had carved a hollow deep enough to hold two people and a small fire if the fire was very small and very brave. Wendell coaxed one from wet scrub with a fingertip, his gift answering as easily as breath, and I sat as close to him as the heat allowed and tried not to think about the boy with the upward rain.

"Sef," I said finally. "Do you think he told them about the vial? About what we carry? Or just where we were?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters because if he told them everything, they know what we're doing. They know we're going to the mountain. And if the Regent knows we're coming—" I stopped, because finishing the sentence made it real.

Wendell fed a stick to the fire. "Sef bargains," he said. "That's all he is, all he's ever been. He sold us cheap enough to save himself and dear enough to stay useful. He'll have given them the location, our descriptions, and a rumor of the vial — enough to whet the appetite, not enough to satisfy. A good broker keeps the buyer hungry." He was quiet a moment. "He offered me passage once, you know. Before you were ever part of this. Years ago. I said no."

"Why?"

"Because his price was a memory too, and I didn't have one I was willing to lose." He glanced at me sidelong, and the firelight made his face flicker between young and old. "I have fewer of those now than I used to."

I didn't ask what he meant. I was afraid I knew.

Above us the stars came out, hard and cold, and somewhere on the slopes below a hunting horn sounded once and fell silent. The temperature was dropping faster than any natural night should drop — I had noticed it for days now, the wrongness of the seasons, winter arriving like an army on the march — and Wendell noticed me noticing.

"The court feeds it," he said. "Every soul they take, every memory they burn, goes into the winter. The Regent stretches the cold further each year. That's why the passes are closing early. That's why the villages empty." He stirred the fire. "We're racing the snow, Mara. If the high passes seal before we cross—"

"Then we find another way."

"There is no other way. There's the under-mountain road or there's nothing."

"Then we walk fast."

He laughed — short, surprised, real. In the firelight, exhausted and smoke-stained, with ash in my hair and the fair's death still clinging to my clothes, he looked at me like I'd said something miraculous. "You copy ledgers," he said. "You burned a ledger to save me. You've been hunted for a week and you say *we walk fast* like it's a chore list."

"I'm a scribe. Lists are how I pray." I pulled my knees up, watching the tiny fire. "Everything ends up on a list eventually, Wendell. Everything gets counted. So we count what we have and we keep moving."

"And what do we have?"

I held up fingers. "One vial. One stolen soul, waiting to go home. Two pairs of boots wearing thin. Three wolves somewhere out there in the dark, spending themselves for us. And—" I hesitated, and then, because the day had been too long for cowardice, "—and each other. Whatever that's worth on Sef's market. Whatever it costs on the mountain's."

Wendell reached over and took my hand, the one I'd been counting on, and folded the fingers closed around nothing, around air, around the shape of everything I'd named.

"More than they can pay," he said. "That's what it's worth. More than they can pay."

We slept in shifts, his arm around me during mine, the vial humming its thin sad note between us like a third sleeper. And in the last hour before dawn, half-dreaming, I heard the wolves again — not howling now but singing, low and sustained, from the ridgeline above our shelter. A sound less like warning than like mourning. A dirge for the fair, maybe. For the boy. For everything burning behind us.

When I woke fully, the song had stopped, and Wendell was standing at the mouth of the hollow looking north, toward the pass, toward the mountain that ate souls and wore memories like jewels.

"The snow's come early to the heights," he said quietly. "The pass is already white. We have perhaps three days to cross before it seals." He turned, and the firelight caught something grim and resolved in his face. "And Mara — there's smoke on the northern ridge. Campfires. Too many, and laid out wrong for hunters."

"Laid out wrong how?"

"Like a siege. Like an army waiting for winter to do its work." He shouldered the vial case. "Someone else is heading for the mountain. Someone with numbers."

We started climbing as the sky went the color of ash, three days of white pass ahead of us and whatever waited beyond it — and behind us, far below, so faint I might have imagined it, a single horn sounded, and then the hounds began to sing.
