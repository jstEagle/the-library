## 11. What I Would Give

The cave found them the way mercy sometimes does — by accident, and too late to feel like kindness.

Wendell had been watching the sky all afternoon, reading it the way Mara read ledgers. The clouds had come in wrong: too flat, too white, moving against the wind like a crowd walking upstream. That was mountain weather. That was *made* weather. By the time the first flakes fell — fat, deliberate, falling in rows as if sown — he had already dragged her off the pass and into a split in the rock face that some older traveler had deepened with fire and tools long ago.

Now the snow came down outside in perfect parallel lines, and Wendell sat with his back to the stone and listened to Mara shiver.

He had a fire going. Small, shielded, fed on the scrub pine they'd gathered in the last hour of light. It gave off more smoke than heat, but it was theirs, and its light moved on the walls like something alive trying to remember how.

Mara had wrapped herself in both their blankets and sat close enough that their shoulders touched. She hadn't spoken since they'd climbed. Neither had he. There was a conversation waiting between them — he could feel it the way you feel a storm behind a ridge — and every hour he put it off made it heavier.

The wolves had settled at the cave mouth. Three of them now, the grey pair from the fair and the young one who had followed them out of the burning. They faced outward, toward the snow, ears up. Sentries. They always knew before he did what was coming, though whether they smelled hunters or simply smelled grief, he couldn't say.

"Mara," he said.

"I know." She didn't look at him. "I've been sitting here for an hour working up to it too."

"Working up to what?"

"To asking you the question you keep not answering." She turned then. Firelight caught the ink stains on her fingers — permanent now, he suspected, part of her the way his scars were part of him. "Your sister. You said her name on the road and then you stopped saying it. You told me she was taken as payment. And then you went quiet for two days, and I let you, because I thought you'd get there when you got there." Her voice stayed level. That was the thing about Mara; her anger and her fear both came out level, like a line of figures that wouldn't balance. "We might die tomorrow, Wendell. On this road we probably will. So tonight you're going to tell me everything, or I'm going to spend the rest of my life — however long that is — inventing it."

* * *

He looked at the fire for a long time. The wolves didn't move.

"You know I'm gifted," he said finally. "You've seen it."

"I've seen you talk a river into carrying us."

"That's the small part of it. The small part is listening — water, wind, animals. Things with no words of their own lend you theirs." He turned his hands over, palms up. Old burns there, old rope scars. "The large part is harder to explain. Everything gifted works the same way underneath. Magic isn't power, exactly. It's *exchange*. You give something true and the world gives something true back. The bigger the ask, the truer the payment has to be."

"And the court beneath the mountain?"

"The court figured out the exchange before anyone else did. Or maybe it never figured it out — maybe it just never forgot, the way everyone else has been forgetting for centuries. The world's magic is dying, Mara. You felt it the day we crossed the border stones. All that color and noise? That's not abundance. That's a world running a fever, spending what's left of itself. And the Regent found the one way to stop the spending." He swallowed. "Souls don't fade. Memories don't fade. A soul is the one currency that holds its value forever. So the court buys them. Trades winter for them. Trades safety, passage, vengeance — whatever a desperate person needs — and takes the payment in the only coin that doesn't wear out."

"The hunt."

"The hunt. Every gifted person taken is brought down and rendered. What they are gets poured into the vats. What they were gets worn." He thought of the Regent's hall, the stories he'd heard from fugitives at the fair — the jewels of memory at the Regent's throat, each one a life someone had sold or lost. "My family lived north of here, in the hill country, when I was fourteen. My sister Lira was sixteen. She was the gifted one, really gifted — she could grow things in dead ground. Our village would have starved three winters running without her."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow shifted and slid off the rock.

"There was a broker," Wendell said. "Came through in a bad year. Offered our father a bargain: the debt forgiven, grain for the whole valley, protection from the crown's tax men. All he wanted was one daughter's soul. Not her body. Not her labor. Just — the soul. They take it out clean, they said. She won't even miss it."

"He sold her."

"He sold her." The words came out flat, and he hated how flat. "And I — I was fourteen, and I loved her more than I loved anyone, and I did nothing. I stood in the doorway and watched them carry her up the mountain road, and she waved at me, Wendell—"

"Wendell."

"—she *waved*, because she thought she was going somewhere fine, because they'd told her—"

"*Wendell.*" Mara's hand closed over his wrist, hard, her grip the only solid thing in the flickering dark. "Breathe."

He breathed. The wolves' ears turned toward him and away again.

"After that I went looking for the court," he said, quieter. "Not to fight it. Fourteen-year-olds don't fight courts. I went to find her. And I found out something worse than anything I'd imagined: once they have your soul, you can't visit. You can't buy it back. There's no price, because the price would be a soul, and souls are what they *keep*. Except—" His mouth twisted. "Except they do trade them. Among themselves. Souls are status down there. A Regent's favorite carries a bright one like a lamp. So I spent six years getting inside. Six years learning their roads, their rules, their ledgers. And when I finally found which vault held hers, I took it."

"You stole a soul out of the court beneath the mountain."

"With a knife and a forged chit and a great deal of luck." He almost smiled. "I got out. I ran until I found the one place in the world magic couldn't follow me — your valley. Dead ground. The vial goes quiet there, invisible. The wolves found me first, actually. Gifted animals hate the court; they can smell what it does. They've been keeping watch over me ever since. Over *it*."

"And the hunt started after."

"Three years after. Long enough that I'd started to believe I'd gotten away with it." He shook his head slowly. "But here's the part I should have told you at the border stones, before I let you burn a ledger and damn yourself for me. The proclamation wasn't coincidence. The court doesn't forgive theft. It's been tightening the net for years — every gifted person flushed out of hiding, every valley that stops sheltering them, every temple that trades names for debt relief. All of it is the machine working. And I handed them the excuse. My theft is why the hunt reached your valley. If I'd left the vial where it was—"

"Stop." Mara said it quietly, but he stopped. "If you'd left it, your sister would still be a lamp in a monster's hall, and you'd be a man who watched his father sell her and did nothing twice. I've copied enough ledgers to know how guilt does arithmetic, Wendell. It always finds the same sum: *everything is my fault*. It's bad math. I've audited better."

Despite everything, a breath of laughter escaped him — small, startled, gone.

"The point," she said, "is that you didn't start the hunt. Greed started the hunt. You just refused to let it win quietly. Those aren't the same crime." She paused. "Where is the vial now?"

He touched his chest, over the heart. Beneath the shirt, on its cord, the little glass shape hung warm. It had been warm since the fair, since the fires. Sometimes he thought he could feel it beating, faint and stubborn, like a second pulse that wasn't his.

"She's in there," he said. "However much of her they left. When we get down — if we get down — I give it back to her. Whatever she is afterward, hollowed or half-remembering, she'll be *hers* again. That's the whole plan. That's all of it."

* * *

Mara was quiet for a while after that. Long enough that he thought she might sleep. Then she said, "I need to tell you something, and you're going to be angry."

"I'm rarely angry."

"You're angry constantly. You just do it politely, at the fire."

That startled a real laugh out of him, brief and rough.

She pulled her knees up under the blankets. "At the fair. Sef's offer. Passage for a memory. You forbade it, and I said I understood, and I did understand — and I also spent the whole night awake, doing sums." Her voice stayed level. Level as a ledger line. "Here's what I know. The road ahead goes under a mountain guarded by a court that eats people like us. We have no guide, no gold, no gift worth the name. What we have is a stolen soul, three wolves, and luck that mostly runs the other direction. And I know that memories of love are the highest coin in that place. You told me that yourself. So I've been thinking about what I'd pay."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"I don't need to hear it. The answer is no."

"The answer," she said, with the patience of someone correcting a transcription error, "is that I would trade every memory I have of you. Every single one. The border stones, the river, the burned ledger, this cave — all of it. If that's what the road costs, I'll pay it, and I'll wake up tomorrow not knowing why I'm walking beside a stranger, and I'll walk beside him anyway, because the stranger will be going where my sister-in-law-to-be is being kept, and apparently I don't abandon people even when I can't remember why I promised to stay." She finally looked at him straight on. "That's the offer. That's what I'd give. Now you can be angry."

The fire had burned low. In the dimness her face was half shadow, half ember-light, and utterly calm, and that calm frightened him more than the hunters, more than the snow, because he knew what it cost her to hold it.

"No," he said again, and his voice cracked on it. "Mara. No. Do you understand what you're describing? You'd be *erased*. Not hurt — erased. The person who burned the ledger, the person who crossed into color holding my hand because she was terrified and did it anyway — that person would be gone, and some stranger wearing her face would finish the errand and never know what it cost. I didn't drag you out of that valley so you could unmake yourself buying my sister back. I never asked you to—"

"You never asked me to burn the ledger either. I did it anyway. That's rather the pattern of me."

"This is different."

"Why?" she asked. "Because it's you being remembered instead of me?" Silence. He had nothing. She nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed an entry. "I thought so. Here's my arithmetic, then, and you can audit it. I spent nine years in that valley copying other people's debts into books so the temple could sell forgiveness by the page. Nine years of a careful, small life, and I told myself it was contentment, and some nights I almost believed it. Then a wolf-keeper looked at the border stones like he was listening to something beyond them, and I burned a book, and for the first time in my life I am doing something that *matters* — and you want me to protect my comfort at the expense of the only two people on this road I love?" Her voice finally broke its level line, just barely. "I will not go back to being small, Wendell. Even if small is safe. Especially if small is safe."

"It's not love if you forget it," he said. "It's just obedience with extra steps."

"Then make it love," she shot back. "Make it so real it survives the cutting. Because I've been thinking about that too — what memory actually is. I copy documents for a living. I know the difference between a record and the thing recorded. The memory is the record, Wendell. The love is the thing. Burn the record and the thing is still true. It happened. It changed me. Some part of me that isn't filed anywhere will still turn toward you in a room full of strangers, and neither of us will know why, and that will have to be enough."

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth and found the argument had gone out of him like heat from a dying fire, and what was left underneath was simpler and much worse: she meant it. She had done the sums and she meant it, and the terrible, unbearable truth was that he understood — because he had spent six years doing exactly the same arithmetic about himself. Trading pieces of himself, piece by piece, for a chance at his sister. He had simply never had anything left that he minded losing.

Until her.

"I can't lose you twice," he said. The words came out before he could weigh them. "I lost Lira when I was fourteen. I've spent my whole life since building myself around that hole. And then you — you walked into the wolf-pens with your ink-stained hands and your terrible fearless ledgers, and I let myself think, for the first time, that the hole might fill in. And now you're standing at the edge of it offering to jump in." His voice dropped. "I love you. I have loved you since before the border stones, since before the proclamation, since the third winter you came out to the pens in the snow to yell at me about feed ledgers and stayed until dark talking about nothing. I love you plainly, with no exchange attached. No payment. No price. Which means I cannot let you buy passage with yourself, because love that lets you do that isn't love. It's just another court."

The cave went very quiet. Outside, the false snow kept falling in its patient rows.

Mara stared at him. For once in her life she seemed to have nothing to transcribe.

"Well," she said finally, unsteadily. "That was — you've been sitting on that for nine years?"

"Longer."

"You absolute coward."

"Yes."

"I love you too," she said, "obviously. Since the pens. Since the snow. I thought I was being subtle."

"You were not subtle. The wolves knew. Wolves always know."

She laughed — wet, cracked, real — and then she was crying, quietly, the way she did everything, and he pulled her in against his chest and she let him, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all. The fire sank to embers. One of the wolves rose, circled, and lay down across the cave mouth like a door closing.

They didn't resolve it. That was the honest truth of that night: they argued until arguing wore out, and the argument lay down between them like a fourth wolf, unsolved, breathing. She would trade her memories if the road demanded it. He would find another way or die preventing hers. Both of them knew the other meant it. Both of them held on anyway.

"Whatever happens down there," he murmured into her hair, near sleep, "we finish it together. Nobody pays alone. Promise me."

"I promise," she said, and he felt the promise settle over them both like snowfall — soft, inevitable, and impossible to dig out from under.

Far below them, past miles of frozen rock, lights were beginning to move in the under-mountain dark. And at the cave mouth, the youngest wolf lifted her head, sniffed the wind sliding down off the pass, and began, very softly, to growl.
