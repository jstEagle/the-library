## 12. The Under-Mountain Road

The wolves came at dusk on the third day above the tree line, and they did not come the way wolves come.

They arrived out of the snowfall without sound — six of them, grey as old iron, moving with a deliberateness that made the hair rise on my arms. Not hunting. Not fleeing. *Arriving*, the way a delegation arrives, or a funeral procession. The largest stopped ten paces from Wendell and sat down in the snow as if it had been sitting there for years and we were the ones late to the meeting.

Wendell went very still.

"Greyspine," he said softly. It was not a name I knew. It sounded less like a name than like a greeting between people who had once been close and were now careful with each other.

"You know this one?"

"I raised her from a pup." He crouched, slow, and the wolf — Greyspine — let him press his forehead to hers. "Before the valley. Before any of it." He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before: shame, worn smooth by years of handling. "I left them when I ran. I told myself they'd be safer without me. Wolves don't get hunted for what they carry."

"And now?"

"Now they've found me anyway." He laughed, but it didn't have any joy in it. "Or they followed the vial. Souls call to each other, Mara. Even locked in glass. Even across a valley that eats magic." He stood. "They're not here to see me."

I looked at the six wolves sitting in the falling snow, patient as stones, all facing the same direction — north, toward the black shoulder of the mountain where the passes ended and the world's maps gave up.

"They're here to take us down," I said.

"The under-mountain road," Wendell said. "The old shafts. Every fugitive hears about it eventually, and every fugitive prays they never have to use it. There's no other way into the court. And no way out of these passes before the winter closes them — a winter that isn't natural, that's being *fed*." He exhaled, breath streaming white. "I hoped we'd find another answer. There isn't one."

* * *

We slept that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, the wolves arranged around us in a ring, and I learned something about wolves that no shepherd in our valley would believe: they give warmth deliberately. Greyspine pressed herself along my back like a wall against the cold, and when I woke in the grey hour before dawn, she was watching me with amber eyes that held something uncomfortably like pity.

Wendell was already awake, sitting at the mouth of the hollow, the soul vial cradled in both hands. In the pre-dawn light the thing inside it glowed faintly, a color I still can't name — not gold, not blue, something like the memory of a summer afternoon seen through water.

"Tell me about her again," I said. "Your sister."

He didn't turn around. "Nell. Nellwen. She was nine when they took it." His voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who has said a thing so many times inside his own head that saying it aloud costs nothing anymore. "Our village owed the mountain. Everyone's village owed the mountain; that's how it works. They came with scales and ledgers, same as your temple, same as everywhere. My gift showed early — small things, warming milk, coaxing a stubborn fire. Worth money. They priced me against the debt and I wasn't enough, so they took the difference from her." A pause. "A child's soul weighs almost nothing, they said. Almost nothing. But it balances a ledger line."

"And you stole it back."

"Took me four years to get inside. Four years carrying wood, hauling ore, being useful and stupid and harmless. And then one night I walked out with my sister's whole self in a bottle, and I ran until I found the one place in the world their magic couldn't follow." He finally turned. "The valley. Your valley. Where I've been hiding behind your temple walls for seven years, keeping wolves, waiting for them to forget me."

"They never forgot."

"No. It was permitted." He said it like a man spitting out something rotten. "That's what I keep coming back to. Everything I thought was luck was allowance. But that's a problem for later. Right now the only problem is getting down the mountain alive."

* * *

The mine entrance was a wound in the mountainside, half-swallowed by snow, framed with rotting timbers carved — I had to look twice to be sure — with names. Hundreds of names, cut deep and then deeper, some scratched over with newer cuts, the way a man scratches out a debt he's paid.

"Miners' marks?" I asked.

"Souls' marks," Wendell said. "Everyone who went down wrote their name at the door. So someone would know they'd been here." He touched one of the older carvings gently, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "Ready?"

No. I was not ready. I said yes anyway.

The first hundred paces were ordinary dark — the honest kind, the kind a lantern fixes. Then the lantern light began to bend. Not flicker: *bend*, leaning toward the walls as if the stone were drinking it, and in that leaned light I saw the first of them.

Souls. Trapped in the rock itself, pressed into seams of quartz like flowers pressed in a book. Small lights, dim and various — some warm, some cold, one a sickly green that made my teeth ache. They weren't bright enough to read by. They weren't trying to be. They were just *there*, the way scars are there, and the tunnel went down and down, and there were more of them at every turn, hundreds, thousands, a geology of the sold.

"Oh," I said. It was all I could manage.

"Don't touch the walls," Wendell said quietly. "And whatever you hear — and you will hear — remember that the mountain lies. That's its native language."

* * *

The whispering started an hour in.

At first I took it for water, the way you do — the mind reaching for innocent explanations. A drip, an echo, wind through a crack. But water doesn't say your name. Water doesn't say it in your mother's voice.

"*Mara.*"

I stopped walking. Ahead of me, Wendell's lantern swayed. Behind me, Greyspine pressed her cold nose into my palm, deliberate as a hand on the shoulder.

"It's starting already," Wendell said. He'd turned back, and his face in the bent light was grim. "It's faster with some people. Scribes, maybe. People who spend their lives listening."

"That's Mother's voice." My own voice came out strange, thin, borrowed. "She's been dead eleven years. She died in the fever winter. I sat with her. I *know*—"

"I know." He crossed back to me, and I hated how steady he was, how he got to be the calm one while the mountain peeled the floor out from under my sanity. "The court harvests memories, Mara. It knows what's in them. Somewhere in everything the mountain has bought and stolen, there's a memory of your mother's voice — maybe even one of yours, sold by someone who heard it. It's bait. It's always bait."

"*Mara, love, it's cold down here. Come sit with me.*"

The voice came from the wall to my left, warm and amused and exact, the little laugh at the end of it, the way she'd say *love* like a secret. Eleven years dead and I would have known that voice blindfolded in a thunderstorm.

"Mara." Wendell's hands were on my shoulders. "Look at me. Not the wall. Me."

"She sounds—" My throat closed. "She sounds *happy*. Why would it make her sound happy? If it wanted to frighten me—"

"Because frightened people run. Grieving people walk toward the voice." He said it simply, without cruelty, and that simplicity was the only thing that saved me. "It's not showing you your mother. It's showing you what grief does. Come on. Keep talking to me instead. Tell me anything. Tell me about the ledger you burned."

So I talked. I told him about the burn — how the parchment caught faster than I expected, how Elder Corrin's face went white, how I stood in the scriptorium holding a candlestick like a weapon while three elders shouted and the smoke smelled of tallow and ruin. I told him about running through the cloister with my scribe's ink still on my fingers, about the border stones humming as we passed them, about how the magic-dead air of the valley had felt, all my life, like a blanket, and how crossing out of it had felt like being born.

He listened. That was the thing about Wendell — everyone in the valley thought he was quiet because he had nothing to say, and the truth was he listened the way other men spoke, completely, with his whole attention, so that when you finished talking you felt *held*. The whispers kept on. I just stopped hearing them over the sound of my own voice and his silence making room for it.

* * *

We made camp in a widened chamber where the shaft forked, and I learned why the fugitives prayed never to use this road.

There were things living in the forks. Not creatures, exactly. Remnants. In the corner of the chamber farthest from our fire — a fire Wendell coaxed from wet timber with a flick of two fingers, openly now, because what was the point of hiding down here — something sat that had maybe been a person. It was shaped like a seated figure and colored like ash, and it rocked slowly, and it whispered a single word over and over in a voice like sand: "*paid, paid, paid, paid.*"

"Don't look directly at it," Wendell murmured. "They're what's left when the mountain takes everything and the body keeps walking for a while. They're not dangerous. They're just — sad. Sad is catching, down here. Guard your sadness like coin."

I fed the fire and tried not to count the trapped souls in the ceiling above me. I failed. Four hundred and eleven that I could see from where I sat, and the tunnel continued beyond the light.

"Wendell." I kept my voice low. "When we get to the court. When you trade yourself for Nell. What happens to me?"

He was quiet long enough that the ash-thing's whisper seemed loud. "You walk out," he said finally. "With her. You take her up the road, past the marks at the door, and you don't stop until you're in sunlight. That's the whole of the plan. That's the only part that matters."

"And you?"

"Mara."

"You fade," I said. "Whatever giving a soul to the mountain means — you fade. Don't dress it up. I've copied enough euphemism in ledgers to know when a number's being hidden."

He looked at me across the fire, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and for a moment he let me see all of it — the fear, the guilt, the terrible arithmetic he'd been doing since the day he stole his sister back. "Yes," he said. "I fade."

"Then we find another way."

"There isn't another way."

"There's always another way. I'm a scribe. I know for a fact there's always another column, another page, another ledger. Numbers lie all the time because people write them lying." My voice cracked and I hated it. "You don't get to decide alone this time. Not anymore. Whatever happens under that mountain, it happens to both of us, and I will not be carried out like luggage while you—"

"*Paid,*" whispered the ash-thing, and then, horribly, in a new voice — my mother's voice, warm, amused — it added: "*come sit with me, love.*"

I was on my feet before I knew I'd moved. Greyspine was up too, hackles high, a growl rolling out of her like distant thunder. The ash-thing rocked. The trapped souls in the ceiling pulsed, all together, like a single vast slow heartbeat, and for one vertiginous moment I understood — actually understood, in my body — that the entire mountain was one organism, one mouth, and we were already halfway down its throat.

Wendell pulled me back down beside him. His hand was shaking. It was the first time I'd ever felt it shake.

"We rest," he said. "Then we go down. And we stay together, and we talk, and we don't listen. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said, and did not tell him that even as I said it, some traitorous chamber of my heart was already composing a reply to my dead mother.

* * *

The second day underground, the road began to speak in earnest.

It learned fast. By midmorning it had abandoned my mother and tried Wendell's sister — a child's voice calling *brother? brother, is that you?* — and when that failed, it tried something cleverer: it used my own voice, reading aloud, in the cadence I used for copying, the flat beautiful monotone of a scribe at work. It read entries from the court's soul-ledger, entries I couldn't possibly know, and I understood with a lurch that it was showing me the future, or a future, or a lie shaped like one, and that the distinction might not survive the journey.

By afternoon it had stopped trying to lure and started trying to wear. The whispers became a tide, dozens of voices braided together, the sold and the bought and the buried all speaking at once, and walking through it was like walking through rain made of other people's lives. I kept my hand on Greyspine's back. I kept talking to Wendell. I told him about the temple library, about the smell of vellum, about the apprentice who snored, about anything, everything, a dam of small words against a flood of large ones.

And then, near evening, in a gap between the voices, I heard it again — clear, close, coming not from the walls but from *ahead*, from the deep:

"*Mara. Love. You always were the clever one. Listen carefully — there's a door below the ninth landing, behind the mark of the scales. The Regent fears it. Go through it and save him, save your wolf-boy, save them all.*"

And the voice paused, and laughed my mother's laugh, and said:

"*Trust me. Would I ever lie to you?*"

I stopped dead on the road, lantern trembling, and looked at Wendell, whose face had gone pale as bone.

"It's never offered help before," he whispered. "In all the stories, it never offers help."

Behind us, ahead of us, all around us, the trapped souls flared — every one of them, hundreds strong, pulsing in unison — and far below, faint but unmistakable, something in the mountain began, very softly, to sing.
