## 13. The Court Beneath

The wolves would not go further.

We had followed them down through the dark for what felt like a day and a night and a day again — through mine-shafts where trapped souls burned in the walls like lanterns, through galleries where the whispers of the sold pressed against my ears like cold hands. The grey she-wolf, the one who had led us without hesitation since the fair, stopped at last at a seam of black ice in the rock, a door that had not been carved so much as grown. She sat before it, tail wrapped over her paws, and looked at me with eyes the color of old amber.

"No," I said to her.

She blinked slowly.

"You've come this far." My voice came out rougher than I meant it. "Come the rest."

But she wouldn't. And I understood, in the way you understand things you'd rather not: the mountain did not want witnesses it could not use. The wolves were ours. What waited beyond the ice was not.

"Mara." I turned to her. She stood with her satchel clutched to her chest, the way she always held it when she was afraid — as if the ledgers inside were armor. Her breath fogged in air that should not have been cold this deep, where no winter had any business reaching. But the court beneath the mountain made its own weather. Everyone knew the stories. I had simply never believed I'd walk into one.

"Tell me again," she said.

"The bargain. I offer myself. My gift, my soul, everything they'd want from a gifted man. In exchange, they release my sister's soul from their ledger. They let her go free — free meaning *away*, back up the road, out of their hands. That's all I'm asking for. That's all."

"And me?"

I looked at her. I had been looking at her for years across the temple courtyard, across the wolf pens, across the small careful distance I'd built between us to keep her safe, and I still hadn't learned how to do it without something in my chest going quiet and bright at once.

"You stay out of it," I said. "You stay by the wall. You say nothing. You let me talk."

"Wendell—"

"*Mara.*" I took her shoulders. Her collarbones felt fragile under my hands, though I knew better; Mara Halloran was the strongest person I had ever known, stronger than any gifted fool who could light a fire with his breath. "Everything I'm about to do only works if they have no reason to look at you. You're not on their ledger. You're not in their hunt. You're a scribe who got lost. Make them believe it."

Her jaw set in that way it did when she was about to burn a ledger or steal a key or do some other quietly catastrophic thing in the name of the people she loved. "And if they don't believe it?"

"Then run. Back up the road. The wolves will take you as far as the passes."

"I'm not leaving without you."

"You might have to."

"No," she said, with the flat certainty of someone reading aloud from a text she has memorized. "I won't."

There was no time to argue, and anyway we had both said everything there was to say in the cave, in the dark, holding each other while the snow fell wrong outside. So I did the only thing left. I leaned down and pressed my forehead to hers, and I let myself have three seconds — three seconds of her breath, her warmth, the ink-smell of her hair — and then I stepped back and put on the face I wore for strangers, the empty one, the keeper's face.

I knocked on the ice.

It opened like an eye.

* * *

The hall beneath the mountain was beautiful, and that was the worst thing about it.

I had expected horror. I had spent the whole descent preparing myself for horror — bone rooms, iron cages, the machinery of a place that bought souls to feed its endless winter. Instead the passage opened onto a vaulted cavern hung with chandeliers of blue flame, and the floor was pale marble veined with silver, and music was playing somewhere, thin and sweet, from instruments I couldn't see. Courtiers moved through the light in gowns and coats stitched with frost-pearls, laughing softly at one another's wit. It looked like the ballroom of some impossible northern palace. It looked like a painting of joy.

Then I saw what the laughter was made of.

Every courtier wore memories. That was the fashion here — I understood it within moments, the way you understand a language you thought you'd forgotten. They wore them like jewels. A woman near the entrance had a string of them at her throat, small glass beads that glowed faintly gold, and each bead held a scene I could feel from six feet away: a wedding, a birth, a first snowfall seen through a child's eyes. A young man dancing had pinned memories to his lapels like medals, and each time he spun, the memories flared brighter, and his partners leaned toward him with hungry, admiring faces. None of the glow belonged to them. That was the point. That was the whole economy of the place. They had sold whatever burned in themselves and bought the burning of others, and now they glittered, and the glitter was secondhand.

"They're beautiful, aren't they?"

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, and then from directly ahead, because the crowd was parting, bowing, drawing back like water around a keel.

He was tall and spare and dressed in white so deep it hurt to look at, and he wore no jewels at all. He didn't need them. His eyes were the color of glacier ice and just as old, and when he smiled at me it was with perfect, patient kindness, the kindness of a man who has already read the last page of your book.

"The Winter Regent," Mara breathed beside me, and I wanted to scream at her, *by the wall, by the wall* — but the Regent's gaze had already touched her and slid away, finding nothing worth keeping, or so I prayed with everything I had.

"My lord Regent." I bowed. Keeper's face. Empty hands. "I've come to make a trade."

"Have you." He drifted closer. Up close I could see that his skin had a faint translucence, like wax lit from within, like something that had been burning itself as fuel for a very long time. "And what does a wolf-keeper from a magic-dead valley think he owns that a court might want?"

"A gift," I said. "A true one. Old blood, unregistered, unsold. Me."

Murmurs rippled through the courtiers like wind through wheat. Somewhere a memory-bead chimed as its owner gripped it too hard.

The Regent tilted his head. "Gifts are common enough. We harvest them by the dozen every hunting season. You've seen the snows."

"Not like me." I kept my voice level. Keeper's voice. The voice I used on frightened animals. "You know what I am. You've known for years. You let me hide in your blind spot all this time because you were waiting to see what I'd do with it."

Something flickered behind the glacier eyes. Not surprise. Interest.

"Go on."

"I have something of yours," I said. "Something taken from this court nine years ago. A soul in a vial. I've carried it across half the world, and I've never opened it, and I never will, because it isn't mine. It belongs to a girl named Lira Wendellsdottir, and I want it returned to her — released, freed, struck from your ledger — and in exchange you can have all of me. Every spark. The gift, the soul, the name. Rendered down however you render things. I'll walk into your vats myself."

The music stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-phrase, the way a heart stops, and in the silence I heard the chandeliers humming, and I heard the walls whispering with the voices of the sold, and I heard Mara's breathing go very carefully shallow beside me.

The Regent smiled wider.

"Oh," he said softly, "you sweet, stubborn boy. You think you're negotiating." He began to walk a slow circle around us, and the courtiers turned to follow him with their borrowed light. "Nine years ago a thief slipped into my vaults and stole a soul that was already paid for. Do you know why he lived? Why the hounds lost him, why the borders hid him, why every hunter in my service somehow, mysteriously, failed?" He completed the circle and stopped in front of me, close enough that I could smell the cold coming off him, clean and mineral and ancient. "Because I allowed it. Because a soul in hiding is a hook, and hooks need bait, and bait needs time to grow... attached."

My stomach dropped through the marble floor.

"Lira's soul was the hook," I said. My voice sounded far away.

"Your sister's soul was the hook, yes. And you were the line. And every year you grew more careful, more hidden, more *valuable* — a gifted man who had evaded the crown for nearly a decade, carrying stolen court property. Do you know what such a specimen fetches? I didn't send hunters after you, Wendell of the valley. I sent an invitation. And you've RSVP'd beautifully." He spread his translucent hands. "So. You offer yourself. Delightful. Accepted, even. But the girl's soul stays exactly where it is, because it was never the price. It was the lure. And the lure—" his eyes lifted, past my shoulder, and settled on Mara like a physical weight, "—is not what I want."

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"*No.*"

"She's not gifted," the Regent said, musing, circling again, slower now, a connoisseur at an exhibition. "That's what makes her extraordinary. Do you know how rare it is, an ungifted soul that burns? Most of them are candle-flames. Little copies of little lives. But her — I've been watching her since she crossed my border, and she blazes. Love does that. Ungifted love is the purest fuel in creation, because it chooses itself every single day. No enchantment propping it up. Just a woman deciding, over and over, that a man is worth the dark." He stopped before her. "Memories of love are the rarest stock in my collection, child. They burn brightest. They burn *longest*. And she has years of them. Years."

Mara didn't step back. I will give her that until I die, whichever death it turns out to be — she didn't step back, didn't lower her eyes, didn't do anything but stand there in her travel-stained coat with her scribe's hands hanging open at her sides, and look the Winter Regent of the mountain court dead in his borrowed-light face.

"No," she said.

"Everyone sells eventually," he said gently. "It's simply a question of price. And I pay *magnificently*."

"He offered himself," Mara said. "Take the deal he came to make."

"The deal he came to make was made for him, by me, nine years ago." The Regent turned back to me, and his kindness curdled into something older and colder. "Here is the only bargain on my table, wolf-keeper, and it is generous, so attend closely. Your sister's soul goes free. Truly free — released, restored, struck from my ledger, walked to the surface by my own guard. That I will grant, today, gladly, because it costs me nothing and closes an old account." He raised one finger. "In exchange, the woman surrenders her memories of you. All of them. Every hour, every season, every year. Not sold to me — *given*, freely, which makes them lawfully mine and infinitely sweeter. And then she walks out of my mountain remembering nothing but a long road and a stranger she once traveled with, and you remain here, in my collection, forever. Three lives resolved. One hall enriched. Everyone leaves with something."

The silence rang.

I looked at Mara. She was looking at the floor, at the marble veined with silver, and her hands had closed slowly into fists, and I knew — I knew with sick, total certainty — that behind her eyes the arithmetic was already running. Scribe's arithmetic. Ledger arithmetic. Three lives against one woman's remembering. She was weighing it. She was actually weighing it, because that was who she was, because she had burned a ledger and traded her past piece by piece to get us here, because Mara Halloran had never once in her life saved herself at anyone else's expense.

"Don't," I said quietly.

Her eyes came up. Wet. Furious. Steady.

"It's a good price," she said.

"It's theft."

"It's *arithmetic*, Wendell." Her voice cracked on my name and she hated that it cracked, I could see her hating it. "Your sister walks free. You live. I lose—" she stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "I lose some days. Some years. I've already lost pieces of myself on this road. What's the difference between one more hole and—"

"The difference," I said, "is that those holes don't have me in them."

The Regent watched us with the expression of a man watching a fire he has laid himself finally catch. Around us the courtiers had gone still, their borrowed memories glowing in the blue dark, hundreds of secondhand suns attending our ruin.

"Take a night to consider," the Regent said at last, silkily, turning away, his white coat whispering across the marble. "Guests of the court are given the guest wing. You'll find it comfortable. You'll find, in fact, that you cannot leave it — but comfort is a kind of freedom too, down here. We are civilized." At the edge of the light he paused, and glanced back, and his smile was the coldest thing in the cold. "Dinner is at the eighth bell. Wear something warm. The mountain likes to be fed."

Guards of grey glass formed out of the shadows on either side of us — or perhaps they had always been there, waiting to be noticed. Their hands were gentle and absolute.

As they led us away, down a corridor hung with more chandeliers, past doors of frosted silver, I caught Mara's arm and pulled her close and put my mouth against her ear, and I whispered the only plan I had left, the desperate, paper-thin, scribe-shaped hope I had been carrying since the moment I saw the Regent wear nothing at his throat:

"Everything in this court is written down. Everything they've ever bought. Find the ledger, Mara. Find the ledger, and find his name."

Her fingers found mine in the dark and squeezed once.

Behind us, at the end of the hall, a door opened, and a servant in frost-grey livery stepped out to escort us — a hollow-eyed, gentle-faced woman whose soul-glow had been dimmed to almost nothing, and whose face, when the chandelier light crossed it, stopped my heart in my chest.

Lira.

My sister looked at me with polite, empty courtesy, and said, in a voice I had not heard in nine years:

"This way, please. The guest wing is warm."
