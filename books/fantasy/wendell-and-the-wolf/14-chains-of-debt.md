## 14. Chains of Debt

They called it the guest wing.

Mara understood, within an hour of being shown to her rooms, that the name was a courtesy extended by people who had forgotten what ordinary words meant. The doors were oak and iron-banded. The windows were real glass — she hadn't seen true glass since the valley, where everything was warped and greenish and old — but they did not open. The bed was soft. The fire was warm. There were books on the shelf, real books, their spines cracked with use, and when she pulled one down she found the pages hollowed into a tray that held a single crystal goblet of water, chilled exactly to the temperature a tired traveler would want.

Everything in the guest wing wanted something from her. That was the first rule of the court beneath the mountain, and no one had said it aloud. She'd learned it the way you learn the temperature of water — by putting your hand in.

The door had no lock she could see. It didn't need one. When she tried it, an hour after the servant in grey had bowed her inside, the handle turned freely and the door swung open onto a corridor lined with more doors, all of them shut, all of them presumably as unlocked as hers. She walked twenty paces down it. At twenty-one paces, she was standing in front of her own door again, looking at her own hand on her own handle, though she had not turned around.

She tried twice more, counting steps, watching the sconces. On the third attempt she marked the wall with a scratch from her ring — Wendell's parting gift, a plain iron band he'd sworn was nothing, though his hands had shaken fastening it — and watched the scratch slide away behind her like a boat leaving shore.

The corridor was a wheel. She was the spoke.

"Guests always try," said a voice behind her.

She turned. A woman stood at the end of the hall, or rather, a shape of a woman, dressed in the same undyed grey as every servant Mara had seen since they descended. Her face was pleasant and unfinished, like bread before baking. Her eyes were the color of the corridor walls.

"You'll wear yourself out," the woman said kindly. "The wing doesn't like it."

"What is it?" Mara asked. "Magic? A loop?"

The woman tilted her head, considering whether to answer. "It's *paid for*," she said finally. "That's stronger than magic. Magic can be broken. A purchase only ends when both parties agree." She smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes, because Mara suspected there wasn't far for it to reach. "Supper is at the seventh bell. You're expected to attend."

"I'm a prisoner."

"You're a guest," the woman said, with the patience of someone correcting a child's arithmetic. "Prisoners are kept against their will. No one here has ever been kept against their will. That's what makes this place so very sad." And she drifted off down the corridor, through a door Mara would have sworn wasn't there, humming something in three-four time that made Mara's teeth ache.

* * *

The seventh bell rang itself — she never found who struck it — and Mara followed the sound to a small dining room set for two.

Wendell was already there.

Her whole body reacted before her mind caught up: the lurch of relief so violent it was almost nausea, the urge to cross the room and put her hands on his face and confirm he was solid. But the Regent's servants had dressed him — clean linen, a dark coat, his hair combed — and the sight of him groomed and seated like a courtier frightened her more than chains would have. Chains meant they thought you dangerous. This meant they thought you *theirs*.

"They let us eat together," she said, sitting across from him. "That's either kindness or strategy."

"With this court, those are the same thing." His voice was low and rough, and under the table his knee pressed against hers, and that small human pressure nearly undid her. "Are you hurt?"

"No. Are you?"

He turned his hands over on the tablecloth. They were unmarked. "They gave me a room with a window that shows a garden," he said. "There's no garden. I've been to the window. It's painted light and a memory of birdsong. They're showing me what I could have if I cooperate." He looked up. "Mara. Did they ask you anything yet?"

"Not yet. A servant told me supper was at the seventh bell." She hesitated. "She said no one here is ever kept against their will. That everyone agrees."

Wendell's jaw tightened. "That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. Nobody's dragged down here screaming. They're invited. Debted. Traded. They sign things." He pushed food around his plate — the food was perfect, roasted bird and glazed roots, and neither of them touched it. "My sister signed things."

The words dropped between them like stones into still water.

"She's here," Mara said quietly. "You're sure."

"I've been sure since we crossed the threshold. There's a—" He stopped, pressing his knuckles to his mouth. "When you carry a soul vial for eleven years, you learn its weight the way a mother learns a sleeping child's. And when I came down here, I felt the other half of it. Like hearing your own echo from a room you've never entered." He breathed out slowly. "She's here, Mara. And she doesn't know me. I saw her in the hall yesterday, carrying wine for the Regent's table. She looked straight through me."

Mara reached across the table and took his hand. His fingers were cold.

"Tomorrow," she said. "I'll find her tomorrow. You find the vats. We do this together, the way we planned."

"The way we planned," he repeated, and squeezed her hand once, hard, before the door opened and the grey servants came to take the plates away, untouched, and escort them back to their separate cages dressed as comfort.

* * *

She found the sister the next morning, though not by searching.

Mara had spent the night doing what she did best: copying. In her head, without ink, she built a ledger of the guest wing. The number of steps in each direction before the wheel caught her. The intervals of the bells. Which sconces burned steady and which flickered in a rhythm too regular to be flame. Scribes survived by noticing; it was the one skill the temple had given her that the mountain couldn't tax.

On the second morning, a servant came to bring her linens, and Mara noticed that the woman moved through the corridor's trick without slowing, without counting, the way you walk through your own house. And she noticed something else: a ring of keys at the woman's belt, iron and bone, and hanging from it one key that was different — darker, older, wrapped at the bow with a thread of red silk.

The woman was hollow. Mara saw it immediately, the way you see a burned-out house from the road — the structure standing, the life gone out of it. Her movements were smooth but costless, unattended, like a puppet whose operator had stepped away. When Mara spoke to her, she answered pleasantly about linens and baths and the seventh bell, and when Mara asked her name she smiled blankly and said, "We don't use names for staff."

But when Mara, on impulse, hummed three notes of the tune the first servant had been humming, the woman's hands went still on the folded sheets. Just for a moment. Her fingers trembled against the linen like a compass needle finding north and being forbidden to point.

"There," Mara said softly. "What was that?"

"A song," the woman said. Her voice had gone strange and thin. "I don't— I don't remember learning it."

"Who taught it to you?"

"I don't remember." The trembling spread up her wrists. Then, like a shutter closing, her face smoothed over, and she finished folding the sheets with perfect care and left, and the keys sang against each other at her hip, and Mara sat on her beautiful bed in her beautiful cage and felt grief arrive ahead of understanding.

She knew that hollowness. She had seen its outline in Wendell's face every time he spoke of Lira. And now she understood, with terrible clarity, what the Regent intended for her — not the dungeon, not the knife, but this. A pleasant room. A compliant body. A self sold off piece by piece until what remained was serviceable and smiling and empty, dusted daily by other hollow women, humming songs she couldn't remember learning.

*That* was the future. Not death. Inventory.

* * *

She met Lira properly on the fourth day.

Mara had taken to walking the wheel-corridor each morning, counting, mapping, letting the wing think it had defeated her while she memorized its seams. On the fourth morning, another door in the corridor stood open — genuinely open, which had never happened — and a young woman stood in it holding a stack of napkins, watching her with mild, friendly curiosity.

"You're the new guest," the young woman said. "The one from the valley. They say valley people are very serious." She smiled. Her face was lovely and lit from nowhere, like a lamp with the wick trimmed too low. "I'm Lira."

Mara's heart stopped and restarted somewhere in her throat.

Up close, the resemblance to Wendell was unbearable — the same broad cheekbones, the same gray eyes, though his were always watchful and hers were weather, changeable and calm, calm, always calm. She had his hands. Mara knew those hands; she had held them across a table two nights ago.

"I'm Mara," she managed.

"Do you need help finding your room? The wing does that sometimes. Moves the doors." Lira said it the way you'd complain about weather. "It took me a year to stop getting lost. Or—" she paused, and something flickered far back in her eyes, a fish turning deep under ice, "—or maybe I've always worked here. I can't quite—"

"Lira," Mara said carefully. "Do you have a brother?"

The question landed on her face like snow. For a moment — one long, suspended moment — the lamp-flame behind her eyes guttered and rose, and her lips parted, and her hand drifted up toward her own chest as if reaching for something hung there once, long ago.

Then it closed again.

"I had a family," Lira said gently, the way you tell a child that a pet has died. "Everyone here had a family. That's how you get here. But the court takes care of us now, and it's better this way. Wanting things hurts. Here, you mostly stop wanting." She shifted the napkins in her arms. "You'll see. Give it a season."

"I don't want a season," Mara heard herself say, and her voice cracked on it. "Lira, listen to me. Your brother is alive. He came all this way for you. He carried—" She stopped herself, because *he carried your soul in a bottle for eleven years* was not a sentence for a hallway, and because Lira's face had begun to do something worse than confusion.

It had begun to do fear.

"Don't," Lira whispered. The friendliness drained out of her like water from a cracked cup, and underneath it Mara glimpsed something raw and quick and terrified — the fish under the ice, thrashing. "Don't say things like that. If you make me remember something, they'll know. They always know. And then they come with the ledger, and they write it down, and then it's really gone." Her knuckles had gone white around the napkins. "Please. Please don't be kind to me. Kindness costs."

And she fled — fled, napkins and all, through a door that swallowed her — and Mara stood alone in the wheeling corridor with her heart hammering and her eyes burning, understanding at last the full architecture of the cage.

It wasn't the loop in the hall. It wasn't the locked windows. It was this: the prisoners policed their own memories, flinching from kindness, terrified of their own pasts, because remembering anything the court hadn't authorized got written down and *collected*. The Regent didn't need guards. He had taught the stolen to guard themselves.

Mara pressed her forehead against the cool stone wall and let herself shake, once, briefly, the way you allow a horse to shudder before tightening the girth.

Then she lifted her head.

*No,* she thought. *You don't get to have this. You don't get her fear. You don't get his sister. You don't get my memories, and you don't get my love, and you don't get to turn me into a smiling woman humming a song I can't remember.*

She was a scribe. She had spent ten years copying other people's debts in a temple that sold her name to save its own. She knew ledgers. She knew that every entry had two sides, and every debt could be contested, and every record — *every record* — had a keeper.

Somewhere beneath this glittering court was a master ledger with her name in it. And somewhere above, in a valley that forgot, a girl who copied columns of figures had learned the most dangerous thing a powerless person can learn:

Paperwork runs the world. And paperwork can be stolen.

* * *

That evening, the linen servant came again, keys singing at her hip.

Mara had prepared. She'd spent the afternoon practicing, seated by the false window with her hands in her lap, reaching for the thing she had borrowed — because that was the truth of it, she had no gift of her own, the valley had seen to that, but Wendell had pressed his palm to hers on the road and shown her how a gift could be *lent*, the way you lend warmth by sharing a cloak. It was fading in her, thinning daily, a candle burning down. She had perhaps days of it left.

Days were enough.

She waited until the woman bent over the linens, until the hollow eyes were fixed on the folding, and then Mara reached — not with her hands, but with the borrowed warmth, a thread of it, fine as scribe's ink — toward the ring of keys at the woman's belt.

The dark key. The one wrapped in red silk. The one that didn't match the others, that hung apart from them like a word in a foreign language.

The thread of warmth touched cold iron.

The key grew *light*. That was the only way to describe it — its weight simply transferred, quietly, from the ring to a fold of Mara's sleeve where it lay like a fallen leaf, and the woman never paused, never faltered, folding and stacking and smoothing, because the keys still hung at her hip and jingled when she moved and only the one key's weight had gone elsewhere, and weight, Mara suspected, was the only thing the hollow truly felt anymore.

The woman finished. Bowed. Left. The door sighed shut behind her.

Mara sat very still for a hundred heartbeats. Then she opened her sleeve.

The key lay in the fabric, black iron, cold as riverbed stone, the red silk faded to the color of an old wound. It was heavier than it should have been — heavier than iron, heavy the way a secret is heavy — and when she closed her hand around it, she heard, faint and far, a sound like a bell rung underwater, and somewhere below her feet, deep in the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and *noticed*.

Mara closed her fist tighter and smiled, and it was not a nice smile.

Down the corridor, the seventh bell began to ring.

Time, she thought, tucking the key into the lining of her dress, to go and see what it opened.
