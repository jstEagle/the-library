## 15. The Regent's Gift

They came for me at the hour the court called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

Two servants in grey silk, faces smooth as river stones, and behind them a third figure I hadn't seen before — a chamberlain with keys that chimed like small bells when he walked. The wolves had been taken from me at the gates. I had heard them howling as they dragged me down the last corridor, and the sound had followed me into sleep like a stone follows a body into water.

"His Grace will see you now," the chamberlain said.

I stood. My wrists were bare — they had taken the iron cuffs days ago, or hours; time moved strangely beneath the mountain — and I understood what that meant. They weren't afraid of me anymore. Or they had never been afraid of me at all.

The thought should have frightened me. It only made me tired.

* * *

The Regent's hall was worse than I remembered from the stories the fugitives told around their fires. Stories said it was beautiful. Stories were right, and that was the horror of it.

Pillars of blue ice rose to a ceiling scattered with trapped lights — souls, I realized, each one a small cold star, burning without warmth because they had been taught to burn that way. The floor was black glass, and in it I could see reflections that didn't match: a younger Regent, an older one, a woman's face surfacing and sinking like something drowned. The Winter Court wore its purchases openly here. Every jewel on every collar was a memory someone had sold. Every crown was a ledger.

And on the throne of woven frost sat the Regent himself, and he looked — this was the part no story had prepared me for — *kind*. A long face, silver-bearded, eyes the pale grey of a winter sky just before snow. He could have been a grandfather mending nets. He could have been anyone's lost father.

"Wendell," he said, and my name in his mouth sounded like it belonged to him. "Sit. You've been standing in cells for weeks. Sit."

There was a chair beside the throne. Ordinary wood. Someone had thought about that chair. Someone had decided that a man brought before the Regent in chains would find mercy more disarming than menace.

I stayed standing. "Where is Mara?"

"Safe. Fed. Asleep, I imagine — she hasn't been sleeping well." He said it gently, the way you'd tell a farmer his cattle were warm in the barn. "You'll see her again. That depends entirely on the conversation we're about to have."

"I'm not having conversations with soul-thieves."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you'll listen. You've come all this way to listen. Six years of hiding, two months of running, a burned ledger, a dead hunter, a market burned to ash behind you — all of it to arrive at this room." He spread his hands. "Indulge an old man. Let me show you my collection."

He rose, and the hall changed around him — not magic exactly, but attention, the whole court leaning in like an audience. He walked, and I followed, because Mara was asleep somewhere in this mountain and the fastest road to her ran through him.

He stopped before a pillar of ice and pressed his palm flat against it.

"You stole from me six years ago," he said. "A vial. One soul, out of thousands. Do you know what I remember about that night?"

"Nothing," I said. "You weren't there."

"I wasn't there," he repeated, delighted. "Exactly. And yet I can tell you the color of your sister's hair the day she was taken. I can tell you she sang while the clerks wrote her name. I can tell you that you wept in the snow outside the counting-house and swore you'd make it right." His pale eyes found mine. "How do you suppose I know that?"

The cold in the hall had nothing to do with the temperature.

"The guards let you through," he said. "Every door. Every ward. You were the most gifted thief ever to walk these halls, boy, and you strolled out with a single vial and never once wondered why the locks opened like flowers."

"They didn't open—"

"The third door was warded against everything except a thief carrying stolen goods. Did you feel it? Of course you didn't. You were grieving. Grief makes such wonderful blindfolds." He turned back to the pillar, tracing a line of frost. "We chose you, Wendell. We read the harvest reports and we chose the man whose love for his sister would make him reckless enough to run, and guilty enough to hide, and clever enough to survive in the one place our hounds couldn't follow. Do you know why that mattered?"

My voice came out very small. "Why."

"Because a stolen soul doesn't stay quiet. It *aches*. It calls to the ones who sold it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards — but our instruments are old, and the valley's dead ground muffles even that. So we needed the ache to move. To travel. To gather other things to itself along the way." He smiled, and it was the gentlest expression I had ever been menaced by. "A soul-vial carried by a guilty man draws attention. Hunters follow hunters. Gifteds flee toward gifteds. Fairs form. Communities kindle. And everywhere you went, Wendell, you left a trail of names for my proclamations to harvest. The valley was the one place we couldn't reach — so we made the valley come to us. You were the bait. You were always the bait."

* * *

I don't remember sitting down. At some point the ordinary chair was under me, and I hated myself for it.

"You're lying," I said.

"Am I?" The Regent drifted along the pillars, touching them one by one, and where he touched, images bloomed in the ice: faces, hundreds of them, gifteds I knew. The tinker with the laughing hands. The midwife who could warm a room. Children from the fair. Faces from villages we'd passed through, emptied by the hunt. "Tell me which part. That the proclamation preceded your flight? It did — by four days. Sealed orders, dated before you ever crossed the border stones. That the hunters found you so quickly in the high country? They were following *us*, boy. We fed them your heading like crumbs to geese." He paused at a pillar where a familiar face floated — Sef, the broker, looking younger and terrified. "Even your little broker. Do you think a man sells out a fair full of gifteds to save his own skin by accident? Sef has been on my payroll since before you met him. He was the one who told the fair's elders a guide to the under-mountain road existed. Who do you imagine whispered that rumor?"

The floor tilted. Or I did.

Mara. Every mile of it replayed behind my eyes — her hand in mine at the border stones, her laughter the first time the river sang, her face in the cave when she said *I would trade my memories*, her fingers stealing keys in a cage while I sat in another one believing I'd finally outmaneuvered them. She had burned a ledger for me. She had sold pieces of herself, day by day, memory by memory, and every piece had landed exactly where this smiling old man wanted it.

"She came because of me," I said. "Her memories—"

"Are exquisite." He said it with genuine appreciation, a connoisseur. "Memories of love burn brightest — everyone in the trade knows it — but hers are rare even among those. A scribe's mind. She remembers in *ink*, Wendell. In structure. When she gave up the day she met you, she gave it up complete — every sensory thread intact, catalogued, cross-referenced. My servants fought over who got to wear it." He sighed happily. "I have worn it twice myself. The temple light through a window. The way you looked at her across a courtyard and pretended not to. Extraordinary craftsmanship. She built that memory the way she builds a page."

Something broke loose in my chest and I was on my feet with my hands around his collar before I knew I'd moved, and the guards didn't stop me, and that was the worst part — they didn't stop me because there was no need. The Regent let me hold him, pale and calm, smiling his grandfather's smile.

"Yes," he said softly. "That. That exact fury. That's what we breed for, you understand. Not power — power is common. *Devotion.* A gifted who loves someone enough to walk into the dark for them. Those are the souls worth keeping. Everything else is chaff." He patted my wrist, once, like soothing a dog. "Now. Sit down, and I'll tell you the good news."

"There's no good news under this mountain."

"There's always good news. It's a matter of accounting." He straightened his collar where I'd crushed it. "Here is mine: none of it was wasted. Your guilt, your flight, her sacrifice — it all arrived here, in this hall, and here is the only place in the world where anything can still be *done*. You wanted to steal your sister back. Fine. Steal her. I'll open the doors myself — you know now that I always have. Take the vial. Take the girl. Walk out." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of snowmelt and old paper. "And in ten years, when the winter has eaten the lowlands and the courts above come begging to me for warmth, you'll know that you carried my bait every step of the road yourself. That's the gift, Wendell. Not freedom. *Understanding.* Most people live their whole lives inside a machine without seeing it. You get to see it. You get to be grateful."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the girl keeps selling memories until there's nothing left to sell, and I keep what remains, and your sister stays in her pillar singing to no one, and the machine grinds on exactly as designed, with or without your cooperation." He shrugged, vast and gentle. "Refusal is also part of the design. It usually produces the most interesting outcomes."

* * *

They walked me back through corridors of humming glass, past the vats where the harvested souls turned like slow weather, and I counted doors out of habit and then stopped counting, because I finally believed him. Every door would open. Every lock would bloom. That was the trap's true shape — not walls, but permission. A maze with no walls at all, where every path led deeper and every exit was an invitation.

At the junction where they'd take me back to my cell, the chamberlain paused.

"She asked about you," he said, without turning. "The scribe. This morning. She asked whether you'd eat, whether you'd slept, whether the wolves were warm." A pause. "She doesn't remember asking. She sold the worry yesterday afternoon. But the asking left a mark. It usually does."

They took me away before I could answer, which was merciful, because I had no answer. Only the Regent's voice running in loops behind my eyes like water through a broken mill: *you were the bait, you were always the bait.*

In the cell, in the dark, I put my forehead against the cold stone and did the only thing left that the machine hadn't predicted, couldn't tax, couldn't wear as a jewel.

I grieved. Quietly. For my sister, for Mara, for the boy who'd crept through open doors believing he was a thief.

And somewhere below the grief, in the place where despair turns to ore, a single stubborn thought began to glow:

He had shown me the machine. Every gear. Every gate.

Which meant I now knew, better than any living soul, exactly where it was weakest — and the Regent, in his magnificent pride, had just handed the blueprint to the one man stupid enough, devoted enough, *bred* enough to use it.

All I needed was a scribe.
