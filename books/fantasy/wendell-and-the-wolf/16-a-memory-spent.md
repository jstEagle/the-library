## 16. A Memory Spent

The servant's name was Liss, and she moved through the guest-wing like water finding cracks.

Mara had watched her for three days before she dared speak to her. Three days of learning the rhythm of the cage: the guards who changed at the hour when the mountain's light dimmed to a bruised amber, the corridor that echoed wrong because it was longer than the doors suggested, the way the Regent's courtiers drifted past the guest-wing windows without ever quite looking in — as if the wing itself were something they'd agreed not to see.

Liss saw. That was the first thing. She carried linens and broth and small kindnesses, and her eyes did the arithmetic no one else in this place would do: *these people are prisoners, and I am part of the machine that keeps them.*

She was young. Younger than Mara had first thought — the mountain aged its servants strangely, or perhaps it simply wore them down until age stopped mattering. Her hands were chapped from cold water. There was ink on the cuff of her sleeve, old ink, the kind that came from copying lists.

A scribe, once. Or still, in the hours they didn't give her credit for.

On the fourth day, Mara spoke.

"You count the trays," she said quietly, as Liss set down the evening broth. "Two for us, one for the sister, one extra you leave outside the third door down. You've been feeding someone the guards forgot."

Liss went very still. The broth steamed between them, smelling of nothing at all — food made by people who had sold their tongues' memories, Wendell had said once, and Mara was beginning to understand what he meant.

"I don't know what you mean," Liss said.

"You count everything," Mara pressed. "I've watched you. Trays, keys, steps, guards. A person who counts is a person keeping a ledger. And a person who keeps a ledger—" she let it hang, "—is waiting for the day the numbers stop adding up."

For a long moment the only sound was the mountain breathing around them, that vast slow pulse beneath the stone that Mara had stopped hearing and started feeling, the way you stop hearing your own heart.

Then Liss sat down on the edge of the bed, which no servant in this place was permitted to do, and looked at her with eyes too old for her face.

"The numbers stopped adding up years ago," she said. "I stayed because my mother is in the vats. Third gallery. They keep her memory circulating in the winter engines." Her voice didn't break. It had gone past breaking into something flat and polished. "If I run, they notice. If I help you and fail, they notice faster. So whatever you're about to ask me, ask me knowing I will say no."

"Good," Mara said. "Because I'm going to ask you anyway, and I'd rather you say no honestly than yes out of pity."

* * *

She laid it out the way she'd laid out a thousand ledger reconciliations in the temple archive: plainly, in order, with the discrepancies marked.

Wendell was held in the deep cells below the Regent's hall. She knew this because she'd heard the guards speak of "the wolf-keeper" with the particular hush reserved for dangerous inventory. The Regent wanted his memories of love — Mara's memories, specifically, though Wendell would never agree to let them be taken, which was why he was in the deep cells instead of the glittering negotiation rooms where the court did its prettier work.

The Regent could wait. The Regent had waited three hundred years; he could wait out one stubborn keeper and one debt-bound scribe.

But the mountain was waking. Mara had felt it in the walls since the night Wendell shattered the first vat — a tremor in the stone, a warmth rising through the mineshafts, the trapped souls in the under-road whispering louder, less hopeless. When the mountain woke fully, the court would seal itself, and everyone inside it — captives, servants, the hollowed sister in the garden — would be locked in with a dying god's tantrum.

They had days. Maybe fewer.

"I need him out of the deep cells before the sealing," Mara said. "And I can't do it alone, and I can't do it with force, and I can't do it with magic I don't have. But I have something the court wants more than almost anything in the world."

Liss's eyes flicked to her — quick, involuntary, hungry in a way she clearly hated. "Memories of love."

"One memory," Mara said. "One trade. Enough to buy a key, or a guard's silence, or an hour of the deep cells standing open. Enough to get him out."

"And after?" Liss asked. "After he's out, then what? You think the Regent won't simply take the rest? He doesn't bargain, scribe. He collects."

"He bargains with me," Mara said, "because I hold something he can't take by force anymore. The master ledger. Every soul he's bought, every memory he wears, every name he's buried — including his own." She watched Liss absorb that. "He takes from me, I burn the record of what he is. He knows I know. That's why we're still alive. That's why the guest-wing has good bread and warm blankets. We're not prisoners here, Liss. We're collateral in a negotiation he's losing."

Something shifted in the servant's face — not hope, exactly. Hope was too expensive a word for this place. Recognition, maybe. The look of a bookkeeper seeing, for the first time, that the accounts could be balanced in her favor.

"What memory?" Liss asked softly.

And there it was. The question Mara had been circling for two days, walking the length of the cage, touching the window glass, watching Wendell's sister tend the frost-flowers in the garden below with her gentle emptied face.

The sister who didn't remember her brother. Who smiled at strangers. Who had been hollowed so thoroughly that even grief had been skimmed off her like cream, leaving something sweet and vacant behind.

That was the price of selling love-memories. Not the loss. The loss was survivable — Mara had lost things before, everyone had. The price was becoming the sister. Becoming someone who could look at the man she loved and feel nothing but polite warmth, and smile, and mean it.

"I haven't chosen yet," Mara lied.

* * *

That night she took inventory.

It was a scribe's habit, and she didn't fight it. She lay on the narrow bed in the amber dark and catalogued what she carried, the way she'd once catalogued temple debts: itemized, valued, weighed against what was owed.

Her mother's voice among the mine-whispers — a lie planted by the mountain, she knew that now, but knowing didn't make it hurt less, and she wasn't selling that one regardless. Her father's hands teaching hers the alphabet. The flood year, when the whole valley had mucked out the temple together and she'd learned that community was just shared misery with better songs. The first ledger she'd copied without a single error, and Elder Vane's single nod, worth more than any praise since.

And Wendell.

Wendell was not one memory. That was the trap of it. He was strata. He was sediment. Years of him, layered:

The border stones, and the way he stood listening to them as if the world beyond were a language he'd been exiled from. She'd loved him for a year before she ever spoke to him properly, loving him the way you love weather — constant, unclaimed, part of the shape of her days.

The day the grey wolf limped into the temple yard with a thorn the length of her finger in its paw, and Wendell had appeared out of nowhere, calm as still water, and said, "He'll bite you," and she'd said, "Obviously," and held the wolf's gaze while Wendell drew the thorn, and afterward he'd looked at her — really looked, the first time — and said, "You're either very brave or very foolish," and she'd said, "Both, usually," and he'd laughed. She hadn't known he could laugh. Nobody in the valley had.

The night he'd told her about the wolves being sentries, his voice low, firelight on his face, the stolen soul-vial warm between them like a second heartbeat. The terror and the rightness of it, both at once.

The cave. *I would trade every memory I have,* she'd told him, *if it means you walk out of this mountain.* His hands in her hair. Love stated plainly at last, no more disguising it as contentment.

The crossing into color — the river singing, the light turning predatory and gorgeous, his hand closing around hers as the valley's dead air gave way to a world that hummed.

Each one a room in a house. Sell one and the house still stands, but the floor creaks where the room used to be, forever, and you flinch every time you cross it.

*Choose,* said the mountain, in the pulse of the stone. *You said you would. You told him you would.*

"But not this one," she whispered to the dark. "Not the yard. Not the wolf. Not the laugh."

The dark didn't answer. The dark was honest that way.

* * *

She found Liss at dawn, in the linen closet that smelled of lye and cold, and said, "I've chosen."

Liss's hands stilled over the folded sheets. "Don't tell me which."

"Why not?"

"Because if I know, I'll carry it. Everything you carry in this place gets weighed eventually." Liss turned, and her face was grim and kind, the kindest thing Mara had seen since the valley. "The exchange works like this. I take you to the extraction alcove off the east gallery — there's one the Regent uses for small trades, private ones, debts between courtiers. It's keyed to my badge. You sit in the chair. You think of the memory, all of it, start to finish, and you *give* it — that's the hard part, you have to open your hand, so to speak. The machine does the rest. It'll take maybe a minute. It feels like longer."

"And then?"

"Then it's in a vial, and the vial goes where I say it goes, and the payment comes back the same way. One hour of the deep cells unwatched. The night guard on the third landing owes me; I've counted what he owes me for six years." Liss met her eyes. "Last chance to walk away. Once it's out of your head, it doesn't come back. Not with time, not with trying. I've seen people stand in front of their own mothers and feel nothing. I've *been* that person, for my mother, before they took her. You don't get to be sad about it later, because later-you won't know what there is to be sad about. The hole doesn't ache. That's the cruelest part. The hole feels like nothing at all."

Mara thought of the sister in the garden, smiling at frost-flowers.

"I know," she said. "I've seen the finished product."

"Then why—"

"Because he's in the deep cells because of me. Because he'd never let me do this, which is why he'll never know until he's free. Because I told him I'd trade anything, and I meant it, and I am not going to be a person who says things and doesn't do them." She heard her own voice go flat and steady, the ledger-voice, the voice that balanced impossible columns. "When?"

"Tonight. The change of watch." Liss hesitated, then, quieter: "There's one thing I can offer. The machine takes the whole memory — but the edges are yours to define. Think of it as a frame. Whatever falls outside the frame stays. Most sellers frame tight. Less pain."

"Or?"

"Or you frame wide, and keep the edges. The smell of the yard that morning. The sound of the valley. The fact that you were happy, even if you don't know why." Liss's voice dropped. "It's not much. But it's the difference between a hole and a scar."

* * *

The alcove was small and white and utterly silent, which somehow made it worse than the whispering mines. A chair of pale wood. A brass armature like a reading stand, holding a vial no bigger than her thumb. The glass caught the amber light and threw it back clean, innocent, empty.

Waiting to be filled.

Mara sat. The wood was cold through her dress.

"Think of it now," Liss murmured, standing by the door with her hand on the mechanism. "All of it. Start to finish. Don't skip. The machine takes what you hold, so hold all of it — the mud in the yard, the thorn, the blood on his fingers, the way he laughed. Hold it like you're copying it out. Final draft. No errors."

So she did.

She built it brick by brick, the way she'd built a thousand fair copies: the grey wolf limping through the temple gate with its ears flat, the thorn ugly in the pad of its paw, the elders clucking and retreating, and Wendell coming across the yard with that unhurried stride, saying *he'll bite you*, and her own voice answering *obviously* before she'd even decided to be brave. The wolf's hot breath. The weight of its skull under her palms. Wendell kneeling close enough that she could smell pine smoke on him. The thorn sliding free. The look he gave her after — that long, startled, level look, as if he'd been watching her for a year and had finally been given permission.

And the laugh. Gods, the laugh. Low and surprised, like it had escaped him rather than been released. *You're either very brave or very foolish.* *Both, usually.* The whole yard bright with morning, the valley safe and small around them, and neither of them knowing — mercifully, kindly not knowing — about hunters, or mountains, or vials, or any of the prices waiting downstream.

She held it all. She held it the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room: carefully, desperately, knowing most of it would spill.

"Ready?" Liss whispered.

"No," Mara said. "Do it anyway."

The machine sang — one clear note, high and thin, a tuning fork struck against bone — and the memory left her.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It was like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying: relief and ruin in the same motion, the cup suddenly light, suddenly empty, her hands still curved around a shape that was no longer there.

The vial filled. Amber-gold, glowing faintly, and inside it — she saw it for half a second before Liss capped it — a flash of grey fur, a sunlit yard, two figures small and laughing.

Then it was corked, and wrapped, and gone into Liss's apron, and Mara sat in the white chair in the white room and waited to feel devastated.

She felt nothing.

That was the truth of it, the thing no one could have made her believe: she felt *nothing*. There was a smooth blankness where the morning had been, like a page cut cleanly from a ledger, and the pages before and after were intact, and the absence didn't ache because absence, done properly, is painless. She knew — scribe's knowledge, record-keeper's knowledge — that something had been removed. She could see the stitching. But the content was gone, and grief needs content the way fire needs air.

*The hole feels like nothing at all.*

Except.

At the very edge of the blankness, framed wide, kept — the smell of mud and morning. Pine smoke, though she couldn't have said whose hearth it came from. And happiness, thin and sourceless, a warmth with no story attached, like sunlight falling through a window into an empty room.

She sat with it. She let herself sit with it for ten full breaths, honoring the cost, refusing to pretend it was cheap.

Then she stood up.

"Tonight," she said to Liss, and her voice was steady, and she hated how steady it was, and she used it anyway. "The third landing. The hour of the change."

"It's done," Liss said. "The guard will sleep. The cell stands open from the first bell to the second." She paused at the door, the vial a small gold weight in her apron, and looked back at Mara with something close to reverence. "Whoever he is — whoever he was to you —"

"I don't know," Mara said honestly. "But I kept the edges. I'll find out."

* * *

The hours until the change of watch passed like stones swallowed whole.

She walked the cage. She ate the good bread and couldn't taste it. She stood at the window and watched Wendell's sister in the garden below, hollowed and gentle, and made herself look — made herself see the future she'd just purchased a small piece of, so she'd never forget what she was spending. The sister looked up, once, and smiled at her, sweet and vacant, and Mara lifted her hand in return and felt the scar pull tight across the missing room.

*One memory,* she reminded herself. *One. He's worth a hundred. He's worth all of them. And when the ledger opens, when the Regent's true name is read aloud, when the mountain wakes — I buy it all back, or I die trying.*

Below, the frost-flowers bloomed silver in the sister's hands.

Above, somewhere in the glittering dark, the Regent wore his borrowed centuries and did not yet know that his ledger had been read.

And in the deep cells, behind iron and silence, Wendell waited — for her, though he didn't know the shape of what was coming. Didn't know that tonight the third landing would sleep, and the cell door would stand open for one bell's length, and a woman who no longer remembered meeting him would come down the dark stairs to bring him home.

The first bell rang.

Mara picked up the stolen key, and went to get him.
