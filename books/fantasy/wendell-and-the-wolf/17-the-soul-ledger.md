## 17. The Soul Ledger

The servant's name was Pellam, and he trembled when he took the memory.

Mara had watched him do it — a small glass phial pressed to her temple, the day she and Wendell met dissolving into amber light like honey poured backward into a jar. She had felt it go. Not pain, exactly. A smoothness. A place in her where a rough stone had been, now polished to nothing.

She had thanked him afterward. That was the worst part. Her mouth had formed the words *thank you* while her mind groped for what she was thanking him for.

"You'll want to sit," Pellam said, catching her elbow as she swayed in the corridor outside the guest-wing-that-was-a-cage. His voice was low, pitched beneath the hearing of the walls. The walls here listened; she'd learned that on the first night, when she'd whispered Wendell's name into her pillow and heard it repeated back to her an hour later in the Regent's voice, sweetened and wrong. "The first one is always the worst. After that, you learn to walk around the hole."

"How many have you taken?" she asked.

"From others? Hundreds." He did not meet her eyes. "From myself—" He stopped. Something moved behind his face, the way a fish moves under ice. "I don't remember how many."

*That,* Mara thought, *is what I am becoming.* A person with holes walking around inside her, polite about them.

But she had not done it only to free Wendell. That had been the reason she gave herself, and it was true, but it was not all of it. In the moment the memory left her, something else had gone with it — fear. The day they met was gone, and with it went the girl who had been afraid of what losing him would mean. What remained was harder. What remained was arithmetic.

And Mara Halloran had spent eleven years doing arithmetic in a temple ledger-house. She knew what records could do. Records were how the temple had bound her: not chains, not magic, just ink in a column with her name at the top and a number beneath it that never shrank. Records were how the elders had sold their own congregation. Records were how the court beneath the mountain kept its winter turning — every soul entered, every memory catalogued, every debt tracked to the last farthing of feeling.

Somewhere in this glittering, listening palace there was a master ledger. And if there was a master ledger, then somewhere in its columns was the Regent himself.

---

She began that same evening, because grief makes a poor bedfellow but an excellent work ethic.

Pellam brought her supper — bread, dark greens grown under soul-light, water that tasted faintly of iron — and with it, as promised, a key. Not a real key. A rubbing. He had pressed soft wax into the lock of the servants' stair and given her the impression folded inside a napkin, along with three minutes of his attention and a warning: "The archive rotates its wardens at the change of the watch. You will have less time than you think."

"What happens if they catch me?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw that behind the hollow places he was still a man who had once chosen something. "They don't catch people," he said. "They *file* them. It's worse. Go."

The servants' stair smelled of tallow and old snow. She climbed past floors she had no names for, counting turns the way Pellam had taught her — seven up, landing, four more, the door with the hairline crack in its frame. The wax impression fit a lock she found in a supply closet, absurdly, among mops and buckets of grey meltwater. The court beneath the mountain guarded its jewels with sorcery and its brooms with nothing at all. That, too, was information.

The archive was not what she expected. She had imagined vaults, iron, cold. Instead it was warm. Warm as a living body, and lit from within — shelves upon shelves of glass, thousands upon thousands of vials, each one glowing with its own private color. Amber for memories. Blue-white for souls. Some few, in a locked case near the door, burned a red so deep it hurt to look at, and those, she understood without being told, were the ones taken unwillingly. The whole room hummed like a hive. Like a choir holding one endless note.

Mara stood in the doorway and let herself feel the size of it. Every vial a person, or a piece of one. Somewhere on these shelves was a day of her own life, filed between strangers. Somewhere was Wendell's sister, hollowed and gentle, who smiled at Mara through the bars of the guest wing and asked, kindly, whether they had ever met.

*Yes,* Mara had wanted to say. *You are why he steals. You are why he runs. You are loved so much it started a war.*

Instead she had said, "Not yet," and begun planning.

Because that was the other thing the archive was, beneath the horror. It was a *library*. And libraries had catalogs.

---

The catalog was a book. Of course it was a book. Power always wrote itself down; power could not resist its own reflection.

It sat on a lectern by the door, chained — actually chained, which would have insulted her back in the valley, where the temple chained its holy books out of piety rather than fear. This chain was out of fear. The book was enormous, bound in hide she chose not to identify, its pages ruled in columns of silver ink that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at them. Entries appeared and re-ordered themselves as new acquisitions came in. A living index. She put her hand flat on the page and felt a pulse, faint, like holding a bird.

*Index of Holdings, Court Beneath the Mountain,* read the header. *Being a True and Complete Account.*

"True and complete," Mara murmured. "No record ever is."

She worked the way she'd been trained. Don't read — *scan*. Look for structure before content. Find the organizing principle and the whole book opens like a lock. The entries were alphabetical by acquisition date, which was useless, then cross-indexed by type — souls, memories, years, debts — and it was the debt column that made her breath go shallow.

Because debts had *creditors*. And creditors had names.

She turned pages faster. Souls sold to the court, yes, thousands of them, each with a provenance line: *taken freely / taken in payment / taken as forfeit*. Memories likewise. But running down the margin of every page, in the same silver hand, was a second accounting — what the court *owed*. Winter does not come free. The Regent bought his power, vial by vial, year by year, and everything bought must be paid for, and the payment was—

Her finger stopped.

There, in the oldest section, on a page so worn the silver had gone grey, was an entry unlike the others. Not an acquisition. An *origin*. The ink was different — older, blacker, written by a hand that had pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

*Account opened by:* and then a name.

Mara read it three times. The name meant nothing and everything. It was small. It was human. It was the kind of name that belonged to a boy who had carried water in some mountain village before the winter came down, before he learned that fear could be distilled and sold, before he sold his own name first — the cheapest thing he owned — and kept selling until nothing was left of him but the office.

*Regent* was a title. Titles were armor. But the ledger did not record titles. The ledger recorded *accounts*, and accounts had originals, and the original of the Regent of the Court Beneath the Mountain was a name that could be spoken by anyone, written by anyone, *known* by anyone — and knowledge, in a world built on bought and borrowed power, was the one currency that could not be confiscated.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the lectern until they stilled.

Scribe's training, lesson one: *the person who keeps the books rules whoever signs them.* Old Mother Vess had said it as a joke, tapping Mara's nose with a quill. It was not a joke. It had never been a joke. The temple had used it to bind her; the court had used it to bind the world; and now she stood in the warm humming heart of the machine with her fingers on the one page nobody was supposed to read, and the binding ran the other way.

She needed paper. She needed to copy — not the name alone, a name could be dismissed, denied, but the entry entire, the account opening, the payments, the *structure* of the man. Proof required context. Any scribe knew that.

She tore a strip from the hem of her shift, the way she'd torn strips for notes her whole flight across the high country, and copied in the tiny compressed hand that had won her a place in the temple archive at nine years old. Name. Date. Terms. The clause that made her stomach turn even through the copying: *debtor consents to dissolution of self upon default; remainder to be held in perpetuity as surety against winter.* He had signed himself away *in installments*. He had been the court's first customer and its final collateral, and every winter since had been a payment coming due.

The hum of the vials changed pitch. Very slightly. Very softly.

Mara froze, quill lifted.

Down the long aisles of glass, something was listening. Not the walls here — the walls were far away, and Pellam's route had been chosen well. This was closer. This was the archive itself, the holdings, the thousands of stored lives reacting to something. To her. To the name lying exposed on the open page.

Or to the fact that someone was reading it.

She finished the last line with a steadiness she did not feel, folded the cloth strip into her sleeve, and closed the ledger. The hum settled. The colors in the vials dimmed back to their ordinary glow, amber and blue-white, ten thousand small lives pretending to sleep.

As she turned to go, movement caught her eye — down the aisle, in the locked case of deep-red vials, one flame had flared bright. Closest to her. As if it knew her.

She crossed to it before she could think better of it. The case was warded; the glass sang a warning note under her fingertips. But the label was legible through the red, written in the same old black hand as the origin entry:

*Holding 1. Original debtor. Retained per terms.*

One vial. The first thing the court had ever taken. The part of the Regent that had never been spent, held in perpetuity as surety — the last piece of the boy with the small human name, burning red because it had been taken *unwillingly*, all those centuries ago, from someone who had changed his mind one moment too late.

Mara stood in the warm dark with her stolen copy against her ribs and understood, with total clarity, what she was holding.

Not leverage over the Regent. Leverage over the *man* — the frightened village boy still trapped inside the title, whose power depended on an account that could be called due, whose name could be spoken aloud in the great hall and unmake him faster than any army. Wendell would shatter the vats; she had seen the fury building in him like weather. Fury was good. Fury broke things.

But paperwork *ended* things.

Footsteps, above. The change of the watch, early or late or exactly on time. Mara blew out nothing — there was no flame to blow out — and slipped back toward the mop-closet door, moving the way she'd learned to move in eleven years of debt: quietly, unremarkably, a woman carrying nothing worth noticing.

Except the hem of her shift was shorter now, and her sleeve was heavier, and somewhere behind her a single red vial pulsed in its case like a heart remembering it had once belonged to someone.

In the guest wing, Wendell's sister sat by the window of her gentle cage, watching the false snow fall upward past the glass. Mara knelt beside her, took her hands — cool as the vials, patient as the mountain — and said, "Tomorrow. Tomorrow we're going to read your brother's name to the dark, and the dark is going to answer."

The woman smiled her hollow smile. "I don't have a brother," she said kindly.

"You will," Mara said. "By spring, everyone here is going to remember exactly who they are."
