## 18. Winter's Heart

The hall of the court beneath the mountain was built to make you feel small.

Wendell had known that the moment they were marched into it — that every arch was a jaw, every pillar a tooth, every facet of the glittering ice-glass ceiling angled to catch your reflection and show it back to you diminished. The Regent sat at the far end on a throne of packed snow that never melted, and around him, suspended in glass like lanterns, hung the memories he wore: first snows, wedding mornings, mothers' voices, all of it stolen and strung up like jewelry.

Mara stood beside him with her hands bound in silk — the court's idea of courtesy — and her chin lifted in defiance. She'd been doing that since the cells. Since the sister who didn't know his name. Since the ledger.

*The ledger.* It was hidden in the lining of her coat, pressed flat against her ribs where no one thought to search a scribe for anything but ink. He could feel her awareness of it like a second heartbeat. Every time a guard's gaze drifted toward her, Wendell watched her breathing stay slow and even, watched her become the most boring person in the room, and loved her so fiercely it frightened him.

The Regent rose from his throne of unmelting snow.

"You came down my road," the Regent said. His voice was beautiful. That was the worst thing about him — everything about him was beautiful, assembled from other people's best moments. "You walked through my winter, past my wolves of salt and my doors of whispering stone, carrying my property." His eyes found the vial at Wendell's throat. His sister's soul. "And you thought you would simply take it home."

"I thought I would try," Wendell said.

The Regent smiled, and somewhere in the walls, souls that had been sold began to weep.

* * *

They had planned it in the dark of the guest-wing-that-was-a-cage, in the language of touches because the walls listened. Mara's hand against his palm: *soon*. A squeeze of three fingers: *the vats*. A single finger pressed to his lips: *I will do the rest*.

He hadn't wanted to know the rest. Knowing made it real, and real things could be taken away. But she had looked at him with those eyes that had read ten thousand ledgers and understood exactly what power lived in paper, and she'd said, "You break what's breakable. Let me handle what's written."

So now, as the Regent descended the steps of his frozen throne, Wendell let the gift rise in him for the last time.

It didn't feel like power. It never had. It felt like listening — to the grain of wood, the temper of iron, the fault line running through a pane of glass. All his life the gift had been small things: mending a wolf's broken leg, coaxing a lock, warming a cold room by asking the fire politely. The court had spent centuries telling people like him that such gifts were nothing. That was how they got people to sell them cheap.

But there were forty soul-vats in this hall. Each one held years of someone's life, pressurized, screaming softly at a pitch only the gifted could hear. And glass, however enchanted, was still glass.

"Kneel," the Regent said, "and perhaps I'll let the scribe keep enough of herself to sweep floors."

"No," Wendell said.

And he listened.

The nearest vat sang to him — its flaw, its birthmark, the place where the smith's breath had faltered. Wendell reached out with everything he had ever been too careful to use, everything he had buried in a valley that couldn't follow him, and he *asked*.

The vat shattered.

The sound was not loud. That was the terrible part. It was soft, like an egg breaking, like a held breath released after twenty years — and then the memory inside it unfurled into the hall like smoke given color. A summer orchard. A woman laughing. Someone's whole childhood, loose in the air, refusing to be owned.

The guards froze. The Regent's smile curdled.

"Stop him," the Regent said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and Wendell was already moving.

* * *

He broke them the way rain breaks a window — one after another after another, running the length of the hall with shards singing off his shoulders, guards swinging at air, the gift roaring through him now not as a whisper but as a flood. He had spent eleven years being quiet. He was done being quiet.

Each vat opened a door. Freed memories poured out and did what freed things do: they *remembered themselves*. A soldier's courage flooded back to a hollow man in the third row of prisoners. A lullaby rose from the floor and found the throat of a woman who had sold hers and forgotten she'd ever sung. The hall filled with light that wasn't the Regent's — warm, gold, human light — and everywhere it touched, the false frost retreated like a lie caught out.

"SEIZE HIM!" the Regent screamed, and now his beauty was coming apart at the seams, because beauty borrowed is beauty owed, and Wendell could see beneath it: a thin, grey, hungry shape wearing a century of stolen joy like a coat two sizes too large.

Glass rained. Wolves of salt dissolved as their binding-songs escaped the vats. And through all of it, Wendell kept one eye on Mara — bound in silk, forgotten, *exactly where they'd planned for her to be forgotten* — as she slipped sideways along the wall, small and deliberate, toward the dais where the master ledger lay open on its stand of black ice.

The Regent saw her too late.

* * *

Later — in the collapsing years that followed, in the stories told around thawing fires — people would say the mountain woke with a roar. But Wendell heard it differently. He heard it wake the way a sleeper wakes: a long breath drawn through miles of stone, a shift of weight, a groan of old joints remembering motion.

Because Mara had reached the ledger. And Mara had done what Mara did.

She read aloud.

"Your name," she called across the chaos, her voice cutting through shattering glass like a blade through silk, "is Aldous Merren. Born in the spring. A shepherd's son. You sold your winters first, then your summers, then your name itself — and the court wrote it down, because the court writes *everything* down."

"No," the Regent said.

"Aldous Merren," Mara said again, louder, and the ledger flared white in her hands. "Aldous Merren, who once — page nine hundred and forty-one — paid a debt of six memories to save his brother's farm. Who wept when the broker took his mother's face. Who swore he would never become what bought him."

The Regent staggered. Where his foot fell, the snow of his throne melted for the first time in a hundred years.

"Stop her," he whispered, and there was nothing beautiful left in his voice at all. There was only a shepherd's son, very old and very afraid, watching a scribe read him back to himself.

Guards moved toward her. Wendell put himself between.

He didn't fight them, not really. He simply refused to fall, standing over her with blood in his eyes and glass in his hair while behind him her voice went on, steady as a metronome, reading names — *his* names, the Regent's true names, each one a nail pulled from the coffin of his borrowed power. With every name, the winter weakened. Frost slid from the pillars. The ceiling of ice-glass began to crack along lines of morning light that shouldn't have existed underground but did now, because the mountain above them was remembering the sun.

"The winter ends," Mara said, closing the ledger. "Not because you're defeated. Because you were *never really there*. You've been gone so long, Aldous. Someone else has been wearing your life."

The Regent — the thin grey hungry thing inside the beautiful coat — looked at his own melting throne, and then at Wendell, and something almost like relief crossed what remained of his face before the fury took it back.

"Then I will end the winter *my way*," he snarled, "and bury every soul in this hall under a thousand feet of snow—"

The mountain chose that moment to shrug.

* * *

The whole hall lurched. Chandeliers of frozen memory swung on their chains. Somewhere far above, tons of ancient ice let go of the mountainside with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and the false winter — the Regent's winter, fed for a century on purchased souls — broke apart all at once, unable to hold its shape without its master's name holding it together.

Snowmelt ran down the stairs in silver rivers. The great doors of the hall, sealed for generations, groaned open onto air that smelled — impossibly, wonderfully — of pine and wet stone and *spring*.

Around them, the freed captives stood blinking in the ruin of the court, holding pieces of themselves that had come home. The hollowed woman with the lullaby was singing. The soldier was weeping without shame. Wendell's sister stood near the shattered vats with her hand pressed to her chest, frowning, as if trying to remember why her heart suddenly felt crowded.

And the Regent — Aldous Merren, shepherd's son — knelt in the meltwater of his own throne, small and grey and mortal, whispering a name that was only his own.

Mara came to stand beside Wendell. Her hands shook now that it was done; he took them and held them, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to plan and no words big enough yet for what they'd survived.

Then the mountain groaned again — deeper, angrier, a sound with teeth in it — and the floor beneath the shattered vats split open with a crack that ran the length of the hall.

The waking mountain, it seemed, was not finished. Something below was stirring. Something old that the court had built itself upon, something that had been promised a payment long ago and had waited a hundred frozen years to collect.

The crack widened. Cold air breathed up out of it — not the Regent's manufactured winter, but something older, patient, and vast.

"Wendell," Mara said quietly. "What did they build this place on top of?"

He looked down into the dark, and the dark looked back, and he realized with perfect clarity that the price had not yet been paid in full.

The mountain was awake. And it was waiting to be *owed*.
