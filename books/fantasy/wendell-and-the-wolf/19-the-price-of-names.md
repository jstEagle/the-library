## 19. The Price of Names

The mountain was waking, and it wanted a name.

I felt it the way you feel a held breath — in the pressure behind my eyes, in the groan of stone that had been stone for ten thousand years and was suddenly remembering it could be something else. The Regent's hall was coming apart around us in slow, glittering ruin. Chandeliers of frozen light fell and did not shatter; they simply came apart, like breath on cold glass. The soul-vats Wendell had shattered were draining into the floor, rivers of pale fire running through cracks that widened as we watched.

And beneath it all, under everything, a voice.

Not the Regent's voice. Not any voice I had ever heard. It was older than the court, older than the winter, older than the ledger I still clutched against my chest with both arms like a child holds a doll. It was the voice of the mountain itself, and it said only one thing, over and over, in a language my bones understood before my mind caught up:

*A debt remains.*

"Of course it does," I whispered. "Of course."

Everything in this world came down to debts. The temple's ledgers. Sef's bargains. The Regent's jewels of bought memory. And now this — the oldest creditor of all, stirring beneath us, demanding payment for its long sleep.

Wendell stood at the edge of the broken vats with his sister's empty vial in one hand and his other hand still bleeding from where the glass had cut him when he smashed them open. He wasn't looking at the collapsing hall. He was looking at me.

"Mara." He said my name the way he said everything — quietly, completely, as if there were no other words worth the breath. "The ledger. Read me what it says about the mountain's founding."

I fumbled the book open. My hands were shaking so badly the pages blurred. Somewhere behind us, the freed captives were streaming toward the mine-shafts, herded by wolves who seemed to know the way out better than any of us did. His sister — Lira, hollow-eyed and gentle, clutching the vial that now held her own returned soul — had been carried out by two of the grey wolves not five minutes ago. She was safe. That much was done.

But the ledger never lied, and it didn't lie now. I read aloud, my voice cracking on the old formal script:

*"In the year of the first frost, the mountain accepted a soul in payment for its waking service. One soul, freely given, to seal the sleep. Let none wake it without offering the same."*

The words sat in the air between us.

"One soul," I said. "Freely given."

"No." Wendell crossed to me in three strides and took the ledger out of my hands — gently, always gently, even now, even at the end of everything. He closed it. He set it down on a fallen slab of the Regent's throne dais, which was already crumbling into ordinary rock, its borrowed majesty gone with its borrowed power. "No, Mara. Listen to me."

"I'm listening."

"You're calculating. I can see it. You're doing that thing you do with numbers, looking for a way to make the sums come out fair." His voice was steady but his eyes weren't. "There isn't one. There is no version of this where we both walk out."

"There might be—"

"There isn't." He took my face in his hands. His palms were warm despite the cold flooding down from the breaking ceiling, warm despite everything. "I've spent eleven years paying other people's debts. My sister's. The hunt's. The Regent's, though I didn't know it. I am *done* letting the people I love be currency."

*The people I love.* He'd said it plainly, the way he'd finally learned to say it in the cave in the high passes, and even now, even here, some small traitor part of me warmed at it.

"Wendell—"

"The mountain doesn't care whose soul it gets," he said. "It cares that the debt is paid honestly. Freely given — those are the terms. And I have a soul that has been owed somewhere for eleven years." He smiled, and it was the smile I'd first seen across the temple courtyard, the quiet one, the one that made him look like a man listening to music no one else could hear. "I've been carrying it around waiting for the right buyer. Turns out it was the mountain all along."

"No." I grabbed his wrists. "No, you listen to *me*. You don't get to decide this alone. You don't get to make yourself the price while I stand here holding a book. I'm a scribe, Wendell. I know how contracts work. If the debt needs a freely given soul, then either of ours will serve, and mine is *mine*, and I choose—"

"You choose nothing," he said, "because I was faster."

He kissed me.

It was not gentle. For once in his careful, guarded, patient life, Wendell was not gentle. He kissed me like a man memorizing, like a man burning a page after reading it, and I understood — too late, far too late — that this was goodbye wearing the shape of a kiss.

When he pulled back, his hand was pressed flat against my sternum, and light was moving under his skin.

"What did you do?" My voice came out very small. "What did you just do?"

"Bought you time," he said. "A few minutes. Enough to walk you to the shaft and put you on a wolf's back. You'll hate me for it. I've made peace with that."

The light under his skin was spreading, and I could see now what it was: his soul, rising to the surface, answering the mountain's call before I could answer it myself. He had spoken the terms aloud. *Freely given.* He had made the offer first, and the mountain, that ancient patient creditor, had accepted.

"You asked me once," he said, backing away from me step by step, the light climbing his throat now, his jaw, "to forget you gently. In the cave. You said if it came to trading memories, you'd want me to remember for both of us." He was smiling again, and tears were cutting clean lines through the ash on his face. "I'm asking you the same thing. Forget me gently, Mara. Don't carry me like a debt. Debts are heavy, and you've carried enough."

"Don't." I was walking toward him and he was walking backward toward the great fissure that had opened in the hall's heart, where the drained vats had emptied into the mountain's own deep veins, and the light was pouring off him now like heat off summer stone. "Don't you dare do this and then ask me to be *gentle* about it—"

"I loved you," he said. "Since the first day you dropped your ink pot in the courtyard and swore at it in three languages. Eleven years, and I never once regretted staying quiet, because watching you was enough. But it wasn't, in the end. It turns out it was never enough, and I'm glad. I'm glad I told you. I'm glad I got to—" His voice broke, the first time I had ever heard it break. "I'm glad I got to be loved back."

The fissure opened wider. The mountain's voice rose around us, not hungry now — satisfied, almost tender, the way an old woman sounds taking in a stray. And Wendell stepped to its edge and turned, and the light of him filled the whole ruined hall, and every wolf in the shadows lifted its head and sang — a low, aching sound, the sound they'd made at the border stones, the sound of something saying farewell to its keeper.

"Lira gets her life," he said. "You get yours. Everyone walks out. That's the sum, Mara. Check my arithmetic."

"Your arithmetic is *wrong*—"

"It's the only sum there is." He opened his arms. The light took him at the edges first, his hands going bright and then translucent, and still he looked at me, only me, his grey eyes steady as they had been across every quiet morning of our small careful lives. "Forget me gently. Or don't forget me at all. Either way — live loudly. Promise me."

"I promise," I said, because I would have promised him anything, because it was the last gift I had left to give. "I promise, I promise—"

"Good girl," he said softly, the way he spoke to the wolves. "Goodbye, love."

And he stepped backward into the light, and the mountain closed around him like a hand closing around a coin, and the singing stopped.

* * *

Afterward, there was silence.

Not true silence — the mountain was settling, the deep groans fading to murmurs, to rumbles, to the ordinary creaks of stone being stone again. The false winter was dying. I could feel it going, the terrible cold that had fed the court for centuries loosening its grip, warmth seeping up through the rock like blood returning to a numb limb.

I stood where he had left me. I don't know how long. The ledger lay on the ground where he'd set it down, and I couldn't look at it, because I was a scribe, and I knew exactly what had just been written in a book no one would ever copy: *one soul, freely given, to seal the sleep.*

"Mara." A hand on my arm. Lira — carried back by the wolves, her new-old soul flickering in her face like a lamp finding its wick. Her eyes were clearer than they had been in the guest-wing cage, clearer than they had probably been in eleven years, and full of a grief she didn't yet have the memories to explain. "Mara, we have to go. The shafts are opening. The wolves say the thaw won't hold the tunnels long."

"The wolves say," I repeated dumbly.

"They've always talked," she said, as if that were a normal sentence, as if anything about this were normal. "You'll learn their accents. He did." Her face crumpled and reformed. "Come on. Come *on*. He bought us the way out. We don't waste it."

She pulled me toward the shafts, and I went, because my body obeyed her even though the rest of me stayed kneeling at the edge of that fissure forever. The wolves pressed close around us as we climbed — big grey shapes in the dark, warm as hearthstones, one of them nudging my hand with its nose until I gripped its ruff and let it take my weight.

Behind us, the last of the Regent's hall folded inward with a sound like a sigh, and above us, faint but unmistakable, came something I hadn't heard in the mountain's dark since we'd descended into it:

Birdsong.

Real birdsong, filtering down through cracks that were becoming daylight, through stone that was remembering sky.

We climbed toward it. The captives climbed with us, dozens of them, hollow-eyed men and women blinking in the growing light, souls newly returned or memories newly restored, all of us refugees of the same machine. Lira walked beside me with her hand fisted in my sleeve like a child's, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing to say that wasn't his name.

At the mouth of the final shaft, dawn was waiting — a real dawn, gold and unashamed, pouring across a hillside that was already running with meltwater. The snow that had fallen unnaturally early was retreating in visible ribbons. Somewhere below us, the mountain hummed, and if I pressed my palm to the rock — and I did, I couldn't help it — I could feel it, faint as a heartbeat under bandages:

Him. Or the shape of where he'd been. A warmth in the stone that hadn't been there before.

"He's not gone," Lira said quietly beside me. "Souls don't go. They change hands. That's the whole rotten economy of this world, and it's also — maybe — the hope in it." She squeezed my arm. "The mountain paid its debt, Mara. It woke honest. And things that wake honest sometimes give things back."

I looked at her — this stranger who was his sister, who had his same grey eyes and none of his memories — and I saw she believed it. Completely. Stubbornly. The way he had believed things.

"In the valley," I said, my voice rough as gravel, "there's a border stone that magic can't cross. Or couldn't. When we fled, it cracked under his hand." I swallowed. "If magic is coming back to the world — if the winter is truly broken —"

"Then the valley won't stay dead much longer," she finished. "And neither will anything else."

I turned my face into the dawn and let myself cry, finally, hugely, without gentleness — because he had asked for gentleness and I had never once done what he asked, and I wasn't going to start now.

Then I wiped my face, picked up the ledger from where one of the wolves had carried it faithfully in its jaws, and turned to the freed captives gathered blinking on the hillside.

"I'm a scribe," I said, and my voice held. "Every one of you has a name in this book, and names are debts, and debts can be *closed*. Follow me home. We have paperwork to do."

And as we started down through the melting snow toward the valley — toward the cracked border stones, toward the temple, toward the small careful life that would never be small or careful again — the wolf who had carried my weight through the dark fell into step beside me and would not leave.

It kept looking back at the mountain.

So did I.

Because deep in the stone, faint but unmistakable, something was humming a tune I knew — three notes, over and over, the little song he used to whistle to his wolves at dusk.

The mountain was keeping him.

And I intended to get him back.
