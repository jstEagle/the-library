## 1. The Weight of Small Charms

The rain came down on Vellamere like a confession no one wanted to hear, and Casper Hartwell stood behind his counter counting the lies he sold for a living.

Dried lavender in a silk pouch — *for restful sleep*, the little card promised. It did nothing. A knuckle of riverstone on a leather cord — *protection from ill fortune*. Also nothing. A vial of rosewater and ground glass, sold as *tears of the blessed moon*. That one was practically fraud with a bow on it. He arranged them all in the window with the practiced tenderness of a man arranging alibis.

Outside, the wardens walked their rounds in oilskin cloaks, their boots striking the cobbles in that particular cadence Casper had learned to read the way other men read scripture. Two beats, pause. Two beats, pause. The rhythm of a city holding its breath.

*They're turning onto Lantern Row,* said a voice at his ankle. *The fat one and the one who picks his teeth.*

Casper didn't look down. Looking down was how you got caught. Instead he adjusted a jar of dried nettle and murmured, barely moving his lips, "How many?"

*Four. And a dog.* The rat — she called herself Sable, though Casper suspected she'd stolen the name from a dead woman whose pantry she'd haunted — *And the dog is nervous. Dogs are always nervous around you. They can smell what you are.*

"What I am," Casper said under his breath, "is a shopkeeper."

*A shopkeeper who talks to rats.*

"A shopkeeper," he repeated, "who is very good at his job."

Sable made a sound that rats should not be able to make — something dry and skeptical, like a hinge in need of oil. She scurried up into the shadow beneath his display shelf, and Casper turned his attention to the window just as the wardens passed.

He knew them by their silhouettes. Everyone in Vellamere knew the silhouettes now. Five years ago, when he'd come stumbling over the border with nothing but a false name and a burn scar on his left palm where a warden's brand had only grazed him, the city had been in the middle of its great purging. *The Quiet Edicts*, they called them — the laws that stripped magic from the streets the way frost strips leaves from branches. Beast-speakers first. They were considered the filthiest of the gifted, the ones who consorted with vermin and carrion-birds, who might carry whispers between houses like fleas carrying plague.

So Casper had become a man who sold nothing. A harmless little shop of harmless little charms, each one certified inert by the Office of Purity, each one stamped with the grey wax seal that said: *There is nothing here. There has never been anything here. This man is nobody.*

He was very good at being nobody. He'd had five years of practice.

The bell above his door chimed once — a small, tin sound — and Casper looked up expecting a customer wanting a love charm or a cure for warts, something easy and empty he could sell with a straight face.

Instead, a stranger fell through the doorway.

* * *

He was tall, or had been, before whatever was killing him finished its work. His coat was fine — court-cut, dove-grey, embroidered at the cuffs — but soaked through and torn along one sleeve, and beneath the wet wool his side bloomed dark. Blood, diluted by rain, spread across Casper's floorboards in a thin pink tide.

"Lock it," the man rasped.

Casper's hands moved before his mind agreed to anything. The bolt slid home. Old habit. Old fear.

"I haven't any medicine worth the name—"

"No medicine." The stranger caught the edge of the counter to keep himself upright. Up close, Casper could see the damage wasn't only the wound. The man's eyes were wrong — not clouded, not blind, but *thinned*, like a page written on so many times the words had gone pale. "Listen to me. There isn't time. They'll have followed me from the palace district, and when I don't report, they'll know."

"Report to whom? Who are you?"

"My name was Aldous Vane." *Was.* The past tense sat strangely in the sentence, deliberate as a gravestone. "I was beast-speaker to the crown. Before there was nothing left of the crown to speak to."

Casper felt the floor tilt beneath him. *Beast-speaker.* The word landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well — he heard it fall and fall and never heard it land.

"You're mistaken," he said. "I sell charms. Lavender. Riverstone. Nothing here is—"

Vane laughed, and the laugh cost him; he folded forward, one hand pressed to his ruined side, and when he straightened there was blood on his teeth. "Boy. Your rat has been sitting under your shelf this whole conversation, feeding you every word the wardens said outside. You nodded twice. You thought no one saw." His pale eyes fixed on Casper's, and in them Casper saw recognition — the terrible, exhausted relief of a drowning man finding another swimmer. "I've spent thirty years listening to animals. Do you think I can't hear one of my own?"

The shop was very quiet. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere beneath the floorboards, Sable had gone utterly still.

"I'm going to open that door," Casper said quietly, "and you're going to leave, and I never—"

"They hollowed the king."

The words stopped Casper's hand halfway to the bolt.

"Not killed him. Worse. There is a thing in the palace — woven out of voices, out of *stolen selves*. It sings to him every night, and every morning he wakes with less of himself inside. It eats memory, boy. Identity. It drinks a person until all that's left is a smiling shape that signs whatever it's told to sign." Vane fumbled at his coat, fingers clumsy with cold and blood loss. "I found it. I listened too closely. And now it knows my voice, and it has sent its chorus to collect me."

"The wardens—"

"The wardens are its hands and don't know it. Most of them." He pulled something free from inside his coat — a fold of oilcloth, stained dark, heavy in a way paper shouldn't be heavy. "Take it."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's rather the point of it." Vane seized Casper's wrist and pressed the bundle into his hands, folding his fingers closed over it with a strength that surprised them both. The blood between their palms was warm. "The vaults beneath the throne room. The counter-song. My notes are all there — everything I learned listening at the walls of that place. You're the only one left who can hear it, Casper Hartwell. The only one left who can answer."

"How do you know my—"

But Vane's legs were giving way. Casper caught him, lowered him down against the counter, and the man's head lolled back against the wood. His thinned eyes found Casper's one last time, and there was something almost gentle in them.

"The crown is singing," he whispered. "God help us all — the crown is singing."

Then he was still.

* * *

Casper knelt beside the body for a long moment, rain drumming the roof, the oilcloth bundle heavy in his fist.

Burn it. That was the sane thing. Burn the map, drag the corpse out the back door into the flooded undercity where the river took what the river took, scrub the blood from the floorboards, and go on selling lavender to fools until the day he died safe and small and *nobody*.

He was reaching for the stove iron when the beasts began to scream.

Not aloud — never aloud, not in a city with ears everywhere — but in that pressure behind the eyes, that chorus of voices that lived in the walls and gutters and eaves of Vellamere and had spoken to him, secretly, shamefully, for five years. Now they spoke all at once.

*Don't.* Sable, from beneath the floorboards, her voice sharp as a snapped bone. *Don't you dare burn it.*

*We carried him,* said the pigeons roosting under the eaves, dozens of voices braided together, ragged with exhaustion. *Roof to roof, district to district, three nights. He asked for you by name. He told us what would happen if you refused.*

*The king fed us from his own hand when we were starving,* croaked an old gull who wintered in the harbor and gossiped like a washerwoman. *Before the singing started. Before he forgot our faces. You will not let the last of him be forgotten too.*

"Burn it," Casper said aloud, to the empty shop, because saying it aloud made it real. "Burn it and live. That's the whole trick of surviving this city. You burn things and you stay small and you *live*."

*You've been alive five years,* Sable said, and her voice softened into something almost cruel in its gentleness. *Tell me, shopkeeper. Have you been living?*

Casper stood in the middle of his little shop of lies, blood drying on his floorboards, a dead king's servant cooling at his feet, and outside the rain kept falling and the wardens' boots kept their two-beat rhythm on the cobbles — closer now, two beats, pause, two beats, pause, turning onto Lantern Row.

Slowly, because his hands were shaking, Casper unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside lay a map — inked on skin, he realized with a lurch, tanned and marked in a dead man's blood — showing the bones of the palace: the throne room above, and beneath it, descending in careful, annotated lines, the vaults. At the lowest level, drawn in a circle and labeled in a cramped, urgent hand, a single word:

*CHORISTER.*

And beneath the word, smaller, almost an afterthought, a line that made the hair rise on Casper's arms:

*It has already begun to sing your name.*

The knock came at his door. Three blows, unhurried, official.

"Casper Hartwell," said a voice through the wood. "Open in the name of the Office of Purity."

Under the floorboards, in the walls, in the drowned dark of the undercity below, every beast in Vellamere went silent — waiting to hear what he would do.
