## 2. What the Crows Remember

The map smelled of blood and river.

Casper had laid it out on his workbench at midnight, weighting the corners with candle stubs, and still it seemed to breathe — the ink shifting like something alive beneath the water-stains. It showed Vellamere from below: not the city of spires and sanctioned bells that the wardens patrolled, but its drowned twin, the undercity where the old canals had been bricked over and forgotten. A route wound through them in faded red, ending at a mark shaped like a small crown.

He wanted to burn it. He had wanted to burn it since the stranger died against his counter with one hand pressed flat to the wood, leaving a print Casper still hadn't scrubbed away.

*The crown is singing,* the man had whispered. *Don't let them take my voice too.*

"Bad idea," said a voice from the windowsill. "Burning it."

Bramble sat in the gap where the glass was cracked, feathers slicked black as spilled ink, his single eye catching the candlelight like a coin at the bottom of a well. He'd been coming around for three years now, ever since Casper had pulled a fishhook from his foot and been too soft-hearted to shoo him off after.

"You're a crow," Casper said. "You'd eat the map for the glue."

"I'm a crow with *standards*." Bramble hopped down onto the bench, talons clicking, and studied the red line with his good eye. "That's Aldous Wren's hand. I'd know it anywhere. He fed me cheese in the palace gardens for eleven years."

Casper went very still. "The palace."

"Court beast-speaker. Last one they had, before the Silencing Acts made our kind of conversation a hanging offense." The crow ruffled his feathers, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its dryness. "He stopped coming two years ago. I thought he'd died. Turns out he only just got around to it."

"He said the crown is singing."

"Oh, it is." Bramble turned his eye on Casper, and there was something in it that made the candle seem suddenly thin and far away. "And if you burn that map, nobody sings back."

* * *

They went down at dawn, through the grate behind the tannery that the rats had told him about years ago — *the loose one, third stone, smells of dead dog but it opens* — and into the undercity, where the water ran black and slow and the ceiling dripped in rhythms like a patient counting.

Casper carried a lantern he didn't trust and the map he couldn't destroy. Bramble flew ahead along the flooded galleries, calling directions in a voice pitched low.

"Left at the broken arch. Mind the third step; it lies."

"The step *lies*?"

"All steps lie. That one lies fatally."

The undercity was older than the Silencing Acts, older than the king's grandfather. Casper walked through halls where the plaster still bore painted birds, their wings faded to ghosts, and felt the strange ache he always felt in old places — the sense that magic hadn't been driven out of Vellamere so much as driven *underground*, into the water and the brick, waiting.

At the second marker on the map — a door carved with a wren in flight — they found Aldous Wren's cache.

It was a hollow behind the fallen door, lined with oilcloth, and it held papers. Dozens of them, written in a cramped, urgent hand. Casper knelt in the muck and read by lantern-light, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the water.

*It has no body of its own,* Wren had written. *It wears the palace like a coat. They call it the Chorister because of the sound — a harmony, always a harmony, dozens of voices braided together. But the voices are stolen. It feeds on memory. On identity. It eats a person the way moth-eats-wool, from the inside out, and what's left smiles and waves and signs whatever it's told.*

*I have watched the king forget his own daughter's face. I have watched him laugh while it happened.*

*It cannot hear beasts. That is why I am alive to write this. That is why whoever finds this must be someone who can.*

Casper's hands were shaking. "Bramble."

"I know," the crow said quietly. "I read it the first night. Why do you think I've been sitting on your windowsill for three years, boy? Waiting for you to grow a spine."

"That's cruel."

"That's *honest*. You hide in your little shop selling love charms to girls who don't need them, and every rat in Vellamere knows you could do more, and you pretend you can't hear us saying it." Bramble hopped closer, mud beading on his feathers. "Wren spent two years building an escape route for the truth. And you're the only door it fits through."

Before Casper could answer, the lantern guttered.

Not from wind. From *silence* — a sudden, total absence of the drip-drip-drip that had counted the whole way down. Even the water seemed to hold its breath.

"Wardens," Bramble breathed. "Above. Many."

* * *

They came down the maintenance shafts in grey cloaks, six of them, boots finding the stones without sound. Casper knew that silence. Every beast in Vellamere knew it. The wardens trained themselves out of noise the way other men trained into it, and the rats called them *the quiet ones* and refused to nest near their barracks.

"There," one said, and his voice was wrong — smooth and harmonized with itself, like two men speaking in perfect unison. "The speaker. Take the bird too. The Chorister likes variety."

Casper ran.

He ran through the flooded gallery with the papers clutched to his chest and Bramble screaming overhead, and the wardens came behind him at that terrible gliding pace, unhurried, certain, the way wolves walk when the herd is already tired. His boot caught a submerged chain. He went down hard, lantern spinning away, and the light swung across the walls and showed him —

Rats. Hundreds of them, lining the upper ledges of the gallery, eyes gleaming in the dark. Watching him. They had followed him down from the tannery grate; they had been following him for years, quietly, the way family follows family.

*Help me,* he thought, wildly, uselessly. *Please—*

And then he stopped thinking and *spoke*, aloud, in the open dark, for the first time since he was nine years old and his mother hid him in a root cellar while men with iron collars searched the farm:

"*Now! All of you — NOW!*"

The gallery erupted.

Rats poured off the ledges like rain falling upward, a living flood of teeth and fury, and the wardens' harmonized voices broke apart into screams. Casper scrambled up and ran, and the swarm flowed around him like a river parting for a stone, driving the grey cloaks back, tangling their legs, swarming their lanterns. One warden tore free, face exposed in the light — and Casper saw that the man was smiling. Smiling widely, serenely, even as the rats covered him, even as he reached out with both hands toward Casper with an expression of perfect, empty joy.

"We hear the song," the warden sang, in that doubled voice. "You will hear it too. Everyone hears it, in the end. It's so *beautiful*—"

Bramble dive-bombed the man's face and the smile broke apart into blood.

* * *

They didn't stop until they were three galleries deep, wedged in the hollow beneath a collapsed stairwell, Casper bleeding from his palms and shaking so hard his teeth chattered. The rats had melted back into the shadows, but he could feel them near, a warm pressure at the edge of his senses, waiting to see what he did next.

"They're already eaten," Casper whispered. "Those wardens. Whatever they were before — the Chorister took it."

"Now you understand what Wren found," Bramble said. He landed on Casper's knee, and for once there was no mockery in him at all. "It doesn't kill, boy. Killing leaves a grave, and graves make people ask questions. It *empties*. It fills the palace with smiling things that used to be people, and it's working its way outward, gallery by gallery, mind by mind. Two more years and there won't be a Vellamere left to silence."

Casper looked down at the papers in his bloody hands. At the red route winding toward the mark shaped like a crown.

"I'm a shopkeeper," he said. "I sell fake charms. I'm nobody."

"You're the last beast-speaker in Vellamere," Bramble said, "and the thing in the palace can't hear you coming." He tilted his head, that one bright eye unblinking. "Wren died getting this map out. The least you owe him is the end of the road."

Far above, faint through the stone, Casper heard it for the first time — a sound like many voices singing one note, beautiful and wrong, threading down through the bricks of the city like water through a crack.

The crown was singing.

And somewhere in the flooded dark, the rats began, softly, to sing back.
