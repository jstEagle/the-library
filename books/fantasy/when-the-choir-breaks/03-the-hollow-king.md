## 3. The Hollow King

The lamplighter's coat smelled of tallow and someone else's sweat. Casper had traded a silver hairpin and three lies for it in the weavers' quarter—a widow's dead husband's coat, she said, though her eyes said otherwise—and now it hung off his shoulders like an apology. The brass badge pinned to the breast read *Vellamere Palace, Office of Lights*, and the pin was real, which frightened him more than the forgery of everything else. Bramble rode the inside of his collar, one eye gleaming through the gap at his throat like a coal that had learned patience.

*Third lamp on the colonnade is dark,* the crow murmured. *Warden by the fourth. He picks his teeth with a bone needle. Disgusting creature.*

"Can you smell the vaults from here?" Casper whispered into his own collar, lips barely moving.

*I can smell fear, old cheese, and something sweeter underneath. Like honey left too long in the sun. That'll be your Chorister.*

Casper carried his pole of lit wicks down the eastern colonnade as evening settled over Vellamere like a lid. Above him, the palace rose in pale tiers, its windows throwing long gold blades across the courtyard. Somewhere in that gilded mass, the king of Vellamere sat upon his throne—or what remained of him. The map's final markings had led through the flooded undercity, past drowned chapels and rat-kingdoms that still owed Casper their gratitude, up through a disused cistern stair into the palace's oldest bones. The stranger's bloodstained ink had ended there, at a small drawn circle and two words in a shaking hand: *he listens.*

A warden at the colonnade's end glanced up. Casper lifted his pole with the easy boredom of a man who had done nothing else his whole life, and the warden looked away. It was the gift working without being asked—the quiet animal truth of him, the thing that made dogs trust him and wardens dismiss him. Five years he had spent burying it under counterfeit charms and careful dullness. Now it kept surfacing like a drowned man's hat.

*Left here,* Bramble breathed. *The throne room smells wrong. Like a church after a fire.*

The doors were taller than any Casper had ever seen, carved with a procession of kings whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of reverent hands. They stood ajar. Lamplight spilled through the gap in colors Casper had no name for—not gold, not amber, but something that moved, that *breathed*.

He slipped inside.

The throne room was vast and cold and empty of courtiers, and it took him a long moment to understand why: because no one came here anymore except the thing that fed. The king sat slumped upon the throne like a coat hung on a hook. His crown sat slightly askew. His eyes were open, fixed on some middle distance where nothing lived, and his mouth—his mouth was smiling.

It was the smile that undid Casper. Not madness, not pain. *Contentment.* The vacant, milk-warm smile of a man who has forgotten he ever had anything to lose.

Above the throne, where the vaulted ceiling opened into a rose window of black glass, the Chorister hung.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. A shape woven from light and voice, all ribbons and radiance, like a chandelier made of singing—dozens of voices braided into one luminous body, each thread a different timbre, a different life. As Casper watched, the threads shifted, and for half a heartbeat he heard them distinctly: a woman laughing, a child counting, an old man reciting something solemn and lost. Then they collapsed back into harmony, and the harmony bent downward toward the king like a mouth toward a cup.

The song poured into the king's open mouth. Where it touched him, his skin glowed faintly, and behind his eyes something flickered and dimmed, a candle guttering in a draft only he could feel.

"He used to hate this room."

Casper spun. A woman stood in the shadow of the pillars—tall, gray-gowned, her hair bound in a coronet gone silver before its time. Queen Isolde. Her face was famous from every coin and proclamation in the city, but the face before him was wrong: one eye clear and sharp and grieving; the other clouded, milky, and *smiling* even when the rest of her did not.

"Your Majesty," Casper began, bowing, lamplighter's pole clutched like a pilgrim's staff.

"I know what you are." She moved closer, and her good eye caught the strange light. "You came up through the cisterns. The rats have been whispering about you for days. A boy who speaks beast. Do you know how long it has been since I heard anyone speak plainly?" Her voice cracked on the last word. "Everything here sings now. Everything lies in tune."

"The king," Casper said. "Can he be saved?"

She laughed—a broken, human sound swallowed quickly. "Saved. You use the word like a coin you've counted." She gestured at the radiant thing above the throne. "It comes every night at the hour of bells. It drinks him. Memories first, then names, then the self itself. What sits on that throne is the rind of a man." Her clouded eye turned toward Casper, and he felt it see him anyway. "And when the king is finished, it will need another voice. It has been tasting the city for months. The gifted ones vanish first. Smiling. Empty. You've seen them."

He had. The cheerful beggars on the corners. The washerwoman who hummed the same three notes until her hands bled. He had thought it plague or grief.

"Why haven't you run?" he asked.

"Because I am already half its supper." She touched her clouded eye. "It took my sister's voice out of me first. Then my wedding day. I hold what's left together by hating it, and hatred is running low." Her good eye narrowed. "But you—you carry something it cannot digest easily. Something rare. The beasts obey you not because you command but because you *listen*. Do you understand the difference? Everything it has stolen, it stole from mouths that were shouting. No one ever taught it a listening voice."

Bramble stirred against Casper's throat. *Careful, lad. She's selling something.*

"There is a counter-song," the queen said. "Older than the Chorister. My husband's grandmother sealed it in the vaults beneath this room, because she feared what her descendants might become. It can unbind what the chorus has bound. But it must be sung *from inside the song*—and ordinary magic shatters against that thing like eggs against stone. I have tried. Others have tried. We are threads in its weave now." Her voice dropped. "You could enter where we broke. Your gift is a door it doesn't know it has."

Above the throne, the Chorister pulsed. One of its threads flared—a woman's voice, bright and warm, singing a lullaby in a dialect from the southern hills—and Casper's whole body went cold, because he knew that lullaby. He had heard it every night of his childhood, sung by a woman with flour on her hands and a smudge of ash on her cheek, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and rain.

"That voice," he whispered. "Where did it get that voice?"

The queen followed his gaze, and pity moved across her face like weather. "From someone it ate. Someone from the southern hills, perhaps. There are so many."

But Casper was no longer listening. His mother had vanished seven years ago, the same season the persecutions crested, the same season he fled Vellamere with nothing but his gift and his guilt. He had told himself for seven years that she was alive somewhere, safe, forgetful of him by choice. And now her lullaby hung braided into a monster above a hollow king, and the monster turned—slowly, sweetly, its thousand threads rippling—and looked at him.

It knew he could hear it. Of course it did.

*Come closer,* the song said, in a voice like his mother's warmed by honey and wrongness. *I have so many things to give back to you.*

"No," Casper said aloud, and hated how young his voice sounded.

"You'll do it," the queen said quietly. It was not a question. "Because if you don't, it will eat him, then the city, then everything that remembers how to be itself. And because you want to matter. I saw it the moment you walked in. The hungry ones always want to matter."

The Chorister descended a single ribbon of light, delicate as a spider's courtesy, and touched Casper's brow.

His mother's kitchen bloomed behind his eyes—the bread, the rain, the flour-white hands—and then, gently, lovingly, it began to *unpick*. The smell of the bread went first. Then the sound of the rain. Then her laugh, fraying thread by thread, and Casper felt himself reaching after it, grasping, screaming silently—

*That's the price,* sang the Chorister, tender as a lullaby. *Every note you take from me, you pay in memory. Shall we begin?*

In the dark of Casper's collar, Bramble pressed close and whispered, very softly, the way one speaks at a deathbed:

*Then we'd best learn the song fast, lad. Before it learns you.*
