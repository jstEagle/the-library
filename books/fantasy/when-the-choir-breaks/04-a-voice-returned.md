## 4. A Voice Returned

The vaults beneath the palace were not made of stone. Casper understood that the moment Bramble's talons tightened on his shoulder and the crow whispered, *Don't look at the walls too long, lad. They're listening back.*

They were made of memory — compressed, spun, woven into something like marble. Veins of silver light ran through them, and when Casper pressed a palm to the cold surface as they descended the spiral stair, he felt a flicker of someone else's summer: cut grass, a woman laughing, rain on a tin roof. He snatched his hand away as if burned.

*That's what it does,* Bramble said softly. For once there was no sarcasm in the crow's voice. *It doesn't kill. It keeps.*

The Chorister was waiting for them at the bottom.

It had no true shape. It was a column of light and sound, rising from a basin of black water at the vault's center, and it was beautiful in the way a frozen lake is beautiful right before it takes you under. A thousand voices moved within it — men's, women's, children's, and beneath them all, fainter, the unmistakable timbre of beasts: dogs, horses, songbirds, things that had once spoken through throats like Casper's own. They sang in perfect unison, and the song had no words, only a hunger shaped like harmony.

At its edge knelt the king.

He was smaller than his portraits. His crown sat crooked on a head bowed low, and his eyes were open but vacant, fixed on nothing, a smile playing at his mouth like a stain that would not wash out. Every night for years, Casper realized, this man had been brought here and *drunk from*, and every morning he had been carried back up to his throne wearing someone else's patience, someone else's mercy, someone else's face.

*Casper Hartwell.* The Chorister did not speak so much as resonate, each word arriving already inside him. *The beast-speaker. I have wanted your voice for so long. You are the rarest note in Vellamere.*

Casper's mouth went dry. He thought of running. He thought of the map, now ash in his pocket where he'd half-burned it weeks ago before the rats had shamed him into keeping the rest. He thought of his mother's hands kneading bread dough in their kitchen in Marrowfen, flour to her elbows, humming off-key —

*Yes,* the Chorister breathed, tasting the thought. *Start there.*

"No," Casper said aloud, and his voice cracked the silence like a dropped glass. "You start there. That's how this works, isn't it? You can't take what isn't offered. That's why you need the gifted — why you need *me*. Ordinary memories you can siphon through the wards, but a speaker's gift is a door, and doors only open from the inside."

The light pulsed. Somewhere in its depths, a child's voice sang a lullaby Casper almost recognized.

*And will you not open?*

"I'll do better." Casper stepped forward, boots splashing in the shallow water ringing the basin. Bramble dug his claws in — *Lad, don't* — but Casper lifted his arm and let the crow glide down to perch atop the king's crooked crown instead. "I'll open everything. But I choose the order of the song."

He began to sing.

It was not a grand thing, at first. It was the counter-song the dying stranger had pressed into his blood along with the map — a melody of deliberate imperfection, a tune built to snag and tear, every note placed just slightly wrong so that harmony could not close around it. The Chorister flinched. The whole vault flinched; the walls of woven memory groaned like ship's timber in a storm.

And then Casper gave it what it wanted.

He sang his mother's kitchen into the light. The smell of yeast and woodsmoke. Her voice, off-key and glorious. The way she'd said his name — not *Casper* but *Caspy*, soft, secret, a name that belonged only to her. He felt the Chorister take it, felt the memory peel away like bark from a wet branch, and the pain was not sharp but vast, an absence opening in him with room enough to get lost in.

He kept singing.

His first conversation with a beast — a one-eared cat named Sorrel who'd told him the neighbors were lying about him. The night he'd fled Marrowfen with wardens' torches behind him and pigeons shouting directions overhead. Five years of small charms sold to people who needed magic and didn't know it. Each memory he fed into the song, and each one the Chorister swallowed greedily, growing brighter, fuller, more radiant, and each one left Casper lighter and less certain of his own edges.

*Who are you?* he thought at some point, and could not find the answer. There was a shop. There was a crow. There was a melody he was holding onto with both hands because it was the last thing in him that was still *his*.

*Bramble,* said a voice in the dark of him. *Your name is Casper. Hold the line, lad. Almost there.*

Almost where? He couldn't remember. But he held the line.

Because this was the bait, and the trap was this: the Chorister could not resist a gift-speaker's memories. It drank them too fast, too deep, and in drinking them it opened itself — and threaded through every memory Casper surrendered, invisible, patient, was the counter-song's hook. The wrong notes. The snag. With every stolen treasure, the creature swallowed a little more of the melody that was quietly unmaking it from inside.

The light began to stutter.

A voice in the chorus missed its entrance. Then another. The perfect unison frayed like rope under strain, and through the fraying Casper heard something new: confusion. Fear. The beast-voices first — they remembered being hunted, being silenced, and the counter-song sounded to them like a door thrown open after years of dark.

Then the king stirred.

It was small, at first. His head lifted. His vacant eyes found Casper and narrowed, and in them Casper saw a spark catch — a fragment of himself that the Chorister had swallowed along with the bait and could not digest, a memory of coronation day, of trumpets, of a young man swearing an oath with his whole heart. Casper caught the fragment as it spilled loose from the stuttering chorus and, weeping without knowing why, sang it home.

The king stood.

"Theral," the king said — a name, his own name, tasted like a man tasting bread after famine. And then, louder, rougher, a voice rusted by years of disuse but unmistakably *royal*: "I remember my daughter's laugh. I remember my daughter's *name*. Give them back."

He joined the song.

Two voices should not have been enough. But the king's voice carried the weight of every memory the Chorister had ever taken from him, and Casper's carried the shape of the counter-song, and between them they found the chord the creature had been avoiding since the beginning of its hunger — the one note of grief it could never harmonize, because grief meant loss, and loss meant it was not infinite, and it had built its whole shining self on the lie that it was.

The chorus cracked.

Not gently. It shattered like ice on a thawing river, a thousand voices peeling apart into a thousand separate songs — and then into a thousand separate *people*, each memory tearing free and flying home through the vault, up through the palace, out into the night air of Vellamere like migrating birds. Casper saw faces in the light as it died: the smiling empties of the city, the hollow-eyed, the ones who'd forgotten their children's names, all of them suddenly clutching their heads, their chests, weeping or laughing or both.

The Chorister's last sound was not a scream. It was a single, small voice — a dog, maybe, or a fox — singing one clear note of freedom as it came apart into ordinary darkness.

Then the basin was just water, and the walls were just stone, and Casper Hartwell sat down hard in the shallows and could not remember his mother's face.

But he could remember the melody. And he could remember Bramble landing on his knee, pressing his scarred forehead to Casper's chest, saying, *There you are. There you are. I've got the rest of you, lad. Let's go home and sort out which bits are missing.*

* * *

Three weeks later, Casper unlocked his shop door at dawn and did not turn the sign to CLOSED.

The decree had come down from a restored king with ink still warm on it: the practice of gift-craft was lawful, protected, and — to the astonishment of everyone, Casper most of all — to be taught. Not hidden in cellars. Not whispered between rat-warnings and shuttered windows. Taught.

They came at first light, as the crows had promised they would. A girl with straw-colored hair who claimed the street cats told her secrets. A baker's boy who hummed to the pigeons and swore they hummed back. An old woman who had spent forty years pretending she couldn't hear her own garden growing. Seven of them, nervous on his doorstep, clutching their fear like parcels they'd forgotten how to put down.

Casper looked at them and felt the holes in himself — his mother's face, gone; his first winter in Vellamere, gone; the taste of the bread she used to make, gone. He would spend the rest of his life finding other people's memories of kindness and borrowing them, and that would have to be enough. It was, he decided, watching the girl's eyes widen as a sparrow landed on the doorframe and greeted him by name.

It was more than enough.

"Well," he said, stepping aside to let them in, while above the shopfront Bramble stretched his wings against the morning sun and, for the first time in five years, sang out loud where anyone could hear. "Come in, all of you. Lesson one: the city has been talking about you for a very long time. Today, we start learning how to answer."
