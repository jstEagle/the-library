## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The traveler came over the western pass at dusk, when the light went the color of old honey and the mountains of Hallowmere folded their shadows around the valley like a mother drawing a blanket up over a sleeping child. Quentin watched him from the crook of the alder tree where they had spent the better part of the afternoon doing nothing at all — which was, in the valley, a full and honest occupation.

He was a wool merchant by the look of him: broad-shouldered, road-worn, leading a mule whose packsaddles clinked with the soft music of trade goods. He walked the way travelers always walked once they crossed the border stones — slower, a little puzzled, like a man who had come into a room and forgotten why. The forgetting would take him gently. It always did.

Quentin slid down from the alder and fell into step behind him, quiet as falling leaves.

The valley itself made it easy. Hallowmere had no magic in it — that was its whole nature, its whole curse, depending on who you asked. Magic could not live here the way fish cannot live on stone. Spells unraveled at the border like thread pulled from a hem. Enchantments curdled into plainness. And memory — memory was the most delicate magic of all, so fine-spun that the valley drank it down like rain into thirsty earth. A traveler could walk in carrying forty years of living and walk out with perhaps thirty-five, the edges gone soft, the middle thinned to gauze. They never knew what they'd lost. That was the mercy of it. The valley kept no ledger, and neither, officially, did anyone else.

But Quentin kept one anyway.

They followed the merchant down the switchback trail, past the terraced barley fields going gold in the last light, past the shrine to some saint whose name the valley had eaten generations ago — the villagers still left candles there, out of habit, for a god they could no longer remember. The air smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and the particular green sweetness of the river shallows. Somewhere downhill, a woman was calling children home to supper, her voice bouncing off the cliffs and coming back doubled, trebled, a small choir of one.

Quentin let the merchant get as far as the waystone at the valley's throat — the flat grey marker where the road narrowed between two leaning boulders, the last place a traveler might reasonably stop before the village proper. Then they stepped out onto the path ahead of him, hands open, face arranged into the expression they thought of as *harmless*.

"Evening," Quentin said. "You'll want water before the last stretch. The spring's just there, behind the stone."

The merchant blinked at them. Dusk was settling into his eyes now; Quentin could see the forgetting working in him the way you can see wine working in a man's face. "I — yes. Thank you." He frowned faintly, patting his own temple as though something had buzzed there. "Strange. I had a thought, just now, and it's gone."

"They say the valley does that," Quentin said pleasantly. "Takes your worries with you. That's why people come."

It wasn't why people came. People came because Hallowmere sat across the only low pass through the mountains for fifty leagues, and because maps were vague about it, and because by the time a traveler understood what the valley took, they were already through it and telling themselves they hadn't lost anything important. But *it takes your worries* was kinder, and kindness cost Quentin nothing, and kindness opened people like oysters.

The merchant knelt at the spring and drank. Water sounds filled the little hollow — the trickle and chuckle of it, the mule stamping, the wind worrying the alder leaves overhead. And beneath all of it, so quiet you'd mistake it for silence if you didn't know better, Quentin listened.

Every mind made a sound, if you knew how to listen. Not sound exactly — more a warmth, a shimmer, the way a sunlit wall shimmers when you look at it too long. Memories had textures. Grief was rough wool. Fear was cold iron. Love was — love was the worst to steal and the best to hold, warm as bread from the oven, heavy as a sleeping child.

The merchant's memories shimmered above him like heat-haze, and Quentin reached into that haze the way you'd reach into a stream to cup water, gentle, unhurried, and lifted out the brightest thing floating near the surface.

A wedding.

It came away whole: a daughter in a dress the color of new buttercups, dancing with her father in a barn strung with lanterns, both of them laughing so hard the fiddler slowed down to watch. The smell of hay and spilled cider. The weight of the girl's hands in his — she'd been a baby yesterday, he'd have sworn it, and now here she was, married, glowing, pressing her forehead to his chest and saying *thank you, Da*, and him not being able to speak at all, just holding her, the lanterns blurring gold around them—

Quentin caught their breath. Even after all these years, the taking still did this to them: a moment of standing inside someone else's joy, wearing it like a coat still warm from another body's heat.

Then it was theirs, sealed in the glass of them, and the merchant straightened up, wiped his mouth, and smiled at the evening with the mild, unburdened contentment of a man who has never danced with anyone at a wedding.

"Good water," he said.

"The best in the valley," Quentin agreed, and felt the familiar sick-sweet turn in their stomach, guilt and hunger braided together so tightly they'd long since stopped trying to tell them apart.

* * *

The market in Undermere ran every seventh day in the shadow of the mill, and it was a modest thing — eggs, barley, salt fish from the traders' carts, wool, honeycomb, a tinker selling knives that would not hold an edge but would never rust. Quentin arrived with nothing visible to sell and left, as always, with bread, cheese, a twist of salt, and three copper coins that the baker's wife pressed on them out of pity disguised as payment.

"For the stories," Marta said, wrapping the loaf in cloth. She was sixty and round as her own ovens, and she believed Quentin earned their keep by trading news from the road. In a way, Quentin supposed, it was true. "What did the last one carry? News of the war?"

"There's always news of the war," Quentin said. "The king's levies are taking boys from the coast villages. There's famine east of the Serpent's Teeth. A comet was seen in the spring, red as blood, and the priests in Corvale say it means a change is coming." All true. All secondhand, gathered from travelers the way a spider gathers flies — except that Quentin's web caught things far finer than news.

Marta clicked her tongue over the comet and gave them a heel of cheese besides. Kindness again. The valley was kind to Quentin the way you're kind to a stray cat: fondly, generously, without ever quite asking what it did at night.

The truth was that everyone in Undermere knew, in the wordless way valleys know things, that Quentin Vane was useful and strange and best not examined. The elders tolerated the thefts because the thefts were invisible. Travelers passed through lighter than they came and never knew to complain. And Quentin traded carefully, always carefully — a memory of a mountain sunrise to the innkeeper, who had never seen a mountain; a memory of the sea to old Tam, who had been born in the valley and would die in it and had once asked Quentin, drunk on plum brandy, *what does water that big sound like*, and wept when Quentin gave it to him, and thanked them, and forgot by morning that he had ever been given anything, though he never forgot the feeling of having been given something. That was the trick of it. The valley ate memories, but feelings lingered like scent in a coat.

So Quentin moved through the market square in the amber afternoon, and the villagers nodded to them, and the children trailed them hopefully, because sometimes Quentin told stories so vivid you could taste the salt spray, and nobody — not one soul in Hallowmere — knew what those stories cost, or where they came from, or that the storyteller was the richest person in the valley and the poorest both.

* * *

Quentin's cottage stood at the edge of the barley fields, half-swallowed by a briar hedge they cultivated deliberately, because privacy was the first currency of their trade. Inside it smelled of lamp oil and dried lavender and, underneath, faintly, of glass.

The jars lived in the cellar.

There were forty-one of them now, arranged on shelves Quentin had built themselves from alder wood, each jar squat-bellied and stoppered with waxed cork, each one holding a stolen memory the way a jar holds fireflies: a soft glow in the dark, pulsing gently, alive. Quentin had discovered early — they couldn't remember discovering it, which was its own small irony — that a taken memory didn't have to stay in the head. Poured into glass, sealed from the valley's thirst, it kept. It glowed. And on nights when the loneliness got large enough to have a shape, Quentin could go down the ladder, take a jar from the shelf, uncork it, and pour a little of someone else's life into themselves, and be, for an hour, somebody's daughter at a wedding, somebody's son on a ship, somebody's lover under a foreign moon.

Tonight they carried the wedding down with them.

The cellar was cool and smelled of earth. Quentin lit the stub of candle on the shelf's end and looked at their hoard a while before choosing — a habit, a ritual, a miser counting coins. The jar from the left held a fisherman's memory of his first catch, his father's hand big on his shoulder. Next to it, a bride's memory of her mother braiding her hair, humming. A soldier's memory of snow, of home, of a letter read by firelight. A child's memory of a fair — sugar and drums and a painted horse going round. Forty-one small fires against the dark.

And Quentin sat on the stool among them, and uncorked the wedding, and let it rise.

Gold light. Lanterns. The fiddle. His daughter's laughing face, the buttercup dress, the cider, the hay. *Thank you, Da.* Quentin wore it like a garment, moved through it like a house they knew, and it was beautiful, and it was agony, because it was not theirs and never would be. That was the thing about stolen joy: it fit perfectly everywhere except the heart. You could stand in the middle of a stranger's happiest day and feel the walls of it, feel that you were a guest, a ghost, a thief at a feast where every plate was set for someone else.

When it faded back into the jar, Quentin sat in the dark a long time, listening to the candle spit.

Here was the arithmetic they never said aloud, even to themselves, even alone, even in the cellar where the jars glowed like patient eyes: Quentin owned four hundred lives in fragments and had none of their own. They could not remember their mother's face. They could not remember whether there had been a mother. Their earliest memory was of the valley itself — the border stones, the alder tree, age eight or nine or twelve, standing alone on the road with no name in their mouth until they found one scratched into the leather of their own belt buckle: *Q. VANE*. The valley had eaten them before they ever learned to steal, and everything since had been scavenging. Filling the hole with other people's shapes. Wearing other people's weddings because they had none of their own.

Contentment, Quentin told themselves, was a discipline. They had bread. They had a roof. They had the respect of Marta and the tolerance of the elders and the whole quiet green valley wrapped around them like a hand. Most people in the world had less.

Most people in the world had *something of their own*, whispered the undertow.

Quenthin stoppered the wedding jar, set it back in its place between the fisherman and the bride, and climbed the ladder, and did not look back at the shelf, because looking back was how the hunger got its hooks in.

* * *

Sleep came late and thin. Toward dawn, Quentin dreamed — not a memory, never a memory, dreams were the one country the valley couldn't strip and the one place Quentin's own mind still ruled — of a coronation.

This was not unusual. The dream had been coming for months now, patient as weather. A great hall of white stone. Bells so many and so vast that the air itself seemed made of ringing. A young man walking up an endless aisle toward a throne, crowned before ten thousand voices, and the crown settling onto his brow like —

Like a mouth.

In the dream, Quentin always stood somewhere in the crowd, and the crowned man always turned his head, at the very end, and looked directly at them across all those faces, and his eyes were not his own. Something behind them was looking out. Something old, and vast, and pleased.

Quentin woke before the look finished, as always, heart hammering, the cottage grey with early light, the briar hedge scratching at the window in the wind.

Just a dream. Dreams were cheap coin; everyone minted them. Quentin put water on for tea and told themselves the small familiar lie that the day would be ordinary — that there would be travelers to meet and jars to fill and bread to earn, and the valley would stay quiet, and the hunger would stay manageable, and nothing in the wide world beyond the border stones had any reason to come looking for them.

Down in the cellar, unlit and unseen, forty-one jars glowed softly in the dark.

And on the top shelf, in the newest jar — the wedding, the lanterns, the buttercup dress — the golden light pulsed once, twice, and then settled into a rhythm that was almost, if you watched it long enough, like breathing.

Almost like something waking up.
