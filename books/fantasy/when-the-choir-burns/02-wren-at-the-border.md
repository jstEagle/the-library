## 2. Wren at the Border

The border of Hallowmere was not a wall, not a river, not even a line drawn in the old maps. It was a feeling. You walked east from the mill road until the world went quiet — not the quiet of stillness but the quiet of subtraction, as if someone had reached into the air and lifted out everything that made sound worth hearing. Birds still sang there, but their songs fell flat and died a foot from their beaks. Wind moved through the pines without carrying their scent anywhere. Travelers said it was like walking into a room where a conversation had just ended.

Wren had stood that border for eleven years, and they had stopped noticing the silence the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

They noticed it tonight, though. Tonight it felt thinner than usual, stretched, as if something on the far side were pulling at it.

The moon was three days from full and hung low over the western ridge, laying a pale road across the heather. Wren stood at their post beneath the split oak — the old ward-tree, its bark scarred with oath-marks that glimmered faintly when magic came near, which was to say: never. That was the point of Hallowmere. The valley was dead to magic. Whatever craft had been worked here in the elder days had been burned out or buried out or simply starved, and what remained was a sanctuary for people who wanted nothing to do with any of it. Wren's order — the Wardens of the Quiet Edge — existed to keep it that way. Their oath-marks, inked into the skin of both forearms in spirals of silver-grey, were supposed to be inert here. Dormant. A warden's marks only woke when they crossed out of the valley, and no warden crossed if they could help it.

Wren's marks were itching again. They had been itching for two days.

That was why they were at the border past midnight instead of asleep in the warden-house with the others. Not duty. Duty was the story they told. The truth was an itch under the skin and a suspicion walking beside it, and both pointed toward the same person.

Quentin Vane.

Wren heard them before seeing them — or rather, heard nothing where there should have been something. Footsteps on dry heather should make a sound, even soft ones. Quentin's never did. They moved through the valley's quiet like a fish through water, native to it in a way that had always struck Wren as slightly wrong, slightly uncanny, though they'd never found the words to say so and wouldn't have dared.

Then the shape resolved out of the dark: slight, hooded, a satchel slung across one shoulder, moving west toward the border stone with the particular hunched urgency of someone who knows they are doing something forbidden and has decided the knowing is not going to stop them.

"Vane."

Quentin stopped. Didn't startle — that was another thing about them; Wren had never once managed to startle them — but stopped, shoulders dropping a fraction, the way a person does when they recognize a voice they've been expecting and dreading.

"Warden." Quentin turned. The hood fell back. In the moonlight their face was all sharp angles and watchful dark eyes, younger than their years and older than their face, both at once. "Out late."

"Could say the same." Wren stepped onto the path, boots crunching deliberately loud. With Quentin, you made noise on purpose, because they never did. "Where are you going?"

"Walk." Quentin shifted the satchel higher on their shoulder. The motion was casual. It was also, Wren noted, the exact motion of someone protecting something fragile.

"At midnight. Toward the border. With a bag."

"People take walks with bags."

"Not people who walk like they're being chased by their own shadow." Wren closed the distance until they stood an arm's length apart. Up close, in this light, Quentin looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleeplessness — hollowed under the eyes, the kind of tired Wren had seen in soldiers after long campaigns and in widows after the first year. "Open the satchel, Quen."

Something flickered across Quentin's face at the name — the short name, the one only Wren used, the one that had survived from years ago when neither of them had been anything but two lonely children at the edge of a lonely valley. For a moment Wren thought they would refuse. Then Quentin sighed, a small defeated sound, and loosened the strap.

Inside, nested in wool, were six jars.

Small ones, fist-sized, stoppered with wax and bound in copper wire. And inside the jars — Wren's breath caught, the way it always caught, the way it had caught the first time, four years ago, when they'd caught Quentin doing exactly this — light. Not firelight, not moonlight, nothing with a source. Each jar held a soft glow the color of a different hour: amber like late afternoon, blue like the minute before dawn, one that shimmered faintly gold-green and made Wren think, absurdly, of a kitchen and bread and someone humming.

Memories. Stolen, bottled, glowing like fireflies drowning in honey.

"You're taking them out," Wren said. Not a question. Their voice came out flatter than they intended, and they hated that, because flatness was the tone of accusation and they weren't sure yet whether they were accusing or pleading.

"I'm trading them," Quentin said. "There's a peddler comes to the pass twice a season. He pays in salt, needles, news. Real news, from outside. Do you know what we get here, Wren? We get the elders' version of the world, dusted off once a year at the harvest telling. I trade a jar and I learn whether there's war in the lowlands. Whether the roads are safe. Whether anyone's asking questions about this valley." They met Wren's eyes. "You should thank me."

"The gift is forbidden—"

"There is no gift in Hallowmere." Quentin's voice went quiet and precise, reciting the valley's oldest law back at them. "Magic cannot live here. Everyone knows. Everyone says it every day, like a prayer, like a wall." A bitter half-smile. "Everyone except me."

The words hung between them in the subtracted silence.

This was the heart of it, then. The thing they had circled for four years and never named outright. Wren had caught Quentin twice before at this border with jars like these — once in autumn rain, once in deep winter when the heather wore frost like a shroud — and both times the confrontation had slid past the truth into safer country: contraband, smuggling, rules. Never the word underneath. *Gift.* Never the admission that the valley's great sanctuary, its sacred magic-deadness, had a hole in it shaped exactly like Quentin Vane.

"Third time," Wren said.

"You're counting."

"I'm a warden. Counting is the job." Wren looked down at the jars rather than at Quentin's face. The gold-green one was closest, and looking at it made their chest ache in a way they refused to examine. "First time, I told myself you didn't know what you were carrying. Second time, I told myself you'd stop." They finally looked up. "What do I tell myself now?"

"That you tell yourself stories," Quentin said softly. "Same as everyone in this valley. Same as me, some days." They held the satchel open between them, an offering or a dare. "Go on, then. Take them. Break them. Report me to the elders. You know what happens to bearers."

Wren knew. Everyone knew, though no one in living memory had needed to know. The valley had laws left over from the burning times, and the burning times had not been gentle. A bearer found in Hallowmere would be given a choice between exile and the pyre, and the elders would call it mercy either way.

And Wren would have to stand there and watch, because Wren was a warden, and the oath-marks on their arms meant something, or were supposed to.

"They're not hurting anyone," Wren said. It came out quieter than they meant. "The memories. Whose are they?"

"Travelers'. People passing through. They don't miss them — that's the craft of it. I take the extra ones. The duplicates. The memory of a wedding where the bride cried, kept alongside the memory of the same wedding where she laughed. Nobody needs both." Quentin touched the amber jar through the wool, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "This one's a daughter's wedding. Whole thing, start to finish. The father's view. He had it twice somehow — once as it happened, once as he dreamed it afterward, better than it happened. I took the dream. He'll never know it's gone. He'll just feel a little lighter, the way you do when you put down something heavy."

"And you sell his daughter's wedding for needles."

"For bread, mostly." Quentin's voice didn't harden; that was the worst part. It stayed level, almost gentle, as if explaining arithmetic to a child. "You know what I was before the miller took me in. You know what the streets of the low towns do to children who can do what I do without knowing they're doing it. People forget things around such children. Keys, names, their own mothers' faces. They call them cursed. Sometimes they drown them." A pause. "Hallowmere took me in when nowhere else would. So I keep my craft small. Six jars a season. A wedding here, a first kiss there. I feed myself, and I feed the silence, and nobody burns."

It was a good argument. It had been a good argument the first time and the second time, and each time Wren had stood in the wet grass or the frozen mud and lost to it, and let Quentin go, and hated themselves a little for the letting.

The itch under their skin climbed another notch. Wren glanced down at their forearms. In the moonlight, faint as breath on glass, the spiral marks had begun to shimmer.

Quentin saw it too. Wren watched them see it — watched the dark eyes fix on the glowing spirals and widen, just slightly, with something that wasn't fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. Hunger, possibly. Quentin always looked at Wren's marks the way other people looked at locked doors.

"They're awake," Quentin murmured. "Your marks. Why are they awake? We're inside the border."

"I don't know." Wren flexed their hands. The shimmer brightened, dimmed, brightened again — pulsing, almost, like something breathing. Eleven years dormant and now this, on this night, at this border, standing next to this person. "They started two days ago. Itching first. Now this."

"Two days ago." Quentin's gaze sharpened. "What happened two days ago?"

"A traveler came through. Sick man, coming from the east, from the capital. Half-dead. The elders put him in the hospice at the mill." Wren frowned, assembling the connection even as they spoke it. "I sat with him a while. Wardens sit with the dying — it's part of the office. He talked. Fever-talk. About bells. About a king who couldn't remember his own coronation. About—" Wren stopped, because the next part was the part they'd dismissed as delirium, and saying it aloud in the presence of six glowing jars suddenly felt dangerous. "About a crown that eats."

The marks pulsed. Bright. Dim. Bright.

And the nearest jar — the gold-green one, the one that smelled like bread and sounded like humming — flickered in answer. Once. Twice. Like a throat clearing. Like something inside it turning over in its sleep.

Neither of them moved.

"Did you feel that?" Quentin whispered.

"Yes."

"That's not supposed to happen. Nothing answers anything in this valley. That's the whole—" Quentin crouched, brought their face close to the jar, and Wren watched their expression change as they listened to whatever the light was saying. The sharp watchfulness drained away and left something younger, more frightened. "Someone's in this one," Quentin said slowly. "Not just a memory. There's a... a piece of someone. A witness. Something in this memory is looking back at me."

"Memories don't look."

"This one does." Quentin stood abruptly, and for the first time since Wren had known them, their hands were shaking. They thrust the satchel at Wren — actually pushed it against Wren's chest, jars and all, as if the weight of it had become unbearable. "Take them. Take all of them. Report me. Burn them, break them, I don't care. Just—" They stopped. Swallowed. When they spoke again their voice was stripped down to bare wire. "Just don't make me carry that one home alone."

* * *

They ended up sitting side by side under the split oak, the satchel between them, because Wren could not in good conscience destroy the jars without examining them, and examination required light, and light required admitting they were going to be here a while, and sitting was what you did when a while became a night.

The gold-green jar sat apart from the others on the flat root. It hadn't flickered again, but Wren couldn't stop watching it. The light inside moved wrong — not swirling like fireflies but shifting like a face behind frosted glass, like a presence pacing a small room.

"Tell me about the first time," Quentin said, into the silence. "When you caught me. Why didn't you report me?"

Wren considered lying. Considered the warden's answer, the clean one: *procedure, discretion, insufficient evidence.* But the marks on their arms were glowing, and a stolen memory was pacing in a jar a foot away, and the night had already gone too strange for clean answers.

"Because you were crying," Wren said. "You tried to hide it. You stood there in the rain with your contraband and your chin up, giving me the whole speech about peddlers and news, and your eyes were streaming and you thought I couldn't tell rain from tears." They picked up a twig, snapped it, dropped the halves. "I grew up in the warden-house. Orphan ward, they called it. Raised on oaths and rules and the difference between mercy and weakness, which is supposedly that mercy has a limit. And there you were, seventeen years old, breaking the oldest law in the valley, and more afraid of being sent away than of being burned. Because being sent away meant being alone again." Wren shrugged, the gesture heavier than shrugs usually are. "I knew what alone looked like. I'd been practicing it my whole life. So I let you go, and I told myself it was discretion."

"And the second time?"

"The second time it was raining too. Did you plan that?"

Quentin smiled — small, crooked, real. "No. But maybe the valley did. Maybe it rains whenever you're about to be merciful. Keeps your face unreadable."

"My face is perfectly readable."

"To everyone else, maybe." Quentin looked at the jar, and the smile faded into something more careful. "Why do you do it? The warden thing. You could have left. Wardens are allowed to resign after ten years. You're past due."

The question landed in a place Wren kept locked. They sat with it a moment, listening to the wind fail to move the pines.

"Because the border needs guarding," Wren said finally. "And because—" They stopped. Started again. "Because when I stand here, at the edge, I'm the last thing between the valley and everything outside. War, plague, witch-hunters, kings. All of it stops at this line, and I'm the line. It matters. Being the line matters." A pause. "And because if I left, who would catch you?"

Quentin turned to look at them fully. In the moonlight their expression was unreadable for a long moment — and then readable, entirely, all at once, the way a window lights up.

"That's the most honest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late." Quentin drew their knees up, wrapped their arms around them. "Can I tell you something honest back? Since we're being reckless?"

"Apparently that's the theme of the evening."

"I don't steal memories because I need bread." Quentin's voice was very quiet. "Or not only. I steal them because when I hold one — really hold one, a whole life in a jar — I'm not alone for a little while. I get to be the father watching his daughter dance. I get to be the bride. I get a hundred lives, Wren, and none of them are mine, and all of them are better company than mine." They laughed, a thin sound with no humor in it. "The elders think the gift is a corruption. Maybe it is. But mostly it's just loneliness with a skill attached."

Wren looked at them — at the sharp face and the tired eyes and the hands that shook and steadied and shook — and felt the familiar treacherous warmth rise in their chest, the one they had spent eleven years filing under *duty.*

"Quen—"

"Don't." Quentin held up a hand, not unkindly. "Whatever you were going to say. Don't say it at a border, at midnight, next to a haunted jar. Say it someday in daylight, somewhere with bread, when the world isn't ending." A pause. "If the world isn't ending."

"It's not ending."

"No," Quentin agreed, looking at the gold-green jar, where the light had begun to pace faster, agitated, like something that could hear them talking about it. "But I think it might be starting."

Wren followed their gaze. The jar's glow had changed — brighter now, and warmer, and at its center something like a shape: a hall, vast and vaulted, ranks of figures in gold, and above them all a crown that seemed to drink the light around it. The coronation. The dying traveler's fever-talk. *A king who couldn't remember his own coronation.*

The traveler had died three nights ago. These fragments had come out of him — Quentin must have taken them at the hospice, or bought them, or however Quentin's terrible craft worked. And now the coronation was awake in a jar on the border of a valley where nothing was supposed to wake, and Wren's oath-marks were answering it like a bell answered by a bell.

"Give me the jar," Wren said.

"No."

"Quen—"

"If you take it to the elders, they'll break it, and whatever's inside gets loose, or dies, or worse." Quentin was already repacking the satchel, quick and certain, the merchant reasserting itself over the frightened child. "It stays with me. I'm the only one here who knows how to hold a memory without spilling it. That's not bravery, it's logistics." They stood, slung the satchel, and looked down at Wren with an expression that was half apology, half defiance, wholly familiar. "So. Third time. What's it going to be, Warden?"

Wren looked up at them. At the border behind them, pale road and silent pines and the whole sleeping, forgetting valley. At the marks on their own arms, still faintly glowing, still itching, still pointing — they realized with a cold settling certainty — not at the jar at all, but at Quentin. At the person. As if the magic in Wren's blood had finally learned what it had been straining toward all these years, and it wasn't the contraband. It was the thief.

"Go home, Quen," Wren said. "Keep the jar. Keep it far from me, far from the hospice, far from anywhere the elders gather. And Quen — if that thing in the jar looks at you again..." They rose, brushing off their knees, and made their voice into the flat iron of office, because the alternative was a voice that shook. "You come find me. Not the elders. Me."

Quentin studied them a moment longer. Then they nodded, once, and turned, and walked back into the valley without a sound, the way they did everything, leaving Wren alone under the split oak with six fewer jars and one more secret.

The marks on their arms faded slowly, reluctantly, like a lamp being carried away down a corridor.

Wren stayed at their post until the sky went grey, telling themselves the ache under their ribs was only the cold.

Three miles east, in a locked room above the mill, Quentin Vane set the gold-green jar on a shelf between its sleeping siblings — and the light inside it flared, brief and deliberate, like an eye opening.

And looked at them.
