## 3. What the Traveler Carried

The traveler came into Hallowmere at dusk, when the light went the color of old honey and the border stones drank the last of it. Quentin saw him from the ridge above the mill road, a dark shape leaning on a staff, moving with the careful, deliberate gait of someone who had been walking for a very long time and had stopped expecting to arrive.

They knew that walk. They had learned to read travelers the way other people read weather — by the set of the shoulders, the drag of the feet, the way a head turned toward the valley's silence as if listening for something it had swallowed. A man walking like that was either running from something or carrying something, and in Quentin's experience the two were rarely different.

He would die soon. Quentin could see it in him the way you can see rain coming in the grain of wood. Not tonight, perhaps, but soon — and a dying man's memories came loose at the edges, easier to lift, sweeter to hold. Like fruit past its first frost.

Quentin did not think of themselves as a vulture. They had made peace with what they were years ago, in the small dishonest way people make peace with anything: by not naming it. They took. The travelers mostly never knew. And the valley ate what Quentin brought it, because the valley forgot everything and needed feeding like any hungry mouth.

They followed him down through the birches, quiet as falling leaves.

* * *

The inn at the crossroads was called the Drowned Bell, though there was no bell and nothing about it suggested drowning except the way sound died inside it. The walls were thick with river clay, and conversations conducted there felt like confessions — muffled, half-heard, already fading. It was the best place in the valley to do what Quentin did.

The traveler sat alone near the hearth, his staff propped against the bench, his boots steaming gently. He was older than he'd seemed at distance — sixty, perhaps more, with a face like a map of somewhere mountainous. He ordered soup and bread and ate slowly, and between spoonfuls he looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger he was politely tolerating.

Quentin took the table beside him. Close enough to touch, far enough to be nobody.

"Bad roads this time of year," they said, which was the traditional opening, the valley's version of *good evening*.

"Worse than bad." His voice was gravel over something deeper. "Roads that don't remember where they're going." He smiled faintly at his own joke, then stopped smiling, as if the effort of it had cost him something. "You live here?"

"All my life."

"Gods." He said it without mockery, with something closer to pity. "I couldn't. A place with no yesterday. I'd feel like I was falling all the time."

"And you don't?" Quentin asked, because it was their trade to ask such questions, gently, the way you'd turn a key.

The traveler considered. He turned his cup in his hands — tin, dented, older than he was probably. "I've carried things so long I've forgotten they're heavy," he said. "That's not the same as not feeling them." He looked up, and his eyes were pale gray, the color of the sky before snow. "What's your name, child?"

"Quentin."

"Quentin." He tried it out, nodded. "I'm Aldous. Or I was. Names wear thin if you travel long enough." He laughed, a dry rustle. "I've crossed your border three times now. Do you know what happens to me each time? I lose a day. Just one day, gone, like a coin dropped down a well. I've started writing things down." He patted a leather satchel at his hip. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case I stop being able to tell which days are real." He said it lightly, but his hand stayed on the satchel a moment too long.

Quentin felt the familiar pull begin — low in the chest, behind the sternum, where the gift lived. It was always like this: first the noticing, then the wanting, then the terrible patience. Somewhere inside Aldous there were decades of days, whole seasons of living, and most of them would rot in the ground with him unclaimed. It seemed almost a kindness, what Quentin did. A harvest rather than a theft. That was the story they told themselves, and like most stories told often enough, it had worn smooth spots where the truth used to show through.

Aldous began to cough.

It started small — a tickle, an apology, a wave of his hand — and then it deepened into something wet and structural, the kind of cough that comes from the bottom of a man. He bent double over the table. The innkeeper glanced over and away; in Hallowmere, people were practiced at looking away from dying, since grief didn't keep here any better than anything else.

"I'm sorry," Aldous gasped, waving again. "It's — the roads. The cold—"

"Let me help you upstairs," Quentin said. "There are rooms. You shouldn't die in a soup bowl."

He laughed, wheezing, and let them take his arm.

* * *

The room was small and smelled of tallow and lavender gone to dust. Quentin got him onto the bed, propped him against pillows, fetched water he barely touched. The coughing came in waves now, longer each time, and between waves Aldous lay still with his eyes open, watching the ceiling beam as if reading something written there.

"You know what I am," he said suddenly. Not accusing. Curious.

"A traveler."

"Mm." His gray eyes found Quentin's and held them, and Quentin felt, absurdly, caught — though there was nothing yet to be caught at. "My grandmother was from a country that doesn't exist anymore. She used to say there were people who could lift a memory out of you like a splinter. She called them *takers*. Said they weren't evil. Said memory wants to move, like water, like birds. Said a taker was just a door that opened both ways." He coughed, softer this time. "She said you could always tell them by how carefully they listen."

Quentin said nothing. Their heart was doing something complicated.

"I'm dying," Aldous continued, conversationally, as if discussing the weather. "I've known for a month. There's a thing growing in my chest that has opinions about my future. So." He gestured vaguely at himself, at the room, at the whole forgetting valley. "Here's what I'll offer you, door-that-opens-both-ways. Take what you want. Take it all. Better it live in someone than nowhere." He closed his eyes. "Consider it a will. Consider it a tip. Consider it whatever lets you sleep."

And then, quietly, he added: "But leave the jarred ones till last. They're fragile."

Quentin went cold. "Jarred ones."

"People like me," Aldous murmured, drifting, "we collect too. Different craft, same hunger." His breathing was slowing, going shallow and even, the rhythm of a man sliding toward sleep or toward the other thing. "Satchel. Left side. Don't shake them."

Then he slept — or something like it — and Quentin sat in the tallow-smelling dark with their hands trembling slightly, which was new, which they decided not to examine.

* * *

The satchel held eleven jars.

Not Quentin's rough market jars, corked and waxed and labeled in charcoal. These were glass, thin as eggshell, blown by someone who understood that memory is heavier than it looks. Each one held a soft glow, the color varying — amber, green-gray, one that was nearly violet — and when Quentin lifted the satchel the jars shifted against each other with a sound like distant bells heard underwater.

They set them on the floor in a row and knelt before them, and the hunger rose in them so strongly it was almost pain. Eleven lives. Eleven strangers' worth of days, preserved by some other bearer's craft, carried across borders that should have dissolved them. Magic-dead Hallowmere, where no enchantment could follow — and yet these had crossed. Which meant they weren't quite magic, or weren't only magic, or were the kind of thing that walked through wards the way a rumor walks through a locked house.

Quentin reached for the nearest jar — amber, warm, humming faintly against their palm — and uncorked it.

The memory poured into them like light into water.

*A wedding. No — a coronation.*

The shift happened mid-taste, the way dreams change location without warning. One moment Quentin stood in a sunlit courtyard watching a bride — no, not a bride, a queen, young and laughing, her crown not yet on her head but waiting on a velvet cushion — and the crowd was singing, and the bells were ringing, and the smell was incense and cut grass and candlewax, thousands of candles, a cathedral full of them, and the choir was singing a note so low Quentin felt it in their teeth—

—and the crown was lowered onto the king's head—

—and the memory *stopped*.

Not ended. Stopped. The way a sentence stops when the speaker realizes someone else is in the room.

Quentin hung in the frozen moment — the crowd mid-song, the candles mid-flicker, the young king's face tilted up beneath the descending crown — and felt, for the first time in their life, the sensation of being *watched from inside a memory*.

It was not the king who watched. The king's face was already beginning to smooth, the way wax smooths, the features softening toward blankness even in this single held instant. It was the crown. The crown, caught in the act of touching his head, and around it — Quentin could not say how they perceived this, only that they did, the way you perceive a shape in smoke — something had turned its attention outward along the memory's thread, back through the jar, back through the uncorking, back through Quentin's own grasping mind, and it was *looking at them*.

It had no eyes. That was the worst of it. Looking implies eyes, implies a face, implies distance between seer and seen. This was none of those things. This was attention itself, vast and patient and terribly interested, folding around Quentin's awareness the way a hand folds around a moth — not crushing, not yet. Measuring.

*Oh,* thought Quentin, in a voice that felt very small and very far away. *Oh, it knows I'm here.*

The frozen coronation trembled. In the cathedral's depths, the choir's low note — that impossible sustained tone — began to bend, to sharpen, to resolve into something that was not music. It sounded almost like a word. Almost like a name. Almost like —

Quentin wrenched themselves free.

They came back to their body gasping, on their knees on the inn room's floorboards, the amber jar still open in their hands, its glow guttering like a candle in wind. Their heart was slamming. Sweat ran cold down their spine. Outside, the valley lay silent as it always did, indifferent, forgetting, and Quentin had never been so grateful for its emptiness or so afraid of it.

They corked the jar with shaking fingers. Corked it, and then — because corks suddenly seemed insufficient, because seals seemed insufficient, because nothing seemed sufficient — wrapped it in their scarf and set it apart from the others, at the far end of the row, the way you'd isolate a sick animal.

The remaining ten jars glowed softly, innocently. But now Quentin looked at them differently. Now they saw that one of them — the violet one, third from the left — was glowing wrong. Its light pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

"No," Quentin said aloud, to the room, to themselves. "No, that's just the light. That's just—" But their voice died, because the pulsing was quickening, matching something, and it took Quentin far too long to realize what it matched.

Their own heartbeat.

They scrambled backward. The violet jar shivered on the floorboards. The glow inside it swirled, gathered, pressed against the glass from within — and Quentin saw, or dreamed they saw, or would spend the rest of their life unable to decide whether they saw, a darkness inside the light. A shape. Not a face. Never a face. But a *regard*, folded tight in that small prison of glass, and it was looking at them the way the first one had, and it was *patient*, and it had been patient for a very long time.

The jar shattered.

Not thrown, not dropped. It simply burst — a soft, wet-sounding explosion of glass and light, shards skittering across the boards, and the memory inside it did not spill out the way spilled memories should, dissipating like breath on glass. Instead it *rose*, a column of violet gloom, hanging in the air of the little room like a held note, and Quentin felt every hair on their body stand upright as the regard unfolded fully, finally, and looked upon them with something that was not malice, exactly.

It was worse than malice. It was *recognition*.

As if it had been looking for them. As if it had crossed kingdoms and borders and the inside of a dying man's chest for this, for the finding, for this exact kneeling figure in a tallow-lit room in a valley that forgets.

The column of light bent toward Quentin.

Behind them, on the bed, Aldous stirred and moaned — a small, lost sound, the sound of a man whose dreams have been entered without permission. The violet light flickered toward him, then back to Quentin, weighing, choosing.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the floorboards, from the walls, from inside Quentin's own skull — came the choir's low note again. The one from the coronation. Sustained, bending, resolving.

Into a word.

Into a name.

"*Quentin.*"

Their own name, spoken in a chorus of stolen voices, in the voices of everyone whose memories they had ever taken, layered and harmonized and utterly wrong, and Quentin Vane, who had never been afraid of anything in the forgetting valley because there was nothing there to fear, discovered what fear was.

They threw themselves sideways. The violet light struck the floor where they'd knelt and spread there, pooling, searching — and in the moment before Quentin's shoulder hit the boards, before the world went white at the edges, they saw the light begin to dim, to gutter, to starve.

Because the valley was eating it. The memoryless valley, the magic-dead valley, the great blind mouth of Hallowmere, was drinking the intruder down, sip by sip, the way it drank everything.

The light fought. Dimmed. Fought. And as it thinned to a last violet thread, Quentin heard the chorus speak once more — quieter now, strained, but unmistakable, a promise or a summons:

"*We see you, taker. We will find you where the forgetting ends.*"

Then it was gone. The room was just a room. Shards of violet glass on the floor. An old man breathing raggedly on the bed. The ordinary, merciful silence of Hallowmere settling back over everything like snow.

Quentin lay on the floorboards and shook, and after a while they crawled to the row of jars and counted them twice, and wrapped every one in wool, and did not sleep.

Near dawn, Aldous woke briefly, lucid, his gray eyes clear. He looked at Quentin's face and read it the way he'd read roads.

"It looked at you," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Then run, door-that-opens-both-ways." He closed his eyes. "Or don't. Some doors are built to be found."

By morning he was dead, peaceful, his heavy cargo finally set down. And Quentin sat by his body as the valley's silence crept in to drink even the fact of him away, holding eleven jars — ten now, ten survivors — and understanding, for the first time in their careful, hidden life, that they had not been the hunter.

They had been the lure.
