## 4. Proclamation at the Mill

The bells came before the riders.

That was the first wrongness. Hallowmere had no bells — the valley swallowed sound the way it swallowed memory, and anything rung or sung within its borders arrived at the ear already softened, already half-forgotten. But that morning Wren heard them from the warden's post at the northern ridge: a peal rolling down out of the pass like a stone dropped into still water, each strike arriving a half-beat late and slightly wrong, as though the valley itself were flinching.

Wren stood up from the cold hearth where they'd been mending their cloak and went to the window, and there they were — six riders in crown livery, black and gold, coming down the old drover's road at a walk. Not a charge. A procession. That was worse. Men who ride hard want something specific; men who ride slowly want everything.

They wore the sun-and-sword of the Inquisition on their breasts, and behind the last of them dragged a cart, and on the cart stood a thing Wren had never seen inside the valley: a wooden frame, tall as two men, with iron rings set along its crossbeam. It took Wren a long moment to understand what they were looking at, because the shape was familiar from border-stations and garrison yards — a posting frame for proclamations — but scaled wrong, built too large, too strong, the rings too many.

It was not built for paper.

* * *

By the time the riders reached the mill at the valley's heart, half of Hallowmere had gathered, drawn by the same instinct that draws people to fires. The mill was the one place in the valley where everyone met without meaning to — grain had to be ground regardless of what anyone remembered or didn't, and so the millstone turned through droughts and funerals and the slow erosion of every grudge. Its yard was packed earth worn smooth by generations of waiting feet.

Wren arrived in uniform, because that was the point of them. The wardens of Hallowmere were few — four, when all could be found — and their duty was written plainly enough in the oath-marks that curled along their forearms like frost ferns: *keep the edge, keep the peace, keep the strangers from harm until they pass on.* Wren had taken those marks at seventeen, inked with a needle dipped in something the elders would not name, and in nine years they had never once been asked to enforce anything harder than a curfew.

The riders dismounted in the mill yard with the unhurried precision of men who had done this in a hundred towns. Their leader was not the Inquisitor — Wren would learn that name later, Hale, and learn it the way you learn the name of a storm after it has passed over you. This was a herald, a narrow man with a voice trained to carry across courtyards, and he climbed onto the mill's loading step and unrolled a scroll weighted with lead seals.

"By decree of the Crown," he read, "in the matter of the gift of unmaking—"

He got no further, because the valley did what the valley does. Halfway down the first sentence, the words began to slip. Wren watched it happen across the faces of the crowd — the way a listener's brow furrows, the way a neighbor leans in to whisper *what did he say?*, the way the herald himself faltered, blinked, looked down at his own scroll as if the ink had rearranged itself. Memory could not survive long here, but neither, it seemed, could a proclamation built entirely upon remembering what was forbidden.

The herald was prepared for this. Wren had to give him that. He produced a second scroll, and then a third, reading the decree again and again, and each time he reached the operative clause faster, as though he were carving a channel through resistance by repetition:

"—all bearers of the gift are to be surrendered to the Crown's officers, or burned where they stand."

*Surrendered or burned.* On the third reading, the words finally caught, the way a match catches on the third strike, and Wren felt the crowd change around them. It was not a sound. It was a subtraction — the small shuffling noises of a village crowd ceasing, the coughing stopping, the babies even going quiet, as if the whole gathering had been holding one communal breath.

Someone asked the question. Wren never learned who; the voice came from the middle of the pack, deliberately anonymous. "What gift?"

"The gift of unmaking," the herald said. "Memory-craft. Theft of the mind. Those who take what others remember." He let his gaze travel the crowd, and it was a practiced gaze, gentle almost, the gaze of a physician delivering a diagnosis. "You will know them, perhaps, by small signs. By luck that is too good. By griefs that heal too fast. By strangers who stay."

*Strangers who stay.* Wren felt the words land in the yard like a dropped anvil, felt the ripple move outward from the mill steps — not toward the riders, but sideways, neighbor to neighbor, a current of glances passing hand to hand. And Wren knew exactly where every one of those glances would eventually come to rest, because there was only one stranger in Hallowmere who stayed.

There had always been only one.

* * *

The elders convened within the hour, which told Wren everything about how the wind had turned. In nine years of wardenship, Wren had seen the elders convene twice: once for the flood year, once for the fever. Both times it had taken days of argument to gather them. Today it had taken less than an hour, and they met in the mill's upper room with the shutters closed, and Wren was summoned — *summoned*, a word never before used toward a warden — to stand in the corner and answer questions.

Old Maren spoke first. She was the eldest, a woman like a fence post weathered gray, who had taught Wren to read the border-stones. Her hands were folded on the table and very still.

"Warden. How many travelers crossed north this season?"

"Eleven that I counted. Twelve, if you count the tinker who came twice."

"And how many remain?"

"One." She said it flatly, without looking at anyone, which was its own kind of cruelty. Everyone knew who she meant. Everyone had known since the herald climbed the steps. "One remains, and has remained longer than any traveler I can recall, and I can recall—" she paused, and her brow creased, and Wren watched her lose the thread of her own sentence and find it again, "—longer than is customary."

"They trade at market," said Tam Broadhurst, whose family milled the grain. "Bread for — for oddments. For stories." He frowned, hunting the thought. "I've sold to them myself. They're quiet. Harmless."

"Then why," Maren said, still not raising her voice, "did the crown send six armed men to a valley of two hundred souls?"

No one answered, because the answer was obvious and no one wanted to hold it in their hands. Six armed men do not ride three days through mountain passes for a harmless stranger. Either the crown knew something about Quentin Vane that Hallowmere did not, or the crown knew nothing at all and burned indiscriminately, and of those two possibilities, only one offered the elders any action to take.

Wren spoke from the corner. "The oath says keep the strangers from harm until they pass on. It doesn't say surrender them."

Maren turned and looked at Wren then, fully, and her eyes were terrible in their mildness. "The oath was written before there was a crown decree, child. Oaths bend. Valleys don't." She gestured at the walls around them, at the whole invisible basin of Hallowmere. "Two hundred people live inside this forgetting because it keeps us safe. Nothing crosses our border and stays true — not armies, not plagues, not kings. That is the covenant. We are forgotten, and so we are free." Her hands unfolded at last, and lay open on the table like something surrendered. "But a bearer of the gift is not forgotten, is she? A bearer *carries*. Whatever she takes, she keeps. She walks out of this valley with more than she walked in with, every time, and we have let her, because she was useful, because she traded fair, because—" and here Maren's voice did something Wren had never heard it do, which was falter, "—because she was kind to us, and kindness is a debt, and debts are heavy."

Tam shifted. "We're not voting to burn anyone."

"No," Maren agreed. "We're voting to give her up. There's a difference. The difference is that we sleep afterward."

The vote was not called a vote. It was called *a weighing*, because the elders were careful with language the way surgeons are careful with knives. Each elder laid a stone on the table — white for surrender, black for shelter — and Wren stood in the corner and watched the stones come down one by one, and tried to memorize each face as it chose, because someone in that room ought to remember, and Wren understood with a sick, rising clarity that it was going to have to be them.

White. White. White. Tam Broadhurst hesitated longest, his stone hovering, and Wren wanted to shout at him, *you sold her bread, you laughed at her jokes about your mother-in-law, you—* but Tam set the white stone down gently, apologetically, the way a man puts down a weight he has carried too far.

Black: one. Old Sef, who kept the shrine at the southern ford, a man so old that the valley's forgetting had eaten most of his own history and left him serene as deep water. He set his black stone down and said only, "She fixed my roof. I don't remember when. But my roof doesn't leak, and that's a fact the forgetting can't touch."

White. White. White. White.

Nine to one.

Maren rose. "Warden. You will bring the stranger to the mill at dawn tomorrow. The crown officers will collect her here, in the open, peacefully. No one burns. No one bleeds. Hallowmere goes on being forgotten, and therefore goes on." She gathered the stones into a pouch, and the soft clatter of them sounded obscenely like coins. "If you cannot do this, warden, say so now, and we will find someone who can."

Every face turned to Wren. Nine elders, and the miller, and the dust turning in the light through the shutter-cracks, and somewhere below in the yard the crown riders' horses stamping, patient as tide.

"I'll bring her," Wren said.

And hated the relief that moved through the room like warmth through a house, and hated more that some sealed chamber in their own chest had felt it too — not relief at doing right, but relief at having chosen, at last, between the oath and the person, and finding that the choice could be survived.

* * *

Wren did not go home. Warden's discipline said report, rest, prepare. Instead Wren walked the long way, out past the tannery and the bee-fences, up the sheep path toward the crooked cottage at the valley's edge where the stranger lived among shelves of jars.

The light was failing. Down in the valley, hearth-smoke stood straight up in the windless dusk, one column per household, and Wren counted them out of habit — the counting was part of the oath, *know your flock* — and thought: *two hundred columns of smoke, and every household under them willing to hand one person to the fire so the smoke may continue to rise.*

At the cottage, lamplight showed through the window, and a silhouette moved against it — slight, hunched over a table, doing whatever it was Quentin Vane did in the evenings with those jars. Wren stood in the lane for a long time, hand raised to knock, and did not knock.

Because here was the truth Wren could not put down: the elders were wrong about the danger, or right about it, and either way Wren had known for years. Had known since the third mercy at the border, when they'd waved Quentin through with a satchel full of other people's weddings and funerals glowing faintly through the glass. Had known and said nothing, because the knowing was entangled with something warmer and less defensible, and now the crown had arrived to price that silence, and the price was a pyre.

Tomorrow at dawn, Wren was supposed to bring Quentin Vane to the mill yard in chains of law if not of iron.

Wren turned away from the door. There was another road from the cottage — the drover's track, north, toward the pass, toward the border-stones Wren had sworn to keep. It was a bad road at night and a worse idea at any hour, and it led out of the only home Wren had ever known, and it was the only direction in which the oath-marks on Wren's arms did not itch and burn.

Wren knocked after all. Three times, soft.

The silhouette at the window went still.

And from inside, in a voice Wren had heard a hundred times across a border-table and had somehow never once heard afraid: "Who's there?"
