## 5. The Gift They Never Named

The door opened on a chain's width of dark, and then the chain slid free, and then it was just Wren standing in the doorway with the night behind them like a second cloak.

"You knocked," Quentin said. It was not what they had meant to say. What they had meant to say was *go away*, or *come in*, and the words had fought each other in their throat until this small stupid thing had won.

"Wardens knock," Wren said. "It's in the oath somewhere. Between *I will keep the border* and *I will not lie to my elders*, there's a line about knocking."

"There is no such line."

"No," Wren agreed, and stepped inside, and closed the door behind them as though the night were a thing that could follow a person in. Maybe it could. Quentin had never tested it. The valley kept its borders so well that nothing from outside ever got past the stones — no wind with another country's smell on it, no birds off-course, no dreams. Nothing came in. Except travelers, who always left something behind whether they meant to or not, and except Wren, who had been coming through Quentin's door for eleven years now and had never once explained why.

The cottage was one room and a loft, and it smelled of tallow and dried yarrow and, faintly, of the jars — that green-glass coolness, that scent like rain held too long. Quentin moved to put themselves between Wren and the shelf where the jars sat in their rows, glowing faintly, and hated themselves for the motion even as they made it.

Wren saw it anyway. Wren always saw.

"Sit down," Quentin said, to cover it. "You look like you've walked here from the border."

"I did walk here from the border."

"In the dark. On the drover's track." Quentin fed the stove a knot of pine and watched the light climb Wren's face — the sharp jaw, the mouth that did most of its work by refusing to move, the oath-marks climbing both forearms where the sleeves were pushed up, as they always were, because Wren claimed the marks itched under wool. The marks looked tonight as they always looked: fine lines of faded silver, like veins in a leaf pressed flat between the pages of an old book. Like writing. Quentin had thought so for years and had never said so, because some observations are doors you cannot close again once they're open.

"The elders voted today," Wren said.

"I know."

"They voted to give up any stranger among them. Any traveler who overstays. Anyone the hunt might name." Wren sat down slowly on the stool by the stove, the way people sit when their legs have carried them farther than their courage wanted to go. "They drew up a list, Quentin. Three names. A tinker who's been in the valley two seasons. A widow from over the pass. And—" Wren stopped.

"And me," Quentin said. "I'm not a stranger, but I'm not from here either. I arrived. That's enough. In Hallowmere, arriving is a crime you never finish serving."

Wren said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

Quentin turned back to the stove. The pine spat and settled. Outside, the valley did what the valley always did at night: nothing. No owl called, because owls forget how to call here within a generation of arriving; no dog barked, because dogs in Hallowmere forgot their own names and answered to whatever you gave them last. Even the river ran quiet, as if it had forgotten, somewhere upstream, that it was supposed to make noise. The silence of the valley was not emptiness. It was fullness — a room so packed with quiet that every sound had been pressed out of it, the way a flower is pressed flat and keeps its shape but loses its scent.

That was the valley's magic-dead heart, everyone said. Magic couldn't live here. That was why the crown had never troubled them. That was why the hunted came here to hide, if they were clever, and why hiding worked, mostly, for a while.

Quentin had spent twenty years believing it, the way you believe the floor beneath your feet.

"Wren," they said. "Why did you really come?"

* * *

There was a version of this conversation Quentin had rehearsed for years, usually at night, usually lying awake in the loft listening to the jars hum their almost-inaudible hum. In the rehearsed version, Quentin was brave about it. In the rehearsed version, they said the true thing plainly and Wren nodded and the world absorbed the truth without breaking.

In the cottage, with the stove ticking and Wren watching them with those gray eyes that missed nothing, Quentin found that bravery was a currency they'd never learned to counterfeit.

So instead they went to the shelf.

They took down the jar they always took down when the loneliness got loud: the wedding. A merchant's daughter, married under a sky the color of unripe plums, her father weeping into his beard and pretending it was the wind. Quentin had lifted it from him at the border-table three summers ago, trading him a memory of his own choosing in return — he'd asked to forget the debt-collectors, and Quentin had obliged, and the man had gone east lighter by half and lighter still by the daughter's face, which he would never miss because missing requires remembering.

The jar glowed warm gold, the way wedding-memories always did. Inside it, if you knew how to look, the scene turned slow and endless: the plum-colored sky, the fiddle music, the father's tears. Not an image. A living piece of an afternoon, folded small enough to fit in glass.

"This," Quentin said. "Do you know what this is? Truly?"

"A stolen memory." Wren's voice was careful. "Everyone at the market knows you trade in them. Half of them have sold you something they didn't know they had."

"Everyone at the market thinks I trade in *trinkets*. Little charms. Pretty things I find on travelers." Quentin set the jar on the table between them, and the gold light moved across Wren's face and made the oath-marks shine. "Nobody asks how I take them. Nobody asks how I hold them. Because asking would mean noticing, and noticing, in this valley, is dangerous."

"Noticing what?"

"That the valley is wrong." Quentin sat down across from Wren, knees nearly touching in the small space. "You've felt it. Don't tell me you haven't. You've stood at the border-stones your whole adult life, and you've felt the moment a traveler crosses in — felt something go out of them. Everyone says it's the valley drinking their magic. That's the story. Magic can't survive here, so it drains away, harmless, like heat into stone."

"It's not the story?"

"It's a lie we tell ourselves so we can sleep." Quentin reached out and laid two fingers against the glass of the jar. The memory inside stirred toward their touch like a moth toward lamplight. "Magic doesn't drain away at the border, Wren. It gets *taken*. By someone standing at a table with a ledger and a friendly smile. And it doesn't dissipate. It goes into jars. And the jars go to market, or they stay on a shelf in a thief's cottage, humming."

The silence that followed was the valley's own silence, imported indoors.

Wren's eyes had gone to the jar, then to Quentin's fingers on the glass, then up to Quentin's face. "You take them," Wren said slowly. "Not charms. Memories. From travelers crossing the border. You take them yourself."

"At the border-table. Where I keep the ledger. Where nobody looks twice at me, because I'm the one who writes down names and tolls." Quentin laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Eleven years, Wren. Eleven years you've sat across from me while I did it. You watched me lean forward and ask a traveler to *tell me about their happiest day* — and I'd listen, really listen, the way no one else listens — and while they talked, I'd reach into the telling and lift the whole thing out, and they'd walk on east feeling strangely light and unable to say why. And you sat there. And you never saw."

"I saw *something*," Wren said quietly. "I saw travelers leave your table looking like men coming out of church. Lightened. Unburdened. I told myself it was kindness. That you listened to them and it helped."

"It helped *me*." Quentin's hand curled away from the jar. "That's the confession you came for, isn't it? Why you really walked the drover's track in the dark. Not the elders' vote. Me. You came because you finally understood what I am, and you needed to hear me say it."

"What are you?" Wren asked, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse. There was only the question, bare and open, the way Wren asked everything — as if the answer, whatever it was, would be received without flinching.

And so Quentin said the word. The one that had lived in them for twenty years, unnamed, because naming it in Hallowmere would be like striking flint in a powder store.

"Memory-craft," they said. "The taking and keeping and giving-back of what happened. The oldest craft there is, maybe, and the rarest, because — do you know why it's rare, Wren? Every other gift burns bright and dies fast. Fire-craft, storm-craft, the healing hands — the crown hunts them because they're *loud*. But memory-craft is quiet. It hides inside ordinary gestures. Listening. Remembering. Forgetting. The crown has burned whole villages looking for bearers who could throw lightning, and all the while the bearer it should fear most was sitting at a border-table, trading a farmer his grief for a loaf of bread."

The stove ticked. The jar glowed. Somewhere far off, the river remembered itself for a moment and made a sound, then forgot again.

"And the crown," Wren said. Their voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice of someone walking on ice and hearing it speak. "The crown that's sending witch-hunters over every border in the kingdom. Hunting 'the gift.'"

"Hunting it. Yes." Quentin met their eyes. "Now you see why the elders' vote frightens me more than fire would. If the hunt comes to Hallowmere, they won't be looking for a stranger. They'll be looking for a *craft*. And crafts leave traces, Wren. Jars leave traces. Twenty years of travelers walking out of this valley lighter than they came in — that's a trail a blind man could follow."

* * *

Wren was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Quentin began composing apologies in their head — for the deception, for the years, for making Wren complicit without consent, for sitting across from them at the border-table with theft hidden in every smile. They had composed three separate apologies and begun a fourth when Wren spoke, and what Wren said was none of the things Quentin had prepared for.

"Show me your hand."

"...What?"

"The hand you touch the jars with. Show me."

Bewildered, Quentin held out their right hand, palm up, over the table. Wren took it — Wren, who touched people perhaps four times a year, always accidentally — and turned it over, and traced one finger along the lines of Quentin's palm, and then along the fine pale scarring at the base of each finger where twenty years of glass and craft had left their marks.

Then Wren pulled back their own sleeve.

"Look at mine," Wren said.

Quentin looked. They had looked a thousand times before — at the border-table, at the midsummer festivals, across campfires during the years they'd traveled the perimeter together checking the ward-stones. Fine silver lines, climbing from wrist to elbow, branching like frost on a window, like roots in shallow soil, like—

Like writing.

"I was marked at thirteen," Wren said. "Every warden is. We're told it's the oath taking root — that the words spoken over us sink into the skin and grow there, and that's what lets us feel the border, feel when something crosses, feel when magic tries to pass. It's the order's oldest mystery and we're taught never to question it." Wren's finger hovered above their own forearm without touching. "But I've stood at those stones for fifteen years, Quentin, and I've never once felt magic *die* there. I've felt it *move*. Feel the difference? Dying is an ending. What I feel at the border is a passing — something leaving one side and arriving on the other. All these years I assumed it was the valley draining travelers, the way we're taught. But the valley doesn't drain anything."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "The travelers cross," they said slowly, "and you feel the memory-craft move through the stones—"

"—because it's moving *through me first*." Wren's voice stayed level, but their hand had begun, very slightly, to shake. "The marks don't guard the border. They *conduct* it. Whatever crosses — whatever gift, whatever craft — passes through the warden's marks like water through a channel. I've been carrying every scrap of magic that ever crossed that border, Quentin. Fifteen years of it, flowing through me and out the other side, and I never questioned where it went, because it always went somewhere. It always went to—"

They stopped. Both of them looked at the same instant at the shelf of jars, glowing their soft colors in the dark — gold and green and one deep bruised violet — and at Quentin's hand, still lying palm-up on the table where Wren had examined it.

"To me," Quentin finished. "It came to me. Your marks don't stop the crossing. They *gather* it. They gather it and pass it to the nearest vessel, and the nearest vessel has always been—" They laughed again, and this time the laugh was closer to wonder than fear. "Wren. Do you understand what this means? Your oath isn't a wall. It's a *funnel*. The order didn't mark you to keep magic out of the valley. They marked you to *harvest* it. To catch every gift that crossed and pour it into whoever stood ready to receive it. Into a thief with jars."

"That's not possible." But Wren said it the way people say things they already believe. "The order is old. Older than the crown's hunt. The first wardens predate the capital itself—"

"And who do you think wrote the rite that marks you?" Quentin was leaning forward now, the cold excitement of a pattern completing itself moving through them like river-water. "Someone had to design the funnel, Wren. Someone had to want a border that gathered instead of guarded. Ask yourself who benefits. Ask yourself why the valley — the one place in the kingdom where magic supposedly cannot live — has produced, in secret, for generations, a steady trickle of exactly the thing the crown fears most. Ask yourself why the hunt has never once come here, in all the centuries. Not mercy. *Farming.* You don't burn the field you're harvesting."

The words fell into the room and lay there, and neither of them picked them up for a long moment. The stove had burned low. The jars hummed. And Quentin became aware, gradually, of Wren's hand — that it had not returned to Wren's own sleeve, but had come to rest on the table beside Quentin's, near enough that the silver marks and the pale scars were an inch apart, two scripts in the same dead language.

"We should have seen it years ago," Wren said at last. "We should have been asking these questions since we were children."

"We were children who were given answers and told they were walls." Quentin turned their hand over, palm down, so that the backs of their fingers nearly brushed the marks. "Walls are comfortable. Walls mean the danger is outside."

"And now?"

"Now I think the danger was never outside the valley at all. I think it's been flowing through your arms for fifteen years and sleeping on my shelf for twenty." Quentin looked up. "I think the crown hasn't come to Hallowmere yet because the crown doesn't know what grows here. But the elders voted today, Wren. They put my name on a list. Which means soon, someone from this valley is going to carry word to the riders — a name, a description, a direction. And when the hunt comes over those stones, they won't be guessing. They'll be *collecting*."

Wren's jaw tightened. "Then we have time. Days, maybe. The riders post their decrees at the mill and ride the circuit; they won't return for—"

The jar shattered.

Not the wedding-jar — that one sat untouched between them, gold light spilling across the wood. It was the third jar on the top shelf, the one Quentin had taken down only that morning, the one holding what remained of a dying traveler's hoard — fragments, glittering shards of a life, among them something strange Quentin hadn't yet dared examine closely. Fragments of a coronation.

The glass burst outward with no sound at all, which was the worst part — a shattering that made no noise, as if the valley's silence had swallowed even that. The memory inside did not spill and fade the way spilled memories did. It rose. A thin ribbon of light, gold shot through with threads of black, lifting from the broken glass like smoke from a doused candle — and turning, deliberately, unmistakably, toward Quentin.

Toward Quentin, the way a head turns.

"Wren," Quentin whispered. The ribbon hung in the air between them, and inside its shimmer Quentin could see the great hall again, the throne, the crown — and the crown was looking at them. Through the memory. Across miles and years and the magic-dead heart of a valley that was supposed to be safe. "*Wren, it sees me.*"

The ribbon darted — out the gap beneath the door, through the wall, gone, faster than any light had a right to move — and the cottage was suddenly only a cottage, dark and ordinary, smelling of tallow and yarrow, two people sitting very close together at a table with their hands almost touching.

Neither of them moved for a long time.

"Quentin," Wren said finally, and their voice was the voice from the border-table, steady, the voice that had talked travelers through their worst crossings. "Tell me everything about that traveler. Everything. Tonight."

Outside, far to the south, so far it might have been imagination, a bell began to ring — though there were no bells in Hallowmere, and never had been.
