## 6. Hounds of the Hunt

Wren had learned the border the way other people learned their own heartbeat: without thinking, without mercy, and always from the inside.

For eleven years they had walked the rim of Hallowmere at dawn and dusk, reading it the way a physician reads skin — for color, for heat, for the small betrayals that came before sickness. The valley's edge was not a wall. It was a silence with a shape. Beyond it, the world hummed with magic the way a marsh hums with insects: ley-light in the hedgerows, blessing-scar on old church stones, the faint sweet rot of spellwork left to spoil in ditches. Inside, nothing. The valley drank magic and gave back quiet. That was its covenant, its whole reason for being. Sanctuary meant sanctuary *from* — and what it kept out could not cross.

That was the first law Wren had been taught as a child, kneeling on cold flagstone while an oath-mistress pressed her thumb to the hollow of their throat: *What is dead to magic cannot be hunted by it.*

Wren had believed it the way one believes in gravity. Until the morning the hounds came through.

* * *

They felt it first as a wrongness in the birdsong.

Hallowmere's dawn chorus was a reliable instrument. Wrens and thrushes, the fat gray doves that nested in the mill's eaves, all of it tuned by years of unbroken habit into something almost liturgical. But this morning the song stuttered, thinned, and then stopped altogether along the eastern march — not fading but *cut*, as if a blade had passed through the sound itself.

Wren was already moving before their mind caught up with their body, boots finding the deer path down from the watch-crag, hand going to the iron-tipped staff they carried more for comfort than use. The mist lay thick in the low fields, the kind of valley fog that ate sound and made every step feel like wading through milk. Their breath came out white. The air smelled wrong — under the wet earth and woodsmoke there was something else now, something metallic and hot, like a forge left burning in a sealed room.

Magic. Faint, leashed, and *moving*.

Wren stopped at the boundary stone, the old moss-eaten marker that divided the humming world from the silent one, and pressed their palm flat against it. The stone was warm. Stones in Hallowmere were never warm. The valley drank everything down to room temperature, to stillness, to the temperature of forgetting — and this stone burned like a fevered brow.

Through the mist on the far side, shapes were coming.

Dogs first. Huge, black, wrong — their coats moved as if seen through water, and where their paws touched the ground, the frost did not melt but *blackened*. They cast no shadows. Wren's stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a boat taking on water. Shadow-hounds were crown work, bound beasts, creatures stitched together from a hunter's need and a sorcerer's craft. They should have unraveled at the border like thread pulled through cloth. They should have come apart into smoke and screaming.

They crossed the boundary stone without slowing, and the silence of Hallowmere closed around them like a fist.

Behind them came riders. Six, seven — dark cloaks, visored helms, the crowned-eye sigil of the Royal Inquisition stamped in lead-gray on their breasts. And at their head, a man on a pale horse who did not wear a helm, because he wanted to be seen.

Wren had heard the name whispered at the border markets, in the furtive voices traders used for things that might be listening: *Inquisitor Hale.* They had imagined a monster — some scarred giant, some zealot with fire in his fists. What emerged from the mist was worse. He was ordinary. Middle-aged, gray at the temples, with the mild, patient face of a schoolmaster or a country priest. He rode with his hands loose on the reins, unhurried, as though he were arriving at a parish supper.

The only remarkable thing about him was at his throat.

A collar. Wide, dull iron, fitted close to his neck like a yoke, worked with script so fine it looked at first like engraving and then, when the light shifted, like *writing that was moving* — characters crawling slowly around the band of metal, chasing each other's tails. And from it, Wren felt it even across forty paces of fog, came the hum. The same hum as the world outside the valley. Magic, carried like plague in a locked box, brought deliberately into the place where magic could not go.

*He isn't breaking the rules,* Wren thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *He's wearing them.*

* * *

The elders gathered at the mill within the hour, because word traveled fast in a valley that had nothing else to travel.

Wren stood at the back of the granary loft, where the meetings were always held, watching the faces below in the slanted morning light. Old Maren with her shawl the color of ash. The cooper Bramm, whose hands wouldn't stop moving, worrying a splinter of oak. The miller's widow, Tessa, holding her youngest against her hip as if distance alone could keep him safe. Eleven elders, and every one of them looking older than they had at harvest.

"They cannot stay," Maren was saying. "Whatever they are, whatever they carry — they cannot be allowed to make camp inside the lines."

"Make camp?" Bramm laughed, and it came out cracked. "Maren, they've already watered their horses at our well. The tall one asked me for bread and paid with coin I've never seen — stamped with the king's new face. They're not passing through. They're *arriving*."

"The law holds," said Tessa quietly. "It has always held. Magic dies at the border. It has died there for three hundred years."

"And yet." Bramm flung his arm toward the east window, toward the distant smudge of the hunters' campfire, which burned too steadily, too blue. "And *yet*."

Wren said nothing. Wren was doing arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

The collar. If the collar let a man carry magic across the line like contraband sewn into a coat lining, then the law hadn't broken. It had been *outmaneuvered*. And things that could be outmaneuvered once could be outmaneuvered again — bigger, colder, in greater numbers. The valley's sanctity had never been a wall, Wren realized. It had been a lock. And someone in the capital had finally built a key.

There was another arithmetic underneath that one, darker, and Wren kept trying not to look at it directly: the hunters had come *here*. Of all the valleys and hollows and hidden places in the kingdom, the hunt had ridden straight for Hallowmere, as if following a string tied to something inside it. Wardens guarded borders. Wardens also, in the oldest formulations of the oath, *reported*. Wren pushed the thought down where it couldn't breathe.

Below, the talk had turned, as Wren had known it would.

"There are strangers among us," said Aldous the reeve, in the careful voice of a man stepping onto ice. "Travelers who stayed past season. We have sheltered them because the valley shelters everyone."

"No," said Tessa.

"The decree was read at the market cross two days past," Aldous went on, relentless, gentle, terrible. "All bearers of the gift are to be surrendered to the crown's justice. Sheltering one is to share their fire. You all heard the rider read it. You all saw the seal."

"It said *bearers*," Tessa said. "None of ours bear anything. We're farmers. We're *nobody*."

"The travelers," said Maren, and would not meet anyone's eyes. "The ones who pass through. The ones we trade with. If any among them carry the gift—"

"They are guests."

"They are *strangers*," Maren said, and the word fell into the granary like a stone into a well, and everyone heard how deep it went. "My grandchildren sleep thirty feet from where you sit, Tessa. The hounds are a mile east. I will not choose a stranger's safety over theirs. None of us should be asked to."

"But you're asking," Wren said.

Their own voice surprised them — level, cold, coming from the shadow at the back of the loft. Eleven faces turned upward. Wren came down the ladder slowly, feeling every rung, letting the silence stretch until it hurt.

"You're asking the valley to become the thing it was built to hide from," they said. "Sanctuary that ends at the first hard question isn't sanctuary. It's a tavern with good manners."

"Warden." Aldous inclined his head, respectful and unmoved. "Your charge is the border. The border has failed. What remains to us is the people inside it."

"The border hasn't failed," Wren said. "It's been *picked*. There's a difference, and the difference matters, because a failed wall can't be fixed but a picked lock means someone has a key — and keys can be stolen, broken, melted down." They looked around the circle, at the fear dressed up as prudence, at Maren's averted eyes. "Give them a stranger, and you teach the hunt that Hallowmere opens from the inside. Do that, and no traveler will ever be safe here again — including the ones with nothing to hide. Including your own children, when they grow restless and leave and try to come home."

Silence. The millwheel turned somewhere below them, slow and patient, grinding grain no one would taste today.

"We'll vote at sundown," Aldous said at last, softly. "As the law provides."

Wren walked out into the gray light and found, waiting by the door, leaning against the wall with their arms crossed and their jaw set, Quentin Vane.

* * *

They had watched the meeting from the loft shadows; Wren had known they were there the whole time. Quentin had a way of occupying empty space that made it less empty — a thief's talent, or maybe just a lonely person's. Now they fell into step beside Wren on the path toward the eastern fields, and for a long while neither of them spoke. The fog was lifting. The hunters' camp was visible as a wound of wrong-colored fire against the tree line.

"You shouldn't have spoken for the travelers," Quentin said finally. "Now they'll look at you sideways. A warden defending strangers looks like a warden hiding something."

"I'm not hiding anything."

Quentin glanced at them sidelong, and there was something in the look Wren couldn't parse — amusement, warning, grief, all folded together like paper. "No," they said. "Of course not."

Ahead, the hounds had begun to move.

They poured out of the camp like spilled ink, six of them, flowing low across the frosted field — and they weren't hunting randomly. Wren watched them cast, fan, narrow, converge, and understood with a lurch that they were working a scent-line. Not toward the travelers' cottages. Not toward the market. Toward the *border road*. Toward the places where things came and went. Toward, if the line held true, the exact stretch of wall where a certain smuggler had slipped through the stones three times without consequence.

Three mercies. Wren counted them the way a gambler counts debts.

"They're tracking," Quentin breathed. "Inside the valley. How are they tracking inside the—"

"Because he brought the rules with him." Wren nodded toward the pale horse, where Inquisitor Hale sat watching his hounds work with the mild attention of a man watching weather. Even at this distance, the collar at his throat seemed to drink the morning light. "Everything the valley keeps out, he's carrying on his neck. He's made himself a walking piece of the outside world. Where he stands, the old law doesn't apply."

"That's not possible."

"Neither is this." Wren's grip tightened on the staff. "Watch."

The lead hound reached the boundary road and stopped, nose down, at a place Wren knew intimately — the gap in the stones, worn smooth by years of one particular body easing through it. The beast began to *dig*. Frost and black soil flew. And rising from the torn ground, visible even from fifty yards, came a shimmer — faint, gold-green, unmistakable to anyone who had spent years learning what leaked memory looked like when it escaped a jar.

Residue. Old thefts, ground into the dirt of the crossing place like wine into a tablecloth.

The hound threw back its head and made a sound that wasn't a bark. It was closer to a bell struck underwater, and every hair on Wren's arms rose to hear it, and across the field Inquisitor Hale turned his pale horse with unhurried grace and began, at last, to ride toward the village.

"He knows," Quentin whispered. All the flippancy had gone out of their voice; what remained was thin and young and frightened. "Wren, he knows something crossed here. He knows *what* crossed here. When he traces it—" They stopped. Swallowed. "When he traces it, he won't find a stranger. He'll find a *route*. And at the end of the route—"

"Is a person who's been very careful for a very long time," Wren finished quietly.

They stood together in the dying fog, warden and thief, watching the Inquisitor come on at his patient pace, the crawling script at his throat catching the light like a ring of tiny teeth. Behind them, the mill bell began to ring the alarm — three strokes, pause, three strokes — and doors could be heard closing all across the valley, one after another, like eyes shutting.

"Quentin." Wren kept their gaze forward, their voice low enough that only the two of them and the frost could hear. "Whatever you're carrying. Whatever's in those jars of yours. Today would be the day to decide what you can live without."

Quentin was silent a moment. When they answered, their voice had a strange texture, half bravado and half something rawer.

"You assume there's anything in my hoard worth an inquisitor's ride." A beat. "You assume it's about the jars at all."

Wren turned to look at them then, and found Quentin already looking back — and behind their eyes, deep down, in the place where a person keeps the thing they're most afraid of, Wren saw a flicker of genuine bewilderment. As if Quentin had felt something move inside them recently. Something that wasn't theirs.

Across the field, the hounds had gone still, all six heads turning as one — away from the border road, away from the crossing place — to fix their shadowless eyes on the two figures standing in the lane.

Inquisitor Hale smiled, raised one hand in courteous greeting, and rode on toward the village square, where the elders were gathering to vote.

Sundown was four hours away. Wren suspected none of them would get to use it.
