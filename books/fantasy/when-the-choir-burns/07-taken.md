## 7. Taken

They came at the hour when the valley forgets itself most thoroughly — that grey seam between night and morning when even the river seems unsure which way it flows. Quentin knew the moment the hounds crossed the border because the jars woke.

All of them. At once.

In the little house at the edge of the elder-row, where Quentin had lived alone for eleven years among shelves of green glass and clay-stoppered jars, every stolen memory began to glow like a jarful of startled fireflies. The wedding of a miller's daughter from six summers past. A fisherman's last sight of his father waving from a dock. A child's first snow, borrowed off a peddler woman who'd traded it for a warm meal and never known what she'd given. They flickered and shivered in their glass, and Quentin sat up in bed with their heart going like a trapped bird and understood: something was coming that remembered better than the valley did.

The valley forgot. That was its mercy and its curse. Roads went in crooked and came out straight; travelers arrived with full satchels of yesterdays and left lighter, unburdened, paid in bread and silence for whatever they could spare. Magic could not live here — the elders said so every solstice, in the tone of people reciting a prayer they no longer believed — and so nothing that hunted by magic could follow across the border stones.

But the jars were glowing. And under the glow, faint as a bruise through cloth, Quentin felt the other thing — the coronation fragments, the ones that had shattered their own jar and poured themselves somewhere Quentin couldn't find, couldn't hold, couldn't sell. They weren't glowing. They were *listening*.

"Come out, bearer." The voice carried across the fields the way voices do at dawn, flat and enormous, stripped of all softness. "The crown knows what sleeps in Hallowmere. Surrender it, or we will wake every stone until we find you."

Quentin dressed in the dark with hands that would not steady. They took down three jars only — the wedding, the snow, the fisherman's father — and pressed them into the loose earth beneath the floorboard where the turnips should have been. It was an old habit, hiding things. It had never once mattered. But the hands insisted.

Through the shutter-slats, Quentin saw them come up the elder-row: twelve riders on horses too fine for the mud of Hallowmere, and behind them two iron wagons whose sides caught the early light and gave none of it back. The men wore grey, unmarked but for a collar each of them wore at the throat — black iron, close-fitted, and around those collars the air bent slightly, the way it bends above a hot road. Even at this distance Quentin's teeth ached looking at them. The collars were doing something to the valley. Or the valley was failing to do something to the collars.

At their head rode a man who did not wear grey but black, and did not wear a collar but a chain of small linked rings that ran from his throat down beneath his breastplate. He sat his horse like a nail sits in wood — driven, not placed. His face was long and patient and terribly awake.

"Inquisitor Hale," one of the riders said, though there was no one to tell him. He simply knew. Some part of Quentin understood, in the animal way, that this man had been told everything about the valley already, and that no forgetting here would touch him, because he carried his memory outside himself, in that chain, in that collar-logic, in some craft Quentin had no name for yet.

*You will learn its name,* whispered something inside Quentin that was not Quentin. *I knew it before you were born.*

Quentin pressed both hands over their mouth and did not scream. Screaming would have been a relief. What came instead was stillness — the particular stillness of a thief hearing the floorboard creak above the hiding place.

* * *

They found Wren first.

Of course they did. Wren lived at the border station, the last roof before the standing stones, and the riders reached it before they reached the village. By the time Quentin slipped out the back and into the reed-beds along the river, dragging a sack of jars they hadn't the sense to leave behind, the wagons had stopped at the wardens' post and Wren was standing in the doorway in their nightclothes with their oath-marks bare on their forearms — those pale spiral scars that every warden bore, inked in ash and salt at seventeen, sworn to keep the border and keep it blind.

Quentin had seen those marks a thousand times. Had traced them once, in a season neither of them ever spoke of directly, when the nights were cold and the border station's stove was warm and Wren had said, laughing into Quentin's hair, *they're just scars, Q, they don't mean anything anymore.* Quentin had almost believed it.

Now an Inquisitor's gloved hand was turning Wren's arm in the grey light, reading the spirals like a merchant reading coin.

"Third-rank oath," Hale said. His voice was quiet in a way that made everyone lean in to hear it, which Quentin supposed was the point. "A finding-oath. Do you know what your order swore you to, warden?"

"To guard the border," Wren said. Their voice was level. Quentin knew that voice. It was the voice Wren used when they were most afraid — the flat, careful voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "To keep the valley forgotten and let it forget."

"To find," Hale corrected gently. "Your marks are a compass, warden. Every bearer who ever crossed your stones rang against them like a bell. Your order sold the ringing to the crown two hundred years ago, and the crown has been patient." He released Wren's arm. "Where is the thief?"

"I don't know what you—"

"The one who steals. The one whose hoard lit up like a festival when my hounds crossed the line. I have been following that light since the last ford." Hale turned in the saddle, slowly, surveying the village, the reed-beds, the river running silver and stupid and forgetful toward the lowlands. "Warden. You have a choice older than your oath. Bring me the bearer, and ride home to your order with your marks intact. Refuse, and I will read everything those spirals have ever touched — every crossing, every mercy, every time you looked the other way while contraband memory went over the stones."

He paused, and his patience was worse than any threat.

"There have been three such mercies," he said. "Haven't there."

In the reeds, Quentin's whole body turned to ice water. Three. Wren had never told anyone. Quentin had never told anyone. It had been theirs — the third mercy especially, the one where Wren had opened the inspection box, looked at the smuggled jar of a dead woman's lullabies, closed it again, and said *nothing today but reeds, Vane* without meeting Quentin's eyes.

And now it was currency on an Inquisitor's tongue.

* * *

They took the village first. Not violently — that was the horror of it. Hale's riders moved door to door with the courtesy of tax collectors, and the elders assembled in the mill-yard within the hour, because the valley had always obeyed uniforms; forgetting made obedience easy, made authority feel like weather, something that simply happened to you.

Quentin watched from the loft of the tannery, jars cold against their chest, and heard the vote.

It was not even a hard vote. Old Mother Cass, who had bought a dozen memories off Quentin over the years — her late husband's laugh, mostly, worn smooth as a river stone from replaying — stood up first. "We don't know any stranger," she said. "We don't know anyone strange. That's the law of the valley." And heads nodded, forty heads, sixty, the whole drowsy congregation of Hallowmere agreeing that no one they couldn't remember deserved protecting.

That was the thing about a valley that forgot. It forgot its debts too. Its kindnesses. Every memory Quentin had ever sold these people, every borrowed joy they'd carried home in glass — gone, spent, unowned. Quentin had thought they were giving these people back their dead. The valley had eaten the gift and kept only the hunger for more.

"They're in the reed-beds," said a boy's voice, high and terrible, and Quentin recognized Tam Ossel, fourteen, who had once traded a whole afternoon of his mother alive for a jar of it, and had cried when Quentin gave it back to him free. "By the tannery sluice. I saw the glass shine."

Children remember best. That was the oldest rule of the trade, and Quentin had broken it by being kind to one.

* * *

They dragged Quentin out of the reeds by the sack, and the jars broke against the stones of the sluice, and Quentin learned what it sounds like when thirty lives spill into mud — a sound like rain, like applause, like a crowd leaving a church. The memories rose off the wet ground in pale wisps and hung there, confused, orphaned, and then the nearest rider lifted his collar-chain and the wisps streamed into it like smoke into a flue, and his eyes rolled white, and he laughed, briefly, in a voice that was partly a miller's daughter and partly a child's first snow.

"No," Quentin said. It came out very small. "Those aren't — they were traded, they were *paid for*, they were—"

Hale dismounted and crouched beside Quentin in the mud, and up close his face was worse than it had been at distance, because it was kind. There was real kindness in it, banked and buried, the way embers are buried — deliberately, to keep them from dying or catching.

"Do you know what you are?" he asked.

"A trader," Quentin said. "A thief. Whatever word pays best."

"You are a vessel," Hale said, "and the crown has been looking for a vessel of your depth for a very long time." His gloved hand hovered near Quentin's sternum, not touching. "Something is hiding in you right now. Something old. My instruments are screaming and you are sitting in the mud looking bored." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "We'll get to that."

Then Wren hit the Inquisitor's horse.

Not Hale — the horse, a screaming sideways shoulder-check that sent the great animal rearing into the wagon-team, harness tangling, riders shouting, and in that chaos Wren was there, hauling Quentin up by the arm, and for one bright stupid second Quentin thought: *we run, we know the fens, we can lose them in the fens—*

Wren got four steps.

Then the warden stopped as if they'd walked into glass. Quentin saw why: the oath-marks. The pale spirals on Wren's forearms had gone dark, ink-black, and were crawling — actually crawling, like insects waking — and Wren made a sound Quentin had never heard from them, a small folded-up sound, and dropped to their knees in the mud with their arms held out from their body like branches heavy with ice.

"Oath-breaker's price," Hale said, not unkindly, standing and brushing off his coat. "Your marks are crown-work, warden. Did you truly think the order would leave its compasses free to point wherever they liked?" He gestured, and two riders took Wren under the arms. "You've been his leash since the day you were inked. Today he pulled it."

"I'm sorry," Wren said. To Quentin. Mud on their face, blood at their nose, the oath-marks still writhing. "Q, I'm sorry, I didn't know what we were, I didn't—"

"I know," Quentin said, and found they meant it, and hated meaning it, because there would be time later to be angry and right now there was only the wagon.

* * *

The wagons were lined with salt.

Quentin understood this in the worst possible way, which was gradually. They were put in the second wagon — Wren in the first, separated, and Quentin's protest died when a rider simply looked at them — and the floor was paved in coarse grey crystal that bit through clothing, through skin, and where it bit, it drank. Quentin felt it happen. Felt the stolen years leach out through their palms and knees, the residue of every theft soaked into their body over eleven years, drawn out grain by grain. It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part. It felt like setting down weights after so long carrying them that you'd forgotten you were carrying anything — a lightness, a floating, a horrible ease.

By the time the wagons rolled out through the standing stones, Quentin could not remember the name of the miller's daughter whose wedding they'd sold. Could not remember the fisherman's face. The valley slid away behind them, green and grey and already closing over its own tracks, and Quentin realized they could not remember which floorboards hid the three jars they'd buried, or whether they had buried any at all.

The salt took the thefts. All of them. Everything Quentin had ever taken from another person drained away into that grey crystal like color out of cloth.

But the coronation stayed.

Quentin felt it settle deeper as the salt worked — felt the fragments of that impossible crowning pull inward, wrap themselves around something at Quentin's center, and go quiet and dense as a swallowed stone. *Hide,* they seemed to say, in a voice that was not a voice. *They cannot take what I am keeping.* And Quentin, hollowing out wagon by wagon, mile by mile, understood with perfect clarity that they were now the only jar left. That the parasite-memory — the king's crowning, the thing that had looked back at them through a dead traveler's eye — had chosen their body for its glass.

The strange part — the part Quentin would have been ashamed of, if shame required a self to be ashamed with, and the salt was working on that too — was the relief.

Because here was the truth under the terror: Quentin had been leaving for years. Every market day, selling other people's yesterdays to buy bread in a valley that would forget the sale by supper. Every night, alone with shelves of glowing jars, richer than anyone in Hallowmere and owned by no one, known by no one, kept by no one except a warden whose mercies were a debt neither of them could name. The valley had never wanted Quentin. It had tolerated a useful ghost.

Now the ghost was being carried away in an iron wagon, emptied to the walls, and ahead — the riders said, talking easily among themselves as if their cargo were deaf — ahead was the capital, the city of bells, the court, the king. Ahead was whatever the thing inside Quentin wanted badly enough to hide for.

The road climbed out of the fen-country and the last of Hallowmere sank behind the hills. Quentin pressed their forehead against the wagon's iron wall, cold as a gravestone, and watched the salt crystals on the floor drink the last pale wisps of a life's thieving.

Somewhere behind them, Wren was shouting Quentin's name from the first wagon, over and over, as if repetition could hold a person in the world.

Somewhere inside them, older than kingdoms, something curled contentedly around Quentin's heart and began, very softly, to hum a coronation hymn.

Quentin did not know the song.

Their mouth knew the words anyway.
