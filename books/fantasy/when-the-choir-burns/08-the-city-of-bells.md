## 8. The City of Bells

The wagons stopped smelling of salt on the third day, but Quentin's skin kept remembering it — a prickling hollowness along the arms, as if the leeching had taken something they hadn't known they carried and left the shape of it behind. They rode with their wrists bound in iron that hummed faintly, a sound like a struck tuning fork held against bone, and they watched the road unspool toward a horizon that had begun, sometime past noon, to glitter.

That was the first wrongness: the glitter.

Hallowmere did not glitter. Hallowmere absorbed light the way its soil absorbed rain — gently, without argument, everything softened at the edges until even firelight seemed apologetic. But ahead of them the world had been dipped in polish. Roofs caught the low sun and threw it back like coins. A river wound through the middle distance, and where it turned, the water flashed white as a drawn blade. And above it all, rising out of the haze like the masts of a drowned fleet, stood the towers.

Bells, Wren had said once, long ago, in one of those border conversations that were half interrogation and half something else neither of them would name. The capital rings itself hoarse every dusk. You can hear it from the high passes if the wind is right. They say it's devotion. They say the king loves the sound.

Quentin had not thought about that in years. Now, hearing the first far-off toll roll across the plain — a sound so vast it seemed less heard than weathered, arriving in the chest before the ears — they understood that Wren had been repeating something they'd been told, and that whoever told it had also been repeating, and somewhere upriver of all that repetition the truth had been dropped like a stone no one wanted to carry.

Because the bells were not ringing for devotion. Quentin could feel it from three miles off, feel it the way they felt a memory worth stealing: a pressure, a sweetness, a wrongness dressed as beauty. Each toll passed over the land like a hand smoothing a rumpled sheet. And beneath the hand, faint but unmistakable to anyone who had spent their life handling other people's pasts, the sheet was full of lumps. Of things being pressed flat.

*Wards,* they thought, and the word arrived whole, unbidden, in a voice that did not quite feel like their own. *Every tower is a ward.*

The wagon jolted. The iron at their wrists sang a little higher.

* * *

They came into the city at dusk, which was, Quentin would later understand, deliberate. The hunt wanted the streets empty when the bearers were brought through. What they got instead was a city pretending to be empty — shutters closed, doors barred, but behind every gap a sliver of face, an eye, the pale gleam of someone watching the iron wagons pass and memorizing nothing, because the bells had rung at sundown and the memorizing had been taken from them before it could settle.

The streets were wide enough for six carts abreast and swept clean, and this too was wrong. Quentin had grown up — had they grown up? The thought snagged, the way thoughts snagged now, on a hook of salt-emptiness — in places where memory pooled like rainwater: doorsteps worn hollow by the same feet, walls stained at shoulder height by generations of leaning hands. This city had been scoured. Not by brooms. By sound. Every surface gleamed because no one's past was allowed to cling to it. The gutters ran clear. The corners held no dust, no cobwebs, no chalk marks, no old blood. It was the cleanest place Quentin had ever seen, and it made their stomach turn the way a scrubbed corpse turns the stomach — cleanliness achieved by removal of everything that had ever lived in it.

And threaded through the gleam, rot. That was the second thing. Where a bell tower's shadow fell longest, the polish failed. Paint blistered there. Plaster crumbled in fine gray snow. In one such shadow, a beggar sat motionless against a wall, mouth open, eyes tracking the wagons with the slow vacant drift of someone watching weather. As the wagon passed, the man's lips moved. Quentin, who could read the shape of remembered words on any mouth — a thief's trick, a survival's trick — caught only fragments: *my name is my name is my name is.* Repeated like a prayer with the middle missing. Like a man feeling along a wall in the dark for a door he'd been assured was there.

"Look away," said the hunter riding beside the wagon. He was young, and his collar — every hunter wore one, a band of dull metal at the throat — sat crooked, and he looked as though he hadn't slept since the valley.

"I'm looking," Quentin said. "You should too."

The young hunter's jaw tightened. He did look, briefly, at the beggar in the bell-shadow, and whatever crossed his face, he killed it quickly, the way one kills a spider — fast, and with a small private apology.

The wagon rolled on. Above them, the nearest tower released its hour-bell, and the sound fell over the street like warm water, and Quentin felt it slide against their mind and find — nothing. No purchase. The salt wagons had seen to that; they were, for now, poor fare. But they felt the ward *search*, felt it move through the crowd of their stolen selves like a landlord through a tenement, tapping walls, checking locks — and then withdraw, unsatisfied, and somewhere in the withdrawal Quentin heard, or imagined they heard, a note of frustration in the bell's great bronze voice. A hunger disappointed.

The coronation fragments stirred.

They had been quiet since the valley — the shards of some stranger's grandest day that had hidden inside Quentin when the hunters confiscated the jars, tucked into the folds of them like smuggled letters sewn into a coat lining. Quentin had not dared examine them. Examining felt like lighting a lamp in a cellar where something breathed. But now, under the bell's searching hand, the fragments shifted, and one rose close to the surface of Quentin's mind, and for a heartbeat they saw through a stranger's eyes again: gold, incense, ten thousand faces upturned, and above them all —

The vision folded itself away before Quentin could hold it, quick as a fish turning, and left behind only an afterimage: a face. High forehead. A mouth made for smiling. Eyes the color of rain over slate.

Quenten knew that face. Had carried it for weeks without knowing what it meant, the way one carries a splinter too deep to dig for.

They were still trying to decide whether to be afraid when the wagons turned a corner and the citadel opened before them, and the question stopped mattering, because the answer was standing on a balcony four hundred feet up, waving at a city that could not remember him.

* * *

The palace was built of white stone gone cream with age, and it should have been beautiful. From a distance, in the last copper light, it was beautiful — terraces descending like frozen waterfalls, banners stirring, windows catching fire from the sunset. But Quentin had spent their life reading surfaces for what lay underneath, and underneath the palace's beauty lay the same scouring that marked the streets, magnified. The stone was too clean. The gardens, glimpsed between balustrades, were geometric to the point of violence — every hedge identical, every path straight, not one fallen leaf permitted to lie where it fell. Nothing was allowed to accumulate. Nothing was allowed to *be* anywhere long enough to leave a mark.

It was a house where no one had ever been allowed to have a yesterday.

And on the highest terrace, framed against the burning sky, stood a figure in cloth-of-gold, and beside him a herald whose voice carried down through the strange acoustics of the plaza — carried too well, Quentin thought, shaped and amplified the way the choir-loft in a cathedral shapes a voice, and filed that thought away without knowing why it mattered.

"—your king greets you, children of the realm, your king who loves you, your king who remembers—"

The word landed strangely. *Remembers.* The herald said it the way one says a password one has been drilled in, with a hair's breadth too much care around the consonants.

Then the king raised his hand, and the crowd — thin, fearful, herded to the edges of the plaza — raised theirs in answer, and the bells began.

All of them. Every tower in the city at once, a score of bronze throats opening together, and the sound was not sound anymore but a tide, a physical thing that pressed the air flat and pressed everything under the air flatter still. Quentin felt it go through the plaza and through the people in it, felt memories being skimmed off the crowd like foam off waves — small ones, daily ones, the price of bread, a neighbor's grudge, the name of a dog dead twenty years — skimmed and lifted and drawn upward, and the bells drank it, and the towers glowed faintly at their crowns like lamps fed on something no one would name.

And the king stood in the middle of it, arms spread, golden, drinking.

No. Not drinking. *Being poured into.*

Quentin saw it because they had spent a lifetime seeing the invisible architecture of memory — how it moved, where it pooled, what drew it. And what they saw, in that terrible ringing moment, was that the king was not the source of the warding. He was its mouth. Its throat. Everything the bells skimmed from the city went up and out and home to him, into him, and he stood there receiving it with his arms spread and his face lifted, and his face —

His face was the face from the coronation.

High forehead. The mouth made for smiling. Rain-over-slate eyes. Older, heavier, silver threading the dark hair, but the same — the same man whose crowning Quentin had carried in a jar among the firefly-glow of other people's days. Quentin knew the exact moment the memory held: the crown coming down, the crowd roaring, the young king's face alight with terror and joy in equal measure, alive, *so* alive, a face you could read like an open book because every page was written on it —

And the face on the balcony had no pages.

It was smoothed. That was the only word. Smoothed like river stone, like wax held near a flame, like a wall replastered over a door someone didn't want found. The features were all correct and none of them were connected to anything. When the king smiled — and he smiled, broadly, beautifully, at the appointed moment in the herald's litany — the smile used every muscle a smile required and contained nothing a smile was *for*. It was a smile performed by a face that had forgotten, or been made to forget, what smiling meant, and was going through the motions from a manual.

The vertigo hit Quentin like cold water.

Because they knew both faces. The living one and the hollow one. They held them side by side inside themselves — the coronation shard rising unbidden now, blazing, refusing to hide — and between the two images stretched thirty years of absence, and somewhere in that absence, something had eaten a man from the inside and left the shape of him standing on a balcony, waving at a city it fed on.

*It looks out through him,* thought Quentin, and did not know where the thought came from, and the not-knowing frightened them more than the thought.

The king's gaze swept the plaza. Idle. Ceremonial. The gaze of a man acknowledging subjects.

It crossed the wagons.

Stopped.

For one second — one second measured against the rolling of the bells, so that it seemed to stretch and thin like taffy — the vacant eyes fixed on the iron wagon where Quentin sat bound, and the smooth face did not change at all, and that was the horror of it: no narrowing, no recognition, no malice. Just attention. Pure, weightless, total attention, the way a lens attends, the way an open mouth attends to what falls into it.

And inside Quentin's chest, the coronation fragments did not cower.

They *rose*. All of them at once, gold and incense and roaring crowds, the young king's living face, the weight of the crown descending — they rose like a congregation standing, and Quentin felt them turn, all together, toward that distant balcony, and what they turned with was not fear.

It was grief. Old, enormous, patient grief — the grief of a memory for the man it had belonged to, meeting the thing that wore him.

The eye on the balcony moved on. The bells rolled their tide. The herald's voice resumed, praising the king who remembers, and the king waved his golden sleeve, and the crowd, skinned of their small thoughts, cheered because cheering was the last thing the bells had left them.

Quentin sat very still in the rattling wagon with their heart slamming and the fragments settling slowly back into their hiding places like birds into reeds, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: whatever wore the crown had noticed them. Not their face — their cargo. Somewhere in the great skimmed flood of the city's memories, one thread had tasted of coronation, and the mouth at the top of the towers had paused mid-swallow.

Second: the fragments had hidden from the hunters' salt. Had hidden from the bells. Were hiding still, deep in Quentin, alive and loyal to something — and Quentin needed, with an urgency that felt older than themselves, to know what a memory wanted. What it was protecting. Why, of all the thousand stolen days in their hoard, these alone had chosen to survive.

The wagons groaned through gates of black iron into the palace's shadow. Behind them, the bells rang on, sweet and monstrous, pressing the city flat.

A guard with a collared throat and careful eyes unlocked the wagon. "Bearers," he said, "come down. The Inquisitor will see you at morning."

Above, in the high dark, the last bell of dusk tolled once and went silent, and in the silence Quentin heard — or dreamed they heard, and would spend many sleepless hours deciding which — a single voice threaded through the bronze decay, thin and human and unbearably far away, saying:

*Help me.*
