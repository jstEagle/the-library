## 9. Cells and Confessions

The cell was not built for holding people. It was built for holding what people carried.

Wren understood this within the first hour, lying on the stone shelf that served as a bed, watching the walls. They were salt-pale, veined with something darker — old iron, old grief, the residue of every prisoner who had ever been kept here and come out less than they went in. The mortar between the stones glittered faintly when the torchlight in the corridor guttered. Salt again. The wagons had been lined with it; so were these walls; so, Wren suspected, was the whole underbelly of the Citadel of Bells, a city above them ringing its hours while below it the crown quietly drank its prisoners dry.

They had taken Quentin to the left. Wren had heard the door, heard the boots, heard — worse than either — the silence afterward, the particular quality of quiet that meant someone was being made smaller somewhere out of sight.

Three days now. Or four. Time moved strangely in a room that ate memory from the edges of the mind. Wren had begun writing things on their forearm in charcoal scraped from the wall's base: *My name is Wren. I am a warden of Hallowmere. I swore at the border-stone.* The words were already smudging. They rewrote them each morning like prayers, or like stitches keeping a wound closed.

*Quentin Vane. Sharp elbows. Steals memories in jars. Laughs once for every ten times they want to.*

That line they wrote twice.

* * *

On the fourth day — or the fifth — the lock turned without sound, which was somehow more frightening than any grinding of iron would have been, and Inquisitor Hale came in carrying two chairs.

Two chairs. He set them down facing each other with the unhurried care of a man arranging an altar, and then he sat in one and gestured to the other as though this were a parlor in some provincial town and they were neighbors who had let a disagreement over a fence go on too long.

"Sit," he said. "You've been standing at that wall for three days. It doesn't help. The salt doesn't care whether you watch it."

Wren did not sit. "Where is Quentin?"

"Alive. Fed. Untouched." Hale folded his hands. Up close, in the honest light of the cell's single lamp, his face was worse than it had been in the valley — not crueler, just emptier, the way a house is emptier after the furniture has been sold off one piece at a time. The collar at his throat caught the lamplight along its seam. "You have my word, warden. Though I suspect my word means little to you."

"It means nothing," Wren said, and sat anyway, because standing was a posture of strength and strength was a currency they could no longer afford to spend.

"Good. Honesty is faster." Hale leaned forward. "I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to decide how much of it is true. That's all confession is, in the end — a story told to the right listener."

"I don't confess."

"Everyone confesses eventually. Most people only need the right question." He smiled, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was the most terrible thing about him yet. "Here is mine: what do you think we're hunting?"

"The gift," Wren said. "You posted it on every mill door from here to the border. Bearers of the gift are to be surrendered or burned."

"Yes. And what is the gift, Wren of Hallowmere? Say it plainly. You've seen it work. You've watched it work for years and called it smuggling."

The charcoal words on Wren's forearm seemed suddenly loud beneath their sleeve. Memory-craft. The theft and keeping of what other minds held. The rarest strain, the kind the old texts named only in cipher because even naming it invited it.

"You burn bearers," Wren said carefully. "That's the decree."

"The decree is paper," Hale said. "Paper burns beautifully. That's rather the point of decrees." He rose, crossed to the wall, and pressed his palm flat against the salt-veined stone. "Do you know why we line these cells with salt? Not to keep bearers in. Bearers are rare, and most of them are weak — hedge-witches who can nudge a neighbor's temper, dull a debt collector's arithmetic. No. The salt keeps the *crown* out."

Wren went very still.

"The king," Hale said, still facing the wall, "has not spoken an original sentence in eleven years. Oh, he speaks. He signs. He rides through the capital on feast days and smiles at crowds he does not recognize, including, sometimes, members of his own family. His physicians call it a melancholy. His priests call it a trial of faith. And the men who actually run this kingdom call him *the Vessel*, and thank their gods nightly that he is manageable." He turned. "He isn't ill, warden. He's *occupied*. There is something in that crown that eats memory the way fire eats wood, and it has been eating him since his coronation, and it has grown fat, and lately—" his voice dropped, and for the first time Wren heard something underneath the Inquisitor's calm that sounded, horribly, like fear— "lately it has grown hungry again. Hungry beyond him. Whole districts forgetting their dead. Parishes where no one can recall the name of the saint they've prayed to for six hundred years. It is reaching through him now, out into the kingdom, and it will not stop."

"So you hunt bearers," Wren said slowly, "to feed it."

Hale's expression flickered — pain, quickly mastered. "No. That's what I believed too, once. That's what I was told, when they put this collar on me and emptied me of everything I loved until obedience was the only thing left in the space. We hunt bearers because a bearer of the true gift — memory-craft, the deep kind, the kind that can *take a thing out of a mind entire* — is the only force in this world that can take the crown off the king's head without taking his head off with it." He sat back down. "We are not hunting bearers to destroy them, Wren. We are hunting for one bearer strong enough to unbind the king. Everyone else is fuel. Collateral. Ash to keep the search warm."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and salt, a bell began to ring the hour, and Wren thought of Quentin in a cell much like this one, and of the jars, and of the coronation fragments that had hidden themselves inside Quentin's flesh when the wagons took everything else — fragments Wren had never been told about but had felt, in the wagon, like heat from a banked stove.

"And if you find such a bearer," Wren said. "If your search succeeds. What happens to them afterward?"

Hale was quiet for a long moment.

"They pour everything they are into the crown," he said finally. "Every stolen memory. Every stolen year. All of it, at once, into that hunger — enough to gorge it, enough to tear it loose from the king. And what remains afterward is usually nothing." He met Wren's eyes. "There was one before. A bearer, ninety years ago. She got as far as the choir. The records end mid-sentence. Her sentence. Mid-*scream*, if you believe the older accounts, and I do."

"And you'd ask that of someone."

"I'd ask it of someone who was going to be burned otherwise," Hale said flatly. "Yes. I would. I have knelt before altars, warden. I know exactly what asking costs." He stood, smoothed his coat, and became again the instrument they had collared him into being. "Which brings us to you."

"To me."

"You are not a bearer. Your oath-marks are a lesser craft — binding, warding, the warden's art. Useful. But the crown doesn't want you." He paused at the door, hand on the silent lock. "It wants your testimony. A witness swears that Quentin Vane used the gift upon them — stole from their mind, violated them, endangered the valley. A victim's account carries weight that no inquisitor's report can. Sign it, and you walk out of this citadel free. Refuse, and you burn beside them — and burning a bearer of Quentin's depth requires a great deal of preparation, warden. Weeks of it. You would have weeks to reconsider, in a cell lined with salt, while everything you are erodes one grain at a time. I've done it. I can describe it to you in detail, if you think resolve is something you can keep."

* * *

After the door closed, Wren sat in the second chair for a long time, doing arithmetic.

Not the arithmetic Hale wanted — freedom against fire, self against ash. That sum was easy; Wren had solved it years ago at a border-stone in the rain, the third mercy, the moment they had understood that whatever they felt for the thief with the sharp elbows had stopped being duty somewhere along the way and become something with teeth.

No. The other arithmetic. The one that wouldn't close.

*A witness swears that Quentin used the gift upon them.*

And hadn't they? Hadn't Quentin stolen from Wren's own mind, once, early, badly — a memory of the border in winter, lifted half-playfully and returned the same night, sheepish, when Wren had caught them at it? Hadn't Wren let a hundred thefts pass at the border, each one technically a crime under any law the crown recognized? A testimony needn't be a lie to be a betrayal. That was the elegance of Hale's bargain, and Wren sat with the ugliness of admiring it.

But underneath the bargain, the truth Hale had let slip — perhaps carelessly, perhaps not — rang louder than the bells overhead.

*We are hunting for one bearer strong enough to unbind the king.*

Which meant Quentin was not prey. Quentin was the *answer*. The hunt had crossed into Hallowmere — impossible, magic-dead Hallowmere, sanctuary-quiet Hallowmere — not despite the valley's protections but drawn by them, drawn by the one thing the valley's forgetting-curtain could not hide: a thief hoarding stolen pasts in jars like fireflies, and among those jars, though neither of them had known it, fragments of a coronation. Fragments of the crown itself. Fragments that had looked back.

Wren looked down at their forearm. The charcoal had faded again. They licked their thumb and rewrote the lines, slowly, pressing hard:

*My name is Wren.*

*I am a warden of Hallowmere.*

*The hunt wants Quentin alive.*

And then, after a pause, a fourth line, because honesty was faster, and because the cell deserved at least one true confession:

*I lied to the Inquisitor. I would sign anything. I would swear Quentin stole my childhood, my name, my mother's face — if swearing it bought us both a corridor, a door, an hour.*

They stared at the words. The lie Hale had offered was a lie Wren could make truer than his — a testimony shaped like surrender that was really a key. Confess to the crime, name yourself the victim, and be led, escorted, *moved*. Prisoners were dragged. Victims were tended. And victims of memory-theft were, by the hunt's own doctrine, precious evidence — to be kept close, kept intact, kept *near the bearer*.

Near Quentin.

Wren blew on the charcoal dust and watched it drift toward the salted wall, where it settled and vanished, grain by grain, eaten.

"Hale," they said aloud to the empty cell, testing the shape of the man's name, the shape of the game. "When you come back, I'll have a confession for you. Every word of it true except the one that matters."

Above them, the bells rang the hour again — or perhaps a different hour entirely; time was salt-thin here — and far away, behind another silent door, Wren imagined Quentin sitting in the dark with a coronation sleeping in their blood, and prayed to no god in particular that whatever the crown wanted from them, it would be slow about wanting it.

The lock, when it finally turned again, made no sound at all.
