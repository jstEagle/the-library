## 10. The Thing Wearing the Crown

They dressed me in borrowed silk.

I want to say that first, because everything else that happened in the throne room has a way of crowding it out — the king's face, the flare of memory behind my eyes, the thing that turned its attention on me like a lighthouse beam — but the silk came first, and the silk mattered. It was dove-grey, shot through with thread so fine it moved like water when I breathed, and it was not mine, and no one asked if I wanted it. Two attendants with faces like closed shutters worked the buttons at my spine while a third brushed the road-dust from my hair with a comb of bone-white horn. None of them spoke. In Hallowmere, silence is the valley breathing; here, silence was a garment too, fitted close, worn by everyone.

"You'll kneel when the chamberlain strikes the floor," one said at last, not looking at me. "You'll rise when the king bids you. You will not speak unless spoken to. You will not look above his collar."

"Above his collar," I repeated.

"The crown," she said, and her voice did something small and involuntary, a catch, a hitch, like a stitch pulled tight. "You will not look directly upon the crown."

I had spent nine days in a cell learning the grammar of the capital's silences, and I heard this one clearly: *we are all afraid to look at it, and we have agreed not to say so.*

They led me through corridors where every third stone was set with a dull iron bell, no clapper, just the shape of a bell cast into the wall, and I understood them now for what they were — wards, ears, a city studded with instruments tuned to the gift. My skin prickled as we passed each one. They were listening for theft. Somewhere beneath my ribs, the coronation fragments sat coiled and quiet, the memories I had stolen from a dying traveler on the valley road: gold light, an anointing, a young man's face lit from below by ten thousand candles, a weight descending toward his brow. They had survived the confiscation because they had hidden themselves inside me, folded into the marrow of my own recollections, and I had learned to keep my thoughts flat and grey whenever the bells hummed. I thought about bread. I thought about mud. I thought about nothing with tremendous discipline.

The doors to the great hall were taller than Wren standing on my shoulders, banded in bronze, and they opened without being touched, which told me everything about who was expected.

* * *

The throne room smelled of beeswax and old smoke and, underneath both, something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room. Light fell from clerestory windows in long dusty blades. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, hundreds of them, jeweled and still, and not one of them looked at me. They looked at a point six inches above my head, uniformly, the way a field of sunflowers tracks a sun that isn't there.

And there, at the far end, beneath a canopy of black velvet worked with silver stars, sat the king.

I knew his face.

That is the sentence I could not get past, in those first seconds, kneeling on cold marble while a chamberlain read out my crimes in a voice like a ledger being balanced. I knew his face. Not the way one knows a face from coins and proclamations — though his was everywhere in this city, stamped in profile on gates and banners, serene and generic as any king's — but the way one knows a face seen up close, in candlelight, mid-moment. I had watched this man be crowned. I had stood inside a dying stranger's skull on a road in the mountains and felt the hush of ten thousand people, the oil sliding warm on a young brow, the terrible beautiful gravity of a crown coming down.

He had been perhaps twenty-five then. He had laughed, once, when the archbishop's hands shook and the oil dripped onto his nose, and the whole cathedral had held its breath and then broken into laughter too, relieved, human, and he had wiped his nose with the back of his hand before remembering he was a king, and the laughter had doubled.

The man on the throne did not laugh. The man on the throne did not do anything. He sat with his hands open on his knees, palms up, like a man waiting to receive something, and his face —

His face was *smooth*. That was the word my mind kept returning to, inadequate as it was. Not unwrinkled; smooth the way a river stone is smooth, the way something becomes when it is handled constantly, rubbed by years of use until every particularity wears away. His eyes were open and dark and aimed at the middle distance, and behind them there was — I searched for the image and found it, horribly, in my own trade — behind them there was the emptiness of a jar after the firefly has died. A vessel shaped for holding, currently holding nothing.

"Quentin Vane," the chamberlain intoned, "of the valley called Hallowmere, taken bearing the gift unregistered, unsworn, and uncollared. Brought before the throne for judgment."

Stillness. Then the king's head moved.

It was a small motion, a degree or two, but in that hall of held breath it landed like a dropped chandelier. His gaze came down from the middle distance and settled on me, and I felt it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck — physical, specific, warm.

"Let him approach," the king said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part, how pleasant it was, a voice with all the rough edges sanded off, a voice like the silk they'd put me in. "Let the bearer approach and give the kiss of fealty, as the rite requires."

A ripple went through the courtiers, so faint it might have been draft. The chamberlain struck the floor twice with his staff, and I rose, and I walked the length of that hall with my heart going like a trapped bird, past ranks of jewels and held breath, up three steps to the dais, to the king.

Up close, the wrongness got worse. His skin was fine — too fine, unlined, unweathered, a man of forty-odd years wearing the complexion of a portrait. His breath, when I leaned near, smelled of nothing at all. And the crown —

I didn't look at it. I had been told not to look at it, and some animal part of me had already decided to obey, the way you don't stare into a hole you can hear breathing from. But I saw it anyway, the way you see anything at the edge of vision: black metal, older than the silver stars on the canopy, older maybe than the hall, its surface moving very slightly, the way deep water moves. It sat on his brow without touching him. There was a gap, a shadow, a hair's width of air between circlet and skin, and in that gap something *waited*.

"Kneel," said the chamberlain.

I knelt. The king extended his right hand, palm down, for the kiss of fealty, and I took it.

* * *

The coronation flared.

There was no gentleness to it, no slow rising. It went off in me like a struck match in a dark room — the whole stolen sequence, every fragment I'd carried since the mountain road, igniting at once. Gold light. Oil on a young brow. Ten thousand candles. The hush. The laugh, the oil on his nose, the doubled laughter rolling up through the vaulting like weather. The crown beginning its descent, heavy as a verdict —

And then the memory kept going, further than it had ever gone before, because memory is a country and I had only ever walked its borders, and now something took my hand and walked me into the interior.

*I felt what he felt.* The crown settling on him. Not weight — *appetite*. A cold ring of hunger closing around his skull like fingers around a wrist, and then the feeding: his mother's face going first, dissolving like sugar in water; the smell of his childhood kitchen; the name of the nurse who taught him to whistle; his own wedding day, a girl with ink-stained fingers, gone, gone, the grief arriving without its cause, grief with the memory amputated out of it, pure and sourceless and unbearable. He screamed. I remembered him screaming — the traveler's memory had never shown me this, had stopped at the glory of it, because the traveler had died before the truth, or fled it — he screamed inside the applause while ten thousand people cheered the thing eating him, and the crown drank the scream too, drank it like wine, and smoothed his face, and set itself comfortably upon the vacancy it had made.

*A parasite,* I thought, kneeling, lips pressed to the back of a dead man's living hand. *It ate him. It has been ruling through his mouth ever since.*

The pleasant voice above me said words I barely heard — something about mercy, something about the collar, something about cooperation — and beneath the words, beneath the whole ceremony, I felt the memory-flare begin to gutter, and I made the mistake. The mistake I will carry even now, in whatever self I am becoming.

I looked up. Past the collar. At the crown.

* * *

It was already looking at me.

I don't know how else to say it. Eyes are wet and round and sit in faces; this had none of those things, and yet it *looked*, the way a keyhole looks when you feel someone on the other side of the door, the way a jar looks when you know something inside it has turned toward the glass. All the attention of that vast dark circlet gathered on me like water finding a drain, and in the same instant the coronation memory in my chest — *my* stolen fragments, folded into my marrow, hidden from bells and salt and nine days of cells — flared again, deliberately, unmistakably, a lantern uncovered in a window.

*There you are,* said the crown. Not in words. Words would have been merciful. It was more like pressure, like a thumb testing the ripeness of fruit. *There you are, little thief. You have been carrying pieces of me. Pieces of the feast.*

My whole body went cold and then hot. I had been invisible my entire life — that was the gift and the curse of Hallowmere, that was the whole architecture of my survival: take small, stay quiet, be forgettable in a valley that forgot everything. And now I stood on a dais before the entire court of the capital, in borrowed silk, with my lips still against the king's hand, and something older than the kingdom had turned its whole vast hunger on me and *recognized* me, the way a collector recognizes a piece that wandered out of its cabinet.

*Give them back,* it pressed, gentle as rot. *What you stole. Give them home. Open, little thief. I have been so hungry, and you have carried such a long way—*

The king's hand twitched under my mouth. Just once. A spasm, a fish-kick — and in that flicker, underneath the smoothness, I saw him. The young man with oil on his nose. Drowned, buried, pressed flat at the bottom of himself like a flower in a book, but *there*, looking out through his own eyes from very far away, and what was in his face as our eyes met across that impossible distance was not rage or despair.

It was warning.

*Run*, said the last living sliver of King Aldric, with whatever remained of his mouth. *Don't let it know what you—*

Too late. The pressure redoubled, delighted, a child finding a loose thread. *Oh,* said the crown, and the sweetness in it curdled my stomach. *Oh, you're not just a carrier. You can* open. *You can pour. Do you know how long it has been since I tasted a true bearer? Do you know what you would be worth, opened?*

I wrenched my mouth from the king's hand. I don't remember deciding to; my body simply left, the way a hand leaves a stove. I staggered back a step on the dais, and the court gasped as one creature, and guards moved at the edges of my blurring vision, and somewhere far away the chamberlain was crying something about decorum, about the rite, about the king's displeasure—

The king rose from the throne.

He moved wrong. That is the only honest description. He moved the way a puppet moves when the puppeteer stops bothering to pretend — fluid where joints should bend and stiff where bodies flow, and when he smiled, the smile arrived a half-second before his face agreed to it. The whole hall bowed as one, silk and jewels folding downward, and I alone stayed upright, swaying, marked, *seen*, while the thing wearing the crown descended the three steps toward me with its host's feet.

"Bearer," it said, through the pleasant ruined mouth, loud enough now for the whole court, and the word rolled through the hall like a bell with a crack in it. "The crown extends its protection. The bearer will be lodged in the high tower. The bearer will be *studied*."

Studied. The word went through me colder than any blade. Behind it I felt the crown's attention settle over me like a net, patient, proprietary, a collector's hand closing around a jar.

*Soon,* it pressed, one last time, intimate as a whisper against my ear though the king stood an arm's length away. *When the doors are locked and the bells are rung and no one is watching but me. You will open for me, little thief. Everything you carry. Everything you are.*

Guards took my arms. I did not resist; resistance was a coin I could not afford to spend. As they turned me toward the doors, I looked back once — one heartbeat, all I dared — at the king standing beneath the black canopy, palms open, face smooth.

But his eyes. His eyes were still fixed on me, and in their drowned depths, small and desperate and impossibly far away, the young man with oil on his nose was mouthing something over and over, silent, urgent, a message passed hand to hand through the dark:

*The choir. The choir remembers. Find the choir.*

Then the doors closed between us, and the bells in the walls began, softly, to listen.
