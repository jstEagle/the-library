## 12. Escape Through the Choir

The guard's name was Aldric, though he would not remember Quentin learning it.

He stood his post at the mouth of the undercroft stair with the particular stillness of a man who had learned that looking at nothing was safer than looking at something. His helmet was too big for him; it sat on his skull like a pot on a turnip, and beneath its shadow his eyes had the glazed shine of someone who had been in the capital long enough to feel the bells working on him, wearing grooves in him, smoothing him the way water smooths a stone. Quentin had watched him for two days from the shadow of a buttress, learning his rhythms: the way he shifted his weight at the half-hour, the way he touched the hilt of his sword not as a soldier but as a man touching a talisman, the way he sometimes hummed — three notes, always three notes, some fragment of a song whose rest had been rung out of him by the towers.

Quentin knew how to be small. The valley had taught them that. In Hallowmere, where memory pooled and drained like tide-water, a thief who could not make themselves forgettable did not survive their first winter. So they became the kind of absence that guards do not report: a smudge in the torchlight, a draft with a shape, a nothing standing very close behind a something.

They reached out with the gift.

It was never like the stories said, never a claw or a hook. It was more like leaning close to a window on a cold night and breathing against the glass, and watching the fog of your breath bloom there, and pressing your palm into it — and finding that your hand came away wet with someone else's warmth. Quentin breathed against Aldric's mind and felt the fog bloom, felt the man's attention go soft and inward, and pressed.

The memory came away easily. Too easily. That was the thing about the capital that made Quentin's skin crawl: everyone here was already leaking. The bells siphoned them day after day, and what remained sat loose in their skulls like river stones in a flood, ready to roll free at the slightest touch.

Aldric's memory unfolded in Quentin's hands like a map drawn in sweat and lamplight. The undercroft: a long descending stair, thirty-one steps, the thirteenth one cracked and singing faintly underfoot. Salt-lined cells to the left — Quentin's own body remembered those, the leeching burn of salt-iron against the gift. But to the right, past the wine racks gone to vinegar, past the archive door with its triple lock, a narrow passage the guards called the Weepers' Walk because the walls there sweated in winter. It ran beneath the nave itself. And at its end—

At its end, a door no one opened. A door the guards were told was bricked shut. But Aldric had been young once, and curious once, and one night years ago he had put his shoulder to it and found it swung wide onto darkness, and inside that darkness he had heard —

Here the memory shivered. Here Aldric's own recollection flinched from itself, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

He had heard singing. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. A choir with no bodies, singing down in the dark under the cathedral, and every voice in it familiar, and none of them belonging to anyone alive.

Quentin folded the memory closed and slid it into the hidden place inside themselves where the coronation fragments lived, that warm dark pocket where stolen things hid from the crown's hungry white veins. Aldric blinked. Shifted his weight. Touched his hilt. Hummed his three notes — and paused, frowning, because for a moment the song had felt emptier than usual, as if a room he'd known had lost a piece of furniture.

Then the half-hour bell rang, and he forgot even to wonder.

* * *

The stair took Quentin down through layers of the building's history like an archaeologist's cut: dressed stone, then rougher stone, then stone so old it had gone soft-edged as candle wax. The air changed with each descent. Above, the capital's air had been all smoke and bell-bronze and the sweetish rot of a city that gleamed and stank in equal measure. Here it cooled and thickened, and took on a taste Quentin recognized from the valley — the taste of old magic, dead magic, magic that had been poured into walls until the walls themselves became the spell.

The thirteenth step sang under their foot, exactly as promised: a thin metallic whine, quickly swallowed. They froze. Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the bells rang the hour, and the white veins in the walls — those pale mineral threads that laced the cathedral like frost through a windowpane — pulsed gently toward the sound. Even down here they leaned toward it. Even buried, the building worshipped its bells.

The Weepers' Walk was exactly as Aldric remembered, which was to say worse. The walls did sweat, a slow oily beading that smelled of salt and iron, and Quentin kept their shoulders away from it and thought of Wren and hated themselves for thinking of Wren, because thinking of Wren was a wound they kept choosing to press.

Four days since the wagons. Four days since the salt-leached iron had drunk from their hoard through the slats while Quentin sat with their hands empty in their lap, feeling seven jars' worth of stolen lives being poured out of them like wine from a cracked cask — and feeling, beneath the grief, a terrible lightness. That was the shame of it. When the hunters had pried the jars from the wagon and found six of them shattered, empty, their contents vanished as if evaporated, Quentin had felt relief like sunlight. The coronation fragments had hidden inside them, curled up against their heart like cats before a fire, and the crown's leeches had passed over them without a whisper. Some part of Quentin had rejoiced.

Some other part had understood, even then, that things which hide inside you do not stay harmless forever.

The passage ended at the door. Old oak, iron-banded, unbricked despite the guards' certainty — but swollen shut with centuries, and sealed with a lock whose keyhole had been filled with lead. Quentin set their palm flat against the wood.

The gift rose in them unbidden.

Not theft this time. Something else — something older, something the valley had never taught them because the valley had no teachers left. The door remembered being opened. Wood remembers everything; it is the one material that keeps its memories in rings, year over year, and this door had been listening to the dark on the other side for four hundred years. Quentin leaned into that memory the way they leaned into a traveler's, and the door sighed, and its swollen fibers relaxed like a fist unclenching, and it swung inward with a sound like a long-held breath finally released.

Cold air flowed out. And with it, faintly, unmistakably:

Singing.

* * *

The choir of the cathedral of Vespergate had been abandoned for two hundred years, and it had not stopped singing once.

Quentin stood at the threshold of a vaulted space like the inside of a throat — ribbed stone arching overhead into darkness, the great east window above them bricked over in some forgotten purge, so that the only light came from a handful of guttering lamps left burning by no one. Dust lay thick on the curved wooden stalls, the singers' benches, undisturbed except for the small grey drifts of pigeons long dead. Cobwebs hung from the arches like the rigging of a drowned ship.

And the air was full of voices.

Not loud. Not present, exactly. It was as if the room itself had soaked up every note ever sung within it and could not wring them out — two centuries of plainsong and lament and ordinary hymns, layered into the stone the way smoke layers into rafters. Standing in that acoustic, Quentin heard fragments surfacing and sinking: a child's treble stumbling through a psalm; a bass line rolling like a cartwheel; a single soprano, somewhere far back in the accumulated years, holding one pure unwavering note as if she had been holding it since her death and intended to hold it until the end of the world. The voices did not sing together. They sang across each other, through each other, a congregation of the dissolved, and the effect was not horror. That was the strange thing. It was not horror at all.

It sounded like being remembered.

Quentin walked down the center aisle and the voices parted around them and closed again behind, and they understood — with the thief's instinct, the sense for value that had kept them fed through twenty Hallowmere winters — that this room was a hoard beyond any they had ever imagined. Every jar they had ever filled held one life, one traveler, one stolen afternoon. This room held thousands. This room was a jar the size of a soul.

And the crown did not know about it. The white veins stopped at the choir's threshold; Quentin could see them in the lamplight, those pale mineral threads running through the outer walls, and here they simply ended, pruned back, as if the building itself had amputated the parasite from this one chamber. Whatever had happened here two hundred years ago, whatever purge had bricked the window and emptied the stalls, it had also — deliberately or not — built a blind spot. A place the crown could not see.

A place to breathe. Quentin sat down on the lowest stall, in the dust, and let their head fall back against the wood, and for the first time since the iron wagon they laughed — a small, cracked, disbelieving sound that the choir caught and multiplied and gave back to them in fifty voices, so that it seemed a whole congregation of Quentins was laughing in the dark, all of them equally astonished to be alive.

That was when they heard footsteps on the Weepers' Walk.

* * *

Quentin knew the stride before they saw the shape of it. Twenty years of border-watching will teach you a person's walk the way a mother knows a cry in the night, and Wren walked like someone perpetually apologizing to the ground for disturbing it — heel first, weight low, a warden's walk, a hunter's walk, a walk designed to arrive unheard. Except that Quentin had spent twenty years teaching themselves to hear it anyway.

Wren came around the arch in the doorway with a stolen lamp held high and a stolen knife low, and when the light found Quentin sitting in the dust of the choir stalls, the knife dropped to Wren's side and their whole face did something complicated — collapsed and rebuilt itself in the same instant, like a house in an earthquake that decides, mid-fall, to stand.

"You're supposed to be in a cell," Wren said.

"You're supposed to be taking Hale's bargain." Quentin did not stand. It was petty, and they knew it was petty, and they stayed seated anyway, arms crossed, dust in their hair, letting the choir's ghost-congregation murmur around them both. "That's what I heard. Freedom for testimony. All you have to do is say the thief bewitched you. Say the valley poisoned you. Say anything at all, Wren, as long as it ends with me burning."

The lamp trembled. Just slightly. Wren's hands did not tremble; Wren's hands were the steadiest thing in any room they occupied, which was why the trembling of the lamp went through Quentin like a blade.

"I didn't take it."

"You didn't say no either."

"How would you know what I—"

"Because you're here." Quentin stood now, and the choir lifted their voice with them, dozens of phantom throats rising in sympathy, and they hated the drama of it and could not stop. "You're here instead of in a warm room signing your name. Which means you didn't choose me, Wren. You just failed to choose them. There's a difference, and I've been turning it over for four days in a salt cell trying to decide which one I can live with."

The words landed. Quentin watched them land, watched them go in deep, and felt no satisfaction — only the sick heat of having aimed true at the one person they had never wanted to hurt. That was the thief's curse, they thought. You learn where everyone keeps their tender things. You can't unlearn it. And when you're frightened, your hands go to the tender places on their own.

Wren set the lamp down on the stall edge, slowly, the way you set down something that might otherwise become a weapon. "Hale told me things," they said quietly. "In that room. He told me what the hunt is for. He told me—" and here Wren stopped, and looked at the bricked-over window, and at the cobwebbed stalls, and at the dust that had not been disturbed in two hundred years, and Quentin saw the exact moment their warden's composure met something it could not file or categorize.

"The voices," Wren said. "Do you hear—"

"Yes."

"What is this place?"

"A blind spot." Quentin came closer, slowly, the anger draining out of them and leaving behind the tiredness that had been underneath it all along, four days deep, salt-flavored. "The crown's veins don't reach in here. Whatever burned this choir out of the cathedral's memory, it left a hole. A place where we can exist without being seen." They stopped an arm's length from Wren. Close enough to touch. Far enough to choose not to. "So. Did you refuse him?"

Wren was silent for a long moment. In the silence, the choir murmured its centuries — the child's psalm, the rolling bass, the soprano holding her endless note — and the lamp flame leaned and steadied.

"I stood in that room," Wren said at last, "and I realized that everything I was going to trade for my life, I'd already traded. My oath. My order. Twenty years at the border, guarding a valley I was sent to map for the hunt." Their jaw tightened. "I don't know what I am anymore, Quentin. I know what I'm not. I'm not his. But I can't tell you yet what I am, because I don't — I haven't finished finding out."

"That sounds a lot like a maybe."

"It sounds like the truth. You of all people should recognize it. It's ugly and incomplete and you steal better versions of it from strangers every day."

Despite everything — despite the salt cell and the empty jars and the four days of rehearsing accusations — Quentin felt their mouth twitch. The choir caught the almost-laugh and passed it around among the dead voices like bread at a table, and the absurdity of being forgiven-by-acoustics broke something loose in their chest.

"We need to move," they said. "This blind spot won't hold if they come looking. But Wren — while I was in the cells, I stole a guard's memory. The undercroft. The archive. There's a vault down there, past the wine racks, triple-locked, and the guards are afraid of it in a way that isn't normal fear. It's the way you're afraid of a thing that feeds." They held Wren's eyes. "My jars are down there. And I don't think mine are the only ones. I think it's been collecting for centuries."

Something moved through Wren's face then — not surprise. Recognition. The look of a person hearing a suspicion confirmed, and Quentin filed that look away the way they filed everything, in the warm dark pocket where the coronation fragments slept, next to the growing list of things Wren knew and had not said.

"The vault," Wren repeated carefully. "You want to break into the crown's vault. While the hunt is hunting us. With the whole city ringing bells over our heads."

"I want to see what's feeding," Quentin said. "Don't you?"

Above them, through forty feet of stone, the great bells began to ring the change of the watch — and in the choir, for the first time in two hundred years, the dead voices fell silent, every one, all together, as if listening.

As if waiting to find out what Quentin would dare next.
