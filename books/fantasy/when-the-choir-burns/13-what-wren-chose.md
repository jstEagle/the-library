## 13. What Wren Chose

The choir held its breath around them.

Wren had found Quentin by sound before sight — a small, ragged breathing in the dark above the nave, where the abandoned loft of the choir still kept its rotted benches and its broken songbooks, their pages furred with dust like the grey down on a fledgling's throat. The acoustics up here were strange. Every breath Wren drew came back to them a half-second later, softened, as if the cathedral itself were repeating it in a kinder voice. *You are here. You are here.* The stones remembered voices; everyone in the capital said so, though no one sang anymore. Wren could feel the memory of music pressing against their skin like warmth off a hearthstone — a thousand years of hymns soaked into the masonry, waiting for throats that would never come.

Quentin sat on the floor with their back against a pillar, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them. In the thin light that fell through a shattered rose window — glass the color of old wine, of bruises, of dusk over Hallowmere — their face was pale as paper and just as blank.

"You came," Quentin said. Not *thank you*. Not *I'm glad*. Just: *you came*, in the flat tone of someone confirming a debt.

"I always come," Wren said, and heard how it sounded, and wished they could take it back.

They crossed the loft slowly. Their boots left prints in the dust, and behind them, faintly, the floor seemed to echo the pressure of each step a moment too long, the way snow holds a footprint after the foot has gone. The oath-marks along their forearms prickled under their sleeves — a cold, crawling itch, like ants beneath the skin, like frost forming on the inside of a window. They had felt it since they entered the cathedral. They had felt it for years, if they were honest, and had spent those years not being honest.

"Are you hurt?" Wren knelt an arm's length away. Close enough to reach. Far enough to be refused.

"No." Quentin's eyes tracked them. Grey-green, the color of the valley in late autumn, and Wren had thought a hundred times about writing that down somewhere, because everything about Quentin was something Wren was afraid of losing. "Hale didn't hurt me. He doesn't need to. He just—" Quentin's hand made a small, exhausted gesture. "He talks. He talks like salt dissolving into water. You don't notice you've swallowed anything until it's already in your blood."

Wren knew. Wren had sat across from Hale for two hours in a cell that smelled of lye and candle-tallow, and listened, and felt the words go in sweet and slow.

"Quentin." Their voice came out lower than they intended. The choir took it and gave it back gentler, which felt like mockery. "There are things I have to tell you. And I need you to let me finish all of them before you decide what I am."

Something moved behind Quentin's eyes — wariness, yes, but underneath it, worse, a kind of bracing. The look of someone who has carried fragile jars all their life and can tell, by the way another person walks, when the floor is about to shake.

"All right," Quentin said quietly.

Wren sat. Fully sat, cross-legged in the dust, because this confession deserved a body that wasn't poised to flee. The rose window threw its wine-colored light across both of them, and somewhere far below, a bell that no one rang hummed anyway, a low bronze note like a struck memory.

"Hale offered me a bargain," Wren began. "Yesterday. Before I found you."

Quentin went very still.

"He offered me what he has. A collar — but a collar freely worn, he called it. Service instead of burning. He said the hunt doesn't want bearers dead, not really. It wants them *kept*. He said I'd keep my self. My name. My memories. Everything I am, in exchange for everything you are." Wren's hands had closed into fists without their permission; they opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way they'd been taught to still a shaking bowstring. "And I want you to know exactly what happened in that cell, because you deserve the exact truth and nothing shaped prettier than it."

"Which is?"

"For a moment," Wren said, "I considered it."

The word landed between them like a stone dropped into a well. Wren watched it fall and fall and never heard it hit bottom.

"Not long," they pressed on, because stopping now would be the true betrayal, the coward's mercy of leaving things vague. "Not even a minute. But it was a real minute, Quentin. It was a whole minute in which I sat there and did arithmetic with your life. Weighed what I am to you against what I am to myself. Counted the years we've known each other against the years I might have left if I said no." Their throat tightened. The choir caught their next breath and returned it trembling. "I looked at the collar and I thought: *he looks calm. He looks whole. Maybe it isn't so bad.* That's what survival does — it makes its own face look reasonable. And then I thought about you in the valley, at the market, trading a stranger's wedding day for bread, and how you always gave the traveler back more than you took, some little kindness-memory you'd saved from someone else, so they wouldn't ride out feeling poorer. And I understood that whatever I kept by saying yes, it wouldn't be a self anymore. It would be a cage with my name painted on it."

Quentin hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked enough. Dust settled on their shoulders like the first snow of a season that hadn't started yet.

"So I refused him," Wren said. "I told him no. And I got out, and I found you, and that part is true and whole and mine. But I'm not telling you this to be forgiven for almost. I'm telling it because there's a second thing, and the second thing is worse, and you have to hear it from me before you hear it from anyone else."

"Say it."

Wren pulled back their sleeves.

The oath-marks stood out dark against their forearms in the wine-light — the winding script every warden of Hallowmere received at their swearing, inked in a dye made from border-stone and ash, coiling from wrist to elbow like vines of black iron. They had lived under that skin for eleven years. They had stopped seeing them the way you stop seeing your own nose. Now, in this light, with what they knew, they looked like what they were.

"When I took the warden's oath," Wren said, "the elder who inked me told me the marks were protection. A ward against the valley's forgetting, so a warden could hold their own name steady at the border where memory thinned like ice in spring. That's what I believed. That's what *you* believed. It's why you trusted me to walk the edge while you walked the market."

"Wren—"

"The marks aren't a ward." The words came out of Wren like splinters coming out of flesh — slow, tearing, necessary. "I found Hale's papers. After I refused him, I stole them — I'm still a thief's partner, apparently, even sworn. The warden's oath is crown-work, Quentin. It's old crown-work, older than the valley's charter, older than the elders' grandmothers. The script isn't a protection. It's a *resonance*. It's tuned — tuned like a bell is tuned — to the gift. To memory-craft. Every warden who ever swore at that border stone was a tuning fork planted in the wilds, and every time a bearer worked the craft within a league of us, our marks *sang*. Quietly. Below hearing. And the song carries. It carries to the capital, to the vault, to—"

Wren couldn't say it. Their mouth tried twice.

"To the crown," Quentin finished for them. Very softly. Very evenly.

"To the crown," Wren whispered.

The silence that followed was not empty. The choir filled it — with the ghost-pressure of ten centuries of sung masses, with the hum of the unstruck bell, with the sound of two people breathing in a ruined loft while a kingdom forgot itself below. Wren watched Quentin's face and saw the truth arrive there the way frost arrives on glass: not all at once, but in spreading, branching lines, beautiful and ruinous.

"Every theft I ever made," Quentin said. Each word placed down carefully, like a jar set on a shelf. "Every traveler. Every trade at the market. Eleven years of it. And you were standing at the border the whole time, wearing a signal fire under your skin, and you didn't know."

"I didn't know." Wren's voice cracked on the second word and they let it. "But not knowing isn't innocence. I was the key, Quentin. Do you understand? All those hunters who could never cross before — magic can't follow into the valley, the rules held for three hundred years, and then suddenly Hale walks through like the border was a bead curtain. Because it was. For him. Because I *led him to you*. My oath sang your name across the miles, and the hunt followed the song, and I opened the door with my own sworn body and called it duty."

Quentin unfolded slowly. Stood. Walked to the broken rose window, and the wine-colored light poured over them and turned them into something Wren couldn't read — a silhouette, a stranger, a figure in a stained-glass story about someone else's sin.

"How many times?" Quentin asked the glass.

"What?"

"How many times did I steal near you? Near the border? How many songs did I sing into your skin without either of us knowing?" Their voice stayed level, and the levelness was the worst thing Wren had ever heard. "Because I can count them, Wren. I remember every single one. The tinker with his mother's lullaby, forty paces from your watchpost. The pilgrim woman, the one with the drowned village in her pocket — you brought her water yourself. You *handed* her the cup. And I took from her an hour later, twenty paces inside the line, and your marks must have lit up like festival lanterns, and you felt nothing, and I suspected nothing, and the crown heard everything."

"Yes."

"And the elders." Quentin turned. In the colored light their eyes had gone the grey of the valley sky before snow. "The vote at the mill. *Surrender any stranger among you.* They gave me up in an afternoon, Wren. An afternoon! Thirty years of buying their bread, healing their headaches, carrying their griefs in jars so they wouldn't have to. One proclamation and I was goods to be delivered. And now you're telling me the reason the hunters came at all — the reason the vote even mattered — was you. Our oaths. Our *friendship*." The last word came out frayed. "Was any of it real? Or was I just — a moth, flying in circles around a lamp you didn't know you were?"

Wren rose too. Their legs ached; they had run most of the way here through streets that smelled of smoke and rotting bells, and now the ache seemed right, seemed earned, seemed like the least of what they owed.

"It was real," they said. "That's the cruelty of it, Quentin. It was all real. The mercy at the border — three times I caught you smuggling memories out and three times I let you walk, and none of that was the oath talking, because the oath wanted the opposite, the oath was built to *find* you and I kept choosing not to hand you over before I even knew there was a hand to choose. Whatever the crown designed me to be, I spent eleven years being something else instead. Badly. Blindly. But I chose it, every day, without knowing I was choosing." They took one step closer. The dust printed their knee-shapes in the floor where they'd knelt, two hollows like a grave already dug. "I'm not asking you to believe that absolves me. I'm asking you to believe it's true."

Quentin was quiet for a long time. Below them, through the broken floor of the loft, the nave stretched away in pillars like a petrified forest, and far off, a door boomed — hunters walking the aisles, or maybe just the building settling, remembering its own weight.

"The coronation fragments," Quentin said at last. "The ones that hid inside me when they confiscated my hoard. The ones with the thing that looks back." They wrapped their arms around themselves again, and Wren realized their hands were shaking. "They've been restless since we came to this city. Since I got close to the king. And now you're telling me your marks have been singing to them too — all these years, Wren. Even in the valley. Even when we sat at your fire and you told me about your mother and I told you about the first memory I ever stole, the taste-memory, the honey-bread — you were ringing like a bell the whole time, and I was pouring myself into the sound."

"I know."

"Do you?" Quentin's composure finally split, just at the seam, just enough. "Do you know what it means that the only person I ever trusted in a valley full of people who forget me every winter — do you know what it means that that person was the trap? I built my whole life around your constancy. Everyone else's faces slid off me like rain off slate, but you *remembered* me, Wren. You were the one fixed point. And it turns out the fixed point was a nail the crown hammered into the world, and I hung my entire self on it."

The words went in under Wren's ribs and turned there, slow, like a key in a lock that opened onto nothing.

"I would tear the marks off my arms with my teeth if it would undo one mile of the song," Wren said. "But it won't. So instead I'll tell you what I know, all of it, and then you can decide what I am to you — not what I was designed to be. What I *am*. That's the only thing I've ever actually owned, Quentin. The choosing. They wrote the oath. They tuned the marks. But every morning at that border, when I saw you coming down the road with your jars and your hungry eyes, I chose to wave you through. Nobody made me. Not the crown, not the elders, not the oath. Me. And I'll keep choosing it until they burn me for it, if that's what it comes to."

Quentin looked at them for a long moment. The wine-light moved on their face. Somewhere in the walls, the unstruck bell faded toward silence, and the choir's stored voices seemed to lean in, listening, the way the dead listen — patiently, without judgment, having heard every kind of confession a body can make.

Then Quentin's gaze drifted past Wren's shoulder, to the sleeve still rolled back, to the oath-marks coiled dark on their forearm.

And Quentin went rigid.

"Wren," they breathed. "Your arm."

Wren looked down.

The marks were moving.

Not much. Not the slow crawl of ink settling into skin. This was purposeful — the black script tightening, coiling, drawing itself into patterns Wren had never seen in eleven years of wearing it, the vines of iron rearranging themselves like letters being re-set by an invisible compositor's hand. And they were warm. Warm through the linen of the rolled sleeve, warm as a stone pulled from a fire, and beneath the warmth, faint but unmistakable, rising through the skin like a note through the wall of a violin—

The marks were singing.

Not below hearing anymore. Not a whisper meant for distant vaults. Here, in the heart of the cathedral, in the acoustic throat of a thousand years of song, the warden's oath had found its voice at last, and it was singing a name.

Wren knew the name. It was their own.

And beneath it, threaded through it, harmonized into it like a second voice joining a round — Quentin's.

Below them, far below, through floors of stone and vaults of salt and iron, something vast and patient and hungry turned its attention upward, following the sound.

It had found them.
