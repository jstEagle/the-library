## 15. A Kingdom with No Yesterday

The bells stopped on the third morning.

Wren noticed it the way one notices a missing tooth — not at first, but in the strange smoothness where something used to be. The capital had been a city of bells since before the crown was a crown; every tower rang the hours, the quarters, the warnings of fire and flood and royal death. Now the towers stood silent, their bronze throats wrapped in gray wool by men who could not say who had ordered it, only that it seemed important.

"They're wards," Quentin had said, back when they first arrived, staring up at the bell towers with the flat look Wren had learned to dread — the look of someone reading a room's bones. "Every bell is a memory-ward. They've been ringing protection over this city for six hundred years." A pause. "Someone's unwinding them."

Now the unwinding was done, and the forgetting began in earnest.

* * *

Wren saw the first wave from the window of the safehouse above the dyer's shop, in the tangle of streets below the cathedral hill. It was market day, or what passed for market day in a city gone quiet as a held breath. The square was full enough — fishwives, coal-sellers, a knife-grinder with his wheel — but the noise was wrong. Thin. Like a choir singing from memory of music rather than from the music itself.

Then a woman in the square dropped her basket.

Not stumbled — dropped, deliberately, letting the eggs break because she had forgotten why she was carrying them. She stood in the ruin of them, turning slowly, her mouth working around a name she couldn't find. *Marta? Maren?* Wren read the shape of it on her lips. A daughter's name, maybe. Or a mother's. The woman looked down at the eggs as if they'd been placed there by strangers, as if the whole errand were a riddle someone else had written.

Around her, others faltered. A man mid-haggle went blank between one word and the next, his argument dying in his throat like a snuffed candle. Two children stopped their game and stared at each other with the polite bewilderment of people meeting at a well in a foreign country. An old debt-collector opened his ledger and wept without sound, because the numbers meant nothing to him — not the sums, not the names, not the grievance that had kept him warm for forty years.

The wave passed. The woman gathered what eggs hadn't broken, though Wren watched her hesitate over even that, unsure whether they were hers to take. The knife-grinder resumed his wheel. The city stitched itself closed around the holes.

But the holes stayed.

"Did you see?" Quentin's voice came from the stairwell, low and rough. They'd taken to sleeping in their boots since the vault, since the archive, since understanding the sheer tonnage of stolen past stacked beneath the cathedral like grain in a famine-year storehouse. They moved differently now. Quieter. As if afraid of dislodging something.

"I saw."

"It'll be worse tomorrow." Quentin came to stand beside Wren at the window, close enough that their shoulders touched. They didn't lean in. Since chapter thirteen — since the confession about the oath-marks, about the warden oath being a divining rod the crown had planted in the valley's border all along — closeness between them had become a thing measured out in thimbles. "It comes off the bells first. Then the parish records. Then—" They gestured at the square, at the woman with her ruined eggs. "That. The soft tissue of a life. Names. Debts. Grudges. The things people carry without knowing they carry them."

"You sound like you've seen this before."

"I've *done* this before." Quentin's laugh had no floor under it. "Small scale. A traveler crosses the valley, I take a wedding, a funeral, a first kiss. They walk on lighter and don't know what's missing. That's the mercy of theft, Wren — you can't mourn what you can't remember losing." Their eyes tracked the woman until she vanished into an alley. "But this isn't theft. This is *feeding*. And it doesn't care about mercy."

* * *

They went out at dusk, because Quentin insisted on seeing it up close, and because Wren had stopped pretending they could stop Quentin from anything.

The city after dark was worse than the city by day. Lamplighters worked their rounds in perfect silence, and Wren realized halfway down Candlemaker's Row that they weren't silent by discipline — they'd forgotten the calls, the little songs and shouts that marked a lamplighter's trade. One of them stood beneath a lit lamp with his pole raised and a face like a man trying to recall a dream, and when Wren passed him he asked, politely, desperately: "Is this my street? I know it's my street. I just can't find the reason I know."

"The reason left," Quentin said gently, and the man nodded as if that explained everything, which in Hallowmere's terms it did.

They cut through the Tanner's Court, where the guildhall kept its rolls of debts and dues, and found the doors open and the interior dark and a clerk sitting among scattered papers like a man shipwrecked on his own ledgers. He wasn't crying. He was laughing, very softly, in the way of the truly broken.

"Forgiven," he said when he saw them. "All of it. Every debt in the city — forgiven overnight. Isn't that a blessing? Isn't that grace?" He held up a page covered in figures. "I don't know who owes whom anymore. Nobody does. The baker forgave the miller, the miller forgave the lord, the lord forgave—" He laughed again. "There's no lord. There's no *anything*. We woke up rich and empty."

Outside again, Wren said nothing for three streets. When they finally spoke, their voice came out flatter than they intended. "My order used to say the gift was a plague. Memory-craft. They said bearers hollowed people out for sport."

"And now you've seen the sport done properly," Quentin said. "By a crown. With a bureaucracy."

"Yes." Wren stopped at the mouth of an alley where the cathedral rose against the bruised sky, its spires black, its bells wool-wrapped and dead. "And here's what I keep circling. Hale told me the hunt's purpose was to find a bearer who could unbind the king. He told me that in a cell, wearing a collar, having sold everything he was for the privilege of serving. But he never said *why the king needed unbinding*. He talked about it like weather. Like a fact of the world." Wren turned to face Quentin fully. In the alley-dark, their faces were nearly touching. "You touched his hand. You saw it. Tell me what unbinding means. Not the rite. The cost."

Quentin was quiet long enough that a cat crossed the alley behind them, unhurried, sovereign.

"You already know," they said at last. "You've known since the vault. You just want me to say it so it becomes real in your mouth instead of mine."

"Say it anyway."

"A parasite doesn't die of starvation while there's food." Quentin's voice dropped to almost nothing. "It dies of gluttony. You make it eat until it tears. The crown has been sipping this kingdom for centuries — a memory here, a grievance there, the bells unwound slow so no one noticed the silence growing. It's patient. It's *full*. To kill it, you don't starve it. You give it more than it can hold. All at once. A feast big enough to split it open."

"How big?"

Quentin looked up at the cathedral. At the choir loft, invisible in the dark, where the acoustics held every voice that ever sang there, waiting like a drawn bow.

"Everything," they said. "Every stolen memory in the vault. Centuries of bearers' hoards. And whatever bearer pours it in has to pour through themselves — the rite runs the river through the vessel. So add the vessel's own past to the bill." A breath. "Mine. All of it. The valley. The travelers. The jars. My childhood, my name, my—" They stopped. Started again, steadier. "Me."

The words hung in the alley like smoke.

"No," Wren said. It came out before thought, before strategy, before any of the cold arithmetic they'd been taught in the wardens' houses. Just *no*, naked and useless.

"That's not an answer, Wren."

"It's the only answer I have."

"Then keep it." Quentin stepped back, and the space between them widened like a border crossing. "But understand what you're looking at. Walk with me through it, since you want it real." They began to count on their fingers, and their hands — those quick, light-fingered thief's hands — trembled as they did it. "One: the parasite feeds wider every day. The bells are dead. The debts are gone. By week's end it'll be taking faces. Mothers won't know children. Soldiers won't know why they hold swords, and some of them will still swing them. Two: there is exactly one person in this kingdom who can do the rite, because the rite requires a living bearer with a hoard inside them, and the coronation fragments chose me before I ever knew what they were. Three: the alternative is a kingdom with no yesterday, and then no today, and then nothing at all — a realm of people standing in squares holding baskets they can't explain." They lowered their hand. "So. Grim clarity, warden. That's what you asked for. There it is."

Wren's oath-marks ached. They always ached now, especially near Quentin — the crown's old design humming in their skin, the hunt's key turned inward on itself. They thought of the wardens' creed, recited at every border dawn: *What is forgotten cannot harm us.* They had believed it was wisdom. It was a menu.

"There has to be another way," they said, hating how small it sounded.

"There isn't. I've looked. I've spent every night since the vault looking, and the arithmetic doesn't move." Quentin's voice cracked, just once, and they mastered it. "The only question left is what happens after. Whether anyone remembers there was ever a question."

* * *

They climbed to the safehouse as the last light died, and from the roof they could see the whole city laid out below — ten thousand hearths, ten thousand lives thinning at the edges like cloth rubbed too long.

Another wave came at moonrise.

This one was deeper. Wren felt it pass through their own body like a cold current — not taking anything, the oath-marks apparently proof against the crown's appetite, or perhaps simply beneath its interest — but *near* it, the way a swimmer feels a shark turn in the water below. Across the rooftops, dogs began to howl, all of them at once, animals remembering what their masters couldn't. Somewhere a church congregation spilled into the street because they had risen for a hymn and found the words gone. Somewhere a soldier on the cathedral steps sat down abruptly on the stone, his spear clattering, and stared at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger, and then — this was the worst part — picked the spear back up, because his body remembered its duty even when his mind had lost the war that gave the duty meaning.

Quentin watched it all with the expression of a merchant auditing a ledger they already knew would ruin them.

"It's accelerating," they murmured. "It knows something's coming. It's gorging while it can." A pause. "Or it's making room."

"For what?"

"For me. For the fragments it hid in me. It put them there, Wren — did you understand that? In the coronation memory, that thing looked back at me through a dead man's eyes because it wanted me to *carry* it. A seed in a thief's pocket. It's been ripening me like fruit." Quentin turned, and in the moonlight their face was terrible and calm. "Which means when I walk into that choir, it will be expecting a meal. Good. Let it expect. Let it open wide."

Wren reached out — hesitated — and took Quentin's hand. The first deliberate touch since the confession. Quentin's fingers were cold and did not pull away.

"If you do this," Wren said, "I want something understood between us. Not negotiated. Understood." Their voice shook and they let it. "Whatever you pour out — the valley, the travelers, the childhood, all of it — I will remember it for you. Every jar. Every theft. Every mercy. I was there for enough of it, and I'll wring the rest out of the archives if I have to. You will not vanish into this clean. I will be the record. I will be the *witness*. That's the only part of the rite I'm permitted to write, and I'm telling you now so you can't refuse it later."

Quentin stared at them. Something moved behind their eyes — the old hunger, the loneliness of the valley, the child who had learned that memories could be held in jars because hands always let go.

"That's not grim clarity," they said finally, and their voice was wrecked and grateful. "That's something else."

Below them, across the sleeping, thinning city, the cathedral's dead bells hung silent in their towers — and then, faintly, impossibly, one of them *ticked*. A single metallic click, deep in the bronze, like a great jaw testing its hinge.

Quentin's hand tightened around Wren's.

"It knows we've decided," they whispered. "Tomorrow night, we feed it."
