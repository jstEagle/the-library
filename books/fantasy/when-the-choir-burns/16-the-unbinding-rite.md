## 16. The Unbinding Rite

The choir did not want me there.

I felt it the moment I climbed through the broken grille behind the reliquary — a pressure like walking into deep water, the air itself thick with everything it had swallowed. Every voice that had ever sung beneath that vaulted stone still hung in it, layered and layered, centuries of hymns pressed flat as flowers in a book. When I breathed, I breathed other people's songs. When I moved, my footsteps echoed wrong — doubled, tripled, answered by voices I hadn't made.

Wren would have hated it. Wren hated any place where silence couldn't hold. But Wren was two floors down, standing watch at the undercroft stair with Hale's stolen keys in their fist, and I was alone with the acoustics and the dead and the one jar I had left.

I set it on the choir floor between my knees. The coronation fragments swam inside the glass like minnows in dark water, gold-flecked, restless. They had been hiding in me since the wagons, since the salt-lined iron that leeched every other memory out of my hoard and left these clinging to my ribs like burrs. They didn't want to be taken. They wanted to be *seen*.

"All right," I whispered, and the choir took the word and passed it around the vaulting like a secret being shared hand to hand. "All right. Show me."

I pried the wax seal loose. The smell came first — incense and hot metal and something underneath both, sweetish, organic, the smell of a wound kept clean too long. Then the memory opened, and I fell into it the way you fall into any stolen past: all at once, without permission.

* * *

Candles. Thousands of them, guttering in a draft, and the cathedral not ruined but whole, its windows blazing with saints whose faces were all the same face. A woman stood where I stood — in the choir, before the altar steps — and she was old in the way bearers get old, which is to say she looked hollowed and polished at once, like driftwood. Her hands were stained with wax. Her name, when someone shouted it from the nave, was Maren.

She was singing.

Not a hymn. Not anything with words I knew. It was a rite-song, and even secondhand, even filtered through her dying throat, it did something to the air that made my teeth ache and my stolen memories stir in their jars like animals smelling a storm. She sang and the candles bent toward her. She sang and the king — young then, or younger, his face still his own — rose from his throne at the nave's end and walked toward the choir with the slow, dragging gait of a man wading upstream against himself.

And above him, on his head, the crown *leaned*.

I had seen it lean before, in the fragments that hid inside me. I had felt it notice me through its own stolen coronation. But this was different. This was the crown before it had finished eating him — this was the moment it should have been stopped — and Maren sang because she meant to stop it, and everyone in that cathedral knew it, and no one moved to help her.

The rite poured out of her in the memory the way water pours from a broken jug. I caught what I could of it, the way I'd catch anything from a traveler's past: not by studying but by swallowing. The structure of it settled into me whole. You do not pour memories into the parasite one at a time. That is how it feeds. That is how it has always fed — sip by sip, bearer by bearer, century by century, the vault below full to bursting with hoards it drinks at its leisure. No. To kill it you must make it gorge. Every stolen memory at once. Everything you have ever taken, given in a single breath, a flood instead of a trickle, so much past pouring into that bottomless hunger that the hunger tears along its own seams.

Maren sang the flooding song. The crown turned toward her like a head turning.

And here is what the memory showed me that no history would ever write down: for one moment — one held, trembling moment — it worked. The parasite gorged. I watched it swell in the memory the way you can see wind move through wheat, a shudder passing through the king's shoulders, up his neck, into the circlet of black metal, which *cracked* — a hairline, a whisper of fracture, a sound like ice on a pond in early spring.

Then Maren ran out of memories.

That was the flaw. That was the whole terrible architecture of it. The rite requires everything, and she had given everything she had ever stolen, and it wasn't enough — because the crown had been feeding for three hundred years, and three hundred years of sips outweighed one woman's hoard, however vast. The crack sealed itself. The hunger turned around and looked at her, and understood her, and *took*.

The memory ends mid-scream. Of course it does. It ends mid-scream because that scream was the last thing she ever owned, and the crown drank it out of her mouth while her mouth was still making it, and whatever fragment of her escaped that day — whoever carried this dying ember of her final hour out of the cathedral and hid it in a jar — carried it because it was all that was left to carry.

I came back to myself on the choir floor with my cheek against cold stone and the empty jar rolling gently away from my hand, and I lay there shaking, and the choir sang my shaking back to me in a hundred borrowed voices.

* * *

I don't know how long I lay there. Long enough for the light through the broken windows to change color, gold going to ash. Long enough for my hands to stop trembling and start doing something worse, which was planning.

Because here was the thing about Maren's failure, the thing I turned over and over like a stone with something underneath it: she had failed not because the rite was wrong but because she was small. One bearer. One hoard. Three hundred years of theft weighed against one lifetime's worth of taking.

But I had seen the vault.

I had stood in the Archive of Stolen Things with Wren's hand locked around my wrist and Hale's keys burning in my pocket, and I had seen the shelves go back farther than lamplight could follow — jars upon jars upon jars, centuries of bearers' hoards, every stolen wedding and funeral and first kiss and last breath the crown's hunters had ever confiscated. The raw material. The feast laid out and waiting. The parasite drank from that vault slowly, at its leisure, the way a man drinks from a river — but if someone were to open every jar at once, if someone were to sing the flooding song *inside* the archive itself, or better, drag the archive's whole drowned weight into the choir and pour—

No. I sat up. My head spun. Think plainly, Quentin. You are a thief, not a general. What do you actually have?

I had the rite, learned whole from a screaming woman's last hour. I had the choir, whose acoustics could carry a single voice into every corner of the cathedral and, if the old songs were true, into the stones themselves. I had Wren downstairs with the keys, and somewhere above me a court full of nobles who thought the gift was being hunted to extinction, and a king who was a door with something wearing the hinges.

And I had myself. Which was the problem. Because the rite required *everything*, and everything did not mean only the thefts.

I had known that from the beginning, in the way you know a thing you refuse to look at directly. Maren gave everything she had ever taken — and the memory showed me, in its last flickering frames, that it took more than theft. The rite doesn't distinguish. Memory is memory. Pour out your stolen pasts and your own past goes with them, pulled along by the same current, because you cannot flood a thing with part of a river. The valley. The market stalls. The first traveler I ever robbed, a peddler with flour on his sleeves. My mother's hands. Whatever childhood I'd had before Hallowmere taught me that forgetting was currency. All of it, out, all at once, into a hunger that would swallow it and keep swallowing until either it burst or I stood empty on the choir floor with nothing left of Quentin Vane but a body and a name someone else would have to give meaning to.

Maren screamed because the crown took her last memory mid-scream. But maybe — maybe — some part of the scream was grief. Maybe she knew, in that final second, exactly what she was losing, and it wasn't just her life.

I got to my feet. My legs held. The choir hummed around me, patient, ancient, full of the dead's best work.

"Wren will hate this," I said aloud, and the acoustics took it apart and gave it back to me in pieces, *hate this, hate this, hate this,* echoing down toward the undercroft stair like a summons.

* * *

They came up when I called — of course they came up; they'd have come up if I'd only breathed their name into the right stone — and found me standing at the center of the choir with the empty jar in my hand and the rite already living in my chest like a second heartbeat.

"You're pale," Wren said. Their eyes went from me to the jar to the broken grille behind the reliquary, reading the room the way wardens read borders. "What did you find?"

"A woman named Maren." I told it plainly, because Wren deserved plainness after everything the truth had cost us both. "A bearer. She tried this before — years ago, when the king still had a face of his own. There's a rite. A song. You pour every stolen memory into the crown at once and it gorges, and if you feed it enough, it tears." I turned the empty jar slowly in my fingers. "She cracked it. For one heartbeat, it cracked."

"And then?" Wren asked, though their voice said they already knew. Wardens know how stories end. It's most of the training.

"Then she ran out. And it took her. Mid-scream." I met their eyes. "The rite needs everything, Wren. Not just the hoard. Everything. And my hoard is gone — the hunt took it — except what hides in me, and what's left in the vault below. So the vault has to be part of it. Every jar. Centuries of it. Poured through me, through the choir, into that thing, all at once."

Silence. The choir held it, magnified it, gave it back to us in a hundred ghost-voices until the silence sounded almost like assent.

"That's suicide," Wren said finally. Flat. Warden-flat, the voice they used for reports.

"It's arithmetic." I smiled, and felt how thin it was. "Three hundred years of stolen pasts against one parasite. Maren lost because she brought one life's worth. We can bring an ocean."

"Through *you*." Wren stepped closer, and their oath-marks caught the gray window-light, faintly luminous, the crown's design written on their skin in ink they'd never chosen. "Every memory in that vault passes through you. You'll be — Quentin, you won't survive being a channel for that much. No one survives—"

"Maren survived long enough to crack it." My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only kind of steady I've ever had. "And I'm not Maren. I've been stealing since I was a child. I know how memories move — how to pour without spilling, how to give without gripping. It's the only craft I have, Wren. Let me spend it on the one thing worth buying."

They stared at me. And I watched the argument die in them — not because they agreed, but because they knew me, knew the undertow of hunger that had always run beneath my contentment, knew that I had spent my whole life taking other people's pasts because I couldn't bear the smallness of my own, and that this — this enormous, annihilating giving — was the same hunger finally pointed in the right direction.

"You'll be empty," they said quietly. "Whatever's left afterward. If anything's left."

"Then fill me back up." I reached out and took their hand, the way I'd taken it on the border, the way I'd taken it in the wagon, the way I seemed to keep doing without ever deciding to. "You remember enough for both of us. You always have."

Wren's grip tightened. For a moment neither of us spoke, and the choir sang our silence for us, and far above, through the vaulting, I could feel the crown stirring in its tower room — turning over in its sleep, the way a great beast turns when it dreams of prey.

It knew I was here. It had known since the coronation fragment first looked back at me through the glass. And it was waiting, patient as hunger, for me to come to it.

Good, I thought. Let it wait. Let it open its mouth.

Tomorrow — or tonight, or whenever Wren finished rigging the vault doors and Hale's last collar bought us the minutes we needed — I would stand in this choir before king and court and parasite, and I would sing Maren's song, and I would give it everything.

Every stolen wedding. Every smuggled border. Every jar of firefly-bright pasts I'd ever hoarded against the loneliness.

And underneath all of it, at the very bottom of the flood, where even the crown wouldn't taste it coming:

myself.

Below us, faint through the stone, a bell began to toll — not the cathedral's bells, but something deeper, older, a sound like a vault door opening.

Hale had started without us.
