## 17. Hale's Last Collar

They kept Wren in a cell that smelled of salt and old iron, and for three days the only visitor was the sound of the bells.

The capital rang them at odd hours — dawn, yes, but also at moments that had no meaning Wren could find, as though the city itself were trying to remember something and failing. Each peal passed through the stone like a hand passing over a face, searching for features. Wren learned to count the intervals between rings. Learned that when the bells went silent too long, the silence felt less like peace and more like a held breath.

On the fourth day, they came for Quentin.

Wren heard it through the wall: boots, many of them, and the particular cadence of an escort that is also a procession. A voice — Quentin's voice, thin through two feet of granite — saying something Wren could not make out, and then nothing. The silence afterward had a texture to it, dense as wet wool. Wren pressed their palm flat against the cold stone and left it there until their fingers went numb, as if touch could travel where sound could not.

*They're taking them to the king,* Wren thought. *Or to the thing wearing him.*

And there was nothing — nothing — that a warden of Hallowmere could do about it, because the oath-marks on their forearms had been the hunt's compass all along, and every step they took toward rescue was a step the crown had already mapped.

That was when Inquisitor Hale came to the cell door.

He did not come with torchlight or ceremony. He came alone, in the dead hours past midnight, carrying a single candle whose flame bent toward him as though leaning in to listen. Up close, in that small light, Wren saw what the collar had made of him.

Hollowed was the word Quentin would have used, and Quentin would have been right, but hollowed was not empty. An empty man has no edges. Hale had edges — sharp ones, worn ones, the edges of a shape that had once been full and now held only its own outline. His skin was the gray of paper left too long in rain. His eyes were still brown, still human, but they moved the way a man's eyes move in a house he no longer recognizes: measuring doorways, counting exits, remembering without being able to feel why remembering mattered.

"You're awake," he said. Not a question. "Bearers sleep badly near salt. I remember that." He paused, and the pause had a crack in it. "I remember that."

"Your collar's slipping," Wren said.

"My collar is *dying.*" He said it the way a man reports weather. "There's a difference. Come to the door. I want you to see something."

Wren did not move. "If you think I'll walk anywhere you point—"

"I don't think anything of the kind." He set the candle on the floor between them, and its light climbed the wall and showed Wren the scratches there — hundreds of them, thousands, tally marks in a hand that had changed over years, growing shakier, growing simpler, until the last marks were barely more than stabs. "I counted my memories once," Hale said, following Wren's gaze. "When I still knew what counting was for. One mark for every day I could name my mother's face." He looked at his own hands. "I stopped at four hundred and eleven. Not because I ran out of days. Because I ran out of mother."

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere above them, a bell began to ring — one low note, then another, the city dreaming aloud.

"Why are you here?" Wren asked.

"Because in three days, maybe four, there will be nothing left in this body that can choose anything. And I have decided—" he said the word carefully, tasting it like a food from a country he'd been exiled from, "*decided* — that whatever I do with what remains, I will do it on purpose."

* * *

He did not open the cell. Instead he sat down across from it, on the corridor floor, his back against the opposite wall, and he talked.

Wren understood later that this was the bargain's true shape: not freedom offered, but truth given, and truth is a currency that costs the giver more than the receiver. Hale talked for hours, and the candle burned down, and he fed it another from his sleeve like a man feeding a fire he both needed and feared.

"I was born in Marrowfen," he said. "Fishing town. You won't have heard of it; half the maps have forgotten it already, and the other half never knew. My grandmother was a bearer. Small gift — she could hold a grief for you, carry it a while so you could sleep. The village called her the Quiet Woman. They brought her their dead children's names and she kept them safe until the mourning could bear them again." His voice did not waver. That was the worst of it. Whatever the collar had left him, it had left him perfectly steady. "I inherited it late. Thirteen. I stole my first memory by accident — my brother's, a summer afternoon, gone out of him like water through a net. He never missed it. That's the cruelty of the gift, isn't it? Only the thief knows what was taken."

"Yes," Wren said quietly.

"And then the hunt came to Marrowfen, and I ran, and they caught me at the ford, and they gave me a choice." He touched the collar — a band of black metal at his throat, worked with sigils that seemed to shift when looked at directly, like fish under ice. "Serve, and keep a self. Refuse, and burn. I was fifteen. I chose the way fifteen chooses." He smiled, and the smile was the most terrible thing in the cell, because it was genuine. "I told myself I'd work against them from inside. Every hollowed bearer tells themselves that. It's practically part of the ritual."

"What does the collar take?" Wren asked. "Exactly."

"Everything, slowly. But it takes in order. First it eats the future — you stop being able to imagine tomorrow. Then the recent past. Then it works backward, decade by decade, like frost climbing a window." He turned the candle so its light caught the sigils. "It leaves the skills. I can still ride, still fight, still read a room for lies. It leaves duty — oh, it loves duty, duty is the cheapest thing to keep a man moving. What it takes first is the reason you'd ever have wanted anything else."

"And yet here you are. Wanting something else."

"Here I am." He let that sit. "Do you know what the crown is? Truly?"

"I know it ate the king."

"The king was the *first meal*, not the purpose." Hale's eyes came up, and for a moment something moved behind them — fast, frightened, human, like a bird crossing a lit window. "The crown is a hunger that learned to wear institutions. It has been feeding for centuries. Every bearer the hunt burns, every hoard the vaults confiscate — do you think those memories are destroyed?" He shook his head. "They're *stored*. The vault beneath the cathedral holds more stolen past than any kingdom should be able to hold. Centuries of it. Whole lives, jarred and shelved like preserves. The crown sips from it. Keeps itself fed. Keeps the king smooth and vacant and governable, and keeps the realm forgetting — debts, grievances, the faces of the dead — because a kingdom with no yesterday cannot rebel against anything."

Wren's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the cell. "Quentin saw the vault."

"Your thief saw a corridor of it. There are nine levels below what they walked." Hale rose, joints cracking like green wood. "And in three days, the court will gather in the burning choir to witness the unbinding trial — which is not a trial. It is a harvest. They mean to pour your friend into the crown the way you'd pour wine into a cask, and the choir's acoustics will carry every note of it into the stone, and the stone will feed, and the crown will grow one ring thicker around the king's throat."

"Then we stop it."

"*We.*" Hale repeated the word as though translating it from a dead language. "You misunderstand your position, warden. You are in a cell. Your oath-marks sing your location to every hunter in this city each time your heart beats faster than walking pace. Your friend is in the hands of the Inquisition. And I am—" he gestured at himself, the gray skin, the dying collar, the outline of a man, "—this."

"Then why tell me any of it?"

"Because," Hale said, and stopped. And Wren watched — actually watched — a man reach into the last drawer of himself and find it not quite empty. "Because four hundred and eleven marks ago, I had a mother's face, and a brother who lost a summer, and a grandmother who carried griefs for strangers because someone had to. And the collar has eaten everything except the fact that she existed, and the fact that she would be ashamed of me, and I have decided that shame is enough to act on. It's more than most of us get."

He picked up the candle.

"In two nights, the vault rotates its wardens. The rotation is keyed to my seal — I built the schedule myself, years ago, back when I still remembered why schedules matter. I can open the lower doors. I can give you the corridor of jars — yours, theirs, all of them. What you do after that is beyond my imagining, because I no longer have an imagination. But I can put you in front of the problem." He looked at Wren with those brown, house-hunting eyes. "Understand what it costs. When I break my seal, the collar will know. It will begin the final eating — not decades, not months. Minutes. I will stand in that corridor and come apart while you run. Do you understand?"

Wren stood. Their legs were stiff from the cell floor, and the oath-marks on their forearms ached dully, the way they always ached before betrayal or blood.

"Hale," they said. "Why?"

He considered the question with the seriousness of a scholar consulting a text that has mostly burned.

"Because someone carried my grandmother's griefs for her," he said finally. "Because the Quiet Woman is the last memory I have that isn't the crown's, and I would rather die holding it than live letting go of it." He opened the cell door with a key that appeared from nowhere and everywhere. "Come along, warden. We have two nights, and I intend to spend mine loudly."

* * *

The corridors beneath the cathedral were older than the cathedral above them, and wrong in ways Wren's body understood before their mind did. The stones sweated salt. The air tasted of other people's childhoods — cut grass, river mud, bread, a specific rain on a specific roof — layered so thickly that breathing felt like trespassing. Lanterns hung at intervals, and their flames burned without flickering, as though even fire here had been taught to forget how to dance.

Hale walked ahead, and the dark seemed to make room for him reluctantly.

"The wardens change at the third bell after midnight," he murmured. "There is a quarter hour when the lower doors answer only to the Inquisitor's seal. That was deliberate, in case one of us ever needed to hide evidence quickly. I designed a system for hiding crimes, and I am going to use it to commit one." He almost laughed. Almost. "There's a joke in that. I can tell there's a joke in that. I can't find it anymore."

They descended. Stair after stair, and with each level the air grew heavier with memory until Wren's eyes watered and their own past began to slip sideways — they thought of Hallowmere's border pines and saw instead a traveler's memory of pines, borrowed, secondhand, and could not say which recollection was truly theirs. The vault did not distinguish. The vault only kept.

At the seventh landing, Hale stopped before a door of black iron banded with silver, and pressed his palm to it, and spoke a word that Wren's mind refused to retain — heard it, knew it had been spoken, and lost it between ear and memory like water through a net. Only the thief knows what was taken, he had said. Now Wren understood that the vault took from everyone.

The seals flared white. The door groaned open on shelves.

Nine levels, Hale had said. This was the eighth, and it stretched away farther than the cathedral above could possibly contain — aisle after aisle of jars, thousands upon thousands, each one glowing faintly with the color of the life inside: wedding-gold, grief-gray, childhood-green, the deep bruised violet of things remembered at the end. Dust lay undisturbed on some. Others gleamed, recently fed-upon, their light dimmed to embers. And among them — third shelf, fourth row, exactly where the confiscation ledger said they would be — Wren saw the firefly-jars of Hallowmere. Quentin's hoard. The stolen weddings, the first snows, the daughter's dance. Shelved between a dead woman's lullabies and a soldier's whole war.

"There," Wren breathed.

"There," Hale agreed. "Take what serves you. Leave what doesn't — weight matters, and time matters more." He turned to face the corridor behind them, drawing himself up, and Wren saw that he had positioned himself precisely between the open vault and the stair. "The collar knows. Can you feel it? Like a hook setting." He rolled his shoulders. "Go. Gather. I will hold the door."

"Hale—"

"Warden." His voice cracked down the middle, and through the crack came something young — fifteen, at a ford, choosing. "I have perhaps ten minutes of being a man who can decide things. Do not waste them on gratitude. Gratitude is a memory, and in ten minutes I won't have anywhere to put it."

So Wren gathered. Jars into the satchel, jars into their coat, the coronation fragments — which were not in any jar, which lived in Quentin, hidden — leaving a hole in the shelf shaped like dread. Their hands shook. The oath-marks burned. Somewhere far above, the bells began to ring off-schedule, fast and wrong, and boots began to echo down stairs that had been empty minutes ago.

Hale met them at the threshold, and he was already grayer, already thinner at the edges, his outline softening like chalk in rain. But his eyes found Wren's and held them, brown and human and *his.*

"The choir," he said. "The acoustics hold every voice that ever sang there. If your thief means to do what I think they mean to do, the choir is the only place the rite can be sung loud enough to choke it." He pressed something into Wren's hand — a key, black iron, warm as a living thing. "The sacristy door. It opens from the inside. After that, I am done deciding, and you are not."

Boots on the stairs now, close. Torchlight climbing the walls.

"Hale," Wren said, and this time did not finish it, because there was no sentence large enough.

He smiled the terrible genuine smile. "Tell the Quiet Woman's story," he said. "Someone should remember her, and soon it won't be me."

Then he turned to face the stairwell, drew the sword he no longer remembered learning to use, and planted himself in the narrow way like a word set in stone — and Wren ran, jars clinking, heart hammering, up through the sweating dark toward the burning choir, while behind them the sounds of the last free minutes of Inquisitor Hale rang off the salt-stoned walls: steel, shouting, and once — clear above it all, unmistakable, impossible —

laughter.
