## 18. The Burning Choir

The choir had been waiting for a voice.

Quentin felt it the moment they stepped through the broken arch — the sense of standing inside an instrument too large to be played by anything with lungs. The vaulted stone rose above them in ribs of pale granite, and every rib was carved with mouths: singing faces, hundreds of them, their lips open on notes no one had given them in two hundred years. The air itself was thick with what they held. When Wren's boots scuffed the mosaic floor, the sound came back changed — softened, multiplied, as if a congregation long dead had shifted in their seats to make room.

"They're all still here," Quentin whispered. "Every voice that ever sang in this room."

The acoustics took the whisper and carried it up into the vaulting, and the vaulting gave it back wearing harmonies Quentin had never made. Somewhere above, dust sifted down through shafts of light like slow gold rain.

Wren set the jars down at the center of the choir, where the mosaic showed a sun with its rays worn to nubs by centuries of kneeling feet. Twelve jars. Quentin counted them twice, though counting was a habit of hands that had spent years tallying what could not be written down. The coronation fragments were not among them — those had never left Quentin's body since the night the dying traveler's jar shattered, since the thing inside the memory had looked back at them through its own recollection and *known*. They sat behind Quentin's sternum now like swallowed coals, warm, patient, hungry in the way that only borrowed things can be.

"Say it again," Wren said. "The rite. All of it."

"You've heard it four times."

"I want to hear it while I can still look at you." Wren's voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence and came out flat. "Humor me."

So Quentin said it again, standing in the light with dust settling on their shoulders like a benediction they had not asked for and could not refuse:

*A bearer stands in the place of voices. A bearer opens every door they have ever closed. What was taken is given; what was given is poured; and the hunger, meeting a feast too great to swallow, drowns in its own gorging.*

"And after," Wren said. "What comes after. Say that part."

"The rite doesn't say." Quentin made themselves smile. "The last bearer who tried didn't finish writing it down."

"No," Wren said. "She ended mid-scream. You told me."

"I told you she ended mid-*song*," Quentin said. "It's nearly the same thing, but it sounds better."

That got the almost-laugh he wanted from them — small, cracked, real — and then the bells began.

* * *

They felt it first in their teeth. Every bell tower in the city was a memory-ward; they had learned that in their first stolen hour in the capital, crouched in a doorway while a peal rolled over the rooftops and scraped something essential off everyone who heard it, leaving the passersby blinking and mild as milk. But this was not a warding peal. This was the whole city ringing at once, every tower, every chapel, every cracked bell in every forgotten campanile, and the sound did not scrape — it *summoned*.

"He knows," Wren said. "He felt you come into the choir."

*It* knows, Quentin thought, but did not say it. There was no time anymore for the distinction between the king and the thing that wore him, and besides — the distinction was about to become the only thing that mattered.

The doors at the far end of the nave boomed. Not opened — *boomed*, as if struck from outside by something that did not need handles. Torchlight spilled down the central aisle, long and wavering, and into it came the court.

They came in procession, because everything the crown did was procession: the lords in their gilded collars, the guards in salt-grey steel, the priests with censers swinging smoke that smelled of nothing at all — incense burned so often in this city that even its smell had been eaten. And at the head of them, walking with the slow ceremonial gait of a man being moved rather than moving, came the king.

Up close, in the torchlight, he was worse than the glimpse Quentin had stolen from a rooftop. His face was smooth the way a riverbed is smooth — worn, not rested. His eyes were open and aimed forward and contained nothing that looked back. On his brow the crown sat low, and it was not gold, whatever it pretended to be in the distance; up close it was the color of old teeth, of candle-fat, of the inside of a jar, and it seemed to *lean*, the way a leech leans when it feels warmth beneath it.

Behind the king walked six hunters in white, and behind them, carried on a bier like a relic, wrapped in chains of braided salt-iron, was Quentin's hoard — the confiscated jars, all the ones Hale had not freed, glowing faintly through the wicker of their basket-cages like fireflies that had learned despair.

And behind the jars, limping, bloodied, one arm hanging wrong, came Inquisitor Hale.

Alive. He caught Quentin's eye across the length of the choir and did not smile, did not nod, only touched two fingers briefly to the collar at his throat — the gesture of a man confirming a debt — and took his place among the hunters as if he belonged there, which, in every way that mattered now, he did.

"Bearer." The voice came from the king's mouth but it did not move his face, and the sound of it filled the choir without echo, because nothing in this room echoed anymore except the dead. "You have brought stolen things into the house of their owner."

"It's not your house," Quentin said. "It never was. You just ate the family."

A ripple went through the court — fear, or laughter strangled at birth, impossible to tell. The king's face did not change. But the crown *moved*. It was very slight, a settling, a leaning-forward, the way a spider shifts its weight when the web trembles, and Quentin felt the coronation fragments flare behind their sternum like a struck match.

*There you are,* said something that was not a voice, arriving not through the ears but through the memories themselves, through the stolen wedding and the stolen first snow and the stolen lullaby, touching each jar of Quentin's inner hoard the way a merchant thumbs coins. *I know these. I know this thief. Come down from the singers' bones, little magpie. Bring what is mine.*

"Come and take it," Quentin said aloud, and stepped back onto the raised choir platform, into the exact center of the mosaic sun, where the last bearer had stood with her song half-finished and her scream still living somewhere in the stone.

* * *

The rite had no music. That was the first thing the dead bearer's memory had taught them, and the strangest: the unbinding was sung *a cappella*, alone, because accompaniment was a wall between the voice and the giving, and there could be no walls. The second thing it taught was the order. Coronation first — the parasite would turn toward its own past like a beast toward blood, and while it turned, it opened. Then everything else. Everything. Held nothing back, kept no cellar, no attic, no locked drawer of the self. A bearer who rationed fed the thing instead of drowning it.

Quentin had rehearsed the opening note for three days in cells and corridors and Wren's arms, and now, with the court assembled below like a congregation that had come to watch a sacrifice and found instead a wedding, they understood that all the rehearsal had been for nothing. You could not rehearse emptying yourself. You could only begin.

They looked at Wren.

Wren stood at the edge of the platform with their hand on the hilt of a sword they had already decided not to draw, oath-marks faded to pale scars along their forearms — renounced, burned away in the undercroft, the hunt's key broken in the lock. Their eyes were doing the thing they did instead of crying, bright and furious and absolutely steady.

"Don't stop," Wren said. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see. Don't stop until it's done."

"I won't."

"And Quentin—" Wren's voice broke its own rule, dropped below hearing, meant for one person in a room of hundreds. "When you get to the valley. When you get to us. Sing that part last. Let it be the last thing you pour."

Quentin nodded, because lying would have cost more breath than they could spare.

Then they opened their mouth, reached behind their sternum for the coronation — for trumpets and oil and a young man kneeling with terror and hope on his unspoiled face, for the crown descending and the first *touch*, the first sip, the king's own mother's name sliding off him like rain off glass — and sang.

* * *

The choir took the note and made it legion.

One voice went in and a thousand came out, every carved mouth in the vaulting suddenly true, every singer who had ever stood on this stone rising through the acoustics like water finding its old channels. The sound hit the court below and knocked the front rank to its knees. Torches guttered. The censers swung wild. And the king —

The king *turned*.

Not his body. Something behind his body, vast and coiled in the hollow where a man used to keep himself, swung toward the sound with the whole weight of its attention, and Quentin felt that attention land on them like a falling cathedral. They did not stop. They poured the coronation out — the trumpets, the oil, the young king's heartbeat quickening under the crown's first kiss, the queen-mother weeping in the front row and forgetting, before the ceremony ended, why she was weeping, whose face she was weeping for —

The parasite *lunged*.

Quentin felt it take the memory the way a drain takes water, felt it gorge, felt the terrible pleasure of it rolling back up the connection between them like heat from an open furnace. It thought this was a gift. It thought the magpie had finally come to surrender the shiny things. It drank the coronation down to its dregs and reached for more, and Quentin, shaking, weeping without pausing for breath, gave it more.

The valley. The valley next, as the rite demanded, because the valley was the deepest well.

They sang the quiet — the magic-dead hush of Hallowmere, the way sound fell soft there, the way travelers crossed the border and forgot their errands halfway across the ford. They sang the market stalls with their jars of other people's yesterdays glinting green and amber. They sang the first theft — gods, they had never let themselves remember how young they'd been, nine years old, starving, pressing their palm to a wool merchant's temple in the dark of a barn and feeling his daughter's wedding pour into them like warm wine, and the shame afterward, and the bread the shame bought, and how good the bread tasted anyway, and how that taste had haunted them longer than any face they ever stole.

The parasite drank it all. And it grew.

Quentin could see it growing now — see it the way you see a shape in smoke, vast in the hollow of the king, swelling fat and gleaming on two hundred years of other people's mornings. The crown was tightening on his brow like a fist. The court was screaming or praying; the sounds had merged. The hunters in white had drawn steel and were starting up the aisle, and Wren met them at the steps of the choir platform with a stolen guard's pike and no intention whatsoever of losing, and Quentin tore their eyes away because stopping was death and distraction was stopping.

More. The travelers. All of them.

The dying man with the coronation in his blood. The woman who remembered her son's laugh and nothing else. The soldier who traded a battlefield for a bowl of stew and slept afterward without dreaming. The bride whose wedding Quentin had sold at market and bought back years later, unable to bear its absence, keeping it in the finest jar like a jewel they had no right to. Every jar. Every firefly. Every stolen morning poured out through the choir's thousand throats, and the parasite drinking, drinking, swelling against the walls of the man it wore —

*Enough,* it said, and for the first time the not-voice had a crack in it. *ENOUGH.*

"No," Quentin sang, and the choir made the word a mountain. "Drink. *Drink.*"

Because here was the truth the rite had built toward, the truth the Quiet Woman had died mid-song trying to finish: a hunger that has never been refused does not know how to stop. Feed it past its measure and it cannot close its mouth. And Quentin had one thing left that was bigger than any theft, deeper than any traveler's hoard, older than the valley itself.

Their own childhood.

* * *

They had promised Wren it would go last. But promises are made by people with futures, and Quentin's future had narrowed to this single sustained note, and the childhood rose in them unbidden — the mother whose face they had kept alive in no jar at all because some things you do not trade, the smell of her bread, the sound of her calling their name across a field at dusk, the fever that took her, the border that took the grief, the long gray years of learning to live on other people's yesterdays because their own had been taken too soon and too completely —

They sang it. All of it. Holding nothing back, keeping no cellar, no attic, no locked drawer, exactly as the rite demanded, exactly as Wren had begged them not to, and it did not matter, none of it mattered, because the self was not a treasury to be defended, the self was a *song*, and a song exists only while it is being sung —

The parasite gorged.

It had never been refused and it could not refuse now. It swallowed Quentin's mother's voice, Quentin's ninth year, the barn, the bread, the shame, the whole gray-gold archive of a small life honestly ruined, and it swelled past the shape of the king, past the shape of anything, a blind fat god of appetite crammed into a hollow built for a man, and Quentin felt it begin — distantly, the way you feel an avalanche begin in your teeth — to tear.

The crown cracked.

Sound like a glacier calving. A line of light split the crown from brow to peak, and through the crack poured everything it had ever eaten — two hundred years of a kingdom's names, debts, grievances, lullabies, coronations — flooding back into the man beneath it all at once, every stolen year coming home.

The king screamed.

It was the most human sound in the room. It was the first human sound Quentin had heard from him, and it broke over the court like a wave over a seawall, and men fell, and priests fled, and the hunters in white dropped their swords and clutched their heads as their own collars, suddenly masterless, began to feed back everything they had ever leeched. Hale tore his collar off with his good arm and hurled it to the mosaic floor, and it burst like a jar, and he went down laughing again, weeping again, both at once.

And Quentin sang the last of it — the valley, the border, the jar, the hand, *us*, the part Wren had asked for, the part that was theirs alone — pouring it out into the drowning, tearing, dying dark, holding nothing, keeping nothing, until there was no Quentin left to hold the note, and the note held itself a moment in the vaulting, in the thousand carved mouths, in the memory of the stone —

and ended.

* * *

Silence. Real silence, the kind the choir had not known in two hundred years, because the acoustics had finally given back everything they held.

The crown lay in pieces on the mosaic sun, cooling, ordinary, looking suddenly like what it perhaps had always been at its heart: a very old ring of metal that had once been loved and had learned, horribly, to love back.

The king knelt where he had fallen, and he was weeping — great, ragged, undignified sobs, the weeping of a man to whom every year of his life had returned in a single instant, and Quentin, watching from somewhere above their own body, from somewhere with no name and no past and no weight, thought: *there he is. There's the young man from the coronation. He was in there the whole time.*

Then the thought finished itself and there was no one left to think it.

The last thing — the very last thing, a single ember drifting down through the dark where a person used to be — was a pair of eyes at the edge of the choir platform, bright and furious and steady, and a voice saying a name. Quentin's name. Said the way you say a word you are trying to teach someone, patiently, the way you will have to say it many times, the way you are beginning, even now, to say it again:

"Quentin. Quentin. Look at me. *Quentin.*"

And the person who had been Quentin looked — because looking was the one thing left that needed no memory at all —

and did not know who was looking back.
