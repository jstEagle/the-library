## 19. Empty

The choir was still burning when Wren reached it.

Not with flame — the fire had gone out of the candles, the tapestries, the great bronze doors, all of them untouched — but with a heat that lived in the stone itself, radiating up through Wren's boots like the memory of a fever. The acoustics that had held every voice ever sung in that place were ringing still, a vast and fading chord, and beneath it Wren could hear something else: a sound like a jar breaking very slowly, over and over, somewhere behind the world.

Quentin lay at the center of the crossing, where the nave met the transepts, on the exact spot where the choir's oldest stones had been worn smooth by centuries of kneeling. They were on their back. Their eyes were open. Their chest rose and fell.

Wren did not remember crossing the last thirty feet. One moment they were standing in the ruined doorway with Hale's blood still tacky under their fingernails; the next they were on their knees beside Quentin, hands hovering, afraid to touch.

"Quentin." Their voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Quentin, look at me."

Quentin looked at them.

That was the worst of it, later — that Quentin looked at them *immediately*, without hesitation, the way one looks at any stranger who shouts. There was no fear in it, no recognition, no flicker of the old wariness that had always been there even in tenderness, the wariness of a thief who has learned that everyone is a door that can be picked. The eyes were the same grey-green, the same ones Wren had watched across a border post for six years, the same ones that had narrowed at Wren's mercy and softened at Wren's lies and gone wide and bright in the dark of the undercroft when Wren said *I chose you*. But nothing moved behind them now. They were windows with the shutters taken off, showing only sky.

"Who," Quentin said slowly, tasting each word as if it might be spoiled, "are you?"

* * *

The king wept for three days.

Wren learned this secondhand, from the physicians who came and went from the royal apartments, from servants who spoke in the hushed voices people use near shrines. Aldric Vane — and it was strange, Wren thought, to learn the king's name only now, after everything, to know him first as a man rather than a crown — woke on the floor of his own throne room with two hundred years in his head. The crown had taken his coronation oath, his wedding, the faces of four children, the plague year, the famine year, the names of every servant he had ever loved, the treaty he had signed with Hallowmere's founders that had made the valley what it was. It came back into him all at once, the way floodwater comes back into a dry riverbed, and the man who stood up from the stones was not the smoothed, vacant figure Wren had seen at a distance from a prison cart. He was old. He was grief-stricken. He was, by every account, awake.

On the third day he sent for Quentin.

They brought Quentin to the audience chamber in borrowed clothes, because their own had been burned by the hunters' salt-wagons weeks ago, and Wren walked beside them because no one had told Wren not to and Wren had decided, with the particular stubbornness of the hollowed-out, that no one would. Quentin came willingly. That was another wound: the old Quentin would have needed coaxing, or theft, or a lie dressed up as truth. This one simply followed, curious as a child, pausing to look at the banners with an open face.

"Your Majesty," said the chamberlain, "the bearer who freed you."

"I know what they are," the king said gently. "I knew their grandmother's grandmother. Sit down, child. Both of you." And then, seeing Wren's hand hover near a sword hilt that wasn't there anymore: "Please. I have spent two hundred years being spoken *about*. I would like, just once, to be the one speaking."

He sat on the steps of his own dais rather than the throne above it, which Wren understood, watching him, was its own kind of statement. Up close the king looked like a man who had been drowned and revived: everything in him slightly too vivid, colors too sharp, as if he were seeing the world through new eyes and could not stop staring.

"You gave me back my life," he said to Quentin. "Every hour of it. I woke up remembering my daughter's laugh, and I wept until the physicians feared for me, and then I laughed, and then I wept again. I have been saying goodbye to her for three days. Her name was Maren. She died at nine years old, and I had not known I had a daughter at all until Tuesday." His voice caught and steadied. "You cannot imagine —"

"No," Quentin said. It was not rude. It was simply true, said with the flat honesty of someone who has no past to soften words with. "I can't."

The king looked at them for a long moment. Then he turned to Wren, and his eyes were wet and terribly kind.

"They don't know what they did," he said. "Do they."

"No, Your Majesty."

"They poured themselves out. Everything. Not only the stolen memories — those went first, a feast to make the parasite gorge — but when the hoard ran dry, the rite takes the bearer's own—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles briefly against his mouth. "I felt it happen. From inside. Like watching someone give away their coat in winter, piece by piece, and then the skin off their back. I screamed. I don't know if anyone heard me scream."

"I heard you," Wren said quietly.

"The vault," the king said. "You know what's in the vault."

Wren's stomach turned over. They had seen it — the long galleries of jars, centuries of bearers' hoards, every stolen wedding and christening and last breath, shelved and labeled and fed to the thing in the crown grain by grain. "Yes."

"Then you understand me." The king leaned forward. "Everything Quentin ever took is down there. Every traveler's memory, every jar from Hallowmere, all of it confiscated and catalogued by men who thought they were gathering fuel. It is *there*, child. All of it. Your valley. Your thefts. The wedding of the cooper's daughter. The dying man's coronation." He reached out and took Quentin's hand — Quentin let him, watching the gesture with mild interest, a person learning what hands are for. "I am offering you restoration. All of it. You will walk out of this room tomorrow remembering every day of your life, and mine, and half the kingdom's besides. No one else alive could offer you this. No one else alive knows what was taken from you well enough to give it back."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, in one of the bell towers, a ward-bell shifted in the wind and did not ring.

Quentin looked at the king's hand around theirs. Looked up at his face. And asked, in a voice with no suspicion in it at all, which was somehow more terrible than screaming:

"Why? What did I do to deserve this?"

* * *

Wren did not sleep that night. They sat in the antechamber outside Quentin's room, on a bench built for ambassadors, and watched the candle burn down, and tried to find the shape of what they were feeling, and failed.

Grief, they decided finally, was the wrong word. Grief required a dead person, and Quentin was not dead. Grief required memory, and Wren had all of theirs, which meant Wren was grieving alone, twice over — once for the person lost, and once for the fact that they were the only one left who knew there was anything to mourn. It was like standing at a graveside with the whole village, except the whole village was empty, and the grave was full of someone breathing.

They remembered things. That was the cruelty of it. Wren remembered the border post in autumn, the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, Quentin sliding contraband jars out of their coat with the shameless grin of a cat presenting a dead bird. Wren remembered the third mercy — how they'd stood there with the law in one hand and Quentin's wrist in the other, and chosen the wrist, and hated themselves for how easy it was. Wren remembered the undercroft, the confession, the fracture of trust, the reconciliation that had lasted exactly long enough for both of them to walk into a burning choir together. Wren remembered Quentin's voice saying *if I forget you, find me anyway* — said as a joke, hours before the rite, because Quentin had joked about everything right up until the end, because that was how they carried fear.

And Quentin remembered none of it. Worse: Quentin did not know there was anything to remember. A mourner at least knows the shape of the absence. Quentin was absence all the way down, serene as snowfall.

Near dawn, the door opened. Quentin stood in it, barefoot, wrapped in the borrowed blanket, hair sleep-tangled.

"You're the one from the throne room," Quentin said. "The one who keeps looking at me like I'm a fire that went out."

"Yes."

"Did I know you? Before?" They asked it the way they seemed to ask everything now — directly, without armor, because they had never learned yet that questions could cost anything.

"Yes," Wren said.

"What was I to you?"

Wren looked at the candle stub. At the grey light starting in the window. At the person in the doorway who was and was not the person they had crossed a kingdom for.

"A thief," Wren said. "A liar. The bravest person I ever met. Mine."

Quentin considered this with grave attention. "Which one are you mourning?"

Wren's breath stopped.

"I don't mean to be cruel," Quentin went on, and there was something underneath the blankness, some faint sediment of the old self, a kindness that had survived the flooding. "But you look at me the way the king looked at the banners. Like you're seeing something that isn't here anymore. And everyone keeps telling me what I *was*, and nobody asks what I am. So. Which one are you mourning? The thief, or the brave one, or yours?"

"All of them," Wren said, and their voice broke on the last word, and Quentin crossed the room — unasked, unprompted, guided by whatever ghost of instinct remained — and sat down on the bench beside them, and did not touch them, but stayed.

They stayed until the candle went out.

* * *

In the morning, the king's answer waited for Quentin, delivered not in the audience chamber but in the vault itself, because the king insisted that such an offer should be made standing among the thing offered.

Wren walked down with them. The stairs spiraled into the earth beneath the cathedral, past wards that flared and dimmed at the king's approach, recognizing — Wren realized with a chill — their own stolen property coming home. The air grew cold and mineral. And then the galleries opened up, shelf upon shelf upon shelf, receding into darkness beyond the lamplight, ten thousand jars glowing faintly with the light of other people's yesterdays.

Quentin stopped walking.

They stood very still in the aisle, head tilted, and Wren watched something move across their face — not memory, not quite, but its shadow, the way a body remembers a road the mind has lost.

"It's warm," Quentin said wonderingly. "All of it. Why is it warm?"

"Because it's yours," the king said softly. "Or it was. Or it could be again." He gestured, and the nearest shelf lit brighter: jars labeled in the hunters' careful script. *Hallowmere — market trade, assorted. Traveler, dying — coronation fragments.* "I will not pretend this is simple. Some of what is here was stolen from people who wanted it kept. Some of it belongs in the ground. But it is the only self you will ever be offered, child, and I owe you a self. Name your price. Take all of it, or take none, or —" his gaze flicked to Wren, and back, and there was something almost sly in the old man's gentleness — "or take counsel from the one who remembers you, and decide together."

Quentin turned. Looked at Wren. And in the cold vault, among the hoarded centuries, asked the question that would decide everything:

"If you were empty," Quentin said, "and someone put all of this in front of you — whose life would you come back as? Yours? Or theirs?"

Wren opened their mouth.

And found, to their horror, that they did not know the answer — because every memory they might offer, every story of the valley and the border and the burning choir, was itself a jar on these shelves, and handing it over would not be restoration at all.

It would be theft. The only kind Quentin had never committed: a theft given freely, that the thief would carry forever.

"Take your time," the king said, and left them alone in the dark with the warm, waiting light of ten thousand yesterdays.
