## 21. Return to Hallowmere

The road out of the capital was longer than the road in.

Wren had counted the days on the way south — nine wagons, nine nights of salt-lined iron, nine mornings waking to the taste of copper and forgetting. The way back they walked, and the way back had no count at all. It was simply one foot and then another, Quentin a half-step ahead or behind or beside, never quite settled, like water finding its level in a vessel it did not remember choosing.

They had left the city before dawn, before the bells. That had been Wren's one condition, spoken quietly to the king in the grey light of the restored throne room: *no bells, no proclamations, no parades.* The king — Aldric, he kept reminding himself to say, Aldric, a man with a name again — had agreed with a weariness that looked almost like gratitude. He had wanted to give them titles. Land. A place at court. Wren had watched Quentin's face during the offer, that smooth unlined face with its patient, open attention, and seen nothing there but politeness. The person who would have flinched from crowns and courts was gone. What remained was someone new, and new things could still be shaped by old honors if no one was careful.

So they had refused everything except passage and silence, and the king had given them both, and a purse neither of them asked for, and one thing more: a small sealed letter, addressed in his own shaking hand, that he pressed into Wren's palm at the gate. *For when they ask you who they were,* he'd said. *And they will ask.*

Wren carried it now against their chest, inside the coat, over the heart, where the oath-marks used to burn.

Because the marks were still burning. Even here, three weeks north, past the last bell tower, past the vineyards and the salt flats and the river towns where people stared at Quentin's face as though trying to place it in some half-remembered dream — even here, the oath-marks itched along Wren's forearms like scars remembering how to be wounds. Thin lines of silvered script, wound twice around each wrist and once around the throat, inked into them at fourteen by warden hands in a warden hall, words in a dead liturgical tongue that everyone swore meant *guardian* and actually meant *key*. Hale had told them that, near the end, in the vault, with the doors opening and what was left of him pouring through those doors like sand through fingers. *You weren't guarding the valley, warden. You were its lock. And locks are made for other people's hands.*

Wren flexed their wrists under the wrapped bandages. The script beneath was fainter than it had been in the capital — distance starved it, maybe, or the crown's fall had cut the current running through it — but fainter was not gone. And tonight, camped in the last forest before the border, watching Quentin sleep sitting up against a beech trunk with their chin tipped toward a sky full of indifferent stars, Wren finally admitted what they had been carrying since the choir stopped singing.

The marks had to come off. Not covered. Not endured. Off.

* * *

Quentin woke the way they always woke now: all at once, without transition, eyes open and searching before the body finished surfacing. Wren had learned not to startle them. You didn't reach toward a person with no past until they'd found the present on their own.

"Morning," Wren said, though it was barely that — mist still pooled in the hollows between the trees, and the birds were only beginning their argument about whether the day was worth having.

"Morning." Quentin's voice was rough with sleep. They looked around at the forest, cataloguing it, and Wren recognized the gesture for what it was: the same motion they'd once used to inventory a traveler's pockets. Old habits surviving the self that made them. "Are we close?"

"Half a day. Maybe less, if the ford's low."

Quentin nodded and began rolling the blanket with quick, precise movements, and then paused, hands stilling on the wool. "Wren."

"Yes."

"You've been looking at your wrists all morning. All week, really. Since the vault." They said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "What happens when we cross?"

Wren sat down across the cold ring of last night's fire. There was no easy way to say it, so they said it the hard way, plainly, the way you pull a splinter.

"The marks come off today. Before we cross. I won't carry the hunt's key into the valley — not even a dead key. Not even a rusted one." They unwound the first bandage as they spoke, letting the morning light find the silvered script. It lay quiet against their skin, coiled and patient. "There's a rite. Wardens learn it in the first year and are told they'll never need it, because no one renounces. It's called *unswearing*, and it hurts, and after it's done the order has no claim on me and the crown has no thread to follow back through my skin. After it's done, I'm just—" They stopped. Started again. "Just a person walking someone home."

Quentin watched the marks with an expression Wren couldn't read — couldn't, because reading faces had been part of the theft, part of knowing what a person most feared to lose, and that knowledge had burned in the choir with everything else. But their voice, when it came, was steady. "Will it hurt you badly?"

"Like pulling teeth. Like pulling roots." Wren managed something that was almost a smile. "I've had worse."

"No," Quentin said quietly. "You haven't. I don't remember that you have. But I believe it anyway." They reached out — slowly, telegraphing it, the way you approach a deer or a stranger — and laid two fingers on the mark at Wren's left wrist. Their touch was cool. "Do it while I watch. If I can't remember why you matter to me, I want to at least see what you gave up so I could keep existing."

Something in Wren's chest turned over, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting in a riverbed. "That's not how it works. You don't owe—"

"I know I don't owe." Quentin withdrew the hand. "I don't know much else. But I know that."

* * *

They did it at noon, at the edge of a clearing where a stream came down off the hills in small bright steps, the water so clear it seemed less like water than like the idea of water, remembered from somewhere better.

The rite required three things: the warden's blood, the warden's name spoken backward, and a witness who could hold the renounced oath long enough for it to die. Wren had assumed the third would be impossible — Quentin had no memory, no craft anymore, nothing but themselves. But when Wren explained it, hesitantly, Quentin only nodded and knelt down across from them in the wet grass and said, "Tell me what to hold."

So Wren opened a vein along the old scar-line of their left forearm — wardens' blades were made for this, a single clean channel — and let the blood run into the stream-water cupped in their joined hands, and began to speak their name in reverse.

It is a strange thing, unmaking a word. *Ner-w.* The syllables fought. The marks woke.

Silver fire climbed Wren's arms like ivy in fast-forward, every letter of the buried oath lighting up at once, and the pain was exactly what they had promised Quentin it would be — roots, yes, roots torn from living soil, twenty years of structure and belonging and *you are a wall between the innocent and the dark* ripping free of the meat of them. They kept speaking. *Ner-w. Ner-w.* Their vision whited at the edges. Somewhere very far away, Quentin's voice joined theirs, holding the shape of the name the way you hold a bird — firmly enough that it cannot fly away, gently enough that it does not crush — and Wren understood, dimly, through the roaring, that memory-craft never entirely leaves a body. That whatever Quentin had poured into the parasite, some residue of the gift remained, the way a riverbed remembers rain.

The marks lifted.

Not faded — *lifted*, peeling away from the skin in threads of light, each letter separating from flesh with a sound like a page being torn, and Quentin caught them. Caught the whole unraveling oath in their cupped, bloody hands, held it writhing for one long breath, two, three — and then opened their fingers over the stream and let the current take it.

The water flashed silver for thirty feet downstream and went clear.

Wren sat in the grass and shook. Not weeping, exactly. Something adjacent to weeping. Their forearms were bare and pale and utterly blank, and when they looked at them they felt the vertigo of a man removing a pack carried so long the shoulders had grown around it. Lighter. Ungoverned. Free in a way that felt, for the first few minutes, indistinguishable from loss.

Quentin washed the blood from their hands in the stream, methodically, and then sat back on their heels and studied Wren with that open, patient attention.

"It screamed," Quentin said. "The oath. When it died. Did you hear it?"

"Yes."

"It sounded like the choir." Quentin tilted their head. "Was that true? Was it always screaming?"

Wren thought about the warden halls. The cold dormitories. The lessons recited in unison at dawn, forty children's voices making one obedient sound. The way no one ever asked what the marks were *for*, because asking questions was the first disobedience and disobedience was the first rot.

"Maybe," Wren said. "Maybe it always was."

Quentin nodded slowly, accepting this, filing it somewhere in that rebuilt architecture of a self. Then they stood, and brushed the grass from their knees, and looked north, where the trees thinned toward the high moor and the border that waited beyond it like a held breath.

"Then let's go home," they said. And then, catching themselves, with a small crooked smile that was entirely new and entirely theirs: "Or — wherever this is. You'll tell me when we get there."

* * *

The border of Hallowmere did not look like anything. That was the point of it. No wall, no gate, no standing stones — just a line on the moor where the wind changed character, where the grass grew a shade too green and the clouds moved a shade too slowly, where the world forgot itself so thoroughly that forgetting became a kind of weather.

Wren felt the crossing in their teeth, the way they always had. The valley's quiet closed over them like the surface of a pond closing over a thrown stone. Sound went soft. Thought went soft. Somewhere behind them, the whole loud century fell away.

Beside them, Quentin stopped dead.

"What is it?" Wren asked, though they knew.

"It's *quiet*," Quentin whispered. "Inside, I mean. My head. It's been loud since the choir — everything echoing, everything reaching for something that isn't there. And here it's just—" They pressed a hand flat against their own sternum. "Empty. But a good empty. Like a room after rain."

"That's the valley," Wren said. "It takes the noise. It took mine for years, before I understood that was mercy and not theft." They hesitated. "You lived here. Before. This was yours."

Quentin looked out over the moor, at the descending folds of the land, the smoke of chimney fires rising thin and blue from the village hidden in the next fold, and their face did something complicated — grief reaching for a shape it couldn't find.

"Then it knows me better than I do," they said. "Let's see if it remembers."

* * *

The valley received them gently. That was the only way to describe it, and Wren, who had spent their life among institutions that received people with ledgers and interrogations, never quite got over the wonder of it.

Maren the baker saw them first, at the bend where the road dropped into the village street, and she did not shout or run or fetch the elders. She simply set down her basket, looked at Quentin for a long moment with her head tipped like a thrush listening for worms, and said, "You've been away." Not *where have you been*. Not *what happened to you*. Just the fact of it, offered like bread.

"I think I have," Quentin said carefully.

"Mm." Maren picked up her basket. "There's honey loaf today. Come by when you're settled. First one's free for anyone coming home, that's the rule, always has been." She walked on. Halfway down the street she paused, without turning. "Good to have you back, Vane."

Quentin stood very still. "She knew my name."

"She knew you," Wren said. "Names are just handles. The valley holds the whole jar."

Old Tam at the mill touched his cap. The children playing hoops in the lane stopped to stare at the strangers and then, with the fluid indifference of children, absorbed them into the game — one of them, a gap-toothed girl of six, handed Quentin her hoop without being asked, as though it were obvious that any adult walking up the road might need something to roll. The elders, when they came — drawn not by alarm but by the slow gravity of community, four of them moving down the street like weather — looked at Wren's bare wrists, looked at Quentin's blank face, and conferred in low voices for less time than it took to brew tea.

"There's a cottage above the tannery," Elder Sorrel said at last. "Stood empty since spring. It wants a tenant who'll fix the roof." She looked at Quentin. "You strike me as someone who fixes roofs."

"I don't know if I do," Quentin said honestly.

"Well," said Sorrel, "you'll find out."

That was all. That was the whole ceremony. By evening the cottage had a fire in it, contributed in pieces by six different neighbors who appeared at the door with armloads of split birch and never once asked why, and by nightfall the smell of Maren's honey loaf sat warm on the table between them, and Quentin ate it slowly, with the concentration of someone tasting bread for the first time, which in every way that mattered was true.

"They're not afraid of me," Quentin said at last. "I can feel it. Everyone in the capital was afraid, even the ones who loved me — even *you*, sometimes, I could tell, you'd go careful, like I was a jar that might crack. But these people just—" They gestured vaguely at the room, the fire, the loaf, the whole improbable gentleness of the evening. "Why aren't they afraid? I stole memories here for years. I fed on travelers. I was a thief of the only thing this place values."

Wren turned the honey loaf knife over in their fingers, thinking about how to answer, and decided — as they had decided more and more often since the choir — that the truth was lighter than any kindness built to replace it.

"Because the valley forgets, and they've made peace with that. They know that whoever walks in tomorrow isn't whoever walked in yesterday, and they love them anyway, fresh, every time. It's the hardest faith there is." They met Quentin's eyes. "You're not the person who stole from them. Neither am I the person who hunted you. The valley doesn't hold us to our pasts. It just asks what we'll do with today."

Quentin was quiet for a long time. Outside, the first stars came up over the fells, and somewhere down the lane a mother called a child home, and the sound arrived soft, worn smooth by the valley's long forgetting, like sea glass.

"Then today," Quentin said finally, "I'd like to learn everyone's names. Starting with yours. Properly — not the way you told me on the road, like a fact. Tell me again. Slow. Like it matters."

"Wren," Wren said, and their voice caught on it, on the weight of a name freshly unsworn and newly chosen, a name that belonged to no order and no oath and no hunt. "My name is Wren."

"Wren." Quentin said it back the way you set a stone in a wall — testing it, seating it, keeping it. "I'm going to keep that one."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to its red arithmetic and Quentin slept — deeply, dreamlessly, the valley's mercy settling over them like snow — Wren sat at the window and took out the king's letter.

They had forgotten it for three weeks. Now, in the small hours, in the house that smelled of other people's firewood, it seemed suddenly heavy. *For when they ask you who they were,* Aldric had written on the seal. *And they will ask.*

Wren turned it over once, twice. Through the paper they could feel the thickness of many pages, a whole history folded flat — the coronation, the valley, the travelers, the childhood, everything Quentin had poured into the dark and everything the vault still held besides. One seal broken, and the past would come flooding back. A person restored. Complete. Whole.

And smaller. Wren thought of the marks lifting from their arms, the scream of the dying oath, the strange clean lightness of the space it left behind. Some things grew back wrong when you returned them to their old shapes.

Downstairs, in the dark, Quentin slept without a past and had asked, today, only for names. Only for today.

Wren set the letter on the sill, unopened, weighted with a river stone — not destroyed, not yet, but waiting, the way seeds wait — and told themselves they would decide when Quentin asked. If Quentin asked. The valley, after all, was patient. It had taught a whole kingdom of refugees how to begin again with nothing, and it had never once needed to know what they'd lost to love them.

But as Wren banked the fire and lay down in the loft above the sleeping stranger they loved, one thought followed them down into sleep like a lantern carried through fog:

Tomorrow, Quentin would wake and not know the cottage, the village, the fells — and would turn to Wren, as they did every morning, with those open, patient eyes, and ask the question that was becoming the rhythm of their days, the question Wren was learning to answer like a prayer:

*Who was I?*

And someday soon — Wren could feel it coming, patient as weather — the question would change. Someday it would be: *Who was I — and is there a way back?*

And Wren would have to decide, with the letter on the sill and the stone upon it, what a person was allowed to keep from the person they loved.
