## 22. What We Keep

**Quentin**

The traveler came up the border road at dusk, when the valley had already begun to soften the edges of the world.

Quentin watched her from the market steps the way they once watched for coin-purses and unguarded thoughts — a habit worn smooth by a year of trying to wear it differently. She was old, gray-braided, leading a mule loaded with salt fish, and she walked like someone who had decided long ago that the road owed her nothing and was therefore free to give her anything.

"New face," Quentin said, falling into step beside her. "Hallowmere doesn't get many."

"Hallowmere." The woman tasted the name. "That's what they call it now? I knew it as Merrow's Hollow, before the war, before—" She stopped, frowning at her own sentence like a door that wouldn't open. "Before something. It's gone. Funny how it's gone."

The valley did that. Even now, even after everything, Hallowmere kept its quiet appetite for the past. But it was gentler about it these days, or perhaps Quentin was only gentler about noticing.

"You've lost something," Quentin said. Not a question. They could see the absence the way you could see a missing tooth in a smile — a small dark gap where the light came through wrong.

"A wedding," the woman said, surprising herself. Her hand went to her chest as if the word had been pulled out on a string. "My daughter's. I know there was one. I have the ring somewhere. But the day itself is just... smoke."

Quentin's hands remembered their old work. The reach, the lift, the small bright theft that felt like breathing. They could take that memory from her without her ever feeling the loss — had done exactly that, once, to a father on this same road, and traded his daughter's wedding for three loaves and a jar.

They didn't reach.

Instead they said: "I can't give it back. What's lost here stays lost. But if you tell me about it — the parts still floating loose around the edges — I can hold them for you. Keep them safe. So the shape of the day doesn't go entirely."

The woman looked at them for a long moment. Travelers always took a moment; there was something about Quentin's face that made people want to confess, Wren said. Something hungry-looking that had learned to look like listening.

"It rained," she said finally. "All morning. And then at noon the clouds tore open like a curtain, right over the chapel, and the light came down gold, and my girl stood in it like she'd ordered it herself. Stubborn as her father. She laughed so hard during the vows the priest had to start over twice."

And there it was — freely given, pressed into Quentin's hands like bread into a beggar's. Quentin caught it gently, cupped it, felt its warmth settle into them the way stolen things never had. Stolen memories sat cold in the chest, coins in a miser's pocket. Given ones burned.

"I'll keep that," Quentin said. "Rain, gold light, laughter, twice-repeated vows. As long as I'm standing, it won't be nowhere."

The woman smiled, and behind her the first lamps of the market were being lit, and the valley breathed its slow forgetting breath, and Quentin walked beside her toward the inn because some things you carried best by walking with their owners a while longer.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren found them at the border stone an hour past midnight, which was where Wren always found them, because Quentin had never once in their life slept through the hour when travelers were most likely to cross.

"You're supposed to be resting," Wren said, sitting down on the wet grass beside them anyway. The oath-marks along their forearms had faded to pale silver threads since they'd renounced them — scars now, or almost. Some nights Wren still reached for the old hum of them, the way an amputee reaches for a hand.

"So are you." Quentin didn't look away from the road. Moonlight lay on it like spilled milk. "Anyone come through?"

"One peddler, asleep on his cart. He'll wake up tomorrow having dreamed very vividly of his mother's kitchen, because he told me all about it unprompted and I kept every word." Wren leaned back on their palms. "You're building quite an archive."

"It's not stealing if they hand it to me."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's stranger than stealing. Stealing I understood."

But that wasn't true anymore, and they both knew it. Nothing about any of this was understood. That was the truth Wren had carried home from the capital like a stone in the boot: that they had spent their whole warden life being a key without knowing the lock existed, that love could be wounded by truth and still choose to stay, that a person could be emptied to the walls and loved into a new self — and that the new self would be no less real than the old one. Possibly more real. The old Quentin had been built from other people's pasts taken in the dark. This one was being built from other people's pasts handed over in the light, and from Wren's stories, told nightly, patient as water:

*You were born in the eastern hollows. You came to the valley at nine years old with nothing but a tin whistle and a grudge. You stole your first memory at eleven — a ferryman's memory of drowning, though he hadn't drowned, though he'd only dreamed it, and you cried for a week because you thought you'd broken him.*

*You hate honey but love the smell of it. You whistle when you're frightened, badly. You once traded a bishop's entire childhood for a pair of boots, and the boots were worth it.*

Quentin listened to these the way other people listened to scripture — half believing, wholly needing.

"Wren." Quentin's voice changed, went careful. "Tell me the one about the jars again."

Wren closed their eyes. This was the hardest story, and Quentin asked for it most often, and Wren had learned not to ask why.

"You kept them on a shelf behind your hearth. Forty-one jars, glass gone green with age. The memories moved inside them like fireflies — little gold flickers, each one pulsing at its own speed. A first snow. A mother's voice. A hanging, gods help us, you never told me whose. You'd sit in front of them at night and watch them glow, and I used to think you were greedy." Wren opened their eyes. "I was wrong. You were starving. There's a difference, and it took me twenty years and a burning cathedral to learn it."

Quentin was quiet a long time. Then: "Do you think I chose right? In the choir. Giving all of it up."

"I think," Wren said, "that you gave a kingdom its yesterday. And I think yesterday isn't the only thing a person can be made of." They touched Quentin's hand, the one that had held the crown's hunger and poured it full until it tore. "Look what you're made of now."

Overhead, the stars did what stars do in Hallowmere — shone without comment, keeping none of it.

* * *

**Quentin**

Spring came up the valley floor like a rumor, and with it came the travelers, and with them the gifts.

Quentin kept them differently now. No jars — jars were for hoarding, and hoarding was the parasite's grammar, the crown's whole language written small. Instead Quentin kept given memories the way the choir had taught them: in the body, in the breath, sung silently under the skin. Each gift became a note. Together, if Quentin was honest with themselves in the hour before sleep, they were becoming something like a song. Not a rite. Just a song. A person, maybe, set to music.

The miller gave them the memory of his brother, dead ten years, laughing at his own terrible jokes. The midwife gave them the sound of a first cry in a snowbound cottage. A child — children were the strangest givers, because they gave carelessly, the way they gave everything — a child gave Quentin the exact color of a beetle's back, iridescent green-black, a thing of no consequence whatsoever that Quentin treasured above coronations.

And Wren gave. Every night, a piece. Not memories exactly — Wren's own past was mostly intact, oath-marks notwithstanding — but the memory *of* Quentin, which was a different thing entirely. *The first time I saw you, you were arguing with a goose.* *The third mercy, at the border, when I let you walk away with forty-one stolen lives in your pack and told myself it was duty.* *The wagon, the salt, your hand finding mine through the slats.*

"Why do you keep giving me those?" Quentin asked once. "You should keep them yourself. They're yours."

"Because," Wren said, "a memory of loving someone does no good locked in one skull. Love's not a possession. It's a trade route. It has to keep moving or it dies on the road."

Quentin had no memory of the wagon. But they had Wren's memory of the wagon, given freely, and so in a way they had been there — two prisoners holding hands through iron slats while the salt leeched their hoard away and something ancient hid itself in the marrow of them. They carried that now. It was theirs. It was Wren's. The distinction had quietly stopped mattering.

* * *

**Wren**

The rumor arrived at the border in the mouth of a tinker in the last week of thaw.

"They say the king woke," the tinker said, hunched over the fire at the waystation, warming hands scarred by solder. "Truly woke. That he stood up in court and wept for six hours, naming the dead, naming debts, undoing edicts his own hand signed while he was — whatever he was. They say the crown cracked clean in two and what came out of it screamed loud enough to blister paint off the cathedral doors." He spat into the fire. "They say a lot of things."

"And the hunt?" Wren asked, carefully casual. "Inquisitor Hale?"

"Gone. Disbanded, mostly. Some say Hale himself opened the vault doors before the end and let the hoards out — all those centuries of stolen pasts, released into the wind like seed. Whole cities remembering things their grandfathers paid to forget. Debts forgiven because no one can prove they existed. Grudges dying of starvation." The tinker shrugged. "He didn't survive it, far as anyone knows. Or he did, and he's someplace quiet, learning how to be a person with what's left. Either way."

Wren said nothing. But that night they told Quentin about Hale — the collar, the bargain, the hollowed man who had chosen, at the very end, to spend his remainder buying two strangers a corridor through fire. Quentin listened with their whole body, the way they listened to everything now, as if listening itself were a form of keeping.

"He was what I might have been," Wren said. "If I'd said yes."

"But you said no."

"I nearly said yes." Wren's honesty was a discipline now, practiced daily, like the old sword drills. "I want you to know that. The offer was warm and the alternative was ash, and I stood in that cell with my hand halfway raised. What stopped me wasn't courage. It was you. I couldn't testify against you because every true thing I knew about you was also a true thing about me, and burning you would have burned the witness too."

Quentin reached across the table and took Wren's scarred forearm, tracing the faded silver threads of the renounced oath. "Then we owe him," they said. "Not a debt. Debts are the crown's currency. We owe him a keeping. When we sing the song — our song, the one I'm becoming — he's in it. Him opening the doors. Him choosing."

Wren blinked hard, once. "You remember that?"

"I remember what you gave me. All of it. Every night you add a verse." Quentin smiled, and it was the smile of someone assembling themselves deliberately, plank by plank, nail by nail, and finding the house sound. "You're not teaching me who I was, Wren. You're teaching me who I am. Those turned out to be different lessons."

* * *

**Quentin**

On the anniversary of the burning — though neither of them called it that; they called it *the day the bells went quiet*, which was truer — a traveler crossed the border at dusk who made Quentin's breath catch.

She was young, travel-worn, carrying nothing but a satchel and a look in her eyes that Quentin recognized from the inside: the look of someone who had given too much away and was walking on the far side of empty.

"You're the keeper," she said, when she saw Quentin at the market. Word traveled, even across the valley's forgetting. Especially across it, maybe — Hallowmere had become a place people came to *on purpose* now, carrying memories they couldn't bear or couldn't hold, seeking out the thief-turned-keeper who would take them as gifts and hold them safe. "They said in the lowlands there's someone here who keeps what can't be kept."

"I keep what's given," Quentin said. "What is it you're carrying?"

The girl sat down slowly on the market steps. Her hands shook.

"My brother died in the capital, in the troubles. And I have his whole life in my head — every day we spent together, every fight, every joke — and it's too much. It's crushing me. I can't eat, I can't sleep, every time I close my eyes I'm drowning in him." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "I don't want to forget him. I want someone else to hold half of him, so I can breathe. Can you do that? Can you carry him too?"

Quentin knelt. This was the work now. This was what all of it — the thefts, the jars, the crown, the choir, the emptying — had been training for, though no one had planned it that way.

"Yes," Quentin said. "Give me the best day. Just one. The one that shines the brightest and weighs the most."

The girl gave it: a riverbank, a summer, two children skipping stones, her brother laughing with a gap in his teeth. Quentin received it like communion, felt it settle into the song beneath their skin, a new note in a chord that had been building for a year.

"There," Quentin said softly. "He's safe. Both halves of him. Yours to visit whenever you want — come back any season, knock on my door, and I'll sing you a verse of him. And mine to keep, for as long as I stand."

The girl breathed — really breathed, maybe for the first time in months. "What do I owe you?"

"Nothing." Quentin helped her up. "That's the whole point of me now. Nothing."

* * *

**Wren**

Late that night, Wren stood at the border stone alone, looking north toward the capital, where the rumor said the cracked crown was sealed in glass in a museum of horrors, and schoolchildren filed past it daily, and no one remembered to be afraid.

Behind them, footsteps in the wet grass. Quentin, wrapped in a blanket, hair silvering early the way it did now — the price of the rite, the healers said, though Quentin wore it like embroidery.

"You're brooding," Quentin said. "I know brooding. I invented brooding. It's in my song."

"The valley's changing." Wren nodded at the road, where lantern-light showed a caravan of travelers camped for the night — pilgrims, almost, come to Hallowmere to give away their griefs. "It used to be a place that ate the past. Now it's a place that shelters it. I don't know when that happened."

"You know exactly when." Quentin came to stand beside them, shoulder against shoulder, the oldest geometry of comfort. "It happened in a burning choir, when someone emptied themselves completely and discovered that what remained was enough to build on. You were there. You carried what was left home."

Below them, the valley lay in its springtime green, lamplit and forgiving. Somewhere in it, forty-one empty jars sat on a shelf behind a cold hearth, kept now not for what they'd held but for what they'd cost — the only relic Quentin had asked the king's men to return, and the only one.

"What happens to us?" Quentin asked. It was the question they asked least often, which meant it was the question that weighed most. "People like us. A keeper and a key. Do we just... keep going? Year after year? Taking what's given, telling what's true?"

Wren considered the road, the stars, the sleeping caravan, the whole improbable architecture of the life they had chosen and would choose again tomorrow, and the day after, because that was the nature of the thing — inheritance wasn't what you received, it was what you renewed.

"We keep going," Wren said. "We keep what we keep. And every morning, we choose it again."

Quentin was quiet. Then, softly, under their breath, they began to sing — the song beneath their skin, the one made of rivers and weddings and beetle-shells and open vault doors, the one that was, note by given note, a self. Wren listened, and added the next verse in their heart, as they did every night, and would tomorrow, and the day after.

Far to the north, a bell rang once in a city of bells — not a ward, not a warning, just the hour, told plainly to anyone who cared to count.

Here, no bell rang at all. Here, the valley simply held the moment, and the two of them in it, and let the moment go — trusting, as they had learned to trust, that what mattered would be given again.
