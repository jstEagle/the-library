## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley woke the way it always did: slowly, and without magic.

Juniper Almeida had lived all her nineteen years inside that silence, and still, every morning, she felt it settle over her like a second skin. It was not an absence she could point to — no wall, no gate, no line drawn in the frost where wonder stopped. It was simply that the Hollow Vale refused to hold anything more than itself. Rain fell as rain. Fire burned as fire. A bird sang because a bird must, and not one note of it carried further than the wind allowed.

Beyond the ridgelines, she knew, the world was otherwise. The Wardens' oldest texts spoke of lands where a spoken word could kindle flame, where rivers could be argued into new courses, where the dead sometimes lingered out of stubbornness or love. Juniper had never seen any of it. None of them had, not in living memory. The valley saw to that. Whatever crossed the pass came down the switchback road diminished, its strangeness wrung out of it like water from cloth, until by the time it reached the guildhall door it was only a traveler, tired and ordinary, asking for soup.

She had asked Maren about it once, when she was small enough to sit on the kitchen flagstones while bread proved under a cloth.

"Why can't magic follow us in?"

Maren had been kneading dough, her knuckles white with flour, and she had not looked up. "Because we built a place where it can't," she said. "That's all you need to know."

"But why did we build it?"

"Because something needed somewhere to be forgotten." And then Maren had changed the subject with the practiced ease of a woman who had changed that subject ten thousand times before, and Juniper had learned, the way children learn the shapes of locked doors, not to ask again.

Now she stood at the top of the eastern terraces with her breath steaming and the first grey light coming up behind the mountains, and she counted the choir-stones the way a shepherd counts sheep: not because they might wander off, but because counting was the work, and the work was what held everything together.

There were forty-one of them. There had been sixty once; the ledger said so, in ink gone brown as old blood. The missing nineteen were not lost — they were *failed*, which was different. A choir-stone did not crack or crumble. It simply went quiet, the way a bell goes quiet when the bronze forgets it was ever meant to ring. You could put your ear against one and hear nothing at all, and nothing at all, in a valley built on silence, was the loudest sound there was.

Juniper walked the row with her satchel of tallow and linen, her chalk, her little brass tuning-fork worn smooth by four generations of thumbs. She pressed her palm to the first stone. Cold, as always. She struck the fork against her knee and held it to the stone's face, and the note died instantly, swallowed whole, and she marked the ledger: *Stone 1, responsive, dampening intact.*

Stone by stone, terrace by terrace, down through the morning. The ritual of it was the closest thing she had to prayer. Her hands knew every seam and lichen-patch; she could have done the rounds blind, and some fog-bound dawns she nearly had. The stones themselves were older than any record — taller than a man, roughly hewn, each carved on its western face with a single line of script that no scholar had ever satisfactorily translated. Maren called them verses. The guild called them songs. Juniper, in the privacy of her own head, had long suspected they were neither, and that the truth was something with teeth.

*Stone 12, responsive.* *Stone 13, responsive, faint.* She paused at thirteen. Faint was new. She struck the fork twice more, held it longer, watched the tines shiver and go still too quickly. She wrote *faint* again, and then, after a moment, added a small mark in the margin — three dots, the sign the old ledgers used for *watch this*. Her mentor would pretend not to see it and then bring it up at supper with terrifying precision.

From the third terrace she could see the whole vale laid out below: the river running flat and silver, unbothered by any spirit; the village of Lowmere with its chimney-smoke rising in perfectly ordinary columns; the fields lying fallow and honest under frost. And at the valley's heart, half-swallowed by ivy and centuries, the guildhall of the Wardens of the Hollow Choir — slate roof, bell-tower without a bell, windows lit now for breakfast.

Nine chimneys smoking, she counted. Nine people. When the guild had been founded — however long ago that truly was; even the founding charter gave three different dates — there had been two hundred voices in the Choir. Now nine. Two had died in Juniper's childhood. Three more were past seventy. Recruitment was a word from another age; no young person climbed the pass anymore wanting to tend stones that did nothing, in a valley that forgot everything, for a title that meant nothing beyond the ridgeline.

We are a song being sung by fewer and fewer mouths, Juniper thought, until one day there is no one left to hold the last note. And then what happens to the silence?

She did not let herself finish the thought. She never did. Finishing it felt like knocking on that locked door from the inside.

* * *

Breakfast was barley porridge and argument, which was the traditional fare.

"The thirteenth stone's going faint," Juniper said, ladling honey onto her bowl. "I marked it."

"I saw." Maren sat at the head of the long table, though *head* was generous — the table seated twenty and they rattled around it like peas in a drum. She was seventy-one years old and built like the mountain she'd come from, all angles and weathered grain, with hands that could still crack a walnut one-handed and eyes that missed nothing, which was the problem with living with her. "How faint?"

"Faint enough to mark twice."

Maren chewed this over along with her porridge. Around them the hall went about its small business: old Brother Callum arguing with the stove; Sister Ivet mending a net that hadn't touched water in a decade; Tam, the youngest Warden besides Juniper, reading last autumn's newspapers from over the pass as though the world beyond might have improved since.

"Three stones failed the year I took my vows," Maren said at last. "Fourteen the decade after. We've lost six in your lifetime. The rate is quickening."

"You say that every winter."

"And every winter I'm more right." She set down her spoon. Her voice, when it came again, was quieter, and that was worse than shouting. Maren's loudness was performance; her quietness was load-bearing. "Juniper. The songs are failing. Not the stones — the *songs*. The dampening isn't a wall. It's sung. Every verse those stones carry, every rite we keep, every dawn-round and dusk-round — it's all one long note held by generations of mouths, and our mouths are running out, and the note is going flat."

Juniper kept her eyes on her bowl. "Then we sing louder."

"With nine voices? With five, in ten years? Child—" Maren stopped herself, which she almost never did, and Juniper looked up to find her mentor watching her with an expression she couldn't name. Grief, maybe. Or guilt wearing grief's coat. "There are things I should have taught you years ago. Things I told myself could wait until you were older, and then older than that, and now—"

The bell-tower creaked. Everyone flinched; everyone pretended not to. The tower had no bell — it had been sold or stolen or melted down, depending on which drunk in Lowmere you asked — but in certain winds it made a low groaning note that traveled straight down the spine, and this morning the wind was right for it.

Maren rose, collected her cloak, and paused beside Juniper's chair. For a moment her hand hovered near her apprentice's shoulder — a touch withheld, or postponed.

"Dusk rounds are yours today," she said. "All forty-one. Count them slow."

And she left, out into the cold, toward the archive under the hall, where the things that waited decades presumably kept their own ledgers.

* * *

Dusk came down the valley like blue ink poured into water.

Juniper walked the rounds alone with her lantern, and the loneliness of it was familiar enough to be almost comfortable. This was the hour she loved best and trusted least: the light going soft, the stones throwing long shadows eastward like sundial fingers pointing at nothing, the village sounds fading behind her until it was just her boots on frozen grass and her breath and the great patient silence of the vale.

She loved it here. That was the thing she could never say aloud, the confession with no confessor. She loved the honest cold and the unmagical rain and the way the river ran without secrets. She loved Lowmere's lamplighters and the guildhall's bad porridge and Brother Callum's terrible singing at vespers, which stayed terrible precisely because no enchantment could improve it. Nineteen years of loving a place will grow roots through a person, and Juniper felt hers every time she imagined the world beyond the pass — vast, brilliant, dangerous, singing.

And underneath the love, quiet as sediment, lay the other thing. The suspicion she carried like a stone in her boot.

A cage is only a cage if you know you're in it, she thought, striking the fork against her knee at the foot of Stone 27. And if the bars are made of silence, and the silence is all you've ever heard—

The fork hummed. Died. *Responsive,* she wrote. But her hand hesitated over the page, because for half a heartbeat — less than half, a sliver of a heartbeat — she had thought she heard something answer the fork from deeper in the stone. Not an echo. An echo comes back. This had been more like... attention. Like something on the far side of a wall turning its head.

She stood very still in the blue dusk, listening to nothing, hearing nothing, telling herself firmly that nineteen years of routine could make a girl hear patterns in static.

Stone 31. Responsive. Stone 32. Responsive. Stone 33—

She stopped.

The stone was warm.

Not sun-warm; the sun had been down an hour, and the warmth came from within, faint as a sleeping animal's flank. Juniper pressed both palms flat against the granite and felt it: a low tremor, rhythmic, slow. Almost — she hated herself for the word even as it arrived — almost like breathing.

Her training said: note it, report it, do not speculate. Her training had been given to her by Maren, who had spent forty years teaching her that the vale's silence was safety, and safety was the whole of the oath.

But her training also said the stones were dead things that carried songs, and dead things did not breathe.

Juniper wrote in the ledger, in a hand that only shook a little: *Stone 33, responsive. Note: residual warmth, cause unknown. Recommend watch.*

Then she lifted her lantern to check the carved verse on the western face, the line of ancient script she had traced a thousand times without ever once wondering — really wondering — what it said.

The frost on the carving was melting. Only on the carving. The letters glistened, dark and wet, as though the stone were sweating words.

Far off across the valley, thin as a thread pulled through cloth, came the groan of the empty bell-tower — though there was no wind now, none at all.

Juniper stood in the failing light with her heart going hard against her ribs, and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives only when dread has finished sharpening itself, that the silence she had loved her whole life was not peace.

It was a held breath.

And somewhere beneath the vale, something had begun — very softly, very patiently — to exhale.
