## 2. A Knock at Vespers

The evening rite began, as it had for as long as anyone could remember, with the lighting of the tallow lamps along the choir-walk.

Juniper Almeida went down the row of them with a taper cupped in her hand, shielding the flame from a wind that never quite came. That was the thing about the valley — even its weather was polite. The air moved in the mountains above them, she'd been told, tore itself into storms and shrieked through the passes like something grieving. But down here, between the two ridgelines that cradled Hollowmere, the wind arrived already tired, and by the time it reached the guildhall it did nothing more than stir the lamp flames and make them lean, all together, toward the east.

She counted the lamps as she lit them. Eleven. There had been nineteen when she was a child, and thirty-one in Maren's girlhood, and the old ledgers spoke of a time when the choir-walk had needed so many lamps the tallow alone consumed half the guild's winter stores. Now eleven was generous. Now there were nights when Juniper lit them for no one but herself and the stones.

The choir-stones stood in their long crescent below the hall, forty-one of them, each taller than a man, each worn smooth on one face as though generations of hands had rested there. Which they had. Juniper's own palm knew the third stone's flank the way it knew her own knee — the faint warmth of it, the grain like closed eyes. She had been touching these stones since before she could read. She had sung to them every evening of her life.

And they had never once answered.

That was not how she thought of it, most days. Most days it was simply the shape of things: the Wardens sang, the valley kept its silence, and the silence was the point. *Magic cannot follow you here,* Maren had told her when she was six and crying over a cantrip another child's cousin had performed at market, sparks dancing off his fingers like flies of light. *That is not our poverty, little wren. That is our grace.* Juniper had not understood then. She understood better now, or believed she did: the valley was a held breath. Whatever wildness lived in the world beyond the ridgelines — the hedge-craft and hearth-charms, the weather-singing of the highland clans, the Crown's licensed battle-mages with their iron-cased words — none of it could cross into Hollowmere. Here, everything a person did, they did with their own two hands and their own mortal voice.

It should have felt like safety. Some evenings it did.

Other evenings, standing among the stones with the lamps burning low and her own voice the only sound in the world, it felt like singing to a room where everyone had died with their eyes open.

* * *

Vespers proper began at the ninth bell, when the light had gone the color of ash-water and the first stars were pricking through over the western ridge. Juniper took her place at the crescent's center, before the first stone, and Maren came down the hall steps behind her, slow on the stairs now, one hand on the rail, the other carrying the ledger no one wrote in anymore except to record deaths.

There were four Wardens left. Juniper, Maren, old Brother Callow who had lost his high range to age and sang the descant an octave down where it sounded like a man mourning across water, and Sister Ivet, who had come to Hollowmere forty years ago fleeing something she never named and who still flinched, sometimes, at loud noises. Four voices for a choir built for sixty. They had stopped pretending otherwise after the last of the pilgrim-Wardens died — old Mother Sef, three winters past, who had gone to sleep in her chair during the afternoon rite and simply not woken, her mouth still shaped around a word.

Maren settled onto her stool beside the first stone. She was small and brown and dry as a seed-pod, with white hair braided flat against her skull in the old Warden fashion, and eyes that had seen too much to be either kind or unkind — just accurate. She opened the ledger out of habit, though there was nothing to enter, and nodded once.

Juniper breathed in.

The evening songs were nine verses long, and they had not changed in a thousand years, because they could not change — that was the whole architecture of them, Maren said. A song that changes is a conversation. A song that never changes is a wall. The Wardens of the Hollow Choir had been building the same wall, note by note, since before the kingdom had a name. Juniper knew the verses the way she knew her own heartbeat: the opening line that rose like smoke, the second that folded back on itself, the long third verse that walked down the scale step by patient step, as if descending a stair into the dark beneath the valley.

She sang the descant tonight, taking the high line that Callow could no longer reach, and Ivet took the drone beneath, and Maren's cracked old voice carried the melody, and the four strands braided together in the cold air and went nowhere. They always went nowhere. They fell against the stones and against the hillsides and against the held breath of the valley, and they stopped, and the silence afterward was total, and that totalness was called peace.

Verse five. Verse six. In the sixth verse there was a phrase Juniper had always privately loved — a falling minor third, repeated, repeated again, like a hand smoothing the same wrinkle from a bedsheet — and she gave it what voice she had, which was more than it had been a year ago. Maren said her voice was coming into its true weight. Maren said many encouraging things. But Juniper had noticed, lately, the way the old woman watched her during the sixth verse specifically, with an expression Juniper could not file under any name she knew.

They were four lines into the seventh verse when the knock came.

Three blows, uneven, small — a fist too little to make a proper sound, striking wood in the rhythm of someone who has knocked at doors before and been afraid of every one of them.

Everyone stopped. That was the first strange thing. In forty years of vespers, Juniper had never known the rite to be interrupted; travelers did not come to Hollowmere after dark, because travelers did not come to Hollowmere at all unless they were born here or had business with the wool-factor, and the wool-factor kept civilized hours. The silence where the seventh verse should have continued was enormous. It seemed to press inward from all sides, from the ridge-lines, from the sky, as if the valley itself had turned its head.

Ivet's hand had gone to her throat. Callow set down his walking stick and looked, for the first time in years, fully awake.

"I'll go," Juniper said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt.

"Take the lamp," Maren said. Her face, in the lamplight, was unreadable in the old accurate way. "And Juniper." A pause, long enough that Juniper turned back. "Look at what stands in front of you before you decide what it is."

Which was a strange instruction, and Juniper carried it up the choir-walk with her like a stone in her shoe.

* * *

The guild door was oak, older than the hall around it, banded in iron gone soft with centuries of hands. Juniper lifted the lamp and put her eye to the shutter-slot, and for a moment saw nothing at all — just the dark of the forecourt, the drizzle beginning to silver in the lamplight, the wet flagstones shining like scales.

Then the child stepped into the light.

Later, trying to describe that moment, Juniper would find that her memory of it had arranged itself into details without any order among them: the mud, first, because there was so much of it — caked to the knees, to the elbows, plastered in the hair, a child who had been pulled out of the earth or climbed out of it; the eyes, which were a gray so pale they seemed to hold the lamplight rather than reflect it; the size, which was small, seven years old perhaps, eight, the age at which children are mostly collarbone and stubbornness; and the hands, which hung at the child's sides, open, empty, deliberately so — held away from the body slightly, the way you hold your hands when you want whoever is watching to know you carry nothing.

No pack. No cloak. Shoes that might have been shoes once. And no horse, no cart, no companion in the dark of the forecourt — nothing, no one, a child arriving at the end of the world alone, at nightfall, in the rain.

Juniper unbarred the door because the alternative was leaving a child standing in the rain, and whatever else the Wardens of the Hollow Choir had forgotten how to be, they had not yet forgotten that.

The child did not run inside, as Juniper expected. Did not burst into tears, or collapse, or clutch at her skirts. The child stood at the threshold, dripping, looking up at her with those pale eyes, and studied her — actually studied her, the way an adult studies a stranger, cataloguing — and then tilted her head, very slightly, as if listening to something behind Juniper, or within her.

"You're safe," Juniper said, kneeling, keeping her voice low, the voice she used for frightened animals and frightened people, which in her experience were often the same creature wearing different shapes. "You're — do you have a name? Can you tell me your name?"

The child opened her mouth.

What came out was not crying, though it had the shape of distress. It was not babble, though it had the music of it. It was language — clearly, unmistakably language, syllables with weight and intention, vowels that rolled and consonants that struck like knuckles on a door, rising at the end into something that was almost a question and almost a plea. Three phrases, maybe four, spoken quickly, urgently, the child's small muddy hands coming up now and shaping the air as if the sounds were objects she could lift and offer.

And Juniper understood none of it, and yet some animal floor of her mind, some cellar below understanding, recognized the shape of what she was hearing the way you recognize a face in a crowd you've never met — a wrongness, a familiarity, a thing out of place that is also a thing out of *time*. The sounds were old. That was the only word she had. They sounded old the way the stones looked old, the way the mountain looked old; they sounded like they had been waiting somewhere for a very long time for a mouth to live in again.

"I'm sorry," Juniper whispered. "I don't — I can't understand you."

The child's face did something terrible and brief — the flicker of hope going out, the particular grief of the young, who have not yet learned to expect disappointment and so receive it whole — and then she tried again, slower, louder, as if Juniper were deaf rather than foreign, and the words rang off the flagstones and into the hall behind them, into the corridor, down the choir-walk—

—and the stones answered.

Juniper felt it before she heard it: a trembling in the floor, in the soles of her feet, in the bones of her ankles, a vibration like the moment before a bell is struck when the bell already knows. Then the sound came, rolling up out of the crescent below, out of forty-one stones that had stood silent through every rite, every funeral, every year of Juniper's entire life — a hum, deep and layered, more felt than heard, a chord with no instrument behind it, rising.

Not all the stones. She would understand this later, though in the moment it was chaos: it began at the far eastern end of the crescent, the oldest stones, the ones the ledgers called the First Nine, and it spread westward along the row like fire catching along a fuse, stone answering stone, each joining the chord a half-beat after its neighbor, until the whole valley floor was humming and the lamp flame in Juniper's hand stood perfectly straight, unmoved, as if the air itself had decided to hold still and listen.

The child made a sound that was not language at all — a small broken note, terror or recognition or both — and pressed both muddy hands flat against her own chest, over her heart, and stared past Juniper down the corridor toward the choir-walk, toward the crescent, toward the sound, with an expression Juniper had no name for and would spend a long time failing to find one for.

Behind her, footsteps. Maren, coming fast, faster than Juniper had seen her move in years, the ledger abandoned on its stool, Callow and Ivet crowding the corridor behind her with faces like open wounds. The old woman stopped at the threshold. She looked at the child. She looked down the walk, where the stones were singing — *singing*, there was no other honest word, the stones were singing a note the Wardens' songs had only ever described — and Juniper watched her mentor's face go through fear and arrive somewhere beyond it.

"How long," Maren said quietly, to no one, to the stones, to a hundred years of dead colleagues, "has it been since you made a sound?"

Nobody answered her. The hum filled the doorway, filled the valley, filled the space where the seventh verse should have been, and it was beautiful, and it was the most frightening thing Juniper had ever heard, because it meant the wall they had spent their lives maintaining was not a wall at all. Walls do not answer. Walls do not wait. Whatever the stones had been doing all this century of silence, they had not been sleeping.

The child swayed. Juniper caught her — mud and bone and a heartbeat hammering against her palms like a bird against glass — and the moment her skin touched the child's skin, the hum changed. Deepened. Settled, somehow, the way a dog settles when it finds the person it has been looking for. The nearest choir-stone, twenty paces down the walk, grew warm enough that Juniper felt it on her face like an opened oven door.

"Maren," she said. Her voice came out strange, thin, very young. "The stones know her."

Maren had gone the color of the ash in a cold hearth. She was looking at the child — at the mud, at the pale eyes, at the small hands still pressed over her heart — with the expression of a woman reading a letter she had written herself, decades ago, and prayed would never be delivered.

"Yes," the old Warden said at last, and the word cost her visibly, like a tooth drawn without wine. "I believe they do. Get her inside, Juniper. Get her warm. And then—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were the old accurate eyes, and what was in them now had a name Juniper finally recognized, because she had seen it once before, on the day Mother Sef died: grief, arriving ahead of its own explanation.

"And then," Maren said, "you and I are going to have a conversation I have spent fifty years hoping I would die without needing to have."

The stones hummed on into the dark, and the rain came down over Hollowmere, and nothing in the valley was quiet ever again.
