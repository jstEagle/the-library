## 3. What Maren Buried

The child had not slept.

Juniper knew it the way she knew weather coming — a pressure behind the eyes, an old apprentice's instinct for the small wrongnesses of the guildhall. She had put Wren in the narrow bed off the infirmary, the one that had been hers when she first arrived, and had tucked the wool blanket to the chin with the particular care one gives to something breakable. The child had let her do it. Had watched her with those ash-grey eyes that were not empty but *full*, so full they seemed about to overflow, and had said nothing at all, because there was nothing in any living tongue she could say.

But through the wall, past midnight, Juniper heard her breathing change. Not sleep. Something more deliberate than sleep. And beneath it — faint as a pulse felt through a wrist — the choir-stones in the eastern yard were humming again.

They had hummed at vespers, when the child first spoke. They had not stopped since.

Juniper sat up in her cell and did not light the lamp. She listened instead, the way Maren had taught her to listen in her seventh year: not with the ears, which wanted to name things, but with the chest, which only wanted to feel them. The sound was low and round and patient. It was the sound the stones made when the Wardens sang to them properly, except no one was singing. Except the child was singing, or something in the child was, in a register below hearing, and the stones were answering like dogs answering a whistle too high for human throats.

She thought: *I have lived twenty-six years in this valley and I have never once heard it sing on its own.*

She thought: *Maren will know what this means.*

And then, because she was honest with herself even when honesty hurt: *Maren already knows what this means. She has known since the moment the child opened her mouth.*

* * *

Maren's room was at the end of the north corridor, the coldest one, which she claimed to prefer and which everyone else privately considered penance. A candle still burned under its clay shade; Maren kept late hours now, had kept them ever since vespers, and when Juniper knocked — two soft raps, the old rhythm, *it's me, it's only me* — the door opened before the second rap finished falling.

"You heard it too," Maren said. Not a question. Her face in the candlelight looked older than it had that morning, older than it had looked in years, as though the day itself had been sanding away at her.

"The stones haven't stopped."

"No." Maren stepped back to let her in. "They won't stop. Not while she's here." She said it flatly, the way she announced frost dates or the death of a ewe, and that flatness frightened Juniper more than trembling would have.

The room smelled of tallow and dried feverfew and the faint mineral coldness that clung to everything near the choir-stones. Books everywhere, of course — Maren's room had always been half library — but tonight they were not shelved. They lay open across the bed, across the desk, stacked in leaning towers on the floor, and Juniper saw with a lurch that several of them were books she had never seen before. Not the guild's teaching texts, not the hymnals with their worn leather corners that every apprentice memorized by rote. These were older. Their spines were cracked and sewn with sinew. One of them had pages of vellum so thin the writing showed through from both sides like veins in a leaf.

"You went into the undercroft," Juniper said. "You never go into the undercroft."

"I went into the undercroft." Maren lowered herself into her chair by the window, and Juniper noticed her hands — those steady hands that could hold a tuning-fork against a stone without a tremor — were folded very tightly together, knuckle over knuckle, as if holding something down. "Sit, Juniper."

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes." A ghost of the old dry humor. "You would." Then it left her face entirely, and what remained was something Juniper had never seen there before, and it took her a long moment to name it, because it had no business on Maren's face at all.

It was shame.

"Tell me what language she's speaking," Juniper said. "That's why you're awake. That's why these are out." She touched the oldest book without picking it up. Its cover was scaly to the touch, some cured hide she didn't want to think too hard about. "You recognized it. At vespers. I watched your face. You didn't look confused, Maren. You looked—"

"Like a woman hearing a voice she buried fifty years ago." Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she did not wipe them, and somehow that was worse than anything. "It's called Old Draconic. Or it was called that, in the texts we weren't allowed to keep. It is the speech of dragons, child, or the nearest thing to it that a human mouth can make. It predates the kingdoms. It predates the *kings*. There are perhaps four people alive who can parse a sentence of it, and until last night I believed I was one of them, and I believed I would die without hearing it spoken aloud again."

Juniper's hand came off the book as if the hide had warmed. "Dragons are gone."

"Yes."

"Gone two hundred years. Everyone knows—"

"*Everyone knows*," Maren said, with sudden and terrible gentleness, "what the Wardens taught them to know. Sit down, Juniper. Please. What I have to tell you will want your legs under you."

Juniper sat on the edge of the bed, displacing a stack of forbidden books, and did not pick them up, and waited.

* * *

"You know our founding story," Maren began. "Every apprentice learns it before they learn their first verse. Tell it back to me."

Juniper recited it automatically, the words worn smooth as river stones from a thousand repetitions. "In the age of the Dragon Wars, when fire laid waste to the lowlands, the first Wardens — the Nine Singers of the Hollow — came to this valley, where the stone itself refuses magic, and they built their hall beside the silent choir-stones. And there they composed the Lullabies: songs of such power and compassion that the last dragons, hearing them, laid down their fire and slept, and the war ended not in slaughter but in song. And the Wardens swore to keep the songs alive forever, so that no dragon would ever wake to rage again, and so that men would never again have cause to fear the sky."

"Beautiful," Maren said. "Isn't it? I have always thought it beautiful. That was rather the point of it." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the desk, and lifted the thinnest of the old books — a codex bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth. "Now hear the other version. The one written by the people who were actually there."

She read. Her voice, which could carry a ninth verse across a frozen yard without effort, dropped almost to a whisper, as though the words themselves might wake something.

"'The valley was chosen not for its silence but for its *deafness*. No working of power holds here; what is bound within its bounds stays bound. Let the prison be built where the prisoner cannot be reached.'" She looked up. "Do you understand what you've just heard?"

"A metaphor," Juniper said, but her voice shook. "Some old scribe being dramatic."

"It is a survey report. It is a *site assessment*. The first Wardens were not singers, Juniper. They were engineers. Jailers. The Crown of that age — whatever crown it was, crowns rot and rename themselves — charged nine of the most gifted song-wrights in the world with a single task: the last dragon-fire could not be killed, and could not be allowed to live free, and so it must be *held*. Bound into vessels. Chained into bloodlines. And the binding-songs — the things we call lullabies, the things I have sung to you since you were seven years old — are not lullabies at all. They are locks. Every verse is a lock. Every harmony is a chain-link. We are not a choir." Maren's voice finally broke, just slightly, on the last word. "We are a gaol. We always have been. We are the only gaol in the world whose jailers were taught to believe they were priests."

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere far off, muffled by stone and wool, the choir-stones hummed their patient note.

Juniper found she was gripping the bedframe. "The vessels," she said. "Bound into bloodlines. You said—" and then she stopped, because she had heard it, she had actually heard it, and the next question climbed her throat like something drowning. "Maren. The child."

"The child speaks Old Draconic because the fire remembers its own tongue. The stones sing to her because they were built to answer exactly one thing." Maren set the grey codex down with exaggerated care, the care of a woman whose hands wanted very much to shake. "I don't know how the vessel passed to her. Bindings thin across generations; that was the design — each renewal required a Warden's song, and the renewals stopped, or were hidden, or were done in secret by Wardens who'd sold their oaths, I don't know, I have spent fifty years *not knowing*, because asking questions was the one thing our order never taught." She laughed, short and awful. "Do you see the elegance of it? They didn't just bind the fire. They bound *us*. An order of singers who never ask what their songs are *for*. I sang the ninth verse for thirty years before I found this book, and when I found it, do you know what I did?"

Juniper couldn't speak. She shook her head.

"I put it back. I put it back and I dusted the shelf and I went to evening rites and I sang the locks and I told myself the founding story one more time, because it was *beautiful*, and because my whole life was built on it, and because if it was a lie then everything I had ever sung was a cage-door closing, and I could not—" Her breath caught. "I could not bear it. So I chose the beautiful lie. For fifty years. And now a mud-caked child walks out of the winter dark speaking the language of the thing we imprisoned, and the lie has come knocking at our own door, and it has *her eyes*, Juniper."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The candle burned. Outside, the wind moved down the valley the way it always did, funneling between the ridgelines, and it sounded no different than it had sounded every night of Juniper's life, and yet it sounded completely different, the way a familiar room sounds different after a death in it.

"My mother gave me to the guild when I was six," Juniper said at last. Her own voice sounded foreign to her. "Because it was an honor. Because the Wardens kept the peace. Because the songs were mercy."

"They were mercy," Maren said quickly — too quickly. "That part may even be true. Perhaps the first Singers believed they were merciful. Perhaps binding the fire *was* kinder than slaughtering it. I have turned that over ten thousand nights, Juniper, and I still don't know. But mercy that lies to itself stops being mercy. It becomes just a softer shape of cruelty." She came and sat beside her, on the edge of the narrow bed, close enough that Juniper could smell the feverfew and the tallow and, beneath them, the faint scorched smell that had clung to Maren's robes since vespers. "I am not telling you this to wound you."

"You're telling me because you have to."

"I'm telling you because tomorrow — or the day after, or however long it takes — someone else is going to come asking about that child, and they will not be old women with bad knees and guilty consciences. And you cannot defend her with a story you believe in. You can only defend her with the truth." Maren took Juniper's hands. They were warm. Juniper had expected them to be cold. "Your inheritance is not the songs, child. I am sorry. I am sorry with everything left in me. Your inheritance is a debt, two hundred years deep, and it has just walked up to our gate wearing a child's shoes."

"The oath of silence," Juniper whispered. "First vow. *What is sung is sacred; what is sacred is not spoken.*"

"We were swearing to keep the prisoners' secret," Maren said. "Not ours. Think about that. All these years, all those vows, and we were the ones keeping the dragons' silence *for* them."

Juniper thought of the morning rounds. Of the chalk-soft stones, the verses she had fed them like water to a garden, the pride she had taken in the purity of the valley's quiet — the clean air, the honest work, the absence of anything uncanny. She had loved it. She loved it still, in the way one loves a house one grew up in, blindly, bone-deep. And the house had been a lock all along, and she had been the oil in its hinges.

"Wren," she said suddenly.

"What?"

"The child. She needs a name. I'm not calling her *the vessel*." Juniper heard her own voice harden and did nothing to soften it. "There's wrens in the hedgerow by the well. Scrappy little things. They sing in winter when everything else has given up."

Something moved through Maren's face — grief and tenderness and a terrible tired gratitude, all at once. "Wren," she repeated, tasting it. "Yes. All right." She squeezed Juniper's hands once, hard, and released them, and when she spoke again her voice had returned to its old steadiness, or something wearing its shape. "Then listen to me, and listen carefully, because there is one more thing in these books, and it is the reason I am still awake at this hour instead of sleeping like a sensible traitor."

Juniper waited.

"The bindings fail," Maren said. "All bindings fail — that's in the survey text too, in the founder's own hand: *no lock outlasts its key*. The valley's deafness is failing — you've seen it, the out-of-season bloom by the south wall, the well running warm. I told myself it was a mild winter. It is not a mild winter. And a vessel that speaks, a vessel the stones answer — Juniper, if the fire in that child is waking, then it is waking *here*, inside the one place in the world built to smother it, and the place is starting to crack around her like ice around a stone." She paused. "And there are people whose entire office, whose entire *reason for existing*, is to notice cracks like that."

"Inquisitors," Juniper said. The word came out small.

"The Crown never forgot the fire," Maren said. "The Wardens were its gaolers, but the Crown was always its intended armory. If they learn what she is before we understand what she's becoming—"

She did not finish. She didn't need to. Down the corridor, through two walls and a closed door, from the narrow bed where a mud-caked child lay wide-eyed beneath a wool blanket, came a sound that stopped both their hearts:

not words,

not Draconic,

but humming — low, round, patient, note for note matching the choir-stones in the eastern yard, as if the child and the stones had found each other in the dark and were learning, together, the first line of a song no one had taught either of them.
