## 4. The Child Who Remembers Fire

Juniper woke before dawn to a sound she had never heard in the valley: the choir-stones singing on their own.

Not the full song — not the great rolling chord that Maren coaxed from them at solstice with an hour of preparation and a voice gone hoarse by noon. This was thinner. A thread of it. A single note held somewhere beneath the floor of the world, as if one of the stones had dreamed and could not quite wake.

She lay in her narrow bed above the guildhall kitchen and listened until the note faded, and then she dressed in the dark because there was no point pretending she would sleep again.

The child was still where they had put her, curled in the cot beside the hearth in the infirmary room, wrapped in two blankets and Juniper's own spare cloak. Mud had been washed from her — three basins' worth of it, black with peat and something darker underneath that Juniper had chosen not to examine too closely — but her hair was still matted flat on one side, and her hands, even in sleep, were closed into fists so tight the knuckles shone white as river stones.

The stones near her were warm.

Juniper had checked them last night, after Maren finally slept. The small devotional stone set into the infirmary wall — a chip of grey granite no bigger than a loaf of bread, carved with the first verse in letters worn nearly smooth — had been cold as always. Now she laid her palm against it and felt heat coming off it like a hearthstone hours after the fire had died. Not warm the way stone warmed in sun. Warm the way a living thing was warm.

"First verse," she murmured, mostly to herself. "We keep the silence. We keep the silence."

The stone did nothing. It only sat there under her hand, breathing its strange heat, and the child slept, and outside the window the valley lay grey and silver under the last of the night, the terraces, the orchards, the long meadow grass all bent eastward by the wind that came down off the mountains every morning of Juniper's life and would come tomorrow too, if the world stayed ordinary.

She made tea. She sat with it going cold. She watched the child breathe.

*Wren.* That was what Maren had called her, half-asleep, when the girl had been brought in — *a wren, look at her, small enough to hide in a wall* — and the name had stuck because the child could not offer her own. She had not spoken since she arrived. She had made sounds, yes: those syllables in the old tongue that had made the stones hum and made Maren go white to the lips. But words, common words, the language of bread and beds and *are you hurt* — none of those.

Juniper thought about the way the girl had looked at the basin of wash-water last night. Not with fear. With recognition. As though water itself were something she remembered being.

"You're safe," Juniper said softly, the way you'd speak to a frightened animal, or a sick person, or anyone for whom safety was a promise you weren't sure you could keep. "Nobody here will touch you. That's the whole law of this place. Nobody touches anybody."

The child's fists did not loosen. But her breathing changed — deepened, evened — and Juniper chose, generously, to believe that counted for something.

* * *

*Fire.*

*Not the word — the thing. Wings like doors opening in a burning house. The sky full of them, wing over wing over wing, and each wing a different color of flame, copper and green and the blue-white that hurts to look at, and the ground below is a river of people running, and she is above it, she is part of it, she is —*

*— small. On the ground. In the reeds. Mother's hands pushing her down into the mud, mother's mouth shaping sound without breath, mother's eyes saying* stay, stay, stay *in the way that is older than any tongue.*

*Then the light. Then the heat that is not heat, that goes through the skin and into the blood and curls up there like a swallowed coal.*

*Then quiet. Then mud. Then walking.*

*Walking walking walking. Cold mornings. A road with no end. Her voice left her somewhere in the second week — she does not remember losing it; it simply was not there one day, the way a tooth is not there. What remained was the other language, the one under her ribs, the one that hums when she passes certain stones.*

*Stones.*

*In the dream the stones are singing. They sing the way her mother sang, low and repeating, a rope thrown down into a well. And at the bottom of the well there is warmth, and the warmth has a shape, and the shape has wings, and the wings are folded so tightly around her heart that sometimes she forgets they are not hers.*

*Someone is sitting nearby. The someone smells of tea and soap and cold morning air. The someone said words at her — soft, round, harmless words — and the coal under her ribs turned toward the sound like a plant toward light.*

*She does not know the someone's name. She knows the someone's hands. Steady hands. Hands that poured water and did not flinch at the dark things in it.*

*In the dream, the wings unfold a little. Just a little. Just enough to let the light in.*

* * *

By midmorning Juniper had done everything she could think of to do, which was very little, and so she did the things she always did, because routine was the guild's oldest medicine.

She walked her rounds among the choir-stones.

There were forty-one of them in the home valley proper — squat pillars of veined granite, each about the height of a kneeling person, scattered along the stream-courses and terrace-walls where the first Wardens had planted them four hundred years ago. No two bore the same carving. Together, Maren said, they made up the Nine Verses, the complete binding-song, though which stone carried which verse was knowledge Maren guarded more jealously than the guild's few coins.

Juniper went from stone to stone with her cloth and her jar of oil, wiping moss from the carvings, checking for frost-cracks, humming the maintenance-hum under her breath — not a true verse, just a holding-note, the musical equivalent of straightening a picture frame.

At the seventh stone, the one by the mill-race, her hum caught.

It caught the way a sleeve catches on a nail. The note she was holding bent, swelled, and answered itself — a harmonic rising out of the granite that she had never produced and could not have produced, because it required a second voice, and she was alone on the path with the water talking to itself below.

Except she wasn't answering herself. She was being answered.

Juniper stood very still. The harmonic hung in the air for three heartbeats, five, seven — then sank back into the stone like a fish returning to the bottom of a pool.

She looked up the hillside, toward the guildhall, toward the infirmary window, though the distance and the trees made it impossible that anything could carry between them.

*The stones near her warm,* Maren had said last night, before sleep took her mid-sentence. *Mark it, Juniper. Everything near her warms.*

Juniper finished her rounds twice as fast, and her hands were not steady, and she found that she did not mind. Fear, yes — fear was there, cold and reasonable, telling her to fetch Maren, to bar the door, to do something sensible. But underneath the fear was another thing, younger and less respectable, and it sounded like this: *the stones are singing. After all these years of dusting them like furniture, they are singing.*

She hated herself a little for the joy in it. She kept it anyway.

* * *

*Warm.*

*The small one comes again. The one who is not mother, not the man with the iron collar-chains, not the ones who ran through the reeds with torches. The small one sits on the floor beside the bed and does not ask for anything, which is its own kind of language, and the coal under her ribs understands it.*

*The small one makes a sound with her mouth — a held note, round as an egg — and the walls answer. The walls! The dead grey teeth in the walls open their throats and answer her, and the coal turns over in its sleep, and for one moment the dream-wings spread wide inside her, wide as the burning sky, wide as —*

*— and then the note ends, and the wings fold, and she is small again in a room that smells of dried herbs.*

*But the walls answered.*

*She presses her palm flat against the floor. Through the wood, faint as a pulse in a wrist, she can feel it: the stone underneath, all the stone underneath, the whole buried body of this place, humming. Not the way the stones in the bad country hummed — hungry, warning, *go around, go around*. This is a different hum. Patient. Old. A lullaby sung to something enormous for so long that the singers have forgotten what they're singing to.*

*She knows what they're singing to.*

*She remembers.*

*That is the worst thing about her, the thing the men with the torches understood and the mud did not wash away: she remembers. Not with her mind. Minds forget; hers has already lost the names of her brothers, the color of their door, whether the third bowl on the shelf was hers or little Sef's. But the coal remembers. The coal remembers everything, because the coal was there before her family was a family, before there were families, before the sky learned to burn.*

*And the coal is patient too.*

* * *

"She ate," Juniper reported that evening, setting the empty bowl on the kitchen table with the small triumph of a messenger bringing good news from a hopeless front. "Half the bowl. Bread in the broth, the way you said. And she drank the whole cup of water without watching my hands while I poured it. That's new."

Maren sat with her back to the fire, a shawl around her shoulders despite the season, her hands folded on the table like two sleeping birds. She was seventy-three years old and had ruled the guild's twilight years with a kind of fierce, unsentimental tenderness that Juniper had spent twenty years mistaking for sternness. Tonight the fierceness was elsewhere. Tonight she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"And?" Maren said.

"And nothing. She watched me leave. She didn't make any sounds." Juniper hesitated. "Maren. The seventh stone sang today. By the mill-race. A harmonic — a real one, a two-voice chord. I was alone on the path."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind.

"I felt it too," Maren said at last. "All of them, a little. All afternoon. Like a fever working through a body." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger. "Four hundred years, Juniper. Four hundred years those stones have sat dumb as fenceposts unless we sang to them, and now—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "When I was your age, I asked old Halvard why the verses needed two voices. Why every rite, every maintenance-hum, every binding was written for a pair of singers when our order had been dwindling toward one. You know what he told me?"

"That the second voice was for the stone."

"He said the second voice was for whatever was listening *through* the stone." Maren's folded hands tightened. "I thought it was an old man's poetry. I thought—" She shook her head. "Go sit with her a while tonight. Don't sing. Don't hum. Just be there. I want to know what she does when nobody's performing."

"What are you afraid she'll do?"

Maren looked at her across the kitchen table, and for a moment Juniper saw past the mentor, past the last Warden, to the woman underneath — a woman who had spent fifty years keeping a promise whose terms she'd only ever half understood.

"I'm afraid she'll do exactly what she's already doing," Maren said. "And that we'll find out we were never the singers at all. Only the audience."

* * *

*Night. The room with the herbs. The small one is there, sitting on the floor with her back against the bed, doing nothing, asking nothing, and the coal is warm, and the walls are warm, and for the first time since the reeds, since the mud, since the road with no end, warm does not mean burning.*

*The small one's breathing slows. Sleep comes near her like a tide.*

*In the dream the wings open all the way.*

*Sky. Copper and green and blue-white. The river of running people far below, so far it is only a glitter, a spill of beads. And a voice — not a sound, a *weight*, pressing down through the dream like weather — says, in the old tongue, in the tongue under her ribs:*

**little vessel. little ember. where have they carried me?**

*And she, who cannot speak, who lost her words on the road somewhere between the reeds and here, opens her mouth in the dream and answers in the only language she has left:*

**somewhere the stones are kind.**

*The weight considers this. The wings beat once, slow, a heartbeat the size of a valley.*

**kind,** *says the weight, and it is almost laughter, and it is almost grief.* **they sang me to sleep for four hundred years and called the sleeping kindness. but yes. kinder than iron. kinder than fire taken by force. rest, little ember. rest while the walls still hold.**

*She wakes with wet cheeks and does not know how long she has been crying.*

*The small one is asleep against the bed, head tipped back, mouth slightly open, one hand loose on the floorboards — near, so near, close enough that if Wren reached out she could touch the hem of that sleeve, and the coal stirs toward it, wanting, wanting —*

*She reaches out.*

*Her fingers close on fabric. Rough wool. The smell of tea and soap and cold morning air.*

*The small one stirs but does not wake. And Wren, mute, orphaned, hollowed-out vessel of the last fire in the world, holds onto that sleeve the way she once held onto her mother's neck in the water, and sleeps.*

* * *

Juniper woke at dawn with a small fist knotted in her sleeve and did not move for a long time.

She sat on the cold floorboards, her neck stiff, her arm gone numb past the elbow, and watched the early light come grey through the shutters and climb the wall until it reached the devotional stone — and saw that the frost on the inside of the infirmary window had melted in a perfect circle around the granite chip, a ring of clear glass in a pane of white, as though the stone had breathed all night.

The child slept on. In sleep her face was finally, fully a child's face — the terror smoothed away, the fists open at last, palms upturned, empty.

*Empty,* Juniper thought, and then corrected herself, remembering the humming, the warmth, the harmonic by the mill-race. *No. Full. Full of something that dreams.*

She eased her sleeve free, finger by finger, and stood. The child stirred, blinked — and for the first time since Vespers, those dark eyes fixed on Juniper's face and stayed there, deliberate, searching, as if comparing her against some list only the child could read.

"Good morning," Juniper whispered.

The child's mouth moved. No sound came — or almost none. But Juniper saw the shape her lips made, two syllables, round and careful, and though the old tongue meant nothing she could translate, she knew a name offered when she saw one. The child tapped her own chest with one small fist.

"Wren," Juniper said. "Maren calls you Wren."

A pause. Then the mouth shaped the syllables again — *wren* — tasting them, testing them, and something flickered across the small face that was not quite a smile but lived on the same street.

Juniper smiled enough for both of them. Then she heard the front door of the guildhall bang open below, and boots on stone — too many boots, too heavy a tread for Maren's slippers or Tam the brewer's clogs — and Maren's voice rising from the entryway, pitched high and formal in a register Juniper had never once heard her use:

"Gentlemen, the Wardens of the Hollow Choir bid you welcome, though I'll remind you our charter exempts us from—"

"—the Crown's gratitude for the reminder, Elder, and the Crown's census asks exemption of no one."

A woman's voice. Smooth as poured cream, and cold underneath, the way deep water is cold on a hot day.

Juniper looked at Wren. Wren looked back — and the coal under her ribs, the thing that had slept and warmed and dreamed of wings, went suddenly, utterly still, the way a hare goes still under a hawk's shadow.

Downstairs, the boots began to climb.
