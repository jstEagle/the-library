## 5. Riders on the Ridgeline

The census came on a morning so clear it felt like an accusation.

Juniper saw them first from the bell-tower walk, where she had gone to check the third choir-stone's pitch — a small errand, one of forty that made up her days — and stopped with her hand still on the rail. Eight riders on the ridgeline, moving at the unhurried pace of people who had never once needed to hurry. Their banners were furled, which was somehow worse than if they'd flown them. The Crown did not unfurl its colors for villages. It unfurled them for witnesses.

She counted again, as though the number might improve. It didn't. Eight riders, gray cloaks cut in the military fashion but without insignia, and at their head a figure on a pale mare whose posture Juniper recognized from engravings in Maren's old law-books: the straight-backed seat of an Inquisitor of the Second Chair.

They were three days' ride from the pass. Which meant they had crossed the pass. Which meant someone in the low country had decided the Hollow Vale was worth the crossing, and no one crossed the Serevan Pass in early thaw for a census.

"Juniper." Maren's voice came up the tower stair, thin and controlled. "I felt you stop moving. Come down."

---

By the time the riders reached the valley floor, the guild had arranged itself into what Maren called *presentable* — which was to say, every trace of Wren had been folded away like winter linens. The child was in the root-cellar beneath the kitchen, a place Juniper hated sending her because the dark made Wren clutch at her sleeve with fingers that left bruises, but there was no better hiding place in a guildhall built by people who had wanted, above all things, to be overlooked. The choir-stones along the inner wall had been draped with drying herbs, as they often were this season; nothing about the hall would look unusual to a stranger. Nothing except the silence where a census should have found children, farmers, a village. The Wardens had always been few. They had never been *this* few.

"We are nine," Maren said, standing at the great door with her hands folded. "You are old, I am older, and the rest are scattered on rounds. Say nothing you don't have to. Answer everything politely. And Juniper—" she caught Juniper's wrist as she passed, her grip dry and strong as bark, "—if Voss asks about songs, we sing her lullabies."

"Who is Serah Voss?"

Maren's eyes went somewhere far away and came back changed. "No one living should know that name. Which means she told us herself, or she wants us to know she knows we know. Either way — politeness, child. Politeness like your life depends on it. It may."

---

They came up the cart-road at midmorning, eight horses and the pale mare, and the sound of hooves on the old stones was the loudest thing the valley had heard in Juniper's lifetime. She hadn't realized how quiet the Hollow Vale truly was until something dared to be loud in it. Even the birds had gone off the air. Even the stream seemed to be listening.

Serah Voss dismounted before her horse had fully stopped, which was either discourtesy or a demonstration that courtesy was hers to spend or withhold as she pleased. She was perhaps forty, narrow as a drawn blade, with iron-gray hair pinned back so severely it looked less like grooming than architecture. Her cloak was good wool, unadorned. Her gloves were spotless. When she smiled — and she smiled immediately, warmly, at Maren on the steps — the smile reached all the way to her eyes, and that was the most frightening thing about her, because Juniper had expected coldness and gotten instead something that looked almost like delight.

"Lady Halloran," Voss said, and Maren's eyebrows rose a fraction, because Maren had not been *Lady* anything in fifty years. "Forgive the intrusion. I am Serah Voss, of the Office of Enumeration. We're conducting His Majesty's census of the high valleys — a formality, I'm afraid, tedious beyond describing, but the Treasury does love its numbers." She produced a document with a flourish, wax seal glinting red as a wound. "Your charter, I believe, obliges cooperation with royal enumeration. Article Four, if memory serves."

"It serves well," Maren said smoothly. "The Wardens of the Hollow Choir welcome the Crown's officers. We are few, but we keep honest accounts."

"I'm sure you do." Voss's gaze drifted past Maren's shoulder, into the hall, with the casual thoroughness of a woman reading a room the way other people read letters. "Fewer than the last survey, though. Thirty years ago the rolls listed fourteen souls. Now nine? The high country empties so quickly. It's the young ones, isn't it? They all go down to the cities and forget to write home."

"The young ones always did," Maren agreed. "Will you come in? The kettle is on."

"Oh, I couldn't impose—" said Voss, already walking through the door.

---

The tea was poured and untouched, four cups on the long table, and Juniper learned within the first quarter hour that the untouched cup was the whole point of it. Voss sat with her hands around her cup as though warming them, though the morning was mild, and asked questions the way a surgeon asks *does this hurt* — gently, precisely, watching not the answer but the flinch.

"So many stones," she observed, nodding toward the herb-draped choir-stones along the wall. "Memorial markers?"

"Our founders," Maren said. "Guild tradition. Each Warden takes a stone at death, and the apprentices tend them."

"How lovely. And how heavy, tending the dead forever." Voss turned her cup a quarter-turn. "Your charter is a remarkable document, Lady Halloran. Pre-kingdom foundations, renewed under Aldric the Third, exemptions from tithe, from quartering, from *inquisition* — forgive me, that word has such unfortunate modern associations. Your founders negotiated extraordinary protections."

"They served the crown faithfully in hard years," Maren said. "The exemptions were earned."

"Mm. Earned." Voss smiled at her tea. "And yet the Office of Enumeration finds the charter's language ambiguous regarding *succession*. A guild must maintain quorum to hold its privileges, you understand. Nine members. The last full roll I can locate lists fourteen. If a guild falls below quorum, its charter lapses, and its lands and obligations revert to the Crown." She looked up, and her eyes were kind, and they were terrible. "You see my difficulty. If I record nine, I may be recording the end of the Wardens of the Hollow Choir. Six hundred years of history, ended by an arithmetic error. I would hate to be responsible for that."

"Then record generously," Maren said. "Our members travel. Rounds take seasons."

"Of course. Of course." Voss set down her cup untasted. "I'll need names, naturally. For the roll. All nine."

Juniper, pouring a second pot no one wanted, kept her face still. Names. Names could be checked against parishes, against ferry manifests, against the memories of innkeepers down-valley. Names were the one thing they could not afford to give, and the one thing refusal would make conspicuous.

Maren didn't hesitate. "Brother Callow. Sister Anthe. Old Tam, though he's half-deaf now. The twins, Pell and Sorrel. Brother Ede. Sister Marguerite. Myself. And my last apprentice, Juniper Almeida." She recited them like a rosary, and Juniper understood, with a small cold shock, that these were names from the guild's older rolls — Wardens dead thirty, forty, sixty years, resurrected by Maren's flawless memory into a census that would never be checked this far from anywhere.

Except that Voss had come three days over the Serevan Pass in thaw, and nothing about her suggested faith in distances.

"Wonderful," Voss said. "I'll want to meet them, of course. A census is people, not paper — that's what I always tell my clerks." She gestured vaguely at the two gray-cloaked aides who had stationed themselves by the door like furniture. "We'll billet in the village below, such as it is, and return tomorrow to complete the household schedules. Perhaps see those famous stones properly. I do love old things." She rose, and everyone rose with her, and her smile widened as though the rising itself had pleased her. "Lady Halloran. Apprentice Almeida. You've been gracious."

At the door she paused, one gloved hand on the frame, and looked back with an expression of perfect, harmless warmth.

"Oh — one small thing. On the road up, we passed a shrine at the second switchback. A little cairn, very old, with offerings. Bread, mostly. Fresh bread." She let that sit in the air like smoke. "Someone in this valley has been feeding the mountain. A charming custom. I wonder who keeps it."

"We all do, in turn," Juniper heard herself say, and was surprised by the steadiness of her own voice. "It's how we ask the passes to stay open."

"What a sensible god," said Serah Voss, and rode away.

---

The hoofbeats faded down the cart-road, and the silence rushed back in behind them like water filling a footprint, and Juniper stood in the doorway until Maren's hand closed on her shoulder.

"She took the bait on the names," Juniper said. "Didn't she?"

"No." Maren's voice was flat as slate. "She let us think she did. There's a difference, and the difference is tomorrow." She turned Juniper gently from the door. "The bread at the cairn. That was yours."

"Three days ago. I — it's an offering, it's tradition—"

"It's a date, child. Fresh bread means someone passed that shrine within a day or two of the Inquisitors riding up. She's telling us she can read our valley the way we read our stones." Maren walked to the window, looking out at the ridgeline where the banners stayed furled over the village below. "Tomorrow she comes back with household schedules. The day after, she'll want to see every member on her list. And somewhere in between, she will find the one thing we cannot drape herbs over."

Juniper thought of Wren in the root-cellar, in the dark, making no sound at all because Wren had learned somewhere — before the valley, before any of them — that silence was survival. The thought arrived with its twin, unbidden: *the stones hummed when she spoke.* The choir-stones that had been mute for a century had answered a child's alien syllables, and Juniper had stood in that wonder and called it a miracle, and miracles, she understood now, were just events that hadn't yet been explained to the wrong people.

"Maren. What happens when an Inquisitor of the Second Chair finds a vessel of dragon-fire in a guild whose charter exempts it from inquisition?"

Maren was quiet long enough that the kettle ticked as it cooled.

"Then the charter becomes paper," she said finally, "and paper burns at the same temperature as everything else. Go down to her, Juniper. Feed her supper. Keep her talking about roads and weather. And whatever you hear from the cellar tonight — however loud it gets in your head — do not go to her."

"To Voss?"

"To Wren." Maren's eyes, when they met Juniper's, held something Juniper had never seen in them before: fear wearing an old woman's patience like a mask that no longer fit. "The stones have been humming since sundown. All of them. Every stone in this valley, singing under the floor like water under ice. She hears it too, whoever she is down there. And Serah Voss came three days through thaw because someone told her exactly what that sound means."

Below, in the root-cellar's dark, faint and unmistakable, the child began to speak — and every herb-draped stone in the guildhall answered her, one clear note rising through the floorboards like a name being called.

Down the cart-road, at the edge of hearing, a horse whinnied.

Voss had not yet reached the village.
