## 6. The Ninth Verse

The song had nine verses, and Maren had only ever been permitted to sing eight of them.

That was the shape of her whole life, she thought now, standing at the window of the guildhall with the child's voice still ringing in her ears like a struck bell that refused to finish its decay. Eight verses given freely. The ninth kept behind a door in the throat, locked with an oath so old that no one living remembered what it had cost to swear it — only that it must never be sung aloud, never written, never taught except mouth to ear at the moment of necessity, and that the moment of necessity was defined by no one, because the ones who had defined it were fifty years dead.

Fifty years. She had been twenty-three, and she had believed she was singing a lullaby.

* * *

The valley had smelled of rain that autumn, the way it always did when the season turned — wet stone and cold loam and the sweet rot of apples nobody harvested because the orchards belonged to the silence. Maren had come up from the low country three years before, a raw-boned girl with a voice too big for her body and a hunger for the old songs that had made her mother cross herself at supper. The Wardens had taken her because she could hold a drone for eleven minutes without breathing wrong, and because she asked no questions about why the choir-stones stood in their rings across the valley floor like the vertebrae of something buried.

She had asked questions anyway. That was her sin then, as it was her sin now; she had simply learned, over five decades, to ask them silently.

On the night of the ninth verse, she had been summoned to the deep hollow beneath the guildhall — the one the apprentices were told was a root cellar, though no root ever grew there and nothing was ever stored. She descended the worn steps behind Elder Corvane, carrying a candle whose flame bent toward the dark as if pulled by a current of air that had no source. The hollow opened into a chamber she had not known existed, circular, its walls veined with pale stone that glimmered faintly when she breathed, as if the room itself were inhaling her.

And in the center, on a bed of woven rushes, lay a dragon.

Not a dragon as the ballads painted one — no mountain of scales, no furnace jaw. What remained of it was smaller than a plow horse, grayed to ash-color, its wings folded tight against a body gone gaunt as famine. It was dying. She understood that immediately, the way one understands grief in a stranger's face: not by evidence but by recognition. Its eye found her across the chamber, and it was the color of a coal two days after the fire — dull orange buried under gray, holding its heat out of stubbornness alone.

"She came down from the eastern ranges," Corvane said quietly. "Wounded. Hunted. There are perhaps four of her kind left in the world, and men with Crown charters are making certain the number reaches zero." He folded his hands. His knuckles were enormous, a stonemason's hands on a singer's frame. "She is carrying something, Maren. Not an egg. Something worse and better. Her fire — the last true fire, the fire that remembers the first dawn. When she dies, it dies with her unless we take it."

Maren's candle trembled. "Take it where?"

"Into a vessel that can carry it unseen." Corvane's voice was gentle, which frightened her more than any severity would have. "A bloodline. Human. The fire will pass mother to child, child to grandchild, sleeping, generation upon generation, until the world is ready for it again — if the world is ever ready. We will sing it down. We will bind it into quiet."

"And the people who carry it?" she had asked. Twenty-three years old, and even then the question rose in her like water finding a crack. "Do they know?"

"They will know they are different. They will know they dream of burning." Corvane looked at the dying dragon, and his face did something complicated — pity and resolve braided together until neither could be separated from the other. "It is mercy, Maren. For her, that her fire endures. For the world, that it sleeps. For the vessels—" he paused, and the pause lasted long enough that she should have understood everything from it, "—for the vessels, it is a life. They live. They love. They grow old. They simply carry what they cannot name."

The dragon made a sound then. Not speech — Maren would learn later that Old Draconic lived less in words than in the space between them — but a sound like a door opening in a house long shut. And the Wardens began to sing.

They sang the eight verses every Wren knew by heart, the lullaby-verse and the stone-verse and the verse of the long grass, and the dragon's dimming eye closed, and the air in the chamber grew warm as a hearth. Then Corvane nodded to her — *her*, the youngest, the strongest drone — and sang the first line of the ninth verse, and passed it to her mouth to ear as the oath demanded, and Maren sang words she had never heard, in a tongue older than the kingdom, older than the stones, and the fire left the dragon like a tide going out.

She had felt it pass through her chest. That was the part she had never confessed, not to Corvane, not to the Elders, not to herself in the honest hours before dawn. The fire had used her as a road. It had moved through her ribs like a traveler hurrying home, and for one impossible instant she had known what it knew: wings, height, the world seen small and bright below, and a loneliness so vast it had a geology, strata upon strata of centuries alone.

Then it was gone, sunk into the young woman kneeling at the chamber's far side — a village girl named Sera, brown-eyed, soft-spoken, who had volunteered, who had been *told* she volunteered, and who wept without sound as the warmth entered her and settled and went quiet as a banked coal.

"Mercy," Corvane said afterward, laying his great hand on Maren's head like a blessing. "Remember that you did a merciful thing tonight."

She remembered. That was the problem. Mercy, she learned over the years that followed, was a word that fit many things loosely, the way a large ring fits many fingers, and tells you nothing about the hand.

* * *

Sera had married a cooper from the low country within the year. The guild had watched the bloodline the way shepherds watch a flock — discreetly, at a distance, through records and rumors and the occasional visit paid under some civic pretext. Sera bore two daughters. One died in a fever at six. The other carried the fire onward, and her children after her, and Maren had kept the ledger of it all her life, a thin book hidden behind the loose stone in her cell, names and dates in her own cramped hand, each entry a small act of witness and a small act of cowardice both, because she recorded and never once intervened.

She had told herself the oath forbade intervention. She had told herself the fire slept best undisturbed. She had told herself a hundred comfortable things in the voice of Corvane, until Corvane's voice was more hers than her own.

And then, last night at vespers, a mud-caked child had stood in the guild door and spoken the pre-tongue of dragons into a silence a century deep, and the choir-stones had hummed, and Maren had felt the ninth verse rise in her throat unbidden — rise and *answer*, the way a mother wakes at a cry in another room — and she had known.

She had gone to her cell after the others slept and drawn out the ledger with hands that would not steady, and read the line she had written thirty-one years ago: *Sera's granddaughter wed to Alric Vane, miller's son, two children, the elder a girl.* And below it, in ink faded to the color of old tea: *girl wed to a fowler from the north woods; family lost in the border fever, records end.*

Records end. She had written that with relief, all those years ago. Records end meant the trail went cold, meant the guild could stop watching, meant the fire had either guttered out or wandered beyond their reach — and either way, Maren had been free to stop thinking about the brown-eyed girl on the bed of rushes, free to let fifty years soften the memory of the fire passing through her chest like a pilgrim through a chapel.

The child in the doorway had Sera's eyes. Maren had seen it at once and refused to see it, the way one refuses a diagnosis, but the refusal lasted only until the stones began to hum. Blood called to blood, and stone called to fire, and the ninth verse had risen in her old throat like water rising in a well that everyone believed dry.

* * *

Now morning came gray through the window, and Maren dressed slowly, feeling every one of her seventy-three years arrive separately, like guests at a funeral. Below, she could hear Juniper moving through the hall — the girl's footsteps had a rhythm Maren could have identified blindfolded, quick and careful at once, a walker who was always listening. Juniper would be tending the stones soon. Juniper would be watching the child. Juniper trusted her elders, still, poor lamb; she believed the guild was what it said it was.

Maren sat at her desk and took out a fresh sheet of paper, and for a long time wrote nothing.

What could be written? *Dear Juniper, the guild you have given your life to was founded as a prison, and I was one of its jailers, and the child sleeping in the south cell is the prisoner, and I helped forge the chains fifty years before she was born?* The truth had a weight that broke whatever carried it. Paper would not hold it. Speech might not either. But silence had held it for half a century, and look what silence had grown: a mute child speaking a dead tongue, a sanctuary cracking at its seams, and an old woman with a murderer's arithmetic in her ledger — not of lives ended, but of lives *shaped without consent*, which might be the same crime wearing cleaner clothes.

There was a knock. Soft. Juniper's knock — two fingers, apologetic even at a door.

"Come," Maren said, and closed the ledger over her empty page.

Juniper entered with steam rising off two cups of barley tea, and set one at Maren's elbow without being asked, and stood looking out the same window Maren had haunted all night. The girl's face was drawn. She had not slept either; Maren could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes, in the way her hands wrapped around her own cup as if for warmth the tea could not provide.

"The stones are still humming," Juniper said. "Faintly. All around the south meadow, in a ring. Like they're... listening."

"Yes," Maren said.

"You knew the language. Last night. Some of it." Juniper turned, and her eyes were the eyes Maren loved most in this world and feared most in this one — clear, patient, unwilling to look away from anything. "You recognized it, Maren. Before any of us. You went white as chalk."

"I am old," Maren said. "Everything makes me go white as chalk."

"Maren."

The girl said her name the way one sets a key in a lock. And Maren looked at her — at this apprentice she had chosen not for her voice, though the voice was fine, but for exactly this quality, this terrible gentle persistence — and understood that she had done to Juniper what Corvane had done to Sera. She had bound something into her without her knowledge or consent. She had let the girl love a lie because the lie was load-bearing, because the whole guild stood on it, because pulling it free would bring the roof down on everyone she —

Everyone she loved. Yes. She might as well use the word, here at the end of things.

"There is a verse," Maren said, and her voice came out rusted, "that I have never taught you."

Juniper set down her cup. Very carefully, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown.

"The songs we sing," Maren continued, staring at her own hands, "the eight verses, the rounds, the lullabies — you've asked me once, years ago, what they were *for*. Do you remember? You asked if they healed the land. I told you they tended it. Tended." She laughed, and the laugh was awful even to her own ears. "A jailer tends his prisoners, Juniper. A shepherd tends his flock right up to the shearing. Every song you have ever sung in this valley has been a bar in a door. The choir-stones aren't instruments. They're locks. And we —" her breath caught, and she forced it through, "— we are not singers. We never were. We are the last keepers of a prison, and the prisoner's family built the prison, and I helped make the chains, and the child downstairs is wearing them."

The silence afterward had texture. Maren heard the fire pop in the little stove, heard wind move the eaves, heard Juniper not breathing.

"The child," Juniper said at last, and her voice was very level, level the way ice is level. "Wren. What is she carrying?"

"Fire," Maren said. "The last of it. Bound into her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother on a night when a dying dragon asked us for help, and we gave her the only help we knew how to give, which was to take everything she was and hide it inside a human bloodline forever." She finally raised her eyes. "I sang the ninth verse, Juniper. I felt it pass through me. I have spent fifty years telling myself it was mercy, and last night, when that child spoke, I heard the truth come back up my throat like bile: we did not save the fire. We stole it. And we hid our theft so deep that we forgot it ourselves, and called the forgetting holiness."

Juniper stood very still. Outside, the gray light strengthened by a degree, and somewhere down the valley a single choir-stone hummed — one note, thin and high, like a wire tightening.

"You sang it," Juniper said. "The ninth verse. Fifty years ago." Not a question. Juniper had already assembled the whole of it; Maren could see the architecture finished behind her eyes, and it terrified her, how fast the girl built. "Which means you can sing it again. Which means you can *un*sing it."

"No one can unsing it. The binding doesn't work that way, it's woven into the blood, it passes down whether we —"

"Whether we want it to or not," Juniper said softly. "Like everything else we inherit." She picked up her cup and did not drink, only held it, and looked out the window toward the south meadow where the stones hummed their century-late answer. "The Inquisitors will come, Maren. You know that. A census, a survey, some polite fiction — but they'll come, because the Crown has wanted that fire since before either of us was born, and last night the stones rang loud enough to wake the dead in the capital itself."

Maren had known. She had known since the moment the humming started, the way one knows a storm is coming from the ache in old bones. But hearing Juniper say it plainly made it real, made it a thing with a date attached.

"Then we have days," Maren said. "Perhaps a week."

"Then we don't waste them." Juniper turned back, and her face had changed — not harder, exactly, but *settled*, like ground after frost. "Teach me the ninth verse."

The request landed in Maren's chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling toward water fifty years deep. Everything in her rose up to refuse it — the oath, the habit, the cowardice dressed as protection, the old Corvane-voice saying *she is not ready, she is too young, the burden is yours alone* — and beneath all of it, quieter than any of it, the truth: that she had carried this alone for fifty years and it had rotted in the carrying, and that secrets do not protect the ones we love, they only postpone the moment the secret does its damage.

That, and one other truth, smaller and sharper: the past was not staying sung-down. It was rising. She could feel it in the humming stones, in the warming air, in the child's voice breaking the valley's long silence — the whole buried architecture of fifty years ago pushing up through the soil like bones after a flood.

Maren rose from her chair. Her knees cracked. She crossed to the door and drew the bolt, and turned the key in the lock that had never once, in forty years, been turned while she was inside the room.

"Sit," she said to Juniper, and her voice did not shake, and she marveled at that. "And open your ears wider than you have ever opened them. Because the ninth verse begins with a word that means *forgive me*, and I have waited fifty years to say it to someone who needed to hear the rest."

Outside the shuttered window, unnoticed by either of them, the well in the courtyard had begun, very faintly, to steam.
