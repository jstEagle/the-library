## 8. Terms of Silence

The lantern trembled in Juniper's hand before she could still it.

She had not heard the woman approach. No one approached unheard in the valley — that was the whole architecture of the place, the whole covenant of stone and silence. The choir-stones announced footsteps the way a struck bell announces a hammer. And yet here was Serah Voss standing at the edge of her light as though she had grown up out of the frost like one of the out-of-season flowers, patient and pale and entirely without apology.

"You walk quietly," Juniper said.

"I listen loudly." Voss's smile did not waver. "There's a difference, Warden Almeida, though most people never learn it. May I trouble you for tea? The census work runs cold this time of year."

It was not a request. It had the shape of one, the courtesy of one, but Juniper had spent thirty years learning to hear what songs were actually saying beneath their words, and this was not a request. This was a key turning in a lock.

"Of course," Juniper said. "The guildhall is humble, but the kettle is honest."

"How refreshing," said Voss. "I so rarely meet either."

* * *

The guildhall kitchen had hosted a thousand small ceremonies — novices' first suppers, Maren's interminable arguments over verse-notation, the winter nights when the whole dwindling company of them had crowded around the stove like moths determined to be warm. It had never hosted anything like this: Serah Voss seated at the long pine table with her hands folded, her grey cloak brushed clean of road-dust, her two Inquisitors dismissed to the yard with a flick of two fingers, watching Juniper measure out leaves with the fixed, pleasant attention of a cat watching a bird that has not yet noticed the window is closed.

Juniper set the kettle on. Her hands were steady. She made them be steady, the way she made her voice hold pitch, the way Maren had taught her — *the note doesn't care how you feel, girl; give it your feeling or give it nothing, but never give it fear half-formed.*

"The water's from the east well," Voss observed.

"The east well is our best."

"Mm." Voss turned her cup when it came, a small precise rotation, handle to the right, rim toward her lips — and then did not drink. She looked into the tea instead as though reading it. "Warm," she said. "In a valley where everything else runs cold."

"The stove's been lit since Vespers."

"Of course it has." Voss looked up, and her eyes were the color of the slate the Inquisitors wrote their findings on, flat and dark and faintly reflective. "You know, I've served the Crown in nine provinces, Warden Almeida. I have taken censuses in fishing villages where every third house held a smuggler, and in mining towns where the foreman sold children to the pit-owners, and once in a bishop's palace where the holiest man I ever met kept a demon in his cellar because it had grown fond of him and he of it. Do you know what all those places had in common?"

Juniper folded her hands in her lap. "Tea?"

A laugh escaped Voss — brief, genuine, startlingly human. "Tea," she agreed. "But also this: everyone lied to me beautifully. Farmers, bishops, demons. Lying is the native language of people who have something to lose. And then I come here—" she gestured at the kitchen, the hanging bunches of dried yarrow, the choir-stones visible through the window as pale shoulders under snow, "—to a valley where the whole guild has sung the same seven songs for three hundred years, where you can recite your own history backward, where no one has ever needed to lie about anything because nothing here has ever *happened*—"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, an Inquisitor's boot crunched frost.

"—and I find myself wondering," Voss finished softly, "what it will sound like. When someone here lies to me for the first time."

The fire popped. Juniper thought of Wren asleep in the loft above them, curled around the small hot secret of herself like a coal banked in ash. She thought of Maren, who had gone to the chapel an hour ago to keep the night-watch and had gripped Juniper's wrist on the way out hard enough to leave marks — *stall her, child, stall her till dawn if you can.* She thought of the ninth verse, which she now knew, which sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and of what it meant, and of who had done it, and of the small mute girl who carried the consequence of a mercy fifty years old.

"I wouldn't know," Juniper said. "I've never lied to anyone."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

* * *

They talked for a while about nothing, and both of them knew it was nothing, and both of them let it be nothing because the nothing was the duel. Voss asked about crop yields and Juniper answered. Voss asked about the guild's charter and Juniper recited it — the Wardens of the Hollow Choir, keepers of the songs, servants of the valley's peace, sworn to silence in matters of the deep past. Voss asked how many Wardens remained and Juniper told her the truth, because the truth was safe: eleven. Nine elders, Maren, herself. A guild thin enough to see daylight through.

"And the child," Voss said, at last, setting down her untouched cup with a click like a door latching. "The mud-child who arrived at Vespers four nights ago. Name unknown. Origin unknown. Speech—" she paused, delicately, "—unusual."

"She's called Wren," Juniper said. "She's perhaps eight years old. She hasn't spoken a word of common since she came. She was found half-dead on the Hollow Road, and we took her in because we are Wardens, and taking in the lost is the first duty of the guild, older than the charter itself."

"All true," said Voss. "And yet."

"And yet."

"Warden Almeida, I am going to speak to you now without ceremony, because I believe you are a woman who respects it." Voss leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight moved on her face like water. "The Crown knows things about this valley. Old things. There are records in the capital older than your charter — records of what the Wardens were founded to guard, and what was done here, and what was promised to whom. Most of my order believes those records are legend. I do not have the luxury of believing things are legends. I have spent my life being the person sent to check."

Juniper kept her breathing even. Beneath the floorboards, beneath the flagstones, beneath the roots of the guildhall itself, she could feel it — faint as a pulse through a wall, unmistakable as a drum at midnight. The stones were humming. They had been humming for four days, ever since Wren crossed the threshold, and they were humming louder tonight, and Serah Voss was sitting six feet above the sound pretending not to hear it, which meant she heard it perfectly.

"What is it the Crown wants?" Juniper asked.

"Honestly? Leverage." For the first time something unguarded crossed Voss's face — weariness, maybe, or its close cousin, honesty. "The eastern ranges are stirring. You may have felt it even here; the earth has been restless since midwinter. There are remnant powers in this world that the old wars never finished killing, and the Crown would very much like to finish them — or better, to *own* them. A weapon that thinks is worth ten thousand soldiers that don't. If such a thing existed. If it could be found." She smiled again, gentle as snowfall. "If it had, say, washed up half-frozen on a mountain road and been taken in by the last guild in the realm sworn to guard exactly such secrets."

"You're asking me to speculate."

"I'm asking you to surrender her."

The words fell into the kitchen and lay there between them, plain and terrible, stripped of all the courtesy that had dressed them until now. Voss did not look away. That was the thing about her that frightened Juniper more than any threat could have — she never looked away, never blinked at her own cruelty, wore it openly the way a soldier wears a scar.

"In exchange," Voss continued, in the tone of a woman discussing grain prices, "the guild receives the Crown's full protection. Charter renewed in perpetuity. Immunity from the inquiries that will inevitably follow — because they will follow, Warden Almeida, make no mistake. When the eastern lords learn what slept in this valley, there will be commissions and tribunals and men with less patience than I have. I can stand between you and all of it. I can stand between you and *me*, which frankly concerns me more. All I require is the child. Delivered to my custody by morning. Unharmed, unspoiled, and — this matters — *willing*. Or appearing willing. Children are resilient. She'll forget this valley inside a year."

"And if I refuse?"

Voss spread her hands. "Then I do my duty. I report what I suspect. The machinery begins to turn, and it is a large machine, and it does not stop for guilds or charters or old women singing in the snow. Eleven Wardens against the Crown's justice." She tilted her head. "You would lose everything you love, slowly, in front of witnesses. Whereas the other path costs you one thing only, and she is, after all, not yours."

*Not yours.*

Juniper looked at her hands. They were a Warden's hands, ink-stained and rope-calloused, hands that had tended stones and mended roofs and held a shivering mud-caked child through her first fever-night in the loft, humming the seventh verse because it was the only song that slowed the trembling. *Not yours.* As though love required ownership. As though the guild's whole three-hundred-year covenant of guardianship amounted to a legal technicality that could be argued away across a cooling pot of tea.

And underneath the anger — she was honest enough with herself to feel it — underneath the anger was the seduction, sweet and low as a lullaby. Protection. Permanence. The guild saved, the valley saved, Maren's last years passed in peace instead of tribunals. One child, weighed against everything. And the child would live, wouldn't she? Voss had said unharmed. The Crown wanted a weapon, not a corpse. Wren would be fed and housed and trained and used, and she would forget the valley, and she would forget Juniper, and she would spend her life as a thing drawn upon rather than a person lived—

*That is what the binding is,* said a voice in Juniper that sounded like Maren fifty years younger. *That is what we have always done. We are being offered the chance to do it lawfully.*

"I need to consult my First Warden," Juniper said. "This is beyond my authority."

"Of course." Voss rose, smooth as water finding its level, and drew her cloak around her. At the door she paused, and looked back, and for a moment the pleasant mask slipped just far enough that Juniper saw the woman beneath it — tired, watchful, and afraid, genuinely afraid, in a way that had nothing to do with dragons.

"For what it's worth, Warden Almeida," Voss said quietly, "I hope you refuse. I have spent twenty years doing my duty, and I have never once watched anyone do the right thing, and I confess I've begun to wonder whether the two have ever met." Then the smile returned, seamless, bottomless. "I'll expect your answer at dawn. Oh — and one more thing."

"Yes?"

"The child spoke today. My man at the woodline heard her. Not Draconic." Voss's eyes were slate and still. "*Common.* Two words, clear as a bell. Would you like to guess what they were?"

Juniper's heart stopped, started again, wrong.

"'Burn bright,'" said Voss. "Said to the east well, at dusk, alone. Almost as though she were greeting someone." She opened the door onto the blue cold of the night. "Sleep well, Warden Almeida. Dawn comes early in the mountains."

* * *

Juniper stood in the empty kitchen for a long time after the door closed. The tea had gone cold in both cups. Neither of them had drunk. Of course neither of them had drunk; the tea had never been the point; the tea had been the table on which the real objects were laid out and examined, and now the table was bare and the choice lay on it like a knife.

She climbed the ladder to the loft slowly, each rung a decision.

Wren was awake. Of course she was — the child always knew, some animal sense in her that predated speech, that predated perhaps speech itself. She sat up among the blankets, small and grave, her dark eyes catching the lamplight, and when Juniper knelt beside her she reached out and touched Juniper's cheek with one finger, the way she did when words failed her, which was always, which was increasingly not.

"Burn bright," Juniper whispered. "Wren. What did you say to the well?"

Wren's brow furrowed. Then, slowly, painfully, the way a bird tests a broken wing, she shaped the sounds: "*Wa... ter... re... mem... ber.*"

The water remembers.

Juniper gathered the child against her chest and felt the heat of her — not fever, never fever, just that deep banked warmth that had been growing for four days, that was warming wells and waking flowers and humming in the stones, that was unraveling three hundred years of silence thread by thread. Above them, through the roof, the stars burned indifferent. Below them, in the yard, an Inquisitor coughed. Beyond the ridge, in the chapel, an old woman was keeping a night-watch and buying them hours with her presence, hours that would run out at dawn.

Dawn. Voss would return at dawn, and Juniper would have to answer her, and there were only two answers in the world — except that Juniper had spent her whole life in a guild that sang seven verses to bind what should have been free, and she had learned, these last terrible days, that every song had another verse, and every oath had a crack in it, and every question with two answers was simply a question no one had been brave enough to sing wrongly yet.

She kissed the top of Wren's head, breathed in woodsmoke and frost and something older, something like sun-hot stone.

"Sleep now," she murmured, lying, gently, for the second time in her life. "No one is coming."

Down in the yard, boots crunched on frost — moving, she noted, toward the east well. Toward the water that remembered.

Juniper blew out the lamp and began, silently, in the dark, to pack.
