## 9. Running the Hollow Road

The last thing Juniper heard from the guildhall was Maren's voice, lifted in the second verse of the Vespers canticle — the one about the shepherd who counts his sheep by candlelight — pitched deliberately too loud, the way a singer does when she wants to be heard through walls and closed doors and the ears of men who should not be listening.

*She's buying us time,* Juniper thought, and her stomach turned over, because she knew what time cost when it was bought that way.

"Keep moving," she whispered to Wren, whose small hand was clamped in hers, cold as riverstone. "Don't look back."

Wren looked back. Of course she did. Children always did; they hadn't yet learned that looking back was how you lost things twice. Her dark eyes caught the light spilling from the guildhall windows behind them, gold and warm and utterly false now, a hearth-glow laid over a den of wolves. Then she turned forward again and ran, her bare feet silent on the frost-stiffened grass while Juniper's boots crunched like breaking bones with every stride.

They had left through the scullery door, the one that stuck and groaned, except tonight Maren had oiled it — *when?* Juniper wondered. *How long has she known this night would come?* — and slipped into the herb garden where the rosemary grew in rows so old their stems had gone woody as kindling. From there, the compost path, then the low wall behind the beehives, then down into the orchard where the apple trees stood black and patient under a sky scoured clean of clouds. The stars were merciless. There was no moon to hide beneath, only starlight sharp enough to read by, and two figures crossing open ground as visible as ink on snow.

Juniper's heart hammered against her ribs like something caged and furious. She had run this valley's paths ten thousand times — at dawn, at dusk, in rain, in the blue hush after snowfall — but never like this, never with her lungs burning and Wren's hand crushing hers and the knowledge, cold at the base of her skull, that behind them Serah Voss sat drinking tea that had long gone cold, listening to an old woman sing, waiting for the song to end.

*The choir-songs are failing,* Maren had said, weeks ago, in this same orchard, her voice thin as winter light. *And when they fail, child, everything they hold will fall out into the world.*

Juniper had not understood then. She understood now. The songs were failing because the thing they held was awake, and it was walking beside her in the body of a seven-year-old girl who had never spoken a word of common tongue.

* * *

The hollow road began at the orchard's far corner, where the land folded inward like a page creased by a careless thumb. It was not a road, properly — the Wardens called it that out of old habit, though no cart had ever traveled it. It was a seam in the valley floor, a sunken track worn by centuries of Warden feet, running between banks of hawthorn and bramble high enough overhead to swallow a rider whole. In summer it was green and close and smelled of crushed leaves. Tonight it was a corridor of frost and thorn, and every branch that raked Juniper's shoulder seemed to whisper: *they will find you, they will find you.*

She counted her landmarks the way Maren had taught her, years ago, on ordinary walks that Juniper now understood had been rehearsals all along. The split boulder shaped like a kneeling woman. The spring that seeped from the bank and froze in tiers like a tiny frozen waterfall. The leaning ash tree with the horseshoe nailed to its trunk — iron, always iron, the Wardens' quiet superstition, though Juniper had never asked what the iron was meant to keep out or keep in.

"The tollhouse," she breathed, more to herself than to Wren. "We make for the tollhouse."

Wren tugged her hand. Stopped. Stood in the middle of the hollow road with her head cocked, listening to something Juniper could not hear.

"What is it?" Juniper crouched beside her, scanning the dark rim of the road above them. "Wren. What do you hear?"

The child's lips parted. No sound came — it never did, not in any tongue Juniper knew — but her throat worked, and her eyes went wide and strange, reflecting stars. And then Juniper felt it too, or felt its absence rather: a wrongness in the air, a silence inside the silence, like a note cut off mid-phrase.

Behind them, faint and frayed, came the sound of singing.

Maren's voice, carried on the still night air from the direction of the guildhall — but not the Vespers canticle anymore. This was older. Deeper. One of the binding-songs, the ones Juniper had sung all her life believing them lullabies, believing them blessings over the fields and the stones and the sleeping valley. Maren was singing the third verse of the Deep Canticle, the one that named the stones by their true names, and her voice cracked on the fourth line, and steadied, and cracked again.

And the choir-stones answered.

Juniper felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it with her ears — a hum rising out of the earth itself, out of the buried spine of stone that ran beneath the valley like a sleeper's backbone. The nearest waymarker, half-hidden in the bramble bank three paces to her left, began to glow. Not brightly. Faintly, fitfully, the way a dying coal breathes red when stirred. Its carved spirals filled with pale light and guttered and filled again, keeping imperfect time with Maren's failing voice.

For a hundred years, Juniper knew, the stones had not answered anything. That was the whole point of the valley — the great working her predecessors had wrought, the silence laid over the land like a shroud, magic unable to follow across its borders. The stones were supposed to be dead things. Memorials. Gravestones for a war no one remembered.

But Wren had come, and the stones had begun to hum, and now, with Maren pouring the last strength of her old voice into the Deep Canticle, they were answering — raggedly, reluctantly, like instruments pulled from an attic after decades, strings rusted, wood warped, playing anyway because the music demanded it.

"She's waking them," Juniper whispered. "She's waking the whole choir." And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, why: every moment the stones sang was a moment the Inquisitors' attention snagged on the guildhall, on the valley's heart, on anything but two small figures fleeing down a sunken road toward the mountains. Maren was making herself the loudest thing in the world.

Wren made a sound then — a small, broken syllable in that ancient language, directed at the glowing stone, and the stone flared once, bright as a struck match, and went dark.

"No," Juniper said gently, taking the child's face in both hands. "No calling them, little one. Not yet. Not here. We have to be nothing. We have to be shadows until we're past the ridge. Can you do that? Can you be a shadow with me?"

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, solemn as a small priestess, and pressed her lips together, and took Juniper's hand again.

They ran.

* * *

The hollow road climbed as it went, winding up the valley's eastern flank through country no one farmed because nothing worth farming would grow there. Juniper's breath tore at her throat. Frost smoke rose from her mouth and hung in the windless air, marking their passage as plainly as banners. She forced herself to think like a hunted animal, because that was what she was now, and the thought kept arriving fresh and unbelievable each time it came: *I am fleeing my own home. I am the thing the valley must be protected from.*

Every landmark passed was a small grief. The kneeling boulder. The frozen spring. The ash tree with its horseshoe, which she touched as she passed without deciding to, her fingers finding the cold iron the way they'd found it a thousand times, and she thought: *will I ever touch it again? Will it still be here? Will I?*

At the top of the second rise, she allowed them both a rest, crouched in the shadow of a hawthorn thicket, and looked back.

The valley lay below like a cupped hand holding a handful of embers. The guildhall glowed gold at the center, every window lit — Maren's doing, flooding the place with light and song so that no eye would drift toward the dark flank of the mountain. And around it, faintly, impossibly, Juniper could see the waymarkers of the outer circuit flickering in sequence, one after another after another, a ring of dying fire tracing the valley's border, pulsing with the rhythm of an old woman's breath.

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing Juniper had ever seen, and it was a death rattle, and she pressed her fist against her mouth to keep from crying out.

Because she could see the riders too. Four of them, torches bobbing, on the near ridgeline — not riding down into the valley, not yet, but pacing along its rim like wolves deciding. And further off, at the valley's western edge, a fifth light burned steady and white where no torch should burn: lantern-light in the tollhouse? No. Too high. Too still.

A camp. They'd established a camp on the far side already. Which meant the net was not behind them. The net was ahead of them, and they were running down its throat.

*Maren,* Juniper thought, *oh, Maren, they were never going to sit in your parlor and drink your tea.*

* * *

"We can't take the tollhouse road," she told Wren, crouched close, speaking against the child's ear so the words wouldn't carry. "There's a watch-fire past the western ridge. We go north instead. The shepherd stairs. It's steep, and it's exposed on the shoulder of the fell, but no one watches it because no one's fool enough to climb it at night."

Wren studied her with those grave, unchildlike eyes, and nodded once.

The shepherd stairs were not stairs, exactly — they were a scramble of switchbacks up a scree slope, a path maintained by generations of Wardens for the sake of the high waymarkers, the ones set into the fells themselves, the sentinels of the choir. Juniper had climbed them perhaps fifty times in her life, always in daylight, always with the easy confidence of a girl who belonged to every stone of this valley.

Now each step was a negotiation with terror. The scree shifted and slid. Somewhere below, a loose stone clattered down a hundred feet of darkness, and both of them froze, flattened against the cold rock, hearts hammering, counting seconds. Nothing followed the sound. Nothing ever follows the sound, Juniper told herself. But she knew better. She had been trained by the best listeners in the valley, and she knew that a good tracker reads a hillside the way Maren read a psalter — fluently, greedily, line by line.

Halfway up, Wren stumbled, and Juniper caught her, and for a moment they simply held each other on the freezing slope, two small warm bodies in an ocean of cold, and Juniper felt the child trembling — not from fear, she realized. From effort. From holding something in. The fire in Wren's blood wanted out; Juniper could feel it now, a faint heat radiating from the girl's skin like a stone pulled from a hearth, and she understood with sick certainty that every mile they fled, every fear that spiked in the child's small chest, brought that heat closer to the surface.

"If it comes," Juniper whispered into her hair, "if it starts to come out and you can't hold it — you tell me. However you can. You squeeze my hand twice. Twice means *now*. And I'll stop, and we'll face it together, whatever it is. I'm not leaving you. Do you hear me? Whatever happens on this mountain, I am not leaving you."

Wren squeezed her hand once. Then, deliberate, deliberate as a vow: twice.

* * *

They gained the shoulder of the fell as the sky in the east went from black to the color of a bruise healing. Below and behind, the valley had gone quiet — the guildhall's windows dark now, the waymarkers dark, the singing stopped. Juniper told herself it meant Maren had simply finished, had sung the choir down and gone to bed, had stalled the Inquisitors with tea and scripture and sent them packing. She told herself this knowing it was a story, a kindness she was telling herself the way you tell a frightened child that thunder is only clouds talking.

The high sentinel-stone stood at the crest of the pass, a monolith taller than two men, its spirals deep-cut and crusted with a century of lichen. It had never once glowed in Juniper's lifetime. The high stones were the oldest bindings, the first verse of the first song, set before the valley itself was warded — anchors, Maren had called them once, in a rare unguarded moment. *Anchors hold the ship. But child, remember: an anchor is also a chain.*

As Juniper and Wren drew level with it, the stone woke.

Light poured up through the lichen like dawn through water, filling every groove of the ancient carving, and the hum that came off it was not the feeble stutter of the valley stones below. It was deep and full and resonant, a chord rather than a note, and it moved through Juniper's chest and bones and teeth, and Wren gasped — actually gasped, aloud, the first voiced sound Juniper had ever heard her make, a single bright syllable in Old Draconic that meant, Maren had guessed once, *home* or *return* or possibly both at once, because in that dead language the two words had been the same word.

"No," Juniper breathed. "No, no — not now, not *here*—"

But the stone was not answering Wren. Juniper saw that in a heartbeat of horrible clarity. The light pulsed outward, away from them, down the northern face of the fell, leaping from sentinel to sentinel along a line Juniper had never known existed — a chain of high stones marching away into the mountains, kindling one after another like a signal-fire, like a message being shouted from peak to peak in a language made of light:

*She wakes. She wakes. She walks free. She comes home.*

"It's calling ahead of us," Juniper whispered. Her legs had gone weak. She leaned against the burning stone — it was warm, warm as a living body, warm as Wren's fevered skin — and stared down the line of kindling peaks into the vast dark of the ranges beyond. "Or it's calling *to* us. I don't know which is worse."

Far to the east, past the pass, past the tollhouse road, past everything — a light answered. Not a stone. Something vaster. A glow along a distant ridgeline, deep red, deep gold, flickering like a heartbeat seen through skin.

Like a fire that had been waiting a very long time to be given permission to rise.

Wren's hand found Juniper's. Squeezed once. Then, soft and sure, twice.

*Now*, it said. *Together. Now.*
