## 10. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

Cold first.

Cold is the oldest thing Wren knows. Cold before the fire, cold in the cage of her own ribs where the burning lives. Cold now, seeping up through the floorboards of a room that smells of dust and mouse and old rain, and she presses her palms flat against the wood because wood is real, wood does not sing to her, wood only holds her weight and asks nothing back.

The woman — Juniper — moves through the dark like a person who has done this before. She does not light a lamp. She lights nothing. She walks with her feet rolling heel-to-toe, soft as snowfall, and she touches things before she lifts them: the shutter, the latch, the edge of a broken crate. Touching first. Asking permission from objects. Wren decides, in the wordless way she decides everything, that this is a good habit. A careful habit. The kind of habit you learn when the world has teeth.

*Safe,* Juniper had signed on the road, hands shaping the guild-sign for it, the one Wren has watched a hundred times through the long gray hours of walking: *safe, here, rest.* Wren does not know if the word is true. Words are weather to her. They come and go and leave no mark she can trust. But the tollhouse is small and square and its door shuts with a sound like a held breath let out, and that is enough for tonight.

She sits where the wall meets the wall, corner to corner, knees up, arms around them. The bundle of her own body. She has been a bundle since the night the men came with their iron hooks and their singing-that-was-not-singing, since the night her mother pushed her through the gap in the goat-pen wall and said words Wren cannot say anymore even inside her own head, because saying them makes the fire rise, and rising fire means finding, and finding means hooks.

Juniper settles across from her. Not close. Not far. Exactly the distance a cat chooses when it has decided not to be afraid of you but is not yet sure about itself.

Wren watches her in the dark. Dark eyes are the best eyes. In the dark you can see the shape of a person without the danger of their face.

* * *

Sleep comes sideways, the way it always does.

Not sleep — falling. She falls into the dream-place where the wings are. Great wings, wider than the sky they beat against, wide as the valley, wide as the world before the world was cut into pieces and parceled out to men who wrote their names on it. The wings are hers and not hers. They belong to something old that sleeps curled around her heart like a second skeleton, and when it dreams, she dreams with it, and its dreams are made of heat.

In the dream there is a mountain. The mountain breathes. She knows this the way she knows the taste of blood, without learning it, without wanting to. The mountain's breathing is slow, slower than seasons, each exhale a hundred years of wind moving through stone. And the thing inside her leans toward that breathing the way a child leans toward a lit window on a winter night.

*Home,* says the thing inside her, in the language that is not a language, the one that lives under all languages, the one the choir-stones hummed to when she first came into the valley. *Home, home, home.*

She wakes with her mouth open and ash-taste on her tongue and the floorboards under her palm warm as bread.

Juniper is awake too. Of course she is. Wren has learned that Juniper sleeps the way birds sleep — half-anchored, ready to burst upward at any sound. In the moonlight through the broken shutter, the woman's face is pale and watchful, and when she sees Wren sitting up she does not ask *what did you dream*, which everyone always asks, which is the worst question, which is the question the Inquisitor-men asked her mother while holding her mother's chin in an iron glove.

Instead Juniper reaches slowly into her pack and brings out bread. Hard bread, road bread, the kind that fights back. She breaks it in two and sets the larger piece on the floor between them and slides it across the boards with one finger, an offering, a treaty.

Wren takes it. Eats. The eating is loud in the quiet and she does not care. Bread is also real. Bread is the second-oldest thing she knows.

* * *

Morning comes gray through the shutter-slat, one bright line laid across the floor like a blade. Wren puts her hand in it. Warmth gathers in her palm. She turns her hand over and watches dust-motes climb, lazily, upward, against every law of falling, drawn to the small furnace of her skin. She closes her fist and they die.

This is what she is. A place where falling things forget how.

She looks up and finds Juniper watching her do it, and waits for the flinch, the crossing-of-hearts, the iron hook. But Juniper only tilts her head, curious, the way Maren used to look at the choir-stones — with love and fear braided so tight you couldn't find the seam — and then she does something stranger than all of it.

She points at herself. "Ju-ni-per." Two sounds. Three. She points again. "Juniper."

A name. Her name, given freely, handed over like the bread.

Wren knows names. Names were the first thing taken. The men took her mother's name off her mother's mouth and put a number there instead; she heard it happen, heard the number spoken in the flat voice of a ledger. Since then Wren has been *it* and *the child* and *that thing* and once, whispered by a frightened innkeeper's wife, *the little vessel*, a word that slid over her skin like pond water.

But this woman gives her name away like it costs nothing. Like names are bread.

Wren opens her mouth. What comes out is rust. It has been — how long? Seasons. A year. The last common word she spoke was *no*, screamed into her mother's shoulder, and after that her voice shut like a door with the fire behind it, because the fire listens to voices, the fire comes when called, and she would rather be mute forever than be a bell that rings for hunters.

"Reh," she manages. It is almost nothing. It is the whole world.

Juniper's face does a thing Wren has no word for — crumples and blooms at once, grief and joy sharing one expression like two people sharing one coat. "Wren," the woman repeats, gently, giving it back to her polished. "Wren."

Her name. Given by her mother, kept alive in one stranger's mouth, returned to her across a dusty tollhouse floor. Wren feels it land somewhere below her ribs, next to the sleeping fire, and the fire stirs but does not wake, and for one moment she thinks: *maybe it can live beside a name. Maybe names are big enough.*

* * *

They stay through the day because Juniper says the road below the ridge will have riders on it, and Wren understands *riders* the way she understands *hook* and *iron* and *run*: in her spine, before her mind. So they wait, and waiting is its own country, and in the country of waiting Wren begins to learn the shape of the woman.

Juniper hums when she thinks no one hears. Low songs, worn smooth, the guild-songs, and Wren notices that when she hums them forward the stones of the tollhouse stay cold, but last night, half-asleep, she hummed one backward, just a fragment, and the hearthstone cracked with a sound like a knuckle. Wren files this away in the place where she keeps important things: the smell of her mother's hair, the weight of her brother asleep against her side, the exact blue of the door of her house. The backward songs matter. The backward songs are keys.

Juniper talks to her, too. All day, quietly, not expecting answers. Naming things. "Road." "Water." "Tollhouse — this is a tollhouse. People used to pay here, to pass." She lays the words out like coins on a table, and Wren picks them up one at a time and turns them over and pockets the ones that fit.

*Tollhouse.* A house for paying. A house for passing. Wren likes it. Everything in her life has been a toll. Someone has always demanded payment to let her pass: her blood, her silence, her family. This ruined room with its mouse-smell and its blade of light is the first place she has ever stood where the payment was already made, long ago, by strangers, and she can simply be here.

Small mercies. She doesn't know those words yet. She will, later, and when she learns them she will think of this day.

* * *

Afternoon. Juniper dozes sitting up, chin on chest, exhausted from the running and the watching and the carrying of a guilt Wren can smell on her like woodsmoke — the guilt of a person who has just learned her whole life was built on a lie and hasn't yet found where to set it down.

Wren explores.

The tollhouse is one room and a collapsed back wall, and beyond the collapse there is a lean-to, and in the lean-to, under forty years of leaves, a sled. Child-sized. Runners of birchwood, gone silver with weather. Someone loved this sled. Someone painted a bird on its front board, and the paint has faded to a ghost, but Wren can see the wing-shape still, one wing, mid-beat.

She kneels beside it and puts both hands on the board and the memory comes up through the wood like water through roots, because that is what she is, that is the curse and the truth of her: things remember near her, and she remembers things.

A boy. A boy with this sled, sixty years ago, laughing down the hill road, and his mother calling him in to supper, and the supper was barley and onions and the lamp was tallow and the boy grew up and paid tolls here maybe, or collected them, and then he didn't anymore, because everyone doesn't anymore, because everyone ends, and the sled stayed, keeping the shape of his childhood the way a bed keeps the shape of a body.

Wren cries. Not loudly. She has never cried loudly; loudness draws hooks. She cries the way rain starts, a few drops, then more, silently, hopelessly, and she cannot have said why — except that she knows why. The boy got a childhood. He got a hill and a sled and a mother calling him in. He got to be ordinary. Nobody ever looked at him and saw a weapon wearing a child's skin.

She wants that. She wants it so badly the fire answers, the way it always answers want, warming her tears on her cheeks until they steam faintly in the cold air, and she scrubs her face with her sleeve and wills it down, *down, down, lie flat, lie quiet, we are hiding, we are small, we are nothing.*

But the fire heard the crying. The fire remembers crying too.

* * *

Dusk. Juniper wakes and finds her at the sled and reads the whole story off her face, or enough of it, because she crouches down and touches the painted wing with one finger and says, softly, "Someone's boy," and Wren nods, and that nod is the first conversation they have ever had.

Then Juniper does the hand-speech. Slowly, so Wren can follow. *Your family. Where?*

And there it is. The question. The question under every question, the one the Inquisitors wanted answered with irons and the kind ones wanted answered with pity and both kinds wanted it the same way: *give us the story, give us the fire's history, give us the map of you.*

Wren should turn away. Every instinct says turn away, seal the door, be mute forever, be a closed book in a burned library.

But Juniper gave her name like bread. And the sled taught her something today: that memories rot alone under leaves, but remembered out loud, shown to another pair of eyes, they become something else. Not wounds. Not quite. Memorials, maybe. Gravestones need someone to read them.

So Wren does what she cannot say.

She goes to the hearth. Cold ashes there, decades of them. She kneels and with one finger she draws in the ash: a house. Square, with a door. Beside it, smaller shapes — she draws four of them, then hesitates, then adds a fifth, the baby, the one she barely remembers except as warmth against her neck.

Juniper comes close. Kneels. Says nothing. Bless her, bless her forever, she says nothing.

Wren points at the house. Then she takes Juniper's hand — the first time she has touched anyone by choice since her mother — and presses the woman's finger into the ash, and drags it, hard, through the drawing. Through the roof. Diagonal. The way the beam fell.

She draws the house again. Draws the five shapes. Then she mimes: men coming, many, boots — she beats her fists softly on the floorboards, *boots, boots* — and then the hooks, she shows the hooks with her curved fingers, and then she takes the five ash-figures and one by one she smears them flat with her palm until the hearth is only ash again, only gray, only nothing, and her hand keeps moving, smoothing, smoothing, long past the point where anything remains, because her hands will not stop knowing what they know.

When she finally looks up, Juniper is crying without any sound at all, tears sliding straight off her jaw onto the ash, and each tear cuts a tiny clean channel in the gray, and Wren thinks: *good. Let the ash have rivers. Let something move through it.*

"My mother," Wren says.

The words come out broken, crooked, a fence knocked down and rebuilt by a child. But they come out. Common tongue. Her first sentence in over a year, offered up in a ruined tollhouse over a grave drawn in ash.

"My mother. My—" she reaches for it, gropes, finds it. "Brother. Brother small. Baby." Her throat closes. She pushes through. "Fire men. Take fire. Take—" she touches her own chest, where the vessel lives, where the stolen burning coils. "Take me. Only me. Why only me."

It is not a question. It is the question. It is the wound that language has just opened wider, because now she can hold it in words instead of only in fire, and words do not burn, words just bleed.

Juniper takes her hands. Both of them. Ash and all. Holds them the way you hold something that might fly apart.

"I don't know why," she says, and Wren watches her mouth make the shapes, and the shapes are honest, the shapes don't flinch. "I won't pretend I know. But I know this." She presses Wren's ash-gray fingers against her own sternum, over her heart, hard enough to feel the beat. "You are not *only*. You are not a *me*. You are Wren. I am Juniper. And from tonight—" her voice catches, steadies, "—from tonight, you and me. Kin. Chosen. Do you know *kin*?"

Wren doesn't. But she watches Juniper sign it in the guild-hand — *kin, chosen, bound-by-promise-not-by-blood* — and she watches the woman's face above the signs, open as a door, terrified as a door, and Wren understands that this is a toll. That Juniper is paying. That choosing her, choosing the child with the fire in her ribs, will cost this woman everything she has left, and she is paying anyway, here, in ash, in a house for passing through.

Wren nods. Once. Hard.

*Kin.*

Outside, full dark now, and somewhere down the valley a sound threads through the broken shutter — thin, distant, wrong. Not boots. Not hooves.

Singing. Many voices, far off, singing one of the guild-songs — but sung *forward*, sung the binding-way, sung by throats that have no right to know it.

Juniper goes very still. Her hand finds Wren's shoulder in the dark and grips it, and Wren feels the tremor move through the woman's arm into her own bones, and the fire beneath her ribs lifts its head and tastes the air.

"They're not searching the roads," Juniper whispers. "They're singing the valley. Wren — they're going to sing the whole valley awake."
