## 11. The Guild That Wasn't

The waystation announced itself before they saw it: a smell of old lime and pigeon droppings riding the wind down the switchbacks, and then the roofline, sagging like a tired shoulder, black with a century of rain that had never quite managed to bring it down.

Juniper had known it existed. Every Warden did. The Hollow Road was stitched with these places — rest-houses for the singers who once walked circuits between the valley and the outer world, back when the guild still believed its work required walking anywhere at all. Maren had described them in the flat voice she used for history: *stone roofs, iron stoves, beds full of mice. Nothing in them but ghosts.* Juniper had imagined ghosts as a child. She had imagined lanterns and hymnals and the warm residue of devotion.

What she found was a door hanging by one hinge, and beyond it a hall where the floorboards had rotted into a mosaic of soft gray pulp, and light falling through a broken skylight in a single dusty column, thick enough almost to lean against.

Wren stopped on the threshold. The girl had not spoken since the tollhouse — not in Draconic, not in her new handful of common words — but she communicated constantly, in the way weather communicates. Now she tugged Juniper's sleeve twice, their signal for *I don't like this*, and pointed at the column of light.

Juniper looked. Dust motes swarmed in it, yes. But they moved wrong — circling, gathering, dispersing in slow spirals, like ash above a fire that wasn't there.

"It's all right," Juniper said, and was surprised to find she half believed it. "Whatever's in here has been dead longer than we've been alive."

Wren considered this with the grave skepticism of a seven-year-old who had recently learned that things older than you are often the things that eat you. Then she stepped inside anyway, because Wren's courage had never once waited for Juniper's permission, and Juniper followed her into the dust.

* * *

They had been walking for two days.

Two days since the tollhouse, since Wren had shown her — in broken words and gestures and one terrible, patient pantomime — what had been done to her family. Two days since Juniper had slept more than an hour at a stretch, waking at every owl-call convinced it was boot-heels on stone. The Hollow Road ran north through country that grew meaner by the mile: pine giving way to scrub, scrub to scree, the mountains shouldering closer as if the land itself were leaning in to listen for them.

Maren was behind them somewhere, alone in the guildhall with three Inquisitors and a smile she could sharpen into a weapon. Juniper made herself not think about it. She failed roughly every eleven minutes.

Now she picked her way across the rotten floor, testing each board with her toe, and Wren drifted ahead with the uncanny surety of children in ruins — drawn, as always, to the thing Juniper would have avoided. In this case it was a door at the hall's far end, half off its frame, opening onto a small room where a desk stood under a window so furred with grime it glowed rather than let light through.

A desk. In a waystation. Wardens carried nothing on circuit but their songs; Maren had been clear about that too. *We travel light. We travel honest.*

"Travel honest," Juniper murmured, and pulled open the first drawer.

It stuck, shrieked, surrendered. Inside lay a candle stub fused to its own wax, a spoon, and a stack of papers gone the color of weak tea. She lifted them carefully. The top sheet crumbled at the corner, but the writing beneath held — a fine, cramped hand, iron-gall ink faded to brown, dated in a calendar Juniper recognized from her lessons: the old reckoning, the one the Crown had abolished two hundred years ago.

She read it. Then she read it again, because the first reading refused to be true.

*Received of Lord Aldous Vane, of Harrowmere, the sum of forty crowns, being payment in full for the binding of his second daughter's gift, per the arrangement made at midsummer. The verse used is the third of the Quieting. Let it be noted the girl wept throughout, and that the father watched without expression. Payment accepted under seal of the Hollow Choir.*

Under seal of the Hollow Choir.

Juniper stood very still in the glowing dark of that little room, holding a receipt for a child's soul, and felt something in her chest tilt slowly, like furniture going over.

"No," she said aloud. It came out as a whisper.

Wren looked up at her.

* * *

There were more. Of course there were more. Drawers beneath drawers, a strongbox whose lock had rusted to decoration, a loose flagstone behind the desk concealing a leather satchel swollen with paper. Juniper knelt on the cold floor while the light column shifted westward outside, and read her guild's confession in its own handwriting.

Forty crowns, Lord Vane, the Quieting Verse, a weeping daughter.

Sixty crowns, the Countess of Marle, "binding of the son's fire-speech, he having shamed the family with talk of burning." Signed with the looped mark of some Warden whose name meant nothing now and everything once.

An entire ledger, its columns ruled in careful ink: dates, sums, verses sold. The Third of the Quieting. The Fifth of the Damping. One entry simply marked *the Long Hush*, price negotiable, buyer unnamed, and beside it in a different hand, later, angrier: *never again*. And then, three entries down, the same hand selling it anyway.

Juniper read until her eyes burned. She read the way you probe a tooth you already know is bad — unable to stop, needing to know exactly how deep the rot went.

Here was the arithmetic of it, laid out in other people's ink: the Wardens of the Hollow Choir had not only kept their prison-valley and sung their failing songs over buried dragon-fire. They had run a trade. Any noble house with a child who flared too bright, spoke in tongues, dreamed in colors that frightened the neighbors — any such family could buy a verse. Could buy silence. Could buy a daughter made docile, a son made hollow, and the guild would take payment and sing the ninth verse over its own conscience and call it stewardship.

"We were jailers," Juniper said. Maren's word, Maren's confession, from that night by the hearth when the world had first cracked. "But I thought — I thought we were jailers who hated the cell."

Wren had climbed onto the desk chair, which groaned but held, and was watching her with those eyes that sometimes caught the light strangely, amber at the rim even now, even with the fire banked low in her blood. She reached out and touched the ledger with one finger, tracing a line of numbers she couldn't read.

"That's money," Juniper said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "That's what your silence cost. That's what a lot of silences cost."

Wren withdrew her finger. Then, deliberately, she wiped it on her shirt, as if the paper itself were unclean, and Juniper laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it — because the child had understood faster than she had. Had perhaps understood all along. Children always knew which adults were safe. Maybe that was the whole of wisdom, and everything else was paperwork.

* * *

She told herself she would stop at the ledger. She told herself she didn't need the letters.

She needed the letters.

They were bundled in oilcloth at the bottom of the satchel, and the oilcloth had done its work; the pages inside were stiff but legible, correspondence spanning decades, maybe generations. A Warden named Corvessa — the signature bloomed across the page like a thornbush — negotiating terms with a house in the lowlands. Another, Ilset, apologizing for a binding that had "taken imperfectly" and offering a refund or a corrective verse, whichever the family preferred, *as befits the guild's reputation.* A third letter that stopped Juniper's breath: a warning, unsigned, from one Warden to another.

*Brother, I write to say what cannot be said at chapter: we have become merchants of muteness. I have counted nine children quieted this year alone. I have heard the masters say the fees fund the vigil, and I ask — the vigil guards the fire, yes, but who guards us? If the Choir falls someday, let it be recorded that some of us knew, and sang anyway, because the singing paid.*

At the bottom, in a different ink, someone had written a reply that was never sent, or was sent and returned: *Then leave. I will hold your rounds. Say the word.*

And below that, in a third hand, dry as dust: *He stayed. They all stay. The valley is comfortable and the road is hard and conscience is cheapest at home.*

Juniper set the letters down with hands that were not quite steady.

Outside, the wind worried at the broken roof, and somewhere in the walls pigeons muttered their idiot liturgy. Wren had curled up on the desk chair, knees to chest, watching Juniper with the patience of someone who had already survived worse than another person's grief.

"I sang those verses," Juniper said to her. Not to Wren, really. To the room. To the dead correspondent and his unsent reply. "Not the ones for sale. But the same songs. The Quieting, the Damping — we sang them every vespers, we sang them to the stones, and Maren told me they were lullabies. I thought—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "I built my whole life on the idea that we were poor and obscure and good. That the obscurity proved the goodness. Nobody buys what we're selling, so what we do must be clean."

The room said nothing. Ruins are honest that way.

Because here was the truth the dust had been keeping: the guild had never been poor. Forty crowns here, sixty there, the Long Hush priced negotiable — compounded over two centuries, that was a fortune, and where had it gone? Into the guildhall's good slate roof. Into the library she'd loved, the bound books with gilt spines. Into Maren's winter cloak and Juniper's own boots, bought new last autumn from a cobbler in the lowland town the Wardens officially never visited.

She had learned her scales on money taken for silenced children.

Juniper put her face in her hands. She did not cry — she was past crying, or not yet there, some suspended state between — but she breathed, long and ragged, and the dust of the dead Wardens moved in her breath, and she thought: *this is what Maren carries. This is why her hands shake. She didn't just bury a secret about dragons. She buried this.*

When she looked up, Wren had slid off the chair and come around the desk, and was standing close, not touching, waiting — the way she always waited, as if touch were something to be offered and never taken. Juniper opened her arm and the girl came into it, small and warm and smelling of woodsmoke and road dust, and Juniper held her and looked at the ledger lying open on the floor like a wound.

"I don't know what we are now," she told the top of Wren's head. "I knew what a Warden was. Bad as it was, I knew. Oath, silence, vigil, valley. You could hate it honestly. But if the oath was for sale the whole time, then breaking it isn't treason, is it? It's just — leaving a shop."

Wren tilted her head back. Her mouth worked, the way it did when she was hauling a word up from somewhere deep.

"Bad," she said. Carefully. Tasting it. Then, with fierce precision: "You — not bad."

Juniper's throat closed.

"You don't know that," she managed.

Wren frowned, dissatisfied with the theology of it, and tried again. "Stones sing. You — listen." She pressed her small fist against Juniper's sternum, right over the heart, the gesture absolute, a child's syllogism: *the listening one is the good one.* Then she added, with the merciless accuracy of the young, "Old one — sing-down. You — sing-up. Different."

Sing-down. Sing-up. Maren's bindings reversed, the phrase Wren had coined in the tollhouse and that Juniper had adopted without examining why it comforted her. Now she examined it, sitting in the wreckage of her guild's honesty, and understood that Wren had handed her the only ethic left standing. Not the guild. Not the charter, not the seal, not the vigil. Just the direction of the song. Everything in her inheritance had pushed sound down into things — into children, into fire, into the valley's throat. Everything worth keeping in Juniper's own two hands had pulled it up and out.

"You could build a life on that," she said quietly. Wondering. "No charter. No seal. Just — which way the song goes."

The pigeons burst from the eaves all at once, a clatter of wings, and Juniper was on her feet with her knife out before her mind caught up with her body, heart slamming, every nerve singing the old fear-song: *riders, riders, riders.* But it was only the wind, a gust through the broken skylight slamming the hall door, and the birds wheeling out into the gray afternoon, and after a moment she sheathed the knife and made herself laugh at her own jumping heart.

Still. The laugh had no conviction in it. Because the fear was not stupid. Somewhere south of here, Serah Voss was asking questions in her courteous voice, and Maren was answering them or she wasn't, and either answer put riders on this road within a day. The waystation was a landmark; landmarks were where pursuers looked. Juniper had known that when she chose it and chosen it anyway, because Wren needed walls for one night, and because some part of Juniper had wanted — needed — to see this place, to touch the proof with her own hands before she decided what she was without it.

She had seen. She had touched. Decision made, then, or making itself in her whether she consented or not: there would be no rebuilding of the guild at the end of this road, no restoration, no *when this is over we'll return and make it right.* There was nothing to return to. There never had been. What remained was a direction — sing-up — and a child, and whatever ethics Juniper could forge between them on the open road with no institution underneath, no seal, no sanction, nothing but the difference between the song that presses down and the song that lets rise.

She gathered the letters, the ledger, the strongest of the receipts, and wrapped them in the oilcloth. Evidence, she told herself. Against whom, she couldn't say. Perhaps against forgetting. Perhaps someday there would be a court that mattered, or perhaps only children someday, asking what the Wardens were, and Juniper intended to be able to answer without flinching.

They slept that night in the hall under the broken skylight, wrapped in their cloaks near a small fire Juniper built low and shielded, and the stars came down through the shattered roof in profusion, indifferent and enormous. Wren fell asleep against her side mid-sentence, chasing some word she'd nearly gotten. Juniper stayed awake a while, watching the sky turn slowly on its axle through the gap, and let the grief finish its work.

Near dawn she dreamed of the guildhall — not as it was but as the ledgers showed it had always secretly been: a counting-house with hymnals, coins stacked where the choir-stones should stand, and every Warden singing with silver in their mouths. She woke with the taste of metal in her own mouth and the sun not yet risen, and lay listening to the pre-dawn silence of the mountains.

Except it wasn't silent.

Far off to the north — too far for ears, she told herself, this was memory or dread or the fire in Wren's blood resonating through the girl asleep against her ribs — she heard, or felt, hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast. And threaded beneath them, faint as a hairline crack in glass, a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat:

Someone was singing the Third of the Quieting. Riding north along the Hollow Road, singing it as a search-song, sweeping the dark for a stray spark of dragon-fire.

Juniper sat up in the gray light, already reaching for Wren's shoulder to wake her, already calculating miles.

"They found the tollhouse," she whispered to the sleeping child. "Up, little ember. We're past due on the road."
