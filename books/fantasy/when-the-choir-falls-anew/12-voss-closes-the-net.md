## 12. Voss Closes the Net

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old stone, and Serah Voss found it intolerable.

Not because it was unclean — it was immaculate, in the way of places tended by people with nothing else to spend their devotion on — but because it smelled of *waiting*. Of a patience so long-practiced it had become architecture. She had been in a hundred rooms like this across nine provinces: monasteries, charter-houses, archive-vaults where some order or other had convinced itself that stillness was a virtue. They all smelled the same. They all smelled like lies that had been left long enough to set.

She sat at the refectory table with her hands folded and let the silence do its work. Her two riders waited by the door — Halric with his hand resting on his belt in the way she had taught him, ostentatiously casual; young Pell, who had not yet learned to hide how much he wanted to be anywhere else. The third chair across from her stood empty.

Maren had asked for a moment to fetch tea. Voss had said of course. That had been eleven minutes ago, and she counted every one of them, because Maren was counting them too, and the game was in who admitted it first.

When the old woman finally returned, she carried a clay pot and two cups, and she poured with the unhurried grace of someone who had decided exactly how much time she was willing to perform politeness for. Steam rose between them. Voss did not touch her cup. Neither did Maren.

"You have a lovely valley," Voss said. "I mean that sincerely. I've crossed six ranges to get here, and I haven't seen grass this green since I was a girl."

"The rain is kind to us." Maren sat. Her hands settled around her own cup without lifting it. "Though I suspect you didn't ride six ranges to discuss weather."

"No."

"Nor census, I think."

Voss smiled. It was a good smile; she had spent years on it, the way other women spent years on embroidery. "No. Nor census."

* * *

She began, as she always began, with something true.

"My grandmother was a laundress in Corveth," she said. "Washed the shirts of magistrates' clerks until her knuckles split every winter. My mother died of childbed fever when I was four, and my father drank himself into the river two years after, and I was taken up by the Order of the Vigilant Flame because they had a habit of collecting clever orphans the way other charities collect coats." She turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the tea shiver. "You will forgive me if I don't pretend to be a court lady. I'm not one. I'm a foundling with a warrant, and the warrant says I am to find a child."

Maren's face did not move. It was a remarkable face, Voss thought — seamed like old leather, eyes the gray of slate under water. Most people, by now, would have begun the small betrayals: the glance toward the door, the swallow, the telltale stillness of someone calculating distances. Maren gave her nothing but attention, patient as the stones outside.

"And you believe this child came here," Maren said.

"I know she came here. A mud-smeared girl, mute, seen on the Hollow Road three days past by a charcoal-burner who owed me a favor. He described her well enough. What he couldn't describe—" Voss leaned forward slightly, "—was what he heard. He said the child made sounds. Not crying. Sounds like *language*, but not any language he knew. He said it made his teeth ache."

For the first time, something moved behind Maren's eyes. It was very small, very fast, and Voss caught it the way she caught everything — by having spent thirty years learning what fear looked like when it was trying to be polite.

"You've heard such speech yourself," Maren said quietly.

"I have heard recordings of it. Wax cylinders from the war archives, made before anyone understood what they were capturing. There are perhaps forty in existence, and I have listened to all of them more times than I can count." Voss's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "Old Draconic. The pre-kingdom tongue. A language that has not been spoken aloud by a living throat in over a century — because the throats that spoke it were burned out of the world, or bound out of it, depending on which version of history pays your wages."

"And you think a child—"

"I think," Voss said, "that three weeks ago, a family was found dead in the lowlands east of the Serevan hills. Burned. From the inside. No accelerant, no spread pattern, no survivors. And I think that among the ashes, someone had gone to considerable trouble to make a small girl disappear — new name, cut hair, sent running west with whatever coin could buy her passage. Someone knew what she was carrying. Someone was trying to save her." She paused. "Or use her. With children, the two intentions look identical from the outside."

The fire popped in the hearth. Outside, through the leaded window, Voss could see the choir-stones standing in their circle, gray and patient, and beyond them the green slopes rising to the pass. Somewhere in this building, or near it, was a girl of perhaps eight years who held the last dragon-fire in the world inside her blood, and everyone in this room knew it, and no one had yet said it plainly.

Voss decided to say it plainly. It was, she had found, the most violent thing one could do in a polite room.

"Maren. Mother Warden. However you style yourself." She folded her hands again. "I am going to tell you what happens to me if I fail. Not to frighten you — though I will take fright where I can get it — but because I think you are a woman who respects precision, and I am about to ask you for something enormous, and you deserve to know exactly what it costs me to ask."

Maren said nothing. But she turned her cup a quarter-turn, and Voss recognized the gesture for what it was: permission, grudging, to continue.

* * *

"The Order of the Vigilant Flame was founded one hundred and twelve years ago," Voss said, "by decree of King Aldous the Second, in the last year of the Dragon War. You may know the official history. Every schoolchild does. The dragons were mad with age and grief; the great wardens of the old orders sang them down; peace came at terrible cost; we light candles every Remembrance Day and feel solemn about it." She shook her head once. "The truth is less tidy. The truth is that the Crown could not kill the last dragons, and could not bind them either — binding was an art belonging to orders like yours, and yours had been hunted nearly to extinction for reasons I suspect you know better than I. So the Crown did what crowns do. It made a bargain it never intended to keep, and it built an order to watch the bargain rot."

"What bargain?" Maren asked. Her voice was careful.

"The fire was to be sealed away — hidden, dispersed, sunk into bloodlines where no army could dig it out. In exchange, the remnant dragon-kind were granted the eastern ranges and left in peace. And the Order was founded to guarantee both halves of that promise: that the fire stayed buried, and that no one ever dug for it." Voss met the old woman's eyes. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? We were not founded as hunters, Mother. We were founded as *wardens*. The word your guild uses for itself — we wore it first. Our whole purpose was to make sure that what happened to that family in the Serevan hills never happened, and that what I believe happened to that little girl never needed to happen again."

Something flickered across Maren's face then — not fear this time, but a kind of bitter recognition, the look of a woman hearing her own scripture quoted back to her by a stranger.

"And now?" Maren said.

"Now the Crown has remembered the fire exists, and wants it. Badly. There are men in the capital — I won't sully your table with their names — who look at the eastern ranges and see acreage. Who look at dragon-fire and see a weapon that ends wars before they start. The Order's founding charter has been reinterpreted eleven times in my lifetime, each reading a little further from its purpose, and I have signed my name to memos I am ashamed of, because the alternative was to stop being useful, and an order that stops being useful stops existing." Voss's voice remained smooth, but she could feel the thing underneath it, the thing she kept caged on ordinary days. "And if I return without the child — if the fire passes out of Crown reach, into the wild, into the ranges — then the Order has failed at the single task it was created for. Do you know what happens to failed orders, Mother? They are dissolved. Their charters are burned. Their officers are questioned about what they knew and when, and the questioning is not conducted by friends. I have watched it done to the Order of the Salt Road. I read the transcripts. Some of those transcripts were written in my hand."

She let that sit. The fire crackled. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with a sound like a sigh.

"So yes," Voss said softly. "I will find this child. I will take her to the capital, and she will be studied, and tested, and eventually — if the men I answer to have their way — opened, carefully, by people who will call it science right up until it becomes surgery. I know what I am. I have never pretended otherwise, not even to myself. But I want you to understand that I am not doing this from cruelty. I am doing it because the alternative is my death and my order's death, and neither of those deaths buys that girl anything. Mercy doesn't enter into it. Nothing enters into it. That is what service is, when you get down to the bones of it — you keep doing the terrible necessary thing until the day it kills you, and you hope, without evidence, that somewhere in the arithmetic there was a reason."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, wearier, stripped of the pleasant deflection she had worn like armor since Voss rode through the gate.

"You're younger than I expected," the old woman said. "When I imagined the Inquisition coming, I imagined monsters."

"We're all monsters," Voss said. "Some of us just have better tailors." She paused. "Where is she?"

* * *

"She isn't here."

"Maren."

"She isn't *here*," the old woman repeated, and there was something in her voice now that made Voss go very still — not defiance, exactly. Something older than defiance. "Sit back, Inquisitor. Pour yourself the tea you've been refusing for half an hour. Because I'm going to tell you a story, and it's a long one, and it begins fifty years ago in this very room, with a dragon screaming in the mountains and a young woman with a voice like mine making the worst decision of her life."

Voss did not pour the tea. But she sat back, and she signaled Halric with two fingers — *stay, wait* — and she listened.

Maren told her about the ninth verse. About bindings passed through bloodlines like debts through estates. About a guild that called itself singers and had been jailers since before any living member was born. About a fire hidden so deep that even its jailers had begun to believe the lullabies. She spoke for a long time, and Voss let her, because every word was a thread, and threads could be followed, and Voss had built her entire life on the patient following of threads.

But when Maren reached the part about the child — about what had been done to her family, about what slept in her blood — the old woman's hands had begun to tremble, and her eyes had gone bright and terrible, and Serah Voss felt, unwelcome and unwilled, the cold beginnings of something like respect.

Because Maren was not confessing. Voss had heard enough confessions to know the difference. Confessions sought absolution, or mercy, or simply an end. This was something else. This was an old woman laying out the whole rotten inheritance of her life on a refectory table, deliberately, piece by piece — and Voss realized, with a slow cold turn in her stomach, that Maren was telling her all of it for a reason, and that the reason had nothing to do with honesty.

It was a stall. It had been a stall from the first cup of untouched tea. Eleven minutes for the tea. How many for the story?

Voss rose smoothly to her feet. "Halric," she said. "The grounds. Now. Search the chapel, the dormitories, the stone circle. Pell, the outbuildings and the road."

Halric went. Pell went. Maren did not rise, did not protest, only watched Voss with those slate-gray eyes, and there was no triumph in them at all — which frightened Voss more than triumph would have.

"You're too late," Maren said quietly. "Isn't that the shape of it? You told me your story so I'd tell you mine, and now we both know everything, and none of it matters, because the girl is already gone, and the road is dark, and the songs are failing." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Voss had ever seen. "Fifty years I kept the verse. Fifty years. And it comes down to one night and one old woman's voice, and whether it's strong enough to sing one last time."

"Sing what?" Voss asked. "Sing what, Maren?"

But the old woman only closed her eyes, and beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath the buried roots of the valley itself, something vast and long-asleep seemed — for just a heartbeat — to hold its breath.
