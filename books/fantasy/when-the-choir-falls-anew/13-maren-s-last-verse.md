## 13. Maren's Last Verse

The tea had gone cold an hour ago, and Serah Voss still hadn't touched it.

Maren had counted the Inquisitor's small habits the way she once counted the beats of a verse — the way Voss turned her cup so the handle faced precisely east, the way her gloved fingers rested on the table without drumming, the way her eyes never settled anywhere long enough to be accused of staring. Courtesy, Maren thought, was just a cage with the door painted open.

"You've been very patient with us, Warden Halloran," Voss said. "I know how precious patience is to women of your years."

"I've buried three guildmasters and two kings," Maren said. "Patience is the only crop that grows in this valley."

Voss smiled — a thin, well-made thing, like a blade kept oiled. Behind her, two of her riders stood by the guildhall door with the studied idleness of men who were not idle at all. The third was outside, Maren knew, walking the choir-stones. She could feel him there the way you feel a hand pressed against a scar: not pain, exactly, but attention.

"The census," Voss said, "has been thorough. Grain stores. Livestock. Souls." She let the word sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "And yet there is one soul my clerks cannot account for. A child. Small. Dark-haired. Arrived at your door at vespers some nights past."

"This is a sanctuary valley," Maren said. "People arrive. That is rather the point of it."

"People arrive," Voss agreed. "Children arrive. But this child, we believe, arrived *singing*."

The fire in the hearth popped. Maren did not look at it. She had spent fifty years learning not to look at things — at the ninth verse written in her own hand and hidden beneath the floor of her cell, at the truth folded into the guild's founding charter like a knife wrapped in linen, at the shape of what she had done when she was young and certain that mercy and binding were the same word.

She looked at the cold tea instead.

"There are no children singing in this valley," she said. "There hasn't been true song here in a century. You'd know that if your order had ever bothered to read our charter."

"Oh, I've read it." Voss leaned forward, and for the first time something moved behind her courtesy — something hungry, or perhaps only tired. "Every word. Including the parts your founders thought to omit. Do you know what I admire about the Wardens, Warden Halloran? Discipline. You kept a secret for four hundred years. Four hundred years of jailers pretending to be singers, and not one of you broke." Her voice softened into something almost gentle. "Until now. Until a child walked out of the ash country speaking Old Draconic, and every listening post from here to the capital heard the stones hum."

Maren's hands were steady on the table. She had made them steady through practice, the way she had made her voice steady through the verses, the way she had made her heart steady through fifty years of mornings when waking felt like a sentence being read aloud.

She thought of Juniper, then. Of the girl's face when Maren had finally told her the truth — *we were never singers, child, we were locks* — and how Juniper had not raged or wept but had simply gone quiet in the particular way of someone re-counting their whole life with new arithmetic. She thought of Juniper now, wherever she was, running the hollow road with a child who carried the last fire of the last dragons in her blood.

And she thought of the ninth verse.

It lived in her body the way a splinter lives in flesh. Fifty years ago she had sung it forward, kneeling in this same hall beside a dying dragon-woman whose name Maren had never learned because names were the first thing the bindings took. The dragon had been old and burned half to shadow, and she had looked at Maren with eyes like cooling coals and said — in the tongue Maren would spend decades pretending not to understand — *you are stealing the ending of a story.* And Maren, twenty-six years old and full of the guild's certainty, had sung anyway. Had bound the fire into a bloodline. Had called it mercy because the alternative was extinction, and mercy was such a comfortable word for what fear does when it puts on robes.

"We have ways of making old women remember," Voss said quietly. "I would prefer not to use them. Not because I am kind — kindness is expensive and I am on a budget — but because you have earned a gentler evening than most. Tell me where the child has gone, and the guild stands. Your charter, your hall, your stones. All of it protected under Crown seal."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the guild falls, and I begin asking questions in rooms where questions are asked less politely." Voss sat back. "You are the last Warden who knows anything worth knowing. Whatever you choose tonight, you choose alone. That is not a threat. It is simply the shape of the hour."

*You choose alone.*

Maren almost laughed. Alone — as if she hadn't been alone for fifty years, as if the whole guild weren't a machine for manufacturing solitude, each Warden sealed inside her own knowledge like a stone in a wall. She looked at Voss's face, young and disciplined and doomed — because Maren could see it now, the way the old see weather coming — and felt something that was not quite pity and not quite kinship but lived in the neighborhood of both.

This one, too, was a lock. And locks, Maren had learned, always believed they were doors.

"May I ask you something, Inquisitor?" Maren said. "Before you decide what I've chosen?"

Voss inclined her head, gracious as a judge.

"Your order — the Crown's listeners. What were they founded to do? Truly. Not the charter language."

Something flickered across Voss's face, quick as a fish under ice. "To guard against the return of dragon-fire."

"Guard," Maren repeated. "Or hoard?"

The silence stretched long enough for the hearth to pop again, and in that silence Maren understood — fully, finally, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the end of things — that it didn't matter. It didn't matter what Voss's order wanted, or what the Crown wanted, or even what the remnant dragons wanted circling somewhere beyond the mountains like storms deciding whether to make landfall. Every faction wanted the same thing: the fire, held, held, *held*. Bound in a child, bound in a weapon, bound in a law. The whole world had been built on holding, and no one — not the guild, not the Crown, not the dragons themselves — had ever once considered letting go.

Except, perhaps, a girl with mud on her boots and a stubbornness Maren recognized because she had helped raise it.

"Give me a moment," Maren said, rising slowly, joints announcing themselves one by one like bells. "An old woman's bladder keeps its own counsel."

Voss's mouth twitched — she knew it was a lie, and let it stand, because courtesy was a cage with the door painted open, and both of them were standing inside it politely.

* * *

Her cell was exactly as she had left it: narrow, cold, the blanket folded with a soldier's corners, the single window admitting a blade of moonlight that traveled the floor like a slow pendulum. Fifty years in this room. She had memorized its every flaw — the crack in the plaster shaped like a river delta, the loose flagstone by the bed, the hollow in the sill where she had worn the wood smooth with her thumb during all those nights when sleep refused her and the ninth verse sang itself over and over in the dark, forward, always forward, the way a debt is always collected.

She knelt by the bed. Her knees cracked like green branches.

The loose flagstone came up easier than it had any right to. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay the page. Her own handwriting, younger and more confident, the ink browned to the color of dried blood. The ninth verse of the Binding of Hollow Choir — the verse that had taken the last free fire in the world and folded it small enough to hide inside a bloodline, generation after generation, passed down like a curse dressed as an heirloom until it reached a mute child who had never consented to carry anything.

Maren held the page and did not read it. She knew it by heart. That was the cruelty of the guild's training: they made the verses part of you, deeper than memory, down where forgetting couldn't reach. She could sing the ninth verse forward in her sleep, had probably done so, had woken some nights with the taste of ash in her mouth and the certainty that her sleeping self had been practicing its prison-work without her permission.

But the guild had taught her something else too, something the founders had hoped would stay theoretical: every binding is a song, and every song can be sung backward.

No Warden had ever done it. The texts were clear about why. A binding sung forward takes its strength from the singer's years — that was why Wardens bound young and grew old in service, pouring their decades into the lock. Sung backward, the verse gave the years back. All of them. At once. To whatever the binding held.

The founders had called it *the Unmaking*. They had forbidden it in the same breath they recorded it, which told Maren everything about how tempted they had been.

She was seventy-eight years old. She had maybe ten winters left in her, and she had already decided, somewhere in the deep arithmetic of her heart, that she did not want them. Ten more winters of this cell, of these stones, of lying to Inquisitors over cold tea while a child ran through the dark carrying a burden Maren herself had fastened onto her bloodline. Ten more winters of being a lock.

Or one verse, sung true.

Downstairs, faintly, she heard Voss's voice rise — not alarmed yet, but questioning, the tone of a woman checking a lock and finding it rattled. One of the riders must have noticed the empty chair, or the silence from above, or simply grown suspicious of how long an old woman's errand could take. Time, then. Less of it than she'd hoped. There was never more time than you needed; that was the valley's oldest lesson, the one the stones hummed every dusk.

Maren unwrapped the page anyway. Not to read it — to burn it after. Some inheritances should end as smoke.

She climbed the stairs to the choir loft on legs that shook, and she did not hurry, because hurrying was for people who expected to arrive. The loft opened over the great hall where the choir-stones stood in their ancient ring, and through the high windows the moon laid silver across them, and they were humming — softly, brokenly, the way an old man breathes in his sleep. They had been humming since the child arrived. For the first time in a hundred years, the stones remembered what they were for.

They were not cages, she understood now. Or they had not begun as cages. The first Wardens had built them as instruments — resonators, amplifiers, a choir meant to sing *with* the dragons, not against them. The binding had come later, the way most corruptions come: gradually, reasonably, each step defensible, each verse a little tighter, until one day the singers looked up and found they were jailers and could not remember choosing it.

Maren stepped into the center of the ring.

Below, the guildhall door opened. Boots on stone. Voss's voice, calm and rising: "Warden Halloran. This is unwise."

Everything unwise, Maren thought, is also free.

She filled her lungs — and was surprised to find them full, truly full, fuller than they had been in decades, as if the air itself had been waiting for her to ask. She thought of the dragon-woman's cooling-coal eyes. *You are stealing the ending of a story.* Yes. And tonight, at last, she would give the ending back — not the whole story, not the fire itself, that was not hers to give, but the *binding*, the theft, the lock she had forged with her own young voice. She could unmake her own work. That much was hers. That much had always been hers, waiting, like a debt owed in the opposite direction.

She began to sing.

The ninth verse, backward.

It was like walking down a staircase she had only ever climbed. Each note familiar and inverted, each phrase a mirror, and the moment the first inversion left her mouth she felt the years stir in her like birds hearing dawn. The stones flared — not the dull dutiful glow of the rites but a white heat, a joy, a sound like a held breath released after a century. Somewhere below, Voss shouted something, and boots thundered on stairs, and Maren sang louder, because loudness was all the armor she had ever needed.

The first decade went back into the world as warmth. She felt it leave — her hands smoothing, her spine remembering straightness — and with it went the winter of her forty-eighth year, the year the guildmaster died and she took his chair and learned that authority was just loneliness with better furniture.

The second decade went as light. Her hair, white these thirty years, darkened at the roots like ink spilled in water. With it went the gray morning she had found the first gray hair and wept in this loft, alone, for no reason she could name except that grief needs vessels and the body volunteers.

The third decade went as sound. Her voice, cracking and thinned, swelled toward the register of the woman she had been at twenty-six, kneeling before a dying dragon, and with it went — oh, this was the payment that mattered — with it went the lie. The beautiful, reasonable, merciful lie that binding was protection. She felt it leave her chest like a hook leaving flesh, and what rushed into the wound was not peace but *clarity*, brutal and clean, and she sang through it because clarity was the whole point, clarity was the only thing worth dying for.

The stairs. Boots. Voss burst into the hall below, and Maren saw her face turn upward — saw the Inquisitor stop dead at the base of the ring, sword half-drawn and useless, because what do you draw steel against a song?

"Warden—" Voss's voice broke its discipline for the first time. "Stop. You'll kill yourself."

"I know," Maren said, between phrases, and laughed — actually laughed, a young woman's laugh rising out of an old throat mid-transformation, absurd and glorious. "That's rather the point, Inquisitor. Everything has a price. I'm merely paying mine *forward*—"

Backward, she meant. Backward, backward, the verse turning over on itself like a snake swallowing its own tail, and the fourth decade left her in a rush that buckled her knees, and she sang from the floor now, on her knees in the ring of blazing stones, and the fifth decade—

The fifth decade was the dragon's eyes. Cooling coals. *You are stealing the ending of a story.* And Maren, twenty-six again in every fiber, sang the final phrase directly into that memory, into those eyes across fifty years of distance, and said aloud, in Old Draconic, the words she should have said then:

*I was wrong. Fly.*

The last note left her like a bird leaving a cage with the door painted open — no, with the door *open*, truly open, paint and all — and the binding she had woven half a century ago tore along its length with a sound like a mountain sighing, and far away, beyond the mountains, beyond the valleys, something that had been folded small unfolded one seam wider, and a girl running in the dark with a child in her arms might have felt, for one heartbeat, the road grow lighter beneath her feet.

Maren fell.

Not the way the old fall — sideways, apologetic, a slow surrender. She fell the way the young fall: forward, sudden, all at once, as if the earth had asked and she had answered *yes*. The stones caught her, or seemed to; the hum wrapped around her like arms.

Voss reached her first. Maren felt gloved hands turn her over, felt the Inquisitor's composure fracture against what it found — because the face looking up at Serah Voss was not an old woman's face. It was the face of a woman in her twenties, dark-haired, unlined, astonished — the face of everyone Maren had been and none of them, all the borrowed years spent at once, given back to the world in a single inverted verse. A life paid in full, receipt rendered.

"You—" Voss's voice was strange. Younger, suddenly, matching the face below her. "You gave it back. To the binding. Why—"

"Not to the binding." Maren's mouth barely moved now. The words came out slow, each one a coin spent carefully from a nearly empty purse. But she found she was smiling, and the smile was easy, the easiest thing she had done in fifty years. "To the *story*. So it can end properly. Find your order's founding charter, Inquisitor. Read what your masters omitted. You are guarding a prison... and calling it... a treasury..."

The moonlight lay across the choir-stones, and they were singing now — really singing, the old harmonies rising out of them like water finding its level, and Maren realized dimly that they were singing the ninth verse too, backward, all together, finishing what she had started, the instruments remembering their purpose at the exact moment their player set them down.

Free, she thought. The word arrived without weight, without cost, without the fine print the guild had attached to everything. Free. Not the guild's freedom, which had always been a locked room with good lighting. This was the other kind — the kind you can't keep, only enter.

Her last sight was the high window, and the moon in it, and beyond the moon's brightness the faintest smudge of the eastern peaks, where the world went on, unwritten.

Her last thought was of Juniper, and it was not a prayer, because prayers were bindings too, little songs thrown at the future to hold it still. It was simpler than a prayer. It was a wish with no rope on it:

*Let her choose. Let her choose. Let her—*

And then the verse ended, and Maren went with it, and the choir-stones rang on alone in the empty hall like bells after a wedding, like bells after a war, like bells that no longer knew the difference and had decided, at last, that there wasn't one.

* * *

Serah Voss knelt a long time beside the body that was not old.

Her riders waited at the edge of the ring, and she was distantly aware of them waiting, aware of the protocol she was violating — evidence untouched, scene preserved, report filed within the day — but she could not seem to stand. The last note of the stones' song was still moving through the floor, through her knees, through her ribs, and in it she kept hearing the old woman's laugh: a young woman's laugh, rising out of a throat that had paid everything to make it.

*A prison and calling it a treasury.*

She had read the charter. She had thought she'd read all of it. But there were archives beneath the capital, and her order's founding documents were kept under triple seal, and she had never once — not once in nineteen years of service — asked why the seals were needed, or who they were keeping out.

She rose. Her knees ached. Outside, the moon was setting behind the western ridge, and the eastern sky had begun, very faintly, to change.

"Captain," she said. Her voice came out level. She was grateful for that, at least — that the cage of courtesy still held her, even now, even here. "Send a rider to the tollhouse road. And another to the hollow road junction. The fugitives will have gone to ground somewhere between here and the pass."

"Yes, Inquisitor. And the Warden's body?"

Voss looked down at Maren Halloran — at the young face, the easy smile, the hands folded as if in sleep after honest work.

"Leave her," Voss said. "She's finished her report."

She turned toward the door, and paused, and looked back once at the choir-stones, which had fallen silent now, all together, as if listening.

Somewhere east, she thought, a binding had just loosened. She could feel it the way sailors feel weather — a pressure change, a wrongness in the air, or perhaps a rightness so old it registered as wrong. Her order would call it catastrophe. Her executioners would call it failure.

But kneeling there in the dust of the ringing stones, Serah Voss allowed herself, for exactly one breath, to call it what the dying woman's face suggested it was:

A beginning.

Then she straightened her gloves, and went to hunt two fugitives through the dark — one of them carrying the last fire in the world, and the other, if the old woman's faith was worth anything at all, carrying something far more dangerous.

Carrying a choice.
