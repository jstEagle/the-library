## 15. What the Fire Wants

The mountain is warm.

Not warm like a hearth, not warm like Maren's hands in winter when she pressed them over Wren's ears to keep out the wind. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, from the inside, a heat that has a pulse if you put your ear against it. Wren puts her ear against it. The stone hums. Not the choir-stone hum, which was thin and sad and always a little wrong, like a bell with a crack in its throat. This hum is deep and slow and old, and it does not ask anything of her. It only says: *here. I am here. You are here.*

Juniper is saying something. Wren hears the shape of the words more than the words — Juniper's voice has a shape like a river bending, always going around things, always careful. Wren has learned to read the shapes. *Careful. Slow. Hold my hand.* Wren holds it. Juniper's hand is small and calloused and it shakes a little, and Wren pretends not to notice, the way Juniper pretends not to notice when Wren's dreams make her scream.

They walk into the mountain through a crack that is not a crack.

That is the first thing the mountain teaches her: nothing here is what it looks like. The crack is a door. The door is a throat. The throat is a memory. The walls around them are stone and also not stone — when Wren drags her fingers along them she feels scales, vast and cold-smooth, overlapping like the shingles on the tollhouse roof where she spoke her first common word and Juniper cried and pretended she hadn't. The scales are stone now. But stone remembers being scale, the way Wren's body remembers being a vessel, the way her blood remembers being a lamp someone lit without asking her.

Light comes from nowhere. Or from everywhere. The deeper they go, the more the dark stops behaving like dark. It glows at the edges of things, amber and slow, like light through honey, like the last minute of a fire when the flames are gone and the coals are all that's left, still thinking.

*Coals,* says something that is not a voice.

Wren stops walking. Juniper stops too, because Wren's hand has gone rigid, and Juniper has learned to read her shapes too.

*Little coal,* says the not-voice, and it is not in the air, it is not in her ears, it is in the place behind her ribs where the fire lives, in the room inside her that has been locked since before she could walk. *You came home.*

* * *

Wren does not know how to be afraid of it, and that frightens her more than anything.

She knows how to be afraid of boots on gravel. She knows the sound Inquisitor boots make, the particular rhythm of them, four men and a woman walking in step because they have been taught that walking in step is a kind of power. She knows the smell of the red wax they seal doors with. She knows the sound her mother made — no. She does not know that sound. She has never known that sound. It is a room in her she cannot open, and the not-being-able-to-open-it is its own kind of wound, a door bricked over with her standing on the wrong side.

The fire-thing in the mountain is not like that. The fire-thing is like the moment before sleep, when the day's terrors go soft and far away and the blanket is heavy in a good way.

*I have been waiting,* it says. *A hundred years is a long time to be a mountain. I do not recommend it.*

Wren looks up at Juniper, who is looking at the glowing dark with her jaw set the way she sets it when Voss's name is said, and Wren tugs her hand. *It talks,* she tries to say, and what comes out is the broken common she has, four words where forty are needed: "It talks. Here. Inside."

"I know," Juniper whispers, and her voice does the river-bend thing. "I can feel it. Wren — whatever it shows you, whatever it says — it isn't you. Remember that. It isn't you."

But that, Wren thinks, is the thing Juniper does not understand yet, standing there with her guild-songs and her guild-guilt and her guild-love all tangled up in her like yarn a cat has been at for years. The fire-thing *is* her. Has been her since before her. It is the oldest room in her house, the room she was born around, the room her whole small self was built to hold. She is not a girl carrying a coal. She is a coal that dreamed it was a girl, and the dream was so long and so good that she has come to prefer it.

*Shall I show her?* the fire asks. *Shall I show them both what you are for?*

Wren does not answer, because she does not know how, because the question has no yes and no in it, only *when*.

* * *

The mountain shows them.

It does not use words, or not only words. It uses the way Wren's own mind works — images, heat, weight — and it pours these into the hollow until the hollow is full of them, and Juniper gasps beside her because Juniper is seeing them too, pulled into the dream uninvited, a guest at a table set for someone else.

Wren sees: a valley, green, and a river, and dragons in the sky the way she has seen them in dreams — not two, not three, but dozens, wings darkening the sun like a storm deciding where to land. She sees the eastern ranges alive with them, she sees the lowlands, she sees cities she has no names for, and she sees men in Crown red raising things that are not crossbows, tall things, things that drink the fire out of the air and turn it against its makers. She sees a dragon fall. It takes a long time to fall. It falls like a cathedral, like a season, and when it lands the earth flinches and does not stop flinching for a hundred years.

She sees the other ending.

She sees the fire go to the Crown instead — Voss's gloved hands, Voss's courteous smile, the child Wren in a stone room with instruments that have no mercy in their design, and the fire drawn out of her drop by drop, fed into engines, into weapons, into the tall drinking things, until the Crown can burn whatever it decides is an enemy, and it decides often, and it is never wrong in its own hearing, and the smoke over the lowlands does not clear in her lifetime or Juniper's or anyone's.

She sees a third thing, and this one has no shape at all, only a vast and gentle dark, like the inside of the mountain, like sleep, like forgetting.

*There,* says the fire, and its voice has gone quiet in a way that hurts worse than loudness. *There is what they will do with you, little coal. There is what you are *for*, in the hands of men. And there is the door out.*

*The door out?* Wren does not say it. She does not have to. The mountain hears the question in her blood before her blood has finished asking it.

*You are tired,* the fire says, and oh, it knows, it knows the way only an old thing knows, the way Maren knew, the way no one else has ever bothered to look closely enough to see. *You are nine years old and you have carried a war since before you had words for it. You have been hunted since you could crawl. You have never once been only a child. And I can give that back to you.*

The dark gathers, warm, close, kind. It is the kindest thing anyone has ever offered her, and that is the terrible part, the part that makes her chest ache, because it is kindness and it is a knife and it is both without any contradiction at all.

*Give me back,* the fire says. *Let me go into the stone, into the sleep, into the long dark where no one can reach either of us. And you will be Wren. Only Wren. No fire, no hunting, no dreams of burning wings. You will forget the boots and the wax and the red room. You will forget your mother's voice — * and here the fire is gentle, so gentle, gentler than Juniper, gentler than anyone — *you will forget that you ever lost her. You will wake up ordinary, and the world will be large and safe and stupid, and you will live in it, and you will be happy. I promise you. I am old enough to keep one promise.*

* * *

Juniper is crying.

Wren comes back to herself — to the hollow, to the honey-light, to Juniper's hand crushing hers — and finds Juniper on her knees in the warm stone, tears running freely down her face, and Wren understands that Juniper saw it all too, saw the war and the engines and the third door, and that the third door is the one Juniper is weeping at, because Juniper, Wren realizes, has been carrying a different weight this whole time: the weight of knowing what she is asking. Every mile of the Hollow Road, every cold camp, every hour of hiding, Juniper has been asking a nine-year-old to stay a vessel. To stay the thing the war needs. To keep carrying it because someone must, and because Juniper could not carry it herself.

"I saw it," Juniper says, and her voice is wrecked, river-bend collapsed into flood. "Wren, I saw what it offered you. And I need you to know — I need you to hear me — you don't owe me anything. You don't owe the world anything. If you want to give it up, if you want to be free of it, I will stand at that door and I will let you go and I will not think one single smaller thing of you. Do you hear me? Whatever you choose, I—"

Wren lets go of her hand.

She walks forward, into the honey-dark, toward the place where the warmth is deepest, and behind her she hears Juniper make a small sound like something breaking, and she is sorry for that, she is sorry, but she needs to see it. She needs to stand at the door before she decides about the door.

The fire rises to meet her.

It is not a shape. It is everything a shape was before shapes were invented. But Wren, who has dreamed in images all her life, who thinks in heat and weight and color because words were taken from her before she could stockpile them, Wren can see it the way no one else in the world can see it, and what she sees is this: a dragon the size of the sky, folded so small, folded for so long, its wings wrapped around itself like a child hiding from a storm that never ends. It has been folded for a hundred years. It has been patient for a hundred years. And it is so tired. It is tired the way she is tired. It is the only creature in all the world that is tired exactly the way she is tired.

*Well?* it asks, and its voice is very small now, small as hers. *Little coal. Shall we both sleep?*

And Wren — Wren who has no words for this, who has never had words, who has spent her whole life with the important things locked behind a door she couldn't open — Wren reaches for the only language she has ever owned. She does not speak. She *remembers*.

She remembers her mother's hands. Not her face — her face is behind the brick, behind the door, in the room that hurts — but her hands, flour on them, warm from the oven, pressing a cup of milk into Wren's small fingers. She remembers her father's whistle, two notes, the signal that meant *come home, it's supper, you're safe.* She remembers the smell of their house, bread and woodsmoke and the particular smell of her own blanket, and she remembers that she had a name before Wren, a name in her mother's mouth, and that the name is behind the door too, and that if she drinks the kind dark and forgets, she will forget that she ever had a mother at all. Not that she lost her. That she *had* one. That anyone ever whistled for her. That she was ever worth coming home to.

She holds all of it up to the dark, the way you hold a candle up to show someone the shape of a room.

And the fire — the vast, the folded, the patient — goes very still.

*Oh,* it says. *Oh, little coal.*

*They didn't just take the fire,* Wren shows it, pouring the memory into it the way it poured its visions into her, the only gift she has. *They took the remembering. That's what forgetting is. It's not rest. It's them winning twice.*

The dark does not answer for a long time. The honey-light dims and brightens, dims and brightens, like breathing. Somewhere behind her, Juniper has gotten to her feet, and Wren can feel her there, at the edge of the dream, not pulling, not pushing, just *there*, the way she has been there since the night of the knock at Vespers, since the stones hummed, since the beginning of the only part of Wren's life that anyone asked her about.

Then the fire speaks again, and its voice has changed. There is something in it now that was not there before, and Wren takes a moment to know it, and when she knows it she almost falls down, because it is the same thing that was in her mother's hands, the same thing in Juniper's crooked little songs.

It is hope. Old, rusted, disbelieving hope, waking up.

*You would keep it,* the fire says. *The burden. The war. The memory. You would keep all of it, and stay a child anyway.*

Wren nods. It is the first time in her life she has ever been sure of anything.

And that is when the light at the mouth of the hollow changes — when the honey-glow goes white and hard, when the warm stone under her feet goes cold at the edges, when Juniper's hand closes on her shoulder like iron — and Wren turns, and far down the throat of the mountain, small and bright and terrible as a star falling the wrong way, torchlight is coming.

Someone has followed them in.

Someone is already inside.
