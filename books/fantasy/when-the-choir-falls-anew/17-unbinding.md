## 17. Unbinding

*Juniper*

The hollow breathed.

That was the only word for it. The air inside the mountain's chest moved in slow, tidal swells, warm as exhalation, and every breath carried a scent Juniper had no name for — something older than smoke, older than stone. Petrified ribs arched overhead like the vaults of a cathedral no god had sanctioned, and between them hung the residue of a life: not ash, not dust, but *attention*. The first dragon had been dead for a thousand years, and it was still watching.

Juniper stood in the center of the hollow with Wren's small hand in hers. The girl's palm was fever-hot. It had been fever-hot since the pass, since the fire under her skin had felt the mountain feel it back, and Juniper had stopped pretending she couldn't feel it through her own fingers — a second pulse, deeper and slower, keeping time with nothing human.

Behind them, at the mouth of the hollow, Serah Voss stood with her hands folded and her warrant crumpled somewhere in the pocket of a coat that had seen better decades. She had not drawn a weapon. Juniper kept expecting her to. The not-drawing was worse.

"You understand," Voss had said, an hour ago, in a voice stripped of all its courtroom silk, "that if you do this, there is no going back. Not for you. Not for the guild. Not for the Crown's patience."

"There's no going back anyway," Juniper had answered. "That's what no one in the valley ever understood. We thought stillness was safety. It was just the slowest way to die."

Now she looked down at Wren. The child was looking up at the ribs, and her eyes had gone the color of coals seen through a closed stove door — brown, but lit from behind.

"Wren." Juniper crouched, bringing them level. "You don't have to do this. I need you to know that. If you want to give it up — if you want to be ordinary, to forget, to be just a girl who grows up somewhere quiet — the mountain said it would take it. It would take all of it. No one would ever hunt you again."

Wren's jaw worked. She had words now — broken, stubborn, hard-won words, gathered like river stones over the weeks of their flight — and she spent them carefully, the way she did everything.

"You said," she rasped, "the fire isn't mine. That it was *put* in me. Like — like a stone in a shoe."

"Yes."

"Then I'm not giving anything up." Her chin lifted, and for a moment Juniper saw not a child but something vast wearing a child's face with great patience. "I'm giving it *back*."

Juniper's throat closed. She nodded, because speech had abandoned her, and stood, and turned to face the hollow's heart.

The guild's songs. Twenty-two of them — she'd counted them a hundred times on her rounds, humming each into its stone, feeling them settle like sediment. Maren had taught her that they were lullabies. Maren had confessed, with fifty years of guilt in her mouth, that they were bindings: each verse a knot, each knot a year, each song a wall around a fire that had never consented to be caged. And Maren had shown her, in the last terrible lesson of her life, how a song sung backward becomes an unmaking.

Juniper had never sung them backward. No Warden had. It was the one heresy the guild had never even bothered to forbid, because no one had ever imagined a Warden would want to.

She closed her eyes. Found the first note the way Maren had taught her — not in the throat, but in the floor of the breath, down where the body keeps its oldest memories.

And she began to sing the choir in reverse.

* * *

*Wren*

The sound went through Wren like light through water.

It was not a song, not really, not the way the mute-women in her memory had hummed to her before the soldiers came, before the word *vessel* became the only word that mattered. It was a song being *unsaid*. Every note Juniper made was a note she had made before, somewhere, in some stone, in some year Wren had never lived — and hearing it unwound was like watching a rope fall apart into thread, thread into fiber, fiber into the idea of softness.

Inside her, the fire *answered*.

It had always answered. That was the horror of her whole life — the way it leapt to every call, the way it warmed the stones, the way it bloomed the out-of-season flowers and heated the well water and made the Inquisitors come with their polite boots and their polite questions. It answered everything except her. It had never once asked her what she wanted.

But this — this unraveling song — it did not answer. It *rose*.

Wren felt it come up through her like water finding its level, like the moment a held breath finally breaks. It did not hurt. That was the strangest part. She had spent her whole life braced for the hurting — the burning of the dreams, the burning wings, the burning family whose names she was only now learning to hold without dropping — and the fire, rising, was gentle. It moved through her chest and down her arms and out through her open hands the way warmth moves back into a room when a door is finally opened.

The ribs of the dead dragon began to glow.

Not red. Not gold. A color Wren had no word for, because no one alive had seen it in a thousand years — a color like the memory of color, like the first time anyone ever looked at a sunset and understood it was going away. The glow climbed the arching bones and pooled in the vault of them, and the hollow filled with a light that had weight, that pressed on her skin like a hand, that *knew* her.

And in that light she saw them. Not the dream — the dream was smoke, the dream was always smoke. This was clear as a window: her mother's hands, flour to the wrists. Her brother's laugh, the gap where his tooth had been. Her grandmother singing the binding-song over a cradle, weeping as she did it, because someone had to and because mercy and cruelty had long ago learned to wear each other's clothes.

They had names. She had never been allowed to know they had names.

"Sera," Wren said, and the fire carried her voice up into the glowing dark. "Tomas. Ilse Almeira Vess. That's — that's me. That's who it was put *in*."

The light shuddered. Somewhere in the mountain, something ancient turned its attention toward her like a sunflower turning toward morning.

*Yes*, said the hollow, in a voice that was not a voice. *Yes. That is who you are. Give it home, little vessel. Give it back to the sky.*

* * *

*Voss*

Serah Voss had prepared herself for many outcomes.

She had prepared for the child to refuse, and for the negotiation to collapse into the ugly arithmetic she'd been trained in since she was nineteen years old. She had prepared for the Warden to beg. She had prepared for the mountain to kill them all, for the fire to consume the vessel, for the Inquisitors' reinforcements — two days behind her, following her marked trail, because she had marked it deliberately, because a part of her had wanted witnesses to whatever she chose — to arrive too late for anything but a report.

She had not prepared for the singing.

The Warden's voice was not remarkable. That was what undid her. Voss had expected — had *needed* to expect — something theatrical, something that justified the machinery of the Crown, the burnings, the quiet disappearances, the whole edifice of the Inquisition built on the premise that what was being contained was too terrible to be trusted to the world. And instead a tired young woman stood in the ribs of a god's corpse and sang, badly, in a voice roughened by weeks of flight, and the mountain *lit*.

The light found Voss where she stood and did not burn her. It went through her coat, through her skin, through the scar on her collarbone she had never explained to anyone, and it found — she understood this with the helpless clarity of a drowning woman — the oath.

Every Inquisitor carried one. Not the written oath, the one signed in the presence of the Lord Inquisitor with its wax and its witnesses. The other one. The binding laid into the blood of the order's founding, nine hundred years ago, when the first Inquisitors had sworn to *guard* the dragon-fire and had very quickly, very quietly, amended the word *guard* to mean *possess*. Voss had felt the oath all her life as a kind of gravity, a pull toward every rumor of flame, every whispered vessel, every child who spoke in tongues. She had thought it was conviction.

It was a leash. And it was fraying.

She felt it go — strand by strand, with every backward verse, the founding magic of her order coming apart in her blood like a knot in wet rope — and with it went the thing she had been most afraid of, which was not death. It was the discovery that she had never once, in thirty years of service, made a free choice.

Her knees gave. She caught herself against a rib the size of a ship's mast, and the stone was warm under her palms, warm as a living flank, and she did not let go.

"Voss." The Warden's voice, cracked, still singing between the words. "You should go. If you stay — if you're seen to stay —"

"I have been seen to stay," Voss said, "since the moment I walked into this mountain alone." She laughed, and it came out wrong, and she let it. "Do you know what they do to an Inquisitor who loses the fire? There is a room under the Citadel in Corvane. There is a chair in the room. I have signed the warrants for three colleagues to sit in it." She looked up at the burning vault of the ribs, at the small figure of the child standing in the light with her arms open, and something in her chest that had been clenched for thirty years finally, painfully, unclenched. "I would rather be free in a chair than leashed on my feet. Sing, Warden. Sing the whole damned choir down."

And then, because treason, once chosen, is a very simple country to live in, Serah Voss opened her mouth and joined the song.

She did not know the verses. It did not matter. The mountain taught her — or the fire did, or the fraying oath did, pouring its own unmaking through her the way a wound pours blood — and her voice, low and trained and utterly unafraid, wove under the Warden's like a second current in the same river.

The hollow filled with two voices and one silence, and the silence was the child, and the child was burning, and it was the most beautiful thing Voss had ever helped destroy.

* * *

*Juniper*

She felt the valley break before she heard it.

It came up through the floor of the hollow — a shudder, deep and total, like the relief of a held breath released after a hundred years. The dampening field. The sanctuary. The thing her guild had built and tended and called *peace*, the great woven silence that had kept magic out of the valley and the valley out of the world, the cage that had also been her home, her whole life, every stone she had ever hummed to —

It tore.

Not all at once. It went the way ice goes off a lake in spring: a first crack like a rifle shot, then a long groaning, then a hundred small detonations racing away into the dark, and Juniper felt each one as a snapping thread in her own chest, because she had been part of the weave. Her songs were in it. Her twenty-two verses, her daily rounds, her mentor's dying instruction — all of it had been thread, and all the thread was coming apart at once, and it did not feel like loss.

That was the terrible part. It did not feel like loss. It felt like *waking*.

The light in the hollow went from glow to blaze. Wren stood in the center of it with her arms flung wide and her head thrown back and she was not screaming — Juniper had braced for screaming — she was *laughing*, a child's laugh, ragged and astonished, and the fire poured out of her in a column that did not consume her because it had never wanted to consume her, because it had only ever wanted *out*.

It went up through the vault of ribs and through the stone above and Juniper felt it pass out of the mountain like a name being spoken after a thousand years of silence.

And then the world answered.

Everywhere. All at once. Juniper felt it through the soles of her feet, through her teeth, through the fine bones of her inner ears: magic flooding back into a world that had been dammed and drained for centuries, pouring through the broken weave of the valley's silence like water through a shattered weir. She felt the choir-stones down in the valley wake and *sing*, unbidden, every one of them, a hundred voices raised in a song no one was conducting. She felt the flowers in the high meadows open out of season, all of them, all at once, a riot of color under whatever stars were over the pass. She felt the well water run warm and then *sing* and then, she was almost certain, laugh.

The last verse left her. She sang it backward to its end and the end was a single note, high and clean, and she held it as long as her lungs allowed, and when it broke, everything broke with it.

The silence in the valley — the true silence, the founding silence, the silence that had been the guild's whole purpose and prison — ended.

In its place: sound. Vast, layered, alive. Wind that carried voices. Stone that remembered. A mountain exhaling a thousand years of held breath. And underneath it all, rising from the east, from the far ranges where the world's old blood still slept in its old beds — a sound Juniper had only ever heard in the guild's oldest, forbidden records, described by a Warden three hundred years dead in a hand that shook:

Dragonsong.

Not one voice. Many. Distant. Waking.

Juniper sank to her knees in the light. Her left ear had gone silent — she would discover later that it would never come back, that the unbinding had taken it as its tithe, one small coin from a very large purse — and through her right ear the world roared like a sea.

Wren collapsed into her arms, ordinary suddenly, human suddenly, a girl of eight years and forty pounds, sobbing and laughing and saying names — *Sera, Tomas, Ilse* — over and over like a rosary, like a spell, like the first verse of something.

Behind them, Serah Voss stood in the dying glow with tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face, and she was not hiding them, and Juniper understood that the Inquisitor had died in this hollow along with everything else, and that what stood there now was something that had no name yet, and no warrant, and no order left in the world to belong to.

The mountain's ribs went dark. Gently, the way embers go dark: not extinguished, but *resting*.

And far to the east, faint but unmistakable, the dragonsong rose one note higher.

* * *

*Juniper*

They came out of the mountain into a night that had been remade.

The stars were wrong — or rather, they were *right*, brighter than Juniper had ever seen them, because the valley's silence had dimmed more than magic; it had dimmed everything, a gauze over the whole world, and the gauze was gone. The air tasted of ozone and blossom. Somewhere below, in the valley she would never again be able to call home, the choir-stones were singing to each other across the broken fields, and the sound reached the pass like bells across water.

Wren's hand found hers. Small. Cool now, for the first time since the pass. Ordinary.

"Juniper." The girl's voice, rough with weeping, steady underneath. "What happens now?"

Juniper looked east, where the horizon held a glow that was not dawn — too red, too early, too alive. She thought of Maren, who had died singing so that this could be sung. She thought of the guild, of the oath, of the valley that forgets, which would forget no longer, and would have to remember everything. She thought of Voss, standing apart from them at the edge of the pass, looking east too, with the expression of a woman reading her own death warrant and finding, in the fine print, a strange and terrible freedom.

She thought of the war everyone said would come now. The war she had been told, her whole life, was the only alternative to the silence.

"Everything," Juniper said. "Everything happens now. And no one — not the Crown, not the remnant, not any guild or order or oath — gets to decide it alone ever again." She squeezed Wren's hand. "We go down. We tell the truth. All of it. To whoever will listen."

The glow in the east brightened. And then, unmistakably, impossibly, a shape rose against it — vast, winged, trailing fire like a banner — and the first free dragon in a thousand years turned its head toward the mountain where its elder slept, and sang.

The song rolled over the pass and through the broken valley and into the chests of three people standing in the ruins of an old world's silence.

Wren squeezed back. "Then we'd better start walking."

They did.
