## 18. When the Choir Falls

They had made it perhaps a mile down the western face when the world began to come apart.

Juniper felt it first in her teeth — a low, grinding hum, like the note a tuning fork keeps after you've struck it against something that should not have been struck. She stopped on the switchback trail, Wren's small hand still folded in hers, and looked back the way they had come.

The pass was glowing.

Not with dawn — dawn was hours off, and it had come from the east anyway, from the ranges where the new fire was rising. This glow came from below them, from the valley itself, seeping up through the soil like light through the skin of a lantern. Every hollow and fold of the land she had walked her whole life was suddenly legible, lit from underneath, as though someone had lifted the valley's floor to see what had been written there.

"Oh," Juniper said. It was all she could manage. "Oh, Maren."

The choir-stones were singing.

She heard them before she understood what she was hearing — a sound she had known since infancy rendered strange by context, the way a familiar room becomes unfamiliar when seen from outside its window. All ninety-one stones across the valley floor, each one tended by hand for nine hundred years, each one silent in the way stones are silent, were now sounding together. Not the binding-verse. Not any verse the Wardens had ever sung. Something older, something that had been held under the songs the way a river is held under ice, and now the ice was going, and the river remembered which way it ran.

"It's the valley," Wren said beside her. Her voice was thin and ordinary and human, and Juniper clung to the ordinariness of it. "Juniper. It's the *valley*. Listen."

"I'm listening."

"No." Wren tugged her sleeve, and her eyes were wide and wet and terribly awake. "Listen to what it's *saying*."

And Juniper listened, and understood, and wished with her whole body that she hadn't.

The stones were not singing a song. They were singing a *name* — or rather, they had been holding a name down for nine centuries, pressing it flat beneath the ninth verse and the eighth and all the verses before, and now that the binding was broken the name was rising up out of the ground like a drowned thing surfacing, unfolding, taking up the space it had always occupied. The valley had never been empty ground. The valley had been a *held breath*. And every stone, every rite, every generation of Wardens kneeling in the frost at vespers and matins had been part of the hand pressed over a mouth.

"We lived on top of it," Juniper whispered. "My whole life. We *sang to it*."

The glow beneath the valley brightened. Somewhere far below — deeper than cellars, deeper than wells, deeper than anything the guild's oldest maps admitted to — something exhaled.

Then the first stone broke.

It was the Third Stone, the one at the head of the processional path, the one Juniper had polished every morning of her apprenticeship until she knew its grain better than her own palm. Even at this distance she saw it happen: a single clean crack splitting it top to bottom, a flash of white light along the fracture like a struck match, and then the stone simply *came apart*, folding outward into dust and shards and a sound —

— a sound that arrived a heartbeat later and took her left ear with it.

There was no warning. One moment the world was full; the next, the left half of it was gone, replaced by a high thin whine that seemed to live inside her skull rather than enter it. She staggered. Her hand flew to the side of her head and came away dry, unmarked, and somehow that was worse — no wound, no blood, just an absence, a door in herself quietly closed forever.

Wren was shouting something at her. Juniper watched the girl's mouth move, watched her face crumple with alarm, and caught only fragments through her remaining ear, warped and distant: "*— Juniper — your ear — can you hear me —*"

"One side," Juniper said, and heard how loud she was, too loud, the volume of someone who cannot calibrate a silence anymore. "I can hear on one side. I'm all right. Keep moving — we need to be higher before—"

Before what, she didn't know. Before the rest of it came down. Because the Third Stone had not broken alone; down the length of the valley, in a slow wave rolling north to south like a wind made visible, the other stones were going. The Eleventh. The Fortieth. The pair at the ford that travelers used to mark the safe crossing. Each one cracked, flared, and collapsed into gravel, and each collapse sent its report rolling up the mountainsides, and the reports began to overlap and merge until the whole valley rang like the inside of a bell.

And under the ringing, the name finished surfacing.

Juniper did not hear it so much as feel it arrive in her chest, in the soles of her feet, in the roots of her teeth. It was not a word in any language. It was the shape of a presence — vast, patient, immeasurably old, and *leaving*. That was the thing she would carry the rest of her life: not terror, but the unmistakable sensation of something enormous turning away. The first dragon's remnant, the consciousness sunk into the mountain and the valley both, the elder whose petrified body they had stood inside — it had waited out nine hundred years of lullabies, and now, released, it did not rage. It simply rose, gathered itself, and went, pouring upward and eastward in a column of pale gold light that Juniper saw reflected in Wren's tear-filled eyes even as her ruined ear heard nothing of it at all.

The light passed over them like warm water. Then it was gone, and the valley was only a valley.

For about four seconds.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, and Juniper spent years afterward trying to slow it down in memory, and never quite could.

The guildhall went first. From this height she could see the whole roofline she had grown up beneath — the slate tiles she'd helped re-lay at fourteen, the bell-cote where Maren rang the hours, the deep eaves under which the apprentices dried their boots in winter. The building did not burn and did not shatter. It *sank*, gently, the way a boat settles when it finally takes the water it has been refusing, the walls folding inward with a groan that reached her good ear as almost tender, almost apologetic. Dust bloomed up around it in a soft grey cloud, and when the cloud thinned, there was no hall. There was a depression in the earth, shaped like a foundation, already filling with the pale gold light that poured from everywhere and nowhere now that the binding that had dammed it was dead.

The orchard followed. Nine hundred years of trees that had fruited exactly on schedule, never early, never late, obedient to the valley's enforced stillness — they erupted into blossom all at once, in the dark, out of season, flowers tearing open so fast they shed petals in drifts that the updraft caught and carried spinning into the sky. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was also the most violent thing Juniper had ever seen, spring happening in the space of a minute, life compressed and released like a fist opening after decades clenched.

"The well," Wren said hoarsely. "Juniper, look at the well."

The old well by the eastern wall — the one that had run warm two weeks ago, the first sign either of them had ignored because ignoring was what the valley taught — burst its casing in a geyser of steam and silver light, water climbing thirty feet into the air and hanging there, suspended, glittering, before falling as rain that smelled of lightning and deep stone.

And then the silence came down.

That was the part that undid her. Not the noise — the noise she could have borne, the cracking and the groaning and the bell-roar of a world unstoppering itself. But when the last stone fell (the Ninety-First, the little one behind the kitchen garden where she'd taught the youngest apprentices their scales) the sound cut off all at once, everywhere, completely, and what rushed into its place was a silence unlike any the valley had ever held. For nine hundred years the valley's silence had been a *held* silence, a silence maintained, policed, sung into place. This was nothing held at all. This was the silence of an ordinary place at night — crickets somewhere near the ford, wind in the blossoming trees, water finding its old channels. A silence with living things in it. A silence that belonged to itself.

Her home had died, and in dying had become merely a landscape.

Juniper sat down on the trail without deciding to. Her legs simply ended their argument with gravity. The stone under her was cold and real and utterly indifferent, and she put her face in her hands and wept the way she had not wept since she was six years old and Maren had found her crying over a dead wren in the herb garden — ugly, graceless, whole-body grief with no dignity in it anywhere.

Because here was the truth she could not argue with, the truth that had been waiting for her at the bottom of every choice she'd made since Voss first rode the ridgeline: *you cannot save a thing and keep it.* She had known it when she chose the third path. She had known it singing the verses backward while Wren opened the vessel of her blood. She had known it, and she had chosen anyway, and knowing had not prepared her for the specific weight of watching the guildhall — Maren's chair, the ledger with its nine hundred years of names, the chalk marks on the practice-room wall where Juniper's own hand had copied out the fifth verse at age eight — sink into the earth like a coin dropped into deep water.

Maren had stayed behind to buy them time. Maren had sung the ninth verse backward and paid for it with fifty years of her life in a handful of minutes, and had died free of the guild in a way Juniper was only beginning to understand. And now the guild itself was gone, and Maren's sacrifice and Juniper's choice had landed in the same place: a depression in the earth, filling with light.

"You saved us," Wren said.

Juniper shook her head against her hands.

"You *saved* us." The girl knelt in front of her, and her voice cracked down the middle. "It's gone and I'm — I'm *here*. Do you understand? Yesterday I was a vessel. My whole family were vessels, that's all we were, that's all anyone let us be, and now—" She stopped. Her breath hitched. When she spoke again it was barely above the wind. "Now I'm just a girl sitting on a cold hill in the dark, and my ears work, and my heart is only mine, and it hurts so much, and it's *mine*, Juniper. The hurt is mine. Everything is mine."

Juniper lifted her head. In the light still seeping from the broken valley, Wren's face was streaked with tears and absolutely alive, and Juniper saw it plainly for the first time: not the vessel, not the child who spoke Old Draconic in her sleep, not the last ember of a war older than kingdoms. Just a girl. Ordinary. Weeping. Human.

"That's the whole point," Juniper said, and her voice came out wrecked. "That's everything. That's the entire point."

Wren fell into her, and Juniper held her, and they stayed like that on the cold switchback while their home finished becoming a memory below them — the orchard raining petals, the well-water falling back into channels it hadn't touched in centuries, the foundation-shaped hollow glowing softly like a hearth in an empty house.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Juniper noticed that the ringing in her left ear had faded to a faint sea-sound, and that beyond it, in the silence where the valley's song used to live, she could hear — very far off, very faint, from the eastern ranges — the new dragon still singing.

Not a binding. Not a lament. Something with no translation yet.

A beginning.

* * *

They descended the rest of the way in the grey hour before dawn, when the light coming off the ruins had softened to embers and the petals had begun to settle on the paths like snow that refused to melt.

Juniper walked slowly. Partly for Wren, whose legs were short and whose body, freed of the fire, kept betraying her — stumbling, shivering, hungry in a way Juniper suspected she had never been permitted to be. But mostly because she wanted to see all of it. If she was going to lose her home entirely, she would at least refuse to lose it carelessly. She catalogued as they went: the ford, running clear and cold and slightly luminous, the way water probably ran everywhere magic had returned. The herb garden, gone riotous, plants flowering and seeding and flowering again in the same bed. The road down the center of the valley, split in places by pale veins of new-grown stone that gleamed like the inside of shells.

At the site of the guildhall, she stopped.

The depression was shallow now, the light within it dimming as whatever had flooded the world settled into soil and root and river. But at its center, untouched, sat Maren's chair — ironwood, older than Maren, older than Maren's mentor, hauled out onto the hall's threshold every summer evening because the old woman liked to take the air. It had not burned. It had not sunk. It sat in the middle of the ruin with a fine dusting of ash across its seat, upright, patient, absurd, like a mourner who had arrived early and intended to stay.

Juniper went to it. She brushed the ash away with her sleeve, and in doing so her fingers found something wedged in the join of the armrest — a scrap of paper, folded small, tucked there in the way Maren tucked things, which was to say in plain sight where no one thought to look.

Her heart slammed once, hard.

She unfolded it with hands that would not quite obey. The handwriting was Maren's, cramped and slanting, the hand of a woman writing fast and knowing it:

*If you're reading this, the valley sang its last verse, and I am glad of it, and I am sorry for it, and both of those are true, which is the whole of what I know about anything.*

*The ledgers are lost. Good. Let the guild be a story with no evidence; stories are kinder than records.*

*One thing remains that isn't yours to bury and isn't mine to keep. Under the hearthstone of the practice room — if the practice room still exists, and if it doesn't, then under wherever the hearthstone lies — you'll find the First Verse. Not the binding. The original. What the song was before we made it a cage. I never taught it to you because teaching it felt like forgiveness, and I hadn't earned the right to forgive anyone, least of all myself.*

*You have. Sing it to her.*

Juniper read it three times. On the third reading, her vision blurred past usefulness, and she lowered the paper, and looked at the hollow where the practice room lay buried under however many feet of fallen earth, and understood that Maren had written this letter knowing — *knowing* — that the hall might be rubble by the time Juniper found it. Knowing, and leaving the instruction anyway, because Maren Halloran had never once in her long life given a task without trusting her apprentice to find a way.

"Juniper?" Wren stood at the edge of the hollow, wrapped in Juniper's spare cloak, her face pale and swollen from crying and her eyes unbearably clear. "What does it say?"

Juniper folded the letter and put it inside her shirt, over her heart, where the guild's oath used to sit.

"It says," she said, and had to stop, and start again, "it says our songs used to mean something else. Before we made them locks. It says there's a first version — the one the Wardens forgot on purpose." She looked east, where the sky was going from black to the deep bruised blue that comes before gold, and where, faint and far and unhurried, the new dragon was still singing its untranslated song. "And it says I'm supposed to teach it to you."

Wren wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Beneath the exhaustion and the grief, something kindled — small, wary, unmistakable. The same look she'd had the first night at the guild door, mud-caked and mute, when the choir-stones had hummed in answer to sounds no one living should have known.

"Then we'll need to dig," Wren said.

"Yes," Juniper said. "We'll need to dig."

Behind them the sun crested the eastern ridge, and its first light fell across the ruin of the Hollow Choir, across the ironwood chair standing faithful in the ashes, across the valley that had forgotten how to forget — and Juniper Almeida, last apprentice of a guild that no longer existed, turned toward the buried hearthstone and began, at last, to look for a shovel.
