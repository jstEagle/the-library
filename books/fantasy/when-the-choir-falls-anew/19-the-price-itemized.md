## 19. The Price, Itemized

The mountain was still breathing when Serah Voss came down from the hollow.

Juniper heard her before she saw her — not footsteps, for the ground itself had gone strange since the unbinding, soft as ashfall, swallowing sound — but the small honest noises of a woman putting herself back in order: the creak of a sword belt being cinched one notch tighter, the click of a buckle, the deliberate exhale of someone rehearsing a sentence she would rather not say.

She stood at the mouth of the hollow with Wren asleep against her shoulder, the child's weight a warm and ordinary thing, and watched Voss pick her way across the scree. The Inquisitor's white-and-gold coat was scorched along one hem. Her braid had half-collapsed, grey strands escaping at her temple, and she made no move to repair it. That, more than anything, told Juniper how far past the edge of the map they stood: Voss had stopped performing.

They regarded each other across twenty feet of broken stone. Behind them, the mountain exhaled — a long, tectonic sigh through vents that had been sealed for nine hundred years, carrying the smell of deep iron and something older, something like a hearth relit after generations of cold.

"You're alive," Voss said. It was not quite a question.

"Mostly."

"And the child?"

"Asleep. Ordinary." Juniper felt the word settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. "The fire is gone out of her. Gone *out*, entirely. It went home."

Voss looked up at the mountain then, and Juniper watched her do the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since the choir fell — the warrant in her saddlebag, the empty vessel where a weapon should have been, the report she would have to deliver to a Crown that did not accept failure as a category of event. Her face did not change. Inquisitors' faces were trained not to. But her hands, hanging loose at her sides, curled slowly inward, as if around something she could no longer hold.

"I have served the Order of the Sealed Flame for thirty-one years," Voss said. "Do you know what our founding charter actually says? Not the version they teach novices. The original, under seal." She laughed once, without any humor in it. "Neither did I, until I rose high enough to read it. It says we are to guard the fire *until such time as it may be recovered for the realm*. We were never wardens, Halloran. We were custodians of an armory, waiting for someone to find the key."

"And now there is no armory."

"Now there is no armory," Voss agreed. "And the woman who lost it will be asked, very politely, in a room with very good acoustics, to explain how."

Wren stirred against Juniper's shoulder and murmured something in her sleep — common words now, broken but real, a fragment of a dream about bread or birds or nothing at all. Voss's eyes went to her, and for a moment the courtesy dropped away entirely and something raw showed underneath: not hunger, not anymore. Grief, maybe. Or its close cousin, envy.

"I should take you both down the mountain in chains," Voss said quietly. "It is what my oath requires. It is what will keep me alive another ten years, perhaps."

"But you won't."

"No." She said it flatly, the way one admits to a debt. "I watched you sing a nine-hundred-year-old binding apart with your bare voice and your own blood in your mouth. I watched the fire choose to leave rather than be fought over. I have spent my career believing magic was a thing to be *held* — that everything unruly was a weapon awaiting a hand. You've made a liar of me, and I find I'd rather retire a liar than die an honest fool." She straightened her coat with two brisk tugs, armor going back on piece by piece. "So here are terms, Warden. Listen carefully, because I will only offer them once."

Juniper shifted Wren's weight. "I'm listening."

"I ride south tomorrow. I report that I tracked the fugitives to the pass and found the binding already collapsed — old magic, failing of natural decay, no fault of the Order. I report that the vessel-child died in the collapse, body never recovered, which is conveniently true of the girl she *was*. And I report that Juniper Almeida, last of the Wardens of the Hollow Choir, perished with her guildhall." Voss's gaze was steady and terrible. "In exchange, you were never here. I was never here. And you will never speak of what I did in that hollow — that I stood witness, that I raised no hand, that I let treason happen while wearing the Crown's colors. My silence for yours. Symmetry."

"You want absolution by contract."

"I want *survival* by contract," Voss said. "Absolution isn't on the table for either of us. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise." She paused, and something almost gentle crossed her face, quickly buried. "There will be others looking, eventually. Not for you — you'll be dead on paper, and dead women make poor quarry. But for the fire. The eastern ranges are already stirring; my order's far-stations have been sending impossible readings for a week. When the Crown comes asking about dragons, and they will, remember that you owe me a silence, and pay it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then we hang together," Voss said simply. "Which is the only kind of partnership either of us has left."

She extended her hand. Juniper looked at it for a long moment — the gloved fingers, the ink stains beneath the cuff where a lifetime of warrants had been signed, the faint tremor she was too disciplined to hide completely — and then took it. Voss's grip was dry and cool and brief.

"For what it's worth," Voss said, releasing her, "your mentor sang the ninth verse backward to protect a child she'd helped bind fifty years ago. I have read every account of the Wardens ever filed, and in none of them did I expect that." She turned away, then stopped, her back to Juniper, the wind pulling at her ruined coat. "Maren Ondris was the only honest jailer your guild ever produced. Bury her facing the valley. She kept faith with it longer than it deserved."

Then she was walking down through the scree, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge she was a speck, and by midmorning she was nothing at all.

* * *

Juniper slept badly and woke to Wren watching her.

The child sat cross-legged at the hollow's mouth, wrapped in Juniper's spare cloak, her dark hair full of grit from the night's tremors. She was looking out at the valley below — at what remained of it — with an expression Juniper had no name for yet, because Wren's face was only beginning to learn expressions that weren't survival.

"It's gone quiet," Wren said. Her voice was still an astonishment, thin and rough from disuse, every common word placed like a foot on uncertain ice. "Down there. Before, even when nothing happened, it hummed. Now it's just... land."

"Yes," Juniper said. She crawled over to sit beside her. Below, the valley lay open under morning light, and for the first time in her life she could see all of it plainly — no haze of dampening, no soft blur where magic should have been and wasn't. The river ran silver and ordinary. The terraces climbed the slopes, brown and fallow. And where the guildhall had stood, where the nine choir-stones had ringed the hilltop like a crown of teeth, there was now a field of rubble, pale and scattered, as if a giant had knelt there and wept until the stones came apart.

The silence coming off it was not the old silence. The old silence had been a held breath, nine hundred years long. This was just quiet — the quiet of a place with nothing left to hide.

"Wren." She waited until the girl looked at her. "We have to go down today. There's something I have to do first, and then we walk south, away from here, and we don't come back. Do you understand?"

Wren considered this with the grave deliberateness she brought to everything now, as though language were a task she refused to do carelessly. "Burying the old woman's things," she said. "You said last night. When you thought I was sleeping."

Juniper's throat tightened. "Yes."

"I'll help." Wren began folding the cloak with fussy precision. "You shouldn't dig alone. Digging alone is how people start talking to the dirt."

And despite everything — the dead guild, the deafened ear, the valley of her whole life reduced to a scree field and a debt — Juniper laughed. It came out cracked and startled, more grief than humor, but it was a laugh, and Wren looked so pleased with herself for producing it that Juniper pulled her close and held on until the shaking stopped, whichever of them it was shaking.

* * *

She went down alone anyway, in the end, because some work does not admit company.

The climb to the hilltop took an hour that used to take ten minutes, the paths she had walked since childhood now ambiguous slopes of loose stone. Her right ear gave her trouble the whole way — a low ringing that had lived there since the unbinding, the physicians' term for it unknown to her, the price written directly into her body in a currency she hadn't agreed to spend. Sounds arrived at her slightly wrong, slightly distant, as if the world were speaking through a wall. She suspected it always would.

The guildhall's remains were worse than rubble. Stone remembered shape, and the fallen stones remembered theirs: here the threshold she had crossed ten thousand times, there the corner of the kitchen where Maren had burned the morning bread for forty years running, there — intact, absurdly — the iron hook where the charter had hung, its parchment gone to ash, its meaning gone wherever meanings go when their institutions die.

The choir-stones had not shattered so much as *exhaled*. Each one had split along lines that looked almost deliberate, opening like seed pods, their interiors hollow and pale. Juniper knelt at the largest fragment and put her palm against it. Cold. Utterly, honestly cold, for the first time in recorded memory. Whatever had lived in these stones had either died or gone home with the fire, and she found she could not decide which possibility hurt more.

She had brought down from the hollow what remained of Maren Ondris: not a body — the ninth verse backward had seen to that, unmaking the old woman as thoroughly as it unmade the binding, leaving nothing but her robes, folded by Voss with unsettling neatness, and the contents of her pockets and satchel, laid out on the wool like relics on an altar.

Juniper knelt among them now and itemized.

A wooden comb, three teeth missing, worn smooth as riverglass. A tin of salve for arthritic hands, half-used, smelling of comfrey and wintergreen. A stub of red sealing wax. A bone stylus, chewed at one end — Maren had chewed her stylus through every lesson Juniper had ever taken, and Juniper had spent years finding the habit disgusting, and would have given her remaining ear to see her do it once more. A small knife with a broken tip, good for apples and bad for anything else. A folded page torn from a hymnal, so old the notation predated modern staves, and in the margin, in Maren's cramped hand: *forgive us, forgive us, forgive us*, written over and over until the words degraded into a single long stroke, like a wound.

And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger.

Juniper unwrapped it with hands that had begun, without her permission, to shake. She knew what it was before she opened it. Every Warden kept one — the tally-book, the record of bindings maintained, verses sung, oaths renewed. Hers was in the ruins somewhere, ash now, and she found she did not mourn it.

Maren's ledger covered sixty years. The early pages were meticulous: dates, stones, the health of the valley's silence, notes in a young hand confident and clear. Then, gradually, the entries changed. The intervals between them stretched. The notes grew shorter, then bitterer, then sparse as winter grass. On a page dated forty-one years ago, a single line:

*Sang the ninth verse over the Almeida infant. The mother wept. I told her it was a blessing. God forgive me, I told her it was a blessing.*

Juniper closed the ledger.

She sat among the fallen stones for a long time, the ringing in her ear loud as surf, and let the arithmetic finish itself. Maren had bound her — had bound the fire into Juniper's own bloodline, generation upon generation, a chain of infants blessed into servitude, until the chain ended in a mute child who spoke Old Draconic in her sleep. Juniper had been raised inside the cage and taught to call it a garden. She had loved the garden. She had sung its songs every evening of her life, and the songs had been bars, and the bars had been beautiful, and the woman who forged them had died breaking the last one open.

None of it redeemed. That was the thing no one told you about sacrifice, Juniper thought, staring at the comb, the salve, the chewed stylus. It paid debts forward, not backward. Maren's death had freed Wren. It had not unbound the forty years of lies. There was no verse, forward or backward, that could do that. Some costs were simply costs, entered in a ledger that balanced nowhere, and the living carried them because the dead could not.

She dug the grave with the broken-tipped knife and a shard of choir-stone, in the exact center of where the circle had been, facing the valley as Voss had instructed — and she hated that she followed the Inquisitor's instruction, and did it anyway, because it was also what she herself would have chosen, and honesty required admitting that even Serah Voss got to be right sometimes.

Into the earth she laid the comb, the salve, the wax, the stylus, the knife, the hymnal page with its degradation of forgiveness into a single stroke. The ledger she kept. Someone had to carry the accounts, and Maren was past carrying anything.

She marked the grave with the largest intact fragment of the first choir-stone, and on its flat face, with the stylus — she could not bring herself to bury that particular object — she cut no verse, no binding, no prayer. Only four words, plain as a fencepost:

*MAREN ONDRIS. SHE SANG.*

Let the valley forget everything else. Let it keep that.

* * *

Wren was waiting at the bottom of the hill, sitting on a fallen lintel with her knees drawn up, and she did not ask how it went. She looked at Juniper's dirt-caked hands and the raw palms and the set of her shoulders, and she simply stood, and took one end of the bedroll without being asked, and started walking south.

They went down through the valley Juniper had known all her life, which was no longer hers.

It was stranger than grief. She had expected grief, had braced for it like weather. What she had not expected was the *ordinariness* — the way the river still ran, the way a hawk still rode the thermals above the terraces, the way the light came down gold and indifferent on a place that had spent nine centuries holding its breath and now, breath released, was simply landscape. The valley had never belonged to her. That was the final entry in the ledger, the one she wrote in her own mind as they walked: *you did not lose a home; you lost the belief that a cage was one.* It did not help. But it was true, and she had decided, somewhere on the mountainside, that she would deal only in true things from now on. Everything else had gotten too expensive.

At the valley's southern edge, where the road passed between the last two hills and the world beyond opened up — roadless, chartless, the lowlands smoking faintly in the distance with the first cookfires of a spring that would be unlike any other — Wren stopped.

"Juniper." The girl was looking back the way they'd come, at the hilltop where a new marker caught the afternoon light. "When we get wherever we're going. Will you teach me the songs? The real ones. Not the cage ones."

"They're not written anywhere," Juniper said. "The real ones. They only exist if someone sings them."

"Good," said Wren. "Then nobody can take them away again."

She turned and walked on, small and resolute, and Juniper followed, and behind them the valley settled into its long honest quiet, and ahead — far to the east, beyond the ridgelines, where the sky had begun to carry a shimmer that was not weather — something vast and patient was waking, and learning, note by note, to sing.

Somewhere down in the lowlands, other ears were beginning to hear it too.
