## 20. Wren Speaks

The silence inside Wren was the strangest country she had ever lived in.

She had carried the fire for so long that its absence felt like a missing tooth — her tongue kept going to the place where it had been, probing the hollow, expecting heat and finding only her own small self. She was light. She was hungry. She was cold in the ordinary way a child is cold on a mountainside, and when she shivered, it was only Wren shivering, no ancient thing coiling beneath her skin to warm her from the inside out.

She had expected to feel empty. Instead she felt *roomy*.

And into all that room, for the first time since the night the soldiers came to the farmstead, the memories came home.

They did not arrive all at once. They came the way water comes back to a dried streambed — first a damp darkening of the stones, then a trickle, then a running. Wren sat wrapped in Juniper's cloak at the mouth of the hollow, watching the sun come up over the shattered valley below, and the trickle began.

Her mother's hands. That was the first thing. Not her mother's face — faces were still shy, still hiding behind a curtain of ash in her mind — but her hands, flour-dusted, pressing dough against the side of a wooden bowl with a rhythm like a heartbeat. *Pat-pat-pat. Turn. Pat-pat-pat.* Wren's own hands made the motion without asking her permission, kneading the cold mountain air, and something in her chest cracked open in a way that hurt and helped at once.

Her father whistled through his teeth when he worked. Two notes, up then down, over and over while he mended fence wire or split kindling, and he never knew he was doing it, and her mother never asked him to stop, and the whole house ran on that little falling song the way a mill runs on its wheel.

Her brother —

Wren pressed her fists against her eyes.

Her brother had a name. She could almost see it, hanging in the fog of the lost years like a lantern someone had left burning in an empty window. She reached for it the way you reach for a branch across a stream, afraid to put your weight down, more afraid not to.

*Colm.*

The name came whole. Colm. Six years old, gap where his front tooth had been, terrified of thunder and pretending not to be, who slept sideways in his bed like a compass needle that couldn't decide. Colm, who had hidden under the floorboards with her when the riders came, his hand clamped over his own mouth so hard she could still feel the shape of his fingers if she let herself.

He had held his silence better than any Warden ever had. He had held it until they pulled the boards up.

Wren made a sound then, low and animal, and folded forward over her knees, and the sound became words before she understood she was making them, because the fire was gone and the silence it had demanded of her went with it:

"Colm. His name was Colm."

Juniper's hand landed on her back between one breath and the next. The woman hadn't slept either; Wren could hear it in her voice, that raw-sanded quality, and she could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes, and in the way her left ear tilted slightly toward Wren as though listening with the one that still worked. The deafness on the other side had come with the unbinding, along with everything else the valley had paid. Juniper never mentioned it. Wren noticed anyway. Noticing things was what she had instead of talking, all those years, and the habit stayed even now that the words came easy.

"Say it again," Juniper said softly. "If you want. Names like keeping."

"Colm." It steadied her, saying it twice. A name said twice was a name planted. "He was six. He bit his nails down past the quick and lied about it. He wanted to be a falconer when he grew up but he was scared of heights, and Papa said that was all right, that the best falconers keep their feet on the ground and their eyes on the sky."

The memory ran clean as spring water. No ash over it. No dragon-fire smelting it into symbol and warning. Just a boy on a fence rail with a stick falcon on his wrist, shouting *haaaawk* in a voice that cracked, while their father laughed so hard he had to sit down in the dirt.

"I had a sister too," Wren heard herself say, and stopped, astonished. "I didn't know I had a sister. I thought — I thought it was just me and Colm. But there was a cradle. There was a cradle by the window and I used to stand on a stool and look into it."

*Isolde.* The name arrived with a smell — milk and lavender soap and the particular warmth of a sleeping baby's head. Isolde, three months old, born in the last week of harvest. Born into a bloodline that had carried a dragon's fire for nine generations without knowing it, because knowing would have been death and not-knowing was only ever a postponement.

"They came for the fire," Wren said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, level and terrible. "I understand that now. They weren't bandits. They had papers. One of them read from a paper while the others — " She swallowed. "He read our family name off it. All of it. Three names long. Like we were a deed to something."

"You were," Juniper said quietly. "That's what a binding makes of people. Property with a pulse." Her jaw tightened. "My guild wrote those deeds. We just called them hymns."

Wren looked at her. This was the thing she had learned about Juniper Almeida in the weeks since the world broke open: she did not flinch from the ugly arithmetic. She stood next to it and counted out loud. Wren had decided, somewhere in the tollhouse with its smell of dust and old tolls unpaid, that this was what trust was made of. Not kindness. Kindness was common as rain. It was this — a person willing to stand in the same accounting as you and not round the numbers in their own favor.

"My mother's name was Sera," Wren said. "Sera Albrecht Vane. My father was Tomas Vane. And my sister—" the name caught, and she pushed it through, the way you push a splinter the rest of the way out, knowing the sting means it's leaving— "my sister was Isolde Vane. And me. Wren Vane." She said her own last name and felt it settle onto her shoulders like a coat she'd outgrown but refused to throw away. "Nobody's said any of it out loud in two years. I think the names were waiting. I think they were standing outside a shut door the whole time, and the fire was the door."

Juniper's hand moved on her back, slow circles. "Then open it every day," she said. "Names fade fast in the dark. Say them until they're furniture. Until they're weather. Until remembering them costs exactly what it should and no more."

"What should it cost?"

"I don't know yet." Juniper smiled, tired and crooked. "I'm still pricing Maren."

* * *

They buried nothing on the mountain — there was nothing left to bury, only effects, and those Juniper carried in a satchel against her heart: Maren's tuning fork of grey iron, her prayer-knots, the little bone whistle she'd used to call the guild cats, a lock of her own white hair she'd kept from before, though before what, she'd never said. Wren watched Juniper touch the satchel sometimes, absently, the way you touch a bruise to check if it still hurts. It always did. That was the point of touching it, maybe.

They ate the last of the waybread sitting in the sun, and then there was nothing between them and the downward path except the downward path itself.

"No map," Juniper said, standing, brushing crumbs from her skirt. She said it lightly, but Wren heard the weight underneath, the same way she'd once heard the choir-stones humming under their silence. "I've never walked anywhere without a charter telling me where and why. It occurs to me I don't actually know which way east is without a stone to sing at."

Wren pointed. "Sun comes up there. Papa taught me. He said a map is just somebody else's memory of the road, and roads don't stay put long enough to deserve it."

Juniper laughed — really laughed, short and surprised out of her, the first one Wren had heard since the valley died. "Your papa sounds like he'd have argued Maren into the ground."

"He argued with everybody. Mama said arguing was how he loved people." Wren paused. "It's strange. I can hear his voice now. I couldn't, before. Before it was all — smoke. Everything important was behind smoke. Now the smoke's gone and the voices are just *there*, ordinary, like they never left." She hugged her knees. "I hate it and I want to live inside it forever."

"That's grief," Juniper said. "Maren told me once it's love with nowhere to go. I think she was wrong, though. I think it's love finding new places to go. Slower places. Inside places." She shouldered the satchel. "Come on, Wren Vane. Let's go be slow."

* * *

The path down from the pass was old — older than the guild, older than the Crown, cut into the mountain's flank by feet that had come to the first binding when it was fresh and terrible. In places the steps were worn to shallow bowls, each one holding a little cup of yesterday's rain. Wren counted them as she walked, because counting was calming, because numbers were the same in every language and hers had been taken away and given back and she liked owning something so reliable.

Two hundred and eleven steps down, she started talking, and found she couldn't stop.

She told Juniper about the farmstead — the low slate roof her father re-slated every third summer, the dog named Bristle who was afraid of sheep, the way the kitchen smelled on bread days, which was flour and woodsmoke and the green onion smell that came in under the door from the garden no matter how tightly it was shut. She told her about the festival in the nearest village where they strung lanterns between the wells and her mother danced with Isolde swaddled against her chest, swaying, both of them asleep by the third song. She told her about hiding under the floorboards, and how the dust had tasted, and how Colm's heartbeat against her shoulder had slowed and slowed and finally stopped speeding up, which meant he'd fallen asleep, which meant for one merciful hour he didn't know what was happening above the boards.

Juniper listened to all of it. All of it. She never once said *you don't have to tell me this* or *that must have been hard* or any of the soft deflections adults used to protect themselves from children's griefs. She just walked and listened, and when Wren cried she walked closer, and when Wren laughed — and she did laugh, twice, helplessly, at the story of Bristle and the sheep — Juniper laughed with her, and neither thing was treated as a betrayal of the other.

By midafternoon they had come down out of the bare rock into the first scrub pines, and the air changed, thickened, smelled of resin and thaw. Wren stopped on the trail.

"Do you smell that?"

Juniper lifted her chin, tasting the wind with her good ear cocked as if scent could be heard. "Pine. Thaw. Something flowering early — the seasons are going to be drunk for a few years, Maren would've said. Magic coming back into ground that forgot it. Why? What do you smell?"

"Warmth." Wren frowned, searching for the word. "Not sun-warmth. Under-warmth. Like standing near the well after it went hot." She looked east, where the pines thinned toward a long blue distance of ranges beyond ranges. "There's something out there. Far. I can't feel it the way I used to — the fire's gone, I'm just me — but I remember what it feels like, and something out there feels like that. Like the first morning of a fire being lit in a cold house."

Juniper was quiet a moment. Then: "East. Toward the eastern ranges." She said it the way you say a diagnosis. "Where the dragons went to ground. Where they'll be waking, now that nothing holds them down."

They stood together in the pine-shadow, two small figures on an enormous slope, and Wren felt the future arrive the way weather arrives — not as a single event but as a change in the pressure of the air.

"Some people will call it war," Juniper said slowly. "When word spreads. When the first village sees wings. Some people will start sharpening things, and some people will start selling protections, and some people will write charters." Her hand drifted to the satchel, to Maren's tuning fork inside it. "And some people will just have to learn to share a world with creatures that burn. That's harder than fighting. Fighting is easy. Sharing is the hardest verse there is."

"Which will you do?" Wren asked.

Juniper looked at her — a long look, taking her measure, or maybe taking her own. "I don't know yet," she said. "But I know I'm done singing other people's songs. Whatever comes next, I'll write the verse myself." She turned down the trail, into the trees, into the blue distance where something vast and warm was stirring. "First, though — a town. Bread that isn't waybread. A bath. And someone who can tell us what's happened in the lowlands while we were up here ending the world."

Wren followed her down, one hand trailing along the pine branches, letting the needles brush her palm like small green blessings. Behind them the mountain breathed its slow stone breath. Ahead, far off, faint as a memory of heat, the east waited.

And Wren Vane — daughter of Sera and Tomas, sister of Colm and Isolde, keeper of four names and no oaths — walked toward it speaking the whole way, because the words were hers now, and she intended to spend every one.
