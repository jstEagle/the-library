## 21. Dragonsong, Unbound

They had been walking for six days when the mountain began to sing.

Juniper felt it first in her feet — a tremor too deep to be a landslide, too rhythmic to be anything accidental. It came up through the soles of her boots and settled in her bones like a second heartbeat, patient and vast, and she stopped mid-stride on the goat track above the tree line and stood very still until Wren noticed and stopped beside her.

"What is it?" Wren asked. Her voice was still new — three weeks old, more or less, counting from the tollhouse, from that first cracked *stay* spoken into the dark like a coin set down on a table. She used it carefully now, the way a child uses a knife for the first time, aware of its edge.

"I don't know," Juniper said.

It was not entirely true. She knew what it was not. It was not the choir-stones; those were ash and rubble back in the valley, buried under the collapse of everything Maren had spent fifty years holding shut. It was not the binding-songs, because there was nothing left in the world to bind. And it was not silence, which had been the only other sound Juniper's life had ever reliably contained.

It was something beginning.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, and the singing grew louder as the sun went down. That was how Juniper understood it was not a sound at all, or not only a sound. It rose with the dark the way warmth rises off stone at dusk, and it did not come through her ears — her left ear was still a shell of ringing quiet where the unbinding had taken its price — but through the whole of her, skin and marrow and the scar-tissue of her grief alike.

Wren heard it too. Juniper could tell by the way the girl sat with her knees pulled up and her chin resting on them, staring east, her eyes gone wide and glassy with a light that had no source in the sky.

"It's far away," Wren said. "But it knows we're here."

"Does it know your name?"

"No." Wren considered this with the grave deliberation she brought to all questions now, as if language were a country she was still learning to walk through without stepping on things. "It doesn't need names. It just — opens. Like when you open a window and the room isn't separate anymore."

Juniper fed the small fire another branch. They kept fires now without shame or ceremony; there was no dampening field left to betray, no Inquisitors' instruments to catch the flare of magic in the smoke. Voss had ridden west two weeks ago with her warrant unsigned and her treason folded away inside her like a letter she would carry to her grave or her executioner, whichever came first. The world was simpler and infinitely more dangerous than it had been.

"Do you miss it?" Juniper asked. "The fire."

She had asked before, in different words, on different nights, and Wren had always answered with her shoulders or her hands or a long look toward some horizon only she could read. Tonight the girl was quiet for a long time, and then she said:

"It's like missing a fever. I remember how it felt. I don't want it back. But I remember."

* * *

On the seventh day they crested the pass above the high meadows, and Juniper saw the eastern ranges burning.

Not burning — she corrected herself even as her heart lurched — *lit*. Far off, past three ridgelines and a river valley and a distance that would take a week of hard walking, the peaks glowed from within, seams of gold opening along their flanks like veins of ore catching sunlight. And rising from them, threading up through the cold blue air in columns that bent and swayed like banners, were shapes.

Dragons. Small ones, young ones, or perhaps simply distant ones — it was impossible to say. There were dozens. Perhaps more than dozens. They circled the eastern heights in slow spirals, and every wingbeat sent out a pulse of that deep song, and the song arrived where Juniper stood not as noise but as recognition, the way you know a voice calling from across a crowded square even if you cannot make out the words.

Wren made a sound Juniper had never heard from her. It was half laugh and half sob, and both halves seemed to surprise her equally. She pressed both hands flat against her own sternum as though holding a door shut from the inside.

"They're coming *out*," she said. "The eggs. The ones that never hatched while the fire was locked up. They waited." Her voice caught. "Nobody told them they'd been waiting. They just knew."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I knew it too." Wren turned to look at her, and in the girl's face Juniper saw both the child she was and the ancient vessel she had been, layered together like two images held to a lamp. "All my life I thought I was waiting for something. I didn't know what. Now they're not waiting either. None of us are."

Juniper looked east for a long time.

She thought about what the world below would make of this. She thought of the Crown's heralds already drafting proclamations, of garrisons being counted and crossbows oiled, of priests in three kingdoms reaching for prophecies that had gathered dust since the pre-kingdom days. She thought of Serah Voss standing before her superiors, reporting failure, knowing that failure was the kindest word available to her. She thought of noble houses with bought bindings in their cellars — the guild that wasn't, the honor that was partly myth — suddenly discovering their purchased chains had rusted through overnight.

Some would call it war. Most would, probably. War was the story people reached for when something vast and old returned to a world that had arranged itself around its absence. War had a shape everyone recognized. It required no imagination, only fear.

And yet.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Juniper said. "Maren spent her whole life believing the songs were lullabies. Then she learned they were bindings, and she believed they were cages. But maybe they were neither. Maybe they were a promise someone forgot how to keep — *we will hold this safely until the world is ready*. And nobody ever decided when the world was ready. So the songs just went on, and on, and the holding became the whole point instead of the promise."

Wren frowned, working through it. "And now?"

"Now the holding's over. Which means the promise finally has to mean something, or it never meant anything at all."

* * *

They descended through the high meadows over the next two days, and the song grew stronger, and the world grew stranger, and Juniper found herself cataloguing miracles the way she had once catalogued chores.

Flowers bloomed along the path in the wrong season — mountain avens in autumn, defiantly white against the browning grass. A stream they crossed ran warm as bathwater, and small silver fish crowded its shallows in numbers no honest stream should hold. Once, at dusk, a fox sat on a boulder twenty paces away and watched them for a full minute with an attention that felt less like animal curiosity and more like assessment, before it dipped its head — actually dipped its head, Juniper would swear to it later — and trotted away.

Magic was returning to the world, seeping back into soil and blood and weather, filling spaces that had been dry for a thousand years. And everything that had lived quietly in the margins of a mundane world was lifting its head.

"It's going to be hard," Wren said one evening. "For people. Isn't it."

"Yes."

"The villages. The farms. Things growing wild. Animals acting strange. People being afraid."

"Yes."

"And the dragons will be afraid too. Because people will come with weapons, and the dragons won't understand why, because from their side they're just coming home."

Juniper looked at her. Three weeks ago this child had been mute, carrying a language older than kingdoms and a fire older than the mountains, unable to say a single word of the tongue everyone around her spoke. Now she sat by a small fire dissecting the geopolitics of a returning age with the weary clarity of someone who had watched empires rise and fall from inside a dream.

"When did you get wise?" Juniper asked.

Wren almost smiled. "When I got words. Words are where wisdom lives. Before that I just had pictures, and pictures don't argue with each other."

* * *

On the ninth day they came down out of the high country into the river valley, and Juniper saw the first signs of the world reacting.

A village — forty roofs, a mill, a shrine with a faded sun-disc over its door — stood with its gates shut at midday. Watchfires burned on its palisade though the sky was clear. As they passed along the road at a careful distance, Juniper saw figures on the wall, and every one of them faced east, toward the glowing ranges, spears in hand.

An old woman drawing water at the village well saw them and called out, "Are you from the east? Have you seen them? Are they coming here?"

Juniper weighed her answer. Every word was a stone she could lay one way or the other.

"They're coming home," she said. "This was theirs before it was ours. But they're not coming for us."

The woman stared at her. "Then what are they coming *for*?"

"A place in the world," Juniper said. "Same as anyone."

The woman spat, but she did not laugh, and she did not turn away, and after a moment she said, quietly, "My grandmother used to hum a song. Said her grandmother taught her. Nobody knew what it meant." She lifted the yoke across her shoulders, the buckets swaying. "It had nine verses. We only ever sang eight."

Then she walked back through her gate, and the gate closed behind her, and Juniper stood on the road feeling the ground tilt beneath everything she thought she knew.

Nine verses. The ninth verse — the verse Maren had sung backward to die free, the verse that had been the keystone of the oldest binding. Scattered through the lowlands like seed, carried by grandmothers who never knew what they were humming. The guild had thought it was the last keeper of the songs. The guild had been wrong about nearly everything, but it had been wrong about this most of all: the songs had never belonged to the Wardens. They had escaped long ago. They had been living in kitchens and cradles all along, waiting.

"Wren," Juniper said slowly. "What happens if ordinary people sing the ninth verse forward? Not as a binding. Just — as a song."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then: "I think it depends what they mean when they sing it."

"That's terrifying."

"Yes," Wren agreed, and this time she did smile, small and crooked and entirely human. "Isn't it wonderful?"

* * *

That night they camped within sight of the village walls, close enough for its watchfires to be visible, far enough to be left alone. Juniper lay awake long after Wren slept, watching the eastern sky.

The glow on the far peaks had not dimmed. If anything it had strengthened, and now she could see movement in it — wings crossing before the light, the way birds cross before a bonfire. More of them each night. Hatched or waking or descending from wherever the old ones had endured, gathering in the high places of the world like a parliament convening.

And beneath the sight, beneath the earth itself, the song went on. She could feel it in the ground under her bedroll, a pulse like a great slow heart. Some nights it sounded like threat. Some nights like mourning. Tonight, for the first time, it sounded like neither.

Tonight it sounded like a question.

*What will you do with us?*

And Juniper understood, lying in the dark with a sleeping child beside her and her dead guildhall a hundred miles behind her and her mentor's ashes folded in cloth in her pack, that the question was not really aimed at kings or councils or armies. Those would answer with what they always answered with. The question was aimed at everyone. At the old woman at the well with her inherited song. At the villagers on their walls deciding whether to loose arrows or lower them. At children who would grow up in a world where the sky had owners again.

At her.

She had unbound the fire. She had broken the cage, killed the sanctuary, paid the price itemized and then some — an ear, a home, a guild, a mother-figure, the entire architecture of a life. She had done it because she believed freedom was better than safety, that a shared world was better than a hidden one, that refusing to fight an ancient war might end it where ten centuries of fighting had not.

But belief was not a plan. Freedom was not peace. She had opened the door, and now the door could not be closed, and everything that poured through it would be shaped by what people did next — by whether the first meeting between a farmer and a dragon was a shared field or a corpse, by whether the ninth verse got sung as a promise or a weapon, by whether somewhere in the years ahead someone built a school with no charter and no secrets, where children learned songs that were gifts instead of chains.

Someone would have to teach them. Someone who knew both verses — the binding and the blessing — and knew why one had to die so the other could live.

Juniper stared at the eastern glow until her eyes burned, and she did not feel hopeful, exactly. Hope was a word for people who expected things to go well. What she felt was closer to responsibility wearing hope's clothing, or hope wearing responsibility's — she was too tired to untangle it. The future was unwritten. That was not comfort. That was the whole terrifying point. A written future was a binding, and she had sworn off bindings.

But unwritten could become anything. Including something good.

Beside her, Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something — a fragment of Old Draconic, still, sometimes, in her dreams, though the fire was gone from her. Juniper listened to the unfamiliar syllables and found, to her surprise, that she could almost parse them now. Not the meaning. The music.

*Tomorrow,* the syllables said, or seemed to say. *Tomorrow, tomorrow.*

Far to the east, the mountains glowed, and the dragons wheeled, and the song rolled westward across the sleeping world like a tide coming in — and somewhere below them, in the valley where the village slept behind its walls, Juniper would have sworn she heard a single voice join it. Thin. Human. Uncertain.

Singing the ninth verse.

Forward.
