## 22. The First Verse of Something New

The school stood where the tollhouse used to be, or near enough that Juniper sometimes forgot which building she was standing in. That was the trouble with rebuilding on the bones of a memory: the new walls never quite agreed with the old ones in her mind. She would reach for a door that wasn't there, or turn down a hallway that had been open sky when she was young, and for a heartbeat she would be twenty-six again, running through the dark with a mute child on her back and Inquisitors' lanterns swinging behind her like low, hungry moons.

Then a child would laugh somewhere down the corridor, and the past would let go of her.

It had been nine years. Nine years since the valley's silence shattered; nine years since the choir-stones fell into rubble and the guildhall's roof came down like a held breath finally released; nine years since Maren's last verse unmade her faster than any winter could have. Nine years since Serah Voss rode away with an empty warrant and a treason folded small enough to fit inside her coat. Nine years, and the world had not ended. The world had, against every expectation Juniper had carried out of her old life, *continued* — messily, loudly, with magic threading back into it like water finding the cracks in dry stone.

She had not planned to become a teacher. She had planned, insofar as she had planned anything in those first raw months, to walk until walking stopped meaning anything, and then perhaps to stop. But Wren had been eight years old and hungry, and hungry children do not care whether their guardians have finished grieving. And there had been other children, too, once word spread down from the eastern ranges — children born after the Unbinding who hummed notes that made candle flames lean toward them, children whose shadows moved a half-second out of step, children the villages didn't know what to do with because no one alive remembered what to do with such things.

So Juniper built a school. Or rather: she put up walls around something that had already begun happening, which is, she suspected, how most true things get built.

* * *

Morning came late to the high hollow in autumn. By the time Juniper climbed the path from the cottage she shared with Wren, frost still clung to the grass in silver rime, and the mountains to the east were burning with early light — the same ranges where, all those years ago, they had heard the first new dragonsong rise. She could hear the school before she saw it: the particular racket of thirty-odd children being children in a place that permitted it, which was itself a kind of miracle she never stopped noticing.

The building was modest. Stone at the base, timber above, a slate roof that leaked in exactly one corner no matter how many times it was mended, as if the roof had decided the leak was tradition. There was no charter nailed above the door. No seal, no sigil, no oath carved into the lintel. Juniper had been offered charters — three times, by three different authorities, each more bewildered than the last by her refusals. A charter, she had learned, was a beautiful word for a cage with paperwork. The Wardens of the Hollow Choir had had a charter too, and a founding secret beneath it, and fifty generations of good people singing themselves into jailers one verse at a time.

No secrets, then. That was the whole architecture of the place. The children knew everything — why the old songs had been written, what they had really done, what they had cost. They knew about the binding of the last dragon's fire into a bloodline. They knew about Wren. They knew about Maren, whose name Juniper spoke aloud in lessons the way you speak the names of the dead you refuse to let become only dead. Some visiting scholars found this scandalous. *Children should not carry such weight,* they said. But Juniper had watched what happened to adults who discovered, all at once and too late, that the ground under their lives was a lie. Weight carried knowingly was a burden. Weight discovered suddenly was a collapse. She chose burdens.

Inside, the main room smelled of woodsmoke and chalk and porridge. Benches faced no altar, no lectern — just a wide circle of floor worn smooth by nine years of feet. On the far wall hung the only ornament Juniper had ever allowed herself: a single shard of choir-stone, mounted on a plank of salvaged guildhall beam. It did nothing now. It was just rock. She kept it the way you keep a scar — not to worship it, but so you never pretended it hadn't happened.

"Juniper! Juniper, Tam made the window sing again and he says he didn't mean to but he was *smiling* while he said it—"

"I was not smiling."

"You're smiling *now.*"

Juniper raised a hand, and the room settled the way water settles — not instantly, but completely. Thirty faces turned toward her, ranging from six years old to nearly grown. Among them, in the second row, sat a girl of seventeen with dark hair going prematurely grey at one temple — a mark, the healers thought, of what had lived in her blood and left it. Wren met her eyes with the level, unhurried gaze of someone who had once carried a dragon's fire and set it down, and who remembered both the carrying and the setting-down.

"Tam," Juniper said gravely, "did you make the window sing?"

"...Maybe."

"Was anyone hurt?"

"No."

"Did it sound terrible?"

A pause. A reluctant grin. "Kind of beautiful, actually."

"Then we'll add it to the lesson." She turned to the circle. "That's the difference, everyone. Write it somewhere in your heads if nowhere else. The old songs asked *what can this power do for us, or to us.* The songs we learn ask *what does this power want to become.* First question makes tools and weapons and bindings. Second question—" she shrugged, "—makes mornings like this one."

* * *

They sang the first verse of the morning song together, and Juniper listened the way she always listened: with half her attention on the music and half on the silence underneath it, listening for the old resonance, the binding-hum, the sound the choir-stones had made when songs were chains dressed as lullabies.

It wasn't there. It had never come back, not once in nine years, and she had come to understand that this absence was the truest proof the Unbinding had worked. These children could make windows ring and candles lean and shadows behave strangely, but nothing they sang could hold another living thing against its will. The grammar of compulsion had gone out of the world's music, or perhaps out of theirs — she honestly didn't know which, and had stopped needing to know. What mattered was that a child could sing to a frightened animal now and the animal could leave afterward. Freedom had a sound, and it was this: imperfect voices, slightly out of tune, holding no one.

Wren's voice rose among the others, low and steady. She had a fine singing voice, though she rarely performed; she preferred the percussion of the teaching kitchen, or long silent walks up the mountain trails, or — most of all — sitting with the youngest children at dusk, telling stories. Not the old Draconic, though she still dreamed in it sometimes; she'd told Juniper once that the dreams had changed over the years, the burning wings growing gentler, more like memories of weather than memories of war. She told stories in common now. Stories about her family. Their names — Sela, Doran, little Ivet — spoken easily, the way you speak names that have finished hurting and become simply beloved.

That had been the last gift of the fire's leaving, or maybe the first gift of its leaving: memory without the burn. For years after the Unbinding, Wren had remembered almost nothing of her family — the vessel had held its cargo so tightly that emptying it had scattered even the ordinary human recollections packed alongside the flame. Then, slowly, season by season, they had returned. Not all at once. Like frost melting off a window, clearing from the edges inward. The day Wren had first spoken her mother's name aloud without flinching, she and Juniper had sat up until dawn doing absolutely nothing important, drinking bad tea, and it had been one of the best nights of Juniper's life.

* * *

Midday brought visitors, as middays often did.

They came up the winding road in ones and twos: a hedge-witch wanting to compare notes on the new wild-magic blooming in her valley; a nervous clerk from the provincial capital carrying yet another inquiry about the school's legal status; two sisters, ten and twelve, whose parents had heard rumors that here was a place that wouldn't call their youngest daughter cursed. Juniper fed them all. This was policy. No one had ever learned anything important on an empty stomach, and besides, the stew pot was enormous, a relic of guildhall days when the Wardens had fed forty mouths, and it seemed wrong to let it shrink to the size of her solitude.

The clerk was the interesting one today. He picked at his stew and avoided her eyes and finally blurted it out between spoonfuls, the way uncomfortable questions always arrive.

"They keep asking me at the ministry, you know. For the records. Whether your school answers to any authority. Whether it holds a charter, a license, a — a *something.* And I tell them no, and they don't believe me, because everything must belong to something, don't you see? Everything must have a chain of custody. So I'm meant to ask you directly, and please forgive me, but — who do you answer to? What guild are you?"

Juniper set down her spoon.

She thought about the guildhall, and the stones, and Maren's hands going papery and ancient in the space of a single backward verse. She thought about Voss, wherever she was now — retired, rumor said, to some damp coastal town, still keeping the secret that had cost her everything and bought her nothing except the knowledge that she'd chosen rightly. She thought about the ledger they'd found in the ruined waystation, page after page of Wardens selling bindings to noble houses, honor revealed as partly myth, and how she had spent one long bitter night deciding that a myth exposed was still better than a myth maintained, because only a truth can be repaired.

"No one," she said. "I belong to no guild."

The clerk blinked. "But — surely—"

"I answer to these children," she said, "while they're mine to teach. To the villages below, when our work touches them. To whatever's true, on the days I can tell what that is. That's the whole charter. You're welcome to write it down, but I warn you, it won't fill a page, and ministries hate short documents."

He laughed despite himself, made a few hopeless notes, and left lighter than he'd arrived. And Juniper stood in her doorway in the cold bright afternoon and realized — not for the first time, but freshly, the way you can re-realize a thing you've known for years — that she had meant it. Every word. *None,* and gladly. The debt she'd inherited had been paid down across nine years of ordinary mornings, and what remained was not an inheritance at all anymore. It was a gift. Freely given, freely passed on, chained to nothing.

* * *

Evening. Lessons done, visitors gone, the younger children collected by parents and older siblings until the school stood quiet. Wren banked the stove while Juniper tidied the benches, and neither of them spoke, because they had long ago passed beyond the need to fill comfortable silences.

It was Wren who broke it, eventually, in the doorway, pulling her coat on for the walk home.

"The eastern ranges," she said. "You heard it again last night. Don't pretend you didn't — you were on the porch till midnight."

Juniper smiled. "I hear a lot of things at midnight."

"Dragonsong."

"A long way off. New voices. Young ones, maybe." She hung the last bench-cushion on its peg. "Some of the villages below are frightened. They sent word asking whether the school should teach warding-songs again."

"And what did you tell them?"

"That we'd teach listening-songs first." Juniper joined her at the door. The sky over the eastern peaks was doing its slow violet-to-indigo shift, and somewhere far beyond sight, something vast and young and free was singing to the dark. "Warding-songs are just bindings wearing armor. If the world has to share itself with dragons again — and it does, that decision was made nine years ago whether anyone voted on it or not — then the first skill anyone needs is the ability to hear what's actually being said."

Wren was quiet a moment. Seventeen years old and carrying more history than most historians, looking out at the mountains where her family's story had begun and ended and begun again.

"Do you think it'll be war?" she asked. "Everyone down in the valleys thinks it'll be war. Or salvation. They only ever seem to have the two words for it."

"I think," Juniper said, "that it hasn't been written yet. And I think the people writing it are mostly children right now — some of them in my classroom, learning to sing promises instead of chains. So no. I don't think it's war. I think it's a first verse." She bumped her shoulder gently against Wren's. "And nobody knows how a first verse goes. That's what makes it one."

Wren huffed a laugh, old beyond her years and young beyond her history all at once, and started down the path toward the cottage, calling back over her shoulder that Juniper had better not stay up on the porch all night listening again, her hearing in the left ear couldn't afford the cold.

Juniper stayed on the porch anyway. Just for a while.

Below, the valley lights came on one by one — real lights now, in a valley that had forgotten how to hold them, in a world where magic ran free and unbound and dangerous and alive. Far to the east, faint as memory, the dragonsong rose and fell. Somewhere behind her, inside the school, the shard of choir-stone sat silent on its beam of fallen guildhall timber, doing nothing, promising nothing, remembering everything.

Juniper pulled her coat close, and began, very softly, to hum the first verse of something new — no words yet, no shape, just a note going out into the dark like a hand extended — and waited, glad and unafraid, to hear what answered.
