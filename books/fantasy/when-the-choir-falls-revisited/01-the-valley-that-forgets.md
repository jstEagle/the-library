## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgets, and I have learned to let it.

That is the first lesson of Hollowmere, and the hardest: you do not fight the forgetting. You water your herbs. You mend your fences. You let the fog take the ridgelines and the years take the rest, and if some morning you wake and cannot quite remember the sound of thunder — real thunder, the kind that answers when you call — then that is not a wound. That is the treatment working.

I have been here twelve years this autumn. I know because I count on the fence posts, one notch each year, cut into the third post from the gate where the wood is softest. Twelve notches now, and the post is going soft in other ways too, rot creeping up from the frost line, and someday soon I will have to replace it, and what then? Start the count again? Carve twelve fresh notches into new wood like a woman forging her own history? Or simply stop counting, which is what the valley would prefer, what the valley does to everything it touches?

I replaced the gate hinge instead. Coward's arithmetic.

* * *

My name here is Marren. Just Marren, no family name, because family names invite questions and questions invite memory and memory, in Hollowmere, is a kind of trespass. The valley sits in a fold of hills that no road bothers with, ringed by ridges of grey stone that hold the clouds like a cup holds water. Nothing magical can cross those ridges. I did not build them; I could not have built them; I am not entirely sure anyone built them at all. They simply are, the way a scar is, and whatever happened here long ago to make magic die at the boundary stone, it happened thoroughly. A candle flame burns the same inside as out. A dowsing rod hangs limp. And a storm-caller —

Well. A storm-caller is just a woman with bad knees and an herb garden.

I sell simples. Comfrey and calendula, yarrow for the bleeding, willow bark for the aching, valerian for people who cannot sleep, which in Hollowmere is nearly everyone, though they blame their beds or their marriages rather than admit the truth, which is that a place without magic has a silence in it that gets into the blood like cold water. They come to my cottage with their jars and their coins and their small complaints, and they call me a good herbalist, and I let them, because being good at something small is the safest thing a person can be.

Old Tamsin came Tuesday for her lungwort tincture and stayed an hour to tell me about her son's pigs. I listened the way I listen to everything now — fully, patiently, with the particular attention of someone who has nothing else she is allowed to want. Her son's pigs are fine. One of them is clever with latches. I could tell you more about that pig than I could tell you about the last twelve years of the world outside the ridges, and I have sometimes wondered whether that was the plan all along. Not exile as punishment. Exile as erasure, self-administered, daily, until the woman who once brought lightning down on the old capital was nobody at all, just Marren with the good comfrey and the crooked gate.

Some nights I test myself. I sit by the fire and try to remember the exact feeling of it — the gathering, the pressure behind the sternum, the sky leaning down toward me like a great animal lowering its head to be touched. I can remember that it happened. I can remember that I felt it. But the feeling itself slips away like water through cupped hands, and I am left holding only the shape of the holding.

I used to think that was mercy. Lately I think it might be theft.

* * *

Autumn came early this year, or so the villagers said, though the villagers say that every year, the way they complain about the miller's prices every year and forgive him every spring. I noticed it differently. I noticed it in the herbs. My feverfew bloomed two weeks shy and my sage went woody before I'd cut half of it, and the bees were restless in a way bees should not be in a valley where nothing changes. Bees are honest creatures. They know things about the weather that we have to pay almanacs to guess at badly.

And there was the light. I don't know how else to put it. The light coming over the western ridge had a color to it I hadn't seen since — well. Since before. A bruised, low, expectant light, the kind the sky makes when it is thinking about storms it hasn't been invited to have. In Hollowmere no such light should exist. The ridges keep the weather simple here. Rain, fog, the occasional honest snowfall. Weather with no ambitions.

But three mornings running, that light sat on the western ridge like a stranger on a wall, and on the third morning, I felt the tremor.

Not an earthquake. Hollowmere doesn't have earthquakes; even the ground here has agreed to behave. This was smaller and stranger — a shiver under my boots while I was digging angelica root, a single long pulse that traveled up the spade into my wrists and stopped, abruptly, like a word bitten off mid-syllable. The chickens complained. A slate slid off my roof and shattered on the path, and I stood looking at the pieces for longer than broken slate deserves, because the tremor had come from the west, from beyond the ridges, from the world I had spent twelve years pretending was a story I'd once heard.

Things outside do not reach into Hollowmere. That is the entire point of Hollowmere. The war passed us by — the war, the usurper, the burning of the archives, all of it, all of it happening somewhere over the horizon like weather in another country. The valley forgot the world and the world, in its turn, forgot the valley. That was our covenant, unspoken but absolute. We stay small. It stays away.

The tremor broke the covenant. Or announced its breaking. I couldn't tell which, standing there with dirt to the elbow and a dead slate at my feet, and I still can't, and it has been nine days.

* * *

Here is what I did about it, and I record it now because I am ashamed of it: nothing. I dug up my angelica. I hung it to dry. I told myself the tremor was deep mining, or a rockslide, or the hills settling their old bones, and I made supper, and I banked the fire, and I lay awake listening to the silence of the valley the way you listen to a sleeping child you suspect is only pretending.

Because here is the thing about safety that no one tells you when they promise it to you: after enough years, it stops feeling like safety and starts feeling like burial. You are warm. You are fed. You are, in every measurable way, alive. And yet there is a part of you — the part that called storms, the part that mattered, the part that was feared and needed and hunted — that lies under the floorboards of your life like a coffin lid nailed down from the inside, and every quiet day drives another nail.

I chose this. Let me be clear about that, because I will not have it said — I will not have *me* say — that it was done to me. I burned the granaries at Corvess. I called the lightning down on the old capital and I knew, I *knew*, what it would strike besides the armory, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was worse, or because I believed it was worse, or because I was twenty-six years old and certain, which amounts to the same thing. The exiles who are dragged out in chains get to hate their exilers. Those of us who walk out on our own legs get only ourselves to hate, and we are far less merciful judges.

So: the valley. The herbs. The fences. The deliberate smallness, practiced daily like scales on an instrument I swore never to play again. And underneath it, always, the ache — dull as an old break aching before rain, except that here, in the rainless quiet of Hollowmere, it never quite had weather to blame.

* * *

On the ninth day after the tremor, I walked up to the boundary stone.

I hadn't been there in four years. There is no reason to go; the stone marks nothing useful, just the place where the valley's silence begins, a squat grey pillar older than any kingdom, furred with moss on the north side. The villagers avoid it the way they avoid saying certain words. Children dare each other to touch it and come back reporting nothing, because there is nothing to report, because it is a rock. I had told myself for twelve years that it was a rock.

It took me most of the morning to climb there, which is also shame — a healthy woman taking a whole morning over a ridge walk, stopping twice to "admire the view," giving herself a dozen dignified reasons to turn back. I did not turn back. I crested the last shoulder of the pass in the bruised early-afternoon light, and there it was, the stone, exactly as I remembered, and I stood ten paces from it like a girl at a dance afraid to ask.

Then I felt it.

Not the tremor this time. Something thinner. A vibration in the air itself, in the space between the stone and my skin, faint as a plucked string heard through a wall. And beneath my feet, under the moss and the mountain and twelve years of careful forgetting, something answered — not rising, not waking, nothing so dramatic, just turning over in its sleep. The way a great animal turns over when it hears a familiar voice at the edge of a dream.

I knew that feeling. Gods help me, I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. Magic. Real magic, moving somewhere beyond the ridges, close enough that even the dead air of Hollowmere shivered at its passing. And it was not random. Storms are random. Wars are mostly random. This had direction. This had *intent*. Something out there was searching, methodically, the way you search a house room by room, opening doors.

I stood at the boundary stone until the light went copper and then grey, and the feeling faded, or moved on, or was swallowed by the ridges again. Then I walked home in the dark without a lantern, because my hands were shaking too badly to strike flint, and it wasn't fear. That is the confession underneath the confession. My hands were shaking and it was not fear, or not only fear, and the part of me that was not afraid was awake for the first time in twelve years and *hungry*, and I lay in bed that night hating it and listening to it and knowing, with the terrible clarity that comes at three in the morning, that nothing in Hollowmere would ever be small again.

* * *

Two days later, the girl arrived.

But I am ahead of myself, and a woman who has spent twelve years practicing patience should be able to practice it a little longer. Two days after the boundary stone, I went back to my routine. I cut comfrey. I mended the fence by the goat pen, which had developed opinions about escape. I sold yarrow to the cooper's wife and lied pleasantly about the weather. I carved nothing into the fence post, because the year was not over, and because I found, examining the twelve notches, that I no longer wanted to count. Counting implies you believe the years will continue in the same key. Something in the air over the western ridge had changed the key.

On the second evening, the fog came up the valley thicker than usual, thick enough to drink, and I was banking my fire when I heard the gate creak — the hinge I had replaced, the coward's arithmetic, singing its one note into the dark.

I opened the door expecting Tamsin, or the cooper's wife, or a lost peddler.

The doorstep held a child. Maybe seven years old, barefoot, half-frozen, her lips gone the blue of late twilight, wearing a coat meant for someone three times her size. She looked up at me, and her eyes were wrong for her face — too old, too steady, eyes that had already seen the thing children should never see and had decided, somewhere on the road, to keep walking anyway.

And the valley — the silent, magic-dead, carefully forgetting valley — cracked around her like thin ice under a boot.

I heard it happen. A sound like a distant bell struck underwater, a single clear note that no bell in Hollowmere could make, and the fog on my doorstep bent toward the child the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone, and every hair on my arms stood up, and twelve years of buried instinct rose in me so fast and so hard that I nearly fell against the doorframe.

She looked at me. She opened her mouth. And she spoke — words in a language I had not heard since my grandmother's kitchen, a language banned before my mother was born, syllables that predated every crown on every head in every kingdom, and I understood none of it and all of it at once, the way you understand a song from before you learned words.

The child said something. Once. Twice. Patiently, the way you speak to someone deaf.

And the last nail in the floorboard of my life pulled loose with a sound only I could hear.
