## 2. A Child at the Threshold

The frost came early that year, which I took as a personal insult. Twelve years in the valley had taught me its moods the way a wife learns a husband's silences: the way fog pooled in the hollows before rain, the way the goats grew sullen before an earthquake no one else would feel. The valley was magic-dead, but it was not dead-dead. It had opinions. Mostly it opinioned about my fence posts.

I was mending one of them when the light went strange.

Not dark—strange. The kind of wrongness you feel in your teeth before your eyes understand it. Like a bell rung somewhere below hearing. I straightened up, hammer in hand, and looked west toward the ridge, because that's where trouble always came from. Trouble and tax collectors, though we'd been too poor and too forgotten for the latter.

Nothing there. Just pines and the grey smear of cloud and the particular silence the valley wore like a second skin.

That was the thing about Hollowmere—the name was a joke some dead settler had made and everyone since had been too tired to correct. Magic did not work here. It didn't sputter or fade; it simply refused to exist, the way sound refuses to exist underwater. I had chosen it for exactly that reason. A storm-caller without her storms is just a woman with bad knees and a herb garden, and for twelve years I had been the best bad-kneed herbalist in three parishes.

So when the silence *cracked*—when I felt it crack, the way you feel ice give under your boot—I dropped the hammer and ran for my cottage like a woman half my age, which is to say badly.

* * *

Let me tell you what twelve years of deliberate smallness looks like from the inside, so you'll understand why my hands were shaking on the latch.

It looks like this: chamomile drying in bundles from the rafters. A ledger kept in deliberately boring handwriting. Conversations with the miller's wife about turnips, conducted with the careful enthusiasm of a woman who has practiced having no opinions. Every morning I woke and told myself, *you are nobody*, the way other people say their prayers, and every night I fell asleep grateful that nobody is the safest thing a person can be.

And underneath all of it, like a stone at the bottom of a well: the ache. Because safety, I had learned, feels exactly like burial if you live in it long enough. You get comfortable. You get *good* at being comfortable. And then some ordinary Tuesday the light goes strange and you realize you've spent twelve years holding your breath.

I opened the door.

There was a child on my step.

She was maybe seven, small for it, dressed in clothes that had been fine once and were now mostly mud and frost. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, the way you'd cut it running. Her lips were blue. She was standing—not sitting, *standing*—perfectly upright on my doorstep like a little soldier presenting herself for inspection, and she was looking at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors I'd stopped owning years ago.

The expression of someone deciding whether to trust you, and losing.

"Inside," I said, because whatever else she was, she was freezing, and I am many terrible things but I am not cruel to children. "Come inside, love."

She didn't move. She said something instead—a string of syllables, quick and liquid, with a rhythm to them that snagged on something deep in my skull like a fishhook catching on old cloth.

And the valley *screamed*.

Not aloud. Nothing so honest. But every hair on my arms stood up, and the frost on my railing slid off all at once as if shaken, and in the well behind the cottage the water rose two inches against a rope that hadn't moved in years. I felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm front—the whole valley's dead air shuddering around this small frozen child like a struck bell trying desperately to remember how to ring.

Magic. She was soaked in it. Dripping with it. Standing on my doorstep radiating it into a place that had spent centuries refusing to acknowledge such things existed, and the refusal was *breaking*, cracking around her like thin ice over a pond, and through those cracks I could hear—

Oh, I could hear it.

You spend twelve years not hearing something and you think you've forgotten. You haven't. True magic sounds like the moment before lightning. It sounds like the held breath of the sky. I had called storms down on the old capital with that sound in my ears, and men had died, and I had sworn—on ash, on graves, on the only oath that ever meant anything to me—that I would never hear it again.

Here it was. On my doorstep. Seven years old and hypothermic.

"No," I said, to her, to the valley, to whatever gods were still taking messages. "No, absolutely not."

She said the syllables again, more urgently, and reached out and grabbed my sleeve with a hand like a bird's foot, and pulled.

And I—fool, coward, ex-storm-caller, current herbalist with a reputation for excellent cough syrup—let her pull me inside.

* * *

Warming a half-frozen child is one of the few pieces of knowledge that transfers cleanly between lives. Blankets. Fire built small and fed slow. Warm water with honey, sipped, not gulped. I'd learned it in another country, in another life, patching up soldiers whose names I'd been careful never to ask. My hands remembered even as the rest of me stood apart, arms crossed, watching myself do it and thinking: *this is how it starts. This is exactly how it started last time.*

Because here is what nobody tells you about exile: it doesn't take away what you are. It just makes you responsible for pretending, every single day, forever. And the pretending gets easier until suddenly it isn't, until a child grabs your sleeve and the ice cracks and you remember that under the herbs and the fence posts and the turnip conversations there is a woman who once made the sky kneel.

She drank. She shivered. Color crept back into her face by degrees, and I sat across from her doing breathing exercises I'd invented for myself and had never once needed until now.

"Can you tell me your name?" I tried. Common tongue, slow and clear.

Syllables back. Liquid, rolling, with a cadence that rose at the end like a question or a lament. Not the trade-tongue. Not the lowland dialects. Not the clipped imperial speech the usurper's criers used, not the border patois the traders brought through twice a year. I knew perhaps six languages well enough to curse in and two well enough to dream in, and this was none of them.

But—and here my stomach dropped through the floorboards—it wasn't *unfamiliar*. That rhythm. That rise-and-fall, like water finding its level. I had heard it before, or the ghost of it, in a book I had burned the pages of one by one over a period of four years because keeping it whole was death and throwing it away whole was unthinkable.

Grandmother's primer. Banned before I was born. I'd memorized what I could and destroyed the rest, and what I could was fragments—nursery rhymes, mostly, and one prayer, and a list of words for kinds of weather that don't have names in any living tongue.

The girl said something else, and one word in it rang that buried bell.

*"Vessia."*

I went very still. Outside, the wind moved through the pines, and beneath the wind, faint as a pulse under a wrist, I felt the valley's silence straining—holding, but barely, the way a dam holds, the way a lie holds.

"You know that word?" I asked, stupidly, in a language she didn't speak.

She looked at me. And then, with the grave patience of the very young dealing with the very slow, she pointed one chapped finger at herself and said the word again. "*Issel.*"

Her name. Or part of it. Or the word for *child*, or *key*, or *the end of everything*—I couldn't know, not yet, not from nursery rhymes and a burned book. But she said it the way people say their own names, with ownership, and I filed it away in the part of my mind that had once catalogued troop movements and now catalogued which neighbors liked which tea.

"Issel," I repeated.

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch. Trust, or exhaustion wearing trust's coat. Hard to tell the difference at seven.

She slept eventually, curled in my bed like a comma, and I sat by the fire and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Here is what I should have done, and I want it on the record that I knew it even as I failed to do it. I should have waited for morning, wrapped her in every blanket I owned, carried her down to the miller's wife—who had lost children of her own and had softness to spare—and let the valley absorb her the way it absorbed everything. Let her be a foundling. Let her be ordinary. Ordinary was the only protection this valley had ever offered anyone, and it worked, it had worked for me for twelve years, and I owed it nothing less than to hand this child the same armor.

Instead I sat by my fire listening to the silence strain around her sleeping body like a net around a living thing, and I thought about the word *Vessia*, and I thought about my grandmother's voice saying it, once, in a kitchen that smelled of burnt milk, in a language the king's men were already hanging people for speaking.

"The door remembers."

I hadn't thought of that phrase in decades. It was the one line of the primer's opening prayer that had stuck—not because I understood it, but because Grandmother had said it differently from the rest. The rest she recited. That line she *said*, the way you say a thing you believe.

A child walks out of nowhere speaking a dead language, carrying enough raw magic to crack a valley that has been magically dead since before maps, and the first recognizable word out of her mouth is the name of a country that officially does not exist.

I am not a clever woman. I want that understood. Clever women survive by thinking three moves ahead; I survived by running and hiding and growing chamomile. But even I could do that sum, and the answer frightened me in a way the inquisitors never had. They had frightened me for my life. This frightened me for my *quiet*—and I had learned, over twelve long years, that my quiet was the only thing I loved.

The fire popped. The girl murmured in her sleep, and the murmur rippled out through the room, and the flames leaned toward her—all of them, every candle and coal in the hearth, bending gently toward the bed like flowers toward sun.

I put them right with a poker and steady hands and lay down on the floor beside the bed, because clearly I wasn't going to be any use anywhere else.

* * *

Near dawn, the tremor came.

Small. The valley got them sometimes—old bones settling, the miller said, and I let him believe it. But this one rolled through from the west, from beyond the ridge, from the world where magic still lived and kings still burned things, and it carried a frequency underneath it that my body knew the way a dog knows thunder coming.

Something had broken out there. Something large. And things that break large tend to send out ripples, and ripples tend to wash up small frozen children on the doorsteps of women who were promised, *promised*, that the world had forgotten them.

Issel woke at first light. She looked at me, took inventory of the cottage with quick dark eyes—the herbs, the ledger, the iron poker I'd left by the door—and then she pointed at the eastern wall, past the village, past the ridge, past everything, and spoke a full sentence in that rolling dead tongue.

I understood none of it. I understood all of it. Some things translate without words: the pointing finger, the urgent set of the mouth, the way she said the sentence twice, slower, as if I were the child and she was being patient with me.

*We have to go. We have to go now.*

"Not yet," I said, in the common tongue, uselessly. "Eat something first."

And then, because the valley's silence cracked again—audibly this time, a sound like a great sheet of ice giving way somewhere above the world, and every bird in Hollowmere went up off the trees at once, all together, all silent after—I added, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine:

"And then you're going to teach me what you just said."
