## 3. Words Older Than Crowns

The child slept in my bed and I slept in the chair, which told you everything about the order of priorities in my house. Twelve years of careful, deliberate smallness, and in one night I had surrendered my bed, my firewood, and most of a jar of honey to a stranger who spoke like the inside of a tomb.

She slept the way soldiers sleep—flat on her back, hands folded, eyes closed but not *resting*. Twice in the night I checked her breathing by holding a feather above her lips, and twice she opened one eye without moving anything else, which was frankly unsettling. Children are supposed to be noisy sleepers. They're supposed to sprawl and mutter and steal blankets. This girl lay like something placed, not something sleeping.

I spent the dark hours doing what I always did when frightened: working. I stripped and re-dried the yarrow. I mended a fence rail that didn't need mending. I sat at my table with a candle stub and stared at the floorboard under the third knot from the wall, the loose one, the one I had pried up exactly once before and sworn never to pry up again.

Then I pried it up.

Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a book that could get me hanged in three kingdoms and merely exiled in a fourth. My grandmother's primer. *A Child's First Words in the Old Tongue*, the title read, in letters that predated every crown currently claiming authority over me, which was rather the point. The usurper's edicts were specific about banned books—they burned grammars first, then genealogies, then maps, then people—and this book was all four crimes bound in one cover. A grammar of a dead language. Genealogies of keepers. Maps of places that no longer appeared anywhere official. And written in it, between the lessons, in my grandmother's cramped hand, the names of people who had been erased so thoroughly that reading them aloud felt like performing a small resurrection.

I hadn't touched it in twelve years. That was part of the exile's bargain I'd made with myself: no old tongue, no old books, no old magic. Be small. Be forgettable. Be an herbalist with chapped hands and opinions about drainage, nothing more.

But there was a child in my bed speaking a language older than the edicts, and the valley itself—the valley that swallowed magic like sand swallows rain—had cracked around her voice last night like ice on a spring pond. Whatever she was, she was not going to be explained by my stock of explanations. So I needed the book, and needing the book meant admitting what I was, if only to myself, if only at midnight, if only over a candle stub.

Exile, I have learned, does not erase what you know. It just makes you pay rent on it.

* * *

Morning came gray and unhurried. The girl woke when I woke—or perhaps she had never fully slept—and watched me make porridge with the fixed attention of someone cataloguing a strange animal's habits for future reference.

"Porridge," I said, setting the bowl down. "It's oats. You boil them. It's not complicated, even for me."

She looked at the bowl. She looked at me. Then she said something in that language—that low, layered speech where every word seemed to carry furniture underneath it, meanings stacked like stones in a wall—and touched her own chest.

"Your name?" I asked, feeling foolish. "You're telling me your name?"

She repeated it. Two syllables, soft-edged. *Issel.* Or something close; the second syllable slid in a way my mouth didn't want to follow, a sound that belonged to a different architecture of throat and tongue.

"Issel." I tried it again. Her eyes flickered—not quite approval, but a downgrading of alarm. Progress, by the standards of hostage negotiations.

She ate the porridge in small, precise spoonfuls, and while she ate, I opened the primer flat on the table beside my own bowl, angled away from her, as if the book might be embarrassed to be seen. Lesson One of my grandmother's primer began, naturally, with greetings, because grandmothers believe children should be polite before they are literate. *Vesseth anna,* it said. *Peace to you.* The greeting of a country that officially no longer existed, offered to a child who might well be its last citizen.

I worked through the morning the way you work through a locked door: patiently, with bad tools. The primer gave me vocabulary—maybe three hundred words, the survival kit of a language, the words you'd need to buy bread and greet elders and name the parts of a house. It did not give me grammar worth anything beyond simple sentences, and the girl—Issel—spoke in sentences that unspooled long and complex, clauses nesting inside clauses like boxes within boxes. But I had fragments. And she, bless her, had patience and repetition, which is more than most teachers manage.

She said the same phrase over and over. That was the strange thing, the thing that kept the hair on my arms standing at attention. Most children with a word or two of a language repeat what gets them food and warmth. *Eat. Water. Where is my mother?* Issel had a phrase she returned to maybe twenty times that morning, whenever the silence stretched too long, whenever I failed to understand, whenever some private worry crossed her face like weather:

*Ath vessa kirem.*

Three words. I found *ath* in the primer easily enough—it was a possessive article, *the*, but an important kind of *the*, the definite article reserved for things singular and sacred. Not *a door*. Not *any door*. *The* door, the way you'd say *the sun* or *the god*. My grandmother had underlined it twice and noted in the margin: *"Ath" only for what cannot be replaced. Use sparingly.*

*Kirem* was harder. It wasn't in the survival vocabulary. I hunted through the appendices—the primer had appendices, because my grandmother had never met a subject she wouldn't dig beneath—and found it in a glossary of ritual terms, marked with the little symbol she used for *dangerous knowledge*: *kirem*, from a root meaning both *to remember* and *to hold open*. One word for remembering and keeping-open, as if in the minds of the people who built this language those were the same act. As if forgetting was a kind of closing, and memory a kind of doorway.

Which left *vessa*. And *vessa* stopped me cold, because I knew that word without looking it up. Every exiled child of the old blood knew it, the way children know the names of lost pets. Vessia. The country that was. The name that had been struck from maps and records and gravestones until saying it aloud was its own small crime. The Door of Vessia—the story my grandmother told instead of bedtime stories, the passage that stood at the end of everything, the way out that was also a way back—

I set down the spoon. My hand was not entirely steady.

"Ath vessa kirem," Issel said again, watching me, and this time she added something—a fourth word, or a modification, a suffix that curled around the end of *kirem* like a hand closing. I bent over the primer. The suffix was *-enn*, and the appendix said, in my grandmother's handwriting grown suddenly smaller and more careful: *-enn marks the keeper's tense. Used only by those sworn to a thing. "It remembers" becomes, with -enn, "it remembers because I am bound to make it so."*

The child was seven years old, maybe. She had a scrape on her knee healing wrong and a habit of sleeping like a laid sword. And she was conjugating verbs in the keeper's tense.

* * *

By afternoon I had assembled the sentence the way you assemble a shattered bowl—carefully, knowing it would never hold water quite the same way again.

"The Door remembers," I said aloud, testing it in Common Tongue, and it sounded absurd, a nursery rhyme, a fortune-teller's patter. In the old tongue it sounded like a load-bearing wall.

Issel nodded. Not the vague nodding of a child agreeing with adults to end a conversation—a sharp, deliberate nod, the nod of confirmation. *Yes. Finally. You have it.*

"The door remembers," I said again. "And you—" I pointed at her, then fumbled through the primer for the pronoun structure, then gave up on grammar and simply tapped the page where *-enn* was defined, then tapped her chest.

Her chin lifted. Something came into her face that I had no translation for—pride, grief, and duty fused into one expression, worn by a face still round with childhood. She put her hand flat on her sternum, over the heart, the gesture the primer's illustrations showed for swearing witness.

Keeper's blood. The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way true thoughts do, skipping all the reasonable intermediate steps. The keepers of the Door of Vessia had been a lineage, a quiet family of quiet people scattered through the old provinces, and the usurper had spent eleven years hunting them—not because they were dangerous, but because they were *necessary*, and he understood, in his bureaucratic soul, that a lock means nothing if you control neither key nor keyhole. Burn the archives. Burn the speakers. Burn the keepers. Erase the language and you erase the instructions; erase the instructions and the Door is just a shape in a hillside forever.

Except he'd missed one. One small girl, sent—sent where? Sent by whom?—out into the winter, out of the burning provinces, down whatever roads remained unmapped, until she'd walked half-frozen to the door of the one woman in the valley who could still read the primer.

Or not walked randomly. The thought arrived second, colder. *Chosen.* Keepers chose their refuges carefully, my grandmother had written. There were no accidents in the old networks. Which meant somewhere behind this child's silence about her journey was a dead or desperate adult who had known about me—who had known what I was, what I'd been, what slept coiled and dormant under my skin in this magic-dead valley like a seed in frozen ground.

I looked at Issel, and Issel looked back, and I had the distinct impression of being weighed. Measured. Found—possibly—useful.

"Oh, don't look at me like that," I told her. "I'm an herbalist. I treat fevers and I complain about the price of salt. That's the whole of my biography."

*Ath vessa kiremenn,* she said, gentle and implacable, and pressed her hand to her heart again.

* * *

That evening I climbed to the ridge above my house, because I needed to see the valley entire, needed to remind myself of its shape and its promise. The valley that forgets. That was the old name for it, though no map recorded it under any name at all. Magic pooled here like water in a basin and drained away into stone, and anything magical that entered grew quiet and stayed quiet. For twelve years it had been my armor. Inside these borders I was nobody. No storm-caller. No exile. Just Zephyrine Hartwell, who grew good willow bark and bad turnips.

From the ridge I could see the whole bowl of it: my croft, the creek, the terraced herb beds going silver in the dusk. Safe. Small. Buried alive, but buried comfortably, and I had made my peace with the difference—or told myself I had, which in the dark counts for nearly the same thing.

And on the far ridge, where the pass came down from the north, there was a tremor in the air. Not an earthquake. Nothing so honest. It was more like watching heat-shimmer in winter, a wrongness in the light along the ridgeline, as if the world outside the valley were a page and someone were writing on the other side of it. The valley's silence held—but for the first time in twelve years, I saw it strain. Felt it, rather, in the place behind my breastbone where my power used to live, that sealed room in the house of myself. Something was pressing on the walls of the world from outside. Something was coming down the pass, patient as bureaucracy.

Behind me, small feet crunched on frost-brittle grass. Issel had followed me up, wrapped in my spare blanket, and she stood at my side looking north with the same fixed attention she'd given the porridge.

"Ath vessa kiremenn," she said quietly. Not a question. A report. An update from a very small officer of a very old government.

"Yes," I said. "I heard you the first forty times."

She took my hand. Her fingers were freezing, because of course they were; the child treated blankets as optional equipment. And she turned my hand over, palm up, and looked at it—at the faint pale scarring across the heel of my palm that any herbalist would call burn scars and any keeper would call something else—and then she looked up at me with an expression of such grave, ancient patience that I laughed, actually laughed, a short ugly sound the valley probably hadn't heard in years.

"You know what I am," I said. "Don't you. You knew before you knocked on my door."

Issel said nothing, which was answer enough. She tucked my scarred hand under the blanket against her chest, the way children pocket treasures, and we stood together on the ridge watching the wrongness shimmer along the northern pass, and I understood that my twelve years of smallness had just been spent. All of them, at once, like a purse dropped on stones.

Because here is what exile doesn't tell you: the valley can make the world forget you, but it cannot make you forget the world. Somewhere out past that ridge, archives were burning and speakers of old tongues were dying and a child had been mailed through the winter like a letter addressed to the last person who could read her. And tomorrow, or the day after, whoever wrote on the far side of the sky would come down the pass into my valley, following her.

I could stay. I could hide her in the herb beds, teach her turnip cultivation, let the valley drink her secret the way it drank mine. For a while. Valleys forget; armies don't.

Or I could go down to the croft, pry up the floorboard properly, and start reading my grandmother's primer from the beginning—Lesson One, *vesseth anna*, peace to you—as a refresher course in everything I'd sworn to bury. Because the phrase the child carried wasn't a plea. It was instructions. *The door remembers.* And doors, my grandmother had written in a margin I wouldn't find for another week, *doors do not open themselves.*

In the morning, there was smoke on the northern horizon—thin, orderly, deliberate, the smoke of things being burned by people taking their time.

And on the southern road, dust. A traveler. One man, walking, with a rolled bundle on his back that I recognized, even at that distance, even after twelve years, by its shape alone.

A chart case.
