## 4. The Cartographer of Nowhere

There are three ways to lose a country. You can conquer it, which is loud and expensive and leaves scars everyone can point to. You can abandon it, which is quieter, and the scars heal over like old fence posts swallowed by hedgerow. Or you can do what the Empire did to Vessia: you can simply stop drawing it.

No edict. No burning—well, some burning, but that came later. Just a decree from the Office of Imperial Cartography, where I had spent nine years learning to make the world legible to men who wanted it small. *Effective immediately, the territory formerly designated Vessia-on-the-Maps shall be omitted from all future renderings.* I remember the exact phrasing because I wrote it. My hand. My ink. Nine years of apprenticeship, and the first document of consequence they trusted me with was an erasure.

I signed my name beneath it, and then, three months later, I committed the only honest act of my career: I drew the country back.

That is why I was walking up a valley road in the rain, in autumn, with a forged travel writ in my coat and a chart against my chest that would get me hanged in any of the eleven provinces that officially contained no such chart. That is why I was hunting a rumor about a woman who sold herbs at the edge of a place where nothing strange was supposed to happen.

The rumor had come to me in a tavern in Lowmarsh, as rumors do, wrapped in the smell of spilled beer and delivered by a tinker who talked too much. A valley, he said, where his compass spun like a drunk. Where birds flew around instead of over. Where a herb-woman lived alone, and where—this was the part that made me put down my cup—a child had turned up on her doorstep speaking words the tinker swore made his teeth ache.

I did not believe him about the compass. I believed him about the child. Because I have spent my life collecting languages the way other men collect coins, and there are perhaps four people left alive who could speak Old Vessian fluently, and every one of them was supposed to be dead or hiding very well indeed.

So. The valley. Which was not on my map, which was the whole point of maps, and therefore either did not exist or existed so thoroughly that reality itself had agreed to look away.

I found the boundary stone by accident, tripping over it in the dark like a drunkard finding a curb. It was a squat granite thing, older than the Empire's oldest monument, carved with letters worn nearly smooth—and here is the thing I will swear to until they hang me: when I put my hand on it, my compass stopped spinning and pointed, for the first time in two days, directly at a cottage with lamplit windows halfway up the hillside.

Pointed, I should say, the way a dog points. Eagerly. As if the needle had been waiting.

* * *

She opened the door before I knocked, which told me she'd seen me coming, which told me she watched her road, which told me she was careful. Everything about Zephyrine Hartwell—I learned her name later; at the time she was just *the herb-woman with the eyes of a cornered hawk*—everything about her said *careful*. The way she stood in her doorway taking up exactly enough space and no more. The way her gaze went to my boots, my hands, my coat, cataloguing threats in the order a soldier would.

"Good evening," I said. "I'm told you're the person to see about feverfew."

"I'm told wrong, then. Feverfew grows wild here. Anyone could show you."

"But you'd know which plants were worth the cutting." I smiled the smile I use on customs officers. "Wren Oduya. Surveyor. I'm mapping the high passes for—" I produced the forged writ with the flourish of long practice. "—the Provincial Commission."

She read it upside down without touching it, which was either rudeness or competence, and I suspected both. "Provincial Commission hasn't commissioned a survey in six years," she said. "Budget cuts."

Ah. So it was going to be one of those conversations.

"Independent work, then," I said smoothly. "There's always someone willing to pay for good maps."

"There is," she agreed, and something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The particular wariness of one liar meeting another and calculating whose lie is bigger. "You'd better come in out of the rain. If you stand on my step much longer, the neighbors will talk, and my nearest neighbor is a mile off and talks to her goats."

Inside smelled of drying herbs and woodsmoke and, underneath it, something else—something faint and mineral, like the air after lightning. I filed that away. I file everything away; it's a disease of the profession.

And then I saw the child.

She sat in the corner by the hearth, wrapped in a blanket that had been mended more times than it had been whole, watching me with the absolute stillness of a wild thing deciding whether I was predator or weather. Small. Seven, maybe. Dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, with the particular thinness of a child who has walked a long way on too little food.

"Yours?" I asked, though everything about the cottage said *one plate, one chair, one life deliberately shrunk to fit through narrow gaps.*

"She arrived," Hartwell said, in the tone of a woman answering a question she hadn't decided how to answer. "A week ago. Half-frozen. She doesn't speak."

"She speaks," I said. "I heard her."

Hartwell went very still. "You were listening at my window?"

"I was standing on your path working up the nerve to knock, and she was singing. Under her breath. Through the glass." I looked at the child. "That song. Where did you learn it?"

The child stared at me. And then—this is the part I keep returning to, the part that rearranged the furniture of my entire understanding of the last decade—she answered. Not in the trade-tongue, not in Imperial, not in any of the eleven provincial dialects I carry around in my skull like ballast.

She spoke in Old Vessian. Fluent. Unselfconscious. The accent of the eastern coast, the vowel shapes of a language that had been erased from every official record twelve years ago, spoken by a child who could not possibly be older than the erasure itself.

*"Where are the keepers? Has anyone come for me?"*

I am not ashamed to say my knees went loose. I sat down rather heavily on the stool Hartwell shoved behind me, and I stared at this small, impossible girl, and I heard myself say, in a language I hadn't spoken aloud since I was a student copying contraband texts by candlelight:

*"No one has come. But I know the way to the Door."*

The child's face changed. It was like watching dawn happen to a landscape—slow, then all at once. She crossed the room, took my face in her two cold little hands, and said, with the gravity of an empress issuing decrees:

*"Then you must not forget it."*

* * *

After that, Hartwell and I had a conversation in the kitchen while the child slept, and it was less a conversation than two people fencing with the lights out.

"You knew that language," she said. Not a question.

"I knew it once. Academically." I turned my cup on its saucer. "It's not taught anymore. It's not *allowed* to be taught anymore. I learned it from a text I was supposed to be cataloguing for destruction."

"And you kept it."

"I kept lots of things I was supposed to destroy. It's a character flaw." I met her eyes. "Your turn. You understood her too. Every word. And you've been living in a valley where, if my instruments are to be believed, magic goes to die—which means you came here specifically, which means you're hiding from something, which means that name you haven't given me isn't the one you were born with."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked against the shutters. In the corner, the child breathed slow and even, and I noticed that even her sleeping seemed deliberate.

"Hartwell," she said finally. "Zephyrine Hartwell. Whether it's the name I was born with is none of your business."

"Fair. Oduya, Wren. Whether I'm really a surveyor is equally none of yours."

"Except it is," she said, "because you just promised a child you know the way to the Door of Vessia."

The word landed between us like a dropped knife. *Door.* She said it the way believers say the names of saints—with hunger and dread in equal measure.

"So you know what it is," I said slowly.

"I know what it's *called.* I know it's real, which is more than most people can say, and I know that knowing those two facts has kept me awake for twelve years." She leaned forward. "Here's how this works, cartographer. You tell me what you know, and I'll decide whether you're a fool, a spy, or the first useful thing to walk up my road in a decade."

"And if I'm a spy?"

"Then you'll have lied beautifully, and I'll grieve, and the child will still need whatever you actually know. Because I can't give her what she's asking for. No one in this valley can. We're the one place in the world that can't help her, and I think—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the admission— "I think that's why she ended up here. Sent somewhere the thing chasing her couldn't follow."

I should have lied. Professionally speaking, lying was the correct move: reveal nothing, assess everything, keep my exit options open. I have survived nine years in the Office of Imperial Cartography and two years running from it precisely because I never gave anyone the whole truth.

But there was a child asleep in the corner who had taken my face in her hands and told me not to forget, and there was a woman across the table whose fear had the specific shape of mine, and I was tired, gentlemen of the jury, I was so tired of being the only man alive who remembered a whole country.

So I unbuttoned my coat, and I took out the chart.

* * *

I watched her unroll it the way you watch someone defuse something. Her hands hovered over the vellum without touching, tracing coastlines that no longer appeared on any map printed in the last twelve years. Rivers with their true names. The drowned provinces, marked in my own annotation: *levees broken, 4th year of the Usurpation, by order.* And in the northeast, in a fold of hills I had reconstructed from fragments—from a burned atlas page, a mad pilgrim's testimony, a surveyor's marginalia smuggled out in a boot heel—the city that wasn't there.

"Vessia-on-the-Maps," she whispered.

"The Empire calls it nothing now. Officially, the land was always imperial; the charts showing otherwise are forgeries, and possession of them is treason." I tapped the fold of hills. "But it's real. I've spent two years proving it. There's a city there, Hartwell. Full of people who speak dead languages. A whole country of the erased, hiding inside a blank space on the map."

"And the Door?"

"That's the part I don't know." I said it plainly, because she'd know a bluff when she heard one. "Every source agrees it exists. Every source disagrees about where. The keepers guarded its location with their lives, and apparently with their lives' success, because when the soldiers came for them—" I glanced at the sleeping child, and hated myself a little for the glance— "whatever they knew died with them. Except."

"Except it didn't," Hartwell said quietly. "Because she's here."

"Because she's here." I rolled the chart closed. "Now you. You said you know what it's called. You said knowing has kept you awake. Nobody loses sleep over a door unless they know what's behind it—or unless they know what opening it costs."

For a long moment she looked at the fire, and I watched twelve years of careful smallness war with something older and larger behind her face. When she spoke, her voice was low, like someone reading a confession in church.

"My grandmother taught me things the Empire banned. Storm-calling. The old craft. I used it, once, in a way the capital didn't forgive, and I ran, and I found the one place in the world where the craft goes silent, and I buried myself here." Her jaw tightened. "I know what the Door is because the old songs mention it. A passage out. Refuge. For people like us—the ones the new order wants erased."

"A storm-caller," I said. And then, because I am occasionally an idiot: "*That's* why the valley. That's why your instruments—my instruments—"

"Yes. Magic can't follow me in. Neither can anything else that runs on it." She looked at me with those cornered-hawk eyes. "Including, presumably, whatever sent that child."

"Which raises the question," I said, "of what happens when she has to leave."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves.

"That," said Zephyrine Hartwell, "is tomorrow's problem."

But we both knew it wasn't. We both knew it was the only problem, wearing a week of borrowed time like a coat. Two liars sitting at a table over a forbidden map, each having just handed the other a weapon, each needing the other's weapon more than our own. I knew where the Door might be. She knew how doors like that were opened. And between us, sleeping in a mended blanket, was a child who carried the key in her blood and her tongue and her terrible patience.

I stayed the night in her barn, on hay that smelled of rosemary, with the chart under my shirt and my compass on my chest pointing—not north, not south, but steadily toward the cottage, toward the child, toward the northeast and a city that wasn't there.

Near dawn, hoofbeats woke me. Not one rider. Many, moving fast along the valley's western approach, and carrying, if I wasn't mistaken, the particular jingle of imperial tack.

I was up and at the cottage window before my boots were fully on, shaking Hartwell's shoulder, whispering the only sentence that mattered:

"They're not here for us. They're sweeping the valley—and there's a wounded man limping up your road ahead of them, wearing their uniform, and bleeding from it."
