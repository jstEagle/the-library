## 5. What the Usurper Burns

I had been in the valley four days when I understood that I was not, strictly speaking, a guest.

A guest is invited. A guest is offered tea. What I was, as far as Zephyrine Hartwell was concerned, was a problem that had arrived with its own boots and shown no sign of walking back out of them. She fed me anyway. That was the thing about her — she fed you while looking at you the way a woman looks at a weed growing through her floorboards, calculating whether to pull it or simply live around it.

I earned my keep the only way I know how: by drawing. Maps, mostly. The valley had never been properly surveyed — no valley that wants to stay forgotten ever is — and I spent my mornings pacing out distances from the herb garden to the ridge line, sketching the stream's meanders, noting where the soil went sour and where the fences leaned. It was honest work. It was also camouflage. A man with a notebook and a purpose looks like he belongs somewhere. A man standing still in a strange valley looks like exactly what he is: a fugitive with excellent penmanship.

The child watched me work.

Issel — for so Zephyrine had decided the girl was called, having extracted the name on the third day through a process involving bread, patience, and what I can only describe as competitive pointing — sat on the fence rail each morning with her heels drumming against the wood and her eyes tracking my pencil like it might do something interesting if she stared hard enough. Children are honest audiences. They don't pretend your work is fascinating, and they don't pretend it isn't.

On the fourth morning she slid off the fence, crossed the yard, and tapped one small finger on my chart of the valley.

"That," she said, in her language — the old one, the one that predated every crown on the continent — "is wrong."

Then she pointed at the stream, and said something else, and drew in the dirt with her toe a shape that was unmistakably a bend in the watercourse that I had, in fact, drawn incorrectly, because I had surveyed it from the ridge and the ridge lies about water. Rivers always lie to high ground. It's their one reliable virtue.

"You're telling me my map is wrong," I said, in Imperial Common, which she did not speak, and she looked at me with the flat patience of someone who has already explained this twice to idiots, and I felt — absurdly, wonderfully — at home.

I redrew the stream. She supervised. When I finished, she studied it, nodded once with tremendous gravity, and patted my knee, and I thought: well. There it is. Four days in exile and I've been peer-reviewed by a seven-year-old.

It was the best morning I'd had in two years.

* * *

The news came up the valley on the fifth day, riding on a mule.

The mule belonged to a peddler named Osk who came through the western pass perhaps four times a year, trading salt and iron nails for Zephyrine's dried herbs, and carrying gossip the way other men carry plague — carefully, and with an awareness that it would eventually get out. Osk was a small man made almost entirely of weatherproofing, all oilskin and squint, and he had the peddler's gift of knowing things without appearing to have asked.

He also had the peddler's gift of knowing when not to talk, and I noticed that he waited until Issel had been shooed inside before he said anything at all. That should have told us everything. It told me most of it.

"They've started burning the archives," he said, hunkered by Zephyrine's hearth with his hands around a cup he wasn't drinking. "Officially it's a sanitation campaign. Unofficially—" He shrugged, a gesture that contained multitudes and none of them good. "You know how it is. First they burn the records. Then they burn the people who could have corrected the record."

Zephyrine stood by the window with her arms folded, half-turned away, the way she stood whenever the world outside was mentioned — as if it were a debt collector at the door. "Which archives?"

"All of them. Ashfall first. Then the monastery at Corven Hold — the monks got a night's warning, some of them ran, most of them didn't. And now the provincial registries." Osk turned his cup slowly. "There's a list, Hartwell. An actual written list, circulating among the inquisitors' clerks. Languages that are forbidden to be spoken. Not discouraged. Forbidden. Anyone heard using one gets questioned. Questioned people get registered. Registered people get—"

"I know how lists work," Zephyrine said.

"Then you know this isn't like the last purges." Osk's voice dropped, and I saw his hands were not quite steady. "This is thorough. This is a man who learned from the last time. Aurelian Vess doesn't want obedience anymore. He wants amnesia. You can't rule people who remember being something else."

I had gone very still by the fire, and I don't think either of them noticed, because they were both watching Osk, but inside my skull a door I'd kept carefully shut was rattling on its hinges. Because I knew those archives. I had *mapped* those archives — or rather, I had mapped the country that contained them, back when mapping was still allowed to include everything, back before the great erasure, when the Emperor's cartographers' guild received its orders and we all became, overnight, artists of omission.

I had drawn Corven Hold. I had inked its towers onto vellum with my own hand, eleven years ago, in a guildhall that smelled of linseed and fear. And now it was ash, and the monks who spoke the liturgical tongue of the old provinces — a tongue I recognized because I'd once transcribed place-names in it — were ash too, or running, or registered.

"Osk," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The list of languages. Is there a description attached? Of speakers? Of anyone specific?"

The peddler looked at me for a long moment. Peddlers weigh questions before answering; it's how they survive. Whatever he weighed in mine, he apparently found heavy enough to answer honestly.

"There's a general notice," he said. "Anyone with knowledge of the Vessian tongues. Speakers, readers, teachers." He paused. "And there's something else. A specific inquiry. About a child."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a fence post creaked in the wind, and I heard it the way you hear a sound when your life has just tilted.

"A child," Zephyrine repeated. Her voice was perfectly level, which is how I was learning to tell when she was anything but.

"A girl, maybe six or seven years old. Seen traveling alone on the eastern roads. The inquisitors want her alive." Osk rubbed his jaw. "That's the part that worries me, if you want the truth. Dead men are cheap. Alive ones get interrogated, and interrogated people talk, and this child—" he spread his hands, "—a child traveling alone speaks only one language, and it's the one on top of the list. There are posters in Marrowfen. I saw one myself. They're calling her *the vessel*. Like she's cargo. Like she's a reliquary somebody misplaced."

Zephyrine turned then, fully, and looked toward the inner room where Issel was — I later learned — sitting on the bed sorting dried yarrow into little piles by smell, because the child sorted everything, ordered everything, as if order were a language she was fluent in even when speech failed her.

"She's not going anywhere," Zephyrine said quietly.

"No," Osk agreed. "But Hartwell — the sweep. The army's coming through the high passes within the month. Clearing the valleys. All of them. Yours included. Nobody's special anymore; the maps have been redrawn and this valley is now, officially, empty land awaiting settlement. Which means everyone in it is officially nobody, and nobodies can be moved or buried at the state's convenience." He drained his untouched tea at last, grimaced at it as if it had personally betrayed him, and stood. "I'll be back through in three weeks if the roads hold. If they don't hold — well. Then you'll know why."

He left coins on the table for the herbs he hadn't loaded yet, which was the kind of thing Osk did, and I walked him out to the mule because I wanted to ask him something away from Zephyrine's ears.

"The poster," I said. "The child's description. How precise?"

"Dark hair. Small scar over the left eyebrow. Speaks no Common." He swung up into the saddle and looked down at me with eyes that had seen a lot of roads and liked very few of them. "You're not from here, mapman. Neither is she. Whatever you're thinking of doing — do it before the passes close. After that, this whole valley is a jar with a lid nailed down."

He clicked his tongue and the mule carried him westward, and I stood in the yard watching until the pass swallowed him, feeling the exact weight of everything he'd said settle onto my shoulders like wet snow.

* * *

Zephyrine didn't speak to me for the rest of the day. That was fine; I had charts to correct and a child to fail at communicating with, and grief, in my experience, keeps better in silence than in company.

But at dusk she came out to the lean-to where I worked, carrying two cups of something herbal and bitter, and handed me one without asking, which from her was practically a declaration of alliance.

"He recognized the language," she said. "Osk. When Issel talked this morning — I saw his face. He knew it."

"Peddlers know everything," I said. "It's cheaper than owning anything."

"That's not what I mean." She sat down on the chopping block across from me, cup cradled in both hands, and for a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day. "Wren. Tell me what you know. All of it. I've watched you draw your little maps for four days and you flinch every time you write a border. Nobody flinches at borders unless borders have done something to them."

So I told her.

Not all of it — I'm a liar by trade and by temperament, and old habits are load-bearing — but more than I'd told anyone since the guild expelled me. I told her about the Reconciliation of the Maps, the imperial decree that had redrawn the country in ink: entire provinces struck from the record, their names replaced with survey numbers, their histories reassigned to more convenient neighbors. I told her about the guildmaster who'd announced it with tears in his eyes and a writ in his hand, and about the colleagues who complied, and about the one colleague who didn't — who took the master plates of the erased territories and ran, and was caught at the coast, and whose execution I attended because attendance was mandatory and the guild believed in education.

"Wren Oduya," I said. "Cartographer, second class. Convicted of cartographic treason. Which is a real crime, incidentally. I drew a coastline where the Empire said there wasn't one, and for that they took my credentials, my name from the rolls, and nearly my head." I turned my cup. "The coastline was real. That was the whole problem. It's still real. The sea hasn't agreed to be redacted."

Zephyrine listened without interrupting, which I was learning was her way — she gathered information the way I gathered measurements, patiently, completely, before deciding what to build with it.

"And the child's language," she said finally. "You recognized it. In the yard, the first day. You went white."

"I recognized it because I've seen it written." I set down the cup. "In the master archive, before the burning. There were documents in the sealed collection — land grants, boundary stones, a liturgy — in a script older than the Empire's own. The archivists called it Vessian. Pre-imperial. The guild had a theory it was the tongue of a kingdom that existed before the current dynasty decided history began with them." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "They burned the sealed collection last week, according to Osk. So now the only living document in that language is sleeping in your house."

We sat with that for a while. The light died out of the sky in the slow, grudging way of mountain evenings.

"She's evidence," Zephyrine said at last. It wasn't a question. "She's not an orphan. She's not lost. She's a *witness*. Everything the usurper is trying to erase, she carries it in her mouth. As long as she lives and speaks, his version of history has a hole in it." Her hands had gone tight around the cup. "And he knows she exists."

"Yes."

"And he wants her alive."

"Yes."

"Because dead, she's nothing. But alive, she can be made to confirm whatever he needs confirmed — or she can be made an example, or she can be—" Zephyrine stopped. I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and respected her for it, and hated the sentence anyway.

"She's seven," I said. "Whatever they want from her, she can't give it. She doesn't even know what she *is*. She thinks my maps are pretty and my spelling is questionable."

"Then she's luckier than either of us," Zephyrine said, "because at least she doesn't know what she is." She stood, abruptly, and paced to the edge of the lean-to, and stood looking east toward the darkening ridge, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened out, stripped of the wry deflection I'd been using as armor and she'd been using as a wall. "Twelve years, Wren. Twelve years I have kept my head down and my hands busy and my mouth shut, and it worked. Do you understand? It *worked*. The world burned and raged and redrew itself and I grew cabbages. And now the world has sent a child to my door who is a walking violation of an imperial edict, and the army is coming to this valley within the month, and there are posters in Marrowfen with her face on them."

"Invisible was always a temporary condition," I said. "Everything is. Even countries. Especially countries."

She turned and looked at me, and in the last grey light her expression was something I couldn't read — anger and fear and something underneath both that might have been relief, terrible relief, the feeling of a decision arriving that you've been dreading and secretly waiting for.

"Then we stop pretending," she said. "Tomorrow. We sit down, all of us, and we plan like people who intend to survive. You know things about the erased country. I know things about staying alive in it. And she—" Zephyrine's voice caught, just slightly, on the word. "She knows the way out. Whether she knows it or not."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat up in the lean-to with a candle stub and the one thing I owned that mattered: a folded sheet of oilcloth, and inside it, a map.

Not an official map. Not a guild map. A map I had drawn from memory in the two years since my expulsion, working from the master plates I'd memorized before they were destroyed, adding corrections from refugees and smugglers and one very drunk former archivist. It was the country that no longer existed, drawn in full — every drowned province, every unmapped fold of hills, every road the Empire had unmade. Cartographic treason, complete and updated. Enough to hang me twice.

And in the northeast corner, in a fold of hills marked on no surviving chart, I had inked — from rumor, from a smuggler's tale, from a fragment of pre-imperial text I'd copied before the sealed collection burned — a symbol. A door-shape. Beneath it, in the old script, a phrase I had never been able to translate.

Until tonight. Until I sat across the yard from a house where a child slept who spoke that script aloud, fluently, in her sleep.

I took the map out to the kitchen table the next morning and laid it flat, and when Zephyrine came in with the bread she stopped dead, and when Issel padded in behind her the child went absolutely still, staring at the corner of the chart, at the door-shaped symbol, and then she said — clear, certain, in the language older than kingdoms, in a voice that had no business sounding that sure —

*"The door remembers."*

And then she pointed at the hills on my map, and said one word more, and I did not need a translation to understand it, because some words are the same in every tongue:

*There.*
