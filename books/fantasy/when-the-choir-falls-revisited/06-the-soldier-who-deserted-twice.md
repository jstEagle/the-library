## 6. The Soldier Who Deserted Twice

The first time I deserted, it was easy. That's the part nobody believes when I tell it, which is why I mostly don't tell it.

Easy like walking out of a room you've decided you hate. The usurper's army had just finished burning the granaries at Lowmarsh — officially they were rebel granaries, though the only rebellion I ever saw in Lowmarsh was a miller's wife who spat at a lieutenant and got her hands for it — and I looked down at my boots, which were standing in ash that had been somebody's winter, and I thought: *not this.* And then I walked. No plan, no map, no grand philosophy. Just a man following his boots away from a thing he could not un-see.

That was three years ago. They caught me in six weeks, of course. You can't hide a deserter in a country where every village has a bounty board and every bounty board has your face drawn by an artist who makes everyone look guilty. They gave me the choice they give everyone: the post, or the penance battalion. The penance battalion meant carrying siege stones until your spine gave out. The post meant going back to burning things with better discipline.

I took the post. That was the second desertion — not from the army, but from myself. I signed the paper, put the uniform back on, and told myself the lie all good soldiers tell: *I'll do less harm on the inside.* It is a comfortable lie. It fits the mouth well. I wore it for three years the way you wear a boot that's half a size too small, telling yourself you'll break it in, knowing all the while that it's breaking you.

* * *

The night I left properly — the second time, the real time — began with a map.

I want to be honest about what kind of man brings a map to his own desertion, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I'm spending it here, in this valley, on strangers who owe me nothing. I didn't take the map out of conscience. I took it because Sergeant Vell had it spread across the command tent table and I was standing close enough to read it upside down, and reading things upside down is a habit I picked up as a quartermaster's clerk before anyone trusted me with a spear. The map showed the autumn offensive. It showed the sweep schedules for the border provinces, colored in red ink like wounds. And there, in the northwest corner, small enough that you'd miss it if you weren't looking, was a valley with no name and a notation in Vell's cramped hand: *sweep scheduled, 9th cohort, twelve days.*

A valley with no name. In the imperial cartography bureau, which I once tried to join and was rejected from for "insufficient orthodoxy" — their word, and I've treasured it since — a place with no name means one of two things. Either it's worthless, or it's hidden. And the empire does not spend a cohort sweeping worthless ground.

Twelve days. I had been counting them down like a man watching a fever climb.

What actually broke me wasn't the map, though. Maps are just paper; I'd seen a hundred of them and burned nothing but candle wax over any of them. What broke me was Corporal Denny Ash, who was nineteen and laughed at his own jokes before he finished them, sitting across the fire from me two nights before the sweep, sharpening a knife and saying, cheerful as a lark, "They say there's folk up in that valley what still do the old magic. Real old. Sarge says we're to take any speakers alive."

"Alive," I said.

"For questioning." He said it the way you'd say *for supper*. Then he tested the edge on his thumb and grinned at me, and something in my chest that had been creaking under strain for three years finally went.

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the decision already made, the way you remember waking already standing in a cold room. I took the map off Vell's table because leaving it felt like leaving a lit lamp in a powder store, and I took my kit, and I walked north into the hills with Denny Ash's grin stitched somewhere behind my eyes where I couldn't dig it out.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about deserting twice: the second time, you know exactly what you're losing. The first time, you're young enough to think the world will make room for you. The second time, you know the world has no room, that the roads are watched, that your face is on boards from here to the capital, that the penalty is the post or worse, and that worse has recently been getting more creative. You walk anyway. But you walk knowing, and the knowing sits on your shoulders like a second pack.

I walked four days. My left leg — which I'd ruined two winters back falling into a flooded trench, badly set, badly healed — announced its objections around noon of the first day and never really stopped. By the fourth day I was past caring about stealth, moving through high country on a leg that had filed a formal complaint, eating nothing, drinking from streams that tasted of iron and snowmelt, following a hunch I couldn't have defended to anyone, least of all myself: *the nameless valley, go where the map goes red, because the empire's cruelty is the most reliable compass there is. Wherever they mean to hurt people, people are worth finding.*

On the fifth night it rained. On the sixth morning I crested a ridge with my vision swimming and saw green below me — deep, deliberate green, terraced gardens, a crooked chimney smoking, fences mended with the kind of care that says *somebody loves this ground* — and I thought, with the clarity that comes from exhaustion, *well. Either I've found sanctuary or I've found the exact spot where they'll hang me, and either way I'm done walking.*

Then the leg made the decision for me. It buckled on the descent, and I went down the hillside in a series of undignified collisions with gorse and rock and one extremely offended sheep, and came to rest in a ditch at the bottom with my head ringing and my flask empty and the sky doing a slow, contemptible rotation above me.

That's how I arrived: not as a soldier, not even much as a man, but as a heap of wet wool and bad decisions at the edge of someone's herb garden.

* * *

She found me before I could find her. Of course she did. I learned later that Zephyrine Hartwell notices everything that crosses her boundary stone, and I have since stopped being surprised by anything that woman knows.

I heard footsteps — unhurried, which struck me as either very brave or very foolish, given the state of me — and then a woman's voice, dry as August: "If you're dying, do it past the fence. I grow things here, and corpses are bad for the soil."

I turned my head. She stood at the garden gate with her arms crossed, grey-streaked hair tied back, looking at me the way a farmer looks at weather: assessing, unimpressed, already calculating what it would cost her.

"Ma'am," I said, or something like it. My mouth wasn't working at full capacity. "I'm not—"

"You're imperial. The coat gives you away, even filthy. Even torn." Her eyes went down to my leg, then back up. "Deserter?"

There was no point lying. Lying takes energy, and I had perhaps enough left to blink. "Yes."

"Hunting us?"

"No." And then, because it seemed important that she understand the shape of the danger, and because my filter had drowned somewhere on the ridge: "But they're coming. Twelve days. Maybe fewer now. A full cohort, sweep order. There's a child here — I don't know how I know, I heard talk on the road, rumor runs ahead of soldiers like rats run ahead of a flood — and the orders say any speakers of the old tongues are to be taken alive. For questioning." I said it the way Denny had said it, and hated that my mouth remembered his cadence. "Ma'am, they aren't planning to leave anyone in this valley to tell the tale."

Something moved behind her face — quick, controlled, gone. She looked at me a long moment.

"You brought this to my door," she said, "on purpose?"

"I brought it because—" I stopped. Because what? Because I'd spent three years doing arithmetic with other people's lives and I'd finally hit a number I couldn't carry? Because a boy with a knife and a grin had made me complicit in a way that no amount of *following orders* would wash out? "Because I stole the map," I said finally. "Off the sergeant's table. It's in my coat. Every route, every timetable, every unit designation. I figured — if I was going to be a coward again, I could at least be a useful one."

She was quiet for so long I thought she might simply walk away and let the valley do what valleys do to trespassers. Then she came through the gate, crouched beside me with a efficiency that spoke of field medicine done in worse places than ditches, and pulled the folded map from inside my coat without asking.

Her face changed when she opened it. I watched her read it — fast, thorough, the way you read when you already know most of the words and are hunting for the ones you don't — and I watched her arrive at the same corner I had, the nameless valley, the red notation, the twelve days.

"Nine days now," I offered helpfully. "Maybe eight. I walk slow."

"I noticed." She folded the map with great care, the way you handle a thing that might bite. Then she looked at me again, and whatever she saw — the shame, probably; I've been told it leaks out of me like sap from a cut branch — made her sigh. "Can you stand if I help you?"

"With the leg? Ma'am, I can't stand *without* you helping me. That's rather the situation."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An ancestor of a smile, maybe, preserved in amber. "Inside, then. And Kettle—"

I hadn't told her my name. I must have babbled it somewhere in the ditch. I genuinely could not rule it out.

"—if you're lying to me, the valley itself will know before I do. Things work strangely here. Remember that."

I remembered it. Later, I understood it. At the time I was mostly thinking about how good it would feel to be horizontal indoors instead of vertical outdoors, and about how strange it was that a woman who had every reason to put a knife in me was instead putting her shoulder under my arm.

* * *

Her house smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something I couldn't name — a smell like the air before lightning, trapped in the walls. She sat me by the fire, cut my boot off with more mercy than the boot deserved, and examined the leg without commentary, which I appreciated. Commentary on the leg is something I provide free of charge and in abundance; I didn't need hers.

While she worked, I talked. I hadn't meant to. But there is a particular loneliness in carrying what I carried, and her kitchen was warm, and she listened the way soldiers never listen — not waiting for her turn, not scanning for weakness, just listening — and the whole ugly account came out of me between sips of a bitter tea she pressed into my hands.

Lowmarsh. The signing of the paper. Three years of *less harm on the inside*, each year requiring a slightly larger lie to sustain. Denny Ash and his knife. The map. The ditch. All of it, laid out on her table like goods for inspection.

When I finished, she was quiet for a while, feeding the fire.

"Do you want absolution?" she asked at last. "Because I don't sell it. I sell comfrey and willow bark, and neither one touches what you've got."

"No, ma'am." I looked into the fire. "I don't know what I want. I think — I think I want to be the sort of man who'd have run the first time. And I can't be him retroactively. So I'm trying to be the next best thing, which is a man who runs the last time, hard, in the right direction, carrying something heavy enough to matter."

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing a soldier has said in this house in twelve years."

I didn't ask who the last one was. Some doors you can see are locked from the inside.

* * *

I convalesced. That's the polite word. Really I lay on a pallet in her back room listening to the valley breathe, learning its rhythms: the herbalist's dawn rounds, the wind coming down off the western pass with a sound like a held note, the utter, unnatural quiet that sat over everything. I noticed the quiet before I understood it. A valley should hum — insects, birds, the small electric noise of living land. This one didn't. It was like standing in a room after the music stops and realizing the music had been playing your whole life.

And I noticed the child.

I saw her maybe three times in those first days, always at a distance, always watching me with the grave, total attention that children deploy on new objects. Dark-haired, seven or eight, moving through the garden like she'd been born to it. Once I caught her murmuring to herself by the fence, and the sound of it stopped me mid-limp.

It wasn't any language I knew, and I knew a lot of languages — quartermasters learn the tongues of every province they requisition from, and I'd requisitioned from most of them. This was older. It had a weight to it, a gravity, like each word was a small stone dropped into deep water. And stranger still, when she spoke it, the valley's great silence seemed to *lean toward her*. Like ice underfoot, flexing. Like something vast turning over in its sleep.

I mentioned it to Zephyrine that evening, carefully, the way you mention a storm cloud to a farmer.

"I know," she said. Her face did the thing it did — the quick, controlled movement, feeling kept behind glass. "Her name is Issel. And Bram — the language she speaks hasn't been spoken openly anywhere in this country since before the usurper's grandfather was whelped. Which means either she's a ghost, or she came from somewhere the maps gave up on." She looked at the fire. "And now the army is coming here for her specifically. Alive. For questioning."

"The map," I said. "You still have it."

"I have it."

"Then let me earn my keep. I know how they move. I know what a sweep looks like from the inside — the spacing, the timing, where they'll be lazy and where they'll be thorough. I know Vell's habits; he drinks before dawn and trusts his scouts more than he should. Give me a table and a candle and I'll tell you exactly how those twelve days will unfold." I hesitated, then said the harder thing. "And when you decide what to do — run, fight, hide — I'll help however you'll have me. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking for a job."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the wind came down the pass with its held-note sound, and somewhere in the garden the child called out — one bright syllable in that old, old tongue — and I swear the fire leaned toward the window.

"There's a man," Zephyrine said slowly, "who passed through years ago. A cartographer. Talked too much, drew beautiful maps, lied about half of what he said. He may come back. If he does, he'll want what you have." A pause. "Between the three of us, we might almost make one honest person. It'll have to do."

It wasn't trust. I knew that. Trust is earned over years; what she was offering was a rope thrown to a drowning man, held loosely, ready to drop. But hanging from a rope beats drowning, and besides — I'd spent three years learning that the water rises faster than you can swim.

"Eight days," I said. "Give me eight days and a candle, and I'll show you exactly where the knives will fall."

Zephyrine nodded once and went to fetch the candle, and I sat by her fire with my ruined leg stretched out and the stolen map heavy in my mind, and for the first time in three years the arithmetic in my chest came out even.

Then the door banged open, and the child Issel stood in the doorway, pointing west with a finger that did not tremble, and said — in plain, accented, unmistakable Common, her first words I'd ever heard her speak —

"They're coming faster than your map says."
