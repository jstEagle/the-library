## 7. Four at a Table

I want it on the record that I did not invite any of them.

The soldier bled on my good bench. The cartographer rearranged my kitchen without asking, which is its own kind of violence. And the child sat in the corner eating honey off a spoon with the solemn concentration of someone who has never once been told *no* by anyone who mattered.

The rain had come in with Bram Kettle—proper valley rain, gray and apologetic—and by the time I'd stitched his shoulder and Wren Oduya had finished explaining, at length, why my house was oriented wrong for the light, it was clear that nobody was leaving tonight. Not because of the weather. Because of the word sitting in the middle of my table like an uninvited guest: *Vessia.*

"Say it again," I said.

Wren turned his cup a quarter-turn, then another quarter, as if he could find an angle from which the evening made sense. He was a long man, folded up like a letter someone meant to send but didn't, with ink stains to the second knuckle and the particular pallor of a person who has spent years arguing with paper. "Vessia-on-the-Maps," he said. "The Door of Vessia. The girl's language is Vessian. Pre-imperial. It predates every kingdom currently pretending it invented civilization."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I'm the only cartographer alive who ever mapped a country that officially does not exist." He said it flatly, the way a man says *I have a limp.* "Twelve years ago the Empire struck Vessia from every record. Burned the archives, renamed the roads, redrew the coastlines. Officially there is a bay where Vessia was, and no cities on it, and no people. I was the junior surveyor on the commission that did the erasing." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I kept a copy."

"You committed treason," I said.

"I committed accuracy." He drank. "There's a difference. One of them gets you exiled; the other just gets you tired."

I looked at Issel. She looked back, unbothered, honey at the corner of her mouth, and said something in that old tongue—the same phrase she'd been saying since the night she arrived on my doorstep half-frozen, the phrase I'd spent three nights with my grandmother's banned primer trying to crack. *The door remembers.*

"She keeps saying it," I said.

"It's not a warning," Wren said. "I've been listening. It's an introduction. She's telling us what she is." He set down the cup. "She's a key, Mistress Hartwell. A living one. And keys don't get sent out into the world alone unless the lock they belong to is in danger."

Bram Kettle made a sound from the bench—a low, pained noise that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been amputated from him somewhere along the road. He was maybe thirty, built like something meant for carrying heavy things, with a deserter's beard and a wound I'd sewn shut under his collarbone. Two days he'd been in my house. Two days of watching him flinch at the sound of his own name.

"You know something," I said to him.

"I know timetables." He shifted, and the bench creaked its objection. "Third Legion sweeps this valley in nine days. Maybe ten, if the mud argues with them. They're calling it a census." He spat the word like a bad tooth. "It's not a census. It's a cull. Anyone who speaks an old tongue, anyone who keeps old books, anyone whose grandmother knew a song the Empire doesn't like. Inquisitor Sarel's orders. You burn the archives first, then you burn the archivists, and then—" he gestured vaguely at the child, at me, at the whole damp green valley that had kept me safe and small for twelve years, "—then you burn whatever's left."

"The valley," I said slowly. "They're coming *here.*"

"They're coming everywhere, mistress. That's rather the point of an army." Bram looked at his hands. "I deserted twice, you know. Once from the old king's army, when I saw what we did at Harrow Ford. Then they conscripted me back—deserters get found, deserters always get found—and I told myself, fine, fine, I'll keep my head down, I'll carry what I'm told to carry. And then they gave me papers to carry. Rosters. Lists of villages. And I thought: this is how it happens. Not with orders to kill. With orders to *count.*" His jaw worked. "So I ran again. Twice-deserted. There's no punishment left for me but the one they give everyone else now, so I suppose I'm finally free."

"That's the most depressing definition of freedom I've ever heard," Wren said.

"You're a traitor too, map-man. Pot, kettle."

"Kettle," I said, before I could stop myself. "Your name is actually Kettle."

"My mother had a sense of humor," Bram said, with the ghost of dignity. "And a forge."

*

Here is what nobody tells you about exile: it doesn't end. You don't finish it like a sentence. You just keep living inside it until the walls grow so familiar you mistake them for your own skin.

I had built those walls carefully. Herbs instead of storms. Mended fences instead of called lightning. Twelve years of being a woman whose greatest ambition was a decent harvest of feverfew. And now three strangers and a child were sitting in my kitchen dismantling it brick by brick, and the terrible thing—the thing I couldn't say out loud—was that some buried part of me was *grateful.*

"So let me assemble this," I said, because if I didn't say something practical soon I was going to say something true, and true things are dangerous in kitchens. "You—" I pointed at Wren. "Know where the Door is."

"I know where it was when I mapped it. Geography is more stable than governments, but not perfectly stable."

"You—" at Bram. "Know when the army arrives."

"Nine days. Ten with mud."

"And she—" at Issel, who was now stacking my spoons into a tiny gleaming fortress, "is the key."

"In her blood, yes. Keeper's line. The last keepers sent her away when the soldiers came—that's the shape of the story her phrases make. I can't read all of it. But 'the door remembers' isn't a plea, Hartwell. It's a *claim.*"

"Which leaves me," I said, "as the hand that turns it. According to you."

"According to the primer your grandmother wasn't supposed to own," Wren said mildly. "You read Vessian. You recognized the girl's speech within hours. And you have hands that once—" he paused, delicately, the way men pause when they're about to be rude, "—called lightning down on the old capital."

The room went very quiet. Even Issel stopped stacking spoons.

"I was nineteen," I said. "And the capital was burning either way. I just chose which fire."

"I'm not judging you. I'm reading you. It's what I do with terrain." Wren leaned forward. "A storm-caller, a keeper's blood, a map, and a timetable. Do you know what that adds up to? It adds up to the only four people in this country who can open that Door before Sarel's census reaches it. The usurper isn't hunting the girl because she's a loose end. He's hunting her because the Door is the one thing his armies can't burn. Something outside his reach. Something that remembers the country he erased. He can't rule forever over people who have somewhere else to be."

"And what does the Door open?" I asked. "Exactly. Since we're all being accurate."

Wren opened his mouth. Closed it. For the first time since he'd arrived, the cartographer looked genuinely lost—which, I'll admit, improved his face considerably.

"I don't know," he said. "Nobody knows. That's rather the point of a door that leads out of a country that officially no longer exists. Out to *where?*"

Silence again. Rain on the roof. Issel resumed her spoon-fortress with the patience of masonry.

"I'll tell you what I know," Bram said quietly. "I know that in the Third Legion, the old-timers—the ones who fought in the Vessian campaign, before the erasure—they won't camp near certain hills. Won't say why. Just move their tents. And I know that when the order came down to sweep this valley, two men in my unit laughed. Not cruelly. Like men hearing a joke they'd hoped was still funny." He rubbed his face. "Something about this country doesn't want to die. And the Empire knows it, and it terrifies them, and terrified empires do what ours does. They count. They list. They burn."

"Well," I said. "When you put it that way, staying home seems suicidal."

"It usually does," Wren agreed, "right around the time leaving becomes inevitable."

"Don't be glib. Leaving means—" I stopped. Here it was, then. The wall I'd built, and the truth standing on the other side of it. "Leaving means crossing the boundary stone. And everything I am comes back the moment I do. Twelve years I've been nothing but an herbalist with strong opinions about drainage. Out there, past the ridge, I'm Zephyrine Hartwell again. Storm-caller. The woman on Sarel's list, probably near the top. Every step I take outside this valley, I get louder. And loud things get found."

"Then we walk softly," Bram said.

"You can't walk softly through drowned provinces with a child who speaks a dead language and a deserter whose face is on half the checkpoints between here and the coast."

"No," Wren said. "But we can walk *accurately.*" He pulled a leather tube from his pack—the way other men draw swords—and slid a chart onto my table, weighting the corners with salt cellars and a spoon. Ink lines bloomed in the lamplight: a coastline that matched no map I'd ever seen sold or seized, hills folded into folds, and a bay marked with a single careful word in a dead alphabet. "Every official map says empty water here. This says otherwise. I've spent twelve years being ashamed of the erasure I helped draw. This is the un-drawing. It's not much of an apology. But it's the one I have."

I looked at the chart a long time. Somewhere under those careful lines was a door, and behind the door was—who knew. Refuge. Memory. Nothing. A story old women told children to make exile bearable.

And here was the other thing nobody tells you about exile: eventually, the safety starts to feel like burial. You water your herbs and mend your fences and you are *fine,* and fine goes on year after year, and one morning you realize that fine is just a smaller word for gone.

Nine days. Ten with mud.

"All right," I said. "Council's in session. Rules first. Nobody lies in this kitchen—not even by omission, not even tactically. If we're going to die for each other, we'll do it informed. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Wren.

"Agreed," said Bram, after a moment, in the voice of a man agreeing to something harder than it sounded.

We both looked at Issel. She considered us from behind her spoon-fortress, then reached out and placed one small honey-sticky hand flat on the chart of the country that did not exist, directly over the bay, directly over the word in the dead alphabet.

"The door remembers," she said—in our language, this time, the first words I'd heard her speak in anything but the old tongue. Her voice was small and clear and absolutely certain, a bell rung in an empty church. "That's what I'm *for.*"

Then she went back to her spoons, as if she hadn't just rearranged the furniture of all our futures, and Wren Oduya made a strangled noise, and Bram Kettle laughed—actually laughed, rusty and startled, like a gate opening after years shut—and I stood in my own kitchen among strangers who were, apparently, as of that sticky handprint on the map, not strangers anymore.

Not family. Let's not be sentimental. Family implies trust, and trust takes longer than an evening.

But necessity, I thought, watching the rain come down on the valley that had forgotten how to hold magic and was about to remember everything else—necessity will do for a start.

Outside, far off toward the western pass, thunder muttered. Old habit. Or perhaps, though I'd never have admitted it, an answer.

Ten days with mud. I started packing that night.
