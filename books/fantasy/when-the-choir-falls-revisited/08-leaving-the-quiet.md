## 8. Leaving the Quiet

I had spent twelve years learning how to be small, and it turns out that unlearning takes considerably less time. About one evening, in fact, if you measure from the moment a child speaks a dead language at your kitchen table to the moment you find yourself packing a satchel by candlelight like a woman planning her own funeral.

Which, knowing my luck, was roughly what it was.

The house did not want me to go. Houses never do; they develop opinions after enough years of your boots on their floors. The third stair creaked its objection. The loose shutter on the east window rattled as if clearing its throat to argue. I ignored them both with the practiced rudeness of someone who has had twelve years of conversations with a building and lost most of them.

I took almost nothing. That was the strange part. I had imagined this night for over a decade—usually at three in the morning, usually after too much elderflower wine—and in those imaginings I always packed furiously, greedily, cramming twelve years of accumulated life into bags and bundles. In practice I stood before my shelves of dried herbs and found I could carry what mattered in two arms' worth. Yarrow for wounds. Willow bark for fever. A twist of salt. My grandmother's primer, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Everything else—the mended fences, the tidy rows of chamomile and comfrey, the garden I had weeded into perfect obedience—could stay and rot politely without me.

Issel sat on my bed watching me pack, knees drawn up, wearing the coat I'd cut down for her three days ago. She didn't speak. She rarely spoke, even now that we'd begun to understand each other, and when she did it was in that language that made the air feel thin, like altitude. Tonight she just watched with those grave dark eyes and held something in her fist.

"What have you got there?" I asked, in her tongue—my pronunciation still atrocious, but she'd stopped wincing, which I chose to count as fluency.

She opened her hand. A smooth grey pebble, river-worn, nothing more. She said a word I hadn't been able to find in the primer. Then she pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers over it, with the solemnity of a bishop handing over a relic.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, though I hadn't the faintest idea what I was thanking her for. I put the stone in my pocket. It weighed nothing. It has since occurred to me that everything important I carried out of that valley weighed nothing.

* * *

Wren Oduya arrived an hour past midnight, which for Wren constituted punctuality, since he'd promised to come "sometime tonight" and had therefore fulfilled his obligation with a full hour to spare. He came around the back, through the herb rows, moving with the peculiar apologetic grace of a man who has spent his life apologizing to furniture he's about to hide behind.

"You're sure about the pass?" I asked.

"I'm sure about the map." He unrolled it on my table—a proper cartographer's chart, inked in his own cramped, beautiful hand, though every official seal on it was forged and he made no pretense otherwise. "Western pass, then down along the dry wash to the old drover's road. Nobody maps the drover's road because nobody uses it. Which is precisely why we will."

"And beyond the boundary?"

His pen-hand hesitated, just slightly, over the ridge line. Wren Oduya could draw a mountain range from memory and make you smell the snow off it, but he drew borders the way other men drew snakes: carefully, from a distance, acknowledging they existed without wanting to touch one.

"Beyond the boundary," he said, "is the world."

"Yes," I said. "I've heard rumors."

He looked at me then—not at the map, not at Issel, at me—and I saw him doing the arithmetic he'd been doing since the day he walked into my valley pretending to be a spice merchant and doing it badly. Two liars circling each other, I'd thought once. But somewhere in the last week the circling had slowed, and what remained wasn't quite trust. Trust is a finished thing. What we had was more like two people standing back to back in a fight, having discovered independently that neither of them intended to run.

"The stories about you," he said carefully. "The ones from the capital. Lightning on the old palace. The storm that ended the siege of—" He stopped himself. "Are they true?"

"Some of them," I said. "The flattering ones are exaggerated. The horrifying ones are understated."

He nodded slowly, and I'll say this for Wren: he didn't ask which were which. Some men would have. Some men needed to know exactly what they were walking out with. Wren simply rolled up his map and said, "Then let's hope we get to the Door before anyone makes you prove it."

That was the kindest thing anyone had said to me in twelve years, and I resented it immediately.

* * *

Bram Kettle was waiting at the western fence, and Bram Kettle being early told me everything about how the night felt to him. A soldier learns to be punctual; a deserter learns to be early, because lateness is how you get caught. He stood in the shadow of the fence post with his hood up and his wounded arm bound tight, and when we came up the path he fell into step beside us without a word, the way men do who have marched behind other men for years and can no longer help it.

He'd washed the uniform dye from his hair, but he still moved like the usurper's army. Shoulders squared to some invisible standard. Eyes sweeping the treeline in a rhythm I could set a clock to. I hated how much comfort that gave me—an exiled storm-caller taking comfort from a soldier's habits, the world being the joke that it is.

"Patrols?" Wren murmured.

"Rotated at moonset," Bram said. "We've got maybe four hours before the sweep reaches the valley floor. After that, dogs." He glanced at Issel, riding his shoulders with her small hands laced under his chin, and something crossed his face that I recognized despite myself, because I wore it too. "Dogs don't care what language she screams in."

"Then we won't scream," I said.

It was the closest thing to a speech I'd given, and it seemed to satisfy them, or at least to satisfy the part of each of them that needed someone to sound certain. Every fleeing group needs one person to sound certain. I had been elected by default, on account of being the oldest and the only one with a house to abandon.

We went up through the terraces, past the fences I'd mended every spring, past the beehives I'd leave for whoever came next, if anyone came next, if there was a next. The valley lay silver and silent below us, holding its breath. Twelve years I had lived inside that silence like a pearl inside a shell, and I want to tell you I looked back with love. I looked back with something. Love is a word for afterward.

* * *

The western pass narrows to a slot between two shoulders of granite, and at its mouth stands the boundary stone.

I should explain about the stone, because the histories won't—they've all been burned, and the ones that weren't burned were rewritten by men who thought magic was a tax problem. The valley is dead ground. No magic crosses that stone. Whatever force keeps it so is older than kingdoms, older than the primer's alphabet, possibly older than the concept of doors, which given our destination struck me as grimly funny. Inside the stone, a storm-caller is a woman with good lungs and a bad temper. Outside it—

Outside it, I was Zephyrine Hartwell again. All of me.

I stopped ten paces from the stone. The others went ahead—I heard Wren's boots on the scree, Bram's steady tread, Issel's small weight shifting—and I stood there like a fool talking herself out of a doorway.

Here is what nobody tells you about exile: you get used to it. Not resigned—used, the way a hand gets used to a missing finger. For years the absence ached, and then it stopped aching, and then one morning you catch yourself reaching for something with the space where the finger used to be, and you realize the absence has become a part of you, shaped and smoothed and yours. I had built a life in the shape of what I'd lost. Small. Quiet. Safe as a grave and half as warm.

And now I was going to walk out of it voluntarily, and the worst part—the part I'll admit here, to whatever page survives to read it—is that some traitorous corner of me wanted to.

Not wanted the war. Not wanted the burning provinces, the executed speakers, the child hunted for her own tongue. I wanted the power back the way a drunk wants the drink: completely, shamefully, with full knowledge of the cost itemized and initialed. Twelve years I had sworn never to feel it again—that current under the skin, that pressure behind the eyes, the sky leaning down close enough to whisper. I had called lightning down on the old capital and I had seen what it loved, and I had run here and buried myself alive and called the burial peace.

"Zephyrine." Wren's voice, low, from the shadows past the stone. "The moon's setting."

"Let it," I said. "It's earned a rest."

But I walked forward anyway, because Issel was waiting on the far side, and because the usurper's dogs were coming, and because a woman can only stand in front of her own grave for so long before she either climbs in or walks past it.

I crossed the boundary stone.

* * *

They tell you losing a limb feels like fire. They tell you less often what regaining one feels like, because fewer people survive it to describe it. Blood returning to a starved limb is agony and ecstasy in the same instant, indistinguishable, a single sensation with two names. That is what woke in me at the stone.

It started in my teeth. A hum, faint, like a tuning fork struck somewhere behind my face. Then down my spine, vertebra by vertebra, a cold bright thread being drawn through me by some patient hand. The air changed—I felt it before I heard it, pressure shifting, the whole atmosphere turning toward me like a field of wheat toward sun. Ten miles away a front was sitting over the drowned provinces, fat and slow and waiting, and it felt me cross that stone the way a sleeping dog feels its name.

"Oh," I said. Just that. Oh. Twelve years of vocabulary reduced to a syllable.

My hands were glowing. Not metaphorically—I could see the stormlight under my skin, blue-white, threading the veins of my wrists like rivers on one of Wren's maps. I raised one hand and the wind raised with it, a soft spiral of dust and last year's leaves, obedient, eager, sickeningly eager. The sky above the pass had begun to move. Clouds that had sat inert all night were stirring, gathering, leaning.

"Zephyrine." Wren again, sharper now. There was fear in it. Good, I thought. One of us should be afraid, and I was far too busy being intoxicated.

"It's fine," I said, and the wind repeated it in a whisper through the pass—*it's fine, it's fine*—and that frightened me more than anything, that it would echo me, that it wanted to please me. Power that wants to please you is the most dangerous kind. It was how the old capital had happened. It was why I'd left.

I closed my fist. The wind died grudgingly, like a servant pretending not to hear. The glow under my skin faded to embers, but the embers stayed lit, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, that they would stay lit now until I died or the world did. There was no walking back through that stone into smallness. The valley would take my body back, perhaps, but not the rest. You cannot unknow the sky is listening.

Bram had shifted Issel off his shoulders and put himself between her and me, casually, the way soldiers do things, as if it were just where he happened to be standing. I appreciated the courtesy of it. I appreciated it the way you appreciate a knife laid within reach.

"Do you have it under control?" he asked. Not accusing. Worse—genuinely asking, a man assessing a weapon he'd chosen to carry.

"No," I said honestly. "But I have it contained, which tonight is the same thing."

Issel tugged free of Bram and walked to me through the dark. She took my glowing, fading hand in both of hers, the way she'd pressed the pebble into it hours ago, and she looked up and said something in the old tongue. I caught only one word—*vessia*, door—but I caught the tone perfectly, because children are fluent in tones. It was not afraid. It was not admiring. It was matter-of-fact, the way you'd tell a woman her soup was ready.

Yes, the tone said. This is what you are. We knew. Come on, then.

"Right," I said to all of them, to the pass, to the listening sky. "Down to the drover's road. Wren leads. Bram watches our backs. And if any of you see weather that looks personal—" I flexed my fingers, feeling the storm ten miles off shift in its sleep, rolling over toward me like a great cat hearing its name, "—that's because it is."

We went down out of the pass together, four liars and a key, into a country that officially did not exist, pursued by an army that officially was not coming, toward a door that officially was a myth.

Behind us, the boundary stone stood in the moonless dark, keeping its ancient silence.

Ahead of us, the first far-off thunder answered me like a voice remembering my name.

And I swear to you, on my grandmother's banned primer and every grave I've dug in that quiet valley—I was terrified.

I had missed it so much.
