## 9. The Drowned Provinces

I have drawn maps of places that never existed. I have also, in my more honest hours, admitted that I once drew maps of places that *did* exist and was paid to make them stop.

The drowned provinces were the second kind.

We came down out of the hills on the fourth morning after leaving the valley, and the land simply gave up pretending to be land. The road ran straight into a brown mirror and kept going—raised on an old causeway, thank whatever gods cartographers secretly pray to, so we walked on top of a flooded world like flies on the surface of a bowl of soup. To either side, wheat fields became reed beds became open water, and the water held the sky so perfectly still that for a long while I couldn't tell which way was down.

"Someone did this," Bram said. He'd gone quiet in the way soldiers go quiet, which is not quiet at all but a kind of shouting.

"The levees at Corren's Wash," I said. "And the Marrow sluices. And the great dyke at Halven Ford." I had mapped all three, back when mapping them was a patriotic act. "Three breaches, timed within a single week. The grain harvest drowned before it could be moved east to the resistance towns. Very tidy. It's in no imperial report, of course. Officially, this region suffers from 'seasonal inundation.'"

"Seasonal," Zephyrine repeated. She had Issel up on her shoulders, and the child was staring at the water with the fixed attention she gave everything, as if it might speak first.

"It has now been seasonally inundated for six years."

Bram spat off the side of the causeway. He'd been doing that a lot since we left the valley, as though his old uniform were something he could work out of his system drop by drop. He still wore it under a borrowed fisherman's coat, because burning it would have been sensible and Bram Kettle has never once in his life been sensible when shame was involved. He carries his guilt the way other men carry coin—close, counted, and always expecting to be robbed of it.

* * *

The boats found us by midday.

They came drifting along what had once been the Heddlow road—you could tell because the tops of the hedgerows broke the surface in long green lines, and the boats followed them like beads on a string. Families, mostly. A man poling a flat-bottomed skiff stacked with furniture and hens. Two women sharing a punt, one of them holding a loom shuttle like a relic. An old fellow towing his goat behind him on a rope, the goat swimming with the resigned competence of an animal that has done this before.

They didn't beg. That was the thing that got under my skin and stayed there. They nodded to us as they passed, the way you nod to neighbors across a fence, four families adrift on their own fields, and one of the women called out, "Water's higher at Marrowfen, if you're bound south. Causeway floods at the third mile."

"Thank you," Zephyrine called back. And then, because she is Zephyrine: "Are there places taking people in?"

The woman laughed—not bitter, exactly. Tired in the marrow. "Places, yes. Taking, no. You go to Marrowfen, you'll see."

Issel watched the boats until they were dots, then watched the water where they'd been. She said something in her old tongue, soft, and Zephyrine went very still above her.

"What did she say?" Bram asked.

"She said—" Zephyrine paused, and I watched her choose between the truth and a kindness, and choose the truth, because that is the kind of person she is even when she pretends otherwise. "She said the houses are sleeping. Not drowned. Sleeping. The keepers' word for it. As if the country is only waiting to be woken."

I am a mapmaker. I know precisely what it means when a country is waiting to be woken and nobody has put it on a chart in six years. It means someone is drawing it out of existence one blank space at a time, and blank spaces, given enough time and enough ink withheld, become the truth. Erasure isn't fire. Fire leaves ash, and ash can be read. Erasure is the survey that never gets commissioned, the name that stops appearing in the gazetteer, the schoolbook printed next spring with one province fewer and no footnote explaining why. I know because I held the pen.

"You're doing the face," Zephyrine said to me.

"What face?"

"The one where you're having a feeling about geography."

"I'm always having feelings about geography. It's the occupational disease."

* * *

We made camp that night on the roof of a barn.

That sentence deserves its own paragraph, so: we made camp that night on the roof of a barn, because the barn itself stood to its eaves in water, and the roof was the driest ground for two miles in any direction. The family who owned it had left a rowboat tied to the ridge beam and a chalk mark on the chimney—a circle with a dot, which in these parts means *empty, take what you need, leave what you can*. Someone had left what they could: a sack of turnips going soft, a coil of netting, half a candle stub in a jar.

Bram took first watch, sitting on the ridgeline with his coat off and his uniform sleeve showing, and I lay on the warm shingles looking at the stars doubled in the floodwater below us, so that we seemed to be camping in the space between two skies.

"I surveyed this valley," I said to him, because the dark makes confession cheap. "Before. The Heddlow. There were eleven villages. A bell tower at Saint Ferren's that rang a quarter-hour chime the whole country copied. I put every one of them on the new imperial chart."

"And then?"

"And then the chart went to the engravers, and the engravers went to the ministry, and the ministry returned it with corrections. Eleven villages became four. Then the second edition made it two. The current edition—" I turned the candle jar so the flame threw my shadow long across the shingles. "The current edition shows open water and a note: *terrain unsurveyed.*"

"That's not your fault."

"No," I agreed. "It's just my penmanship."

He didn't laugh. Bram doesn't laugh at that sort of thing, which is either a virtue or a failure of imagination; I've never decided. But he said, "My unit helped break the Halven dyke. I stood on it. I want you to know that, since we're trading sins. I didn't open the sluice. I just stood there and made sure no one else stopped it. Turns out standing somewhere is also a thing you do."

I thought about that for a while. The water below lapped softly against the barn walls, patient as a creditor.

"In my experience," I said finally, "the men who did the worst are the ones who can't stop talking about how little they did. The truly guilty sleep fine."

"I don't sleep fine."

"No," I said. "I noticed. You snore like a man being interrogated."

That time he did laugh, briefly, into his collar, and the sound carried out over the drowned fields and came back to us from nowhere, the way sounds do over water.

* * *

Trouble arrived the next afternoon wearing our own colors.

We heard the engine before we saw it—a shallow-draft patrol boat, imperial gray, coming up the old canal line with a mounted repeater on its bow and six soldiers slouched along its gunwale. The causeway was narrow there, hemmed by water, nowhere to go but forward or into the flood. Ahead of us, maybe two hundred yards, a cluster of refugee boats was being waved toward the bank, boarded, searched.

"Off the causeway," Zephyrine said low. "Now."

"With what?" I gestured at the scenery, which was water, water, and a decorative selection of water.

"There." She pointed at the drowned orchard to the north—old apple trees standing shoulder-deep, their crowns just breaking the surface. "Trees. We wade, we keep the trunks between us and the boat, we come out past the bend."

"We'll be soaked to the chest."

"Yes."

"It's nearly autumn."

"Yes."

"Wonderful," I said, and started down the embankment.

The water was worse than cold—it was *warm*, bath-warm from weeks of sun on the flood, and that wrongness was somehow more horrible than ice would have been. Warm water full of drowned wheat and drowned bees and, beneath us, the soft silt of somebody's kitchen garden. Issel rode Zephyrine's shoulders with her arms locked around her neck, silent, eyes wide. Bram went last, and that was the mistake, or rather his coat was, because halfway across he shrugged it off to swim better and the current took it, and there he was in the open water in a full imperial uniform, sergeant's stripes and all, waving his arms at us like a signpost.

The patrol boat saw him instantly. Of course it did. Six years of war teaches you to spot your own silhouette anywhere.

"Hoy! Soldier!" A voice through a speaking-trumpet, cracking across the water. "Soldier! Identify yourself!"

Bram froze, chin-deep, and I saw the whole arithmetic of it happen behind his eyes—the easy lie available, *yes, comrade, swept overboard, thank you*, one word and the boat moves on—and I saw him refuse it. Later he'd claim he panicked. I've watched Bram panic. This wasn't panic. This was the closest thing to prayer he knows.

"I deserted!" he bellowed back. "Twice! Ask me about it!"

A beat of pure incomprehension from the boat. Then the trumpet again, angrier: "Swim here, deserter. Slow. Hands up."

"They'll hang him," I breathed.

"They won't," Zephyrine said, and her voice had changed, dropped into a register I hadn't heard since the valley, and the hair on my forearms rose before I understood why. The water around us had begun, very faintly, to move. Not waves. A current, small and deliberate, sliding past our legs toward the patrol boat like a dog slipping its leash. "Get Issel behind that tree. Wren—when I say down, be down."

"Zephyrine—"

"*When I say down.*"

What happened next took perhaps ten seconds and I have spent a great deal of time since failing to describe it properly. The water between the boat and the causeway simply *stood up*—a swell without wind, a hill of brown floodwater eight feet high that rolled out of nothing and slapped the patrol boat broadside with a sound like a door slamming on a cathedral. Soldiers went into the drink. The repeater mount swung wild. The boat shipped a ton of water and wallowed, engines screaming, and in the chaos of six men trying not to drown in six feet of water, nobody was watching a deserter swim hard for the orchard.

"Down!" Zephyrine snapped, and we went flat against a tree trunk as the swell passed over us and collapsed, and the flood went smooth and innocent again, as if it had done nothing at all.

Bram reached us white-faced and gasping. "You—" he wheezed at Zephyrine. "You said you were done. You said—"

"I said I'd left it in the valley." She lifted Issel down onto a low branch, checked her over with quick hands, and only then let herself shake. "It appears it declined to be left. Help me count the soldiers. I want to know how many saw my face."

"None," I said. "They saw a wave. They'll report a wave." I looked at her—at this small, tired herbalist with river water streaming out of her braids—and revised, silently, several entries in my mental catalog of dangerous things. "They'll report a wave, and some clerk will file it under weather, and that will be that."

But Bram was staring south along the causeway, at the refugee boats that had scattered at the commotion, and his face had gone the color of porridge.

"No," he said. "No, it won't. Because the boat was heading somewhere before it stopped for us. Patrols don't wander. It was running a route." He swallowed. "There's a checkpoint at Marrowfen. Toll bridge. And if a patrol reports a storm-caller active in the drowned provinces, that report goes up the chain, and the chain ends with—" He stopped.

"With whom?" Zephyrine asked.

"With the Inquisition," he said. "With Sarel."

I didn't know the name. I wish, with the clarity of hindsight, that I had asked more questions right then instead of making a joke, which is what I did, because jokes are how I hold the pen against the blank spaces.

"A wave," I said, "and one clerk. How bad can the paperwork be?"

* * *

We dried out that night in the loft of a church that stood, alone of everything in the parish, above the waterline—the builders had known something the levee engineers hadn't. Refugees crowded its floor below us; we took the hay loft and shared it with three goats and a family of swallows. Through the floorboards came the sound of the congregation below, and they weren't praying, or not exactly. They were singing. Old songs, in the old cadence—the same cadence Issel used, the same rising lilt Zephyrine had gone pale over in the valley—and the child lay between us on the hay with her eyes closed, mouthing words she knew and we didn't.

"They're singing the province awake," Zephyrine murmured. "That's what the keepers called it. Singing a place back into memory so it can't be erased."

"Well," I said, "it isn't working. I have the current charts. There's nothing here to wake."

"Then sing louder," said Bram, from the darkness, with his back to us, and none of us had anything to add to that.

I lay awake a long time, listening to the water lap the churchyard wall, and did what I always do with grief: I drafted it. Somewhere in my head a map began to draw itself—the drowned provinces, all eleven villages restored, the bell tower at Saint Ferren's, the orchards, the sluices, every name the ministry had struck, rendered in my own careful hand. A chart of a country that officially does not exist. I resolved, lying there in the hay dust, that if I survived this, I would draw it. Properly. Every name. Let them burn that.

Near dawn, hooves on the churchyard gravel woke us all—hard, disciplined, too many. Bram was at the window first, and when he turned around his face told me everything before he said a word.

"Riders," he whispered. "Imperial dispatch riders, heading south. Fast." He hesitated, and I understood, with a sinking I felt in my teeth, that the worst was in what he wasn't saying.

"Bram. Heading south *from where?*"

"North," he said. "They came from the north. From the direction of the valley."

Below us, the singing had stopped.
