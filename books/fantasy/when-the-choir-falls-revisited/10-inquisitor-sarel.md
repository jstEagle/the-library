## 10. Inquisitor Sarel

The trouble with burning archives, I have found, is that fire is an honest worker. It does not distinguish between the ledger of grain tariffs and the genealogy of a dynasty. It consumes everything with the same cheerful indifference, and afterward you are left standing in the ash wondering whether the thing you wanted destroyed was actually in that particular drawer at all.

I do not enjoy burning things. I want to make that clear, because people assume otherwise. They see a woman in grey who asks questions in a soft voice and never raises it, and they decide she must be a sadist, or a zealot, or someone who goes home at night and warms her hands at the memory of smoke. The truth is more tedious. I am a clerk. I believe in records. I believe that a thing written down properly is a thing understood, and a thing understood is a thing that can be governed. Fire is what you use when the writing has failed. Fire is an admission of clerical error, and I resent every one of them.

So when I arrived in the drowned provinces that autumn, I did not burn anything. I set up my desk.

* * *

The desk mattered. His Radiance's other inquisitors worked from saddles and tent-flaps, all urgency and torchlight, and they produced confessions the way a broken mill produces flour—plenty of it, mostly chaff. I had a folding desk of oiled walnut, a brass inkwell, three pens of differing weights, and a ledger bound in grey calfskin with my name tooled on the spine in letters small enough to be missed by anyone not looking for them. On the first page, in my own hand: *Persons of Interest, Western Reaches, Revised.* Below that, one hundred and forty names, each with a line of provenance, a line of status, and a column for disposition.

One hundred and forty names. Of those, perhaps thirty were still alive, though I held out no great hope for eleven of them. The rest were ash already, or river-water, or entries I would one day strike through with a single clean line—not in anger, but the way any careful clerk closes an account.

Near the top of the list, third from the first page, was a name I had been carrying for six years: **Hartwell, Zephyrine.** Provenance: storm-caller, order of the Tempest Choir, attested at the Fall of Coriel. Status: unlocated since the amnesty proclamations; presumed concealed. Disposition: pending.

*Pending.* I had written that word six years ago and I had not yet struck it through. Some people find this obsessive. I find it honest. A name on a list is a debt, and I have never once defaulted on a debt.

* * *

The village was called Sorrel's End, which was optimistic of it, since the levees upstream had been broken two years past and the end had come for everything else long before. Half its houses stood in water to their window-sills. The other half had migrated uphill over the years, plank by plank, like patient hermit crabs, and now huddled around a chapel whose bell tower leaned at the angle of a man listening hard.

I conducted my interviews in the chapel, because it was dry, and because peasants lie less badly in front of their god. Not well, mind you—they lie constantly, fluently, with tremendous invention—but less *badly*. There is always a moment when their eyes flick upward, as if checking whether the roof is load-bearing.

I began, as I always begin, with courtesies. I offered the reeve a chair. I commented on the leaning tower. I asked after his wife's cough, because his wife had a cough—I had made inquiries on the road, from a tinker who owed me nothing and told me everything, because I listen the way other people spend: continuously, and without much thought for tomorrow.

Then I opened the ledger.

"I'm told," I said, "that in the year of the Fall, lightning came down on the capital. Nine strikes, in the space of a quarter hour, each one finding a gatehouse or an armory or a bridge. And I'm told that afterward, when the Choir was scattered, some of its members passed through these provinces heading west." I turned the ledger toward him, though he could not read it; that was not the point. The point was that it existed. "I'm looking for one of them."

"Never seen no storm-callers," said the reeve.

"No," I agreed. "You wouldn't have. They don't look like anything. That's rather the point of hiding." I smiled at him, and I watched him decide, wrongly, that the smile meant warmth. "But here is what interests me, Master Reeve. Two years ago, your neighbor upriver—a man named Doss, fisherman, widower—traded a jar of honey for a book. An old book. Banned inventory, third category. He kept it under a floorboard and read it to his daughter on winter nights. Do you know what happened to Doss?"

"He moved on," said the reeve. "West somewhere."

"He drowned," I said pleasantly. "In shallow water, which is a curious way for a fisherman to drown, and the coroner's report—which I wrote, incidentally, so you may trust its accuracy—notes that he had bruises on his arms consistent with being held under. But that isn't the interesting part either. The interesting part is that before he died, Doss sold a second jar of honey to a peddler, and mentioned—in the way men mention things when they've been reading banned books to their daughters—that there was a woman living in a valley west of the fens who could cure a fever with moss and a word."

The chapel went very quiet. Somewhere above us, a pigeon rearranged itself.

"I don't—" the reeve started.

"You don't know anything," I finished for him. "Of course. Nobody ever knows anything. It's remarkable, really. I travel through a country of forty million souls and not one of them has ever known anything." I closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Let me tell you how this works, Master Reeve, so we can both stop pretending. I am going to sit in this chapel for three days. I will interview every soul in this parish, including your wife, including your children, including old Mother Cass who everyone agrees is simple and whom everyone also tells their secrets to, because simple people are wonderful vaults. And if, at the end of three days, I have learned nothing new about storms or valleys or women who cure fevers with words, then I will write in my report that Sorrel's End is loyal, dull, and of no further interest—and dull parishes get left alone, Master Reeve. Dullness is armor. But if I learn that someone here knew something and withheld it—"

I let the sentence stand unfinished. Unfinished sentences are load-bearing. People build their confessions on top of them.

He gave me the valley by the second morning. Not directly—he never knew he'd given it to me at all. He gave it to me in the negative, in the things he was too careful *not* to say. He told me about the western pass without telling me, describing the parish boundaries with a precision that skipped one stretch of ridge like a tongue finding a missing tooth. He told me about a herbalist without telling me, mentioning every farm on the south road and none of the holdings past the boundary stone. People think lying is concealment. Lying is architecture. Every wall tells you where the treasure is.

By the third day I had a valley, a boundary stone, and a woman of about fifty who lived alone and was very good, according to everyone, at being ordinary.

I wrote in the ledger, beneath Hartwell's entry: *Probable location narrowed. Valley, western fens, beyond boundary stone. Verify.*

And then, because I am thorough and because patience is the only weapon I have ever needed to sharpen, I did not go.

* * *

You must understand something about valleys that hide magic-dead ground. Everyone assumes such places are fortresses. They are not. They are jars, and jars have lids, and lids have edges, and people who live inside jars eventually need honey, salt, nails, news. A woman can bury herself for twelve years—I had read the file; twelve years, four months, since the last confirmed sighting—but she cannot bury her supply lines. So instead of marching a squad of Radiant infantry up to her door and dying stupidly on ground where our own battle-mages' workings curdled and died, I did what I always do.

I bought thread.

Not literally. Well—literally, in part. I commissioned a survey of peddlers' routes through the western fens, cross-referenced against toll receipts, and discovered that a tinker named Fitch had been selling needles and dye to a solitary household past the boundary stone for the better part of a decade. Fitch was not disloyal. Fitch was simply a man who talked to everyone and remembered everything, which in my trade makes him worth more than a regiment. From Fitch I learned the household bought lamp oil in modest amounts, mended its own fences, grew feverfew and comfrey, and—this detail I copied into the ledger twice, underlined—had taken no apprentices in twelve years until, this autumn, a child appeared.

A child. Half-frozen, mute in the imperial tongue, speaking something else. Fitch had seen her only once, through a doorway, and described her the way people describe things that frighten them without knowing why: *small, dark-haired, looked at me like she was counting my teeth.*

I sat with that for a long evening, my pen uncapped, the lamp burning low. A hidden storm-caller. A dead language. A child delivered out of nowhere to the one woman in the western provinces with both the skill and the motive to shelter her. I am not a mystic; I do not believe in omens. But I believe in patterns, and patterns are only omens that have agreed to behave themselves.

There was a file in the central archive—or there had been, before my colleagues with their torches got to it—concerning the keepers of a place called Vessia. Vessia had been struck from the maps in the first year of the usurpation, along with its language, its histories, and its existence. Officially, no such country had ever been. Unofficially, the erasure had never quite taken, because erasure never does; paper remembers even when people burn it. And the last fragmentary record I had recovered—one scorched leaf, rescued from a brazier in a provincial archive by a junior clerk I later had promoted, partly for competence and partly because competent people should owe me—referred to the Door of Vessia, and to a key that was not metal.

*A key of blood and breath,* the leaf said. Whatever that meant. I had spent four years learning what it might mean, and I had learned chiefly that the keepers had raised their children in the old tongue, and that the keepers were now extinct, and that extinction, in my experience, is a word people use before they finish counting.

I dipped my pen and wrote, beneath Hartwell's entry, in my smallest hand:

*Child of keeper lineage probable. If the Door exists, she is the means of opening it. If she reaches it, the erasure fails. Priority: absolute.*

Then I blotted it, capped the ink, and sat in the dark of my quarters listening to the fen-wind worry at the shutters, and I permitted myself, for exactly one minute, to feel the weight of the thing. One hundred and forty names. One child. One woman who had once brought nine bolts of lightning down on a capital city in a quarter of an hour, and who had then done the most frightening thing imaginable.

She had stopped.

Anyone can rage. Rage is cheap; the usurper imports it by the barrel. But a weapon that lays itself down and stays laid down for twelve years—that is discipline, and discipline is the one quality I have never been able to interrogate out of anyone. You can break fear, break love, break loyalty. Discipline doesn't break. It waits.

Very well. I could wait too. Waiting was my native language.

* * *

The dispatch came at dawn, and it was, as dispatches so often are, both a gift and an insult.

His Radiance's army was moving west ahead of schedule—the free cities first, the fens swept on the way, a cordon drawn across the passes before winter. Attached was a directive, stamped in the red wax that meant *not advisory*: all inquisitorial resources in the western reaches to converge on identified pockets of pre-imperial speech. Speakers of erased tongues to be detained. Materials to be burned. Witnesses to be processed.

*Processed.* I do wish the chancellery would choose a register and hold it. We are either a government of laws or a government of bonfires, and the constant switching is terribly hard on the filing.

But buried in the directive, in a paragraph clearly drafted by someone who had read my reports and feared them slightly, was the authorization I had been requesting for two years: authority to detain and transport persons of exceptional priority, with a company of horse at my disposal.

I took out the ledger and turned to Hartwell's page. Twelve years of *pending*. I picked up my pen, and for the first time since I had entered the name, I added to the disposition column:

*Pending no longer. Moving to apprehend before the army arrives. She runs west—to the Door, if my reading holds. Let her run. A list is patient, and so am I, and every mile she travels toward Vessia confirms the map I have been drawing in the dark for six years.*

I blotted the page, closed the ledger, and gave orders for the horses.

Somewhere west of the fens, in a valley that did not appear on any map I owned, a woman was teaching a child herbs, and mending fences, and believing—with that terrible discipline of hers—that quiet was a place you could live.

I intended to introduce her to the truth of it personally, politely, and in writing.

We rode at noon.
