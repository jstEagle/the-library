## 11. Issel Speaks

The woman with the storm inside her is called Zephyrine. I know this because the map-man says it when he thinks I am sleeping, and because she says it herself when she is angry at her own hands.

I am called Issel. I have been called other things too. The keepers called me *little door*. The soldiers did not call me anything. They only pointed.

I will tell it now, in the way I was taught to tell things. The keepers said: *say what you saw. Say what you heard. Do not say what you guess.* But they are all dead now, so maybe guessing is allowed. Maybe guessing is all that is left of anybody.

* * *

Before the soldiers came, there was the sanctuary under the mountain with no name on any map, because the keepers unmade its name so carefully that even the mountain forgot it was named.

I lived there since before I can remember living anywhere else. There were nine keepers when I was very small. Then seven. Then five. They grew fewer the way candles grow fewer — not all at once, but one morning you look and the light has changed and someone's chair is empty and nobody explains, because explaining would mean saying the word *taken*, and the keepers did not like to say that word out loud where the walls could learn it.

Old Mother Vesh was the last one who talked much. She had hands like tree bark and she smelled of lamp oil and dried feverfew, which is a smell I still look for in every bundle of herbs Zephyrine dries, and have not found yet, and do not say that I am looking.

Mother Vesh taught me words. Not the words of the valley people, or the road people, or the soldier people. Older words. She said they were the words the Door was built with, and that a thing built with words can only be spoken to with the same ones.

"The Door remembers," she taught me to say. "Say it."

"The Door remembers."

"Again. It is not a song. It is a key turning."

"The Door remembers."

"Good. Now never say it near water."

I asked her why. She said: because water carries sound farther than air does, and some sounds are invitations.

I did not understand then. I understand a little more now. Understanding is mostly just being afraid of the right things.

* * *

Here is what the sanctuary was like, so that somebody somewhere knows, even if it is only me telling it to the dark inside my own head:

It was stone, but warm stone, stone that had been told a long time ago to be warm and had agreed. There were lamps that burned without oil. There was a hall with pillars carved all over in the old words, and I used to run my fingers along them and the carvings were slightly warm under my fingers, like the flank of a sleeping animal. There was a kitchen where we ate barley and onions and once a year, on the longest night, honey cake. There was a room I was not allowed into, and I obeyed, mostly, except twice, and both times the room felt like standing at the top of a stair in the dark, knowing there were more stairs than you could see.

And below everything, down a stair cut so smooth it was like walking down the throat of something patient, there was the Door itself.

I saw it four times. Keepers are allowed to see it once a year, on the night of remembering. It is not a door like a door in a house. It is a door like a wound in the world that decided to be polite about it. It stands in a frame of black stone and the space inside the frame is not stone and not air and not dark exactly — it is *elsewhere*, folded up small, the way a letter is folded up small and is still a whole letter.

When I stood in front of it, the old words in my mouth felt less like words and more like the Door's own name being remembered by itself.

"What is behind it?" I asked Mother Vesh, the first time.

"It is not behind," she said. "Behind is for rooms. The Door opens onto what was kept safe. That is all I will tell you, and even that is too much."

"But who is kept there?"

"People," she said, after a long time. "People who were erased. Erasing is a thing that can be done to a country, little door, and to a language, and to a person. But it cannot be done all the way. Not if someone keeps the receipt."

I thought about that for years. I think I understand it now, sitting by this fire with the storm-woman mending a sock and the map-man drawing and the soldier watching the dark. The Door is a receipt. The country that officially does not exist — it exists in there. Folded up small. Whole.

* * *

The soldiers came in autumn. I know it was autumn because the birches on the mountainside had gone the color of the honey cakes, and Mother Vesh said, "Eat well tonight," which is what she always said when she knew something bad.

They came from the east pass, and they did not get lost, which frightened the keepers more than their numbers did. The sanctuary had no name and no road and no place on any map. To find it, someone had to be told. Someone had to be made to tell.

There was fighting. I was put in the root cellar with Brother Anselm, who put his hands over my ears, which does not work, ears are not doors, sound comes through hands the same as anything. I heard the lamps go out one by one. Lamps going out make a small sound, a glass-and-silence sound, and I counted them until I lost count, and losing count is one of the worst things that has happened to me.

Then Brother Anselm took his hands off my ears and said, "Issel. Listen. You are going to go up the north stair and out the goat track and down into the low country, and you are not going to stop, and you are not going to come back."

"I want to stay with you."

"Yes," he said. "That is the whole trouble with love. Now listen. You know the words. You are the only one left who knows them whole. The others learned pieces; you learned the whole of it, because you are young and your head is soft and takes the shape of what is poured into it." He almost smiled when he said that. "You must carry the words to the Door's other friends. There is a woman in a valley where magic cannot follow. Find her. If you cannot find her, find anyone who knows the old tongue. If you cannot find anyone, keep the words yourself and stay alive, because a living keeper is worth more than a dead army."

"Why me?"

"Because you are small and the soldiers do not look at small things," he said. "Being overlooked is a kind of armor. Wear it."

Then he kissed the top of my head and pushed me up the stair, and that is the last time anyone from the sanctuary touched me, and I have been keeping count of touches since, the way Mother Vesh kept count of lamps.

* * *

I walked for a long time. I will not describe all of it. Some of it I have decided not to remember, and I am good at deciding, because the keepers trained me to hold or release words as needed, and memories are only words that have not found their mouths yet.

Things I will keep: a boatman who fed me fish stew and asked no questions. A dog that walked with me for two days and then went home. A shrine at a crossroads where I slept and dreamed the Door humming, which meant, I think, that it missed being remembered.

Things I will release: released.

The valley was hard to find because it does not want finding. But the words in my head pulled, the way a hook pulls, gentle and constant, toward the west and down, and I followed the pull, and on the third day of following it I smelled woodsmoke and herbs and I understood: this is the place where magic cannot follow. This is the place where the pull goes quiet. Which means the woman here has made herself quiet, which means she is hiding, which means she is the right woman, because everyone worth finding is hiding.

I knocked on her door. My hands were too cold to knock properly, so it was more like falling against the wood, but she opened it anyway, and when she opened it her face did a thing I have seen on faces ever since: it recognized me before it decided to.

She took me in. She fed me. She did not ask questions for three days, which I respected enormously, because asking questions is how soldiers start.

* * *

Now we are five, counting me. The storm-woman, Zephyrine, whose anger is a held breath. The map-man, Wren, who lies beautifully and draws truer than he talks. The soldier, Bram, who flinches at his own boots. And me.

Tonight the fire is low and the others think I am asleep, and I decide to speak, because Mother Vesh said a keeper speaks when speaking will save more than silence will, and I have been doing sums in my head for days and the sums have come out wrong in the direction of talking.

So I sit up in my blanket and I say, in their tongue, which I have been learning from listening, word by word, the way I learn everything:

"You want to know what I am. I will tell you. But you have to not interrupt, because interruptions make me lose the thread, and the thread is the whole point of a thread."

Zephyrine puts down the sock. Wren puts down the pen. Bram stops looking at the dark and starts looking at me, which is better.

"I lived with the keepers of the Door," I say. "Nine of them, then seven, then five. They taught me the old words, all of them, whole, because I was young and my head was soft. The Door is real. It is under a mountain whose name was unmade. It does not lead out of the country. It leads to where the country was put when they tried to erase it. The keepers called it keeping the receipt." I pause to check the words are landing. They are landing. Zephyrine's face has gone the color of the ash at the fire's edge. "The soldiers came in autumn. The lamps went out. I counted and lost count. Brother Anselm sent me away because I am small and soldiers do not look at small things. He said to find a woman in a valley where magic cannot follow. I found her. Here is the part that matters."

I make my voice be the voice Mother Vesh used for the important parts, the flat steady voice, the voice that does not shake even when the girl using it wants to shake.

"I don't know everything the Door opens onto. I was never allowed in the far room. But I know this: the Door opens for keeper's blood and storm-caller's hand, together, and it closes forever once passed through, and if the usurper gets it, he doesn't get an exit. He gets the country. He gets everything that was ever erased, folded up small, and he unfolds it and burns it properly this time. Erasure twice. Mother Vesh said erasure twice is the only death that sticks."

Nobody interrupts. Good. But Zephyrine's breath has gone ragged, and I watch her hands, and her hands are trembling in the way I have only seen them tremble once before, the night I arrived, when the valley's quiet cracked around me like ice on a pond.

"You're the key," she says. It is not a question. Questions go up at the end. Hers went down, like a stone dropped into a well.

"I'm the key," I agree. "And you're the hand. And the map-man knows where the mountain is, because I've seen his hidden maps, the ones under the false bottom, and one of them has a fold of hills drawn in ink that isn't imperial ink, and the fold of hills has a mark on it shaped like a keyhole, and you thought I couldn't read your marks, Wren Oduya, but Mother Vesh taught me map-marks too, older ones, and yours are descended from ours."

Wren makes a sound like a man swallowing his own name.

"And the soldier knows when the armies move," I go on, because I am not finished, and a keeper finishes. "And Sarel is coming. I heard the word Sarel in the fens, from a refugee woman, spat like a seed. Sarel keeps lists. My name might be on the list. The keepers' names were on the list. That is why there are none of them left."

I stop. My thread is all given away now, every spool of it, and I feel lighter and sicker at the same time, the way you feel after vomiting, which is a comparison Mother Vesh would not approve of but is accurate.

Zephyrine crosses the room and sits beside me and does not touch me, which I appreciate, and then touches me anyway, one hand resting over mine, which I appreciate more.

"How long have you known all this?" she asks quietly.

"Since the beginning," I say. "But knowing a thing and being able to say it are different doors. Tonight the second one opened."

Outside, far off in the direction of the plains, thunder mutters — ordinary thunder, weather-thunder, nothing to do with her, probably. Zephyrine's head turns toward it anyway, quick as a bird's, and I watch her listen, and I understand that she is already hearing it as an answer.

"They'll reach the valley within days," Bram says from the doorway, in his soldier voice, the flat one. "Maybe sooner. We need to leave tomorrow. Tonight, if—"

"Tomorrow," Zephyrine says, and her thumb moves once across my knuckles, back and forth, like she is checking I am real. "We leave at moonset."

I lie back down in my blanket. Nobody tells me to sleep, which is right, because I am not a child tonight; tonight I am the last keeper, small and overlooked and armored in it, carrying a whole country folded up small behind my ribs.

But after the fire burns lower, and the breathing around the room goes slow, I let myself be a child for exactly as long as it takes to cry without sound, into the wool, where nobody counts.

Then I sleep, and I dream of the Door, and in the dream it is humming, and the hum has changed key.

It is not missing being remembered anymore.

It is waiting.
