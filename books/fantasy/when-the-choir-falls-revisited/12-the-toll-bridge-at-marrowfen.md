## 12. The Toll Bridge at Marrowfen

I've been asked, in my time, to do a great many things I'm not proud of. I've burned a granary. I've whipped a boy for sleeping on watch. I've stood at attention while a village was emptied of everything it had and told myself it was policy.

But standing on the Marrowfen road with fog up to my knees and Sergeant Doss Halvard's voice carrying across the water like he'd never left my nightmares, I can tell you honestly: this is the first time I've ever been afraid of doing the right thing.

The toll bridge at Marrowfen is the only crossing for forty miles that doesn't require you to swim through water the color of old bruises. The usurper's engineers rebuilt it after the levees went down—good timber, good stone footings, and at either end, a checkpoint with a brazier, a ledger, and four men with crossbows who are paid by the interrogation, not the hour. Wren had scouted it from the reed bank the evening before, flat on his belly in the mud like a man reading scripture, and come back gray-faced.

"It's Doss," he'd said. "Your Doss. He's running the east checkpoint."

"He's not *my* Doss."

"Then whose is he? Because he's wearing your old company's colors and he's got your description pinned under a weight on his table. I saw it from thirty yards. Red hair, army tattoo on the left forearm, deserter twice over." Wren had wiped mud off his spectacles with a gesture of profound grievance. "You're famous, Bram. Congratulations. Famous men don't cross bridges."

Now we crouched in the reeds while the fog did its slow work, and Issel slept against Zephyrine's shoulder with her mouth open, breathing the small wet breaths of a child who has learned that sleep is something you take when it's offered because it won't be offered twice. Zephyrine's hand rested on the girl's back, rising and falling with her, and I thought: there it is. That's the thing I deserted for the second time. Not an idea. A weight on someone's shoulder.

"We could go around," Wren whispered. "The ford upstream. Two days lost, maybe three, and the water's chest-deep and full of whatever drowned when they broke the levees."

"The army's two days behind us," Zephyrine said. "Maybe less. Sarel doesn't lose time; she spends it." She said the inquisitor's name the way you'd spit out a bone. "We cross here."

"And how," Wren said, "does the most wanted woman in three provinces propose to walk past a checkpoint?"

Zephyrine looked at me.

I want to be clear about what that look was. It wasn't command. It wasn't even request. It was the look of a woman who had already done the arithmetic and hated the answer, handing the last column to the only one of us who could read it.

"She wants me to talk to him," I said.

"Do you have a better idea?"

"I have several worse ones. That's usually how I know one's best."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about deserting: you don't leave the army. You just stop going to it. The army stays in your boots, your posture, the way you count exits in every room, the way you wake at dawn without an alarm. And the people—the people stay worst of all. Doss Halvard taught me how to hold a pike when I was sixteen and all elbows and appetite. He carried me a mile on his back when I took fever at Coldmarsh. He wrote letters for me because my letters came out crooked, and he never once made me feel small for it.

He also stood at attention at Vell's Crossing while the order was read out, and afterward he bought the first round and said, "Orders are orders, Kettle, and the man who argues with them drowns faster than the man who follows them into the water."

I loved him. That's the whole ugly truth of it. And love, in the army, is just loyalty with the thinking removed.

So there I was, walking out of the reeds onto the bridge road with my hood back and my hands visible, the way you approach a checkpoint if you're surrendering rather than attacking, and my heart was going like a drumline, and Doss Halvard looked up from his ledger and his face did something I'll be describing to myself for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

It did recognition first. Then joy. Then, arriving late and ruining everything, duty.

"Bram Kettle," he said. "Dead Bram Kettle."

"Reports of it were exaggerated."

"They usually are." He set down his pen very carefully, the way he did before saying something permanent. Behind him, the other three soldiers had shifted—not alarmed yet, but attentive, the way dogs go attentive. "Walk with me."

We walked to the far end of the checkpoint platform, out of easy earshot, which was a courtesy and we both knew it. The fog moved on the black water below. Somewhere out in the drowned fields, a heron cried once and quit.

"You're on the list," Doss said. "Not the bottom of it, either. Inquisitor Sarel herself flagged you. Twice-deserted, armed, traveling with—" he paused, and I watched him decide whether to say it, and watched him say it anyway, because Doss never flinched from a fact, "—the storm-caller. Hartwell. And a child."

"What happens to the child, Doss? On the list. What does it say happens to her?"

His jaw worked. "That's not—"

"I've seen the lists. I carried them. Speakers get questioned. Children who speak get *processed*. So before you tell me about duty, tell me what the duty says."

He was quiet a long moment. Under the brazier light his face was older than I remembered, or maybe I'd just spent twelve years remembering it young. "You always were good at making the simple thing sound monstrous."

"It isn't the sounding that makes it monstrous, Sarge."

"Don't call me that." But there was no iron in it. There never had been, really. That was the tragedy of Doss Halvard: he'd spent thirty years pretending to be a wall, and he was a door the whole time.

Behind us, I heard the others coming up the road—slow, unhurried, a herbalist with a sick child and a cartographer with a limp, nothing worth a second glance, except that every glance in the world was a second glance now. Doss heard them too. His eyes flicked past my shoulder, and I saw him do the counting. Four travelers. One deserter. One name on his table under a brass weight.

"All right," he said quietly. "Here's what happens. I log you as a smuggler taken at the bridge, held overnight, escaped before transfer. It buys you until morning. Take the south track after the second mile marker, the causeway—it floods at midday but you'll be long gone. And Bram." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "The inquisitor has your faces. All of yours. She drew them herself. She's *good* at it. Whatever you're doing, being seen is the same as being caught. Do you understand me?"

"Doss—"

"That's an order, Kettle. Last one I'll ever give you. Be unseen."

And then, because the gods have a sense of humor and it's a bad one, Corporal Vetch came around the brazier with the ledger page in his hand and said, "Sergeant, this party needs logging. Names and business."

Doss didn't move. For maybe three seconds, the longest three seconds of my life, he stood between Vetch and us with the fog drifting between them, and I watched a man hold two oaths in his hands and feel both of them cut.

"Logged and released," Doss said. "Herbalist taking medicine to the fen villages. Cleared this morning. It's in the day-book."

Vetch frowned. "I don't recall—"

"You weren't on shift. Check the book or don't. I don't write things twice for corporals."

It was a lie. Clean, smooth, thirty years of rank behind it. Vetch shrugged and went back to his brazier, and Doss turned to me, and his face had gone carefully blank, the way it did before whippings, so no one could say the sergeant enjoyed it.

"Go," he said.

"Sarge—"

"*Go.*"

We went. Zephyrine took my arm as we passed—I think partly to steady me, partly to steer, because my legs had opinions of their own—and Issel woke just enough to look up at Doss Halvard with those old, old eyes and say something soft in the language that predates kingdoms. It sounded, God help me, like a blessing. Or a warning. With her it was hard to say which.

Doss stared at her like a man staring at a ghost with paperwork.

Then we were past the checkpoint, and the bridge timbers rang under our feet, and the fog closed behind us like a door.

* * *

We made six miles before I stopped being able to pretend I was fine. Wren called the halt at a ruined kiln off the south track, and I sat down on the cold brick with my back against it and discovered my hands were shaking.

"Adrenaline," Wren said, settling beside me with the sigh of a man whose knees had filed formal complaints years ago. "Perfectly normal. The body spends fear whether or not you authorize the expense."

"He lied for us. To his own corporal. On his own checkpoint." I looked at my hands. "You know what happens when Sarel audits that day-book. She will audit it. She audits *everything*. She'll find the entry that doesn't exist, and she'll know exactly which sergeant wrote it, and she'll ask him why, and Doss Halvard has never once in his life known how to lie under pressure."

Zephyrine stood a little apart, watching the track behind us, but I saw her shoulders change. "You think she'll break him."

"I think she doesn't have to break him. He'll confess. He'll confess *proudly*, because that's who he is—he'll stand up straight and tell her he falsified a record to let a deserter pass, and he'll consider it the honest thing, and she'll—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "She'll process it. Neatly. Like an entry in a ledger."

Nobody said anything. Out in the dark, the flooded fields breathed their rotten smell, and somewhere far off a dog barked itself out.

Issel came and sat down next to me. She didn't say anything—she mostly didn't say anything in any language I understood—but she put her small hand flat on my shaking one, the way you'd steady a cup, and left it there. And I sat there in the ruins of a kiln with a child's hand on mine and understood, finally and completely, the thing I'd been circling since the night I walked out of my unit's camp with my kit and my shame:

Survival isn't free. Somebody pays. Today the price was paid by a man who taught me to hold a pike, and the coin was a lie I needed him to tell, and I would need him to need me to do it again, because ahead of us lay more bridges, more checkpoints, more ledgers, and every single one of them would cost somebody something.

Wren cleared his throat. "For what it's worth," he said, "in my professional opinion as a maker of false documents, that day-book entry will hold longer than you think. Doss struck me as a careful man."

"He's a careful man who just stopped being careful."

"Then we'd best make sure it was worth it." Wren nodded down the dark track, south, toward the hills where a city that wasn't on any map was waiting for a child and a storm. "Every mile we gain is a mile he bought. Spend them well."

Zephyrine finally turned from watching the road. The fog had gotten into her hair, and her eyes had that banked-fire look I'd learned to dread and rely on in equal measure.

"Then we don't sleep past dawn," she said. "We spend his miles fast."

And that was when Issel lifted her head, sharp, like a deer scenting weather, and said one word in her old tongue—pointing back north, the way we'd come, toward the bridge and the brazier and the man we'd left holding our lie in his hands.

I didn't know the word. But I knew the tone.

It was the tone you use for smoke.
