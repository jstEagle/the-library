## 13. Storm Without Permission

There is a particular quality to the sky before a storm that I have spent twelve years learning to ignore, the way a reformed drunkard learns to walk past taverns with his eyes fixed on the cobbles as though they were the most fascinating stones ever quarried. The air goes thick and sweet. The light turns the color of old pewter. Somewhere above the clouds, pressure begins its slow argument with everything beneath it.

For twelve years I had looked at such skies and thought: *lovely weather for drying herbs.*

On the moor above Marrowfen, with inquisitors' crossbows trained on us from three sides and Issel's small hand gripping mine hard enough to grind bone, I looked at the sky and thought: *oh no.*

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because ambushes deserve context and so do mistakes.

We had left the toll bridge at dawn, moving fast through the reed flats while Bram's former sergeant was still deciding whether to report the deserter he'd let pass or bury the memory of him entirely. Wren had navigated us north by a route that existed on none of his official charts and all of his unofficial ones — "the maps I'd burn if the Inquisition ever searched my pack," he'd said cheerfully, which is the sort of joke a man makes when it isn't entirely a joke.

By midmorning we were climbing onto the high moor, a vast brown tableland of heather and standing stones where the drowned provinces fell away behind us like a bad memory finally out of sight. Refugees dotted the landscape below — little clusters of boats abandoned at the flood's edge, families walking in strung-out lines toward Vessia, smoke from cookfires rising thin and hopeful into the morning.

"We'll make the fold of hills by tomorrow night," Wren said, consulting his chart. "If we keep pace."

Issel rode on Bram's shoulders, her fingers laced in his collar, surveying the moor with the grave attention of a queen reviewing troops. She had spoken perhaps six words in two days. All of them were fragments of that older tongue, and all of them, Wren believed, related to direction. *North. Door. Remember.* A vocabulary for a mission, not a childhood.

I remember thinking, as we crested the first ridge: *this is going too well.*

Twelve years in a valley teaches you superstition the way a monastery teaches prayer. When nothing has gone wrong for a suspiciously long time, something is being patient.

The heather moved wrong.

That was all. A patch of it, maybe forty paces off our path, rippling against the wind instead of with it. I might have missed it. Bram didn't. He'd been a soldier long enough to read terrain the way Wren read maps, and his hand came up flat — *stop* — without any sound at all.

"Down," he breathed. "All of you. Down."

We dropped into the heather. And then the moor stood up around us.

They'd been lying flat among the bracken in a wide crescent, a dozen of them, maybe fifteen, in the grey-and-black livery of the usurper's Inquisition — not soldiers, worse than soldiers, the ones who carry ledgers alongside their weapons because every kill must be *documented*. They rose like the moor itself deciding to grow men, and crossbows came up in a smooth, drilled motion, and a voice said:

"Zephyrine Hartwell."

I knew the voice's type if not its owner. Flat. Patient. The voice of a person who has already written your name in a ledger and is merely waiting for the ink to dry.

"You'll come with us," the voice continued. A woman stepped forward — narrow face, spectacles, the calm of someone doing arithmetic rather than violence. Behind her, the crossbows tracked us. "The child especially. The others are incidental. You may live, Hartwell. That is not mercy. It is procedure."

Bram shifted beside me, angling his body between the bolts and Issel. Wren's hand crept toward the knife at his belt with the doomed optimism of a man whose primary weapon is stationery.

And me?

I did the thing I had sworn on my grandmother's grave never to do. I reached for the sky.

* * *

You should understand what reaching feels like after twelve years of not reaching.

Imagine a limb asleep for a decade — not hours, years — and then blood comes back. First the pins and needles, the terrible sweetness of sensation returning, and then the ache, and then the strength, all of it arriving at once and far too much, because the body does not ration what it has been starved of.

The valley had kept magic out of me the way a dam keeps out a river. Out here, past the boundary stone, past Marrowfen, under this bruising pewter sky, there was nothing between me and the storm except my own famously reliable restraint.

I opened one door inside myself. Every other door in the house blew open.

The pressure above the clouds had been arguing with the world all morning, and suddenly I could hear every word of it. The static crawling along the standing stones. The charge gathering in the heather. Twelve years of weather I had politely declined to notice, all of it still owed, all of it still waiting, and the sky recognized me the way a dog recognizes the hand that fed it — with joy, and with teeth.

"Hartwell." The inquisitor's voice sharpened. "Whatever you're considering—"

I called the lightning down on the crossbows.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left of that moment. I aimed. I chose. There were fifteen of them spread across a crescent two hundred paces wide, and I aimed at the six nearest us, the ones whose bolts would reach us first, and I told the storm: *there. Those.*

The storm agreed enthusiastically.

The bolt came down white and total, and the world went silent in the way only very loud things can manage. When my vision cleared, the six nearest inquisitors were down — some dead, some writhing, the heather around them burning in wet black circles — and the rest were scattering, screaming about witches and demons and, in one case that I will remember until I die, *mother*.

And the fire did what fire does on a moor in a dry autumn with wind behind it.

It ran.

* * *

"Stop it," Wren was shouting. "Zephyrine — the fire — stop it—"

"I'm trying."

Here is what nobody tells you about calling storms: stopping them is harder than starting them. Starting is a door flung open. Stopping is catching a river in your hands. The power doesn't live in you; you live in *it*, briefly, a guest in something vast and indifferent, and when you ask it to leave it looks at you the way weather looks at everyone — with no opinion whatsoever.

I pulled. I gathered. I did the things my grandmother taught me in a kitchen that smelled of rain and burnt sugar, the breathing, the shaping, the *naming* of the wind — and the storm slowed, sulked, and did not stop. Clouds boiled. Thunder rolled across the moor like furniture being dragged through heaven. Rain began to fall in fat, furious drops that hissed on the burning heather but did not put it out, because the fire had found the peat underneath, and peat burns slow and deep and mean.

"The camp," Bram said.

I didn't understand him. Then I followed his eyes down the slope, to where the refugee lines had been walking that morning — and where, tucked into a hollow I hadn't seen, hadn't *looked* for, dozens of cookfires now scattered and panicked. Families. Boats hauled up on the grass. Children.

My lightning had struck two hundred paces away. My fire was coming for them at the speed of the wind.

"Zephyrine." Wren grabbed my arm. His face had gone the color of his charts' blank spaces. "The hollow. The wind is driving it straight into the hollow."

I did the arithmetic myself, then, fast and filthy: the storm was mine, the wind was mine, and if I couldn't kill the fire I could at least argue with the wind. I turned my back on the fleeing inquisitors — let them run, let them report, that bill would come due later — and I took hold of the gale the way you take hold of a horse by the bit, knowing it could snap your neck for the presumption.

"*Turn,*" I said, aloud, in the old grammar, the words feeling obscene in my mouth after twelve years of silence. "*Turn, you great stupid beast, turn—*"

The wind turned.

Not all at once. Not cleanly. It swung in a wide, groaning arc, dragging the fire's edge sideways, bending the smoke column like a finger pushing a candle flame — and the flames licked up the slope away from the hollow, away from the refugees, into bare stone and wet rock where they had nothing left to eat and died grudgingly, smoking, spitting.

It worked.

God help me, it worked, and I stood on the moor with my arms raised and rain streaming down my face, feeling more powerful than I had felt since the night in the old capital, and it took me three full breaths to understand why Bram was shouting my name in a voice I'd never heard him use.

* * *

Not everyone got out.

The fire had reached the hollow's edge before the wind turned. Just its edge. Just a few strides of flame, running ahead of the main burn like outriders. Most of the refugees had fled uphill at the first thunderclap — they'd seen the lightning, they'd run from the witch and the fire both, and that flight saved them.

But there had been a woman at the hollow's mouth. I saw her afterward, though I wish with everything in me that I hadn't. She'd gone back for something — a bundle, a goat, a child, I never learned — and the outriders of my fire had caught her at the edge of the tents. The rain had come too late by four minutes. Maybe five.

Four minutes. I have spent nights turning that number over like a stone with a hole in it, looking for what's through the other side. If I had aimed wider. If I had aimed narrower. If I had checked the hollow first. If I had stayed in the valley and died there quietly, an old herbalist with mended fences and no ledger entry anywhere in the world.

Wren knelt by the woman and did whatever cartographers do with death, which is to say he was very still and very precise and afterward he would not speak of it. Bram organized the survivors with the mechanical competence of a man who has done this before, in other places, for other reasons, his jaw working like he was chewing on something that wouldn't go down.

And Issel —

Issel walked through the smoke and the wet ash, past the soldiers and the strangers and the steaming ruin of the heather, straight to where I stood with my hands still raised like a woman surrendering to a firing squad that hadn't arrived yet.

She took my hand. The burned one, though neither of us knew yet how badly it was burned; the pain hadn't reported for duty. She held it the way she had held it during the ambush, and she looked up at me, and she said — in the trade-tongue, her first words in it, rough and unpracticed and absolutely clear:

"You stopped it."

"I started it," I said. My voice came out strange. Small. "Issel, I—"

"You stopped it." Her grip tightened. In her language, the one older than kingdoms, she added a word I didn't know yet, though I would learn it later in Vessia, and it means roughly *enough*, and roughly *mercy*, and roughly *the debt is paid*, all at once.

Children forgive so easily. That is either the most beautiful thing about them or the most terrifying, and I honestly could not tell you which, because I had just been forgiven for something I could not forgive myself, and the forgiveness fit like a coat cut for someone else.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a standing stone, apart from the refugee column, because the refugees watched us the way you watch a dog that has bitten someone. Word travels faster than fire on a moor. By dusk, every family within a mile knew that a storm-witch walked among them, and that the usurper's stories about women like me were, if anything, understated.

Bram sat across the fire from me, cleaning a crossbow he'd taken off one of the fallen inquisitors, because apparently we were scavenging corpses now, because apparently this was my life.

"They'll report it," he said. Not unkindly. Reporters don't get to choose tone. "Sarel will know exactly where we are, what you can do, how much warning you need. I've seen her work. She'll use it."

"I know."

"And the refugees—" He stopped. Started again. "Some of them will talk too. Not to Sarel. To anyone who'll listen. *A storm-caller on the moor.* Fear spreads."

"I know, Bram."

He nodded and went back to the crossbow, and the silence between us was the honest kind, which is rarer and less comfortable than the dishonest kind.

Wren appeared out of the dark with his chart rolled under his arm like a baton. He sat down heavily. For a long time he studied the fire.

"In the capital," he said at last, "when they erased the provinces from the records, there was a debate in the Cartographic Office. Some of us argued the maps should mark what was destroyed. Ruins, at least. Graves, at minimum. The senior cartographers said no — a map is a tool, and tools shouldn't carry grief." He turned the chart over in his hands. "I've thought about that a lot lately. About what maps owe the dead." He glanced at me. "About what power owes them."

"Wren—"

"I'm not accusing you." He said it quickly, and I believed him, mostly. "I'm telling you I understand the argument you're about to make. *If I don't use it, we die. If I do use it, others might.* I've watched you do that arithmetic since the bridge. I just think you should know that I've seen the answer written down before, in a country that no longer exists, and the handwriting was very neat, and it was wrong."

He unrolled his chart, weighted the corners with stones, and traced our route north with one finger. Around the line, in his small exact hand, he had begun marking something new. Little symbols. At the hollow. At the burned stretch of moor.

"What are those?" I asked.

"Graves," Wren said. "A map is a tool. But I am not."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at the edge of the firelight with my bandaged hand cradled against my chest — the burn was shallow, the healers among the refugees had tended it with the grim courtesy people extend to dangerous animals — and I watched the clouds tear open over the peaks to the north and let the stars through, one by one, like a city lighting its lamps.

Somewhere behind us, an inquisitor was writing my name in a ledger. Somewhere ahead, a Door waited that needed exactly what I had loosed today, full and unbridled and terrible. Somewhere in between lay the drowned provinces and the free cities and a war that would not wait for my conscience to finish its deliberations.

Issel crept out of the blankets and settled against my side without asking, the way children and cats establish squatter's rights. Within moments she was asleep, her head heavy on my shoulder, her breath slow and trusting.

I looked at the northern sky, at the weather building out beyond the hills — I could feel it now, always, whether I wanted to or not; that door in me no longer closed all the way — and I made myself a promise with no confidence whatsoever that I could keep it.

*Only when it counts,* I told the storm, silently, in the old grammar, in case it was listening. It probably was. *No more accidents. No more weather without permission. Only when it counts.*

Far to the north, thunder answered.

It sounded, I thought, remarkably like laughter.
