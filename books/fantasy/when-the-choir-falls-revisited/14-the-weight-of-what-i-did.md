## 14. The Weight of What I Did

The rain I had called fell for three hours after the burning stopped, which was either mercy or mockery. I couldn't decide. The dead didn't care either way.

I stood at the edge of what had been the refugee camp and counted the fires still smoldering in the wet. Eleven tents gone. A cook-wagon split like a struck log. And the people—some of them walking, some of them sitting in the mud staring at nothing, and some of them under blankets that other refugees had laid down with the particular gentleness you learn only when you've done it before.

Forty-one. That was the number Bram gave me later, because he'd learned to count casualties the way soldiers do, quickly and without looking away. Forty-one people who had been alive at dusk because they were too poor to be anywhere else, and who were not alive at midnight because a woman with twelve years of practice being small had finally let herself be large.

The inquisitors' patrol that had ambushed us? Four men. Two dead, two fled into the dark. That was the arithmetic of it. Four against forty-one, and I had lost.

* * *

Nobody spoke on the walk back to the hollow where we'd sheltered. Issel rode on Wren's shoulders with her chin tucked against his hair, and Wren walked like a man carrying something heavier than a child. Bram limped—he'd taken a crossbow bolt through the meat of his shoulder during the ambush, before I'd ended it—and he kept glancing back at me the way you glance at a door you're not sure is locked.

I wanted him to stop doing that. I also wanted him to keep doing it forever, so that someone would be watching me, confirming I hadn't simply come apart and blown away like ash off the moor.

The hollow was an old quarry overhang, half-roofed with slate, dry enough to matter. We'd left our packs there that morning with the optimism of fools. I sat down against the far wall, apart from the others, because it seemed only decent. You quarantine a sickness. You quarantine a woman who calls lightning down on children.

The storm-calling was still in me. That was the worst part—the discovery of it. For twelve years I had believed the valley's silence meant my power slept, that it was a thing I could put down like a knife after dinner and pick up again only if I chose. But it hadn't been sleeping. It had been waiting. And now that the moor's open sky had let it back into my blood, it hummed along my bones like a second pulse, patient and enormous, listening for my permission.

I pressed my palms flat against the stone and thought: *never again.* The stone did not answer. It never does. That's why I trust it.

* * *

It was Wren who broke first. Of course it was Wren. He was the one who could not leave anything unmapped, including his own grievances.

"You knew." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. Wren shouting was weather; Wren quiet was a blade being drawn slowly enough for you to appreciate the metalwork. "You knew what you were. Twelve years in that valley, and you told us—what? That you were done with it? That it was behind you?"

"It was behind me."

"Then explain the camp." He turned, and firelight caught the ink stains on his fingers, the ones he'd never managed to scrub out no matter how many years since the guild expelled him. Cartographer's hands. A man who believed the world could be fixed if only it were drawn correctly. "Explain the forty-one."

"I don't have an explanation."

"No," he said. "You have a feeling about it. I can see it from here. Some beautiful tragic feeling about how terrible power is, and how sorry you are, and how none of this would have happened if the world were kinder. Well, the world isn't kind, Zephyrine. The world is the usurper's propaganda made flesh, and tonight you drew their poster for them. *The storm-caller returns. See what she does to her own people.* By morning every garrison from here to the capital will have that story, and it won't even be a lie. That's the genius of it. They won't need to embellish. You did the work for them."

"Wren—" Bram started.

"Let me finish. I have spent six years being erased, Kettle. Struck from the guild rolls, my charts burned, my name cut out of the atlases—I know exactly what it costs to be turned into a story someone else tells. And I watched you do it to yourself tonight, gladly, with thunder." His voice cracked on the last word, and I understood suddenly that his anger was grief wearing armor. He had liked me. That was the wound. "Issel could have died. She was twenty feet from the first strike. Did you even see her? Did you see anything past your own lightning?"

I had not. I said so. It seemed important, in that moment, to be accurate about my crimes rather than comfortable about them.

"No," I said. "I saw the inquisitors. I saw the crossbows leveled at us. And then I saw nothing at all. That's what it's like, Wren. That's what it was always like. It isn't a tool I hold. It's a flood I am, and I spent twelve years building a valley to stand in because I knew—if I ever opened that door again, I couldn't promise what came through it. Tonight I opened it anyway. So yes. Everything you're saying is true. Say all of it."

He stared at me, thrown. I think he had rehearsed this fight on the walk back and I had ruined it by agreeing with him.

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Don't do that. Don't make it easy to hate you. It's obscene."

"I'm not making anything easy. I'm just not going to lie to you. You're the cartographer. You above all people should get the unedited map."

That landed somewhere soft in him; I saw it hit. He turned away and crouched by the fire, feeding it sticks with more force than sticks require, and said nothing else for a long time.

* * *

Bram filled the silence, because Bram always filled silences—it was the sergeant in him, the man whose job had been to hold a line of frightened boys together with his voice.

"She saved our lives," he said. "Ours, and maybe thirty other people in that camp who got clear because she pulled the inquisitors' attention east. You want to draw the map fair, Oduya, draw the whole map."

"The whole map has forty-one names on it."

"And the other map has a hundred and sixty," Bram said. "Camp that size, inquisitor patrol that size—they weren't there to arrest anyone. Sarel's orders are witnesses die. All of them. We found the order on the corporal." He held up a folded paper, water-stained, official. "You want to read it? It's very tidy. Numbers and everything. The usurper's people are wonderful at numbers. What they can't abide is a storm they didn't schedule."

"You're defending her because you deserted twice," Wren said, without heat now, which meant it was almost fair. "You need her to be right. If she's a monster, then you tied your wagon to a monster."

"Yes," Bram said simply. "And if she's right, then I'm a man who picked the right side late instead of early, and either way I'm not leaving. That's what desertion taught me, Oduya. You don't get clean hands. You get to choose which dirt you carry." He looked at me across the fire, and his eyes were kind and tired and older than his face. "Carry it," he said. "But don't put it down where it can't do any good."

* * *

Issel came to me last, as she always did, moving through conflict the way water moves through a room—unbothered by furniture, finding the lowest point, which was me.

She didn't say anything. She rarely did, and when she did it was in that language older than crowns, and lately she had been teaching me it word by patient word, the way you teach a very large dog not to be afraid of stairs. She climbed into my lap—not asked, not hesitating, seven years old and smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool—and took my right hand, the one that had done it, and held it in both of hers.

Then she said a word I knew. One of the few dozen I had. It meant *storm*, but it also meant, according to Wren's notes, *the sky remembering itself*. She said it softly, twice, like a name, and then she pressed her small forehead against my knuckles and stayed there.

I cried. I had been holding it off with the discipline of twelve years of practice, and a child undid it in four seconds flat, which is the way of children and always has been.

"They were singing," I said, to no one, to everyone. "In the camp, before. When we came through, some of the women were singing to settle the little ones. Old songs. And when the lightning came down I heard it stop." I swallowed. "That's the sound I can't get out of my head. Not the thunder. The choir falling silent."

Wren had gone very still by the fire. Bram looked at the ground.

Issel lifted her head and looked at me with those eyes that had seen keepers die, and she said another word I knew. It meant *door*. But Wren had written in his margin notes—his obsessive, beautiful margin notes—that in the old tongue the same word meant *a wound that lets light through*.

A wound that lets light through.

I held that like a coal in my cupped hands for a long time.

* * *

They slept, eventually. Bram first, because his shoulder was poisoning itself with exhaustion and pain in equal measure; Wren last, sitting up with his charts spread on his knees, redrawing the moor by firelight, marking the camp's position with a small careful X, because that was how he mourned—he recorded. Somewhere in the small hours he looked over at me and said, not quite forgiving, not quite accusing:

"Sarel will have the story by dawn. She'll know it was you. She's been hunting a storm-caller for twelve years and you just signed your name in the sky."

"I know."

"There'll be no more hiding after this. No more valleys. Whatever's left of your quiet life is ash on that moor."

"I know that too."

He nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders eased a fraction, and I understood that what he had needed all night was not my guilt but my clarity. A cartographer hates nothing so much as a vague border. I had given him a firm line at last: here ends the woman who hid.

* * *

I stayed awake. The power hummed in me, and I studied it the way you study a scarred animal you've decided to keep—not trusting it, but refusing to pretend it isn't yours.

Here is what I had gotten wrong for twelve years, lying in my safe little valley among my safe little herbs: I had believed the choice was between using the storm and not using it. Between being Zephyrine Hartwell, herbalist, and being the Storm-Caller of the old capital, the woman in the propaganda posters with lightning for hair. Two women, two lives, a wall between.

But the moor had taught me otherwise. There was no wall. There was only judgment—the muscle I had never trained because training it felt too much like preparing for war. The inquisitors on the road: I had called the storm wide and wild because I had called it afraid. Fear makes floods. That was the whole lesson, and it had cost forty-one people, and I would not insult them by pretending I could have learned it cheaper.

So I sat in the dark with my humming bones and I made myself a vow, precise as a surveyor's line, because precision was the only penance I had to offer:

Not never again. Never again *blindly*. The storm was mine—my inheritance, my crime, my country's oldest language—and I would speak it, but only when speaking was worth the cost. Only when the alternative was worse than forty-one. Only when the choir was already falling, and my thunder was the only thing that could catch it mid-air.

I would spend it the way you spend blood: deliberately, knowing the price, never on a whim, never out of fear. And when it was spent, I would count the cost myself, aloud, and not look away, and not ask forgiveness from anyone who hadn't stood where I stood.

Bram had said: choose which dirt you carry. Very well. This was my dirt, measured and weighed.

* * *

Near dawn, Issel woke and crawled back to me without pretense of having slept elsewhere. She looked at my face for a long moment, reading it the way she read everything—with that unnerving, keeper-trained attention, as though I were a text in her old tongue.

Then she pointed east, toward Vessia, toward the Door, and said the word for *door*, and then she said the word for *storm*, and then she did something she had never done: she put the two words together. Made a sentence of them. Her grammar was strange and folded, but Wren's notes had prepared me, and I understood.

*The storm opens the door.*

Or perhaps—her syntax was ambiguous, and ambiguity in a dead language feels less like imprecision than like intention—perhaps she said: *the storm is the door.*

Either way, she looked at me with absolute certainty, this child sent out of a burning sanctuary to carry a key she didn't understand, and she took my hand—the storm hand—and placed it flat against the ground, against the earth of the drowned provinces, as if sealing a bargain between me and the country itself.

Far to the east, beyond the hills, thunder answered.

It might have been ordinary weather. It might have been the army's guns. But it rolled long and low and deliberate, in a rhythm I almost recognized, like a voice testing words in a language older than kingdoms—and Issel smiled, small and grave, and said one more word I didn't know yet.

"Hurry," Wren translated from his bedroll, without opening his eyes. "She says *hurry*. Apparently the sky agrees with her, and in my experience that's the most frightening endorsement available."

We broke camp before sunrise, four travelers and a vow, walking toward a city that wasn't there—and behind us, following at a distance no map could measure, the storm came on.
