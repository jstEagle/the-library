## 15. The City That Isn't There

I have spent my professional life drawing places that do not exist, so you would think I'd have developed an eye for them. A cartographer learns to read a landscape the way a forger reads a signature—the little hesitations, the telltale tremor where truth gave way to invention. And the fold of hills south of the Ashwater, where every imperial survey since the Usurpation shows nothing but scrub and goat pasture, showed me exactly what I expected: scrub and goat pasture.

Which is how I knew we had arrived.

"Stop," I said, and Zephyrine reined in beside me on the ridge. Below us the valley opened like a cupped hand, green and unremarkable, holding perhaps forty sheep, one crooked stone wall, and absolutely no city.

Bram squinted at it from under his bandaged arm. "That's it? That's the famous Vessia?"

"That's the *absence* of it," I said. "Look at the wall."

He looked. He was a soldier, not a surveyor, but he was a clever man pretending to be a dull one, which is its own kind of cartography. After a moment he said, slowly, "The wall's wrong."

"The wall's wrong," I agreed. "It follows no contour. It's not keeping sheep in or wolves out. It's a boundary marker, drawn by someone who wanted travelers to see a wall and stop asking questions." I pulled out my chart—the one that had cost me my commission, my name, and very nearly my neck—and laid it across my saddle. On it, in ink I had mixed myself because no imperial supply clerk could be trusted, the hills were not empty. They held streets. A harbor that wasn't a harbor, because the sea had retreated two miles in the last century and the city had followed it. A temple district with its spires drawn as broken lines, because I had never seen them whole and refused to invent them.

"You're certain this is real?" Zephyrine asked. She said it quietly, the way she said most things now, but her hands were tight on the reins. Since the moor—since the night the sky came apart under her hands and a refugee camp burned—she had been speaking like someone walking on a frozen lake, testing each word before she put weight on it.

"I drew it from a dead man's notes and a smuggler's drunken testimony," I said. "So no. But I've been wrong about it for eleven years, and I'm tired of being wrong in the right direction."

Issel, riding pillion behind Zephyrine, tugged her sleeve and said something in that old tongue—that language older than any crown on any coin I'd ever engraved. Then she pointed down at the empty valley and said it again, louder, with the particular exasperation of a child addressing slow adults.

"What did she say?" Bram asked.

Zephyrine's lips moved, translating. "She says—we're late for supper."

* * *

The trick, Wren Oduya's *Principles of Survey*, unpublished, unwritten, would have said, is that a hidden city does not hide. It simply declines to be found by people who have already decided what they're looking at. We rode down into the valley following the crooked wall, and the wall led us along a dry streambed, and the streambed turned, and turned again, and then the hills folded around us like a book closing, and there it was.

Vessia-on-the-Maps.

I confess I stopped my horse in the middle of the road and made a spectacle of myself. Eleven years I had carried that chart. Eleven years of ridicule in the Cartographic Office, of Sarel's predecessor suggesting—gently, bureaucratically—that Master Oduya's talents might be better employed counting bridge tolls in a province with no bridges. Eleven years of waking at night certain I had invented the whole thing, that grief for my grandfather's stories had curdled into delusion, that a man who maps a country that officially never existed is not a scholar but a mourner with a compass.

And here it was. Slate roofs going gold in the late light. Laundry strung between buildings whose foundations predated the Empire by a thousand years. A bell tower with no bell, hung instead with wind-chimes made of sword-blades, so that the whole city rang softly, ceaselessly, like a conversation held just below the level of hearing.

"Well," said Bram, after a long silence. "That's going to be hell to besiege."

"Bram," Zephyrine said.

"What? It's a compliment. Look at those approaches. Whoever built this wanted it defensible."

"Whoever built this," I said, "wanted it *invisible*. The defense is a side effect." Though he wasn't wrong, and I noted the sightlines despite myself. Old habits. You can take the man out of the Imperial Cartographic Office, but the Office stays in the man, lodged like shrapnel.

They saw us before we reached the first houses. Of course they did—a city of the erased survives by noticing everything. Figures appeared on rooftops, unhurried, carrying the particular casualness of people who are armed. An old woman stepped into the road ahead of us with a distaff in her hands and the posture of someone who could do considerably more damage with it than the distaff suggested.

She looked at our little procession: a storm-caller trying to be nobody, a deserter in a uniform he hadn't yet burned, a child, and me. She looked longest at Issel.

Then she spoke—not the trade-tongue, but the old language, the one that predates kingdoms—and Issel answered, and the two of them had a short conversation over our heads like adults discussing a delivery while the parcel sits on the counter wondering if it's going to be kept.

The old woman turned back to us. "You'll be wanting the Lamplighters' Rest," she said, in accented but fluent trade-tongue. "Supper's at dusk. The child eats free. The rest of you can settle accounts with the council, if the council decides you have accounts." Her eyes went to Bram's uniform. "He can burn that first."

"We've been saying," Zephyrine murmured.

* * *

Here is what I learned about Vessia in the first evening, sitting in a tavern called the Lamplighters' Rest where every lamp was lit whether needed or not, because lamplighting, the innkeeper told us, is a holy act in a city that spent three centuries being snuffed:

Everyone there was from somewhere that no longer existed.

Not destroyed—erased. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole point. Wars destroy countries; that's ordinary, that's history, that happens everywhere. But the Usurper's chancellery had done something more elegant. They had not conquered the old nations; they had *unwritten* them. Borders redrawn, names struck from registers, languages reclassified as dialects, dialects as vulgarities, vulgarities as evidence of sedition. Archives burned not because the paper was dangerous but because the memory was. A generation of children was growing up unable to say where their grandmothers had been born, because the place had been renamed twice and then deleted once.

In Vessia, the deleted had gone on deleting themselves right out of the Empire's reach and into this fold of hills. The innkeeper's family had farmed the Sorrel Marches, which appear on no map made in the last sixty years. The woman mending nets by the door—there was no water for nets within two days' walk, but she mended them anyway, because the nets were the last thing she owned from the drowned provinces—spoke a language her grandchildren were forbidden to learn on pain of the inquisitors' attention. The old man playing a three-stringed instrument in the corner sang songs about a river I had personally surveyed, except his river had a different name, an older name, and when I told him so he gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise and said, "You've *seen* it? You've stood on it? Tell me—is the ford at Kellin's Stone still there?"

"It is," I said. "The water's lower than your song says. But the stone's there."

He sat back down and played, and I swear the song sounded different afterward. Like it had been waiting eleven years for a witness.

I am not a sentimental man. Sentimentality is imprecision, and imprecision in my trade kills people—you draw a river in the wrong place and a column marches into a marsh. But I will admit that I sat in that tavern with my untouched wine and felt something I did not have a word for, and I resented it, because I am a man of words and it declined to be named.

It was the feeling of a map being correct.

* * *

Zephyrine was quiet through supper. Not her usual quiet—I'd learned her silences by then, catalogued them like contour lines. There was the silence of the valley, deliberate and maintained. There was the silence after the moor, which was guilt wearing stillness as a disguise. This was neither. This was a woman listening to a room full of strangers sing in a dead language and discovering that she knew the harmony.

"They're singing the blessing my grandmother used," she said finally, to no one, to the table. "Before meals. I thought she made it up. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "When they exiled me, they took everything. My name from the rolls, my house, my standing. And I told myself it didn't matter, that I'd left all that behind anyway, that magic was the only thing that defined me and I was better off without it. Twelve years I told myself that." She turned her cup in her hands. "But hearing it sung like it's just... normal. Like it's just what people say before bread. It's like finding out the part of yourself you buried was never yours alone."

"That's the entire civic religion of this town," I said. "You'll get used to it or it'll break your heart. Possibly both."

Issel had fallen asleep against Zephyrine's arm halfway through the meal, mid-bite almost, the way children do when they finally feel safe enough to stop guarding their own sleep. Zephyrine looked down at her with an expression I pretended not to study.

"She talked to the gatekeeper," Zephyrine said. "On the way in. In the old tongue. Fluently."

"I noticed."

"She told her we were coming. Or—" Zephyrine frowned. "Or the gatekeeper told *her* something. Issel went very still, and then she nodded, and she hasn't said anything about it since."

"Noted," I said, and wrote nothing down, because some things you keep in the head where they can't be confiscated.

Bram came back from the bar with four cups and a face like a man who has just been recognized and isn't sure yet whether it's the good kind. "The innkeeper knows me," he said. "Or knows the uniform. Third Cohort, southern deployment. His brother died at Marrowfen." He set the cups down harder than necessary. "He didn't spit at me, though. He said the council would want to talk to the man who crossed at Marrowfen, because apparently the story's already moving faster than we are."

"Stories always do," I said. "They travel light. No baggage train."

"And ours?" Bram asked. "Ours is arriving behind us, isn't it. Sarel."

I didn't answer, which was answer enough. At Marrowfen we had bought our crossing with blood and noise, and Sarel did not lose things. She collected them. Somewhere behind us she was assembling us out of witnesses the way I assembled a coastline out of soundings—one point, another point, a line drawn between them. I knew how her mind worked because it worked like mine. We were both people who made maps of the world. The difference was that mine were meant to help things survive and hers were meant to justify their deletion.

"Then we talk to the council tonight," Zephyrine said. "Tomorrow at the latest. Whatever they know about the Door—"

"Ah," said a voice from the doorway, "the Door."

The speaker was a small, neat woman with white hair cut short as a hedge and eyes that had done a great deal of arithmetic. She wore the grey robes of the city's elders without any of the self-importance such robes usually confer; if anything she wore them apologetically, like a coat borrowed for weather. Behind her stood two more elders, and behind *them*, filling the doorway of the Lamplighters' Rest, was half the tavern's worth of quiet attention.

"Forgive the intrusion," she said. "I am Elder Casseline. You are the storm-caller, the deserter, the child, and—" her gaze settled on me with uncomfortable precision, "—the man who drew us."

"I drew a guess," I said. "Eleven years ago. Badly, in places."

"On the contrary." She produced, from her sleeve, a folded paper, and unfolded it on our table with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments. It was my chart. My chart—the one I had carried out of the Cartographic Office in a lining of my coat, the one I had assumed was unique, the one that had gotten me disgraced. This copy was older than mine. The ink had faded to sepia. In the corner, in a hand I didn't recognize, was a date from before my birth.

"My grandfather's notes," I said stupidly. "The dead man. The surveyor who—"

"—who came here in the year the Empire struck Vessia from the rolls, and stayed nine months, and drew the city so that it could be found again by anyone who needed finding." Elder Casseline smoothed the paper flat. "We have waited a long time for his successor, Master Oduya. The council would like to speak with all of you. Tonight, if you're able. There are matters concerning the Door of Vessia, and concerning the army that is—" she paused, and for the first time the arithmetic in her eyes showed its working, "—eleven days away."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the wind-chimes of sword-blades rang on, soft and ceaseless, the whole city murmuring in its sleep.

"Eleven days," Bram repeated.

"And you," Casseline said to Zephyrine, gently, terribly, "have a storm in you that we very much need to discuss."

Zephyrine's hand moved—just slightly—to rest on the sleeping child's shoulder. I watched her do it, and I watched her look around our table: the deserter, the liar with the charts, the girl who was a key, and herself, the lock-breaker. None of us had chosen the others. All of us, I was beginning to suspect, had been drawn onto the same page by the same patient hand.

"We'll come," Zephyrine said.

Elder Casseline nodded, folded my grandfather's chart along its worn creases, and held it out to me. "Keep it," she said. "You'll want accurate maps where we're going. And Master Oduya—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed her face, "—welcome home. I believe that's the phrase. I'm told it means something, in a city of people who've forgotten where home was."

I took the chart. My hands were not entirely steady, and I decided, as a professional courtesy to myself, not to make note of it.

Behind us, the lamps of the Lamplighters' Rest burned on, all of them, every wick, in a city that had learned the hard way what darkness costs—and ahead, up the winding street toward the council hall, the erased were waiting to tell us what we had actually been carrying across the drowned provinces all this time.

I only wish I had enjoyed the welcome longer. Because even then, even in that first hour of belonging, some cartographer's instinct in the back of my skull was already plotting points: *eleven days, one army, one inquisitor who collects things.*

Some maps, I've learned, you finish knowing exactly what's coming.

Those are the worst ones to draw.
