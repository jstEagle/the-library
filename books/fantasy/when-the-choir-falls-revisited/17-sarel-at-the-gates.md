## 17. Sarel at the Gates

I have offered terms to forty-one cities, towns, and one remarkably stubborn monastery, and I have learned this: the words matter less than the pause before them. A herald who rushes his ultimatum is a man who fears the answer. So I stood on the ridge above Vessia-on-the-Maps with the morning fog burning off my shoulders, and I let the silence do its work first.

The city sat in its fold of hills like a coin hidden in a beggar's palm. From up here you could see why no imperial surveyor had ever found it—the valley swallowed it whole, and the hills beyond were arranged with the casual cruelty of terrain that resists being drawn. Wren Oduya had once mapped places like this, they said. Wren Oduya had once been very good at finding things that did not wish to be found. I had a file on him three fingers thick, most of it apologies from men who had underestimated him.

Below me, Vessia's walls were old but honest—stone laid by people who expected to be besieged eventually and had made their peace with it. Smoke rose from cookfires in tidy columns. Somewhere down there, a bell was ringing, and it was not an alarm bell. It was the kind of bell that calls people to argue.

Good. Let them argue. Argument is how cowards spend the time they might otherwise use for courage.

"Shall I read the proclamation, Inquisitor?" asked Lieutenant Hask, my shadow these six years, a man whose ambition was so well-hidden even he had lost track of it.

"No."

"It's rather long. The Chancellery worked on it for—"

"Hask." I looked at him. He was a decent enough officer, which is to say he believed everything he read, including his own reports. "When you offer a drowning man a rope, you don't recite poetry about the rope. You throw it."

He went pale in the way of men who suspect a metaphor contains a lesson aimed at them personally. I took the proclamation myself, unfolded it, and did not read it either. Instead I raised my voice—not thundered, never thundered; thunder is for people who need volume because they lack patience—and spoke in the plain tongue of the provinces, the one the usurper pretended not to speak because it reminded everyone whose country this had been.

"People of Vessia," I called. "You are harboring two fugitives. A child who speaks a dead language. A woman who once brought lightning down on the capital and killed four hundred people in the space of a hymn."

That last part was not strictly accurate. It had been closer to three hundred and eighty, and it had taken slightly longer than a hymn. But precision is a luxury of peacetime archives, and I keep two sets of numbers anyway: the true ones, which I memorize, and the useful ones, which I say aloud.

"Surrender them," I said, "and your city burns slowly. Refuse, and it burns quickly. You have until the third bell after dark."

It was, I reflected, almost generous. Most inquisitors would simply have burned it. But fire destroys records, and records are the only thing I have ever truly cared about. Every archive in the eastern provinces had gone into the flames on the Chancellery's orders, and every time I signed the order I felt something I refuse to call grief. Call it inventory loss. Call it the slow murder of the past. Call it what happens when a country decides it would rather not remember itself.

Vessia was full of remembering. That was its crime.

I turned back toward the siege lines, and behind me I heard the city's bell change its tune—from argument to something faster. Deliberation, ending.

* * *

My tent smelled of ink and wet canvas, as all my tents have smelled for eleven years. On the table lay the list.

Not the official list—the one bound in grey leather with the Chancellor's seal, the one organized alphabetically like a menu of enemies. My own list. Forty names in my own hand, each with annotations in a cipher of my own devising, because I trust no clerk alive and only two dead ones.

Zephyrine Hartwell. Near the top, where she had been since I was a junior interrogator with chapped hands and a supervisor who told me to stop asking inconvenient questions about the capital fire. *Storm-caller. Exiled. Presumed dormant.* I had crossed out *presumed dormant* twice and written it back in twice, because hope, in my profession, is a clerical error waiting to happen.

And beneath her name, added in fresher ink: *Issel. Age approx. seven. Speaker. Possibly last.*

Possibly last. I had burned the primer-houses myself. I had watched old women go into the pits still correcting my grammar. And somewhere in that city below me was a child who carried the whole language in her mouth like a bird carries a song it doesn't know the meaning of.

Hask came in with the evening dispatches and the particular expression of a man carrying news he'd rather not have.

"The council's deliberating," he said. "We have ears inside?"

"We have a scribe," I said. "Formerly of the Vessian archive. He repented of his treason when we explained the alternatives."

"You mean when you—"

"When we *explained*," I repeated, pleasantly, "the alternatives. He'll send word through the drainage culvert before midnight. Sit. Have some wine. It's terrible, but it's ours."

He sat. He drank. He was quiet for a while, which I appreciated, and then he wasn't, which I didn't.

"Inquisitor—may I ask something improper?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends entirely on how improper it is."

"Why the deadline? We could take the city in a day. The walls are old. We have trebuchets."

"Because," I said, "a city that surrenders its fugitives has told us something worth knowing: that it values itself over its principles. Then we know exactly how much pressure every other city can bear. But a city that refuses—" I turned my cup slowly on the table. "A city that refuses becomes a story. And stories, Hask, are the only weapons that survive their owners. The usurper understands force. What he has never understood—and what keeps me employed—is that you cannot burn a story out of people. You can only make them ashamed of it. And shame takes longer than fire."

He nodded as if this were wisdom. It was not wisdom. It was exhaustion wearing wisdom's coat. Eleven years of lists, and I could tell you the exact weight of every name on mine, and I could tell you that the usurper's empire was built on the theory that if you erase a thing thoroughly enough, it stops having happened. I knew better. I had erased things professionally. They always came back. They came back as children speaking dead languages at border crossings. They came back as cartographers drawing coastlines that officially did not exist. They came back, occasionally, as weather.

The culvert message came at midnight, as promised, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of everything a culvert smells of. I read it by lamplight while Hask pretended to sleep.

*The council votes at dawn,* the scribe had written. *They will negotiate. There is a faction arguing to give up the woman and the child—Elder Corvane leads it. He says two lives against ten thousand is arithmetic. Others oppose him but weakly. The soldier Kettle spoke for them and was shouted down. The cartographer Oduya said nothing at all, which those who know him say is more alarming than anything he might have said.*

I set the letter down and found, to my mild surprise, that I was angry.

Not at Corvane. Corvane was honest in his cowardice, which is rarer than courage and nearly as useful. Arithmetic is a respectable religion; I simply don't share the faith. No—what angered me was the shape of it. After eleven years of hunting speakers and burners-of-books, I had built my career on the belief that the erased would stand together, that memory would make them brave. And here was the great refuge of the forgotten, preparing to hand its children to the fire in exchange for a slower burn.

Institutions, I thought, are machines for converting courage into procedure. Give ten thousand people a choice between virtue and survival, and they will form a committee to discover that survival is also virtuous.

And yet.

Kettle had spoken for them. A deserter—I had his file too, twice-deserted, once from the old army and once from ours, which took a certain talent—had stood in front of a council and spent his voice on two strangers. And Oduya had said nothing, and silence from Wren Oduya was a loaded crossbow.

I woke Hask.

"Third bell after dark has come and gone," I said. "Tomorrow at noon, I walk to the gates alone under a parley banner. If they surrender the fugitives, we burn slowly. Prepare the slow way. If they refuse—"

"Yes, Inquisitor?"

I looked at my list. At Hartwell's name. At the small annotation I had added years ago and never crossed out: *Ask her why. Before the end. Ask her why the capital.*

Because I had read the transcripts of the capital fire, the real ones, the ones that didn't survive the purging. Four hundred dead—or near enough—and every account agreed on one detail: she had stopped. She had pulled the storm back mid-strike, when she could have finished, and the stopping had cost her everything. Exile. The valley. Twelve years of herbs and mended fences. People assumed the storm-caller was a monster who showed mercy once. I suspected the truth ran the other direction: a merciful person who had shown the storm once, and been destroyed by the showing.

I wanted to ask her which it was. I wanted it the way other people want absolution.

"If they refuse," I told Hask, "we burn quickly. And I want the child taken alive. Whatever else happens—whatever fires, whatever storms—the girl comes to me unharmed or someone answers for it. Is that understood?"

He blinked. "Even if she—"

"*Especially* if she does something remarkable," I said. "Remarkable is my favorite thing to interrogate."

He left to pass the orders, and I sat alone with the lamp and the list, and I did something I had never done in eleven years of service. I wrote a new annotation, small, in the margin beside Issel's name:

*If the council gives her up, the city has already burned. Everything after is just making it official.*

Then I blotted the ink, sealed my tent against the damp, and listened to the wind coming off the hills. It carried the smell of woodsmoke and rain that hadn't fallen yet, and underneath both, faint as a thread, something that might have been a bell and might have been a voice and might have been nothing at all.

At dawn, the council would choose arithmetic.

But I had read enough surrendered cities to know that councils are not the only voices in a wall. Somewhere in Vessia tonight, I would have wagered my list on it, a storm-caller was deciding that some sums are not hers to let others do.

I hoped so. Gods forgive me—in whatever dead pantheon the Chancellery hadn't gotten around to banning yet—I hoped so. A parley needs two parties willing to lie, and I had grown so tired of monologues.

Noon, then. The gates. The terms.

And if the terms failed—if the gate opened instead onto storm—well. I had always wondered what it would be like, standing in the path of the thing I'd spent my life cataloguing.

I suspected I was about to find out.
