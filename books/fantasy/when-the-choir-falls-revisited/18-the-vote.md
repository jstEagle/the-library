## 18. The Vote

I have stood in front of firing squads with more warmth in them than the Council of the Erased.

That's not fair, maybe. They weren't cruel. They were careful, which in a city that has survived by not existing is the same virtue wearing a different coat. But there is a particular kind of tired that comes from watching old people weigh a child's life on scales they polished themselves, and I was tired down to the marrow by the third hour.

The council chamber had been a granary once, before Vessia decided to stop being on maps. The rafters still smelled of rye. Forty elders sat in a horseshoe of mismatched chairs—there is no furniture in Vessia that matches anything, because everything was carried here by someone fleeing something—and at the center of the horseshoe, on a plain stool because no one would offer her a chair, sat Issel.

She'd been given a translator, an old woman named Maren who spoke the keepers' tongue the way I speak Kettle-ish: badly, from a grandmother, out of guilt. Between them the questions went back and forth like a slow game of tiles.

*Does the child consent to be used?*

*The child does not know the word for used.*

*Does the child understand the danger?*

*The child says danger is a door too.*

I watched Zephyrine's hands during all of it. She sat beside me with her fingers laced so tight her knuckles had gone the color of candle wax, and every time one of the elders said *the storm-caller* in that flat administrative way, her jaw did a small thing, a hinge testing its own rust.

Wren sat on her other side, sketching. Of course he was sketching. The man draws when he's frightened, the way some men drink. He'd filled three pages with the chamber's architecture before the vote even came up, and I knew without looking that somewhere in those pages was an escape route none of us had asked for yet.

Elder Corvane spoke for the negotiation faction. He was a broad, kind-faced man with ink-stained fingers and the unhurried voice of someone who had buried enough friends to stop hurrying for anyone. "Sarel's terms are terms," he said. "We surrender the woman and the child. The city stands. We have four hundred souls behind these walls and two of them are being asked to carry a weight that belongs to all of us."

"Four hundred and six," Maren corrected quietly. "The boats from Marrowfen came in overnight."

"Four hundred and six," Corvane agreed, "and two of them—"

"Two of them," Zephyrine said, "are sitting right here and haven't been asked."

The chamber went quiet in the way rooms do when someone says the true thing too loudly. An elder near the back coughed. Somewhere above us a pigeon rearranged itself in the rafters, unbothered, which is more than I could say for anyone with a pulse.

Corvane turned to her with genuine gentleness. "You were asked, storm-caller. You were asked twelve years ago, when you brought lightning down on the capital, and your answer burned half a city."

I felt her flinch. Everyone felt her flinch; she didn't bother hiding it, which is either courage or exhaustion and by then I'd stopped trying to tell the difference in her.

"That wasn't my answer," she said. "That was their question."

"The distinction will comfort the dead, I'm sure."

"It comforts *me*," she said, "and I'm the one who has to live with them."

And that was the whole argument, really, laid out between them like a dropped knife. Corvane wanted the city to live, and he was right to want it, and his arithmetic was sound: two lives against four hundred and six, and one of the two already owed a debt of fire. I've done that arithmetic myself. I did it the day I deserted the second time, standing over Sergeant Hale's orders for the valley sweep, weighing my oath against a village full of strangers and finding, to my lasting surprise, that the strangers outweighed it. Arithmetic is easy. It's the carrying that kills you.

But Zephyrine wasn't wrong either, and neither was Issel, who chose that moment to say something in her old tongue—short, clear, aimed at the whole horseshoe—and waited for Maren to catch up.

Maren's face did something complicated. "She says—" the old woman stopped, started again. "She says the Door does not open for negotiations. She says the keepers taught her that the Door opens for blood and storm or it does not open at all, and that if it stays shut, everyone in this room ends up in Sarel's list anyway. Just later. And colder."

"She's seven," said an elder near the front, as if that settled something.

"She's seven," Zephyrine agreed, "and she's the only person in this room who actually knows how the thing works. Maybe we could try listening to her instead of counting her."

They voted an hour after that. Paper slips, folded, passed hand to hand down the horseshoe while the pigeon overhead conducted its own private census. I didn't get a slip. Neither did Wren, nor Zephyrine, nor Issel. We were the subject matter, and subjects don't vote, which is a rule as old as councils and exactly as wise.

Thirty-one to eleven, with four abstentions. Negotiation. Terms to be offered at dawn: the storm-caller and the child, delivered to the usurper's lines, in exchange for the city's life.

Zephyrine stood up before the tally was even read aloud. I'll give the elders this—they let her. Maybe they were ashamed. Maybe they just wanted her gone before the shame set in properly.

"I'll go," she said. "Not because you voted it. Because Sarel wants me, and if I'm walking toward her anyway, I'd rather choose the road." Her eyes found Issel, and something passed between them that I have no military vocabulary for. "But the child doesn't go. That's not a term. That's a fact."

"Then the terms fail," Corvane said heavily.

"Then the terms fail," she agreed, "and we're all still here at dawn, and the army is still outside, and somebody in this room should start thinking about doors instead of ledgers."

She walked out. Issel slid off the stool, took one look at the horseshoe of elders—the way you'd look at a fence that had failed to hold—and trotted after her, small boots slapping stone. The pigeon finally left. Even the bird had better sense than the council.

* * *

I found them in the shrine yard behind the granary, where the exiles of Vessia keep their names. It's a strange place, that yard—not graves, exactly, though some of the stones mark actual dead. Mostly it's names carved into slate and river rock: the towns that aren't on maps anymore, the tongues nobody speaks, the people erased twice, once by fire and once by decree. Hundreds of stones. A whole country written small enough to fit in a courtyard.

Zephyrine stood among them reading, the way you'd read a wall of the condemned. Issel had found a low stone and was tracing letters with one finger, mouthing sounds.

"You know they're going to hand you over anyway," I said. "Come dawn, when the terms fail, some of those eleven votes are going to reconsider. Fear makes flexible arithmetic."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

I leaned against a marker for a drowned town called Sel-Harrow, because if I was going to have this conversation I wanted something solid under my shoulder. "Then hear my offer. I spent nine years in the usurper's army. I know how siege camps smell, how they post their watches, where the latrines go and therefore where the sentries *don't* look. There are forty-one trebuchets in that host. I built two of them. I can unbuild them."

She turned. In the grey light coming off the slate stones her face looked older than twelve years of hiding had made it, older than the exile, almost as old as the country carved around us. "Bram—"

"Don't argue yet. Let me finish, because I've rehearsed this and if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve and I hate losing my nerve, it's undignified." I took a breath. "You're going to need a diversion. Nobody walks through forty thousand men and an inquisitor by being quiet. Somebody has to make noise on the far side of the camp, big noise, burning noise, and then stay alive afterward, which is the hard part. I'm volunteering for the noise."

"Bram." Gently now, which was worse. "If they take the trebuchets apart and find sabotage—"

"They won't find sabotage. They'll find bad rope and wet bowstrings and a night's work by men who are cold and careless, because that's what armies believe about themselves. Trust me. I was careless professionally for nine years."

Issel looked up from her stone. "He should come with us," she said—in trade-tongue now, her accent thick but her meaning clean as a bell. Maren had been teaching her for days; children soak up languages like spilled wine soaks through a tablecloth. "All of us should go together."

"We can't all go, little one." I crouched to her level. "Someone has to hold the door of the city, metaphorically speaking, while you're holding yours literally. That's me. I'm good at holding things. It's mostly what soldiers do."

She considered this with the terrifying seriousness of a seven-year-old auditing an adult's logic. Then she reached out and patted my arm twice, the way you'd pat a horse you'd decided to trust, and went back to her letters. I have been decorated once, falsely, and commended twice, also falsely, and neither ceremony touched me like those two pats.

Zephyrine hadn't moved. "You deserted twice," she said. Not cruelly. Just checking the math.

"I did. First time I ran from a war I believed in until I saw it close up. Second time I ran from an order to burn a valley full of innocent people." I shrugged. "Third time I'm not running anywhere. Third time I'm standing still and making trouble. It's a promotion."

Something loosened in her shoulders—a fraction, a hair's width, but I've learned to read soldiers' bodies the way Wren reads coastlines. "All right," she said. "The noise is yours."

* * *

Wren found us at dusk, and he came in fast, which for Wren meant walking quickly while pretending to stroll and examine the masonry.

"I've been thinking," he announced.

"God help us all," I said.

"No, listen." He spread his current sketchbook open against the shrine wall, and there it was—not the granary's rafters this time but the hills east of Vessia, drawn in his quick sure strokes, with the imperial siege lines rendered in the neat little symbols the Survey Corps used for fortifications. Except his version had gaps in it. Careful gaps. Gaps with pencil ghosts underneath, where he'd erased things and redrawn them slightly wrong. "This morning I gave the war council my map of the siege works. This evening I'm telling you the map I gave them is wrong."

Zephyrine stared at him. "You falsified a map for the council?"

"I falsified a map *for the council*, yes, and I'd do it again, because the council voted to hand a child to Sarel and I have decided I'm finished doing accurate work for cowardice." He tapped the eastern gap. "The real weak point is here, in the ravine below the old quarry road. Sarel's pickets thin out there because the ground floods at night—she thinks it's impassable, and she's almost right. Almost is my entire profession." He looked up, and for once the man wasn't performing his own cleverness. He looked scared and certain at the same time, which I recognized, because it's the exact face I wore deserting the second time. "I'm coming with you."

"Wren," Zephyrine said carefully, "you called me a weapon that walks. You said that to my face, the night we met the refugees on the levee road. You said I was the usurper's best recruiting poster."

"I did." He didn't flinch from it, I'll grant him that. "And then I watched you stand in the rain over that burned camp and try to count the dead, and I watched you not run, and I've been revising my cartography ever since. Maps get corrected. That's rather the point of them." He closed the sketchbook. "Besides, you'll need me. Bram knows the camps, yes, but I know the *ground*. Every fold, every flooded stretch, every goat track the empire never bothered recording because the empire only records what it owns. I've spent my career mapping nowhere. Tonight nowhere is going to save us."

I looked at Zephyrine. She looked at the sky, doing whatever calculation storm-callers do when they're deciding whether to trust the weather or the company. Issel, sensing a decision in the air the way dogs sense weather, abandoned her letters and came to stand pressed against Zephyrine's side, watching all three of us with those old, old eyes.

"One condition," Zephyrine said finally. "Nobody comes because they think they owe me. Not for the moor, not for the camp, not for any of it. If you walk into those siege lines, you walk in because you choose it. Freely. I've had enough of people being spent like coin—including by me."

Wren smiled, small and crooked. "Freely. Yes. Although I'll note for the record that freely choosing to do something insane is still insane."

"Noted," I said. "For the record, I've never done anything else."

* * *

We met once more, after full dark, in the shrine yard among the names, because it was the only place in Vessia where no one would think to look for a war council. Four hundred and six people behind walls, and the four of us crouched over Wren's false-true map by the light of a shuttered lantern, planning a hole through an army.

It went like this: Bram burns the siege train on the western slope, an hour past midnight, when the watch changes and the sentries are at their coldest and stupidest. The fires pull the reserve companies west. In the confusion, Wren takes Zephyrine and Issel down through the quarry ravine along the flood line, moving between the picket fires, using the dark and the water and the empire's own blindness to its unmapped ground. They reach the ruined keepers' sanctuary below the eastern crags before dawn. Blood and storm. Door.

"And if the fires don't pull the reserves?" Zephyrine asked.

"Then the reserves come east instead," I said, "and you'll hear them coming, and you'll move faster. I've been in armies my whole adult life, Zeph. Armies are enormous and slow and convinced of their own competence. That's not nothing. That's practically a strategy."

"And if Sarel guesses?"

I didn't have a good answer for that, so I told the truth, which is a habit I'm trying to build late in life. "Then she guesses, and we improvise, and some of us probably die. But we'll die having chosen it. That's worth something. I've seen the other way to die—on orders, for nothing, surprised by it. This is better."

Issel tugged my sleeve. When I bent down she put her mouth close to my ear, as if the stones with their carved names might repeat what she said, and maybe they would; I wouldn't put it past this country.

"When the fires start," she whispered, "I will know it is you. I will be listening."

"You'll be underground, little one, getting through a door."

"I will be listening," she repeated, with the absolute calm of a child stating physics, and I found I couldn't argue. Some part of me was already listening too—already imagining her hearing it, miles off beneath the hills, the crackle and bloom of my fires arriving like a message. Like a letter signed in flame: *we held our end. Hold yours.*

We broke apart before moonrise. Wren went to steal rope and oilcloth. Zephyrine took Issel back toward the sanctuary stairs, and at the edge of the yard the girl turned and waved—not at me, at the whole yard, at the stones, at the whole erased country—as if promising to remember it all.

I stayed a moment longer alone, because I'm a sentimental fool and I've earned it. Somewhere out in the dark, forty-one trebuchets stood in the rain waiting for morning, and I thought about the two I'd helped build, and how strange and fine it was that the last work of my soldiering life would be taking things apart.

Then I went to find bad rope, wet bowstrings, and a very long night.

The fires would start in five hours. Everything after that, the next chapter writes itself—or Sarel does.
