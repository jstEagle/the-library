## 1. The Valley That Swallows Thunder

The bees did not care that Marguerite Oyelaran had once been able to split the sky in half with a word.

This was, he had come to understand over seven long years, the single greatest virtue of bees. They cared about smoke and sugar water and whether he moved slowly enough to seem like a tree. They did not care about the covenant, or the order of storm-callers, or the price on his head, because the price on his head was a matter for the world beyond the ridge, and the world beyond the ridge could not reach into the valley where magic came to die.

He lifted the first hive box of the morning and felt the hum of it against his palms — a small, busy thunder, the only kind permitted to him now.

"Good morning, your majesties," he told them. He talked to the bees the way some men talk to saints: constantly, hopefully, and without much expectation of an answer. "Yes, I know. I'm late. The bread burned. We don't need to discuss the bread."

The valley swallowed his voice the way it swallowed everything else. Sound went in and came out smaller. He'd tested it once, years ago, alone at the far end of the barley field, when homesickness had gotten its teeth into him. He had stood in the rain and whispered one syllable of the old calling-words, just one, barely more than breath.

The cloud above him had flinched. Then it had forgotten how to be a cloud, and simply dripped itself apart, and Marguerite had spent the rest of the day shaking and grateful and sick with relief all at once. The valley ate magic. It had eaten his, bite by bite, until what remained was a man who kept bees and burned bread and was, as far as anyone knew, nobody at all.

Nobody at all. He had hated it for the first year. He had needed it by the third. By the seventh he no longer knew which it was, and had stopped asking.

* * *

The village of Hollowmere had taken him in the way valleys take in weather — without ceremony and without questions. Old Marta had pointed at the empty cottage past the orchard and said, "You'll want wood before winter," which was the whole of his welcome, and the whole of his interrogation. The villagers were mute in the way of people who had decided, generations ago, that words invited trouble. They signed to each other in small economical gestures. They left bread on doorsteps. They did not ask where he came from, and he never told them, and this unspoken treaty was the warmest thing in his life.

He repaid them in honey and in fixing things. He had repaired Marta's roof twice. He had taught Tam, the miller's grandson, to whistle — badly, but with tremendous commitment. On festival nights he sat at the edge of the firelight and let the fiddles wash over him and pretended, successfully, that his chest didn't ache at the shape of songs he could not safely sing along to.

"You're brooding," signed Marta one evening, catching him at it. She was seventy and had hands like tree roots and no patience whatsoever.

"I'm resting my face."

"Your face is resting *wrong.*" She dropped onto the bench beside him and pressed a cup of cider into his hands. "Seven years, bee-man. You going to tell me your name means anything, someday?"

"It means I make good honey."

"Mm." She sipped her own cider and looked at the fire. "Whatever you buried, it's buried deep enough. That's all I ever needed to know." A pause. "But you keep looking at the ridge like it owes you money."

Marguerite laughed — really laughed, which surprised them both — and did not tell her that she was right. The ridge owed him nothing. It was the world beyond it that kept accounts.

* * *

The courier came in the last week of harvest, on a night so still the smoke from chimneys rose in straight gray lines like plumb weights.

Marguerite heard the horse before he saw it — or rather heard the absence of hoofbeats, the wrongness of a stumbling animal trying and failing to be quiet. He opened his door with the lamp already lit and found a woman half-falling out of her saddle, and then fully falling, and caught her mostly by accident.

She was young. Or she had been young recently; whatever rode her now had aged her by decades in a day. Her riding coat was dark with blood that had gone stiff and black at the hem, and her eyes found his face with the desperate precision of someone checking an address against a description.

"You're him," she rasped. "You're — the beard threw me. You're *him.*"

"I'm the beekeeper," Marguerite said, which was true, and also the only identity he had left. "You're bleeding. Come inside before you fall down again, my floor is—"

She was already pressing something into his hands. A leather tube, cracked and stained. Inside it, a map — he knew maps, had loved maps once, in another life — drawn in fine confident ink and blotted here and there with rust-brown fingerprints. Blood. Fresh enough to still smell faintly of iron.

"The covenant," she said. Each word cost her visibly, like coins pried from a frozen hand. "Veyra's hunters took the others. One by one. Singers, warders, the archivists at Drenn — all of them. You're the last voice of the covenant, do you understand? The *last.* If they take you too, it's finished. Everything the old order swore to hold — finished, and her claim becomes true forever."

"I think you've mistaken me for someone," Marguerite began, and the lie died in his throat, because she was looking at him the way people look at a lighthouse. Like he was the only thing between her and rocks.

"Hollowmere," she managed. "They can't find you here. Magic dies here. But the wards on the covenant-stone are failing, and when they fail—" She coughed, and the cough was wet and terrible. "Solmere. Take it to Solmere. There's a cartographer. Sefa. Tell her the map is hers. Tell her I'm sorry I stole it. Tell her—"

Her hand slackened. Marguerite lowered her to the floor with the strange gentleness of a man handling something already broken, and she looked up at him and said, very clearly, "Don't let her win quietly," and then she did not say anything again, ever.

He sat with her a long while. Outside, the bees slept in their boxes, dreaming their hexagonal dreams, and the valley held its silence around the cottage like a cupped hand.

When he finally unrolled the map by lamplight, his hands were steady, which surprised him. The ink showed the whole breadth of the world beyond the ridge — roads and rivers, the free city of Solmere marked with a careful star, and running through everything, in a different hand, older, lines he recognized with a jolt like a hook behind his ribs. Ley-lines. Song-paths. The routes of the covenant, drawn by someone who had stood where storms were born.

Drawn, he realized slowly, in a hand he knew. His mentor's hand. Master Ellery Vane, who had taught him his first calling-word, who had been dead — or so the order had claimed — for eight years.

"Well," Marguerite said aloud, to the courier's body, to the map, to the lamp. "That's inconvenient."

* * *

They came three days later.

He knew it first from the bees. They had gone quiet — not the settled hum of evening but a held-breath silence, and when he walked the orchard row he saw them clustered at the hive entrances, facing west, toward the ridge pass. Bees always know. He had read that somewhere, in another life, in a book about omens, and had laughed at it.

Then the dogs started, all across Hollowmere at once, and stopped just as suddenly, and the silence after was worse than any sound.

Marta appeared at his gate without her usual scowl, her signing sharp and fast: *Riders. Four. Armed. Asking after a stranger.*

*What did you tell them?*

*That we have no strangers. Only neighbors.* Her eyes flicked to his cottage, where the courier lay under fresh-turned earth behind the beehives, beneath a marker stone that said only HERE LIES A BRAVE WOMAN. *Neighbors don't bleed on the road, bee-man. They'll search anyway. You have until they finish their supper.*

Marguerite looked back at his cottage — the crooked door, the herb bundles, the seven years of small safe days stacked up like honeycomb. He had hated this invisibility once. He understood, standing there with hunters' horses stamping at the village well, that he had come to depend on it the way a drowning man depends on driftwood, and that the courier had taken the driftwood away with two bloody hands and four dying words.

*Last voice of the covenant.*

His voice. In this valley, a whisper. Beyond the ridge, a weapon and a target both.

He packed in ten minutes: the map, bread, the knife Marta had given him his first winter, and one jar of honey, because a man should bring something sweet into a bitter world. At the edge of the orchard he paused, laid his palm flat against the warm wood of the nearest hive, and felt the small thunder answer through the grain.

"Keep the valley," he told them. "I have somewhere to be loud."

Behind him, at the well, a hunter's voice rang out, cold and carrying: "Search every house. The storm-caller is here — we will have his tongue by morning."

Marguerite pulled his hood up and walked, unhurried, into the barley field, toward the ridge, toward the world that wanted him dead — and felt, beneath the terror, something he had almost forgotten the name for.

It felt terribly like hope.
