## 2. Road of Borrowed Names

The valley let him go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a school door—reluctantly, with a long look after, as if to say *you'll be back, and I won't say I told you so.*

Marguerite climbed the eastern ridge in the grey hour before dawn, the courier's map folded against his chest where it had spent three sleepless nights learning the shape of his heartbeat. Behind him, the village slept on without him. The bees would be confused by morning. He had left the hives extra honeycomb and an apology, which was more than he'd ever managed for people.

At the ridgeline he stopped and looked back once. The valley lay below like a cupped palm, green and quiet and utterly empty of magic. For seven years it had been his tomb and then, without his noticing, his home. He had hated its silence and then needed it and then—this was the shameful part, the part he buried deepest—loved it.

*You'll be back,* said the valley, or perhaps just his own cowardice wearing the valley's voice.

"Probably," Marguerite admitted, and crossed over.

The change hit him at the boundary stone like a wave hitting a swimmer who had forgotten what water was.

It began as warmth in his chest, which was pleasant, and then became pressure, which was not, and then became the sensation of a song that had been locked in a small dark room for seven years suddenly throwing open every window at once. Magic poured into him—not gently, not like rain, but like a flood finding a dry riverbed, scouring, insistent, furious at having been kept waiting.

He went down on his knees in the wet grass. His hands sparked blue-white against the soil. Somewhere overhead, clouds that had been minding their own business all morning began, unbidden, to gather.

"No," he gasped, pressing his palms flat to the earth. "No, no—we're not doing this. We are being *subtle.*"

The clouds ignored him. They were, in fairness, only doing what they'd been made to do, and what he had spent seven years teaching them not to. The first thunder rolled across the hills like a dropped barrel, and Marguerite pressed his forehead to the grass and breathed through his teeth until, slowly, grudgingly, the sky remembered its manners and dispersed.

He stayed kneeling a long while afterward, shaking. Seven years of stillness, undone in ten minutes. His magic hadn't died in the valley after all. It had only been waiting, coiled and patient, growing fat on absence.

"Well," he said aloud, to nobody. "That's going to be a problem."

* * *

Sefa had learned cartography from her mother, who had learned it from hers, and in the Oyelaran family tradition—or rather, the family she'd married into, names being complicated—the first lesson was always the same: *a map is a promise you make to strangers.*

She thought about that promise now, crouched in the bracken above the border road with her stolen—reclaimed, *reclaimed*, she corrected herself for the hundredth time—spyglass trained on the patrol below. Six riders in Veyra's slate-and-silver livery, moving at a walk, the way hunters move when they already know roughly where their quarry is.

They were following someone. Not her. She'd been careful, sleeping days, walking nights, keeping off every road her own beautiful map described. She knew this country better than its own rivers did, and she had drawn those rivers.

No. They were following the storm-caller. The one whose thunder had cracked across the borderlands two nights past like a shout in a library.

"Fool," she murmured, without much heat. Exiles were always fools. That was practically the job description. She adjusted the spyglass and watched the riders close on a figure limping along the drainage ditch—a boy, by the look of him, small and quick, with hair the color of frost and ears that came to delicate points no hood quite concealed.

Half-fae. Running from something, toward something, and about to be caught by neither, because Sefa had already marked the ford downstream where the ditch met the river, and because six years ago Veyra's people had burned her life's work and called it justice, and because Sefa Marchetti-Oyelaran—no, just Sefa, she'd cut the rest loose—had promised herself one thing on the night she fled with nothing but her mother's compass: *never again watch someone be taken while you hide.*

She sighed, packed the spyglass, and started down through the trees.

"A map is a promise," she muttered. "Nobody ever says what happens when you keep it."

* * *

Marguerite heard the horses before he saw them, and froze in the ditch like a man playing the world's worst game of statues.

They were hunting him. Of course they were. The courier's bloodstained map had named him *the last voice of the covenant*, and Veyra's hunters had arrived at the valley within days, and now here he was, three days out, loud as a struck bell in a world where everyone else was glass. Every mile beyond the border made him easier to find. His magic leaked. It *sang.* He could feel it humming under his skin like a second pulse, broadcasting his location to anyone with the sense to listen.

And then, from the reeds ahead of him, a boy burst out running—pointed ears, frost-pale hair, eyes wide with the particular terror of someone who has been chased before and knows exactly how it ends.

The riders wheeled. One shouted. A crossbow came up.

Marguerite did not think. Thinking was for people whose bodies didn't remember storms the way other men's remembered dancing. He stood up out of the ditch, flung both arms wide, and *pulled.*

The sky answered. It always answered; that had never been the problem. The problem was that it answered *enthusiastically.* Lightning split the road thirty feet from the nearest rider—not aimed, exactly, but *invited*, the way you invite a large dog to stop jumping on guests by shouting very firmly—and thunder detonated overhead with enough force to send all six horses screaming in different directions. Two riders hit the mud. The crossbow bolt went somewhere irrelevant.

"Run," Marguerite croaked at the boy, whose jaw had dropped to approximately his knees.

The boy ran. Marguerite ran after him, which felt structurally backwards but was nonetheless the plan, such as it was. Behind them the riders regrouped with the unhurried competence of professionals, and Marguerite's legs informed him, with mounting urgency, that seven years of beekeeping had not prepared him for this.

The boy ducked into the treeline. Marguerite followed. A root caught his ankle with the cheerful malice of roots everywhere, and he went down hard, and the crashing pursuit behind him grew closer, and he thought—with genuine, absurd clarity—*the bees will never know what happened to me*—

A hand seized his collar and hauled him sideways into a hollow beneath a fallen tree, and a voice said, "Breathe. Quietly. You're leaking."

He was. He could feel it, his magic fizzing off him like a wet log on a fire, painting a great glowing *HERE I AM* across the forest. The hand belonged to a broad-shouldered woman with ink-stained fingers and the expression of someone recalculating whether saving him had been worth it.

"You," she said, "are the loudest man in three kingdoms."

"I'm sorry?"

"Don't apologize. Plug yourself in." She pulled a strip of cold iron from her pack—it looked like a broken hinge—and pressed it into his hands. The effect was immediate and miserable, like plunging his magic into icy water. The fizzing stopped. The hum dimmed to a whisper. He nearly wept with relief.

"How did you—" he began.

"Hold it and don't talk." She peered out through the branches. The riders passed within twenty feet, paused, conferred, and moved on downhill. When the last hoofbeat faded, she turned back and studied him with eyes the color of strong tea.

"You're the storm-caller," she said. "The one from the valley."

"And you're—" He looked at her ink-stained fingers, at the brass compass hanging at her throat, at the rolled leather tube strapped across her back with the unmistakable care of a person carrying their own spine. Something clicked. He drew out the courier's map, and watched her face as she saw it.

Sefa went very still. Then she took the map with both hands, the way you take a child, or a wound.

"This is mine," she said quietly. "I drew this. Line for line. Before they burned it. Before they burned—" She stopped. Breathed. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying courier. He said—" Marguerite hesitated. "He said I was the last voice of the covenant. And that Veyra was killing the heirs. All of them. One by one."

The boy in the hollow made a small sound. They both turned. He had wedged himself into the far corner, knees up, watching them with the wary arithmetic of someone counting exits.

"They came for my mother in spring," the boy said. "The fae side of the family. Veyra's writ says half-bloods are 'covenant-tainted.'" His chin lifted, defiant and terrified at once. "I'm Ilex. I've been running since the thaw."

Silence, in the hollow under the dead tree. Three strangers, each holding a broken name, each looking at the others as if hoping to find a reason to run separately and failing to find one.

"She's not just killing heirs," Sefa said at last, tapping the map. "Look. Here, here, and here—every site marked on this chart, every place the old covenant anchored itself. She's erasing the *anchors*. If she gets them all before anyone reforges the stone..." She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Marguerite felt the missing end of the sentence settle over all three of them like weather.

"So we go faster than she does," Marguerite said.

"We?" said Sefa and Ilex, in perfect, horrified unison.

"I saved your life," Marguerite pointed out at Ilex.

"He saved mine," Ilex told Sefa, aggrieved, as if betrayed by the facts.

Sefa looked at the map in her hands—her map, her stolen life, returned by a fool of an exile who apologized to bees—and closed her eyes briefly, as if praying for patience and knowing it wouldn't come.

"Fine," she said. "But I navigate. And if you make the sky do that again anywhere near me, storm-caller, I'm feeding you to the next patrol myself."

Above the hollow, far to the south, clouds were gathering over the free city of Solmere—clouds that had no business gathering there, in a season of clear skies, in numbers that spoke of armies and ambition. None of them saw it yet.

But the sky, patient as ever, was already beginning to sing.
