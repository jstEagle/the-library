## 3. What the Usurper Cannot Sing

They took him at the ford of the Ashen River, and Marguerite could not even claim they'd been clever about it.

He had felt the net for three days—a wrongness in the air, a hush where birds should have been—and still he'd walked into it, because the map said the crossing was here and Sefa swore by her own ink. The hunters dropped from the pines like black fruit. Ilex screamed something in a language that made the river flinch. Sefa got one hand on his sleeve before a soldier's gauntlet closed over both of them.

And then, absurdly, a man in a silver half-mask had bowed.

"Marguerite Oyelaran," he said, as if announcing a guest at a wedding. "Her Radiance requests the pleasure."

"Tell Her Radiance," Marguerite managed, "I'm washing my hair that decade."

The blow to his ribs was almost polite. Almost.

* * *

Veyra's camp was not what he expected, which was itself unsettling. He had imagined squalor—usurpers were supposed to live in squalor, all grease and grievance. Instead there were clean white tents pitched in perfect rows, cookfires that smelled of rosemary, and soldiers who looked *fed*. A boy no older than twelve offered him water from a clay jug with the solemnity of a priest.

Marguerite drank because refusing seemed crueler than captivity.

They brought him to the largest tent at dusk. Inside: maps, a harp with two broken strings, and a woman standing with her back to him, watching the light die through a gap in the canvas.

"You hum when you're frightened," Veyra said, without turning. "Did you know? The hunters reported it. A little tune, under the breath, all the way across the Ashen." She turned then, and her face was ordinary—tired eyes, a scar through one eyebrow, the kind of face you'd trust with your keys. "Your order called it 'uncontrolled resonance.' They beat a boy in my class for humming once. His name was Tam. He cried so beautifully."

"I don't know what you want me to say."

"Nothing yet. Sit. Eat. I find truth comes easier with bread." She pushed a plate toward him—honeyed figs, real ones—and sat opposite like an old friend at a table too large for two. "Do you know why the valley swallows thunder, Marguerite?"

"The covenant-stone was shattered. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the Choir *said*." Her laugh was dry as old paper. "I was there when it broke. Not the stone—the covenant. Twenty-two years ago. I stood in the circle at Solmere with rain in my teeth, and I watched Archcantor Ilvane Oyelaran stand up before the assembled voices and declare the old pact *corrupted*, *dangerous*, *in need of purification*."

The name landed in Marguerite's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"My mentor," he said. "Ilvane raised me. Taught me to call my first cloud. She used to—" He stopped. His hands had gone cold around the fig.

"She used to sing you lullabies about the wicked outside world. Yes. She was very good at lullabies." Veyra leaned forward. "She didn't purify anything. She *hoarded*. Your order sealed the covenant's power behind seven locks only the Choir could open, and when the other orders objected, she had their storm-voices cut out. All of them. Do you understand what that means?" Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "It means I stood in that circle and opened my mouth and nothing came out. Nothing. For twenty years, nothing."

The tent was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the boy with the water jug laughed at something.

"They called me silenced for treason," Veyra went on, softer now. "But treason is just grief with an army, isn't it? I looked for the heirs of the covenant, Marguerite. One by one. Some joined me. Some died stupidly brave. And you—" she gestured at him, at his beekeeper's calluses, his exile's clothes, "—you hid in a hole where magic goes to die and kept bees. I don't blame you. God help me, I envy you. But the map you're carrying leads to the heart-stone of the old covenant, and I am *asking*, not commanding, for once in my miserable life. Help me finish what Ilvane started—no. Unmake it. Reforge it *right*. With me. Two voices instead of one."

Marguerite stared at the figs. At her scarred eyebrow. At twenty years of nothing, sitting across from him with her hands folded like a penitent.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. Every lonely winter in the valley, every market day where he bit his tongue until it bled rather than let a syllable of power slip—here was a woman offering to make it mean something. To make the exile a prologue instead of a whole story.

"Ilvi—" His voice caught on the old nickname. "She sang to me. When the storms came. She held my hands over my ears and sang so I wouldn't hear the thunder calling back."

"Yes," Veyra said gently. "That's exactly the kind of thing she did."

* * *

He did not sleep. In the grey hour before dawn, a guard walked him—unguarded, which was its own message—to the edge of the camp to relieve himself, and that was where he saw the children.

They came up the road in a line, roped at the wrists, maybe forty of them, herded by six soldiers. Fleeing Solmere's fall, someone said. Conscripts. Veyra's quartermasters needed hands, and hands were hands. The smallest one stumbled, and when a soldier yanked the rope, the child made a sound Marguerite knew intimately—the sound of someone learning, early, that the world was a place you survived rather than lived in.

One of the children turned as the line passed. A girl, perhaps nine, with mud to her knees and a look in her eyes that was not fear but *accounting*—she was counting the soldiers, noting the gaps in their armor, filing it away for later. Marguerite knew that look. He had worn it for fifteen years in a valley full of kind, mute strangers.

Veyra wanted to reforge the covenant *with* him. And her army roped children at the wrists and marched them toward a war.

He thought of Ilvane's lullabies. He thought of Tam, whoever Tam had been, crying beautifully. He thought: *grief with an army.* And grief with an army will always, eventually, rope the children.

His hands were shaking when he found Sefa and Ilex in the holding tent—untouched, unbound, because Veyra wanted them comfortable. Sefa took one look at his face.

"No," she said. "Whatever you've decided, I can see it's terrible. Tell me anyway."

"There's a gap in the east pickets where the horse lines are. The girl conscripts—they'll be moved at dawn. If we go now, we can take the children with us."

"That's not escaping," Ilex whispered, ears flat with terror and something brighter underneath. "That's a *rescue*."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "I'm told the difference matters."

Sefa was already pulling pins from the tent seam, muttering about cartographers and the moral failings of idealists. "For the record," she said, "the map says the heart-stone lies under Solmere, and Solmere is under siege, and we are walking *toward* it."

"For the record," Marguerite said, easing the pin free, "I spent fifteen years learning to be quiet. I'd like to spend the rest being loud."

Above the camp, far off to the west, clouds were gathering that no weather-witch had called—and somewhere inside them, faint as a memory, thunder was learning his name again.
