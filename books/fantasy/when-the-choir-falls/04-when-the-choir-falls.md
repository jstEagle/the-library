## 4. When the Choir Falls

The bells of Solmere were ringing wrong.

Marguerite had heard city bells before—in another life, in another name—and they were meant to be a language: joy, alarm, the hour, the quarter. These rang like someone shaking a jar of coins at the sky. Panic, translated badly.

"They're telling everyone to get to the walls," Sefa said. She stood in the mouth of the aqueduct tunnel, her stolen-then-reclaimed map spread across her knees one last time, her fingers tracing the route they'd bled for. "Or maybe just ringing until their arms give out. Hard to say."

"Optimism looks good on you," Ilex said. The half-fae was perched on the aqueduct's crumbling lip, ears pricked toward the plain below, where Veyra's army had made itself known as a smudge of dust and iron that had been growing all day. "There's a gap in the lines. Northeast. They haven't closed it."

"They will."

"Then we should be rude and go through it anyway."

Marguerite looked at the map. He'd been looking at maps for weeks now—at Sefa's ink, at the courier's bloodstain, at the little annotation in his mentor's hand that had started all of this: *the stone remembers what we chose to forget.* He thought about how he'd once believed the valley had swallowed him whole, and how wrong he'd been. Valleys don't swallow. They hold. There's a difference, and it took him eleven years and three stubborn strangers to learn it.

"The covenant-stone is under the old choir hall," he said. "The map says so. Sefa's map says so." He glanced at her. "Your map."

"My map," she agreed, and there was something fierce and quiet in it, a woman handing herself back to herself. "And if we're going to die in my map, I'd prefer it be somewhere with good acoustics."

* * *

They went through the gap at dusk, when the light did most of the lying for them.

Ilex went first, half-fae quick, moving through the enemy camp's edges like a rumor. Sefa followed with the map rolled against her spine and a cartographer's unerring sense of exactly how lost they weren't. Marguerite came last, because his magic had been rising in him for days like water behind a dam, and he could feel every sentry's torch, every horse's heartbeat, every crackle of static in the dry air, and it took everything he had not to answer them.

A patrol found them anyway. Of course it did. Marguerite was beginning to suspect the universe kept a ledger.

Four soldiers, tired ones, young ones. One of them couldn't have been more than sixteen, and his hands shook around his spear, and Marguerite felt something in his chest crack sideways—not fear, worse, recognition. He had been that age when the order put him on a boat. He knew what it was to be handed a weapon instead of a choice.

He raised one hand, and the sky answered.

Not a storm. Not yet. Just pressure—the air going thick and green-tasting, hair lifting on every neck in a fifty-foot circle. The soldiers looked up. The youngest dropped his spear and ran, which Marguerite considered the single most intelligent act of the entire war.

"Show-off," Sefa muttered.

"You love it."

"I tolerate it at a professional distance."

Ilex laughed—a bright, startled sound, like the first note of something—and pulled them both into the dark beneath the walls.

* * *

The choir hall had burned twice in its long life and been rebuilt twice by people who clearly believed in second chances. Its bones remained: pillars like held breath, a vaulted ceiling furred with pigeon nests, and beneath the shattered altar, exactly where the map promised, the covenant-stone.

It was smaller than Marguerite expected. That was the thing about the objects that run the world—they never look the part. It was a slab of grey rock no bigger than a grave-marker, veined with silver that had gone dull as old pewter, cracked down the middle like a heart that had given up pretending.

Sefa knelt beside it and touched the crack. "It's cold," she said. "Everything else in this city feels like a fever. This is cold."

"It's waiting," Marguerite said. His voice came out strange to him—deeper, resonant, already halfway to being an instrument instead of a throat. "The courier told me. The last voice of the covenant reforges it with a song. And the song—" He stopped.

"And the song?" Ilex prompted, from the doorway, keeping watch.

"The song costs the voice. All of it. Forever." He said it plainly, because he'd spent three days trying to find a way to say it beautifully and there wasn't one. "That's the price on the page. The stone doesn't take a performance. It takes the singer."

Silence, except for the distant bells and the nearer drums.

Sefa stood. She was not a woman who comforted easily; she showed love the way she drew maps, in precise lines you had to lean close to read. "You've been silent for eleven years," she said. "In the valley. You told me. You didn't sing, you didn't call, you lived quiet as a beekeeper." Her eyes were wet and furious about it. "You already gave it up once. Why give it twice?"

"Because the first time was taken," Marguerite said. "This time it's mine." He managed a smile. "Also, I've always wanted to go out on a high note."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"He's right, it's a little funny," Ilex said, and then crossed the hall and hugged him, hard, the way only someone who'd run from everything understands how to hug. "Come back after," Ilex whispered. "Voiceless. Grumpy. Whatever. Come back."

* * *

Veyra's army reached the gates at moonrise.

Marguerite climbed onto the covenant-stone barefoot, because it seemed correct, and because his boots had a hole in them anyway. Below, beyond the broken wall, the host stretched black to the horizon—battering rams, siege towers, and above them all, on a ridge, a figure in storm-grey robes with lightning coiled around her fists like jewelry she'd stopped enjoying years ago.

Veyra. Silenced by the same order that exiled him. Wearing her grievance like armor, and under it, he suspected, the same wound.

*I could offer her mercy,* he thought. *I could offer her a place in the new covenant.* And he meant to. He opened his mouth to sing the opening—that low gathering note his mentor had taught him, the one that sounded like weather deciding—and what came out instead was not a battle-song at all.

It was a lullaby. The one the valley women sang to the bees.

He almost stopped. Then he understood: the stone wasn't asking for power. It was asking for truth. And the truest thing in him, after everything, was not thunder. It was a valley where magic went to rest, and hives humming in the dark, and people who took in a stranger without asking his name.

So he sang the lullaby into a storm.

His voice rose through the choir hall's ruined acoustics and out over the city, and the sky came apart—not in fury but in release, rain falling like forgiveness, wind combing through the army below and taking the fight with it. Siege towers folded like paper boats. Banners lay down. On the ridge, Veyra raised her hands and called her own storm, and Marguerite sang louder—not against her, *for* her, pouring into the gale every silenced year she'd carried, and he felt her falter, feel her hear it, feel the moment her lightning turned to rain.

The wards of Solmere blazed up along its walls, silver as the veins in the stone, renewed, awake. The covenant held.

And Marguerite Oyelaran sang the last verse, and the last word, and then nothing—his voice going out of him like a tide, gently, completely, the way a candle ends when the wax is done.

* * *

He woke to the smell of honey.

For one merciful moment he didn't remember. Then he tried to say *where am I* and produced a sound like a door hinge in need of oil, and it all came back, and grief sat down on his chest with its full weight.

"There he is." Sefa's face swam into view, smudged and triumphant. "Don't talk. You can't. It's very inconvenient for everyone."

Ilex appeared on his other side, holding a cup of something steaming. "You've been asleep two days. The city threw a festival. You missed it. There was a parade. Someone dressed a goat as Veyra. It was deeply disrespectful and I loved it."

Marguerite gestured weakly at his own throat.

"We know," Sefa said, softer. "We know what it cost." She pressed something into his hand: a small wooden pendant, freshly carved, a hexagon—honeycomb. "From the market. For the bridge between realms. That's what they're calling you. I'd have picked something less grandiose, but the goat set a high bar."

He looked past them, through the infirmary window, at Solmere rebuilding in the rain-washed morning—wards shimmering faintly over the walls, fae-light and lamplight mingling for the first time in a generation. Somewhere beyond the mountains, the valley waited, magic-dead and safe and full of people who would need to learn why their quiet beekeeper had left, and what he'd bought them with his silence.

He would go home. Not exiled. Chosen. He'd carry letters between worlds now, ward-songs written down for those who couldn't sing them, news of Solmere to the valley and valley honey to Solmere. A bridge doesn't get to choose which shore it loves more.

Ilex squeezed his hand. "Ready to go home?"

Marguerite smiled, and nodded, and said nothing—

—and in the silence, clear as a bell, deep in his chest where his voice used to live, he heard the bees singing back.
