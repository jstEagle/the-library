## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The soup wanted to be admired.

That was the first thing Yara Underhill learned about it, standing at the king's left hand with the silver spoon balanced on her tongue—before she learned anything else about it, before the deeper truths came swimming up through the broth like fish rising to feed. The soup was vain. It had been made by a cook who loved it, and love clung to food the way salt clung to skin.

She let it sit on her tongue for three heartbeats. That was the rule. Three heartbeats, then swallow or spit, and never let your face change either way.

Underneath the vanity: leek, smoked trout, cream from a farm two valleys east whose cows were contented enough that their milk still hummed faintly of clover. Underneath all of that, threaded thin as a hair through the whole warm body of the dish—

Nothing.

No poison tonight. Yara swallowed, inclined her head a precise half-inch toward the high table, and stepped back into the shadow of the pillar where tasters stood when they were not needed. Her job, officially, was her mouth. Unofficially, it was everything but.

"You look grim, Underhill," murmured Serrek, the under-taster, a soft-bellied man who chewed each sample like he was settling a grudge against it.

"I always look grim. It's how I keep my wages up."

"Ha." He went back to his bread.

Yara folded her hands and watched the hall without watching it. This was the discipline, the real one—not the tasting, which was easy, but the not-seeing. A hundred courtiers glittering in candlelight, and every single one of them carrying their small true selves around inside them like stones in their pockets. She could hear them if she let herself. Not words. Weights. The steward by the door was honest down to his boots. The lady in green with the laugh like struck glass was lying about something right now, had been lying about it for years, and the lie sat in her chest so heavy Yara could almost name its shape. She didn't try. Naming things was how you got caught naming things.

Her gift had no grand name. The Guild would have called it *hearing what things truly are*, because the Guild loved its phrasings, but Yara thought of it more plainly: things spoke to her tongue, and she'd learned young that most people did not want to know what their bread had to say.

So she hid. She hid the way she'd been hiding since she was nine years old and told her mother the well water was sad, and her mother's face went white as a scraped bone, and her mother said, *Never say that again, Yara. Never say what you hear.* Hiding in plain sight was a craft like any other. You practiced it daily. You kept your face smooth, your answers dull, your gift folded up small and quiet as a moth in a closed fist.

The king laughed at something the ambassador said—a real laugh, booming, unpoisoned—and the hall laughed with him, and Yara counted the courses remaining on her fingers. Fish pie next. Then the sweet. Then she was free until dawn, and the evening was hers, and the other life began.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its hall behind a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, up a staircase that smelled of tallow and old paper, past a door with no lock because, as Aldous liked to say, *no one robs a room they cannot find.* Inside, the guildhall was long and low and lit by lamps turned down to a conspiratorial glow, and it was full—fuller than usual—of people who did not exist.

Not literally. But nearly. The census had no line for any of them. The tax rolls skipped them like a stone skipping water. There was Merrin, who could unpick any lock by asking it politely, listed nowhere; there was Osk and his sister Fen, twins who could trade dreams across a table; there was old Bess Hollow, who heard storms three days before the sky did. Eleven members, twelve counting Aldous, and every one of them a ghost on paper, which was precisely how they liked it.

Yara liked it too. She reminded herself that she liked it as she climbed the stairs, as she pushed open the door, as the smell of ink and beeswax wrapped around her like a coat she'd owned all her life.

"Ledger-keeper," Aldous called from the head of the long table, not looking up from his correspondence. He was a narrow man gone soft at the edges, with ink-stained cuffs and a voice built for lecture halls he'd never been allowed inside. "You're late."

"I'm exactly on time. Your clock is fast because you wind it out of anxiety."

"My clock is *ambitious*." He waved her toward the shelf. "Trades came in today. Two of them are nonsense. One of them is worse than nonsense. Have a look before you balance anything."

The ledgers lived on their shelf like sleeping animals, spines worn pale where hands had gripped them. Yara took the current book—the living one, the one that breathed—and carried it to her seat at the far end of the table, where the lamp burned cleanest.

The Guild of Impossible Things traded, as its name promised, in impossibilities. Not goods. *Certainties.* When a farmer needed rain by Thursday and had nothing to offer but his grandfather's memory of a song, the Guild found someone who needed a memory of a song and could promise rain. When a widow wanted one hour of her husband's voice back, the Guild found a man dying who wanted to be remembered by a stranger, and brokered the exchange, and both parties walked away paid. Every trade was written down: what was given, what was received, what it cost, what it was worth. Worth was the hard part. Worth was why they kept a royal food-taster on retainer, because Yara could hold a thing on her tongue—even a thing that wasn't a thing, even a memory, even a promise—and tell you if it was true.

Tonight's entries:

*Merrin, trading to a locksmith's apprentice the secret of the Marlowe vault, in exchange for the apprentice's fear of enclosed spaces. Fear received in good order. Apprentice reports sleeping better already.*

Yara read that twice. Trading away a fear. It was legal by guild rules—it was the kind of trade the Guild existed for—but her tongue remembered the weight of fears, and fears were heavy, and she wondered where in the ledger a discarded fear was stored. There was a page for that. There was a page for everything. That was the point of the ledger: nothing given, nothing taken, ever vanished. It all went into the book, and the book grew heavier every year, though it never gained an ounce.

The second entry was Osk and Fen, brokering a dream between a grieving mother and a lighthouse keeper. Standard work, cleanly priced. The third entry made her sit very still.

*Aldous Vane, guildmaster, receiving from an unnamed party one guarantee of continued anonymity, in exchange for consideration to be named.*

Consideration to be named. That wasn't a trade. That was a placeholder. That was a door left open in a house full of people who survived by keeping doors shut.

"Aldous," she said. "What is this?"

He didn't look up. "What is what?"

"The third entry. *Consideration to be named.* You don't write placeholders, Aldous. You've lectured me for six years about placeholders. *A ledger with a hole in it is a lie with good posture,*" she quoted, and watched his shoulders draw in slightly, the way a man's do when a coat doesn't fit anymore.

"It's a formality." Now he looked up, and smiled, and the smile was fine, the smile was practiced, and Yara's tongue—her treacherous, truthful tongue—tasted the air between them and found it wrong. Not poisoned. Just not true. "There's a gentleman who's approached me about certain assurances. Nothing has been agreed. When something is agreed, I'll write it properly, and you'll balance it, and we'll all go on."

"What kind of gentleman approaches the Guild for *anonymity*? Anonymity isn't bought, Aldous. It's kept. Who's selling it to us?"

"A friend of the crown." He said it lightly, the way you'd say *a touch of frost*, and went back to his letter.

Yara looked around the hall. Merrin was showing Tam—the youngest of them, sixteen, all elbows and hungry eyes, gifted with finding lost things—how to read a lock's mood. Bess Hollow dozed in her chair with her knitting unfinished in her lap, because Bess finished nothing now except predictions. Fen was laughing at something Osk whispered. Twelve impossible people in a lamp-lit room above a chandler's shop, and every one of them trusting the ledger, and the ledger holding a hole shaped like a man's fear.

She wrote the entry into balance as best she could—*pending, pending, pending*—and hated every word of it.

Later, near midnight, Tam drifted over to her end of the table, the way Tam always did, drawn to Yara like a cold hand to a stove. "Did you taste anything strange today?" Tam asked. Tam asked it every week. It was their ritual: the taster's report, delivered in whispers over the ledgers, the closest thing either of them had to confession.

"Soup was vain. Wine was lonely. The king's physician is frightened of something, but he's been frightened for months, so that's not news." She paused. "Aldous is hiding something."

Tam's eyebrows went up. "From us?"

"From the ledger. Which is worse."

Tam considered this with the seriousness the young bring to catastrophe, having less practice surviving it. "Do you think—" Tam started, and stopped, because the bell above the chandler's shop downstairs began to ring.

It was midnight. The chandler's bell rang at midnight every night, one slow toll, regular as breath. Yara had heard it hundreds of times. She knew its voice the way she knew her own.

This toll was wrong. It rang backward—she felt it in her teeth, in the ledger under her palms, in the milk-hum of the city itself—a sound pulled in instead of pushed out, a bell rung the wrong way through time. Down in the street, dogs began to howl. In the hall, Bess Hollow woke with a gasp and said, clearly, to no one and everyone:

"They're going to make us count."

The bell finished its backward stroke. Silence rushed in after it like water into a broken hull.

In the morning, Yara knew—she tasted it already, bitter on the air, criers warming their throats in the palace yard—the crown would proclaim its Edict of Purification. Every gifted soul in Vessany's memory would be commanded to register, to be named, to be numbered.

And the Guild of Impossible Things had spent thirty years making sure that none of them existed to be counted.
