## 2. A Bell Rung Backward

The bell rang backward at dawn.

I don't mean it cracked, or that some apprentice pulled the rope wrong. I mean the sound itself came out wrong—each note falling instead of rising, like water running uphill, like a word spoken in reverse. Anyone else in Vessany's capital would have heard only a strange, sour peal and gone back to sleep. But I heard what it truly was: a summons. The palace had ordered the great bells of Saint Ferren tolled in mourning for something that hadn't died yet.

They were ringing for us.

I stood at my window above the tallow-chandler's shop with my hands pressed flat against the cold glass, and I listened to the city wake up confused beneath that inverted song. Bakers lit their ovens. Gulls argued over the fish market. A drunk sang three streets over, badly, about a girl from the coast. All of it ordinary. All of it oblivious. And threaded through it, growing louder as the sun came up: criers. Dozens of them, moving through the warrens in pairs, unrolling proclamations nailed to boards they carried on their backs.

I couldn't hear the words from my window. I didn't need to. I could taste them already, bitter as copper, sitting on my tongue since midnight—since the kitchen boy had come to me after the king's supper, whispering that the Lord Chancellor had summoned every steward in the palace and told them to prepare for "a season of cleansing."

A season of cleansing. Even secondhand, through a frightened boy's mouth, those words had a flavor I knew. I'd tasted it once before, in a soup meant for the old queen, back when I was new to the tasting bench and still naive enough to think poison always came in vials.

* * *

By the time I reached the guildhall, the proclamation had beaten me there.

It was pasted to the door of the Lamplighters' Rest next door, and a small crowd had gathered to have it read aloud by anyone who could read. I didn't stop. I let my shoulder carry me through the knot of people like water around a stone, and I read it in passing, the way I read everything—the true shape of it sliding under the printed words.

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN:** Let it be known throughout the realm that certain persons exist who possess unnatural faculties—hereafter called *the Gifted*—being an abomination against natural order and a peril to the sovereign's peace. All such persons shall present themselves at any temple of the Crown Faith within thirty days to be registered, examined, and assigned lawful station. Those who fail to register shall be declared *unregistered*, and hunted accordingly. Reward offered: fifty crowns per head.

Fifty crowns per head. I did the arithmetic without meaning to. Our guild numbered thirty-one souls. Fifteen hundred and fifty crowns. Enough to buy a ship. Enough to buy silence from a magistrate. Enough, in the poor quarters where most of our members lived, to buy betrayal from a neighbor you'd known your whole life.

The crowd murmured. Someone said it was about sorcerers, fire-callers, the ones who'd burned the granaries in Marrowfield last spring. Someone else said no, it was broader than that, much broader—*unnatural faculties*, plain words, plain words chosen on purpose. A woman clutched her son's hand tighter. An old man spat and walked away.

And underneath all of it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, I heard the true text of the thing—not the words but the want behind them. Fear. Cold, administrative fear, dressed in law. Whoever wrote this wasn't hunting power. They were hunting the unknowable. And I had spent twenty-nine years making myself as knowable as bread.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its hall behind a false wall in the cellar of a disused countinghouse on Candlewick Lane. You entered through a door disguised as a shelf of ledgers—which, in fairness, it also was—and you descended into a long room that smelled of ink, tallow, and the particular dust of secrets kept too long.

We were not fire-callers. We were not storm-singers or bone-readers or any of the dramatic gifts the edict's drafters had surely imagined when they wrote *abomination*. We were accountants. We were the quiet gifts: the woman who could find anything lost, the man who could tell when a contract lied, the girl who heard what objects truly were. We kept ledgers of impossible trades—the debts owed between things that shouldn't be able to owe each other. A debt of one hour of borrowed luck, repaid. A debt of a name, returned after forty years. A debt of grief, transferred, with interest.

It was honest work, in its way. It was invisible work. It had been invisible for sixty years.

When I came down the stairs, the hall was already full, and it was full wrong. Thirty-one people, and nobody talking. Just the sound of breathing and the scratch of Tam's quill—he'd been writing all night, copying the master ledger against loss, though none of us had said out loud what loss we feared.

Tam looked up when I entered. He's nineteen, all elbows and anxious eyes, with a gift he thinks is small: he can hear the age of things. Put a coin in his palm and he'll tell you whose pocket it slept in last. He'd been holding the same ledger for an hour, I could tell, because the ledger was telling him how afraid we all were, soaked into the paper by our touching it.

"Yara." He said my name like a question.

"I know," I said. "I heard the bells."

Aldous Vane stood at the head table, and Aldous was smiling.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was that his coat was buttoned wrong—one button off, which on a man as vain as Aldous meant he'd dressed in the dark or in a hurry or with his mind somewhere else entirely. He was sixty, silver-bearded, handsome in the way of portraits that have been touched up. He'd founded nothing; he'd inherited the guild from his mother, who'd inherited it from hers, and he wore that inheritance like a crown he believed he deserved.

"Friends," he said, spreading his hands. "Sit. Please. What you heard this morning is a scarecrow—a figure set up in a field to frighten crows. Crowns have made noises like this before. Under old King Emmerich there was a purge of hedge-witches, yes, and what came of it? Nothing. A few arrests, a few show trials, and within two years the temples were quietly buying charms again because the faithful demanded them." He laughed, warm and easy, a laugh I'd heard him deploy across negotiating tables for a decade. "The crown cannot hunt what it cannot see. And we—" he tapped the ledger before him, "—are the unseen. That is the entire architecture of this guild. We survive by being impossible to point at."

Around the room, shoulders loosened. I watched it happen—watched him pour calm into them like wine into cups, and watched them drink. Maren the finder nodded slowly. Old Sef, who could tell when a contract lied, actually smiled. Even Tam sat back down.

I stayed standing.

"Aldous," I said. "What did you burn?"

The room went very quiet.

His smile held. It was a professional smile; it could hold through almost anything. "I beg your pardon?"

"The chimney smoke. When I came down Candlewick Lane, the countinghouse chimney was smoking, and the countinghouse has no tenants, and the hearth in this hall hasn't been lit since winter." I turned to face the others, because they deserved to hear it plainly. "He's burning records. This morning. While telling us there's nothing to fear."

"That is a gross mischaracterization—"

"Of what?" I asked. "Tell them what was in the pages, Aldous. Tell them why the master ledger's newest entries are missing."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched the smile do its work, flexing, adjusting. Then he sighed, the sigh of a reasonable man burdened with unreasonable people, and he said, gently, "Yara has a gift for suspicion. It's why she's such an excellent taster. But let me ask her this: if I believed the edict would destroy us, would I be standing here telling you all to keep calm? Would I not rather be packing?"

It was a good answer. It was the kind of answer that wins rooms. Several people visibly relaxed further, and Maren even chuckled, and I felt the moment tipping away from me like a plate sliding off a table's edge—

And then Tam, without looking up from his ledger, said: "The paper's still warm."

Everyone looked at him.

"The ash bin," Tam said. His voice shook, but he kept going, because Tam, for all his nerves, had never once learned how to lie. "In the corner. By the coal store. It's full of burnt vellum and the ash is still warm and vellum doesn't burn easy—you have to want it to burn." He swallowed. "I can hear the binding wax on the fragments. It's guild wax. It's ours."

Nobody moved. The hearth ticked. Somewhere above us, a cart rattled over the cobbles, ordinary as anything, while underneath the city our whole invisible life hung in the air like smoke deciding which way to drift.

Aldous's face went through several expressions in quick succession—surprise, calculation, wounded dignity—and settled on sorrow. Genuine-looking sorrow. He was, whatever else he was, a gifted performer; his gift, though he'd never registered it as one, was making people believe him.

"You're right," he said softly. "Forgive me. I burned the pages listing our names and stations—the registry copies, the ones that would hang us if found. I did it alone, at dawn, so that no other hands would be bloodied by it. I intended to tell you once I was certain there was no panic to manage." He spread his hands again, the picture of weary candor. "Yes. I am afraid. There—I've said it. But fear is a counsel we take privately, friends, not a banner we raise together. Register? Never. Flee? Not yet. We hold steady, we go quiet, we wait out the season. That is all I ask."

And the room, wanting so badly to believe him, believed him.

I wanted to believe him too. That's the truth of it, and I've had years now to sit with the truth. I wanted to believe him because the alternative was unthinkable: that the man who'd taught me the ledgers, who'd given a friendless food-taster a family of misfits and a door behind a bookshelf, was already measuring the distance between himself and the rest of us.

I helped bank the lamps. I helped carry the ledgers to the deep archive. And as I passed the ash bin, I looked down into it, and among the black curls of vellum I saw a scrap that hadn't fully burned—a corner of a page, with half a line of ink surviving:

*...the Door of Vessany, being the only egress, requires...*

Vessany. A country I'd never heard of, in a ledger entry someone had thought important enough to burn—or important enough to hide inside a burning.

I took the scrap. I told myself it was tidiness.

* * *

That night the bells rang backward again, and this time I understood them perfectly.

Thirty days. The criers said it in every square: thirty days to register, after which the hunt begins in earnest. Inquisitors appointed. Warrants drawn. Fifty crowns a head.

I lay awake in my narrow bed above the chandler's shop and listened to the city breathe, and I thought about the taste of the chancellor's words in the kitchen boy's mouth. *Cleansing.* Not *searching*. Not *questioning*. Cleansing. You cleanse a wound. You cleanse a stain. Nobody registers a stain.

And I thought about Aldous's chimney smoking at dawn, and the warmth of the ash, and the way his smile never once reached the place behind his eyes where men keep the things they're ashamed of.

At midnight I got up, dressed, and took the scrap of vellum from its hiding place in my flour jar. Half a line of ink. *The Door of Vessany.* I turned it over in my fingers, listening to it the way I listen to everything.

It tasted old. Older than the guild. Older, maybe, than the kingdom that was hunting us.

And underneath the age, faint but unmistakable, it tasted like a door standing open somewhere in the dark—waiting for exactly the kind of person the crown had just promised to hunt.

I put it back in the jar. I blew out the candle.

Thirty days.
