## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

The oldest ledger in the Guild of Impossible Things was bound in leather that had gone the color of old teeth, and it did not want to be read.

I don't mean that the way a poet means it. I mean that when I pulled it from the bottom of the archive shelf, my gift rose up in me like a cat arching its back, and I heard—not words, but a pressure, a low note held under everything else, the way a cellar holds cold. Things tell me what they are. Bread tells me it's honest. Wine tells me whose vineyard it came from and whether the vintner wept that year. The soup at the king's table had told me, only that morning, that it was merely soup, merely saffron and mutton and someone's vanity about saffron.

This ledger told me: *older than you, older than your grief, put me back.*

I didn't put it back.

The guildhall was empty at that hour—the hour between the last of Aldous's meetings and the first of Tam's errands, when the candles burned down to their collars and the building itself seemed to exhale. I sat at the long table where we balanced our impossible accounts—trades no court would recognize, debts measured in favors and secrets—and I opened the ledger across my knees.

The first pages were inventory. That was all any ledger is, at heart: a list of what exists. Barrels of forgetting, three, one cracked. A debt owed to a river. Two hundred years ago some guild clerk with handwriting like bird tracks had recorded the guild's holdings the way any clerk would, because the guild has always been, underneath everything, an act of bookkeeping. We keep track of things the world has decided not to keep track of.

Then, near the middle, the entries changed.

*Received into keeping: one door, disused.*

I read it twice. My candle guttered and steadied.

*Location: Vessany. Status of country: struck from record by decree of the Concord, year 41 of the Long Peace. Status of door: sealed. Method of sealing: song. Condition of opening: a voice that stays.*

The ink was brown with age, but the letters were firm, unhurried. Whoever wrote them had believed they were writing something ordinary. That's what undid me—not the strangeness of it but the calm. *One door, disused.* As if a country could be erased the way a barrel is emptied, and the door left over, and someone had simply noted it for the next clerk's attention.

Everyone knew the story of Vessany, or knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a tooth that's been pulled. There had been a country there once—east of the Salt Marches, past where the maps go shy—and then there hadn't. Not conquered. Not destroyed. *Erased.* The Concord of forty years ago had unmade it on paper, and then in law, and then, somehow, in fact. No ruins. No refugees anyone would admit to receiving. Roads that bent around a space no one could name. My mother used to hum a tune she claimed came from nowhere, and when I asked her where she'd learned it she'd look at me with an expression I only understood much later, after she died, after I learned to listen to silences the way other people listen to speech.

She'd been listening to a country that wasn't there.

I turned pages with hands that weren't quite steady. The entries continued, decades of them, clerks noting the door's upkeep as though it were a roof. *Sang the hinge-verse, feast of the Turning. Door holds.* And then, thirty years back, the handwriting changed—shakier, hurried—and the entries thinned, and the last one read:

*No singers left who remember the whole of it. Door sleeps. If found: do not open. It opens only for a voice that stays, and no one should be asked to stay.*

I sat in the empty hall for a long time after that, the candle burning down between us, the ledger open on my knees like a mouth mid-word.

*A voice that stays.*

I thought: that's a riddle with a body in it. That's a lock shaped like a person. Somewhere east of the Salt Marches there was a way out of this country—for the gifted, for the hunted, for everyone the Purification Edict would soon come for—and it cost exactly one life to open, and the last clerk who'd touched these pages had known it, and had written *no one should be asked to stay* in the same careful hand he'd used for barrels of forgetting.

I closed the ledger. I held it against my chest and felt it hum that low cellar-note, older than my grief, patient as debt.

Outside, the bells rang terce, and under the bells—faint, half a mile away, up the hill—I heard the palace waking. I heard the king's household arguing about fish. I heard a hundred small honest sounds.

And I heard horses. Many horses, coming up the processional road, riding in formation, which is a sound like no other sound: iron keeping time.

* * *

They arrived at the ninth hour, which meant the visit had been planned, announced, and approved, which meant it was ceremony rather than raid. Six riders in the grey-and-silver of the Office of Inquiry, and at their head a man I recognized before I recognized him, the way you sometimes know a chord before you can name its notes.

Cassian Vale.

He'd filled out. His hair was cropped short now, Inquisitor-fashion, and the loose boyishness I remembered had been replaced by something tailored and deliberate, a man built like an argument. But when he swung down from his horse in the palace courtyard and looked up at the windows, he did it with his whole face, the way he used to look at everything, as if the world might improve if he paid enough attention.

Seven years since the seminary at Corvel. Seven years since we'd sat in the same scriptorium, two scholarship students pretending we belonged among the gilded, sharing contraband plums and theories about whether truth was a thing you found or a thing you made. He'd wanted to make it. I'd wanted to find it. We'd both been wrong, probably.

And now he wore the silver pin of the Office of Inquiry, and the Edict had passed the Council two days ago, and every gifted soul in Vessany's former borders—or rather, in the kingdom that had digested Vessany's absence—was required to register name, nature, and address, or be hunted.

I stood at my post beside the king's chair while the chamberlain announced him, and I tasted the room.

Rooms taste different when an Inquisitor enters. Fear has a flavor—coppery, thin, like water from a new pipe—and it moved through the courtiers in waves as Cassian crossed the marble. The king tasted of wine and complacency. The chamberlain tasted of ambition going sour. And Cassian—

Cassian tasted of discipline laid over something else, the way frost lies over running water. Underneath the frost, the water was moving fast.

"Your Majesty." He bowed precisely. "The Office thanks the crown for its cooperation in the matter of registration."

"The matter proceeds?" the king said, around a mouthful of pheasant I had already tasted for him, twice, out of habit.

"Slowly. People lie." Cassian's voice was even, pleasant, terrible. "But the Office has methods. And we have reason to believe a concentration of the unregistered exists in the capital. A—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—a *guild*."

My hands, folded at my waist, did not move. I have spent eleven years learning stillness; it is the taster's first skill, older than the tongue. But inside my chest something went off like a dropped glass.

"We have names," Cassian continued. "Partial ones. We have a location. And within the week, we will have the rest."

The king waved a drumstick, magnanimous. "The crown is pleased. Purification is a holy labor, Inquisitor. See it done cleanly."

"Cleanly," Cassian agreed, and I heard the frost creak over the moving water.

It was then that he looked at me.

Not at the taster. At *me*. His gaze crossed the room the way a searchlight crosses a field, snagged on my face, and stopped. For one second—one held breath—he was not an Inquisitor in a throne room. He was nineteen, in the scriptorium at Corvel, ink on his fingers, saying *Yara, you won't believe what I found in the restricted stacks,* with that look of whole-faced attention, as if I were the restricted stack, as if I were the thing worth finding.

He knew me. I watched him know me. And I watched him decide, in real time, in front of the king and the court and God and whatever the Office of Inquiry kept instead of God, what to do about it.

"A taster," he said aloud, mildly, to the chamberlain. "Your Majesty keeps a food-taster?"

"A precaution," the chamberlain said. "The girl has served six years without incident."

"Six years." Cassian's eyes stayed on mine a moment longer than courtesy allowed. Whatever lived under his frost moved beneath it, fast and unreadable. "Loyal work. Unremarkable work." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "The Office values the unremarkable."

Then he bowed again, to the king, to the room, and withdrew, and his boots on the marble kept perfect time, and I stood at my post tasting nothing at all, because my gift had gone silent with terror, and terror, I have learned, tastes like nothing. Like a tongue scalded blind.

* * *

I did not go straight to the guildhall. I walked the long way, through the kitchen courts and the laundry yards, doubling back twice, listening behind me with the sense I never speak of. No one followed. Or no one I could hear.

In the guildhall, Aldous was holding court over the account table, telling Tam and the others that the Edict was theater, that the Office blustered, that registration was a sieve with holes big enough to walk through. He tasted of brandy and self-regard, as always, but tonight there was something else under it, something quick and animal that I chose—God forgive me, I chose—not to name.

Tam looked up when I came in, and her face did the thing it always did, brightening like a lamp turned up. "You're late. Aldous says the world isn't ending."

"Aldous," I said, "balances books. The world doesn't consult him."

I carried the old ledger to my corner desk, and I did not put it in the archive. I wrapped it in oilcloth, and I tied it with cord, and I set it where my hand could find it in the dark.

Because here is what I know, the way I know bread is honest and wine is wept-over and rooms are afraid: Cassian Vale recognized me, and he let me stand. Either the frost over the water is thick enough to walk on, or he is already deciding what I am worth—what I'm worth against the guild, against the names he says he'll have within the week, against whatever the Office holds over him instead of a heart.

Either way, the clock is running. And somewhere east of the Salt Marches, past where the maps go shy, a door is sleeping, and it wants a voice that stays, and I am beginning to be afraid that I already know how this ledger ends—with my name written in it, in careful clerk's hand, entered under *received into keeping.*

That night, for the first time in six years, I dreamed of Corvel, and in the dream Cassian handed me a plum and said, *you won't believe what I found,* and when I opened my hand the plum was a key, and when I looked up he was wearing grey and silver, and the orchard around us was full of people singing a country back into existence, and every one of them was looking at me.
