## 4. The Guildmaster's Bargain

The Inquisition kept its offices in the old customs house, where the river met the city wall, and Cassian had learned in three years of service that the building's architecture was a lesson. Everything about it said: *nothing passes unexamined.* The windows were narrow as knife wounds. The corridors turned without warning. Even the light came in filtered and gray, as if it too had been interrogated on its way through.

He climbed to the third floor at the appointed hour, past clerks who did not look up, and knocked once on the door marked only with a number.

"Enter."

High Inquisitor Vael sat behind a desk that held nothing but a lamp, a decanter, and two glasses. The emptiness was deliberate. Cassian knew this because he had been trained by this man, and everything Vael did was deliberate, the way a snare is deliberate.

"You wanted me," Cassian said.

"I wanted you an hour ago." Vael poured two measures of plum brandy without asking. "Sit."

Cassian sat. He did not touch the glass.

"Do you know what I admire about you?" Vael said. "You came up from nothing. No family name to protect, no patron to flatter. You built yourself into an instrument. Clean lines. No ornamentation." He slid one glass across the desk. "Instruments are useful. Men with ornaments—men with histories, men with *attachments*—they hesitate. And hesitation, in our work, is how fires start."

"I'm aware."

"Are you?" Vael leaned back. "Because you've been aware for six days now, since the Edict was read from every pulpit in the capital, and in those six days your list of registered names has grown by four. Four, Cassian. A seamstress who glows when she lies. Two boys who talk to rats. An old man who can find lost coins. Meanwhile my colleagues in the eastern districts have filled whole registries. Whole *pages*."

"The eastern districts use informers who are paid per name," Cassian said. "They get quantity. Not accuracy."

"Accuracy." Vael smiled, and it was the smile he used before unpleasant things happened to other people. "Yes. Which brings me to why you're here. We have a name you haven't found. Or rather—" he opened a drawer, unhurried, a man savoring the moment, "—a place. The Guild of Impossible Things. Ring any bells?"

Cassian kept his face still. It cost him something. Somewhere behind his ribs, a door he'd thought bricked over shifted on its hinges.

"It's a rumor," he said. "A tavern story. Gifted beggars telling each other there's a safehouse."

"It's a ledger-keeping operation disguised as a counting-house off Cooper's Lane. They trade in favors between the gifted—cures, concealments, forged papers. And they've been doing it under the crown's nose for years, which means someone has been very good, or someone has been very lucky." Vael set a folded paper on the desk between them. "We want names. Every member. Every client. And we want you to be the one who gets them."

"Why me?"

"Because you're accurate." Vael's eyes were pale and patient, like a fisherman watching a line. "And because you know the city's underside better than anyone I employ. Go tonight. Offer the guildmaster immunity. Full pardon, new papers, a stipend if he wants it. All he has to do is give us his people."

"And if he refuses?"

Vael picked up his own glass at last and drank, delicately. "Then we take the building at dawn and everyone inside it, and you'll write the report explaining why you failed. I'm told dawn raids make excellent copy for the broadsheets. The public loves a rescue it didn't ask for."

* * *

Cooper's Lane smelled of tallow and wet stone, and the guildhall wore its disguise well—a plain counting-house front, shutters half-closed, a sign reading MERCHANDISE ACCOUNTED & BALANCED in paint so faded it looked apologetic. Cassian had walked past it a hundred times in another life, back when he was nobody's instrument, back when a girl with flour on her sleeves used to laugh at him for reading law codes for pleasure.

He told himself the girl was irrelevant. He had gotten good at telling himself things.

Aldous Fen received him in the back room among the ledgers, and Cassian understood immediately why Vael had sent him rather than a squad. Aldous was not what the Edict imagined when it said *gifted*. He was sixty-ish, soft-handed, dressed like a prosperous merchant who had made his money in buttons. His gift, if the rumors were right, was small and vain: objects told him their prices. Any object. He could hold a spoon and know what it had cost, what it would fetch, what it was worth to the man who'd stolen it. A useless talent, unless you built a guild on it.

"Inquisitor." Aldous spread his hands, all welcome, no warmth. "To what do I owe the honor? Come to audit us?"

"Something like that." Cassian took the offered chair without being offered it. Sat. Let the silence do some work. Around them the ledgers stood in ranks, spines cracked, decades of impossible trades recorded in careful hands—one of them, he remembered, in handwriting he'd know anywhere, because he'd once watched it form love letters by candlelight.

He put that away too.

"I'm here to offer you a way out," he said. "The Edict requires registration. Registration means lists, and lists mean the tribunals, and the tribunals mean whatever they mean this month. But the crown values cooperation above confession. Give me the names of your members and your clients, and you walk away clean. New identity if you want it. Money. Immunity, in writing, signed by Vael himself."

Aldous's face went through a small weather system—surprise, calculation, fear, and then something worse: temptation, arriving like a guest who'd been expected all along.

"You think I'm frightened," Aldous said.

"Aren't you?"

"Of course I'm frightened." The merchant laughed, and it was a real laugh, which somehow made it uglier. "I'm sixty-one years old and I've spent thirty years building the only honest institution in this city, and along comes a bell rung backward and suddenly honesty is treason." He tapped the ledger nearest his elbow. "Do you know what's in these books, Inquisitor? A woman who mends broken bones with a song, paid in bread. A boy who finds missing children, paid in nothing, because he likes finding them. This isn't a conspiracy. It's a *pantry*. And you people are coming to burn the pantry because you're afraid of what's stored in it."

"Then refuse," Cassian said quietly. "Refuse, and at dawn the soldiers come, and the pantry burns anyway, and everyone in it goes to the tribunals. Or don't refuse, and you keep your life and your freedom and your buttons." He leaned forward. "I've read your file, Master Fen. There isn't much in it. That's the point of you—you've stayed invisible for thirty years. One signature and you stay invisible forever."

For a long moment Aldous said nothing. His hand rested on the ledger the way a man rests a hand on a dog's head—not affection, exactly. Possession. Ownership. The gesture of someone deciding what a thing is worth.

Cassian watched him and felt the trap close from both ends. Because here was the truth he could not say aloud: he did not want the names. He wanted Aldous to refuse. He wanted the old man to be the kind of person the girl with flour on her sleeves had believed he was, so that Cassian could go back to Vael empty-handed and call it failure and sleep, if not well, then at least honestly.

But Aldous was looking at the ledger the way he looked at spoons.

"There are forty-three names," Aldous said at last. "Forty-three members, and perhaps two hundred clients. Some of them children." He wet his lips. "What exactly does immunity cover? Past acts only, or—"

"It covers you," Cassian said. "That's the offer. You. Not them."

"Yes, yes, obviously." Aldous waved this away, already negotiating with himself, already halfway down the road. "But a man in my position has to think about contingencies. If I cooperate fully—if I'm *thorough*—there should be consideration for thoroughness. A stipend, you said. What magnitude of stipend?"

Something cold moved through Cassian's chest. Not surprise. He'd seen this before, in a hundred interrogation rooms—the moment fear finished its arithmetic and vanity signed the bill. But it had never been someone he'd drunk with, someone whose daughter had once stitched a tear in his coat without being asked, someone whose—

*Stop.*

"How much do you imagine your life is worth?" Cassian asked.

"That depends," Aldous said, "on how much trouble I save you."

* * *

Cassian left the counting-house after midnight with no names and no refusal, which was its own kind of answer. Aldous had asked for three days. Three days to consult his conscience, he'd said, and then he'd laughed again, and Cassian had understood that the consultation was a formality, that the decision had been made somewhere in the middle of the sentence about contingencies, and that the three days were merely the time it took a vain man to convince himself he was a good one.

The rain had started. Cassian stood under the eaves across the lane and looked at the lit window of the back room, where a shadow moved among the ledgers—Aldous, alone with his forty-three names, doing whatever a man does on the last night of his own decency.

Cassian thought about reporting it tonight. *He's ready. Take him now.* It would end the waiting. It might even, if the raid went cleanly, spare the ones who ran fast enough.

Instead he wrote in his log: *Subject noncommittal. Requires further pressure.* Three days bought. He didn't examine why. Instruments didn't need reasons; they needed tasks.

He was turning up his collar when he saw her.

Across the lane, slipping out of the guildhall's side door with a wrapped parcel under one arm and her hood up against the rain—moving the way she always had, quick and quiet, like someone perpetually late for something secret. She paused at the corner, checked the street with a glance that swept it like a broom, and did not see him standing twenty feet away in the dark.

Yara Underhill. Royal food-taster. Guild bookkeeper. The reason he'd learned to read ledgers in the first place, all those years ago, so he'd have an excuse to sit beside her while she worked.

She vanished around the corner toward the palace, and Cassian stood in the rain a long moment, feeling the line go taut, feeling Vael's patient fisherman smile settle onto his own face whether he willed it or not.

Three days, he had bought her guild.

He just hoped to every silent god that Aldous Fen never learned her name.
