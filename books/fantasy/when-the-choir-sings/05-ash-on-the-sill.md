## 5. Ash on the Sill

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Yara had learned the guildhall's rhythms the way she'd learned the king's palate—by listening. The building spoke in creaks and settle-sounds, in the particular groan of the third stair when Tam took it two at a time, in the wet cough of the pipes before dawn. So when boots hit the street outside at an hour when even the lamplighters were asleep, her body knew before her mind did.

She was out of bed with the ledger already in her hands.

Not the ledgers—all of them, the impossible trades, the debts of vanished things, the whole crooked history of people who could hear what objects truly were. Those lived in the strongbox under Aldous's desk, and Aldous had the key, and there was no time. There was only the one book, the oldest one, the one she'd been reading by candle stub for three nights now, the one that described a door in a country that no longer existed.

*Vessany.*

She shoved it into the oilcloth satchel she kept packed for reasons she'd never admitted to herself, slung it across her chest, and pressed her ear to the floor.

Below: the front door splintering. Not a knock after all. A breach.

"Guildhall! By order of the Purification Edict! Open in the name of the crown!"

They hadn't waited for it to open. Yara heard the door give, heard the crash of furniture, heard Aldous's voice rise from his rooms on the ground floor—high, indignant, *aristocratic*, a voice that had never once in twelve years sounded like that at the guildhall—and then something worse. Silence where shouting should have been.

Then Aldous again, calm now. Almost pleasant.

"The register is in the east cabinet. Names, gifts, last known addresses. I've kept it current."

Yara's blood went cold and then hot, so fast she swayed.

He was *helping* them.

* * *

She'd known something was wrong for days, if she was honest. The way Aldous had stopped meeting her eyes over the ledger table. The way he'd burned papers in the courtyard grate two nights ago—not the incriminating ones, she realized now, but the harmless ones, the theatrical bonfire of a man performing innocence while keeping the real evidence dry and folded. She'd thought he was frightened. She'd thought they all were. Fear was the guild's native language; they'd spoken it fluently for years, gifted people pretending to be nothing, hiding their small miracles inside small lives.

But this wasn't fear. This was arithmetic. Aldous had done the sums—one old man against forty names—and decided what he could afford to lose.

Yara moved through the dark of the attic loft on memory alone, past Tam's cot, past the rope ladder to the coal chute, past Marisol's curtain of dried herbs that smelled like every summer Yara had ever loved. The window over the kitchen yard was the escape route they'd drilled until it was boring. She lifted the sash with both hands, slow, silent—

And froze.

In the yard below, torchlight. Four soldiers around a kneeling figure, and the figure was Marisol, and her hands were bound behind her with the cord she used to tie her rosemary bundles, and she was not struggling. She was singing. Low, barely audible through the glass—a lullaby in a language Yara didn't know, the kind of song you sing to keep something alive.

One of the soldiers shifted, uneasy. Even without a gift, some things get into the blood.

Yara let the window down an inch. Her arms shook. Every part of her wanted to go down there, wanted it with a violence that scared her more than the soldiers did. But Marisol had taught her the first rule of the guild years ago, laughing, kneading bread: *You can't save anyone by getting caught beside them. Two captives help no one.*

So Yara did the hardest thing she had ever done in a life full of hard things. She closed the window.

She went up instead of down, out onto the roof tiles, cold as grave stones under her bare feet, and moved along the spine of the guildhall toward the chimney stack, toward the gap between buildings that Tam called the Cat's Way because only someone small and desperate would try it.

Behind her, below her, the guildhall filled with light and shouting. She heard names read aloud from a paper. She heard Oskar deny his gift and be struck for it. She heard a young voice—Tam's voice—yell something defiant and then stop mid-word, and Yara's heart stopped with it, and she nearly turned back, she *nearly*—

But the ledger pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and she remembered what it said about doors, about how a country can survive erasure if enough voices remember its name, and she understood, suddenly and completely, why she was carrying it and why she was alive and why the crown's men had come at dawn instead of midnight.

They wanted everyone. And everyone included whoever ran.

She dropped through the gap between buildings, caught the drainpipe, slid, landed hard in the alley mud, and ran.

* * *

She made it four streets before she stopped shaking. Made it six before she stopped crying. By the time she reached the tannery district, where the stink covered everything including grief, she was walking like a woman with ordinary errands, hood up, satchel flat against her spine, the oldest ledger warm against her back as though it knew it mattered.

She found Tam at the agreed place—the ruined dovecote behind the chandler's, where they'd hidden once as children during a tax raid, back when the worst thing the crown took was coin.

Tam was already there, bleeding from a split lip, eyes wild.

"They took Marisol," Yara said. "Oskar. The twins. I heard—" Her voice cracked. "I heard your name read out."

"I went out the coal chute." Tam wiped their mouth, and their hand wouldn't steady. "I got to the corner and I looked back and the whole hall was lit up like a festival. Like they were *celebrating*." A breath. "Aldous?"

"Gone. He read our names off a list, Tam. He *had a list*. Current addresses. He told them where the register was."

Tam stared at her. Then at the ground. Then at her again, and Yara watched the same arithmetic happen in their face that had happened in Aldous's study, except Tam arrived at a different sum.

"He bought himself," Tam said flatly. "Twelve years. Twelve years of *family*, and he sold us for a pardon and probably a pension."

"I don't know that it was that simple—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to cut. Tam never raised their voice; hearing it now was like hearing a bell ring backward. "Don't defend him. Not today. Maybe someday, when Marisol's not in a cell somewhere singing to herself, you can tell me about how complicated he is. Not today."

Yara closed her mouth. Because Tam was right, and because defending Aldous was the only way she knew to keep from screaming.

They sat in the dovecote's rotting shadow while the city woke up around them, bells ringing for morning services, bakers' carts rattling past, the whole indifferent machinery of a capital that didn't know—or didn't care—that forty of its quietest residents had been torn out of it before breakfast.

"What do we do?" Tam asked finally. Smaller now. Younger. They were nineteen and had nowhere to go and everyone they loved was gone, and Yara recognized the look on their face because she'd worn it once, at fourteen, the day her mother's gift was discovered and her mother was taken and Yara learned to make her own tongue lie.

She pulled the ledger from the satchel and set it between them on the dusty boards.

"We do what the guild does," she said. "We trade in impossible things. And the most impossible thing left"—she opened the cover to the water-stained page, to the careful old script describing a border that no longer appeared on any map—"is Vessany."

Tam leaned close. "That's a story. Grandmother songs. A country that got erased."

"A country that got erased," Yara agreed, "and a door that's still in it. Look here." She traced the line she'd read a hundred times in three nights. "'The Door opens for none who flee it, and closes behind none who remain. It asks a voice that stays.'" She looked up. "Someone wrote this down knowing it cost something. Someone stayed."

Tam was quiet a long moment. "Where?"

"That's the next trade," Yara said. "We follow the songs. The ledger says memory keeps the borders—villages that still sing the old names. We find the singers, we find the road, we find the door."

"And then?"

Yara looked at the ledger, at the words she'd been circling for three nights like a moth around a flame she refused to name.

"And then we find out what 'a voice that stays' means," she said, "when it's ours."

Somewhere across the city, faint on the wind, a bell tolled—and Yara would have sworn, gift or no gift, that it rang backward.
