## 6. Roads Not on Maps

The ledger said: *Follow the song that has no second verse.*

I read that line eleven times before dawn, sitting in the ditch behind a tanner's shed with Tam asleep against my shoulder and ash still under my fingernails from the guildhall. The Vessany ledger was older than the guild itself. Its pages smelled of smoke that had nothing to do with Aldous's fire—smoke from thirty years ago, from a burning that had worked.

*Follow the song that has no second verse.*

Tam woke when the birds did. Sixteen years old, ears too big for his face, gift of hearing what people meant instead of what they said. He'd carried a sack of the guild's silver through a window at fourteen and hadn't stopped apologizing for surviving since.

"Did you sleep?" he asked.

"Enough."

"You didn't sleep."

"I heard things," I said, which was true. My gift didn't stop because my life had burned down. The ditch water told me it had been rain once. The tanner's shed told me about the man inside it, how he'd cried last night over a debt, how he'd thought about the river. The road ahead told me it was walked by fewer feet every year.

Tam nodded like this was normal, because to him it was. That was the thing about the youngest ones—they accepted the impossible faster than the rest of us. Maybe because they hadn't yet learned to be ashamed of it.

"Where are we going?" he asked.

"To where Vessany used to be."

"There's no such place."

"That," I said, closing the ledger, "is the entire problem."

* * *

We walked north through country that pretended not to remember anything.

That was the first thing I understood about an erased nation: erasure doesn't dig up the roads. It doesn't burn the bridges or drain the wells. It just makes everyone agree, very politely and very thoroughly, not to see them. We followed a cart track that every map called a sheep path, and the sheep path grew wider, and then it had milestones, and the milestones had their faces chiseled smooth—but they were still there, standing at their intervals like soldiers who'd been ordered to forget their own names.

In the village of Harrow's End, a woman sold us bread and hard cheese and refused coin for the cheese. "For travelers," she said, and her eyes went distant in a way I recognized. "My grandmother used to sing a walking song. For people going north."

"Do you know it?" Tam asked.

"I know it has no ending." She wrapped the cheese in cloth with hands that moved like they were doing something sacred and embarrassing at once. "She'd get to a certain point and just—stop. Humming. Like the rest of it was somewhere she couldn't reach. She said the song was longer when she was a girl."

I felt the ledger warm against my ribs like a held breath.

*Follow the song that has no second verse.*

At the well in the center of the village, an old man was drawing water, and as the bucket came up he hummed four notes. Just four. Then he caught himself, glanced around, and went back to silence.

Tam looked at me. I looked at him.

"Everyone here knows part of something," he whispered, when we were out of earshot. "It's like—it's like everyone's holding one piece of a plate and nobody will admit the plate existed."

"That's exactly what happened."

"How do you erase a whole country?"

"Badly," I said. "You erase it badly. You kill the archivists and rewrite the treaties and strike the name from every record you can reach. But you can't reach songs. Songs live in mouths, and mouths have children, and children don't know what they're forbidden to carry." I touched the ledger through my coat. "Vessany didn't die. It got scattered. It's been waiting thirty years for someone to put it back together."

"And we're the someone?"

"We're the someones who are still alive," I said. "Which is a start."

He laughed—a small, startled sound, the first since the raid—and something in my chest unclenched by a single degree. Grief doesn't leave. But it makes room, eventually, if you let something else sit down next to it.

* * *

Three days north, we found the first verse whole.

It was in a market town called Duncastle, in the mouth of a blind fiddler who played outside the wool factor's office. He played reels for coins, but between them, when the crowd thinned, he played something else—slow, modal, in a scale that made my teeth ache pleasantly, the way the ledger's ink made my eyes ache. A scale that didn't belong to any kingdom I knew.

Tam stopped dead in the street. People bumped past us. He wasn't hearing the music; I could tell by his face he was hearing underneath it.

"It's a list," he breathed. "Yara. It's a *list*. Rivers. There's a river in it, and a bridge, and—" His eyes were wet. "And a school. Someone's singing a school."

I steered us into the shade of the wool factor's wall and opened the ledger across my knees. The old script swam, then steadied—the way it always did when its truth was near. *The country remembers itself in parts,* the margin said, in handwriting older than the crown that erased it. *Gather the parts. The Door knows its own.*

"The Door knows its own," I read aloud.

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know yet. But the fiddler does, whether he knows it or not." I stood, crossed the square, and knelt by the blind man's case. I put a full silver mark in it—more than the fiddle earned in a week—and asked him to play the slow one again.

His face changed. Not surprise. Recognition, of a kind he'd spent a lifetime being careful never to show.

"You're going north," he said. Not a question. My gift heard what he meant beneath it: *You're one of them. Finally. Finally someone's going.*

"Yes."

"Then learn it right." And he sang the verse through, twice, making me repeat it until my tongue found the shape of words in a language that officially had never existed. Around us the market went on—cabbages, arguments, a dog stealing a sausage—and none of them heard a dead country naming its rivers through a blind man's throat.

Tam learned it too. He learned it faster than I did; his gift was listening, after all, and listening is most of language. By nightfall he had it whole, and he sang it quietly as we walked out of town on the north road, and I swear the milestones stood a little straighter as we passed.

* * *

On the fifth day the road stopped pretending.

There was no announcement. The sheep path simply became a highway, the chiseled milestones became carved ones, and the carved ones bore a word in that same lost alphabet—and my mouth, fed now on three days of song, read it without thinking:

*Vessany. 40 miles.*

Tam sat down right there in the middle of the road.

"It says it," he said. "It actually says it. Everyone always said there was never any such place, it was a story the guild told itself, and it's *right there*, cut into stone—"

"Stone keeps what paper won't," I said. "Come on. We're losing light."

But I sat down next to him anyway, because some things deserve a minute. Thirty years of erasure, and the stone had held. Somebody had cut these letters knowing what it would cost if they were caught. Somebody had decided a country was worth a chisel and a death sentence.

"Aldous would have turned back by now," Tam said quietly.

"Aldous turned back a long time ago. We just didn't see it." I got up, offered him my hand. "That's why we keep moving. Stopping is how they catch you. Standing still is how they buy you."

He took my hand and stood. For a moment he didn't let go, and his gift brushed against mine—not reading my words, reading my meaning—and whatever he found there, he had the grace not to name.

We made eight more miles before dark and sheltered in the shell of a coaching inn that had no business existing, its roof fallen in, its hearth still bearing soot from fires lit within the last month. Other travelers had come this way. Fugitives, maybe. Or pilgrims. Or pieces of a scattered country finding their way home.

Tam sang the verses we'd gathered while I fed a small fire, and in the pause where the second verse should be, the wind through the broken roof made a sound almost like humming.

Like the inn was trying to remember.

"Yara," Tam said, in the dark. "What happens when we find it? The Door?"

I turned the ledger's last legible page toward the embers' light. Beneath the maps and the marginalia, in that ancient hand, one final instruction waited—the one I'd read a hundred times and still couldn't make peace with:

*A voice that stays opens what voices that flee cannot.*

"I don't know," I lied, because he was sixteen and exhausted and had lost everything once already this month.

But far off to the north, thin as thread, we both heard it—carried on the wind down forty miles of forbidden road: singing. Many voices. One note, held.

And under it, fainter, wrong somehow, a bell ringing backward.
