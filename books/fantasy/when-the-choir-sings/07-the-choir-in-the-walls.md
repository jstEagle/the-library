## 7. The Choir in the Walls

I heard the chapel before I saw it.

That's not a figure of speech. Yara says I do that—say things that sound like poetry and turn out to be plain fact. But it was true. We'd been walking since before dawn through a valley that wasn't on any map Yara owned, and somewhere past the third mile the air changed. It thickened. It hummed. And underneath the hum, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, there was singing.

"Yara," I said. "Do you hear that?"

She stopped. She has a way of stopping that makes the whole world stop with her, like she's the hinge and everything else is the door. Her head tilted. Her hand went to the ledger strapped under her coat—the Vessany ledger, the one thing we'd carried out of the ashes of the guildhall, the one thing worth more than our lives and costing us exactly that.

"No," she said. "What is it?"

"Singing." I closed my eyes. My gift isn't like hers. Yara hears what things *are*—the true name of a soup, the honest weight of a coin, the lie folded into a lord's compliment. Me, I hear what things *want*. A lock wants to be opened. A bridge wants to fall. A candle wants, very badly, to go out. It took me years to learn which wants to listen to and which to walk away from.

This want was enormous. This want was old.

"It's coming from over there," I said, pointing at a fold of hillside where nothing stood except bracken and a broken wall. "There's people."

"There's nothing there," Yara said. Then she looked at the ledger in my satchel, and then at me, and I watched her decide to believe me, which is a thing she does more often now than she used to. "Show me."

* * *

The chapel had no roof. That was the first thing. The walls rose up out of the bracken like ribs out of a grave, grey stone furred with moss, and where the roof should have been there was only sky—grey today, patient, the kind of sky that has been watching a place for a long time without being asked to. The doorframe still stood. There was no door in it. Of course there was no door in it. Doors are for places that exist.

But the singing came from inside.

Yara heard it the moment we crossed the threshold—I saw it hit her, saw her stagger slightly and put her palm flat against the cold stone as if to steady herself against the sound. "Oh," she breathed. "Oh, Tam. They're not singing *a* song. They're singing a *name*."

I knew. I could feel it in my teeth. The choir inside the ruined nave was singing one word, over and over, layered voices braided together so that the word never quite ended—one voice finishing it as another began, a circle with no seam. And the word was:

*Vessany.*

"Keep your hood up," Yara whispered, though there was no one to see us but singers. "And whatever happens—don't interrupt them."

We walked down the aisle between fallen pews. There were perhaps thirty of them, the singers: old men with white beards, women with shawls pinned at their throats, a few children who sang with their eyes shut and their small fists clenched like they were holding on to something. None of them looked at us. None of them stopped. Their clothes were fine once—embroidered collars, silver buttons gone dull—and they were filthy now, patched, mended with mismatched thread, the clothes of people who had been wearing the same garments for a very long time.

An old woman sat nearest the altar, or what remained of an altar—a slab of stone worn smooth in the middle, polished by decades of hands. When we came close enough, her eyes opened. She kept singing. But she looked at me, and her look went straight through my skin and into the place where my gift lives, and she smiled around the word *Vessany* like she'd been expecting me for forty years.

The song ended—not stopped, *ended*, the way a wave ends when it decides to become the sea. Silence rushed in and it was louder than the singing had been. My ears rang with the shape of the absent word.

"You hear us," the old woman said. Not a question.

"I hear everything," I said, because apparently I'd decided to be brave today.

"Yes," she said. "You would. That's why you're here." She turned her face toward Yara. "And you're the taster. The one who carries the book."

Yara's hand moved toward her coat, then stopped. "How do you know about the ledger?"

"The ledger knows about us," the woman said. "It always has. Sit down, child. Both of you. My knees are done arguing with me and my voice needs the rest, and what I have to tell you goes better sitting down anyway."

* * *

Her name was Maren. She'd been a girl of nine when Vessany was erased—or unmade, or unwritten; she used all three words and they all made my skin crawl in different ways. She told it plainly, the way you tell a story you've told so many times it's gone smooth as river stone.

"There was a king who wanted our borders and couldn't take them," she said. "So he took the *name* instead. There are ways, if you're cruel enough and patient enough, to make a country forget itself. Burn the records. Kill the mapmakers. Buy the ballad-singers and have them sing other songs. Wait for everyone who remembers to die." She spread her hands. "He waited. He's still waiting. But he made a mistake, the way proud men always do."

"He didn't count the singers," Yara said quietly.

"He didn't count the *gifted*," Maren corrected. "A country isn't stone and soil, girl. A country is a story everyone agrees to keep telling. And some of us—" she tapped her own temple, right where mine hums— "some of us don't just tell stories. We *hold* them. So when they burned the maps and bought the ballads, thirty-one of us came here, to the last chapel standing, and we began to sing the name. And as long as the name is sung, Vessany cannot fully die. It hangs here, in this valley, like smoke that won't scatter. Like a held breath."

I looked around the ruin—at the moss-eaten walls, the empty doorframe, the children with their clenched fists—and I understood, suddenly and horribly, why the roof was gone and the pews were broken and the clothes were forty years of mending. They hadn't been keeping a church. They'd been keeping a *country*, and countries are heavy, and everything else gets sacrificed first.

"How long can you keep it up?" I asked.

Maren looked at me with something like pity. "We've been asking ourselves that for a decade. The answer changes. When we were thirty-one, easily. Now?" She counted silently, her lips moving. "Twenty-six. Four of those are children. Children's voices give out, Tam. And every year the song gets heavier, because the world outside forgets harder, presses down harder. Some nights I swear I can feel the whole valley leaning on my chest."

Yara had the ledger out now. She'd been flipping through it while Maren talked, and her finger had stopped on a page near the end, and her face had gone the particular shade of white I'd learned to dread—the color she'd turned in the guildhall kitchen the night she read Aldous's ledgers and found the second set of books, the ones that proved he'd been selling guild secrets for years.

"Maren," Yara said. "The Door of Vessany. The ledger calls it the Door of Returning. It says—" she stopped. Started again. "It says the door opens from the inside. It says it requires *a voice that stays*."

The silence after that was the worst silence I have ever stood in, and I have stood in the silence of a guildhall burning, so I know silences the way Maren knows songs.

"That's what it says," Maren agreed.

"And what does it mean?"

Maren didn't answer her. She looked at me instead, and her eyes were kind, and kindness was the cruelest possible choice. "Ask him. He already knows. Gifted children always know—they just spend years pretending they don't."

And the horrible thing, the thing I will carry longer than any scar, is that she was right. Because I *had* known. From the moment the choir's song ended and the silence rushed in, some part of me had been doing arithmetic I refused to look at. The song holds the country. The Door is part of the country. The Door opens outward—but a held breath must be held by someone, and if the holder walks through the door—

"The anchor," I said. My voice came out very small. "Someone has to stay and keep singing. Keep holding the name. Or the moment the last person steps through, the whole thing—" I couldn't finish it. I made myself finish it. "The whole thing comes down. The country dies, and everyone left inside dies with it, and the Door closes forever."

"One voice," Maren said gently. "One gifted voice, staying behind, singing the name into the dark so the others can walk into the light. That is the price of the Door of Returning. It was always the price. Why do you think none of us ever tried to leave?"

* * *

Yara argued. Of course she argued. She laid the ledger open on the altar slab and read passages aloud in a voice that shook only a little, hunting loopholes the way she hunted poison in the king's soup—methodically, desperately, tasting every clause for the drop of hope hidden in it. Maybe two voices could share the burden. Maybe the song could be written down and sung by many. Maybe the twenty-six survivors could simply walk out and let Vessany go, let it finally die a natural death, and live.

Maren answered each one the same way: by looking at her, sadly, and shaking her head.

"We are the country, child. All that's left of it. If we walk out, there's nothing for a Door to belong to. And the crown—" her mouth twisted around the word— "the crown that erased us is still erasing. Your Inquisitors burn gifteds now, don't they? Register or die? Where would we even *go*?"

She had us there. I thought of the guildhall, its windows glowing gold against the night, the smell of Tam-the-elder's bread, Aldous's voice saying *we are invisible, child, and invisibility is a kind of safety*—and then I thought of ash on the sill, and Seflin screaming, and the wagon with barred wheels rolling east.

"There's another thing," Maren said, when Yara finally ran out of arguments and sat down heavily on a fallen pew. "The Door doesn't open whenever it likes. It opens when the song is strongest—which is to say, soon. The valley is thinning, Tam. Can you feel it? You can. Don't lie to an old woman." She leaned forward. "Within a season, maybe less, the song will fail no matter what we do. Twenty-six voices can't hold a country much longer. Either the Door opens now, while there's still a Door, or Vessany finishes dying and takes every soul in this valley with it."

"So someone stays," I said. "Someone stays and sings alone, and buys the rest of us the road out. And then what happens to them?"

Maren was quiet a long time. Outside, the patient grey sky went on watching.

"I don't know," she said at last. "No one's ever done it. Perhaps they hold the country alone until they die, and Vessany passes with them, gentle, like a candle going out at the end of a long day. Perhaps—" and here her voice did something I'd never heard it do, something that cracked down the middle— "perhaps a single voice, truly given, freely chosen, is enough to sing the name back into the world. Enough to put Vessany back on the map. Back into memory. Perhaps the sacrifice isn't a death at all. Perhaps it's a coronation."

I felt Yara go still beside me. Not thinking-still. *Deciding*-still. I'd seen it once before, the night she handed me the ledger in the burning doorway and said *run, don't argue, run*—and I had run, and I had hated myself for running, and I swore I wouldn't watch her do it again.

"Yara," I said warningly.

"Don't," she said.

"Yara, I heard what he said too. *A voice that stays.* I'm the one who hears what things want. Let me—"

"Tam." She said my name the way you close a door. Then she looked at Maren, and at the children with their clenched fists, and at the empty doorframe where a door should be, and she said, very quietly, "Take me to it."

And far off across the valley—so far it might have been imagination, except that I never imagine things, I only hear them—a new voice joined the choir. Deep, ragged, half a mile away and closing fast, singing a name it had no business knowing.

Yara went white.

"That's Cassian," she said.
