## 8. Cassian's Choice

The order came on gray paper, sealed in black wax, and it was the shortest thing Cassian had ever been asked to sign his name beneath.

*By authority of the Crown: the persons listed herein, having failed to register under the Purification Edict, are condemned as enemies of the realm. Execution to be carried out at dawn, in the courtyard of the Vessany Road garrison, by the hand of Inquisitor Cassian Vale.*

Nine names. He read them twice, then a third time, slowly, the way you read a letter from someone who has died. Maren Ostler, keeper of songs. Dov and Ilse Perrin, twins who could mend broken glass with a breath. Old Sef, who had once told fortunes so accurately that the guild had made him stop. And at the bottom of the list, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*Tam Underhill-adjacent, apprentice. Age uncertain. Gift unconfirmed.*

Cassian set the paper down on the garrison desk and looked out the window at the courtyard where, in six hours, he was expected to kill nine people whose only crime had been belonging to something.

He had signed worse orders. That was the truth he kept reaching for, the stone he kept trying to stand on. He had signed the order that emptied the lamplighters' hall in Corvess. He had stood in the rain while three sisters who could speak to drowned things were marched onto a cart, and he had told himself the cart was mercy, because the alternative was the pyre. He had built a career out of the difference between mercy and cruelty and had never once asked whether the difference mattered to the people inside the cart.

But this list had Yara's guild in it. Yara's people. The ones she'd balanced ledgers for at midnight, the ones she'd called her family with the same quiet, stubborn certainty she'd once used to say *I don't want much, just Tuesdays*—Tuesdays being the day they'd shared a table at the Gilded Spoon and argued about whether honey belonged in tea.

He knew what she would be doing right now. Running. She'd run the night of the raid; the reports said a woman matching her description had gone east through the tannery district with a book under her coat. Every dispatch since had carried her name in its margins like a stain. *Subject possibly en route to former Vessany territories. Subject believed in possession of restricted documents.*

He had written two of those dispatches himself.

That was the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. Not that he was hunting her—he had accepted that assignment the way a man accepts a wound, knowing it will change him but telling himself he'll survive it. It was that he had done it *well*. He had mapped her likely routes. He had predicted which villages would shelter her, because he knew her, because eight years ago he had been the person she walked those roads with, back when the roads were just roads and the gift she hid was a secret she trusted him to keep.

She had trusted him. That was the sentence he kept circling. She had trusted him, and he had spent four months turning that trust into a net.

Now the crown wanted him to stand in a courtyard at dawn and put bullets—or worse, the sword, because the edict specified *by the hand*, meaning personally, meaning no soldier could be blamed for what came next—into nine people, one of whom might be sixteen years old.

He picked up the order again. Read the ninth line. *Gift unconfirmed.*

Unconfirmed meant they hadn't proven the boy was gifted. Unconfirmed meant they were going to kill him anyway, on the principle that the guild had touched him and therefore he was touched. Cassian had watched the logic of the Purification Edict metastasize for months—the way *register* had become *confess*, the way *confess* had become *name others*, the way the lists grew longer every week while the criteria for landing on them grew thinner. He had told himself it was a fever that would break. Fevers broke. He had waited for the breaking the way you wait outside a sickroom, pacing, useless, hopeful.

The fever wasn't breaking. The fever was the point.

A knock at the door. Lieutenant Brase, young, eager, the kind of officer who polished his buttons before polishing his boots. "Inquisitor. The prisoners are secured. Shall I have them brought up?"

"No." Cassian heard his own voice come out flat and final. "They stay below until dawn."

"Sir, protocol suggests—"

"Protocol," Cassian said, "suggests many things. Go count your ammunition, Lieutenant."

Brase went, looking wounded. Cassian sat alone with the lamp and did the thing he had been avoiding for four months: he let himself remember.

* * *

There had been a night, early, before everything curdled—a night in the guildhall kitchen, when neither of them should have been awake. Yara had come down for water and found him sitting at the long table with Aldous's ledgers open in front of him, because even then he'd been curious about the impossible trades, about the woman who'd sold her shadow for a winter's firewood and bought it back in spring at interest.

"You're reading our sins," she'd said.

"I'm reading your arithmetic," he'd said. "It's better than the treasury's."

She'd laughed—that low, surprised laugh she tried to suppress and never quite managed—and then she'd done the thing that had undone him ever after. She'd taken his hand, turned it palm-up, and pressed a dried fig into it. "Taste it," she'd said. "Tell me what it is."

"It's a fig."

"Taste again. Properly. Like you mean it."

And he had, closing his eyes, and the fig had tasted of something more than fig—of the orchard it came from, of the hands that dried it, of a summer somewhere far south that he had never seen and suddenly missed. That was her gift, he realized later. Not hearing poison. Hearing *truth*. Things told her what they actually were, and for one strange, sweet minute she had lent him the listening.

"That's what it's like," she'd said quietly. "All the time. Everything tells me what it is. The soup tells me. The wine tells me. People tell me most of all." A pause, and then, looking at their joined hands: "You told me. You still do."

He had thought, that night, that he understood her. He had thought understanding was the same as keeping.

Then the edict came, and the choice arrived dressed as duty, and he had chosen the uniform over the girl with the fig, and he had told himself every lie there was to tell: that he could protect her better from inside the machine. That if he took the commission, he could slow the hunts, soften the sentences, be a kind hand in a hard glove. That love was a private thing and duty was a public one and a man could hold both without dropping either.

His hands had dropped both. There was no kind version of what he'd become. There was only the version that wrote accurate dispatches and signed clean orders and told himself the courtyard at dawn would feel like justice.

He knew, now, standing at the window with the first gray light not yet risen, that it would not feel like justice. It would feel like the fig. It would taste of exactly what it was.

* * *

At half past four, unable to sleep, he went down to the cells.

The corridor smelled of wet stone and fear, that particular sourness that collects in places where people wait to die. A lantern burned at the end. The jailer had gone off somewhere—Cassian had sent him, an hour ago, to fetch documents from the records room, a fool's errand designed to empty the building of witnesses, though at the time he'd sent it he hadn't known why. He'd told himself it was routine.

Maren Ostler was awake. Of course she was. She sat on the cell floor with her back to the wall, hands folded, and when Cassian stopped at her bars she looked up at him the way you look at weather.

"Inquisitor," she said. No fear in it. Almost courtesy.

"Maren Ostler. Keeper of songs."

"That's me." She smiled faintly. "Though I'll confess my memory's going. All those verses. They crowd each other out." She shifted. "You're the one who loved her. Yara."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped stone. "I'm the one hunting her."

"Yes," Maren said mildly. "Both things can be true. Most true things are two things at once. That's rather the point of us." She nodded toward the next cell, where the boy slept—actually slept, curled on his side, breathing slow. Tam. Sixteen, maybe. The ledger-carrier's shadow, the reports said, though Cassian suspected the reports had invented that detail to have something to say. "He doesn't know what dawn is," Maren said softly. "We decided not to tell him. The twins know. Sef knows. But we agreed the boy gets one more night of ordinary sleep." Her eyes came back to Cassian and held him, gently, the way a hand holds a bird. "So whatever you've come down here to decide, Inquisitor, decide it quietly."

"I haven't come to decide anything."

"Everyone who comes down here has come to decide something." She folded her hands again. "You have the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"What arithmetic is that?"

"How many of us you can save before saving becomes treason." She said it without accusation, almost tenderly. "We do the same sums, you know. In the guild. How much can we hide before hiding becomes the whole shape of the life? How long can we sing quietly before quiet becomes a kind of dying?" She tilted her head. "You've been singing quietly a long time, I think."

Cassian said nothing. The lantern guttered. Somewhere above, a guard coughed.

"The boy," Cassian said finally. "Tam. What's his gift?"

"He hears doors," Maren said. "Isn't that marvelous? Any door. He puts his ear against it and he can tell you what's on the other side—not what's in the next room. What's on the other side of the *door*. The true other side." She laughed a little. "Aldous thought it was useless. A party trick. Then Vessany happened, and suddenly the only child in the guild who could hear what a door really was became the most important person alive." Her voice gentled further. "He's carrying something east. You know that. We all know that. Whatever she gave him, whatever book—it's why they're running toward the erased country instead of away from it. Nobody runs *toward* Vessany, Inquisitor. Unless they're trying to bring it back."

The words went through him cold and bright, and in their wake came a single image: Yara, pressing a fig into his palm. *Taste it properly. Like you mean it.*

Everything tells you what it is, if you listen. The order on his desk. The boy behind the bars. The country that had been sung out of existence and could perhaps be sung back in. His own life, which had been telling him for months what it actually was, and which he had refused, with tremendous discipline, to hear.

"I need you to do something," Cassian said.

Maren raised her eyebrows.

"When they take you up at dawn. When it happens. Don't give them anything. No names, no last verses, nothing they can wring into a confession. Can you do that?"

"We're rather good at keeping secrets," she said. "It's the family trade." Then, softer: "And the boy?"

Cassian closed his eyes. Behind them: a kitchen, a lantern, a laugh she couldn't suppress. A hand turning his palm upward. *Like you mean it.*

"The boy goes free tonight," he said.

* * *

It was disgracefully easy. That was the thing he would carry afterward, the shame of how little courage it actually required. The jailer was still gone. The rear gate of the cells opened onto the wood yard, and the wood yard gate's lock was old, and Cassian had been picking locks since he was a thief's son in Corvess, before the academy scrubbed the accent out of him and handed him a title instead of a trade.

He woke the boy with a hand over his mouth. Tam's eyes went wide, white-rimmed in the dark, and Cassian put his lips close to the boy's ear and said, "I'm the one who loved her. Which means I'm the one who owes her. Do exactly what I say and be silent as a held breath."

He walked him through the wood yard, past the sleeping sentry whose post Cassian had rotated to the far wall an hour earlier—another small corruption, another stone rolled aside—and out through the drainage gap in the north wall that the garrison maps marked as collapsed because Cassian had marked it that way himself, two weeks ago, for reasons he hadn't examined at the time.

Reasons. He was full of reasons now. They were all the same reason, wearing different coats.

At the gap, he pressed a purse into the boy's hands and a scrap of paper with a route written on it—east, along the river, the villages where the old songs still named Vessany. "Find her," he said. "Yara Underhill. Tell her—" and here his voice did something he didn't authorize, cracked down the middle, "—tell her I finally tasted it properly."

Tam stared at him. "What did it taste like?"

"Like what it was," Cassian said. "Go."

The boy went, quick and silent as smoke, and was gone into the dark, and Cassian stood alone in the drainage ditch with the stars overhead and the garrison at his back, and felt the strangest sensation: lightness. Terrible, irreversible lightness, the kind that comes after a rope is cut.

By morning, the escape would be discovered. By noon, the inquiry would begin. By evening, his name would be on a list—the lists always grew—and the man who had hunted the gifted would be hunted among them.

He straightened his coat, walked back through the wood yard, and began composing, in his head, the report that would end him: *One prisoner escaped. Circumstances unclear. Investigation ongoing.* A lie shaped like a door left ajar.

Somewhere east, a boy was running toward an erased country with a ledger under his coat, and a woman was waiting at the edge of a map, and a choir was gathering in the walls of a ruined chapel, learning a song old enough to raise the dead.

Cassian Vale intended to be there when they sang it. Even if he had to burn his own name to the ground to arrive.
