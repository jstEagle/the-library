## 10. When the Choir Sings

The Door of Vessany was not a door. That was the first lie the ledger told, and the last one it told well.

It was a threshold of air between two standing stones on a hill that no map admitted to, and it hummed like a struck bowl that never finished ringing. I could hear it with my gift—the way I heard everything's true nature—and what I heard was hunger. Not malice. Hunger, patient and old, for a voice willing to feed it.

Behind me, the soldiers' torches crawled up the valley road like a lit fuse.

"Yara." Cassian stood at my shoulder, his Inquisitor's coat traded somewhere along the way for a farmer's gray wool, and he still looked like a verdict. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't." I'd spent three hundred miles hoping there was. Hope is a luxury for people who aren't holding the answer already. "You heard them in the chapel. The Door opens from this side only if someone stays to hold it open. A voice that stays. It's not poetry, Cassian. It's an instruction."

Tam clutched the ledger against her chest like a shield made of paper. She was sixteen and had been mine since the night I pulled her out of the guildhall's smoke, and she was looking at me the way you look at a fire when you're cold—wanting it, hating yourself for wanting it.

"I can stay," she said. "I'm gifted too. I can hold it."

"You can't." I took the ledger from her gently, the way Aldous used to take the day's accounts from me, back when he was still a man worth following. "It needs a voice that knows what things truly are. That's not a gift you have yet, little bird. It took me ten years of tasting poison to learn how to listen." I smiled at her, because if I didn't smile I would have said something true and unbearable. "Besides. Someone has to write all this down properly. You're the only one of us who can spell 'inquisitor' without crying."

That got a wet, furious laugh out of her, which was the point.

* * *

The freed captives came up the hill in ones and twos—guild members Cassian had cut loose from the Inquisition's wagons three nights ago, faces I knew from candlelit ledgers and stolen suppers: Merrit the weather-listener, old Sefa who could mend broken promises, the twins whose gift I never learned because they never stopped talking long enough to use it. Eleven souls. Eleven names in my head, each one a small debt I intended to see paid.

They gathered at the stones and looked at me, and I understood suddenly why kings keep food-tasters. Somebody always has to go first, and somebody always has to know exactly what they're swallowing.

"The choir will sing," I said. "Vessany's survivors are already singing, down in that chapel, and they've been singing for thirty years whether anyone listened or not. When the Door opens, their song comes through. And when it does—" I looked at Tam. "You carry the ledger through. Every page. Because a country that exists only in memory stays only in memory unless someone writes it back into the world. That's your work now. Yours and theirs."

"And yours?" Sefa asked softly, though we both knew.

"Mine is here."

* * *

Cassian walked me apart from the others, twenty paces toward the eastern stone, where the wind came off the erased land smelling of rain that hadn't fallen anywhere in decades.

"I can testify," he said. "Against the Edict. Against the Purification. I have names, dates, orders signed in the Chancellor's own hand. I can burn the hunt down from inside the courtroom." His jaw worked. "But it won't save you. Nothing I do saves you."

"I know."

"Then let me—"

"Stay?" I touched his face, and my gift read him the way it read everything: grief over iron, love under oath, a man who had spent years mistaking duty for devotion and was only now learning the difference. "If you stay, you die on this hill with me, and Vessany gets two ghosts instead of one witness. No. You go back. You stand up in front of the crown and you tell them everything, and when they call you a traitor—and they will—you tell them again. Louder."

"You always were the brave one."

"I was always the one hiding in plain sight." I laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "This is just the same job with worse hours."

He kissed me then, once, brief as a signature sealing a document neither of us wanted to sign, and then he stepped back and became an Inquisitor again, straight-backed and terrible, because that was the shape his courage took.

* * *

The torches reached the foot of the hill.

"Open it," I said.

Tam began to sing.

Her voice was thin at first—a girl's voice, untrained, frightened—but the ledger in her arms seemed to drink the sound and give it back larger, and then Merrit joined, and Sefa, and the twins, ragged harmonies braiding together, and beneath them, rising out of the ground itself, out of the stones, out of thirty years of stubborn memory, came the choir of Vessany: hundreds of voices singing the name of a country that had been unwritten, singing streets and harvests and bell towers back into being, note by note.

The air between the stones tore open like a curtain.

And I felt it take hold of me—not painfully, nothing so kind as pain—an anchor dropping through me into the hill, my voice becoming the hinge, my hearing becoming the lock. I could feel every note the choir sang passing through me like water through a channel, and I understood at last why the ledger demanded a taster. You cannot hold a door like this unless you know, absolutely, what you are swallowing.

I swallowed. I held.

"Go," I said, and my voice came out doubled, mine and the hill's. "All of you. Now."

They went—Merrit first, then the twins still arguing, then Sefa, who pressed her forehead to mine and whispered, "We'll sing you home, girl." Tam went last. She turned at the threshold, tears cutting clean lines down her smoke-stained face, and held up the ledger.

"It'll be the first thing written," she called over the song. "Your name. First line, first page. I promise."

"Second line," I said. "First line goes to Vessany. It earned it."

She laughed and sobbed and stepped through, and the light closed around her, and the Door stayed open under my hands because I was still there, still singing, still the voice that stayed.

Below, the soldiers had stopped climbing. They stood in the torchlight with their mouths open, listening to a choir that was making their maps wrong in real time, and some of them—wept, though none of them could have said why.

* * *

I do not remember dying, if I died. I remember the song getting bigger than my body, and then I remember being the song.

Here is what I know from the far side of that moment, which is also the near side, which is every side, because a country restored to the world does not experience time the way tasters do:

At dawn, cartographers in the capital found their blank western pages filled overnight with coastline they had never surveyed. The Chancellor called it forgery. Then the second map changed, and the third, and a fishing crew came home swearing they'd sold herring that morning in a harbor town called Vessany-Under-the-Hill, where the innkeeper charged fair prices and everyone spoke with an accent no one could place but everyone somehow understood.

Cassian testified for eleven days. They stripped him of rank, then of freedom, then tried to strip him of his story, and failed, because Tam published the ledger—all of it, including the pages about him—and a country that remembers itself does not easily forget its debts. He is in a cell now, or a house arrest, depending which rumor you trust, and either way he is writing letters, and the letters are finding readers, and readers become juries, and juries become history.

And in Vessany, in the chapel on the hill where the survivors kept the name alive for thirty years, there is a new verse in the choir's oldest song. The children learn it before they learn the alphabet. It is only four lines long.

Ask any of them what it means and they will tell you: *a voice that stays is not a voice that's lost.*

### Epilogue — Tam

They gave me the guildmaster's chair, which is absurd, because I am seventeen and cannot grow a beard, and the twins say the chair makes me look like a cat sitting on a throne, which is accurate.

The Guild of Impossible Things has a country now. Let me write that again so it stays true: we have a *country*. Yara's ledger sits in the guildhall under glass, first line reading VESSANY, second line reading YARA UNDERHILL, WHO TASTED THE TRUTH AND SWALLOWED THE DOOR. Visitors touch the case sometimes, and swear the pages are warm.

Last week a girl came to us from the capital, fourteen years old, terrified, gifted—she hears what people are afraid of. She asked me, the way they all ask, whether it gets easier.

I told her the truth, because Yara taught me that a thing's true nature is the only thing worth telling. I said: no. But you will find people who make the fear worth carrying, and if you are very lucky, and very stubborn, you may one day be asked to give something back to the world that hid you.

And if that day comes, I told her, listen for the singing.

Because last month, on the anniversary, every bell in Vessany rang at sunset—and one bell more, a sound with no tower, no rope, no bronze, coming from the direction of the old border stones, clear as a struck bowl that never finishes ringing.

Sefa says it's the wind. Merrit says it's memory.

I say it's Yara, keeping the door open from the other side, waiting to see who else chooses to stay.
