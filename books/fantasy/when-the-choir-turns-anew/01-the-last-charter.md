## 1. The Last Charter

The Guildhall at Waymeet had nine chairs around its table once. Now it had three that anyone sat in, and the other six stood against the walls like mourners who had come to the wrong funeral.

Mirabel Whitlock counted them every morning. It was a habit he had never chosen and could not put down — the way a man counts the teeth left in his own mouth. Six empty chairs. Three occupied: himself, youngest of the order at twenty-six years; old Master Corren, whose hands shook now when he poured the tea but not, notably, when he drew maps; and Halvard, who had stopped speaking above a murmur since the winter two years past when the fever took the eastern chapter whole.

Nine members of the Wayfarers' Guild remained in all the world. Mirabel knew this the way he knew the number of switchbacks on the Gray Stair, or the depth of the ford at Coldwether in high summer. He carried the count with him like a stone in his boot.

The contract lay on the table between them, written on good vellum, sealed in honest wax. That alone made it remarkable. Contracts had grown rare as wild honey — the roads were patrolled now by the king's soldiers under a new seal no one could quite describe, and merchants had learned to trust walls more than guides. But here was vellum, and wax, and coin offered in advance, and a single line of instruction:

*One pilgrim. Thornfell passes. Before first snow.*

"Who is she?" Mirabel asked.

Corren turned the page as though there might be an answer hidden beneath, though they both knew there was not. "Name given as Sef. No house, no order, no letters of introduction. Paid triple rate through a broker in Lowmarsh who wouldn't say who sent him." The old man's eyes came up, gray and steady as slate. "You'll take it?"

It was not truly a question. There was no one else to take it. Halvard's knees were gone; Corren had not crossed the Thornfell line in twenty years and would not pretend otherwise. And Mirabel — Mirabel was proud of his roads the way other men were proud of their children. He knew the shepherd paths that stitched the high fells like scar tissue, the drover tunnels under the Windrow crags, the shrine-steps cut into the mountain's flank by hands so old that even the Guild's records called them *found, not built*. If a road existed between any two points in Thornfell, Mirabel Whitlock could walk it blindfolded by starlight, and half the time he had.

"I'll take it," he said, and heard the quiet pride in his own voice, and hated it a little, because pride felt like something the Guild could no longer afford.

* * *

He met her at the Lowmarsh gate at dawn, in weather that smelled of iron and coming frost.

She was smaller than he expected, wrapped in a pilgrim's undyed wool gone gray with road dust, a staff taller than she was, and a hood drawn low. She did not speak when he gave his name and bowed the small bow the Guild taught — palm flat, fingers toward the road, *I go where you go*. She only looked at him.

That was the first wrongness, though he would not understand it until later: the quality of her looking. Most clients sized up their guide the way they sized up a horse — soundness, temperament, likelihood of bolting. Sef looked at him the way a person looks at a door. As if deciding whether to open it or bar it.

Then she pulled back her hood, and he saw the scars.

They ran from her left temple down across her jaw and throat and vanished into her collar, pale and precise, too straight for any beast's claws. They looked less like wounds than like writing. He caught himself staring and made himself stop, made himself smile the professional smile.

"Mistress Sef. I'm told you don't speak. That's fine — I've guided mutes before, and monks under vows of silence, and one very stubborn goat-herd who communicated entirely in whistles. We'll manage." He tapped the map case at his hip. "Thornfell's closed to caravans this time of year, but not to us. Nine days to the north shrines if the weather holds. Twelve if it doesn't. You'll want to trust me on the twelfth-day routes, because they're the ones that keep us alive."

She nodded. Once. Slowly, deliberately, the way someone nods when they are agreeing to far more than has been said.

They went out through the gate as the sun cleared the marsh fog, and Mirabel, walking backward a few steps to watch her gait — guides learn to read feet before faces — noticed the birds.

Lowmarsh in autumn was loud with birds. Starlings in the hedgerows, gulls riding the thermals off the fen, crows conducting their eternal parliament along the fence posts. But as Sef passed, the hedge went silent. Not scattered — silent. A starling sat on the bramble not four feet from her shoulder and did not fly, did not sing, did not move at all except to turn its head and track her, eye shining black as a dropped bead, until she was past. Then the hedge woke again behind her, all at once, like a held breath released.

Mirabel filed it away. Guides file things away. A client who spooks horses, a bridge that hums in certain winds, a stretch of road where the light comes wrong at dusk — none of these mean anything alone. Together, later, they mean everything.

* * *

The first day was kind to them, which should have warned him.

They climbed out of the marsh country through beech woods turning copper, and Mirabel talked, because silence on a road is a debt that gathers interest. He told her about the roads themselves — their names, their tempers, their grudges. The Old Drover's Rise that flooded ankle-deep after rain but never washed out. The Cut Path that the Guild kept secret from the crown tax-men for two hundred years and would keep secret from them still, charter or no charter. He told her about the shrine-stones, because they'd pass several, and clients always wanted to know.

"The old god-road markers," he said, resting a hand on one as they climbed — a waist-high pillar of gray granite, lichen-crusted, carved with a symbol worn nearly smooth: a doorway, standing open. "Older than the kingdom. Older than the Guild, maybe, though we don't say that loudly. Nobody remembers what god they belonged to. The priests call them superstition. The Guild calls them landmarks." He grinned. "We call them landmarks because they're the only things on the mountain that never move."

Sef looked at the stone a long moment. Her scarred hand rose — hesitated — and then, gently, she laid her fingers against the carved doorway.

The stone hummed.

Not a vibration from the road, not wind through a hollow. A hum, low and resonant, like a wet finger on a bell's rim, and Mirabel felt it through his palm where it rested on the granite's crown, and snatched his hand back as if burned.

Sef did not snatch hers away. She stood with her fingers in the little carved doorway, head bowed, and the hum deepened — swelled — and for one impossible moment Mirabel would have sworn the stone was singing, a single sustained note like the last syllable of a word too long forgotten —

Then it stopped. The wood was quiet. The wind moved in the beeches. Sef withdrew her hand and looked at him, and her eyes over the scars were wet.

"Well," said Mirabel, when he had found his voice. "That's new."

It was not entirely new. Every guide knew the shrine-stones had their moods; the old records spoke of stones that *sang on high holy days*, whatever holy days those had been. But the records also said the singing died out generations ago, and in eight years of walking these roads Mirabel had heard nothing from them but wind.

He filed it away. The file was getting heavy.

* * *

*

They camped that night in the lee of a way-shrine, one of the roofed shelters the Guild maintained at a day's interval along the high route — dry-stone walls, a slate roof furred with moss, a hearth that drew well if you knew which corner to build in. Mirabel built the fire, set barley to swell in the pot, and watched his client sit cross-legged facing the open door of the shelter, watching the dark.

"You can sleep," he offered. "Nothing crosses the high route at night this time of year. Even the wolves go down-valley before snow."

She didn't answer. She rarely would, he was learning, but her silences had texture — some dismissive, some tired, and some, like this one, attentive. Listening to something he couldn't hear.

He ate. She ate a little. He banked the fire and rolled into his blanket, and lay listening to the mountain breathe, and found he could not sleep.

The stars through the shelter's doorless frame were hard and close. Somewhere below, a nightjar called, and somewhere else, nothing answered it — and that absence bothered him more than the day's strangeness. Roads had voices. Roads had company. This road, tonight, felt like a room where everyone had quietly left.

"Sef," he said softly, to the dark. "I'm going to ask you something, and you don't have to answer. Guides notice things. It's the trade. Your birds won't come near you. The shrine-stone sang when you touched it. And you look at the dark like you're expecting a visitor." He paused. "Whatever you're carrying to those northern shrines — I don't need to know what it is. But I need to know if it's dangerous. To you, or to me, or to anyone between here and the pass. Because my oath says I bring my client safe to the road's end. All of my client. Whatever that ends up meaning."

For a long time she was still. Then, in the firelight, she turned and looked at him, and raised one scarred hand, and pointed — not at him, not at herself, but at the darkness beyond the shelter's door. At the road. At the mountains lying black against the stars.

Then she pressed that hand flat against her own chest, over her heart, and held it there.

*What I carry is in me. What I carry concerns the road itself.*

Mirabel lay back down and stared at the rafters, and the stone in his boot — the count of nine, the six empty chairs, the weight of a Guild running out of roads to guard — seemed suddenly much lighter than the other thing he was carrying now, which had no name yet and no weight either, only a hum, low and patient, like a finger circling the rim of a bell.

Two days later, in the chapel at Greyford under the mountain's first shoulder, he would learn what she carried. He would hear a dying woman's prayer answered aloud, in a voice like many waters, and watch Sef fall to her knees and weep for the first time — and he would understand that the world's oldest rules had quietly changed while the Guild slept, and that the last contract of the Wayfarers' Guild was not a guiding contract at all.

But that night there was only the fire, and the silence, and the shrine-stone outside humming softly to itself in the dark, like something remembering how to sing.
