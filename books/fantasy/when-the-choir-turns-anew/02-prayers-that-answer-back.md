## 2. Prayers That Answer Back

The chapel of Saint Aldera-of-the-Snowline had no roof worth the name. Half its slates had slid into the ravine a generation ago, and snow came through the gaps in slow, deliberate spirals, settling on the pews like parishioners who had learned patience. Mirabel Whitlock knew this chapel the way he knew every chapel, waystone, and smuggler's cut between Thornfell's southern shoulder and the high passes — because his father had made him memorize them at an age when other boys were still learning their letters.

*Shelter at Aldera's when the wind turns east,* the old lesson ran. *But never sleep beneath the altar stone.*

He had never asked why. That was the trouble with the Guild: it taught you ten thousand rules and trusted you to carry them like stones in a pack, never weighing what each one held.

Now, with dusk coming down blue and cold through the broken roof, he watched Sef stand before that altar stone, and he understood for the first time that some of those stones had weight enough to crush him.

She had not spoken since they'd left Hollowmere. Nine days of silence, broken only by gestures — *water*, *rest*, *this way* — and once, terribly, by a sound she'd made in her sleep that was not a word in any language Mirabel knew. The scars that covered her arms from wrist to shoulder were old burns, he'd decided. Pilgrims carried wounds; it was practically a credential. He had told himself her silence was grief, or a vow, or simple stubbornness, and he had told himself he did not care. A contract was a contract. Guide the pilgrim through Thornfell before winter closed the passes. Collect the fee. Keep the Guild's ledger from bleeding another red line.

Nine members left. He'd signed the contract with hands that shook, though not from fear — from the strange, vertiginous pride of being the youngest guide ever trusted with a final commission. *The last road the Wayfarers will ever open,* Corren had said, gripping his shoulder. *Make it a good one, lad.*

So why did the birds refuse to fly over her?

He had noticed it the second day. A chough wheeling above the trail would sheer away as if struck when Sef passed beneath, crying once and climbing hard into cloud. By the fifth day the pattern was undeniable: the sky emptied around her, a moving circle of absence, as though she walked inside a bell of silence that only feathered things could hear. And yesterday, crossing the shrine-stone at Wolf's Jaw — one of the old markers, older than the Guild, older than the kingdom, its carvings worn to suggestion — Mirabel had felt it through his boot soles. A hum. Low, sustained, like a struck bowl still ringing somewhere under the mountain's skin.

Sef had laid her palm flat on the stone. The humming had deepened. And she had smiled — the first expression he'd seen on her that wasn't endurance — and looked at him with eyes that seemed briefly lit from behind, amber catching light where there was no light to catch.

*This way,* she'd signed then, pointing up the pass toward Saint Aldera's, and Mirabel, who knew these roads better than any living soul, had followed her anyway. That frightened him more than the birds.

* * *

The dying woman arrived an hour after dark.

Mirabel heard the mule first — the wrongness of its gait, a stumble-and-recover rhythm he'd catalogued across a hundred trails — and went out into the falling snow with his lantern raised. The woman on the mule's back was past seventy, wrapped in wool gone grey with road-dust, and she was not going to survive the night. He saw it the way guides see weather: in the set of her shoulders, the blue at her lips, the way her hands had stopped gripping and merely rested.

"Storm-shelter," she managed, when he lifted her down. Her accent was lowland, river-country, a long way from home. "Bless you. Bless the road."

Inside, Mirabel built up the fire while Sef brought blankets without being asked — moved through the ruined nave with a sureness that unsettled him, as if she already knew which floorboards were sound. They settled the woman in the pew nearest the hearth, the good one, oak that had outlived three congregations. Her name was Maren. She had been walking to Thornfell's northern shrines to pray for her son, who was a soldier, or had been; the word she used was *was*, and she did not explain.

Her breathing grew ragged as the fire climbed. Mirabel had sat with the dying before — it came with the roads — and he knew the particular silence that falls when there is nothing left to do but witness. He knelt beside her. Sef stood at the end of the pew, very still, snowmelt dripping from her hood.

"Is there anyone—" Mirabel began.

Maren shook her head, small movements only. Then her eyes found the altar, the great dark stone beneath its broken canopy, and something kindled in her face that Mirabel had never seen in a dying person: not fear, not peace. *Recognition.*

"She's close," Maren whispered. "Do you feel it? She's come back to the thresholds. All my life I prayed to the silent ones and got silence back. But she—" A cough took her, wet and long. When it passed she gripped Mirabel's wrist with shocking strength. "Girl. Girl with the scarred arms. You're carrying her, aren't you? I can feel the warmth off you like a hearth."

Mirabel's blood went cold. "Madam, you should rest—"

"I have been praying since Hollowmere," Maren said, louder now, her voice gathering itself like wind before a squall. "Every mile. *Lady of the Doorways, Lady of the Crossing-Over, let me see my son again, let me cross to wherever he crossed.* Every mile I prayed and every mile the prayer fell dead on the road like a dropped coin." Her breath hitched. "And tonight it didn't fall. Tonight it *went somewhere.*"

The fire flared.

Not naturally — not with any wind, for the chapel air hung utterly still. It rose straight up, a column of gold, and the shadows of the broken rafters wheeled above it, and Mirabel felt the altar stone begin to hum through the floor, through the pew, through the bones of his own feet, that same struck-bowl note he'd heard at Wolf's Jaw but deeper now, fuller, a chord instead of a tone.

And into that chord, unmistakably, came a voice.

It did not come from the fire or the altar or Sef. It came from everywhere the chapel was old — from the stones, from the mortar, from the threshold itself, the space beneath the door where outside became inside. It was a woman's voice and it was not; it was the sound a doorway makes when it opens onto somewhere you have been homesick for your whole life without knowing it.

*Maren, daughter of the river country. Your prayer was heard at every threshold you crossed. You will cross the last one warm.*

Maren wept. Not the weeping of pain — Mirabel had heard that too often — but the weeping of someone handed back something they'd buried years ago. "Thank you," she gasped. "Oh, thank you, thank you, Lady—"

*Your son waits at the crossing. He has waited patiently. Go to him.*

The chord faded the way a bell fades, by becoming part of the silence rather than leaving it. The fire settled. Snow resumed its patient spirals through the broken roof.

Maren died before midnight, gently, with a smile on her face that Mirabel would carry for the rest of his life whether he wanted to or not.

* * *

He found Sef at the threshold.

Of course she was at the threshold — standing precisely in the chapel doorway, one hand pressed to the doorpost, head bowed, and she was shaking. He thought at first she was cold. Then he heard the sound she was making, small and strangled, and understood.

"You're crying," he said, stupidly. In nine days he had built a whole architecture of assumptions about her silence, and every beam of it collapsed at once.

She turned. Her face was wrecked and open, tears cutting bright lines down cheeks mapped with old burn-scars, and her hands moved — haltingly at first, then with desperate fluency, the sign-language of the far north, which Mirabel only half knew and yet read easily enough, because grief needs no grammar.

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. You weren't supposed to hear that. No one was supposed to hear anything anymore. The gods are dead. We killed them. We were supposed to have killed them.*

"We?" Mirabel's voice came out thin. "Who is *we*, Sef?"

She looked at him for a long moment, snow swirling past the doorway at her back, and whatever she saw in his face decided her. She stepped inside, took his lantern, and used its light to trace on the flagstones — drawing with her finger in the spilled warmth, and where she traced, faintly, impossibly, the light lingered, gold lines glowing on grey stone.

A circle. A mountain range he recognized — Thornfell, drawn true, every peak. Lines running from the peaks down into the valleys, into the towns, into the capital itself, converging on a shape like a crown.

Then she drew a woman. Simple, ancient, the way figures are drawn on shrine-stones: standing in a doorway, arms open.

*Veyra,* her hands said. *Goddess of thresholds. Of doors and crossings and the moment between. Your Guild helped bind her. Four hundred years ago. Carved the binding into the mountains themselves — into the roads, the ways, the shelter-stones. Every threshold in this kingdom became a nail in her coffin.* Her fingers trembled. *I was a keeper at the last of her shrines. When the binding began to fail — when she began to wake — the shard chose me. Or I chose it. I don't remember anymore which. I carry a piece of her, Mirabel Whitlock. I have carried it since the night my village burned, and everything that prays near me can be answered, and I cannot stop it, and neither can she, and there are men coming who want to make sure she never fully wakes.*

The gold lines faded from the stones. The chapel was only a chapel again, roofless and cold, with a dead woman smiling in the pew.

Mirabel stood very still. His father's voice was in his head suddenly, reciting the old lessons: *never sleep beneath the altar stone; mark the shrine-stones but do not touch them; the Guild keeps the roads and the roads keep their secrets.* Ten thousand rules, and underneath every one of them, all along, a cage.

"The soldiers," he said quietly. "The ones burning way-shrines in the valley. The new seal on their banners — I thought it was just the king's vanity. A new reign, a new crest."

Sef shook her head slowly. Her hands, when they moved, were grim.

*It is not a crest. It is a warding-sign. Old Ossivar's mark, the god of endings, redrawn for men who think they serve only a king. They have burned eleven shrines since autumn. They are hunting the prayers themselves. And last night, at the ford, they picked up our trail.*

Mirabel went to the chapel door and looked north up the pass, where the storm was clearing off the high shoulders of Thornfell, and the stars were coming out hard and pitiless over the snowfield. Far below, down-valley, where the road bent around the lake — lights. Torchlight. Too many torches for travelers, too orderly for bandits, moving with the unhurried discipline of men following orders written in a hand that had never been human.

They would reach the chapel by dawn. Maybe sooner.

"Sef," he said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out, when everything beneath the world had shifted its weight. "Can you walk through the night?"

She wiped her face with the heel of her scarred hand, and nodded.

Then, together, they stepped over the chapel threshold — and Mirabel felt it, distinctly, unmistakably, a warmth against his boot-soles like a hand briefly clasping his ankle, as if the doorway itself wished them luck —

— and behind them, in the empty nave, the fire flared once more, and went out.
