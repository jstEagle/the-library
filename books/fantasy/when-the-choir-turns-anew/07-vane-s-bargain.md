## 7. Vane's Bargain

Aldous Vane had not slept in forty-one years.

He had learned to make the sleeplessness useful. In the small hours, while the king's body breathed its slow, borrowed rhythm in the royal bedchamber and the palace settled into creaks and murmurs like an old ship at anchor, Vane walked. He walked the colonnades with their frost-rimed glass, the archive stair where his own footsteps made no echo — Ossivar did not permit echoes; the dead god took all sound into himself and gave back only silence shaped like a voice. He walked and he counted what remained: nine Wayfarers in a guildhall that had once held three hundred. One pilgrim girl carrying a splinter of divinity through his mountains. And one binding, old as the kingdom itself, fraying thread by thread beneath the solstice moon.

He stood now at the window of the map room, watching the first snow come down over Thornfell like ash from a burned book.

"You are afraid."

The voice came from inside his own chest, which was where it always came from. Ossivar did not speak so much as settle, the way cold settles into stone. Vane had stopped flinching at it decades ago. Flinching was for men who still owned their bodies outright.

"I am *concerned*," Vane said aloud, because speaking aloud kept the boundary clear — which of them thought, which of them merely endured. "The girl has taken the second stone. The road beneath Grayhollow gave it up. Do you understand what that means? The road *remembered* him, and let him pass anyway. The Guild's works are turning against us."

*The Guild's works were always compromises,* Ossivar said. *You built cages out of courtesy.*

"We built them out of necessity." Vane's breath fogged the glass. Below, in the courtyard, soldiers drilled in the grey light, their new seals — crown over key, key over thorn — stitched on black surcoats. He had designed that seal himself, sixty years ago, in another man's handwriting. "There is a difference between a cage and a dam. You of all things should know it."

The god said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement. Or contempt. After four decades, Vane had made peace with never knowing which.

* * *

Here was the truth that no chronicle would ever record, because Vane intended to burn every chronicle that might: he had not wanted power. He had wanted, once, only to be a good servant.

He had been thirty-four years old, a chancellor's under-secretary with ink-stained cuffs and a wife named Liss who sang off-key in the kitchen garden, when the prayers began going wrong. That was the year the old shrines woke. Farmers in the Thornfell valleys knelt at stones their grandmothers had used as gateposts and heard voices answer — not the ordered liturgy of the sanctioned temples but something rawer, older, a goddess waking in fragments and reaching blindly through every threshold in the kingdom. Doors would not stay shut. Crossroads filled with mist that whispered names. A miller's daughter walked into her own hearthfire and came out the other side, unburned, speaking in a tongue dead eight hundred years, and did not stop speaking until they buried her.

Veyra, returning. Not as she had been — the gentle warder of doorways, the goddess who blessed departures and welcomed homecomings — but as a shattered thing reassembling itself without pattern or mercy. A god does not come back whole after a killing. She came back the way a fire comes back in a peat bog: everywhere at once, nowhere entirely, consuming whatever lay nearest.

Vane had gone to the archives seeking a remedy and found instead the original sin. The Wayfarers' Guild — the beloved guild, the guild of lanterns and safe passage, the guild whose charter hung in every schoolroom — had carved the first binding into the mountain bones at Ossivar's direction. They had killed her once already, or helped to, and written the deed into the very roads so that every traveler since had walked unknowing across her grave.

And Ossivar had been waiting ever since, patient as sediment, for a man desperate enough to listen.

"You will die," the god had told him, in the crypt beneath the chapel where Liss lay two days dead of the fever that had swept the capital that autumn. "You are mortal and small and you love a kingdom that will forget your name within a generation. I offer you permanence. Hold me, and I will hold the wildness down. Wear me, and I will wear the king, and between us we will keep her sleeping until the world grows wise enough to survive her."

Vane had looked at Liss's shroud. He had thought of the miller's daughter, still speaking in tongues in her grave. He had thought of the doors that would not stay shut, and of his own front door, and of the child he and Liss had never managed to have, and how there was nothing left in his house now that could be taken from him.

"Yes," he had said.

Forty-one years. He had worn three kings in that time — the old one, the father, and now Edric, hollowed gently, so gently, a little more each year, until the boy on the throne was mostly furniture. He had raised harvests against the drought years. He had turned aside two invasions by means the histories attributed to diplomacy. He had kept the shrines quiet, the thresholds dumb, the mist off the crossroads. The kingdom had known forty-one years of the longest peace in its recorded memory, and every hour of that peace had been paid for out of the coin of his own self, spent down to nothing, spent past nothing, into debt.

And now a mute girl with scars like river-mapping was walking north with a shard of the goddess in her chest, and a Wayfarer barely old enough to shave was teaching her the hidden roads, and everything Vane had bought with forty-one years of damnation was coming unraveled at the seams.

*She will not rise gently,* Ossivar murmured, reading the shape of his despair the way water reads the shape of a basin. *You remember what fragments do.*

"I remember," Vane said. "I have not forgotten anything. That is the curse you actually gave me."

* * *

The Wayfarer was being held in the east tower, in comfort, because Vane had given explicit orders regarding his comfort. Fear made men stupid; kindness made them reachable. He climbed the stair himself, unescorted, carrying a covered tray — bread still warm, cheese from the valley dairies, a flask of plum brandy that had survived three chancellors. Hospitality was a language older than any binding, and Mirabel Whitlock, whatever else he was, had been raised in that language.

The young man sat by the window with the particular stillness of someone measuring distances to the ground. When Vane entered, he rose — manners intact even now, which pleased Vane more than he admitted — and watched him with eyes the color of wet slate.

"Chancellor," Whitlock said.

"Master Whitlock." Vane set the tray on the table between them. "I'm told you haven't eaten. I'm told you've asked three times whether the girl is warm enough. You'll be glad to know she is. Better fed than my garrison, in fact. I take care of what matters to me."

"She isn't yours to care for."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly, and sat, and poured two measures of the brandy though he could not taste it — had not tasted anything since the crypt, since Ossivar took his tongue's allegiance along with the rest of him. "Neither is she yours. That is rather the heart of our difficulty, isn't it? She belongs to something that wore a thousand bodies before hers and will wear a thousand after. You've seen what the shard does. The chapel at Hollowmere. The woman whose prayer was answered so thoroughly she died of joy. Was that mercy, Master Whitlock? Or was it a god practicing with instruments it hasn't held in eight centuries?"

The young man's jaw tightened. Good. Doubt was already in him; Vane had read it in the archive reports, in the way the boy had hesitated at the vault door before opening it. Loyalty was a rope, and doubt frayed ropes, and all Vane needed to do was pull at the right strand.

"Let me tell you about your guild," Vane said. "Not the version in the schoolrooms. The true one. Your founders stood in the roots of Thornfell with chisels and covenant-ink and they wrote the binding into the living stone, and they knew — they *knew*, it is in the first archive, sealed under the guildhall floor — they knew what they were burying and why. Every safe road you have ever walked, every pass your lanterns made navigable, every traveler brought home alive through the snow — all of it rests on that foundation. Your guild is not innocent, Master Whitlock. It is *load-bearing*. And if the girl reaches the third stone, the whole edifice comes down, and what rises from underneath will not be the gentle goddess of your grandmother's hearth-blessings."

"You could just kill us," Whitlock said quietly. "You have the soldiers. Why the bread and the brandy?"

Because you are the last honest thing in this kingdom, Vane did not say. Because I was once a young man with ink-stained cuffs who believed the roads belonged to everyone.

"Because I am offering you a bargain," he said instead, "and bargains require live counterparties."

He laid it out the way he had laid out treaties, term by term, letting each one land. The Guild restored — charters renewed by crown writ, halls reopened in every city, the nine survivors elevated and the order endowed in perpetuity. The archive opened, the old sin acknowledged publicly, the Wayfarers' name cleansed by truth rather than erasure. And then the last term, the one he had saved, the one he watched the boy receive like a blade slipped between ribs:

"And your father's name cleared. Restored to the rolls. A pension to his family, a monument at the pass where he fell, and the record amended to show what actually happened on that road — that he stayed with his client, that he died keeping faith, and that the guild's censure of him was written by men protecting their own reputations." Vane leaned forward. "I have read that file, Master Whitlock. I know what they wrote about him. I know what you have carried since you were twelve years old. Give me the girl, and I will give you back your father's honor. No one else alive can make that offer. No one else alive even knows it needs making."

The silence stretched long. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the drill-yard called its counts, faint as a pulse.

Whitlock looked at the brandy he hadn't touched. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "And her? What happens to her, in your version?"

"The shard removed. Contained, as it was contained before — carefully, by people who understand the work. The girl herself freed. She is a victim in this, Master Whitlock, whatever she believes she carries. I bear her no malice." This was even true, or near enough to true that Vane could hold it in his mouth without choking. "It is not her I want. It is what wants *her*."

"And if I refuse?"

Vane rose, unhurried, and gathered his cloak around him. At the door he paused.

"Then at solstice, I do what I have done for forty-one years, which is whatever the survival of this kingdom requires. I would prefer not to. I have grown tired of necessary things." He let that sit, then softened his voice to something almost paternal. "You have until the moon clears the ridge tomorrow night. Think of your father. Think of the roads. Think of every traveler who will ever again walk safely because your guild endures — and ask yourself whether one girl's certainty is worth more than ten thousand strangers' safe arrivals home."

He left the tray. In the corridor, descending, Ossivar stirred inside him like sediment shaken in dark water.

*He will refuse.*

"I know," Vane said.

*Then why offer?*

"Because when he refuses, he will know exactly what he refused. And a man who chooses with open eyes cannot spend the rest of his life telling himself he was deceived." Vane reached the bottom of the stair and pushed open the door into the falling snow. "I was deceived, old thing. By you, and by my own grief. I will not put that weight on anyone else. Whatever we do next, we will do it witnessed."

*Sentiment,* the god said, with something that in a living throat might have been wonder. *After all this time. You are still, underneath it, the man from the kitchen garden.*

Vane pulled up his hood and walked out into the white.

Behind him, high in the east tower, a window opened — quietly, the way a threshold opens — and a young man began, with a Wayfarer's patience and a thief's hands, to climb down the outside of the world.
