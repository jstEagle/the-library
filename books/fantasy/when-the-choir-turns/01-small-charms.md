## 1. Small Charms

The trick to selling luck in a city where luck was illegal, Hazel had learned, was to make it look like work.

Anyone could tie a knot. That was the whole genius of it. A knot was just string and patience, and patience was free. So every evening as the sun went down behind the salt-works and Vellsmark's night market lit itself lamp by lamp like a slow constellation, Hazel Eriksen set out his little folding table at the corner of Cooper's Row, arranged his wares with the studied carelessness of a man who had thought about the arrangement for an hour, and became, for six hours, exactly what he appeared to be: a hedge-charmer of no particular talent selling trinkets of no particular power.

He even said so. Repeatedly. It was practically his sales pitch.

"Knots for luck," he'd call, in a voice pitched somewhere between a market crier and an apology. "Well—not luck, of course. Luck is proscribed under Article Nine, we all know that. Call it *encouragement*. A knot for encouragement. The string remembers being tied well, that's all. Nothing in it but good craftsmanship."

The guards on market duty had long since stopped listening. That was the other half of the trick: be boring. Be so relentlessly, aggressively mundane that the eye slid off you like rain off oiled canvas. Three years Hazel had worked that table, and in three years not one grey coat had given him more than a glance—and most glances had been aimed at his kettle, because he kept a kettle going and sold cups of spiced cider at a loss, which made him popular with the cold and forgettable to everyone else.

His fever-salves were the same story. Beeswax, goose fat, comfrey, a whisper of camphor. He'd say the recipe out loud if anyone asked, and people did ask, because Vellsmarkers were suspicious by nature and occupation had made them suspicious by policy. "Comfrey," he'd tell them, "and beeswax, and don't ask me for anything stronger, because stronger doesn't exist." And they'd buy two jars, because their grandmother had sworn by them, and grandmothers outranked Articles.

It was, all things considered, a good life. A small one, carefully folded up small like one of his knots, but good. He had a room above a chandler's shop that smelled permanently of tallow and honesty. He had enough coin. He had, most precious of all, nobody's attention.

Hazel Eriksen had spent three years earning the right to be overlooked, and he intended to die of old age in perfect obscurity, mourned by perhaps four people and one extremely opinionated cat.

Which is why, when the woman came to his table that evening with her dying son in her arms, every instinct he owned stood up and screamed at once.

* * *

She arrived near the end of the market's first bell, when the crowd was thickest and the lamplight was doing its best against the river fog. Hazel noticed her the way you notice weather—a pressure change, a wrongness in the air. She moved through the crowd like a woman who had stopped seeing any of it, a bundle clutched to her chest that was too still to be a sleeping child and too small to be anything else.

She stopped at his table. She stared at the knots.

"Madam," Hazel said gently, "if it's medicine you're after, the physician's house on Tanner's Lane has a night bell—"

"They won't come." Her voice was flat as slate. "I've knocked twice. They want payment in advance and I—" Her jaw worked. "I have four coppers and a button."

Hazel looked at the bundle. At this distance, in this light, he could see the child's face: greyish, sweat-slicked, the shallow fast breathing that every adult in a port city knew the look of and dreaded. Fever, and a bad one. The kind that took children between one dawn and the next and left mothers standing in doorways holding empty blankets.

Every rule he lived by told him to point her toward the charity ward of Saint Aldric's, three streets over, and let the Sisters do what the Sisters did. Every rule he lived by also knew the charity ward was full—it was always full, this time of year, with the lung-rot coming off the marshes—and that the Sisters turned away as many as they took.

"Four coppers and a button," Hazel repeated. His hands were already moving, damn them, reaching for the salve jar before his brain finished voting. "What's your name?"

"Ilse. Ilse Brandt. This is Tam."

"Well, Ilse Brandt. The button I can't help you with—I'm a charmer, not a jeweler—but the coppers will cover the salve and a cup of cider, and I'll tell you honestly what the salve can and can't do. It can bring a fever down some. Ease the breathing. It is *not* a miracle, and anyone who tells you different is a liar or worse."

Ilse counted out the coppers with shaking fingers. As she did, her lips moved—fast, silent, automatic, the way a person prays when praying has become as natural as blinking. Hazel caught only a fragment of it, a half-breath of sound:

*—please, whoever's listening, please—*

And something in the fog behind her seemed to lean closer.

Hazel would spend the rest of his life trying to describe that moment to himself, and never quite manage it. It wasn't a sound. It wasn't a light. It was the sensation—faint as a memory of a memory—that the night had *ears*, and that something vast and patient had just shifted its weight, the way an old dog lifts its head when it hears its name after years of silence.

He handed over the jar with steady hands. His hands were very good at steadiness; he'd trained them like a man trains a nervous horse.

"Half a spoonful, rubbed on the chest and throat, every two hours through the night. Keep him warm but not swaddled—if he sweats through, change the linen. Water, small sips, as much as he'll take." He hesitated, then heard himself add: "And Ilse—talk to him. Fevers are strange country. A familiar voice is a rope back out of it."

"It's just grease and herbs," she whispered. But she was clutching the jar like a talisman, and her eyes had gone from flat to desperate, which was somehow worse. "You're sure there's nothing—"

"There's nothing," Hazel said firmly. "Nothing at all. Craftsmanship and comfrey. Go on now, home with you."

She went. Hazel watched her thread into the crowd, and he told himself the tightness in his chest was nothing but ordinary human worry, the kind any decent man felt watching a sick child carried off into the fog.

He almost believed it.

* * *

The rest of the evening passed the way his evenings usually did. A fisherman bought a knot "for the nets"—encouragement for the nets, Hazel corrected automatically, and the fisherman rolled his eyes fondly because everyone in Cooper's Row knew Hazel's speech by heart and found it comforting, the way you find a church bell comforting. Two apprentices bought cider and argued about whether the usurper's new tax on lamp-oil was tyranny or merely theft. An old man named Piet sat on Hazel's spare stool for an hour, as he did most nights, not buying anything, narrating the market like a man commentating on a war.

"And there goes Marta Voss pretending she isn't looking at the wool-seller's son, and there's the wool-seller's son pretending he hasn't noticed, and there's Marta's mother noticing *everything*—"

"Piet, you should charge admission."

"I should charge *you* rent, boy, I've sat on this stool so long." Piet leaned in, lowering his voice to the register he used for conspiracy theories. "You hear about the Wellers' field? Out past the mill?"

"The blighted one? Everyone's heard about it. Whole crop gone black in a week."

"Gone green again." Piet's eyebrows performed an acrobatic routine. "Overnight. Old Weller swears he prayed over it—properly prayed, out loud, first time since the Occupation. Swears on his mother's grave the ground *warmed under his knees*."

Hazel laughed, because laughing was the correct response, and the correct response was armor. "Old Weller also swears he once wrestled a river-troll off the ferry dock. The troll got away, mind. They always get away."

"You can laugh." Piet tapped the table with one knuckle, suddenly serious in the way drunks and prophets are serious. "But my knee's been telling me a storm's coming for three days, and my knee's never wrong about storms."

"Your knee predicted the tax riot of '09 and it was a Tuesday market dispute."

"A *large* Tuesday market dispute." Piet settled back, satisfied, and returned to his commentary.

Hazel poured himself the last cup of cider and drank it slowly, and did not think about the way the fog had leaned. He was very skilled at not thinking about things. It was, along with knotwork, his principal art.

* * *

He was packing up—the knots into their tin, the kettle wrapped in its cloth, the table folded onto his shoulder—when Ilse Brandt came running back down Cooper's Row.

She was running *toward* him, which was the first wrong thing. People ran away from the market at closing, home to suppers and sickbeds. Nobody ran back. And she was running with her face transformed, tears cutting tracks through the grime, and she was shouting his name—or rather, shouting "*the charmer! Charmer!*"—and heads were turning all down the row, and Hazel felt the bottom drop out of his stomach like a trapdoor opening.

"He's cool." She reached the table, breathless, and gripped his sleeve with both hands. "Tam—he was burning up when I got home, I gave him the salve like you said, and inside an hour the fever just—*broke*. Like a wave going out. He asked for bread, Hazel. He asked for *bread* and ate two slices and fell asleep sweating and *breathing*, really breathing—"

"That's—" Hazel's voice came out strange. He cleared his throat. "That's the comfrey. It does that sometimes. Fast responders, some children. It's in none of the books, but—"

"I prayed." She said it simply, staring at him, and the whole row seemed to go quiet around the words. "While I rubbed it on. I prayed harder than I've prayed since I was a girl. I prayed to whatever would listen. And the fever broke, and Weller's field came back green, and old Sanna's grandson—they pulled him out of the canal this morning, blue as ink, and he coughed up half the harbor and *lived*—" Her grip tightened. "Charmer. What did you put in that jar?"

"Goose fat," Hazel said. "Beeswax. Comfrey. Camphor, a whisper. I told you the whole recipe, Ilse, there's nothing hidden in it—"

"Then why," she whispered, "did it feel like someone answered?"

The lamps guttered, all down Cooper's Row at once, a single soft shudder of flame—and then steadied. Somewhere across the city, a church bell that hadn't rung in years gave one clear note and fell silent, as though embarrassed.

Piet, on his stool, said quietly: "Knee says storm."

Hazel smiled at Ilse Brandt, and it cost him everything he had, because the smile had to be warm and puzzled and faintly amused, the smile of a simple craftsman whose herbs had done their honest work. "It felt like someone answered because you were a mother at midnight with a sick child. Mothers hear answers in the wind and the tide and the creak of a floorboard. Thank the comfrey, and thank yourself—you did the hard part, sitting up with him. Now go home and sleep, both of you, and come tell me in a week how he's mending."

She hugged him—sudden, fierce, smelling of woodsmoke and fear fading into hope—and ran off again, lighter than she'd come.

Hazel stood in the emptying market with his folded table on his shoulder and did not move for a long moment.

Because he had felt it. When she'd gripped his sleeve and spoken of prayers answered, he had felt it again, that leaning-in of the dark, that vast attention turning toward Cooper's Row like a lighthouse beam sweeping toward a rowboat. Something old was awake in Vellsmark. Something was listening to the prayers of fishwives and blighted farmers, and it had brushed past *him* tonight, close as a hand on the shoulder, and it had known—

Hadn't it? Had it known? Or had he only imagined that too, the way Ilse heard answers in the wind?

Three years. Three years of knots and cider and calculated boredom, of being no one so thoroughly that even he sometimes believed it. And in one evening, a mother's gratitude had put his name in a dozen mouths along Cooper's Row—*the charmer, the charmer whose salve broke the fever*—and a dead bell had remembered how to ring.

Hazel walked home through the fog with his table on his shoulder, and above the chandler's shop, lying awake in the tallow-smell dark, he made himself a promise with the shape of a knot in it: tomorrow he would visit Magda. Tomorrow he would ask her, carefully, casually, what she'd heard.

Because Magda always heard everything. And because if the old gods were answering prayers again, then the last living vessel of the Choir needed to know—

Before someone else told him first.
