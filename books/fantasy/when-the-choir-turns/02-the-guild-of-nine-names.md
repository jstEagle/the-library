## 2. The Guild of Nine Names

The Guild of Nine Names had once occupied an entire street in the Salt Quarter—three townhouses and a courtyard with a fountain shaped like a weeping saint, back when the guild could afford saints and the city could afford to let them keep one. Now it was one door at the end of Tanner's Row, wedged between a chandler who burned tallow that smelled like regret and a cobbler whose shop sign read simply SHOES, REPAIRED, MOSTLY.

Hazel stood in front of the door for longer than he meant to, working up his courage the way he worked up everything: slowly, and with a great deal of internal argument.

*She'll know,* said the sensible voice in his head, the one that sounded suspiciously like his mother. *She always knows.*

*That's why I'm here,* he told it.

*That's why you're afraid of her,* it corrected.

He knocked anyway. Three raps, then two, then one—the old pattern, drilled into every apprentice before they were allowed to sweep the floor. It was supposed to be a greeting. These days it was mostly a habit, like leaving out milk for a cat that had been dead for years.

"Come in before you freeze, boy," came Magda's voice from within, "and don't think I didn't hear you standing out there rehearsing."

The interior of the last house of the Guild of Nine Names was a museum that had given up on being dignified. Shelves sagged under jars of dried herbs labeled in three dead hands. A stuffed marsh-owl perched on the mantel with one glass eye and one button, staring at visitors with what Hazel had always felt was unfair judgment. The air smelled of rosemary, beeswax, and the particular staleness of rooms where someone has lived alone too long to notice.

Magda sat in her chair by the fire, small and round and wrapped in a shawl the color of old onions, mending a sock with the fierce concentration of a woman who considered knitting needles a legitimate weapon. She was seventy-something, though she claimed sixty-eight with the same conviction she claimed the guild still existed.

"You look terrible," she said without looking up.

"Good morning to you too."

"It's nearly noon." She bit off her thread. "And you look like a man who hasn't slept, which means either you've been drinking, gambling, or thinking. Given that you have no money and no sense, I'm guessing thinking."

Hazel hung his coat on the peg by the door—a peg that had held the coats of nine generations of charmers—and lowered himself onto the bench across from her. "I sold a luck-knot last night," he said.

"A thrilling career you've built. Shall I alert the historians?"

"Magda."

"I'm listening." But she set down the sock, which from Magda was the equivalent of standing at attention.

He told her about the mother. Ilse. The boy with the fever, four days burning, eyes gone glassy, the physician charging more than the family earned in a month. How Hazel had pressed his best fever-salve into her hands—not his most potent, he'd been careful about that, just his *best*, the one with a little extra of the good stuff folded in—and how she'd clutched it and prayed, right there in the market, aloud, to whatever was left listening.

"And the fever broke," Magda said. Not a question.

"By morning. Completely. The physician is calling it a miracle. Half the Fishmarket is calling it a miracle. And I—" He stopped, started again. "When she prayed, I *felt* it. Like a bell rung somewhere deep underwater. I haven't felt anything like that since—" Since the night his grandmother died, when the whole guild's inheritance had come down on him like a roof falling in, and he'd spent three years since pretending it hadn't.

Magda was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. The owl judged him.

"How many others?" she asked finally.

"That's why I came. I've heard things all week. Old Piet the fisherman swears the sea gave back his nets after he promised Saint Aldric a candle. There's a woman on Cooper's Lane whose blighted well ran sweet overnight. And last night, at the market, a carter told me his lame horse walked sound by dawn after he'd begged anything holy to fix it." Hazel rubbed his face. "It's everywhere, Magda. Prayers are being *answered*. In Vellsmark. Under occupation. With the proscription posters still on every corner."

"Yes," said Magda.

Just *yes*. No elaboration, no lecture, none of the winding stories she usually used instead of answers. Hazel stared at her.

"*Yes?* That's it? The gods are waking up after three hundred years of silence and you say yes like I've asked if you want tea?"

"Do you want tea?" She was already reaching for the pot.

"Magda."

"There's chamomile. There's also the black stuff Corvin's people smuggle in from the coast, but it tastes like boiled boots, so."

"*Magda.*"

She poured two cups anyway, because Magda believed no conversation of consequence should happen without something warm in your hands, and handed him the chamomile. Then she sat back, laced her fingers over her belly, and looked at him with eyes that were older than her face, older than the guild house, possibly older than the city.

"The rumors are true," she said. "Prayers are being answered. All over the free cities, not just here. And you're right that it shouldn't be possible, because the gods are bound, and bound things don't answer." She sipped her tea. "Unless someone is ringing the bell."

"What bell?"

"The one you felt, boy. When Ilse prayed. You described it yourself—a bell underwater. What do you think that was?"

Hazel looked down into his cup. "I assumed it was... me. My blood doing what it does. Hearing a prayer the way a tuning fork hears a note."

"Mm. And what happens," Magda said gently, "when a tuning fork is struck near another fork tuned to the same pitch?"

The question sat there. Hazel felt something cold walk up his spine.

"One rings the other," he said slowly.

"One rings the other." She nodded. "You're not just hearing the prayers, Hazel. You're part of why they're being answered. The bloodline doesn't listen. It *carries*. Every vessel alive is a strand in a rope, and when enough strands start humming..." She shrugged, a small motion that somehow contained the entire collapse of his understanding of the last week.

"But there aren't any vessels left. That's what everyone said. The purges took the great families, the war took the rest, and the gift thinned out generation by generation until—"

"Until it pooled," Magda said, "in the last few vessels strong enough to hold it. Yes. I know exactly how many of us are left, because I've spent thirty years counting them as they died. There was Anselm in Drossen Hollow, dead eleven years. The twins in Carrowmere, dead six. Old Yorick, who drank himself to death rather than feel what you're feeling, bless his cowardly heart. And then there was me." She raised her cup slightly, a toast to herself. "Who is too old and too tired to hum properly anymore. Which leaves—"

"No," Hazel said.

"—the youngest vessel the guild ever recorded, trained by the finest teacher it ever produced, hiding in a night market selling luck-knots—"

"No. Magda, I sell *knots*. I put a thumbprint of craft in them and nothing else. I have spent three years being aggressively, boringly mundane. I file my taxes. I attend the ward meetings. Last month I complained about drainage to a clerk of the usurper's administration and he *liked* me."

"I know. It's disgusting. I've never been prouder." But her humor fell away, and what was underneath made Hazel's stomach tighten. "Boy. Listen to me now, because I'm going to say this once, and I'm only saying it once because repeating it would frighten you more than saying it."

"Everything you've said so far has frightened me more than—"

"*Never pray aloud.*"

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Even the fire seemed to hush.

"Not a whisper, not a mutter, not a joke to your friends about hoping it doesn't rain on market day. Not 'gods preserve us' when you stub your toe. Nothing. Because a prayer from a vessel isn't a request, Hazel. It's a summons. The bound ones can't reach through on their own—but a vessel praying aloud is a hand opening a door from the inside. And there are things on the other side of that door that have been waiting three hundred years, and they are not patient, and they are not kind, and they remember *exactly* who shut them out."

Hazel set down his tea because his hands had begun to shake, and he didn't want Magda to see, and he knew she'd see anyway. "You're telling me my grandmother's bloodline is a prison," he said. "Not a gift. A prison. And we're the locks."

"I'm telling you it's both, which is worse." Magda leaned forward, and for a moment the shawl and the sock and the onion-colored warmth fell away, and Hazel saw the woman who had taught half the surviving charmers of the Salt Quarter, the woman the inquisitors had hunted twice and failed to catch twice. "The guild served the gods once. Truly served. And then the guild learned what the gods were, and what they did to the world when they walked freely, and our founders made a choice that has been eating this family line alive ever since. You'll learn the rest when you're ready. Or when it's too late. Those tend to be the same moment."

"That's ominous."

"I'm an old woman. Ominous is my prerogative." She picked up her sock again, then put it down, which Hazel recognized as a bad sign. "There's more. The answering prayers—they're getting stronger. Louder. Whatever's ringing the bell is ringing it harder each day. And the usurper's people aren't deaf, whatever else they are. Vane's clerks collect reports the way other men collect debts. Sooner or later—and I'd wager sooner, knowing Vane—someone is going to draw a line from a broken fever in the Fishmarket to the charming young man who sold the salve."

Hazel's mouth went dry. "Ilse wouldn't—she wasn't—she was grateful, she wouldn't tell anyone where—"

"People tell the truth when they're grateful, boy. It's gratitude that loosens tongues, not malice. Malice keeps secrets. Gratitude writes letters." Magda's eyes were steady on him. "You need to decide what you're going to do when the grey coats come asking. And you need to decide it *before* they come, because men who decide in the moment decide badly."

"I'll lie."

"You'll lie badly. You blush when you lie. Your ears go red as festival lanterns. I've watched you do it since you were seven."

"I'll practice."

"Hazel." She reached over and took his hand, her grip surprisingly strong, her skin paper-dry and warm. "I watched your grandmother carry this same weight, and her mother before her, and I watched what it did. You wanted nothing to do with any of it—I know, I've always known, it's why I let you run, God forgive me. But wanting nothing has never once stopped the world from wanting something out of you. The obligation comes with the blood. It's not a debt you can decline. It's only a debt you can pay late, with interest."

Outside, the chandler's cart rattled past, and somewhere down the row a dog barked at it with tremendous commitment. Ordinary sounds. Occupied-city sounds. Hazel held his teacher's hand and felt, beneath the fear, the thing he'd been running from for three years settle onto his shoulders like a yoke fitted precisely to his neck: the sense that he was a knot in something vast, tied long before his birth, and that knots exist to hold things together—or to be cut.

"If I pray," he said quietly, "even once, even by accident—"

"Then you'd better mean it," Magda said, "because they will answer. They always answer. That was never the problem." She released his hand and retrieved her tea, and just like that she was a small old woman again, wrapped in onions, judging him mildly from behind her cup. "Now finish your chamomile and take some salve for your hands. You've been chewing your nails like a nervous goat."

He stayed another hour. She fed him barley soup and told him three stories about his grandmother, two of which were probably true, and made him check her chimney flue and rehang a crooked shelf, because Magda expressed affection exclusively through chores. It was almost enough to make him forget the cold thing coiled under his ribs.

Almost.

He was pulling on his coat at the door when she spoke again, quietly, without looking up from her mending.

"Hazel. One more thing. The bell you felt in the market—you said it rang *deep*. Deeper than your grandmother's hearing ever went?"

He nodded.

Magda's needle paused mid-stitch. For the first time since he'd arrived, she looked genuinely afraid, and that frightened him far more than anything she'd said.

"Then it's not just the bound ones stirring," she said. "Something below them is turning over in its sleep. And boy—if the prayers start being answered by *that*—" She shook her head. "Go home. Lock your door. And whatever you hear in the next few days, whoever comes knocking—do not, under any circumstances, answer with a prayer."

He walked home through streets already whispering about miracles, and behind him, faint as a bell underwater, he could have sworn he heard something singing.
