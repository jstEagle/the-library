## 3. What the Mother Knew

Ilse Brandt had never been a lucky woman, but she had always been a practical one, and practicality had carried her through the death of a husband, the loss of a fishing boat, and eleven years of raising Tam alone on mending work and stubbornness. So when her son's fever broke on the third night — broke all at once, like a knot slipping loose — she did not weep with relief the way the neighbors' wives would have. She sat very still on the edge of his cot, watched him breathe, and thought: *That is not how fevers behave.*

Fevers were honest things. They climbed, they lingered, they broke slowly, grudgingly, leaving a child limp as wet laundry for days afterward. This one had simply *left*. One moment Tam was burning so hot that the candle flame near his brow seemed to bend away from him, and the next he was asking for bread and honey in a voice so ordinary it made her laugh out loud, which frightened him, because mothers laughing at three in the morning for no reason was its own kind of terrifying.

"Tam," she said carefully, "how do you feel?"

"Hungry." He said it the way only a seven-year-old can say hungry — as an accusation, a grievance, a moral outrage.

Ilse fed him bread and honey and watched him eat every crumb, and the whole time a small cold voice in the back of her head kept whispering about the charm.

* * *

The charm had cost her two coppers and most of her pride.

She hadn't wanted to go to the night market. Night markets were where desperate people went, and Ilse had spent her whole life being careful not to be desperate, or at least not being seen to be. But Tam had been three days in the fever-sweat, and the physician's boy had come by, looked at the child, looked at her empty purse, and said something gentle and useless about rest and prayer before hurrying off to houses that could pay.

So she'd gone down to the Lantern Row stalls with her shawl pulled low, past the fishwives and the knife-grinder, to a young man selling luck-knots from a folding table that wobbled on a folded sack.

He had been — there was no other word for it — *charming*. Not handsome exactly, though he wasn't hard to look at, with those dark eyes and that way of listening as though what you said mattered. Charming the way a hearth is charming. He'd taken her hands in both of his when she told him about Tam, and his palms had been cool and dry, and he'd said, "Fever, is it? High?"

"Like a stove."

"And three days."

"Three days tomorrow."

He'd nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he's doing arithmetic he doesn't want anyone to see. Then he'd rummaged in a wooden box and come up with a knotted cord, grey wool, nothing special, the kind of thing she could have made herself if her fingers weren't half-ruined from needlework.

"Fever-knot," he said. "Tie it above his wrist. Left side. And Ilse—" he'd known her name somehow; she'd never told him; she'd decided not to think too hard about that, "—you should know I sell these mostly as comforts. A mother sleeping beside a sick child does more good than any knot. The knot just helps you believe it."

He'd charged her one copper. She'd insisted on two, because it felt wrong to pay less than the thing was worth, whatever the thing was, and he'd tried to give the second back and she'd refused, and they'd had a small polite argument about it in front of the pickle stall until he'd surrendered with a smile and said, "Then take this too," and pressed a twist of dried herbs into her hand. "For you. Chamomile. You look like someone who hasn't slept."

And now her son was eating bread and demanding more, and the fever was gone as if it had never been, and Ilse sat in the dark kitchen with the chamomile untasted and let herself finally think the thought she'd been avoiding:

*It worked.*

Not comforted-me-so-I-slept-and-my-sleep-helped-him worked. *Worked* worked. Impossible, sudden, against-every-fever-that-ever-was worked.

She should have burned the knot. That was the sensible thing. Everyone knew the proscriptions — no workings, no charms, no hedge-craft, not since the Usurper's men had strung old Wrenna the herbwife from the mill bridge six years back. Ilse had crossed herself at the funeral like everyone else and gone home and said nothing, because saying nothing was how you stayed alive in Vellsmark.

But she didn't burn the knot. She held it instead, turning it over in her ruined fingers, feeling the little bumps of the knots under her thumb, and what she felt — God forgive her, what she actually felt — was *gratitude*, huge and helpless and glowing, the kind you feel toward a person who has handed your child back to you from the far shore of a river you couldn't cross.

And gratitude, Ilse would learn, is a dangerous thing in a city full of ears.

* * *

Three days later, the clerk came to the door.

He wasn't frightening, which was the worst part. She'd expected grey coats and boots and a voice like a slammed door. Instead there stood a soft-bodied man of maybe fifty with ink stains on his fingers and spectacles that kept sliding down his nose, holding a ledger against his chest the way a schoolboy holds his slate.

"Ilse Brandt?" His voice was mild as porridge. "Clerk Oster, Office of Inquiry. Just a census follow-up. May I trouble you for a few words?"

A census follow-up. In Vellsmark, the census followed you the way a dog follows a butcher. But you did not refuse a clerk at your door; refusing was its own confession. She stepped aside and put the kettle on, because hospitality was armor, and poured him tea she could ill afford, and he drank it with genuine pleasure and complimented her cups, which were chipped, and asked after her health, and her work, and whether she was managing since her husband passed, and it was all so gentle and so human that Ilse nearly forgot to be afraid.

Nearly.

"It's about your boy," Clerk Oster said at last, setting down his cup. "Tam. He was ill, I understand. Fever."

"He's well now." Her voice came out flat as a shutter closing. "Thanks be to the All-Mother."

"Mm." He smiled over his spectacles. "The neighbors say the fever broke very fast. Unusually fast. Old Marta next door swears she heard the boy crying for bread at dawn after three days of burning, and she says — and I quote her, Mrs. Brandt, because I write everything down, it's my whole profession — she says, 'That child came back from somewhere.'"

"The All-Mother is merciful."

"She is," Oster agreed pleasantly. "Though between you and me and this tea, mercy usually takes longer than three days. Now." He opened the ledger across his knee, and the small sound of the cover creaking seemed very loud in the little room. "I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to consider carefully, because I am required to record your answer exactly, and exact answers have a way of becoming official answers. Did you purchase anything, in the weeks before the fever broke? Anything at all. Charms, knots, salves, tokens. From the night market, perhaps."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside a cart rattled past, and a gull screamed over the rooftops, and Ilse Brandt stood in her own kitchen and understood, with terrible clarity, that the next thirty seconds of her life were going to matter for a very long time.

She thought of the knot, tucked in the bottom of the flour jar where she'd hidden it without quite admitting to herself that hiding was what she was doing. She thought of the young man's cool hands and his wobbling table and the chamomile he'd given her for free, and how he'd argued her out of overpaying, and how he'd said *the knot just helps you believe it* — protecting himself even then, wrapping the truth in modesty like a man wearing plain clothes over fine ones.

She thought of old Wrenna swinging from the mill bridge.

"No," Ilse said. "Nothing."

Oster wrote it down. She watched him do it — watched the pen move, unhurried, neat — and something in her chest went tight and sour, because she had just lied to the Office of Inquiry in her own kitchen, and lying to the Office of Inquiry was not a thing that stayed lied.

"Of course," he said mildly. "Mothers protect their children. It's the oldest law there is, older than ours." He closed the ledger and stood, and paused in the doorway with his spectacles sliding down again, and added, almost kindly: "You know, Mrs. Brandt, the Office doesn't much care about knots. Wool and string, honestly, half the peddlers sell them and none of them do anything. What the Office cares about is *results*. A fever that breaks like that isn't wool and string. Somewhere in this city, somebody's prayers are getting answered, and the Office would very much like to know who's answering them." He smiled. "If you remember anything — anything at all — you'll send word. There's a bonus for useful information. Enough to mend a roof."

He tipped his hat and was gone, and Ilse stood in the doorway watching him walk down the lane, small and soft and unhurried, and felt the gratitude in her chest curdle into something else entirely.

Because here was the thing she could not stop thinking, once she started: she had *talked*. Not to the clerk — she'd lied to the clerk, and thank the All-Mother for that much sense. But three nights ago, wrung hollow with fear, she had talked. To Marta. To the fishwife Brenna. To the physician's boy. *My Tam was dying and I bought a charm from a young man at the night market and my Tam is alive.* She'd said it the way grateful people say things — loudly, joyfully, to anyone who'd listen — and Vellsmark was a city built of thin walls and thinner discretion, and every word she'd spoken had traveled outward like ripples, and one of those ripples had evidently reached a desk in the Office of Inquiry and become a line in a ledger.

*I did this,* she thought. *My gratitude did this.*

They would find him. Of course they would find him. A young man selling luck-knots at a folding table on Lantern Row — the Office probably knew his face already, had probably known it for years, waiting for exactly one miracle to attach itself to him like a burr. And when they found him, they would not see a charming man who'd given a tired mother chamomile for free. They would see a vessel. A vessel of the old blood, the forbidden blood, the blood that had gotten Wrenna hanged — and Ilse knew, the way you know things in your bones without ever having been taught them, that the Office did not hang vessels anymore. Hanging was quick. The Office had learned better uses.

She went to the flour jar. Her hands shook getting the lid off. The grey knot lay where she'd buried it, humble as a dead bird, and she lifted it out and held it over the stove, over the coals, ready to end it —

— and could not.

Because it had saved her son. Whatever else it was, whoever else it endangered, it had reached across the dark and pulled Tam back, and you did not burn the hand that had pulled your child from the river. That was not gratitude anymore; that was something deeper and less forgivable. Loyalty.

She put the knot back in the jar. Then she took off her apron, put on her good shawl, and told Tam she had mending to deliver, which was even true, mostly.

There was one thing she could still do. One small, practical, dangerous thing, the kind of thing Ilse Brandt had done her whole life: get ahead of the trouble. Go to Lantern Row, find the young man, and warn him — tell him a clerk had come, tell him Marta's mouth had run, tell him to fold his table and vanish before the grey coats came with their ledgers and their gentle questions.

She was halfway down the lane, walking fast, rehearsing the words, when she saw them.

Two men in grey coats at the corner ahead, standing beneath the shrine-post, speaking quietly to the shrine-keeper. And one of them held open a little book, and the shrine-keeper was nodding, and pointing — pointing down the hill, toward the water, toward Lantern Row, toward the night market.

Toward the wobbling table and the wooden box and the young man with the cool hands.

Ilse stopped in the middle of the lane. Around her, Vellsmark went on with its evening — shutters banging, supper smoke rising, gulls quarreling — and she stood in the middle of all that ordinary noise with her heart hammering and understood that whatever happened next, it had started in her kitchen, with her gratitude, with her loud and joyful and fatal telling.

*Run,* she thought at the young man across the whole distance of the city, as if thought could carry. *Please. Fold your table and run.*

The grey coats began to walk downhill.
