## 4. Grey Coats in the Market

Hazel knew something was wrong before he saw a single grey coat, because the gulls stopped.

Vellsmark's night market had its own weather, and part of that weather was birds — gulls wheeling over the fish stalls in slow, greedy spirals, gulls perched on awning poles like disapproving landlords, gulls screaming at each other over a dropped herring as though it were a matter of theology. Hazel had sold charms under their noise for three years. He'd come to find it comforting, the way other people found rain comforting.

So when the sky above Tanner's Row went suddenly, completely silent, he set down the luck-knot he was tying and listened.

The market kept going around him. Lamplighters made their rounds with long poles and longer sighs. Old Piet argued with a customer about whether smoked eel counted as fresh if you smoked it that morning. A child wailed over a spilled bag of candied ginger while her mother performed the ancient maternal rite of pretending not to care. All of it ordinary. All of it fine.

Except the birds were gone, and down at the mouth of the lane where the cobbles met the harbor road, four men in grey coats were walking uphill at the pace of people who had nowhere else in the world to be.

*Inquisitors.*

Hazel's hands did not shake. He'd trained them not to, years ago, the way you train yourself not to look at your own name written somewhere dangerous. His heart, less disciplined, began hammering against his ribs like a prisoner demanding release.

Grey coats didn't come to the night market for smuggled brandy or unpaid tariffs. The harbor watch handled that, and they wore blue and were cheerfully corruptible. Grey coats came for one thing only, and everyone in Vellsmark knew what it was, even the ones who pretended not to: *the proscription*. No workings. No vessels. No blood of the Nine Names. Three years of edicts nailed to every church door in the free cities, and now here were the nails, walking.

He looked down at his table. Luck-knots. Fever-salve. Dried yarrow bundled with red thread — *for courage*, said the little card in his careful merchant's hand, and nothing about what else the thread was for. Everything on the table was exactly as mundane as he'd spent three years making it. He could burn the whole inventory in front of an inquisitor and the worst they'd find was wool and beeswax.

But Ilse's face floated up in his memory, unbidden. *It worked. Whatever you put in it, it worked.* And the prayer she'd whispered over her son — he hadn't heard it, hadn't needed to. Prayers were the one thing no edict could stop, and lately, apparently, no silence could either.

*They're not looking for me,* he told himself. *They can't know. Nobody knows. Magda barely knows, and Magda is dying faster than she can gossip.*

Then the grey coats reached the first stall, and the man leading them held up a piece of paper, and the lamplight caught the seal at its bottom — black wax, pressed with nine interlocking rings — and Hazel stopped telling himself anything at all.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it is extremely boring for the first few minutes.

Hazel stood behind his table and watched the sweep work its way up the lane, and his mind, denied the dignity of panic, occupied itself with details. The lead inquisitor was young — younger than Hazel, surely — with a jaw like a shovel and the particular walk of someone who had recently been promoted and wanted everyone to enjoy it with him. The two flanking him carried lanterns with shutters, the kind that threw light in narrow bars, and they swept those bars across faces the way you'd sweep a blade across wheat. The fourth carried a ledger. That one frightened Hazel most. Ledgers meant names. Names meant lists. Lists meant that somewhere in a cold office in the citadel, someone had already decided how many people in this market were worth writing down.

"Name and trade," the young inquisitor said to Piet, whose eel argument died mid-syllable.

"Piet Vandermeer. Fish."

"You sell charms?"

"I sell *fish*." A pause. "And my wife sells pies."

The inquisitor looked at the pie cart. The pie cart contained pies. He moved on.

Hazel exhaled slowly through his nose. *Fish,* he thought, with genuine admiration. *That's the spirit, Piet.*

Two stalls closer now. A woman selling ribbon — actual ribbon, dyed wool ribbon, the least magical substance in the known world — was asked to open her cash box anyway. She did, hands trembling, and the inquisitor with the ledger wrote something down, which seemed deeply unfair, since the box contained eleven coppers and a button.

Hazel's fingers found the edge of his table and gripped it.

Think. Options. He ran them the way Magda had taught him to run knots — left over, under, pull tight, check for slack.

Option one: stay. Answer questions. His goods were clean, his papers were clean — forged clean, by a man in Salt Lane who charged a scandalous amount and delivered quality — and his face was the face of a thousand forgettable hedge-traders. Risky but survivable. Probably.

Option two: run. Also risky, because running was a confession, and grey coats loved confessions the way gulls loved herring.

Option three—

Option three arrived on its own, because the young inquisitor with the shovel jaw stopped in front of Hazel's table, looked at the luck-knots, and smiled.

"Charm-seller," he said. It wasn't a question. It wasn't quite an accusation either. It was the tone of a cat placing a paw on something small to see if it moved.

"Hedge-trader," Hazel corrected mildly. "Charms are knotted cord and good intentions. I also have salve, if your men have been marching. It's very good for blisters." He heard his own voice doing what it always did — warm, unhurried, a little self-deprecating, the voice of a man who had never once been interesting. Three years of practice, poured out smooth as oil.

The inquisitor picked up a luck-knot. Turned it over. "What's it for?"

"Luck."

"What's *in* it?"

"Wool. Red thread. And luck, hopefully." A couple of people nearby laughed — the nervous laughter of crowds who want very much for something to be a joke. Hazel let them. Laughter was cover; laughter made you furniture.

The inquisitor didn't laugh. He held the knot up to his lantern bar, and his eyes — pale eyes, patient eyes — moved from the knot to Hazel's hands.

"Show me your palms."

And there it was. The trap, sprung so gently you could almost pretend it wasn't one. Because hedge-traders' palms were cracked and stained and honest. But a charmer's palms — a real one — carried marks the guild had never managed to explain away: faint silver scarring along the lifeline, from years of pulling power through the body like water through a pump. Hazel's own mother had called them *the choir's fingerprints*. He'd spent a decade keeping gloves on in cold weather and learning to hold his hands half-curled, casual, always slightly in shadow.

"Palms?" Hazel said, smiling his furniture smile. "Is that a new regulation? Only I've got salve for chapped skin too, if—"

"*Palms.*"

The lantern bars swung. Somewhere down the lane, a woman cried out softly as a stall was overturned — just checked, probably, just roughly checked, but the sound traveled the way fear travels, fast and downhill. The crowd was thickening instead of thinning, because crowds are stupid and hopeful and always want to see. Hazel felt all of it at once: the press of bodies, the cold coming off the harbor, the paper weight of the knot still dangling from the inquisitor's fist.

And underneath everything, faint as a pulse in a sleeping wrist, he felt something *else*.

It had been there since the fever broke in Ilse's kitchen — since before, maybe, and he'd been too deaf to hear it. A low hum at the bottom of the world, like a choir warming up in a locked room three streets down and also somehow inside his own chest. The old gods, Magda would have said. Answering. Straining. *Listening.*

And gods who listen, Hazel thought with a lurch, can be *asked*.

"No need," he said aloud, and his voice came out strange — steady, but resonant, like a bell struck underwater. "I'll show you mine if you show me yours."

The inquisitor frowned. "What?"

"The palms," Hazel said. "Regulation's regulation."

And while the man's frown deepened into the beginning of an order, Hazel did the thing he had sworn for three years never to do.

He worked magic. In public. In front of an inquisitor.

It was a small working. That was the joke of it, the terrible private joke he'd carry for the rest of his life however long that turned out to be: after all the hiding, all the careful grayness, his first public miracle was a *sleight*. He pulled the hum up through his feet, through the wet cobblestones, through whatever old sleeping thing lay coiled beneath Vellsmark's foundations — and he asked it, silently, wordlessly, the way you'd ask a large dog to please move: *darkness, just there, just for a moment, just enough.*

Every lantern in the lane guttered at once.

Not blew out — guttered, ducked, shrank, as if the flames had all remembered some prior obligation. The lamplighter's pole-flame bent sideways. The inquisitor's shuttered lantern threw its bar of light straight down into the cobbles, illuminating nothing but stone and the inquisitor's own boots. For the length of one heartbeat — two — the whole of Tanner's Row went soft and blind and dark.

And Hazel Eriksen folded his table flat, slung the box of charms over his shoulder, and walked sideways into the crowd like a drop of water returning to the sea.

* * *

He didn't stop until he was three lanes gone and breathing like a man who'd raced the tide.

He'd taken the route he'd mapped in his head years ago and never used — the fish-cleaning alley behind Piet's stall, the low arch where the tanners' drainage crossed under a wall, the gap between the cooperage and the salt shed that was technically somebody's doorway and practically nobody's. His legs had done it without consulting him. His heart was another matter entirely. It was conducting some kind of festival in there, drums and horns and possibly fireworks.

Because — and he made himself admit it, standing in the dark between the salt barrels, box of luck-knots clutched to his chest like a shield — it had *worked*. He had asked the dark to fall and the dark had fallen, instantly, gratefully, the way it hadn't fallen for him in years. Three years of knots and salves and tricks-of-the-trade, three years of being a mundane man with mundane hands, and the moment he'd actually reached for it, the power had come like it had been waiting by the door with its coat on.

Which meant the hum wasn't imagination. Which meant Ilse's fever-break wasn't coincidence. Which meant the prayers across the city weren't rumor.

Which meant the grey coats were going to keep coming, and next time they wouldn't be checking palms at a charm stall. Next time they'd be sealing gates.

"Terror," Hazel said quietly, to the salt barrels, "and exhilaration. Wonderful combination. Very stable. Nothing wrong with this at all."

"That's usually how it feels the first time."

Hazel spun.

A man stood at the mouth of the gap — had been standing there, apparently, for some time, leaning against the cooperage wall with the boneless ease of someone who owned the darkness rather than rented it. Mid-thirties, maybe. Traveler's coat, good boots, a courier's satchel slung crosswise. He had a face built for forgetting: brown hair, brown eyes, a scar through one eyebrow that was somehow the only memorable thing about him.

Except his eyes weren't forgettable at all, Hazel realized. They were watching Hazel with an attention that had weight, like a hand on the shoulder.

"How long," Hazel said carefully, "have you been standing there?"

"Since the lanterns dipped." The man pushed off the wall. "Nice work, by the way. Subtle. Well — subtle-ish. You asked the dark to fall and then immediately committed several crimes in front of an officer of the Inquisition, so 'subtle' may be generous."

"I folded a table."

"You folded a table *while invisible to authority*. There's a word for that where I'm from." The man smiled. It was a good smile, quick and crooked, and it did absolutely nothing to make Hazel feel safer. "Corvin," he said, and offered a hand.

Hazel did not take it. "I don't know you."

"No. But I know you. Or I know what you are, which tonight is more useful." Corvin's voice dropped, easy and quiet, pitched under the distant noise of the market reassembling itself. "You reached for something in that lane, friend, and something answered. Fast. Faster than any hedge-charmer I've seen work, and I've seen a few. That's not a trick you learn off a peddler's wagon. That's in the blood."

Hazel's grip tightened on the box until the cardboard creaked. Every instinct he owned — three years of instincts, honed fine as fishing line — screamed at him to deny, deflect, joke, become furniture. *Wool and beeswax. Fish and pies.*

Instead he heard himself say: "Who sent you?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously."

Corvin studied him for a moment, and something in his crooked smile shifted — softened, almost, though Hazel distrusted softness in strangers more than he trusted blades. "Fair," Corvin said. "Short version: there are people in this city who've been waiting for the lights to dip. Longer than you'd believe. We keep safe houses. We move people the grey coats want. And as of about an hour ago, we've been moving them fast, because the Inquisition just sealed the harbor records and doubled the watch, and that means they're hunting something specific." He tilted his head. "Something with silver scars on his palms, maybe. Something the old blood runs in."

The hum at the bottom of the world pulsed once, faint and vast, like a heartbeat under the cobblestones. Hazel wondered, distantly, whether Corvin felt it too.

"You should go," Hazel said. "Whatever you think I am — you should go home, and I should go home, and tomorrow we both sell completely legal merchandise."

"Sure," Corvin said agreeably. He reached into his coat and produced a small object, holding it out on his palm: a tin whistle, plain and dented, the kind children bought at fairs. "When you change your mind. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever the walls start closing. Third lane past the cooperage, blow twice, wait, blow once. Someone will find you." He set the whistle on top of the salt barrel, stepped back, and spread his empty hands. "No pressure. No strings. Just — for what it's worth, charm-seller, the invisibility you're so proud of?"

He tapped his own eye.

"It's already gone. Has been since the fever broke in the Weavers' Quarter. You're not hiding anymore. You're just deciding what to do about it."

Then he was gone, down the dark lane, boots silent on the stones, leaving Hazel alone with the salt barrels, the tin whistle, and the sound of his own heart refusing, stubbornly, to calm down.

Hazel stood there a long time. Above him the gulls returned, one by one, settling onto the rooftops with indignant murmurs, resuming their ancient quarrels as if nothing had happened.

He picked up the whistle. Turned it over. It was warm, absurdly, as if someone had been carrying it close to their skin for a long while, waiting.

*Run,* a voice had thought at him across the city — he didn't know how he knew that, only that he did, the way he now knew other things he didn't want to know. *Fold your table and run.*

He'd run. It hadn't helped. The grey coats were still walking uphill, somewhere behind him, patient as tide.

Hazel pocketed the whistle, pulled his collar up against the harbor wind, and started toward Magda's house — because if anyone alive could tell him what was happening to his city, his blood, and the locked choir singing under the world, it was the last senior member of the Guild of Nine Names.

And if she couldn't tell him, he thought grimly, at least she'd be dying soon, and wouldn't have to watch what he did next.
