## 5. Corvin's Offer

Corvin Ash had been following the charming young man through the night market for the better part of an hour, and in that time he had learned three things: one, that Hazel Eriksen sold a fever-salve that actually worked, which was either excellent herbcraft or something worse; two, that he haggled like a man who'd rather give the money away but couldn't afford to; and three, that when the grey coats came, he did not run like a guilty man.

He ran like a terrified one. There was a difference, and Corvin had spent enough years learning to tell them apart that the distinction had practically become his profession.

The alley behind the tannery reeked of old lye and older secrets, which was why Corvin liked it. He leaned against the wall with his arms crossed, watching Hazel press himself into the shadow of a rain barrel, chest heaving, hands still faintly glowing at the fingertips.

That was the interesting part. The glow.

"Very nice," Corvin said quietly. "Subtle. Almost."

Hazel spun toward him so fast he knocked the rain barrel over. Water slopped across the cobbles. Somewhere above them, a cat expressed its opinion of the whole evening.

"Who—" Hazel started.

"Shh." Corvin held up a finger. Down the alley mouth, lantern light swung past — grey coat, iron badge, the particular bored stride of a man who would rather be drinking. It passed. Corvin counted to ten out of habit, then thirty out of caution, then let his breath go. "There. You see? This is why we don't talk until the lanterns leave. First rule of not dying."

"I wasn't going to talk," Hazel whispered. "I was going to run."

"You were going to stand there glowing like a festival lamp and then run directly into the second patrol on Tanner's Row. They loop back every quarter hour. I've timed it." He paused. "Well. Sera's timed it. I just carry the numbers around in my head and feel important."

Hazel stared at him. In the thin light leaking from a shuttered window above, Corvin could see him properly for the first time: mid-twenties, ink-stained fingers, the careful plainness of a man who had practiced being unremarkable until it fit him like a well-worn coat. Except the coat was currently on fire, metaphorically speaking, because his hands were still doing the thing.

"Your hands," Corvin said.

Hazel shoved them into his pockets. "It's nothing. Lamp oil. Tallow residue. A condition."

"A condition."

"Yes."

"That makes your fingers shine."

"It's very rare," Hazel said, with the desperate dignity of a liar who has committed to the bit. "You wouldn't have heard of it."

Corvin grinned. He couldn't help it. He'd sat through enough grim briefings in damp cellars to last several lifetimes, and here, finally, was a vessel who lied about glowing hands. Sera would hate him. Sera would also, Corvin suspected, decide within five minutes that he was the most important person in Vellsmark, which amounted to roughly the same thing.

"My name's Corvin," he said. "And you're Hazel Eriksen, hedge-charmer, third stall from the spice seller, best luck-knots in the night market if you believe the fishwives, which I do, because I bought one three weeks ago and haven't been robbed since, which for me is frankly miraculous."

"How do you know my name?"

"You say it when you sell things. 'A charm from Hazel Eriksen, made honest and priced fair.' It's good patter. Very trustworthy. I've been meaning to steal it."

Hazel's eyes narrowed. "You're with the inquisition."

"If I were with the inquisition, would I have told you about the patrol schedule?"

"You might. To build trust."

Corvin blinked. "Huh. That's — actually a fair point. No wonder recruitment's up. Everyone's paranoid now; it does half our work for us." He pushed off the wall and spread his hands, palms out, deliberately empty. "I'm not inquisition. I'm the opposite of inquisition. I'm the thing the inquisition stays up late worrying about."

"The opposite of inquisition isn't a thing. That's just — ordinary people."

"Ordinary people with a cellar, a courier network, and a leader who hasn't slept properly since spring." He let the grin soften into something more honest. "The Lantern Cell. You may have heard rumors. We deny all of them, including the accurate ones."

They ended up walking, because standing still in a tannery alley felt like asking to be arrested, and because Hazel — Corvin noted this with interest — kept glancing back toward the market as though the mother whose son he'd saved might be lying in the gutter behind them.

"She talked," Hazel said at last. "Didn't she. The woman with the boy."

"Fever case. Six years old. Three days burning, and then your salve, and by morning he's asking for bread." Corvin shrugged. "People talk when their children live. It's the most forgivable thing a person can do, and it gets more of us killed than anything the inquisition invents."

"I didn't do anything." Hazel said it fast, automatic, the way a man says a thing he's said to himself so many times it's worn smooth. "Salves work or they don't. Fever breaks or it doesn't. Coincidence."

"Mm," said Corvin.

"It is."

"Of course."

"Stop agreeing with me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like someone who knows better."

Corvin laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and Hazel shot him a look that would have curdled milk if Hazel had any talent for menace, which he clearly did not. He had the look of a man who apologized to furniture he bumped into.

They cut south along the canal, where the water smelled less of lye and more of fish, which Corvin considered an improvement. Lanterns hung from the bridge posts, and under each one stood a grey coat, checking papers, checking faces, checking the shadows behind the fish stalls with the thoroughness of men who had recently been told to be thorough.

"They've doubled the patrols," Corvin observed. "Since tonight. Since your coincidence."

Hazel went pale in the lantern light. "Because of me."

"Because of a rumor about a charmer. Which is you. So yes." Corvin watched him absorb it — watched the shoulders come up, the jaw set, the whole body bracing like a man expecting a blow. "Hey. Look at me. Nobody's dead. The boy's alive. The mother's sorry, probably already, in the specific way people are sorry after they've done the damage. And you are currently the only person in Vellsmark who walked away from a sweep with all your parts attached. Do you understand how rare that is? I've got a list. It's a short list. Mostly it's names of the dead."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No," Corvin said cheerfully. "It's supposed to make you feel rare. Feeling better comes later, ideally somewhere with soup."

*

The safehouse was a chandler's shop on Candlemaker's Row, which Corvin considered the Cell's single genuine joke — a resistance cell hiding inside a shop that literally sold candles. The owner, a deaf old woman named Berta who was neither deaf nor old but played both beautifully whenever officials visited, let them in through the back and gave Hazel a look of frank appraisal before returning to her wicks.

Down the stairs, past the stockroom, into a low room lit by exactly one lantern, because Sera believed light attracted attention and Corvin believed Sera enjoyed making everyone squint.

"This is where you're offering to hide me," Hazel said, looking around at the crates, the cot, the map of Vellsmark pinned to the wall with red thread snaking between districts like a disease chart. "Behind the candles."

"Nobody searches a chandler's twice. The first search ruins your nose for it." Corvin dragged a crate over and gestured for Hazel to sit. "Here's the offer. Plainly, because you have the face of a man who's been lied to recently and I'd rather not add to the pile."

"Recently?" Hazel said sharply. "What do you know about—"

"Later. Offer first." Corvin held up a hand. "The Lantern Cell protects people like you. People the proscription touches. We've moved eleven charm-workers out of the city in two years. Farmers with green thumbs that shouldn't exist. A midwife whose hands could ease pain. A boy who could find lost things, gods rest him, we got him out a week before the sweeps started." He kept his voice level, easy, the tone he used for nervous cargo. "We have routes south. Papers. People on the gates. You'd be fed, hidden, and gone before the next moon."

"And in exchange."

"There it is." Corvin smiled ruefully. "Always an exchange. Nothing, really. You keep your head down. Maybe, someday, if you want — you pass word along. Carry a knot of thread from one place to another. Courier work. Boring, mostly. Occasionally terrifying, but the pay is terrible so it balances out."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. "That's it? Hide me, feed me, and someday I carry messages?"

"That's it."

"Why?"

"Because someone saved my life once and didn't ask why either," Corvin said, and found, to his mild annoyance, that he meant it. He usually kept that story in a locked drawer. "And because there aren't many of us left, and the ones worth saving keep insisting on standing in markets glowing at inquisitors."

"I told you, that was—"

"Lamp oil. Yes. The condition." Corvin leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let the humor drain out of his voice by degrees, gently, like easing a cork from a bottle. "Hazel. I watched you put out a lantern flame from forty feet while running. I watched the light bend around you in the alley like water around a stone. I've spent six years learning what the proscribed gifts look like, and yours isn't hedge-craft. Hedge-craft flickers. Yours *poured*." He paused. "You're a vessel. Or something near enough that the distinction won't save you."

The silence stretched. Above them, floorboards creaked as Berta moved through her shop, steady as a heartbeat. Hazel's hands had gone into his sleeves, hiding, and Corvin pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice was half of what made people trust you.

"If I'm wrong," Corvin added, "then you're a very talented charmer who should still take the offer, because the inquisition doesn't care about the difference. If I'm right —" he exhaled — "then you're the most dangerous person in this city, and the safest place for you is underground with people who know how to hide dangerous things."

"And what do *you* think I am?"

Corvin considered lying. Considered the soft version, the version Sera would have wanted, all hope and destiny and gentle words. But there was something about Hazel's tired, stubborn face that made lying feel like kicking a dog.

"I think prayers are being answered again," he said quietly. "All over the city. I think the old stories are waking up. And I think whatever's answering them is looking for doors — and you, my friend, glow like one."

Hazel flinched as if struck. For a moment Corvin thought he'd bolt — the man's weight shifted toward the door, toward the stairs, toward the market and the grey coats and the short bright life of a fool. But then he sagged instead, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and put his face in his hands.

"Three years," Hazel said, muffled, into his palms. "Three years I've been careful. Careful, careful, careful. Little charms. Nothing that works too well. Nothing that lasts. And one salve, one night, one mother—" His voice cracked, and he laughed, wetly, without any humor in it. "Do you know what the worst part is? When the fever broke, I *felt* it. Something moving through me. Like a bell being rung somewhere deep. And some stupid part of me was glad."

Corvin waited. He was good at waiting. It was, along with carrying numbers in his head, most of his job.

"I never prayed," Hazel said. "I want you to know that. Whatever's happening — I never asked for it."

"I believe you." And he did, which was strange, because Corvin believed almost nobody. "But here's the thing about bells, Hazel. Once they've been rung, they don't unring. The sound goes out looking for ears." He rose, fetched the jug of water and the chipped cup that passed for hospitality down here, and poured. "So. Sanctuary. Soup. A cot that smells faintly of candle wax. All yours, no strings, starting now."

Hazel took the cup but didn't drink. "And if I say no?"

"Then I walk you out the front, wish you luck, and spend the rest of the week feeling sick about it." Corvin shrugged. "We don't recruit at knifepoint. Bad for morale. Worse for retention."

Hazel almost smiled. It was a small, exhausted thing, but it was real, and Corvin felt something in his chest loosen slightly — the feeling he got when fragile cargo made it over a hard stretch of road.

"One question," Hazel said. "Your leader. The one who doesn't sleep. What does she want with vessels like me? Really?"

And there it was — the question Corvin had been hoping to avoid, asked in the flat, clear voice of a man who had already guessed the answer and wanted to see if honesty lived anywhere in this cellar.

Corvin opened his mouth to give the rehearsed answer. *Protection. Freedom. The same thing we want for everyone.*

What came out instead, because Hazel was watching him with those tired eyes, was: "Honestly? I think she wants to change the world. And I think she believes people like you are how." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Whether that's rescue or recruitment depends a lot on which morning you ask me."

Hazel looked down at the cup in his hands. Then, slowly, he drank.

Above them, muffled through floorboards, came the sound of Berta knocking twice on the counter — the signal, sharp and urgent, that Corvin had hoped not to hear tonight.

Grey coats. At the door.

Corvin set down the jug. "Soup's going to have to wait," he said.
