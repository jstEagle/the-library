## 6. Magda's Confession

Magda lived above her shop on Tanner's Row, in rooms that smelled of beeswax and dried yarrow and, lately, of the particular sweetness that means a body is finishing its business and doesn't intend to be hurried about it.

Hazel climbed the stairs the way he always did, slowly, announcing himself with each creak so as not to startle her. She'd told him once that a charmer who sneaks up on an old woman deserves whatever hex lands on him, and she'd said it while holding a ladle, and he had believed every word.

"It's me," he called. "The disappointment."

"Come in, then," Magda's voice came back, thin but still carrying its old iron underneath. "Mind the cat. He's decided the footstool is his now, and I've decided to let him win. One of us should get what we want at this stage."

The cat — a grey, battle-scarred tom named Chancellor, for reasons Magda had never satisfactorily explained — regarded Hazel from the footstool with the flat contempt of a creature who has outlived three owners and expects to outlive a fourth.

Magda was propped up in bed against more pillows than any one person could reasonably need, small and sharp-eyed under a quilt she'd made herself forty years ago. Her hands lay on top of the covers, and they were the thing that frightened him: not trembling, not wasted, just *still*. Magda's hands had never been still in all the years he'd known her. They were always knotting, sorting, stirring, gesturing at the world as though arranging it.

"How are you today?" he asked, which was a foolish question, and they both knew it, and she let him have it because she was kind in her own flinty way.

"Dying," she said briskly. "Slowly enough to be tedious about it. Sit down before you hover. You hover like your grandmother did, and she was worse at it."

He sat on the chair by the bed. There was tea already steeping on the nightstand, which meant she'd known he was coming, which meant she'd sent for him without sending for him, which was very Magda. The woman communicated primarily through implication and baked goods.

"You wanted to talk," he said.

"I wanted you to bring honey cakes." She looked at him over the rim of her teacup. "Did you?"

"There are some in my coat."

"Well, then. We can talk."

* * *

He'd come straight from the market, where the morning had gone strangely. Three people had asked him, separately, whether he'd heard anything. Not heard anything *new* — heard anything. A fishwife had gripped his wrist and said, "They say if you pray at the old shrine by the weir, something answers back," and she'd said it the way you'd say *they say the bread is cheap*, ordinary as weather, and then she'd squeezed his wrist hard enough to hurt and looked at him like she expected him to understand something enormous.

He hadn't understood. Or rather, he understood exactly one thing: Magda would know.

She ate half a honey cake with the concentration of a woman who intended to enjoy it properly, brushed the crumbs from her quilt with two precise flicks of those still hands, and said, "You've felt it. Don't bother looking surprised; you've had the face of a man carrying a secret since you walked in, and it's not the usual face. The usual face is guilt. This one's fear."

"The prayers," Hazel said quietly. "Are they real? Are they being answered?"

"Yes."

Just that. No hedge, no qualification, none of the careful fog she wrapped around everything else. It landed in the room like a dropped stone.

"And only certain people can hear it happening," she went on. "Feel it, rather. Like a bell rung in a house where you're standing. Most folk hear nothing. But vessels—" She stopped. Sipped her tea.

"Vessels," Hazel repeated. "That's the second time you've used that word. First time was last winter, when you were feverish and thought I was someone named Aldric. You said 'the vessel mustn't sing.' I assumed it was the fever talking."

"It wasn't the fever." Magda set down her cup. For a long moment she studied him, and he watched her decide something — watched it happen behind her eyes the way you watch weather change over the harbor. "How much do you actually know about the Guild of Nine Names, Hazel? And don't recite the catechism. I wrote half the catechism, and I'll tell you now it's mostly decoration."

He knew what every guild child knew. That nine generations ago, when the gods still walked openly and their quarrels burned whole provinces, the founders of the guild had learned to *serve* them — to carry divine attention the way a lantern carries flame, lending mortals' voices to immortal ears. That the Choir was the guild's great work: the harmonizing of prayer, the tending of the bond between heaven and earth. That the guild had kept the free cities safe through the Godswars by keeping the gods *listening*. That the empire had destroyed the guild three centuries ago out of jealousy and fear, and the survivors had scattered, and the gift had thinned generation by generation until—

Until him. The last. The final thread of a noble tapestry, keeping small charms alive in a night market, hiding behind luck-knots and fever-salves because the proscription made even a whispered blessing a hanging offense.

"That's what I know," he said. "Serve the gods. Keep the line. Stay hidden."

"Mm." Magda folded her hands. "Now here's what actually happened."

* * *

"The founders didn't learn to serve the gods," she said. "They learned to *catch* them."

Hazel laughed — one short, disbelieving note — and stopped when he saw her face.

"A god," Magda went on, in the tone she used for explaining knots to slow apprentices, "is not a person, whatever the priests like to pretend. It's a vast attention. A pressure. When it turns toward the world, things bend toward it — crops grow or rot, fevers break or spread, rivers rise or fall. The founders discovered that a mortal of the right blood could stand between a god and the world. Could take that attention into themselves and hold it. Shape where it pointed." Her eyes didn't waver from his. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? They didn't build a bridge to heaven. They built a net."

"They bound them," Hazel said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "The Choir isn't — it's not a harmony. It's a—"

"A binding sung in parts. Nine bloodlines, nine anchors, each holding a piece of each god, so that no single vessel could ever turn the whole power to their own use. It was meant to be merciful, if you can believe that. The old gods were *terrible*, Hazel. Not evil — terrible, the way a flood is terrible. The founders looked at a world where divinity walked free and children died of divine moods, and they chose chains. Chained the gods into the Choir and called it stewardship, and their grandchildren called it service, and their grandchildren's grandchildren forgot there had ever been anything to forgive." She picked up another honey cake and didn't eat it, just held it. "I found the truth in the guild archives when I was younger than you. There's a ledger. Founder's hand. The first entry reads: *We have taken the Nine, and they do not consent.*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, Chancellor scratched at a door.

"Three hundred years," Hazel said. "The gods have been prisoners for three hundred years, and the guild sang to them every festival and called it worship."

"We believed our own story. That's the worst of it. I sang at the autumn rite for sixty years believing I was tending a garden, when I was standing guard on a cell." Magda finally bit the honey cake, chewed, swallowed, as though determined to keep doing ordinary things while the world rearranged itself. "And then the empire broke the guild, killed eight of the nine lines inside a decade, and the binding began to fray. Every generation thinner. Every anchor weaker. The gods have spent three centuries pressing on the walls of their prison, and the walls have been coming down, and now—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the sky, toward the city, toward the rumors moving through the markets like smoke — "now the walls are nearly gone, and what's left holding them is—"

She stopped.

Hazel felt it before she said it. Felt it the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects — a lurch, a falling sensation, the ground of his whole life tilting.

"No," he said.

"You're the last of the nine lines. The other eight are ash. Every knot in the binding untied except one." Magda's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than the iron. "You're not the heir of the Choir, boy. You're the final knot in it. As long as you live and the gift sleeps in you, the gods stay caught — stretched thin, muffled, mad with confinement, but caught. And every answered prayer in this city right now is them pushing through the gaps around you."

* * *

He stood up. He didn't remember deciding to stand.

"That's not—" He turned away from the bed, faced the window, the grey light over Tanner's Row, the rooftops going gold at their edges. "I sell luck-knots. I make salves. I've spent three years being *careful*, being nothing, being small, because that's what you taught me — keep the gift quiet, keep the line quiet, wait it out. And you're telling me the whole time I was hiding, I was—"

"A lock," Magda said. "Yes."

"A *cage*." The word came out of him hot. "Every charm I ever made, every blessing I swallowed instead of speaking, every time I bit down on a prayer — I thought I was protecting myself. I was tightening the bars." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "My grandmother. My mother. Did they know?"

"My generation knew. Your grandmother's generation suspected. Before that—" Magda sighed. "Before that, they sang beautifully and asked no questions. It's remarkable, the questions people don't ask when the singing is lovely and the food is plentiful. The guild ate well off those gods, Hazel. Best harvests in living memory, the year the ninth line was completed. Nobody wondered why."

"And the gods." He made himself say it. Made himself imagine it. "What are they, in there? What's it like?"

There was a silence long enough that he turned around. Magda had gone greyer, and her hands had finally begun to tremble, very slightly, on the quilt.

"I touched the edge of it once," she said. "An accident, during a rite, when I was young and arrogant and held the verse too long. For perhaps four heartbeats I felt what they feel." Her throat moved. "It's not pain. Pain would be kinder. It's *constriction*. Imagine the ocean poured into a bottle, and the bottle aware, and the ocean aware, and neither able to stop. Three hundred years, Hazel. They aren't angry the way a prisoner is angry. They're angry the way a drowned thing is angry. And they are waking up, and the only thing between them and the open air is you."

Hazel sat back down, because his legs informed him that standing was no longer among the day's available options.

"Why tell me now?" he asked. "Why not ten years ago? Why not when I was a child?"

"Because telling you changes nothing unless the binding stirs, and the binding hasn't stirred in three centuries." She met his eyes. "And because I am dying, and I will not have you learn this from anyone less fond of you than I am. Vane's inquisitors have the guild records — the ones we couldn't burn. If they find the ninth line, they won't kill you, boy. They'll *use* you. A cage with a willing keeper is worth more than a broken one. And there are others who'll come for you too, gentler ones, with better manners and the same intention. Everyone who learns what you are will want to hold the key."

"So what do I do?" It came out smaller than he wanted. "Magda. What do I *do*?"

"Nothing," she said. "For now. You do nothing. You don't pray aloud — never pray aloud, do you hear me? A spoken prayer from a vessel isn't a request. It's a door opened from the inside. You go home, you sell your knots, you be small, and you hope the fraying is slow." She reached out and took his hand, and her grip was still strong, still Magda. "And you forgive us. Not today. Maybe not for years. But eventually, forgive us. We chained the sea because we were afraid of drowning, and we made you the bottle, and none of us asked whether the sea deserved it."

He stayed until the light went amber and then grey, until she grew drowsy and Chancellor jumped onto the quilt and settled against her side like a sentry taking his post. At the door she spoke once more, half-asleep, her voice drifting after him down the stairs:

"Hazel. If one of them ever speaks to you — if you hear your own name in a voice too large for the room — you come to me. Whatever hour. Promise me."

"I promise," he said, and meant it, and went out into the evening feeling the weight of every charm in his satchel like nine kinds of chain.

* * *

He was halfway home through the lamplit lanes when it happened.

A woman knelt at the old dry fountain on Cooper's Wynd, the one that hadn't flowed since the empire rerouted the springs. She was praying — he heard the words, small and desperate, a sick husband, a rent owed, ordinary sorrows — and the air *moved*. Not wind. Something vaster than wind, passing through the wynd like a whale beneath a rowboat, and every hair on Hazel's arms rose, and deep in his chest, in the place where his gift slept coiled and quiet, something *answered*.

Not with words. With attention. An attention older than the city, older than the guild, vast and furious and *pressed flat*, turning toward him through the walls of its long imprisonment the way a drowning man turns toward a crack of light.

And then — threaded through the pressure, faint as a voice through stone — it spoke.

Not a word. A name.

His name.

*Hazel.*

He stood in the empty lane with his heart slamming and the lamplight swimming, and somewhere across the darkening city a bell began to ring, and he could not have said whether it was a temple bell or an alarm.

He ran home. He locked the door. He sat with his back against it until dawn, listening to his own blood, hearing in every beat that single syllable, patient and immense, repeating like a tide against a wall:

*Hazel. Hazel. Hazel.*
