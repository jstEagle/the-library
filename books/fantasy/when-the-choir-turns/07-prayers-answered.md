## 7. Prayers Answered

### The Drowned Boy

Tomas Brandt had been dead for eleven minutes.

He knew this because he had counted. He was a fishmonger's son and counting was the one thing he did better than anyone on the Salt Quay, and so while his body lay grey and slack on the wet planks, while his mother screamed and the harbor men pumped at his chest with their big useless hands, Tomas counted. Eleven minutes. Then a woman knelt beside him — not his mother, someone else, someone whose hands smelled of tallow and river mud — and she said, very quietly, in a voice like a door opening:

*"Come back now, little one. You're wanted."*

And the water came out of him all at once, a whole gray gull's worth of it, and he breathed.

The quay went silent the way a forest goes silent when a hawk crosses it. Then it did not go silent at all.

"He was under," said Old Piet, who had pulled the boy out himself and knew exactly how long a boy could be under. "He was under too long."

"Prayer," whispered the woman who had knelt. She looked as frightened as anyone, which later seemed strange to everyone. She pressed something into the mother's hand — a knot of braided cord, nothing more — and slipped away into the crowd before anyone thought to thank her properly, or ask her name, or do any of the things that people would spend the next week wishing they'd done.

Tomas sat up, sneezed out half the harbor, and asked if he could still have his supper.

His mother laughed and wept and held him so hard his ribs creaked, and that night she told the story forty times, and by the fortieth telling the cord-knot had become a charm, the charm had become a blessing, and the blessing had become proof — spoken in taverns, in bathhouses, in the whisper-network of washwomen and dockhands that no inquisitor had ever managed to tap — *the gods are listening again.*

Nobody said it loudly. Not yet. But everybody said it.

### The Green Field

Marta Lindqvist had farmed her strip of land outside the North Gate for thirty years, and in all that time she had never once prayed to anything except weather, which is to say she had shouted at clouds.

This year the blight had come early. It always came early now, since the Empire's tax collectors took the good seed grain and left the rotting stuff from the imperial stores, but this year it came vicious, blackening the barley rows overnight like ink spilled through the field. Marta had walked her rows in the grey morning and done the arithmetic every farmer does: enough to eat, barely; nothing to sell; another winter of thin soup and pretending the soup was thicker than it was.

Her neighbor's boy — a dreamy lad called Anselm who was supposed to be helping and mostly talked to the plants instead — had left something at the field's edge. A little woven thing, a circle of grass and thread, stuck on a stick like a signpost for very small travelers.

"What's this rubbish?" Marta had asked.

"It's for the field," Anselm said, with the serene confidence of the young. "My gran says you have to ask nicely. Out loud."

"I'll not stand in my own dirt talking to weeds."

"No," Anselm agreed. "But I might."

And apparently he had, because three mornings later Marta walked her rows and stopped so suddenly she nearly fell over her own feet.

Green. Not the sickly green of blight-fighting barley, but deep, greedy, insolent green, the green of a field that has heard good news. The black patches hadn't shrunk — they'd *reversed*, the stalks standing up out of the rot like swimmers surfacing, shaking themselves off.

Marta walked the whole field twice. Then she knelt down in the dirt, which her knees objected to strenuously, and examined a barley head the way an inquisitor examines a heretic.

"Well," she said finally, to nobody, to the sky, to whatever was listening. "That's showing off."

She told her sister. Her sister told the baker. The baker told everyone, because bakers are the telegraph lines of any city, and by Friday there were pilgrims walking out the North Gate to look at a barley field, which was frankly ridiculous, and by Sunday there were soldiers doing the same, which was considerably less so.

Marta put a fence around the woven circle and told the pilgrims it was a holy relic and they could look but not touch. If the gods were going to perform miracles in her dirt, the least they could do was contribute to the harvest fund.

### The Candle Shop

Yannick sold candles, which meant Yannick sold hope by the inch, and business had never been better.

It had started small. Someone lit a candle for the drowned boy on the Salt Quay. Then someone else lit one for their daughter's marriage prospects. Then a whole family came in and bought out his stock of votives because their father's lung-fever had broken in a single night, "like a knot coming undone," the daughter said, and Yannick, who was a businessman before he was anything else, reordered triple from his supplier in Osterhagen.

By the second week he'd had to hire his nephew. By the third week the shrine-niche behind his shop — a dusty alcove where a chipped statue of some old kindly god had sat ignored for generations, so long forgotten that even the inquisitors hadn't bothered defacing it — had become a destination. People queued. They brought offerings: bread, buttons, a live chicken (which Yannick drew the line at). They whispered prayers into cupped hands like girls blowing on cold fingers.

And here was the thing that kept Yannick awake at night, staring at his ceiling: the candles *stayed lit*. He'd noticed it first as a bookkeeping oddity — his wax losses were down forty percent. Then he'd watched one directly. A stub of candle burned from dusk until dawn without shrinking past the point where it should have guttered out, its flame steady as a painted flame, patient as something waiting.

He didn't tell anyone. He was fairly sure that if he told anyone, either the crowd would grow or the grey coats would come, and both options ended with his shop being a problem rather than a shop.

Instead he dusted the chipped statue, set out a saucer of milk next to the candles — because his grandmother had always said the old ones liked milk, and it cost almost nothing — and got on with selling hope by the inch.

On Thursday, a customer bought a single candle, lit it, and murmured a prayer so quiet Yannick only caught the shape of it. But the flame leaned toward the man like a dog hearing its name, and Yannick felt the hair rise on his arms, and when he looked up the customer was looking at him with wide, terrified, luminous eyes, and neither of them said a word about it, ever, though both of them thought about it constantly for the rest of their lives.

### The Clerk

In the inquisitors' annex on Candlemaker Street — a name that had recently become ironic — Clerk Aldous Fenner copied reports.

Aldous was a careful man. Careful men survived occupations, and Aldous intended to survive everything. So when the reports began arriving — a drowned boy revived on the Salt Quay, a blighted field greened overnight, fever-beds emptying in the Poor Quarter, a shrine-statue reportedly seen to smile (Aldous flagged that one *unverified, likely drink*) — he copied them all in his neat clerk's hand and filed them under the heading he'd been instructed to use:

**RUMOR. SEDITION-ADJACENT. NO ACTION.**

The instruction had come down from the Inquisitor-General's office itself, which puzzled Aldous. In his experience, the Inquisitor-General's office treated rumor the way a cat treats a wounded bird: with enormous, patient interest. Rumor was usually *action item number one*.

But the orders were explicit. Collect. File. Wait.

So Aldous collected and filed and waited, and each evening he added a few more pages to the growing folder, and each evening the folder grew faster. On Monday there were four reports. By Friday there were nineteen. By the following Wednesday, a dockworker had stood up in a tavern and *prayed aloud* — actually aloud, in front of witnesses, invoking one of the Nine Names, the ones you didn't invoke, the ones whose names had been struck from the calendars three hundred years ago — and claimed the beer in his mug had turned sweet.

Aldous filed it. His pen hovered over the page afterward.

*Nineteen reports in five days,* he wrote in the margin, then crossed it out, because margins were for approved annotations only. But he couldn't quite stop himself from adding, in pencil, very lightly, in a corner where no supervisor would look:

*Something is happening.*

Then he sharpened his pen, straightened his papers, and waited, carefully, the way careful men do.

### The Soldier

Sergeant Brenner of the Ninth Imperial Foot had marched through eleven provinces and suppressed four riots, and he knew the sound a city makes when it's about to do something stupid.

Vellsmark was making that sound.

Not loudly. That was what unsettled him. Riots usually announced themselves — the smashed windows, the drunken singing, the tar and feathers phase. This was quieter and worse. It was people *smiling*. It was the washerwomen at the fountain laughing over something and going silent when the patrol passed, but not frightened-silent — *complicit*-silent. It was chalk marks appearing on doors, little woven circles hung above thresholds, strangers in the market pressing knots of braided cord into soldiers' hands and saying, "For luck, love," in a tone that suggested luck was currently available in bulk.

His corporal, a superstitious boy from the borderlands, had started wearing one of the cords under his armor. Brenner pretended not to notice. Then Brenner caught himself touching his own wrist where a market woman had grabbed him, and decided firmly that he had merely been checking his pulse.

"They say the old gods are back," the corporal said one evening, unasked, unwise.

"They say a lot of things."

"They say a boy came back from drowning. They say there's a field outside the north wall that—"

"I've seen the field."

"You've—" The corporal sat up. "You've *seen* it?"

"I've seen a barley crop," Brenner said, "which is the most exciting thing I've seen in this city since arrival." But his voice did the thing voices do when they're lying badly, and the corporal grinned, and Brenner turned away and looked at the walls of Vellsmark glowing in the sunset, beautiful and stubborn and full of people who believed something impossible was waking up.

He'd been ordered to report anomalies. He reported the usual: prices up, grain short, seditious pamphlets, the usual festival-crowd restlessness. He did not report the cords, or the candles, or the way the whole city seemed to be leaning toward something just out of earshot, like a congregation waiting for the first note.

Some things a soldier keeps in his chest, where they can't be used against anybody.

### The Road

They saw it first from the watchtower at Greyford Pass, sixty miles east: dust.

Not the dust of a supply column. Supply columns moved in tidy lines and raised polite amounts of dust. This was a horizon-wide brown haze, rolling toward the free cities like the world's largest and least friendly weather system, and beneath it — when Captain Ilse Varga of the Vellsmark Watch put her spyglass to it — the glint of pikes. Rank upon rank upon rank. The usurper's Third Army, which had spent two years sitting fat and idle in Osterhagen, was on the move.

"Where are they going?" asked her lieutenant.

Ilse lowered the glass slowly. The Third Army wasn't marching toward any border that mattered. Its line of advance pointed, straight and simple as an arrow drawn on a map, at the free cities. At Vellsmark.

"They're not going anywhere," she said. "They're coming here."

"But why now? There's been no provocation, no—"

"There's been *miracles*," Ilse said. She was not a religious woman; the Watch made you pragmatic about divinity, the same way sailors were pragmatic about storms. But she'd grown up on her grandmother's stories, and her grandmother had been very clear on one point, repeated like a warning nailed to a door: *when the old gods stir, the wolves come running, because everyone wants to own the thing that answers prayers.*

Three hundred years ago, the last time the Choir had sung, the war that followed had burned a century off the world.

She sent the rider within the hour. Two riders, in case one fell. Three, in case two fell, because Ilse Varga believed in redundancy the way other people believed in saints.

The message was brief, because urgent messages should be:

*Third Army moving west. Estimated arrival: twelve days. They know something. God help us all — and I mean that literally, and I never mean that literally.*

### The City

And in Vellsmark itself, in the hours when the riders galloped and the clerks filed and the armies turned, the ordinary people did what ordinary people always do with a miracle: they argued about it.

In the Gull's Rest tavern, a herring-woman named Bette held court. "I'm telling you, it's the Charmwright come again. My gran swore by him. Little charms, little kindnesses, and the gods leaning close to listen."

"The Charmwright's been dead three hundred years," said her brother.

"So's the magic, supposedly, and yet Tomas Brandt spat out the harbor and lived, didn't he?"

"That was a lucky pump and a strong woman."

"A strong woman who *prayed*, Joss."

In the Poor Quarter, the fever-house ran out of beds for visitors, because everyone with a sick relative wanted them near the place where the fevers kept breaking. In the temple district, the sanctioned priests of the Concordance preached sermons about superstition to congregations that nodded politely and then went home and hung little woven circles over their doors. In the market, a hedge-charmer's stall stood empty, and people had begun leaving things on it anyway — bread, buttons, a sprig of rowan — the way you leave flowers where somebody died, or was born, or both.

And underneath all of it, threading through the arguments and the prayers and the chalk marks, ran the fear, quiet as groundwater. Because everyone in Vellsmark knew the arithmetic of occupation. Wonder was dangerous. Hope was contraband. And somewhere in the grey offices on Candlemaker Street, folders were filling up, and folders filled up in only one direction.

On the last night of the month, every candle in Yannick's shop burned at once — all four hundred of them, though he'd only lit six — and the light stood in his windows like a held breath, and out in the dark beyond the walls, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of many boots keeping time.

Twelve days.

The city felt it coming the way a field feels rain: everything leaning, everything listening, everything waiting to find out whether what woke up kind — or what arrived first.
