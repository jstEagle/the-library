## 8. The Usurper's Question

The records arrived in three crates and a state of insult.

Inquisitor-General Aldous Vane stood over them in the candlelit archive of the Vellsmark garrison, gloves on, sleeves rolled to the precise midpoint of his forearm, and regarded the mess the way a surgeon regards a wound that has been dressed by an idiot. The crates had come from the Guildhall of Nine Names, seized eleven days ago in the night raid. Somewhere between the guildhall and this room, someone had decided the documents would travel better loose.

"Who packed these?" he asked.

Corporal Brech, who had the misfortune of being the most junior officer present and therefore the answer to every question Vane asked, straightened against the doorframe. "I believe it was the quartermaster's men, sir."

"I see." Vane lifted a sheaf of papers by one corner, as though it might bite. "And did the quartermaster's men also eat some of them? There appears to be a page missing from this ledger, unless the guild kept accounts in which every third entry was simply *gnawed*."

"A rat, possibly, sir."

"A rat with opinions about tallow purchases." Vane set the sheaf down and removed his gloves finger by finger, which was how he expressed displeasure without raising his voice. He had learned long ago that raised voices were what small men used when they lacked patience, and he considered patience the only weapon he had ever needed. "Brech. Find me the man who packed these. Do not punish him. Simply bring him here, let him look at me for a while, and then send him back to his duties. That will be sufficient."

"Yes, sir." Brech hesitated. "Sir—the men say the guildhall's haunted. Since the seizure. Lights in the windows at night, and—"

"The guildhall is empty, Corporal, because we emptied it. What the men are seeing is their own imaginations, lit badly." Vane turned back to the crates. "Though I will grant you this: if anything in this city deserves to be haunted, it is that building. Go."

When the corporal's boots had faded down the stair, Vane was alone with the dead guild's paper bones, and he permitted himself, at last, the smallest of smiles.

He liked archives. He liked them better than interrogations, though he was very good at both. A prisoner could lie; a prisoner could break, and a broken prisoner told you what you wanted to hear rather than what was true. But paper—paper was arrogant. Paper believed no one would ever read it carefully enough. Paper wrote things down in its own hand and then sat there for centuries, waiting to be caught.

Vane pulled the first intact ledger toward him, trimmed the wick of his lamp, and began to read.

* * *

It took him four nights to find it, and he found it, as the best discoveries always were, in something almost too dull to look at twice.

The guild's financial records were meticulous in the way of organizations with something to hide: every candle accounted for, every sack of flour, every fee paid to the city for market stalls. Nine names appeared again and again as signatories across two hundred years—nine family names, recurring like a chorus. And threaded through the mundane entries, in a clerk's cramped hand, were disbursements marked only with a symbol: a circle with nine points.

Nine-point circles bought milk. Nine-point circles bought wool, and honey, and soft-soled shoes in children's sizes. Nine-point circles paid midwives.

Vane sat back and looked at the pattern until the candle guttered, and then he fed it fresh wax and looked longer.

Children. The guild had been paying for children, generation after generation. Not orphans taken in—that would have been charity, and there was no warmth anywhere in these ledgers. This was maintenance. Upkeep. The way a lighthouse keeper might account for oil.

He began pulling the other documents, cross-referencing, and by the second watch of the fifth night he had assembled on his table a chain of inference so clean it was nearly beautiful:

The Guild of Nine Names had not merely served the old gods. It had *held* them. Every folk-tale agreed the gods had gone silent three hundred years ago; every heresy trial record Vane had ever read assumed they had died or fled. But the guild's own private correspondence—what survived of it—used a different vocabulary entirely. *The binding holds. The ninth voice weakens. We must find another throat.*

Another throat.

Vane rose, walked to the window, and looked out over Vellsmark at night: the crooked rooftops, the harbor lamps, the smoke of ten thousand ordinary fires. Somewhere down there, if the ledgers were honest—and paper usually was—a person was walking around with a god folded up inside them like a letter in a pocket, and probably didn't know it, or knew it and was hiding, which amounted to the same thing and was far more interesting.

There was a knock. "Enter," he said, without turning.

His adjutant, Lieutenant Harsk, came in carrying a folder and the particular stiffness of a man delivering news he expected to be shot for. Harsk was not a coward—he had once held a bridge for six hours with a broken arm—but he had worked for Vane long enough to know that the General's anger was never loud, only thorough.

"Report," said Vane.

"The sweep of the night market, sir. Three nights ago. We detained forty-one unlicensed practitioners. Forty are exactly what the files predicted—hedge-charmers, fortune-sellers, the usual residue. One escaped." Harsk laid a sketch on the table. "A young man, charm-seller's stall, east row. Witness statements agree he used a working in the open. Not a hedge-trick, sir. The witnesses disagree on everything except the effect itself. One says the crowd forgot him. Another says the lanterns went out. A third swears the ground *leaned*, which is not a sentence I enjoy writing down."

"And the description?"

"Young. Dark-haired. Unremarkable, by all accounts, which the witnesses repeat with unusual insistence." Harsk paused. "Almost as if the insistence itself were part of the effect."

Vane studied the sketch. It showed a face like a hundred faces in any market crowd—the artist had captured nothing, or been made to capture nothing. He found this more convincing than any portrait would have been. Magic that hid well was magic that had practiced hiding, and magic that had practiced hiding belonged to someone who understood what was hunting it.

"Name?"

"No stall license under any name we can match. The market registrar's book for that evening has a smudge where the east-row entries should be."

"A smudge."

"An ink smear, sir. As if a hand passed over wet ink."

Vane allowed himself a slow breath through the nose. It was almost funny. Almost. Somewhere in this damp, stubborn, fish-smelling city, a young man was erasing himself from the record with a fingertip, and doing it competently, and doing it *calmly*, which meant training, which meant inheritance, which meant—

Which meant the ledgers were right.

"Thank you, Lieutenant. Leave the folder."

Harsk lingered. "Sir, if I may—the men are asking questions. About the miracles. The drowned boy at Tanner's Wharf who started breathing again. The field off the Mill Road. Superstition spreads faster than plague, and half the garrison's from the provinces where the old rites never quite died. They're wondering whether we should be... worried."

"Worried." Vane turned from the window. "Lieutenant, do you know the difference between a miracle and a leak?"

"Sir?"

"A miracle is a wonder. A leak is a wonder that hasn't been assigned an owner yet." He tapped the ledger with one finger. "Something is answering prayers in this city. Something old, something powerful, and something, at present, *unclaimed*. The usurper does not fear wonders, Harsk. He fears wonders belonging to other people. Our task is to arrive at the ownership question before anyone else does. Is that worrying? No. That is Tuesday."

Harsk blinked. "Yes, sir."

"That will be all."

* * *

Alone again, Vane did what he always did before a decision: he made tea.

Not because he wanted tea. Because the ritual of it—water measured, leaves timed, the pot warmed first—kept his mind moving in grooves while the deeper machinery turned underneath. His mother had taught him the ritual, back when she was still alive and still pretending their family was nothing more than it appeared. He thought of her sometimes with something adjacent to affection. She had lied beautifully, right up until the end.

Because that was the truth Vane carried, the one that made him patient where other men were cruel: he knew what vessel-blood felt like from the inside. Distant, diluted, three generations thinned—but real. He had spent his boyhood being careful in ways other boys never had to be, and his manhood turning that carefulness into a career, and now the empire's throne sat occupied by a cousin whose blood ran the same channel as his own, thinner still but flowing, waiting.

The usurper wanted the Choir. Of course he did. Any heir of the Nine Names who learned what the guild had done would want it—it was legacy, it was power, it was the family birthright stolen by a pack of pious clerks and locked away for three centuries. Vane's orders reflected that wanting. Find the vessel. Secure the vessel.

But Vane had read the ledgers more closely than his master ever would, and he understood something the usurper did not: a vessel was not a weapon. A vessel was a *key*. And keys, unlike weapons, could not simply be pointed. They had to be *turned*, gently, by someone who understood the lock.

You did not break a key to teach it obedience. You broke locks.

He poured the tea, drank it standing, and set the cup down with a click that sounded, in the silent archive, like a decision.

"Harsk," he called.

The lieutenant appeared with commendable speed. "Sir."

"Two sets of orders. First: dispatch to the capital, coded, reporting that the guild records confirm active vessel descent in Vellsmark. Phrase it carefully—we do not use the word 'vessel' in writing. Say 'a person of interest matching historical criteria.' The usurper will understand; no one else will."

"And the second set, sir?"

"The second set stays here." Vane crossed to the map of the city pinned above the table, took up a pencil, and drew a slow, deliberate circle around Vellsmark. Then, at each gate, each road, each landing where a boat might slip the harbor in the dark, he made a small, neat mark. "No one leaves the city. No one enters. Merchants grumble, fishermen rage, the bread gets expensive—I don't care. Seal it quietly, seal it completely, and seal it *patiently*. If our young friend in the east row tries to run, I want him stopped without a scratch. If he tries to hide, I want the city shrunk around him until hiding is the same as surrender."

"He'll resist, sir. They always do."

"They always do," Vane agreed pleasantly. "And then they discover that resistance requires allies, and allies require trust, and trust is the easiest thing in the world to intercept. Have the census rolls brought to me tomorrow. Market registries, tax lists, baptismal records going back twenty years. Somewhere in this city's paperwork is a young man who doesn't quite add up, and I am very good at arithmetic."

Harsk gathered his folder, then paused at the door. "And if he won't come willingly, sir? If we have to take him by force?"

Vane looked at the sketch of the unremarkable face, at the smudged ink, at the nine-pointed circles marching down two centuries of ledgers, and he smiled the smile that had made three heretics confess and one provincial governor resign.

"Lieutenant, we are not going to hurt him. Whatever else happens in this city, that young man arrives at my table healthy, rested, and reasonably comfortable. You cannot play an instrument you've cracked." He picked up his tea, found it cold, and drank it anyway. "Besides—if the gods really are listening again, I'd rather they watched us be courteous. Fear makes vessels hide. Kindness makes them curious. And curiosity, Harsk, walks through doors on its own."

The lieutenant saluted and left.

Vane stood a while longer at the map, looking at the little circle he'd drawn around the city, and thought about the drowned boy breathing on Tanner's Wharf, and the field greening off the Mill Road, and somewhere in the tangle of lamplit streets below, a pair of hands that could erase ink with a touch.

"Soon," he told the city softly, the way one speaks to a thing already caught. "Take your time. Finish your errands. Say your goodbyes."

He rolled the map closed with great care, as though it were something living, and blew out the lamp.
