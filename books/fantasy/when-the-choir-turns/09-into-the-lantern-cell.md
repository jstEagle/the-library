## 9. Into the Lantern Cell

The cellar door was behind a fishmonger's stall, which Hazel thought was either very clever or very stupid, depending on how much you liked the smell of herring.

Corvin knocked a rhythm on the boards—two, pause, three, pause, two—and a slot slid open at knee height. A pair of eyes appeared in the gap, young and suspicious.

"Grey weather," Corvin said.

"Wet nets," the eyes replied, and the door swung inward on darkness that smelled of tallow smoke, wet stone, and—underneath it all, faint but unmistakable to a hedge-charmer's nose—old magic. Not his kind of magic. Something older, kept like a relic in a back room.

"After you," Corvin said, with a little bow that managed to be both courteous and mocking. "Mind the third step. It's a liar."

The step was indeed a liar. It looked solid and gave half an inch under Hazel's boot, and he stumbled into the arms of a large woman holding a lantern, who caught him with one hand as though catching stumbling men were a chore she performed daily before breakfast.

"You're the charmer," she said, looking him over the way a horse trader looks at a nag.

"I'm a salve-seller."

"Mm." She handed him off to no one in particular and shouted down the corridor, "Sera! Your stray's here!"

*Your stray.* Hazel felt something twist in his chest—not quite offense, not quite warmth. He had spent three years being nobody to everybody, and there was an odd, disorienting comfort in being somebody's anything, even a stray.

They went down. The corridor sloped beneath the fishmonger's cellar into a warren of rooms that had clearly once been merchant storage and now held pallets, cookfires vented through ingenious clay pipes, crates stamped with grain merchants' marks that Hazel suspected had never contained grain. People moved through the lamplight: a boy mending a net, two women arguing quietly over a map, an old man sharpening a knife with the patience of someone who had done nothing else for years. Perhaps forty souls, maybe more, tucked under the city like coins under a mattress.

And every few steps, someone touched him. A hand on his arm. A squeeze of his shoulder. One girl, no older than twelve, pressed a rag doll into his hands and said, "For luck," and ran off before he could give it back.

He stood holding the doll while Corvin watched him with that insufferable half-smile.

"They know what I am," Hazel said. It wasn't a question.

"Sera told them. Carefully. *A vessel of the old blood has come to us.*" Corvin shrugged. "Half of them have been praying since before the prayers started answering. You're not a man to them, friend. You're a door."

"A door," Hazel repeated. He set the doll down on a crate with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "I've been called worse. Usually by Magda."

*Magda.* The word landed in his own chest like a dropped stone. Three days now since he'd knelt at her bedside and heard what the guild had actually done—heard that the Nine Names weren't servants of gods but jailers of them, that the Choir was a chain sung by mortal throats, and that he was its last knot. He hadn't slept much since. When he did sleep, he dreamed of knots.

"Come on," Corvin said softly, and for once there was no mockery in it. "She'll want to see you before the others do."

* * *

Sera received him in a small room lined with shelves of salvaged books, their spines cracked, their gilt long since peeled away by honest hands or dishonest ones. She was younger than Hazel had imagined—younger than Magda, certainly; younger than he'd expected anyone to be who commanded forty people and a warren full of knives. Dark hair cut short and practical, ink stains on her fingers, a burn scar along her jaw that she wore without apology or explanation.

She was writing when he entered, and she finished her line before she looked up, which told him more about her than any speech could have.

"Hazel Eriksen," she said. "Hedge-charmer. Luck-knots, fever-salves, birth-blessings sold as 'traditional remedies.' Three years clean in Vellsmark, which is more than most of my people can say." She gestured to a chair. "Sit. Please."

"You've had me investigated."

"I've had everyone investigated. It's how we're all still alive." She leaned back. "I'm sorry about your guildmother. Corvin says she raised you from eleven."

"She did." He sat, because his legs decided to without consulting him. "You knew her?"

"We tried to recruit her for twenty years. She told me once—I was nineteen, full of fire—that revolutions are just fires that haven't learned what they're burning yet." Sera smiled, and it was a real smile, tired around the edges. "I hated her for it. Then I grew up and understood she was right, and hated her a little less, and kept the fire anyway. Someone has to."

"And now the prayers are answering," Hazel said. "And you think that's your fire."

"I think," Sera said carefully, "that for three hundred years the empire has told the free cities that the old gods abandoned us. That our defeat was divine judgment. That we should be grateful for the mercy of occupation." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it hummed like a struck wire. "And now, in the space of a season, a drowned child breathes in the Salt Quarter. A blighted field greens overnight in Lowmarsh. Fevers break that no physician can explain. Do you know what that means to people who have been told for generations that heaven has forgotten them?"

"It means hope," Hazel said.

"It means they stop kneeling." She stood and crossed to the window slit, high and narrow, letting in a blade of grey daylight. "Vane sealed the gates last week. Grain prices have tripled. There are queues outside the bakeries at dawn, and do you know what they sing in those queues? Old songs. Songs their grandmothers told them never to sing aloud. The empire banned the names, and the names came back anyway, and now the people are remembering that they were never meant to kneel at all."

Hazel turned the rag doll memory over in his mind—the girl's face, fierce and certain. "And where do I fit in this? Because I've noticed that everyone who talks to me lately wants something. Vane wants a weapon. You want—" He spread his hands. "What do you want, Sera?"

"The same thing you want," she said. "For it to stop. For the free cities to be free. For children to grow up without learning which words get their parents taken in the night." She came back and crouched in front of his chair, so their eyes were level, and hers were steady and warm and absolutely certain. "But I'm not going to lie to you, Hazel. I want you to help. And I'm asking, not ordering, because you're not mine and never will be. But hear me out."

He heard her out.

* * *

The plan, as she laid it out over the next hour, was simple in the way that plans made by desperate people are simple. The harvest festival was coming—the Festival of Ashes, the one day the whole city crowded into the temple square, the one day even inquisitors couldn't disperse a crowd without making martyrs. If the faithful rose together—

"—they need something to rise *around*," Sera said. "Not a pamphlet. Not a speech. Pamphlets burn and speeches die. They need a miracle with a face. They need to see the old blood stand in the square and sing the opening verse of the Choir, the way the guildmothers sang it in the old days, and watch the sky answer."

Hazel's stomach dropped somewhere below the fishmonger's cellar. "You want me to sing."

"I want you to *lead*. Singing is—" she waved a hand, "—the form it takes. Corvin says you've already worked magic in public. That the market charm answered you."

"The market charm was a fever-charm and it barely counted—"

"A drowned child breathed, Hazel." She said it gently, but she didn't look away. "In Lowmarsh. The morning after you passed through. Don't tell me it barely counts."

"That wasn't me. Or—it wasn't *only* me. I don't control it. That's rather the point of everything Magda told me, which I notice you already know, so let's not pretend otherwise." He stood, because sitting made him feel like a man being fitted for a uniform. "You know what the Choir is. Corvin told you. You know it isn't a hymn, it's a *chain*, and you're asking me to rattle it in front of forty thousand people."

"I know what Magda told you," Sera said. "And I know that Magda belonged to a guild that kept secrets from its own vessels for three hundred years, so forgive me if I don't take her version as scripture." She paused, and her voice softened. "But here's what I believe, and I'll say it plainly because you deserve plainness. I believe the gods are waking whether we sing or not. I believe the empire will find some way to use that—or kill everyone trying. And I believe that if the old blood stands with the people instead of above them, then whatever wakes, it wakes *free*, among free people, and we face it together. That's all. No chains. No weapons. Just—witness."

It was a beautiful speech. Hazel gave it full credit. It was also, he noticed, constructed the way his luck-knots were constructed: every knot true, the pattern arranged to lead the eye exactly where the maker wanted.

"And if I say no?" he asked.

"Then you say no." She said it easily, without flinching. "You stay here as long as you like. You eat at our table. Nobody touches you, nobody pressures you, and when the gates open again—if they open—we find you passage south. My word on it, in front of witnesses."

"And until then?"

"Until then," Sera said, "you're one of us. Which means soup at sundown, a bunk that smells of other people, and Maren teaching you knife-work whether you want to learn or not." She smiled again, that tired real smile. "Belonging first, Hazel. Asking second. That's the whole difference between us and the inquisition."

* * *

They gave him a bunk in a side chamber that had once stored pickled things, and if the walls still whispered faintly of brine, it was warmer than any bed he'd had since Magda's house went cold. Supper was barley soup with onions and one miraculous piece of salt pork shared forty ways, and Hazel ate it cross-legged on the floor between the net-mending boy and the old knife-sharpener, who introduced himself as Tam and immediately began critiquing Hazel's posture.

"You sit like a scholar," Tam said. "Scholars die first."

"What do scholars die of?"

"Books falling on them." Tam pointed his spoon. "Also inquisitors. Mostly inquisitors."

Around them the cellar hummed. Someone had produced a battered fiddle, playing soft and low, and a woman was singing an old harvest song with the god-names swapped out for nonsense syllables—the way everyone sang them now, the way everyone had sung them for three hundred years—and halfway through the verse, without seeming to decide to, the whole room picked up the tune and put the true names back in.

*Omma of the deep water, hear us.*
*Bren of the standing stone, remember.*

Forty voices in a cellar under a fishmonger, singing forbidden names at conversational volume, and nobody stopped, and nobody flinched, and the candle flames didn't so much as gutter. Hazel sat very still with his bowl in his hands and felt something in his chest crack along a seam he'd been pretending wasn't there.

Three years of careful silence. Three years of luck-knots and lowered eyes and *never pray aloud, Hazel, never, never*. And here was belonging, served up with barley soup, asking nothing of him but that he eat.

That was the trap of it, he realized later, lying awake on his brine-scented bunk. That was the beautiful, terrible genius of it. Vane would have chained him in iron and been done. Sera simply made him *family*, and family was a knot far harder to cut.

* * *

He found her past midnight, as he'd known he would, bent over her maps by candle stub. She didn't startle when he sat down across from her. She just waited, pen lifted, patient as stone.

"If I do this," Hazel said. "If I stand in that square and sing. What happens after?"

"After the uprising? The gates come down. The garrison surrenders or doesn't. The free cities follow, one after another—they're waiting for a sign, and we'd be giving them one written in fire."

"And the gods, Sera. What happens to the gods?"

The pen hovered. For just a moment—half a heartbeat, no longer—something flickered behind her certainty, some flicker of the thing Magda had seen in her at nineteen. Then it was gone, banked like a covered coal.

"The gods," she said, "will finally be home."

It was a good answer. It was even, probably, a true answer, as far as she knew it to be true.

Hazel nodded slowly, and thought of knots, and of the way a well-made pattern leads the eye exactly where the maker wants.

"All right," he said. "Teach me the verse."

Her whole face lit—not triumph, he noted, or not only triumph, but something brighter and more dangerous, the look of a woman watching a door open onto everything she'd fought for. She reached across the table and gripped his hand, ink-stained fingers against his calloused ones.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Tomorrow we begin."

And far below them, deeper than any cellar dug by human hands, something vast and bound and listening turned over in its sleep—and, hearing its own name spoken gently in a fishmonger's cellar, began—for the first time in three hundred years—to pull.
