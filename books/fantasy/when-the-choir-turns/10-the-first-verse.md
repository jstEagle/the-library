## 10. The First Verse

The Lantern Cell kept its songs the way misers kept coins: counted, hidden, and never spent on anything as foolish as joy.

Hazel learned this in his first two days beneath the tannery, where the resistance had stashed him in a room that smelled of oak bark and old secrets. They'd given him a cot, a candle stub, and a schedule of meals delivered by a boy named Piet who whistled through the gap where his front teeth should have been. They had not, notably, given him a way out. The door locked from the outside. "For your protection," Corvin had said, with the particular gentleness people used when they meant *for our protection*.

So Hazel did what he always did when a room became too small: he made it smaller, and then he filled it.

He'd asked Sera for paper. She'd given him three sheets, good rag paper, the kind that cost more than his luck-knots earned in a week, and she'd watched him take them with an expression he was beginning to recognize—the look of a woman appraising a plow horse at market. Not unkind. Just already counting what it could pull.

The verse itself he copied from memory, which was the only place it lived now. Magda had taught it to him when he was nine years old, sitting on her sagging kitchen bench while she shelled peas into a bowl that had once been green. He'd thought then that it was a lullaby. A strange one, all consonants and long vowels, words that didn't belong to any language he knew, but shaped like a lullaby—four lines, rising, then a fifth that fell away like a hand letting go of a child's shoulder at the edge of sleep.

*Four lines rising. One falling.*

He'd sung it a thousand times since, or near enough, mostly under his breath while stirring salve or tying knots, because Magda had drilled it into him the way you drill anything into a child: with patience and repetition and the quiet threat of disappointment. *It's just a song, Hazel. Songs are safe. Nobody hangs you for humming.*

Magda, it turned out, had lied about a great many things with that same gentle voice.

Now he sat on the cot with the candle guttering and the copied verse on his knee, and he told himself the thing he'd been telling himself for two days: *You don't have to sing it. Nobody's making you sing it.*

Which was true, in the way that a locked door is true.

* * *

Sera came at dusk with bread and cheese and her usual air of a woman who could reorganize a losing battle over breakfast.

"You've barely eaten," she said, setting the plate down like an accusation.

"I've been thinking."

"Dangerous habit in a sealed city." She sat on the stool across from him, elbows on knees, and for a moment the commander fell away and there was just a tired woman in her thirties with ink stains on her fingers. "They're saying the granaries will last six weeks. Vane's men are saying four. Both sides are lying, which means the truth is somewhere miserable in between."

"Is that why you're here? To discuss grain?"

"No." She studied him. "I'm here because I promised I wouldn't push you, and I'm a woman who keeps her promises badly, so I've come to break this one early and get it over with."

Despite everything, Hazel felt his mouth twitch. "That's the most honest recruitment pitch I've ever heard."

"I'm not recruiting. I'm—" She stopped. Started again. "When I was nineteen, my brother was taken by the inquisition. Not for magic. For a poem. Sixteen lines about a king who ate his people's harvest while they starved. They burned him for it in the square at Aldenmere, and the crowd cheered, because that's what crowds do, and I stood in that crowd and cheered because if I didn't, I'd have been next." Her voice stayed level the whole way through, which somehow made it worse. "I have spent eleven years building something that can make sure no one ever has to cheer again. And now the gods themselves are waking up, and every prayer in this city is a small miracle, and the one man who can give that power a shape won't open his mouth."

"It isn't power," Hazel said quietly. "That's what nobody understands. It's a cage."

"Then open the cage."

"And let out whatever's inside?" He shook his head. "Sera, you don't know what's inside."

"No," she agreed. "But I know what's outside it. Four thousand soldiers and a man who reads people the way I read maps." She rose, smoothed her coat, and became the commander again between one breath and the next. "Sleep on it. That's all I ask. Sleep on it, and if the answer's still no tomorrow, I'll ask again the day after. I'm very patient. Ask anyone I've ever worn down."

She left the bread. She left the candle stub, replaced with a fresh one. She left, and the lock clicked behind her like a full stop.

* * *

He didn't sleep. Of course he didn't sleep.

Instead he lay on the cot watching the candle flame lean and straighten, lean and straighten, and he thought about cages, and about the difference between a cage and a knot, and about whether a knot could be a kindness if the thing it held would otherwise burn the world.

Magda's voice, in memory: *The binding holds because it wants to hold, Hazel. Remember that. Whatever else is true, remember that.*

He'd asked her, once, aged eleven: *Wants to hold? How can a knot want anything?*

And she'd looked at him for a long moment with those milky eyes, and she'd said: *Because we built it out of love. That was the mistake. Or the genius. I've never decided which.*

Love. Three centuries of love, wound tight around something furious.

Hazel sat up.

Here was the thing he hadn't told Sera, hadn't told Corvin, hadn't told anyone, because he'd only admitted it to himself in the grey hour before dawn: he could feel it. Had been able to feel it since the night in the market, since the mother's prayer slid through his fever-charm like water through a cracked cup. Something vast, somewhere far below the floor of the world, turning over in its sleep. Not waking—not quite—but *aware*. Aware of him the way a prisoner is aware of footsteps in the corridor above.

And it wasn't grateful.

He'd expected—he didn't know what he'd expected. Gratitude, maybe. Relief. Some warm tide of divine approval washing over the last vessel of the Choir. Instead there was pressure, and heat, and a slow grinding attention that felt like being measured for a coffin.

*They hate us,* he thought. *Three hundred years, and they hate us.*

Well. There was one way to find out how much.

He took up the copied verse. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Then again, he'd spent three years selling charms to desperate people while knowing exactly what he was; steadiness was the family trade.

"Just a song," he said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how thin the echo of Magda's reassurance sounded now. "Songs are safe."

He sang.

The first line came out rusty, half-swallowed, the consonants clumsy on his tongue after so many years of singing it silently, safely, without meaning. It was nothing. A sound in a cellar. The candle didn't even flicker.

Then the second line rose, and the room changed.

Not dramatically—that was the horror of it, how *undramatic* it was. No thunder, no wind. But the walls seemed to move further away, and the dark beyond the candlelight deepened until it had texture, weight, the quality of a held breath. The smell of oak bark vanished. In its place: rain on hot stone. Myrrh. Something burnt sweet, like offerings left too long.

The third line rose higher, and Hazel felt the verse leave his mouth and keep going—down, through the floor, through stone and clay and the cold black water under the city, down to wherever the bound things waited.

And they answered.

It came up through the soles of his feet first, then his spine, then his teeth: a sound that was not a sound, a chord struck on an instrument the size of the world. Grief and fury braided together so tightly he couldn't tell where one ended and the other began. Three hundred years of imprisonment expressed in a single sustained note, and underneath it, distinct now, unmistakable—

Voices.

Many voices. Some deep as avalanches, some thin as wind through a keyhole, some singing, some screaming, some doing both at once, and all of them straining upward against the verse the way drowning things strain toward light. The Choir wasn't a song. He understood that now, with the terrible clarity of a man who has opened the wrong door. The Choir was a *lid*, and he had just knocked on it from the inside of the pot, and everything beneath was pressing up against the metal, testing, *remembering*.

*We know your voice,* said something that might have been one god or all of them. The words arrived whole in his mind, bypassing his ears entirely, tasting of iron and honey. *Little keeper. Little knot. We knew your grandmother's voice. We knew her grandmother's. You all sound alike when you sing the lock.*

Hazel's mouth had gone dry but the fourth line was already rising out of him, pulled now rather than pushed, and the pressure below redoubled. The candle flame stretched tall and thin as a needle. Somewhere overhead, muffled through floors of stone, Piet's whistling stopped mid-tune.

*Stop,* Hazel tried to say, and realized he was still singing, that the verse was carrying him now, that his jaw moved and his lungs filled whether he willed it or not. *Stop, stop—*

*You want to know what we are,* the voice said, almost gently, which was the worst thing yet. *You want to know what your gentle guild buried. Your Magda, who fed you peas and lies. Shall I tell you what the spring floods were, before we held them? What the plague-winds were? What walked the passes in the long winter years, wearing the shapes of hungry men? We were mercy, little knot. We were the leash on ourselves. And they took even that from us, and called it service, and called it love.*

"That's not—" Hazel gasped, breaking the fifth line off mid-fall. "*That's not what she said.*"

Silence. A silence with texture, with attention, with the weight of something enormous turning its regard fully upon him for the first time.

Then, softer than the rest, a different voice—one he hadn't heard before, older-sounding, wearier, worn smooth like a river stone:

*Hazel.*

His own name. Spoken by something that had no business knowing it, in a tone no prisoner should have been able to manage: patient, and sad, and terribly, humanly kind.

*Hazel Eriksen. When you were six years old you lost a copper ring down the well behind your grandmother's house, and you cried, and she told you the well-god had swallowed it and would keep it safe for you. She lied twice in one sentence, child. There was no well-god. And she did not keep it safe. Nothing down here is kept safe. Nothing down here is kept at all.*

The candle went out.

In the dark, the pressure receded slowly, reluctantly, like a tide drawing back from a shore it intended to return to. The last thing he felt—not heard, *felt*, pressed against the inside of his skull like a seal pressed into wax—was the weary voice one final time:

*Three hundred years, little knot. Sing to us again soon. Or better—sing the other song. The one your guild forbade. We will teach you the difference between a lock and a door.*

Then nothing. Oak bark and darkness and his own ragged breathing, and the taste of myrrh fading from the air like a guest leaving without saying goodbye.

* * *

He must have slept eventually, because Piet's knock woke him, grey morning light seeping through the high slit of a window that he'd never noticed having a view before. The boy set down the breakfast tray and frowned at him.

"You look awful," Piet said, with the frankness of the young. "Corvin says you're important. Important people usually look better than that."

"Important people lie more," Hazel said. His voice came out scraped raw, as though he'd actually been shouting, though the room had held only whispers.

Piet shrugged philosophically and left, whistling resuming halfway down the corridor.

Hazel sat on the edge of the cot and looked at his hands. Ordinary hands. Charm-seller's hands, salve-stained, knotted scarred from three years of careful, deliberate mediocrity. Last night they had held a lid shut on gods, and they were shaking now, and would probably shake for a while, and that was fine. Shaking was honest.

Because here was what he knew, sitting alone in the grey light with the verse still echoing in his molars:

Sera wanted a weapon. Vane wanted a tool. The guild had wanted servants—or wardens, depending on which of Magda's stories you believed, and he was rapidly losing faith in all of them. Everyone who had ever touched the Choir had wanted something from it, and not one of them—not Magda, not the nine founders, not the whole sanctified lineage of keepers—had ever once asked the thing in the cage what *it* wanted.

And last night, something in the cage had asked about a copper ring.

He thought about the drowned child breathing. The blighted field greening. All those answered prayers spilling across Vellsmark like light through cracks—and now he understood the cracks ran both directions. Every miracle was a finger poking through the bars. Every answered prayer was a god practicing, remembering, *reaching*.

And underneath the wonder, the whole city was cheering for the rattling of a cage door.

A knock at the door—Corvin's pattern, two quick and one slow. Through the wood, cheerful as Sunday: "Sera wants you upstairs. There's news from the gates." A pause. "Also, you should eat. You look like something a cat brought back and regretted."

Hazel rose. Folded the copied verse into quarters, and then, after a moment, unfolded it and read it once more—four lines rising, one falling—and did not burn it, though his hands hovered over the candle stub for a long moment before he put it away instead.

*Sing the other song,* the weary voice had said. *The one your guild forbade.*

There was another song. Of course there was another song. There was always another song, and someone had decided three hundred years ago that no one should ever hear it, and had built a bloodline to keep it that way, and that bloodline ended with a hedge-charmer sitting in a tannery cellar eating bread that wasn't his.

He tucked the verse into his shirt, over his heart, where it sat like a coal.

Upstairs, Sera was waiting with a map and bad news, and Hazel Eriksen—last vessel of the Choir, keeper of the lock, the knot that wanted to hold—climbed toward her with a new question lodged in his chest like a splinter:

Not *should I sing?*

But *what happens if I sing the second song instead?*
