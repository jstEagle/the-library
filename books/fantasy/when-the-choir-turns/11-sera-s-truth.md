## 11. Sera's Truth

Sera Voss had learned to lie in church.

Not the way most people did—mumbling through the liturgy while their thoughts wandered to bread and debts and other people's husbands. No. She had learned it properly, at her mother's knee, in the little Lantern shrine behind the tannery where the faithful of the old ways still gathered when gathering was punishable by fine. Her mother had taught her that a lie told well was a lantern: you cupped your hands around the flame so the wind couldn't find it, and you carried it exactly as far as it needed to go, and no farther.

*The gods are sleeping,* her mother had said, every week for eleven years, to a room full of people who knew perfectly well that the gods were bound, because saying *bound* aloud was how you ended up in an inquisitor's ledger. *Sleeping, and dreaming of us.*

It was a lie. It was also, Sera had come to believe, the truest thing anyone had ever said in that shrine. Because the gods were dreaming of them. She was sure of it now. She had been sure since the night Hazel Eriksen sang three lines of an old verse in a cellar full of candle stubs and every flame in the room had leaned toward him like flowers toward sun.

She stood at the window of the safehouse above the cooper's yard and watched him below, in the yard, trying to teach Corvin to braid a luck-knot. He was failing. Corvin had the manual dexterity of a man wearing mittens made of hams, and Hazel had the patience of a saint who had never actually been asked to do anything difficult, and between them they had produced something that looked less like a charm and more like a bird's nest after a storm.

"He'll ruin him," said Tam, from the corner, where he was cleaning a crossbow he was not licensed to own. "The vessel of the Choir, last hope of the free cities, and Corvin's teaching him to swear."

"Corvin's teaching him to laugh," Sera said. "One of those is more useful than it sounds."

"And the other?"

"The swearing. The swearing will be useful too, if we're honest."

Tam grunted, which from Tam was a standing ovation. She left him to his crossbow and went down into the yard, because there were things that needed doing, and she had discovered over the past two days that all of them somehow routed through Hazel Eriksen, whether he liked it or not.

He looked up when she came out, and smiled—that particular smile of his, warm and slightly apologetic, like a man apologizing for the weather. He had been in the Cell four days now. In four days he had fixed the leaky roof with a working so small and elegant that Old Piet the cooper had wept, charmed the rats out of the grain store (they had left in an orderly line, which Tam refused to discuss), and taught half the household a children's rhyme about knot-braiding that Sera suspected contained, folded inside its nonsense syllables, at least one genuine invocation.

He did it without thinking. That was the thing about him. He was like a man who whistled without noticing, except that what whistled out of him rearranged the world.

"Sera," he said. "Tell Corvin that a luck-knot is supposed to have *ends*."

"It has ends," Corvin protested, holding up his monstrosity. "These are ends."

"That's a fraying," Hazel said.

"That's *character*."

Sera laughed, and let herself laugh, and hated herself a little for how easily she did it. Four days. Four days of soup and knot-lessons and Corvin's terrible jokes, and she could feel it happening—the thing she had sworn wouldn't happen. They were becoming fond of him. He was becoming one of theirs. And every hour that passed made what she intended to ask of him easier and made what she intended *not* to tell him heavier, until the two weights balanced on either side of her chest like a yoke.

"Hazel," she said. "Walk with me? There's somewhere I want to show you."

* * *

The Lantern Cell kept its oldest secret under a tannery, because inquisitors would search a hundred churches before they searched a place that smelled like that.

Her mother had shown her the door when Sera was nine years old, down a stair that smelled of oak bark and lime, past racks of hides hung like flayed ghosts. At the bottom, behind a false wall, was a room that predated the occupation by two centuries. Predated the usurper. Predated, some said, the guild itself.

The walls were covered in names.

Hundreds of them, thousands, inked and re-inked across generations, in hands that ranged from careful clerk's script to the scrawl of someone writing by feel in the dark. Not the Nine Names of the guild—those were carved above the lintel, formal and cold. These were smaller names. Ordinary ones. *Maren, who held the door at Kessel Bridge.* *Old Jutte, who fed us through the famine winter.* *Bram, ten years old, who lied beautifully and died badly.*

"The Ledger of the Kept Flame," Sera said. Hazel stood very still in the doorway, the way people did in this room. Everyone did. Even inquisitors, if they'd ever found it—and thank every sleeping god they hadn't—would have gone quiet. "Every name is someone who kept the faith when keeping it cost something. My mother added three before I was born. I've added one."

Hazel reached out and touched the wall, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Who?"

"A boy named Wren. He ran messages for us. The inquisitors took him last spring." She kept her voice level. It had taken years to get it level. "He was fourteen. He never gave up a single name. I checked the transcripts myself—I have a friend who cleans the barracks, and she has a friend who copies documents. He gave them nothing but weather reports. Six weeks of weather reports." She smiled, and it hurt her face. "They were accurate, at least. He was a conscientious boy."

Hazel's hand stayed on the wall. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," she said, and heard the edge come into her voice before she could blunt it. "Be *here*. That's what this room is for. It's not a memorial, Hazel. It's a roster. Every name on that wall is a promise that somebody comes next."

He turned and looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the hedge-charmer fall away and something older stand in his place—the bloodline looking out through a tired man's eyes. "You didn't bring me down here to show me names."

"No," she admitted. "I brought you here because this is the only room in Vellsmark where I can say true things."

And there it was. The moment. She had rehearsed it on the walk over, the way she rehearsed everything: *the Choir wasn't a gift, it was a chain; the guild built it; your family forged the links; the gods are prisoners and you are the key.* All of it true. All of it hers to give or withhold.

She thought of her mother, cupping her hands around the lie so the wind couldn't find it.

She thought of Wren, fourteen, giving six weeks of weather reports.

"Hazel," she said, "what do you know about why the gods went silent?"

* * *

He told her what Magda had told him—or part of it. Sera listened with her heart going like a drum in a procession, because she knew the shape of the story before he finished it, and she knew he didn't. He thought he was telling her a revelation. He was telling her the first chapter.

"They bound them," he said. His voice was strange, flat, the flatness of a man standing on ground that has just moved. "Three centuries ago. The Choir wasn't service—it was a cage. My whole line, we weren't priests. We were jailers. Wardens." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "I spent my life hiding a gift I thought was holy, and it turns out it's a set of keys."

"Yes," Sera said.

"And the gods are furious. When I tried the verse, they—" He stopped. She watched him decide how much to say. "One of them spoke my name. Like it had been waiting three hundred years to say it."

"I believe you."

"So what do we do?" He spread his hands. "That's why you brought me here, isn't it? You want to free them. That's what the Cell believes—the old gods returning means liberation, the empire's proscriptions were right for the wrong reasons, the whole rotten binding comes down—"

"Yes," Sera said again. And then, because she had promised herself she would say it, because some small stubborn honest thing in her had clawed its way up her throat: "That's what the Cell believes. It isn't quite what I intend."

The silence in the room had weight. The names on the walls seemed to lean in.

"Explain," Hazel said quietly.

She walked to the wall and put her palm flat against Wren's name. "When the guild bound the gods, they didn't bind their power. You understand? The power is still there—it's the largest thing in the world, it's the reason prayers are being answered again, it's the reason a drowned child in Harrow Lane breathed and a blighted field outside the walls came up green overnight. The binding doesn't stop the river. It just builds the millrace. Directs it. Three centuries ago, the guild aimed that power at protecting the free cities, and it worked, and the price was that the gods screamed behind the dam for three hundred years." She turned to face him. "I don't intend to break the dam, Hazel. I intend to turn the wheel."

His face went very carefully blank. She had seen that look on soldiers. "Aimed at what?"

"At the usurper. At the empire. At every grey coat and every ledger and every gallows they've built in our cities for thirty years." Her voice rose despite herself; in this room, it felt permitted. "You've seen what's coming. Vane has sealed the roads. There are forty thousand men within a fortnight's march, and when they arrive they will burn Vellsmark street by street looking for you, and they will burn everyone who helped you, and they will write it all down in ledgers with neat little crosses beside the names. Unless something meets them that they cannot fight. Unless the god they outlawed stands up out of the ground in front of their army and says *no further*."

"And the gods?" Hazel's voice was soft. "The ones screaming behind your dam? What happens to them in your version?"

"They endure," Sera said. "As they have endured. As they must." She heard how it sounded. She said it anyway. "Freedom is a beautiful word, Hazel. I've buried too many people under it to spend it lightly. If unbinding the gods means the empire wins the war that follows—if it means Vane's armies marching through a world where nobody's prayers work anymore—then liberation is just another word for surrender with better poetry."

"You'd keep them caged."

"I'd keep them *aimed*." She stepped toward him. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything. A cage is cruelty. An aim is purpose. Your ancestors weren't monsters, Hazel—they were desperate people who found the only weapon that could save everyone they loved, and they paid for it with their souls, generation after generation, and the last of them is standing in front of me wondering whether to throw the weapon away."

"And if the weapon doesn't want to be thrown?" he asked. "Or swung? Did anyone in three hundred years think to ask?"

The question landed harder than he could have known, because yes—yes, someone had asked. Her mother, once, near the end, feverish, gripping Sera's wrist: *maybe we should let them out, little wren, maybe we've no right—* and Sera, nineteen and already running the Cell, had smoothed her hair and said *sleep now, Mama,* and her mother had died with the question unanswered, and Sera had answered it herself a thousand times since, in the dark, and always the same way.

Because the answer was Wren. The answer was every name on the wall. The answer was that she loved the cause more than she loved the truth, and she had known that about herself for a long time, and she had decided it was not a flaw. Somebody had to love the cause more than the truth, or the truth would bury them all.

"We're asking now," she said. "I'm asking. Sing the opening verse at the festival, Hazel. Publicly. Let the city see what the gods' return looks like. Let the faithful rise, let the army break against something older than empires, and when it's done—when the free cities are free—we sit down together, you and I, and we decide what becomes of the binding. With the war won. From strength. Not in a panic, with Vane's boot on the city's throat."

It was even mostly true. That was the craft of it, the craft her mother had taught her: the best lies are load-bearing truths with one beam swapped.

Hazel looked at her for a long moment. Then at the wall of names. Then back at her, and she watched him weigh it—she could see the scales moving behind his eyes, hope and horror tipping against each other—and she thought, with a tenderness that startled her: *he wants to say yes. He's so tired, and so lonely, and we're the first people in three years who've offered him a place to stand.*

"I need to think," he said.

"Of course." She smiled, warm as banked coals. "Take all the time the city has."

* * *

He climbed the stairs slowly. Sera stayed below a while longer, alone with the names, doing the arithmetic she always did: Wren's fourteen years against three centuries of screaming; one boy's courage against nine gods' rage; the weight of a lie against the weight of a city.

The lie was lighter. It was always lighter. That was the trap of it.

Tam found her there eventually—he always knew—and leaned in the doorway with his crossbow slung and his eyebrows doing the talking.

"Well?" he said.

"He needs time."

"That's not what I asked."

Sera touched Wren's name one last time. "He'll do it," she said. "People always do, when you show them the wall. That's what it's *for*."

But that night, checking the watch rotations, she noticed Hazel's lamp burning past midnight, and Corvin sitting with him, heads close, voices low—and Corvin, who told everyone everything, going quiet as a closed book when she came near.

She told herself it was nothing. She cupped her hands around the thought, the way her mother had taught her, so the wind couldn't find it.

Outside, far off toward the south gate, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm. The signal. Grey coats in the streets. The sealing had begun.
