## 12. The Sealed City

The gates of Vellsmark closed on a Tuesday, which Hazel thought was a very bureaucratic day for the end of the world.

He heard it before he saw it — the groan of the south gate's iron teeth, a sound the city made maybe once a generation, followed by the particular quality of silence that falls over a crowd when everyone realizes at once that the joke is not a joke. He was standing in Corvin's borrowed kitchen at the time, holding a chipped mug of barley tea, and the silence came through the window like weather.

"Ah," said Corvin, who had a gift for understatement that Hazel suspected was trained into him rather than natural. "That'll be the sealing, then."

"You knew it was coming."

"I hoped it was coming slowly." Corvin set down the knife he'd been using to slice turnips — turnips, because the Lantern Cell's larder had entered the stage of the siege where turnips were considered a delicacy — and went to the window. Below, in the street, a grey-coated crier was unrolling a proclamation with the ceremony of a man who believed paper could frighten people. "By order of the Inquisitor-General, the free city of Vellsmark is closed to ingress and egress until such time as the heresy within it is—"

He stopped reading aloud. Everyone in the street had stopped listening anyway. They were doing arithmetic instead. Hazel could see it happening in their faces: the baker counting his flour, the fishwife calculating how many days her ice would hold, the old man at the corner who sold candles doing sums about candles, which was perhaps the most optimistic arithmetic in the city.

"Until the heresy is what?" Hazel asked.

"Rooted out, presumably. Burned, possibly. The usurper's proclamations always end with a verb you wouldn't use at dinner." Corvin came back to the table and resumed cutting turnips with slightly more force than the turnips deserved. "Vane's sealed cities before. Harrowgate, three years ago. It took eleven days for the food to run short and forty for the city to open itself."

"Open itself how?"

"From the inside." Corvin said it lightly, the way you'd say *the kettle's boiled*, and Hazel understood that the Lantern Cell had been in Harrowgate too, and that whatever had opened Harrowgate had left marks on Corvin he didn't discuss over turnips.

Hazel drank his tea. It tasted like warm nothing. Three days in the Cell's safehouse — a dyer's attic that smelled of mordant and mouse — and he had learned the rhythm of resistance life, which was mostly waiting punctuated by meetings, punctuated by more waiting. Sera believed urgency was a discipline you could practice. Corvin believed patience was just fear wearing a sensible coat. Hazel, who had spent three years perfecting the art of being unnoticed, was discovering that he'd merely been rehearsing for a life where everyone watched him.

"They'll starve us," he said. "That's the plan, isn't it? Not an assault. Vane doesn't want a battle. He wants me hungry."

"He wants you *reasonable*," Corvin corrected. "Hunger is just how he makes the offer. He'll wait until the city hates the heresy more than it hates the empire, and then he'll send someone gentle to talk to you. Someone with bread."

Hazel looked at his mug of warm nothing and thought, not for the first time, that the usurper's people understood human nature better than the resistance did. It was an uncomfortable thought. He kept it, the way he kept all his uncomfortable thoughts, folded small and put somewhere the gods couldn't hear it.

*If they could hear it. If they were listening. If Magda's confession was true and three bound gods hung inside his blood like weights in a clock, ticking.*

He set down the mug before his hand could shake and give him away.

* * *

The market still opened, because markets are like water and find their level even in a siege, but it was a thinner, stranger market now. The luck-knots were gone from Hazel's stall — he hadn't dared sell one since the night Corvin watched him charm a cut rope into holding — and in their place he sold buttons. Actual buttons. He had a jar of them and a needle and thread, and he sat behind his little table mending the clothes of people who could not afford new ones, and it was the most honest work he had ever done, and he hated how much he loved it.

"You're very good at that," said the widow Brandt, watching him close a seam on her son's coat with stitches so even they looked printed. "For a heretic."

"For a *what*?"

"Well." She settled onto the stool across from him, arranging her skirts with the gravity of a woman preparing to gossip, which in Vellsmark was a sacrament. "The whole market knows the grey coats are looking for a charming man. And the whole market knows you, Hazel, and the whole market has noticed you stopped selling luck the same week the grey coats started asking. We're not fools. We're just poor."

Hazel's needle paused. "And yet you're sitting here, in the open, telling me."

"I'm an old woman with a dead husband and a son in the militia," she said. "What are they going to do to me that the siege isn't already doing?" She leaned in. "Besides. My sister's boy was at the fish market the night the grey coats came. Says a young man walked through a wall of soldiers like smoke through a keyhole. Says the soldiers' lanterns all went out at once, every one, in a line, like someone blew them out with a breath the size of the street."

"I heard that story," Hazel said carefully. "I heard the young man was seven feet tall and had a beard."

"Mm. And I heard the Lady of the Deep Weir had hair of living kelp, and then I met her at a funeral and she was five foot nothing with a squint." The widow Brandt held out her hand, and in it, wrapped in a scrap of cloth, was a luck-knot — one of his, one he'd sold her months ago, the fibers worn soft from handling. "My son carries this. He's on the wall tonight. The wall the grey coats are building to keep us in."

Hazel looked at the knot. A small thing. A cheap trick, he'd always told himself. Thread and intention and a whisper of the old craft, nothing that could possibly matter.

"Mrs. Brandt—"

"You don't have to say anything." She tucked the knot back into her bodice and stood, brushing off her skirts. "I just wanted you to know that whatever they're hunting you for, half this market has already decided you're worth hiding. The other half hasn't decided anything because they're too hungry. But that's hunger. It passes. Or people do." She patted his shoulder with a hand like a friendly plank. "Mend my other coat by Friday."

She left him sitting there with his buttons and his heart going like a trapped bird, and Hazel understood, in the way you understand things in a market — sideways, all at once, between transactions — that the siege had changed the arithmetic of his invisibility. Three years of careful smallness, and the city had seen him anyway. Not his magic. Him. The man who mended coats and undercharged and once gave a fever-salve to a woman who couldn't pay.

It was, he reflected, a terrible foundation for a legend. And legends, he was beginning to learn, were exactly what everyone else was trying to build out of him.

* * *

The food ran short the way winter comes: not all at once, but in a slow tightening you only notice when your breath fogs.

By the ninth day the baker's queues began before dawn. By the eleventh, the fishwife's ice was gone and she was selling smoked eel at prices that made sailors weep. By the thirteenth, the Candle Man at the corner had raised his prices twice and been forgiven both times, because a city under curfew burns a lot of candles, and everyone understood that his was the only honest trade left.

Hazel walked the streets that thirteenth evening with Corvin, doing what the Cell called *listening* and what Hazel called *eavesdropping with a conscience*. The curfew bell had rung, but curfew had gone soft at the edges — grey coats couldn't enforce a law against grief, and grief was what everyone was out walking in, quietly, like a fog.

A woman was kneeling in a doorway on Tanner's Row, praying. Not the formal prayers of the new church, all empire and obedience. The old shape of it, the shape Hazel had heard from his grandmother: *Lady of the Weir, hear me, my daughter is sick and the herb-seller wants three silver and I have one.*

Hazel stopped. Corvin stopped with him.

"You shouldn't listen to that," Corvin said quietly.

"I know."

"You especially shouldn't listen. Given. You know. Everything."

"I *know*, Corvin." But he couldn't make his feet move. Because the prayer was rising off the woman like heat off a summer road, and beneath it — faint, strained, furious — something in Hazel's chest was *leaning toward her*. Like a dog at a door. Like three voices pressed against the inside of his ribs, saying *we could help, we could help, let us out and we could help*.

He walked away. He made himself walk away, one foot and then the other, and Corvin matched his pace and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in weeks.

"She'll be all right," Corvin offered, after a block. "The herb-seller on Tanner's Row owes the Cell a favor. She'll get her feverfew by morning."

"You arranged that before we ever walked down this street."

"I arranged it before the gates closed. Sera's been running mercy routes since day one. Bread, medicine, candle-wax." He smiled, a little crookedly. "You can't spark an uprising out of despair, Hazel. Despair just makes people obedient. You need them angry *and* fed."

"And then you light the fuse."

"And then we light the fuse." Corvin said it without apology, and Hazel supposed he'd earned the right not to apologize. Everyone in the Cell had lost something to the usurper. Their anger had a receipt.

But that night, in the dyer's attic, Hazel lay under a blanket that smelled of mordant and listened to the bound gods shift in their sleep inside him, and he thought about the woman on Tanner's Row, and he thought: *Sera wants to aim them. Vane wants to own them. And nobody — not one single person in this entire sealed city — has asked them what they want.*

He fell asleep to the sound of his own heartbeat, ticking like a clock with three hands.

* * *

The meeting happened on the fifteenth night, in the back room of the Drowned Bell, a tavern whose owner had been a Cell sympathizer since before the Cell had a name. Sera stood at the head of a table of eleven people and a map, and Hazel stood at the edge of the room where the lamplight didn't quite reach, which was where he'd been standing for fifteen days and where he suspected he'd stand until the siege ended one way or another.

"Harvest festival," Sera said. "Nine days."

A murmur went around the table. The Festival of Ashes — the old harvest rite, when the city burned the season's chaff in the great square and danced until the ash settled. The usurper's governor had permitted it every year, because forbidding a festival is the fastest way to start the riot you're trying to avoid.

"Vane will permit it too," Sera said. "He'll have to. Forty thousand people in the square, grey coats on the edges, and if he cancels it, the riot comes early and ugly. So we use it." Her finger came down on the map, on the square, on the old ash-pyre circle. "The festival is the one moment this year when every soul in Vellsmark will be looking at the same place, wanting the same thing — a good harvest, a soft winter, a *miracle*. They'll already be praying. All of them. At once."

Hazel felt it before he understood it: the three weights in his blood turning over, like sleeper whales hearing a ship.

"The Choir," said an old ferryman whose name Hazel had never caught, "needs a vessel to open it. That was always the way. The guild's records say—"

"The guild's records say a great deal," Sera said, "and Magda Okonkwo is past confirming any of it. We have the words. We have the rite. And we have—" she turned, and her eyes found Hazel in his corner, and the whole table turned with her, and Hazel wished very sincerely for the gift of becoming furniture "—a vessel."

"I don't sing," Hazel said.

"Everyone sings at the festival."

"That's *different*."

"Hazel." Sera came around the table. She did this sometimes — closed distance the way other people drew weapons — and she crouched slightly so she was looking up at him, which she had learned disarmed him, and which he had learned to notice, and which disarmed him anyway. "The gates are closed. The food has nine days, maybe ten. After that, Vane doesn't need to storm anything. He just needs to wait while this city tears itself apart looking for someone to blame, and then he walks in through the ruins and calls it peace. The uprising doesn't have years. It has nine days. And the only force in the world that can turn forty thousand hungry people into an army that terrifies the empire is the thing you're carrying."

"It's not a *thing*," Hazel said, and heard his own voice crack on it. "They're *people*. Or they were. They're three gods in a cage and the cage has my name on it, and you're asking me to open the door and point them at the enemy like—"

"Like a weapon. Yes." Sera didn't flinch. She never flinched; Hazel had decided it was either courage or a wound that had healed into armor. "I'm asking you to do with the gods what the empire has done with every free city. Point them. Aim them. Because the alternative is that Vane points them, or the usurper points them, or they starve in you while a city dies around a man who could have saved it."

Silence. The ferryman studied his hands. Corvin, at the far end of the table, studied Hazel, and his face was doing something complicated — hope and apology tangled together like the threads of a luck-knot.

"Nine days," Sera said again, gentler now. "You don't have to answer tonight. But answer soon. The city's running out of everything, Hazel. Time most of all."

Hazel looked at the map. At the little ink square where forty thousand people would gather to burn the chaff and pray for a miracle. At the grey-coat positions marked in Sera's neat, patient hand. At the pyre-circle, where the rite would stand.

He thought about the woman on Tanner's Row, praying to a Lady who was a prisoner in his chest. He thought about the widow Brandt's son on the wall. He thought about Magda, dying in her chair, saying *we didn't serve them, Hazel, we caged them*, and about the sound three bound gods make when they lean toward a prayer they're not permitted to answer.

"I need to think," he said, which everyone heard as *no*, and which he himself only half believed.

* * *

He walked home the long way, past the wall the grey coats were building — scaffolding and lantern-light and the ring of hammers, a second gate being bricked shut from the outside, in case the first one opened. A soldier on the scaffold was humming the harvest song, off-key, the same song his own mother had hummed, and Hazel stood in the dark and hated him with a clean, simple hatred that surprised him, and then hated the hating, and then just stood there, tired.

The city smelled of woodsmoke and turnips. Somewhere a baby was crying in the particular exhausted rhythm of a hungry baby, and somewhere a mother was doing the arithmetic mothers do, and somewhere — on Tanner's Row, maybe, or Weir Street, or any of a hundred doorways — someone was praying to the prisoners in his blood.

*Let us out,* the gods said, not in words, in pressure. *Let us out and we will feed them. Let us out and we will heal them. Let us out.*

*And then what?* Hazel asked them, silently, for the hundredth time. *And then whose hand is on the leash?*

They didn't answer. They never answered that one. It was, he was beginning to understand, the only question that scared them.

He was three streets from the safehouse when he saw the girl.

She was small, maybe eight, standing in the mouth of an alley with her arms wrapped around a loaf of bread like it was a rescued animal, and she was being very still in the way children are still when they've learned that stillness is safety. Two grey coats stood at the alley's far end, talking, not yet looking at her. Between the girl and the street lay ten feet of open moonlight, and the loaf had the baker's mark of the Cell's mercy route stamped in the crust.

Hazel did the arithmetic. Ten feet. Two soldiers. One stolen loaf that would get a child's hands broken, or worse, because Vane's men had decided theft during a siege was sedition, and sedition was heresy, and heresy was fire.

He could walk away. He was very good at walking away; he had a three-year apprenticeship in it.

He crossed the moonlight, took the girl's hand, and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the soldiers and no farther, "There you are. Your mother's been worried *sick*." And as he said it, he let the smallest thread of the old craft slip out of him — not a working, barely a working, just a *suggestion* laid over the air like a cloth over a cage — and the two grey coats found something suddenly fascinating about the wall they were building and did not turn around.

The girl looked up at him with the flat, unimpressed stare of a child who has already assessed every adult in her life. "You're the heretic," she said.

"I'm the *coat-mender*."

"Mama says you're the heretic. Mama says when the Choir turns, you'll open the sky." She hugged the loaf tighter. "Are you going to open the sky?"

Down in his ribs, three bound gods went very, very still, listening.

"I'm going to walk you home," Hazel said. "That's the whole plan for tonight."

But the words went into him like a splinter, and by the time he reached the safehouse he knew — with the terrible clarity that arrives only when a child says a thing out loud that everyone else has been careful to leave unspoken — that the nine days were not a decision he was going to be allowed to think about.

Because the next morning, when he came down the dyer's stairs, Corvin was waiting at the bottom with his coat already on and his face gone the color of the turnips, and he said:

"Hazel. There's a woman downstairs. Says she cleaned the inquisitors' barracks for six years. Says she knows when Vane's men move, and where, and how many." He paused, and the pause was the worst thing Hazel had heard since the gates closed. "Says her name is Ilse. Says her son is alive because of you. And says — her exact words — *the Choir turns at the festival, or I turn them in myself, because that boy's fever was no work of any god I want to meet.*"
