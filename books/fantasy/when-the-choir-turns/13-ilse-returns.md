## 13. Ilse Returns

Ilse had rehearsed the apology for three days.

She had rehearsed it scrubbing inquisitor floors, wringing out grey coats' bed-linens, hauling buckets of ash from the barracks hearths while clerks with soft hands and hard eyes walked past her as though she were furniture. She had rehearsed it kneeling at her son Tam's bedside, watching him breathe — *breathe, still breathe, after four days of that rattling wet sound that had meant death in her mother's house and her grandmother's too* — and she had rehearsed it walking through a sealed city where bread cost more than boots and everyone's face had gone thin and watchful as winter.

The apology she'd settled on was simple. It had to be. Ilse Brandt had never been a woman of many words; she was a woman of floors scrubbed white and linens boiled clean and a son fed even when it meant she went without. Words were other people's trade.

*I'm sorry* was the whole of it, really. Everything else was just explanation, and explanations were how cowards dressed things up.

But finding the charming young man from the night market turned out to be harder than apologizing to him would be.

* * *

Vellsmark under seal was a city holding its breath. The gates had been shut eleven days now, and you could feel it in the way people moved — quick, quiet, eyes down, like a household tiptoeing past a sleeping drunk. The usurper's grey coats stood at every corner in pairs, and their faces had changed since the sealing. Before, they'd been bored. Now they were hungry. Bored men took bribes. Hungry men took heads.

Ilse knew their schedules better than they knew them themselves. Six years of cleaning the barracks on Candlemaker's Row taught you things no one thought a laundress could learn: which captain drank, which clerk kept papers under his mattress instead of the locked cabinet (fool), which days the patrols went east and which went west, and — most precious of all, these last weeks — that Inquisitor-General Vane himself rose before dawn and took his walk along the river wall with only two guards, because he believed, correctly, that no one in Vellsmark had the courage left to try anything.

She hadn't set out to learn any of it. That was the thing people didn't understand about servants, about cleaners, about women like her. You didn't spy. You simply *noticed*, because noticing was how you stayed alive and kept your work. And then one day the noticing was worth something, and there was no one to sell it to except a resistance cell full of people who looked at her like she was a weapon they'd found under the floorboards.

She didn't much care for being a weapon. She'd been a tool her whole life; tools at least got maintained.

Finding Hazel Eriksen was another matter. She asked at the night market, careful as threading a needle, and got nothing but blank looks — though the old woman selling dried fish watched her a beat too long, which meant the market remembered him even if its mouth was shut. She asked at three dyers' shops before a boy with ink-stained fingers told her, for a copper, that the hedge-charmer lodged above the Voss dye-works on Tanner's Lane, "but you didn't hear it, and neither did I."

Tanner's Lane smelled the way it always had, like rot and lye fighting to a draw. The dye-works was shuttered; the city's wool trade had died with the gates. But there was a stair on the side, and a door at the top, and when she knocked, a voice she recognized said, "If you're from the cell, Corvin's not—"

He opened the door and stopped.

Up close he was younger than she'd remembered, and thinner. The fever months had not been kind to anyone in Vellsmark, but they'd been especially unkind to this one; he had the look of a man who'd been eating worry instead of food. His hands, she noticed, were stained faintly blue at the knuckles. Dyer's stain. A man who hid behind an honest trade.

"You," he said.

"Me," Ilse said. Her rehearsed speech sat in her throat like a swallowed stone. "Can I come in? There are grey coats on the corner of Tanner and Mill, and they change shift at the bell, and I'd rather not have this conversation where they can count my words."

That, at least, made him move.

* * *

The room above the dye-works was small and cold and neater than she expected. A cot. A table with a chipped bowl and a knife worn down to a nub from sharpening. Bundles of dried herbs hanging from the rafters — yarrow, willow bark, elderflower — and beneath them, laid out with the care of a man arranging his own funeral, a half-finished luck-knot.

"I was going to finish that," Hazel said, following her glance. "Before all this. It's for nobody. It's just — my hands don't know what to do when they're not working."

"My Tam's the same," Ilse said, and then hated herself, because that was not the opening line of the speech. "That's — no. That's not why I came."

She stood in the middle of his room with her cap in her hands, six years of barracks discipline keeping her spine straight, and found that the speech had evaporated entirely.

"Ilse—" he started.

"Let me say it wrong or not at all," she said. "I've practiced it three days and it won't come out right, so it may as well come out true." She breathed. "My son was dying. Four nights of fever, the wet kind, the kind that takes them. My mother lost two children to it. I'd already picked out the board I'd bury him under." Her voice held, mostly. "And then your charm worked. Not the way charms work. Charms are for comfort — I've bought them before, I'm not a fool, I know what a copper buys in a night market. Comfort and a little hope to hold in your fist. But Tam's fever broke, Hazel Eriksen. Broke like a dam. One hour he was burning and the next he was asking for soup. And I was so grateful I couldn't see straight, and gratitude is a stupid animal, it doesn't think, it just runs its mouth."

Hazel had gone very still. Stillness, Ilse thought, was clearly a skill of his.

"So I talked," she said. "To a clerk. A soft-handed little man named Ferren who comes to the wash-house, and I told him about the charming young man whose charm saved my boy, because I wanted someone — anyone — to know that something good had happened in this city, something *holy*, and I didn't know what I was doing. I didn't know what you were. I didn't know there was anything to know." She finally let herself look at him straight. "Three days later the grey coats swept the market. Eleven days later the gates shut. And every one of those days I've known it started with me, standing in a wash-house with wet hands, bragging about a miracle like it was my own good fortune."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, water dripped in the empty dye-vats, patient as a clock.

"The fever might have broken anyway," Hazel said at last, gently. "Fevers do that."

"Don't you dare," Ilse said, with a fierceness that surprised them both. "Don't you dare give me the small version. I've spent three days building the large one and I'll not have you take it apart to make me comfortable. It was you. Whatever it was — god, blood, gift, curse — it was you, and I sold you to save my pride, and my son is alive, and both those things are true and I can't unsay either one." She stopped. Steadied. "So. I'm sorry. That's the speech. It's shorter than I planned."

Hazel looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a small, cracked sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years.

"That's the best apology I've ever gotten," he said. "Most people manage 'sorry if you were offended.'"

"I don't do 'if,'" Ilse said. "It's weasel-work."

"No," he agreed. "I can see that it is." He pulled the stool out from under the table and offered it. "Sit. You've been standing at attention like you're expecting me to flog you. Sit, and then tell me why a laundress climbed three flights to Tanner's Lane, because Corvin says you brought more than an apology, and Corvin doesn't bring me news unless it has teeth."

* * *

She sat. She told him.

She told him about Candlemaker's Row, the barracks she'd cleaned since before the sealing, back when the inquisition in Vellsmark had been eight bored men and a filing system. She told him how it had swelled — forty men now, then sixty, cots squeezed into the granary annex, officers billeted in the confiscated houses on Bell Street. She told him about the rhythms of the place, which no one guarded because no one imagined a washerwoman was listening.

"They move on a nine-day cycle," she said. "Patrols go out heavy the morning after the bell-ringers' practice — always, every time, because the officers complain the bells ruin their sleep and so they want to be gone. The granary stores get counted on the fourth day; that's when the guards around them are thinnest, because half of them are hauling ledgers. And Vane—" she hesitated, because saying the name felt like touching a stove, "—Vane walks the river wall at first light. Two guards. He stands at the third merlon past the ferry stairs and watches the water for a quarter hour. Every day. Rain or frost. I know because I used to take the washing down to the river at dawn, before the seal, and he never once looked at me. Men like that don't look at women like me. Which means men like me can stand close enough to spit on them."

"Why are you telling me this?" Hazel asked quietly. "Why not just give it to Sera?"

"Because I did," Ilse said. "And I watched what they did with it. They're planning something for the festival — you know that, everyone in that cellar knows that, they talk in whispers like whispers are walls. They want to use whatever you are to light a fire under this city, and they'll stand well back from the flames, and it'll be people like me and my Tam standing in the crowd when the grey coats start swinging." She leaned forward. "I'm not telling you this to help them. I'm telling you because I owe you a debt, and I mean to pay it in the only coin I have, and I'd rather pay *you* than pay a cause. Causes spend people like I spend soap. At least you'll feel bad about it."

Hazel stared at her. Then he rubbed a hand over his face, and for a moment he looked less like a vessel of ancient gods and more like exactly what he was: a tired young man in a cold room, out of his depth and swimming anyway.

"You know," he said, "three months ago the hardest decision I faced was whether to charge double for fever-salves in winter."

"What did you decide?"

"To charge the same and lie about the ingredients so people felt they'd gotten their money's worth." He smiled faintly. "It worked. People trust expensive lies more than cheap truths."

"My mother said the same about doctors," Ilse said, and then, because the moment was warm enough to bear it, she reached into her basket and drew out the thing she'd carried up three flights wrapped in a clean rag: a loaf. Real wheat, dark-crusted, hoarded from her own ration. "Here. You're skin and guilt, the pair of you, you and that Corvin. Eat."

He took it like it was something holy, which, given the state of the sealed city, it nearly was.

"There's one more thing," Ilse said, and the warmth went out of her voice, because this part deserved the cold. "The reason I came today and not tomorrow. Tomorrow morning, before first light, Vane walks his wall. And yesterday, in the barracks, I overheard the clerk Ferren — my soft-handed friend, the one I talked to — telling an officer that the General had 'found a thread worth pulling.' That the records from the guild-house named a bloodline, and that the bloodline was in Vellsmark, and that the General intended to 'make an approach.'" She met Hazel's eyes. "An approach. Not an arrest. I've cleaned those barracks long enough to know their words. Arrests are for criminals. Approaches are for *assets*. They don't want to hang you, Hazel. They want to hire you."

The loaf sat untouched between them. Down in the street, the shift-change bell rang, and boots went by on Tanner's Lane in the paired rhythm she knew by heart.

"And here's the worst of it," Ilse said softly. "Ferren said the General wants the approach made *quietly*. No grey coats. Just a polite invitation." She paused. "From a man the General trusts to deliver it. Someone presentable. Someone the vessel might speak to without fear."

Hazel had gone pale. "Who?"

"I don't know yet," Ilse said. "Ferren wouldn't say a name indoors. But he'll write it down — he writes everything down, the fool, and hides it under his mattress in the Bell Street billet instead of the locked cabinet, because he thinks no one checks a washerwoman's linens." She stood, and put her cap back on, and became again the invisible thing she had spent six years learning to be. "I'll know by tomorrow night. And then, Hazel Eriksen, you and I are going to find out who in this city Vane trusts — because whoever it is, they're already closer to you than I am."

She was at the door when he spoke.

"Ilse. Why? Truly. You could take your son and sit this out in a cellar somewhere. Why climb stairs for a stranger?"

She considered lying, or at least decorating. She'd never been good at either.

"Because when Tam was dying, I prayed," she said. "Every night, four nights, I prayed to every god I'd ever been taught about, and none of them answered, and I decided they were dead or deaf or never there at all. And then a tired young man in a night market sold me a knot of string for a copper, and my son lived." She opened the door onto the grey afternoon. "I don't know what you are. But you're the only prayer that ever worked, and I'll not hand you to the first empire that asks nicely."

She went down the stairs without looking back, already composing tomorrow's route in her head: Bell Street, the second-floor billet, the mattress, the fool's hidden papers — and the small, dangerous, ordinary work of a woman with a wash-basket and a memory like a ledger.

Behind her, above the empty dye-works, a luck-knot for nobody lay unfinished on the table, and a loaf went uneaten, and a man who had spent three years learning to be invisible began, very carefully, to plan how to stay alive.
