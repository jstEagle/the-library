## 14. Vane's Bargain

They took him between the tannery and the shrine of Saint Aldric, which Hazel would later decide was the universe's idea of a joke—a man of the old gods dragged down in the shadow of a saint who had never once answered anybody.

He hadn't even been doing anything. He'd been carrying a sack of dried nettle for Ilse's son's poultices, walking the long way around the cistern because the cistern square had become a place where grey coats liked to stand and look at people. And then there had been a hand on his shoulder—not rough, not cruel, just *certain*, the way a door closes—and four of them around him before he'd finished turning.

"Hazel Eriksen," said the one holding his shoulder. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking," Hazel said, because his mouth had always moved faster than his sense of self-preservation.

The man didn't smile. "Inquisitor-General Vane is asking."

*Ah*, thought Hazel. *Well. That's that, then.*

They did not bind his hands. That was the first thing he noticed, being marched through streets gone strange and quiet under curfew—the absence of rope. They walked him like a guest. It was somehow worse than chains. Chains would have let him feel like a prisoner; this felt like being an appointment.

* * *

Vane received him in the customs house on the river front, in a room that had clearly once belonged to some merchant's idea of grandeur—painted ceiling, tall windows, a hearth—and which the inquisition had stripped back to function. A table. Two chairs. A lamp turned up bright. No instruments of torture anywhere in sight, which Hazel found himself cataloguing with a professional sort of disappointment. He'd expected racks. Everyone expected racks. The absence of them was its own kind of message: *we don't need those anymore.*

Vane stood at the window when they brought Hazel in, hands clasped behind his back, watching the river. He was smaller than Hazel had imagined. He'd built himself so large in Hazel's head over the years—a figure out of mothers' warnings, a shape in grey that emptied markets—that seeing an actual man, fiftyish, greying at the temples, with ink stains on two fingers, was almost funny.

"You're the charm-seller," Vane said, without turning. "Night market, east row. Luck-knots. Fever-salves."

"I also do warts," Hazel said. "If we're listing my crimes."

Now Vane turned. He had a face built for patience—flat, unhurried, the face of a man who had learned that waiting wins more often than striking. He looked at Hazel the way Magda looked at a knot she was deciding whether to cut or unpick.

"Warts," Vane repeated. "You're joking with me."

"A little. It's a habit. I find it keeps me from screaming, or running, or doing something stupid." He shrugged. "Usually it works."

"Sit down, Master Eriksen."

Hazel sat. The chair was comfortable, which offended him more than a hard one would have. Everything about this room was designed to say: *you are not in danger. Danger is for people who matter less.*

Vane sat opposite him and folded his ink-stained hands on the table. "I'm going to tell you what I know," he said. "Not to frighten you. Frightened men lie. I want you to understand your position accurately, because I believe—this may surprise you—that you and I want rather similar things."

"You want the Choir," Hazel said. "I want everyone to stop wanting the Choir. Those seem different."

"Do I?" Vane tilted his head. "What makes you think that?"

"The grey coats. The sealed gates. The burning of guild records in the square last month—I watched the smoke from Magda's roof. You've spent three years hunting anyone with a drop of vessel blood."

"I've spent three years hunting *rumors* of vessel blood," Vane corrected mildly. "Do you know how many hedge-charmers and luck-peddlers my clerks have dragged in? Hundreds. Do you know how many were actually vessels?" He held up one finger. "Possibly one. Possibly you. And here is the thing, Master Eriksen—" he leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight caught the flat patience of his eyes— "I have burned none of them. Not one. Interrogated, released, watched, yes. Burned, no."

"That's very generous of the empire."

"It's very *practical* of the empire. Dead vessels are worthless. This is the part the rebels never understood, and I suspect the reason your Lantern friends keep losing couriers." He said it lightly, watching Hazel's face, and Hazel kept his face still with an effort that made his jaw ache. "Yes, we know about the Lantern Cell. We've known since spring. Sera Voss is passionate, resourceful, and predictable. But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me tell you what I know about *you*."

He opened a ledger on the table—of course there was a ledger; of course it was thick—and turned pages with those ink-stained fingers until he found his place.

"Hazel Eriksen, born Vellsmark, father a cooper, mother a seamstress, both dead of the winter fever when you were nine. Taken in by an aunt. Apprentice to a chandler at twelve. Left the chandler at fourteen and vanished from every record for six years—which is a remarkable thing to manage in an occupied city, and suggests either extraordinary care or extraordinary teachers. Reappears at twenty selling charms of a quality that should have gotten you arrested within a month, and yet you were never arrested. Three years of small, careful, deniable magic, always just below the line." He closed the ledger. "Someone taught you to be invisible, Master Eriksen. Someone who understood exactly what the proscription laws could touch and what they couldn't. That someone was Magda Sorrel, was it not?"

Hazel said nothing. His heart was going hard against his ribs, but he'd had practice at stillness too.

"She's dying," Vane went on, conversational. "Our physicians say weeks. Which means the guild of the Nine Names has, as far as we can determine, one living member of consequence left. One vessel. One knot." He smiled—it was a small, dry thing, more apology than warmth. "That phrase means something to you. I can see it does. Good. Then you already know more than my clerks do, and we can skip past the part where I pretend to understand the theology."

"What do you want?" Hazel asked. His voice came out steadier than he'd expected.

"To make you an offer. But first—" Vane rose and went to a cabinet against the wall, and came back with a second book, older, its cover cracked leather gone soft as cloth. He set it beside the ledger with something that looked almost like reverence. "Guild records. Recovered from the fire—partially. Enough. Your Magda was thorough, but paper burns unevenly." He opened it to a marked page. "Do you know what the Nine Names were, originally?"

"A choir," Hazel said carefully. "Servants of the old gods. They sang the rites. Kept the prayers flowing."

"That is what the guild told its apprentices." Vane turned the book toward him. On the page was a genealogy—not of names, Hazel saw after a moment, but of *lines*, drawn like the roots of a tree, branching and rebranching across three centuries. "This is what the guild told its *heirs*. Look at the base of the trunk."

Hazel looked. And the floor seemed to tilt gently beneath his chair.

He knew the name at the root. Of course he knew it. Every child in the free cities knew it: **Aldous Ren**, the Usurper, Emperor of the Iron Throne, the man whose armies had swallowed the free cities one by one while the world called it unification. The name sat at the base of the guild's family tree like a stone at the mouth of a well.

"No," Hazel said.

"Ren was a vessel," Vane said. "One of the last of the old line, before the guild went underground. When the Choir bound the gods, the binding needed anchors—blood to hold the knots. The guild scattered its heirs through the free cities like seeds, and the empire's founding family carried the deepest root of all. Your Emperors have been vessels for three hundred years, Master Eriksen. Most of them never knew. Some of them suspected. The current one—" Vane tapped the page— "knows. He has known since he was a boy, when the prayers started whispering to him in dreams he couldn't explain. He thought he was cursed. Then the gods began answering again this year, and he understood what he actually was."

Hazel stared at the tree of lines. Somewhere in those branches, he supposed, was a twig that was him. And somewhere else, a twig that wore a crown.

"He wants it," Hazel said slowly. "The Choir. For himself."

"He wants what any man in his position wants," Vane said. "Control. The gods are loose, or loosening—the miracles in this city alone, the drowned child, the greened field, the fever that broke in a night. Every day the binding weakens, the empire's claim on order weakens with it. The usurper—" and here Vane used the word without irony, without flinching, as if it were simply a job title— "believes that if the rightful heir of the guild's deepest line takes up the Choir willingly, the binding can be restored. Stronger. Permanent. Gods bound to the throne instead of scattered among hedge-charmers and rebel cells. Peace, Master Eriksen. Real peace. The kind that lasts three hundred years."

"And he sent *you* to ask me."

"He sent me to offer you a choice." Vane folded his hands again. "Here it is. Surrender yourself willingly. Come to the capital, take your place in the rite alongside the Emperor, and the binding is renewed with both roots intact—yours and his. In exchange: the city opens. Today. The curfew lifts, the granaries open, the garrison withdraws to the walls and no further. Vellsmark goes back to being an occupied city instead of a besieged one, and nobody starves this winter. Nobody burns. Nobody hangs from the market gallows." He paused. "And yes, that list includes Sera Voss and her couriers. The Emperor is prepared to be magnanimous toward the Lantern Cell. Amnestied, dispersed, alive. He doesn't need martyrs. Martyrs make rebellions grow."

Hazel sat very still. Outside the window, the river moved, brown and indifferent, carrying ice toward the sea.

It was a good offer. That was the obscene thing about it. It was a *good* offer, wrapped in mercy, priced fairly, and every word of it might even be true. He thought of Ilse's boy, hollow-eyed with hunger behind a barricaded door. He thought of the bread queues, the old men selling furniture for flour, the way the whole city had begun to move like a animal in a trap. Open the gates. Feed them. Spare Sera, spare Corvin, spare the cell full of brave frightened people who'd taken him in.

And all it cost was everything.

Because he knew now—he'd heard it in the dark, in the first verse, in the fury that had spoken his name like a curse and a plea—what the binding *was*. Not service. Not stewardship. A cage. Three centuries of gods straining against knots tied by mortals who wanted divine power the way a miser wants gold: to hold, never to release. And the Emperor didn't want to free them. He wanted to *tighten* the cage, and hang it from his throne, and call the rattling peace.

"You're good at this," Hazel said finally. "The mercy framing. Did you practice in a mirror?"

Something flickered across Vane's flat face—amusement, maybe, quickly filed away. "I don't need to frame it. It *is* mercy. That's what you're refusing to look at. You've decided the binding is a sin, so any offer involving it must be a trick. But consider: the alternative isn't freedom. The alternative is Sera Voss tearing the Choir open in a festival crowd to aim three centuries of imprisoned rage at an army, and the army aiming itself back, and this city—" he gestured at the window, at the rooftops, the smoke, the whole gray sprawl of Vellsmark— "becoming the anvil. You've met the gods, haven't you. In the verse. Tell me I'm wrong about what they'll do if she opens the cage in anger."

Hazel couldn't tell him he was wrong. That was the worst part. He'd felt the fury. He'd felt it straining like a tide against a seawall, and he had no idea whether the gods, freed, would weep with gratitude or drown the world that had caged them.

"They're not weapons," Hazel said quietly. "Whatever they've become inside the binding—they weren't weapons first. They were *gods*. People prayed to them because they listened."

"People prayed to them because the guild made them listen," Vane said. "Read your own history, Master Eriksen. The Choir wasn't devotion. It was domestication." He rose, straightened his coat, and for a moment looked tired—all at once, like a lamp guttering—an ordinary tired man with ink on his fingers who had spent years doing arithmetic with human lives. "The offer stands for three days. After that, the Emperor concludes you've chosen the rebels, and the city stays sealed, and the army comes, and I wash my hands of the arithmetic. Think about it. Think about the boy with the fever. I'm told he owes you his life." 

At the door, Hazel stopped. "Why tell me about the Emperor's blood? You didn't have to. It would've been enough to offer the amnesty."

Vane considered him for a long moment.

"Because you're going to refuse," he said, "and I wanted you to refuse knowing the truth. The rebels will tell you the empire is a machine with no face. It has a face. It has blood, the same as yours, and it is afraid of the same things you're afraid of, and it will do the same terrible things for the same reasons. There is no side in this, Master Eriksen. There are only cages, and men arguing about who holds the key." He opened the door. "Three days."

* * *

They walked him back the way they'd come—unbound, unhurried, a guest again—and released him at the corner of the tannery lane with the politeness of men concluding a business meeting. Hazel stood there a moment with his sack of nettles, absurdly intact, listening to his own heartbeat slow.

Then a hand caught his sleeve and pulled him into the shadow of the tannery wall, and Corvin's voice, low and furious, was at his ear:

"Three days? What did he offer you? Hazel. *What did he offer you?*"

And Hazel, looking at the young courier's scared, earnest face, realized with a cold settling in his stomach that whatever he chose—throne or rebellion, cage or cage—the first person he'd have to lie to was standing right in front of him, holding onto his sleeve like Hazel was the only solid thing left in the world.

"We need to get off the street," Hazel said. "And Corvin—there's something about the Emperor you need to hear. And something about Sera. And I only know how to say one of them out loud."

Corvin's grip tightened. Above them, the evening bell began to toll curfew, and somewhere across the darkening city, faint as a memory, something old stirred in its sleep and listened.
