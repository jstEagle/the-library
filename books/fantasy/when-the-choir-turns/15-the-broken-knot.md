## 15. The Broken Knot

The rope around Hazel's wrists was good rope. He had to give Vane's people that much. It was tarred hemp, the kind that lasted a lifetime, and it had been tied by someone who understood knots—which was almost funny, given everything else.

He sat in a storeroom behind the inquisitors' barracks on Candlemaker's Row, listening to the building settle around him like an old man into a chair. Somewhere above, boots crossed floorboards at intervals: eleven steps, pause, eleven steps back. A sentry with a limp and a habit of counting his own footsteps. Hazel had been cataloguing such things since they'd shoved him through this door two hours ago, because cataloguing was what he did instead of panicking. Luck-knots, fever-salves, small charms. Small observations. He had built three years of invisibility out of noticing things, and he refused to stop now just because he'd been noticed.

The trouble was that noticing couldn't untie a knot. And his hands were bound behind him, and the window was barred, and the door had a lock he could hear but not see.

So when the lock clicked anyway—soft as a moth against glass—he decided he was either dreaming or dead.

The door opened onto Corvin, holding a stolen ring of keys and wearing the expression of a man who had just done something extremely stupid and intended to enjoy it.

"You," Hazel whispered.

"Me." Corvin crouched, already working at the rope. "Before you say anything sentimental, understand that I climbed down a privy shaft to get here, and if you make me feel bad about it I will leave you."

"Through a privy shaft."

"The garrison drains into the canal. Nobody guards their own filth. It's the one honest thing about them." The rope fell away. Corvin studied Hazel's raw wrists, then pressed a flask into his hands. "Drink. You look like something Magda would have refused to treat."

Hazel drank. It was plum brandy, cheap and fierce, and it put a small warm fire in his chest where the fear had been sitting like a stone. "How did you find me?"

"Sera has eyes on every street Vane's people walk. Ilse told us which barracks you'd been taken to—she cleans there, remember? She counted the lamps going out so we'd know when the watch changed." Corvin's grin flickered and went out. "She also said Vane spoke with you alone for an hour. So. Before we run anywhere. What did he offer you?"

Hazel looked at the flask in his hands. Outside, the sentry limped his eleven steps, paused, limped back. The city beyond the walls was starving quietly, gate by gate, and somewhere in the dark the usurper's army was coming with the patience of winter.

"He offered mercy," Hazel said. "His version of it. Me surrendering willingly, the gates opening, bread carts rolling in by morning." He turned the flask over. "And he told me the usurper is guild blood too. Descended from one of the Nine Names' lines. An heir who wants the Choir for the empire."

Corvin was quiet for a moment. In the dark his face was mostly shadow and one sharp plane of cheekbone. "Is it true?"

"That's the worst part. Yes. I felt it when he said it—the way you feel a true rumor in your teeth. There's another vessel. Or half of one. Enough of one." Hazel laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and cracked. "Three hundred years of my family hiding, and it turns out we weren't even hiding from the right people. We were hiding *with* them. The whole time, there was someone on the other end of the knot."

* * *

They went out through the privy shaft after all, because Corvin insisted that dignity was a luxury of the unbound, and Hazel was too tired to argue. The canal water was black and cold and smelled like the truth of Vellsmark, and when they hauled themselves up the far bank into the tanner's quarter, Hazel lay on the stones gasping while Corvin wrung out his coat with the brisk efficiency of a laundress.

"There," Corvin said. "Now you're one of us. Nobody in the Lantern Cell has ever been clean."

Hazel laughed again, and this time it worked properly. That was the thing about Corvin he was beginning to trust: the man made the terror smaller without pretending it wasn't there. He was all elbows and jokes and improbable competence, and being near him was like standing next to a stove in a cold room.

They moved through streets Hazel knew better than his own hands—the shortcut behind the chandler's, the alley where old Piet sold roasted chestnuts until the curfew bells drove him home—and every familiar stone felt different now. He had walked these ways as a ghost, a hedge-charmer, a careful nobody. Now he was a name on an inquisitor's list, a vessel, a knot in a binding older than the city itself. Two men in grey coats passed the mouth of the alley and Hazel's heart slammed so hard he was sure they'd hear it, but the coats walked on, hunting some other name tonight.

The safehouse was a cooper's attic above the tannery yards, reached by a ladder that complained under Corvin's weight. Inside: straw pallets, a shuttered lamp, six resistance couriers playing cards by touch, and the smell of wet wool. Home, apparently.

Corvin got him water and hard bread and a corner away from the others, and then sat down across from him with the particular stillness of a man preparing to listen.

"So," Corvin said. "Vane's bargain. Are you taking it?"

"No."

"You're sure? Because I want to be clear about what I'm asking. Sera will ask it harder than I will. If you walk into Vane's hands, the city eats tomorrow. If you don't—" He spread his hands. "People starve slowly instead. Mothers, children. Ilse's boy, when the rationing bites deeper. You've met that boy. You saved his life once."

"I know what I'd be buying." Hazel tore the bread and ate, because grief was no excuse for hunger; Magda had drilled that into him years ago, over soup, in her crooked house full of dying guild secrets. "But do you know what Vane actually offered? Not freedom. A trade. My body for the city's. And then the empire gets the Choir, and the Choir gets aimed at whatever the usurper aims it at next, and in ten years there's another occupied free city wondering how it happened." He swallowed. "Mercy with a hook in it. I've seen that bait before. My grandmother used it on eels."

Corvin huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then, more gently: "And Sera's version? She'll want you to open the Choir for us. Sing the verses, raise the faithful, throw the gods at the usurper's army like a jug of boiling oil. Is that any better?"

And there it was. The question Hazel had been carrying since Magda's deathbed, since the first verse had risen in his throat in the Lantern Cell's cellar and the bound gods had answered—not grateful, not gentle, but *furious*, straining against three centuries of imprisonment like dogs against a chain.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

* * *

He tried to sleep and failed, and around the hour when even the rats took their rest, he gave up and sat by the shuttered lamp and did what he always did with unbearable things: he took them apart to see how they were made.

Magda's voice, in memory, papery and remorseless: *We didn't serve the gods, boy. We caught them. Nine names, nine knots, and our blood for the cord. Every prayer answered since has been a prisoner doing labor.*

Every miracle in Vellsmark these past weeks—the drowned child breathing, the blighted field greening—had been a captive performing tricks for its keepers' amusement. And the resurgence, the answering, the terrible generosity of it: that was the binding fraying. That was the prisoners pressing their faces to the bars because the cage was finally growing thin, and the only key left alive was a hedge-charmer who had never wanted any of it.

Sera wanted to aim them. Vane wanted to own them. The usurper wanted to inherit them. Everyone, everyone, wanted the leash.

Nobody had asked the gods.

Hazel turned his hands over in the lamplight. Ordinary hands, charm-maker's hands, scarred from knot-work and salve-boiling. The last living vessel. The final knot. Which meant—if Magda had told the whole truth, and she had been dying and past the point of lying—that the binding lived in him as much as in any book or rite. Cut the knot in the right place, and the whole cord came loose.

*Unbinding may release gods with three centuries of rage,* said the sensible voice in his head, the one that sounded like caution and had kept him invisible for three years. *You would be opening a cage not to free songbirds but to free storms. They will be angry. They will be vast. They may level the city that starves outside your window along with the army at its gates.*

"Yes," Hazel said aloud, very quietly, so as not to wake the couriers. "Probably."

That was the honest shape of it, and he made himself look at it straight, the way he made himself look at a fever's true color before choosing a salve. Unbinding might save the free cities. It might drown them. He could not know which, and both Sera and Vane were offering him the same lie dressed in different colors: that certainty was available, that divine power could be held and steered, that the storm could be a tool if only the right hands held the handle.

He was so tired of being a handle.

Here was the thing he kept circling back to, the small stubborn coal at the center of all the ash: he had become a vessel by inheritance, without consent, before he was born. His whole life had been other people's decisions wearing his name—his grandmother's secrecy, the guild's covenant, Magda's duty, Sera's cause, Vane's ambition. Every path anyone offered him led through the same country: *be used.* Be the symbol, be the weapon, be the prize, be the sacrifice. Even martyrdom, he realized, was just another way of being handled.

There was exactly one choice in all of this that would be entirely, unmistakably his.

Not to serve the gods. Not to bind them. Not to give them to empire or rebellion. To let them go—to take the Choir's weight into himself, sing the unbinding, and pay whatever it cost, so that no one, ever again, could hold a god by the throat and call it faith.

If they emerged furious, then they would be furious freely, and the world would have to meet them as itself, unarmed, honest. If they emerged merciful—well. He thought of Ilse's son, breathing again because some imprisoned vastness had bothered to answer a mother's desperate prayer through a luck-knot. Even chained, even used, something in them had chosen kindness. What might they choose unchained?

It was a terrible plan. It was possibly the worst plan in the history of plans, and Magda would have called it reckless sentiment, and Sera would call it betrayal, and Vane would call it theft.

Hazel found, examining the feeling carefully, that it was joy.

It sat in his chest like the first breath after fever breaks—fragile, ridiculous, real. Resolve, born not of courage but of refusal. He would not be anyone's weapon. Not the resistance's, not the empire's, not even history's. Whatever happened at the end of this, it would happen because Hazel Eriksen, hedge-charmer of Vellsmark, seller of luck-knots, had chosen it himself, with his own ordinary scarred hands.

"Corvin," he said softly.

The courier opened one eye from his pallet. He'd been awake the whole time, of course. "Mm?"

"I need to tell you something, and then I need you to help me steal something."

Corvin sat up, grinning in the dark like a man handed a present early. "Now we're talking."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low and the sky behind the shutters went from black to the bruised grey that comes before dawn in a sealed city. Hazel laid it all out: the binding, the knot, the unbinding, the risk. Corvin listened the way he listened to everything—wholly, with his whole crooked attention—and when Hazel finished, the courier was quiet for a long moment.

"They'll hate you for it," Corvin said at last. "Sera. Half the cell. Maybe half the city, once they learn what miracles were. You'd be taking the gods away from everyone who ever prayed to them."

"I'd be giving the gods back to themselves."

"And yourself?"

Hazel looked at his hands. "I don't know what's left after. Magda seemed to think the vessel doesn't survive the unbinding whole. Maybe nothing's left. Maybe just—a man. No magic. No legacy. No name on anybody's list." He smiled despite himself. "Honestly, some days that sounds like paradise."

"It sounds lonely."

"It sounds *mine*."

Corvin nodded slowly, and then did something unexpected: he reached over and gripped Hazel's shoulder, brief and hard, the way you grip a rope you're trusting your weight to. "All right. Then here's my contribution. The guild archive Sera keeps—the stolen records, the rite fragments, everything we pulled from Magda's house before the inquisitors got there. If anyone knows where the binding's true knot sits, it's in those pages. I can get you in tomorrow night. After that—" he grimaced, "—after that I'm officially a traitor to two causes, and I'd like it noted that I never get to be anything boring."

"You could still walk away," Hazel said. "No one would blame you."

"Absolutely not. I climbed down a privy shaft for you. We're bonded now. It's like a blood oath but worse-smelling."

Hazel laughed—really laughed, helpless and quiet, shoulders shaking in the straw—and for a moment the attic was just an attic, and the war was somewhere far away, and two fools were grinning at each other over a stolen lamp.

Then the ladder creaked.

A courier leaned up through the hatch, face pale in the pre-dawn grey, and the words fell into the room like stones into still water:

"Sera's back from the east wall. The army's moving—it'll reach the gates within days, maybe less. She's calling the cell together." The courier hesitated, glancing between them. "She says the festival can't wait. She says the Choir opens at the harvest rites—with or without you, Hazel."

The grin died on Corvin's face. Hazel sat very still, the joy in his chest turning cold and bright as frost.

*Sera's Gamble*, he thought. She wouldn't wait for his consent. She'd never needed it. She had stolen guild words and a festival crowd and three centuries of imprisoned fury, and she meant to light all of it at once.

"She'll kill us all," Hazel said quietly.

"Yeah," said Corvin, already reaching for his boots. "So we'd better steal those pages *tonight*."
