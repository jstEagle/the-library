## 16. Sera's Gamble

Sera Voss had learned long ago that the truth was a currency, and like any currency, you had to decide what you were willing to buy with it.

She sat in the Lantern Cell's back room—the one they called the chapel, though it had never held anything holier than pickled herring and a leaky roof—and turned a stolen page over in her fingers. Guild script, three hundred years old, copied out in Corvin's careful hand. The opening verse of the Choir. The words that, if the old records were true, could crack the binding wide enough for a god to put its shoulder through.

Hazel Eriksen didn't know she had them.

He thought he'd been cagey. She'd watched him be cagey at dinner two nights past, watching her over his bowl of barley stew with those hedge-charmer's eyes, careful and kind and utterly transparent. *I have to think about what the Choir really is,* he'd said. *I have to decide for myself.* And Sera had smiled and refilled his bowl and said, *Of course. Take whatever time you need.*

Then she'd gone upstairs and told Corvin to make the copy anyway.

"You're doing it again," said Corvin from the doorway. He had that particular tone he got when he'd decided to be the cell's conscience for the evening, which was most evenings, which was one of the reasons she kept him around and one of the reasons she occasionally fantasized about assigning him to a fishing village in the far north.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you've already decided and you're waiting for the rest of us to catch up."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You absolutely do. Magda used to do it too, I'm told. It's a guild trait, apparently. You get it along with the arthritis and the habit of hoarding candle stubs."

Sera set the page down. Outside, Vellsmark was settling into its fourth night of sealed gates, and the sounds of the city had changed—fewer carts, more arguments, the particular hush of ten thousand people doing arithmetic on how much bread they had left. Somewhere beyond the walls, the usurper's army was camped in the beet fields, patient as winter.

"Hazel's going to unbind them," she said. "All of them. The whole binding, gone."

Corvin came in and sat across from her, and for once he didn't argue. That was the worst part. She'd hoped he'd argue.

"You're sure?"

"He told me a version of it. He thinks he told me nothing. But he asked me what the guild's records said about what happens *after* an unbinding, Corvin. He asked it the way a man asks whether the rope will hold, when he's already tied it around his waist." She rubbed her eyes. "He's going to walk up to that binding and cut it, and he's going to do it alone, and either it kills him or it frees three gods with three hundred years of grievance and no reason to love any of us. And either way, the Lantern Cell ends up with nothing. No vessel. No Choir. No lever."

"So we take the lever out of his hands first."

"So we take the lever out of his hands first," she agreed. "Before he can break it."

Corvin was quiet for a while. The roof dripped, faithful as a clock. "You could tell him," he said at last. "The truth. All of it. That you've known what the Choir was since before he came here. That you've been planning this since the first answered prayer in the fish quarter. He might even help."

"He might," Sera said. "And then he might not. And either way he'd look at me the way he looked at Magda's confession—like the floor had been pulled out from under a man who thought he was standing on stone." She turned the page over again. "I've watched what truth does to him, Corvin. Every time he learns what he actually *is*, he gets smaller. I'm not going to be one more person who hands him a smaller version of himself."

"So instead you'll hand him a stolen rite and a fait accompli."

"Yes."

"That's not kinder. That's just a different shape of the same knife."

Sera laughed—she couldn't help it; Corvin had a gift for making her laugh at exactly the wrong moments, which was probably why they'd survived as long as they had. "No," she said. "It's kinder. It's just not *cleaner*. There's a difference. He gets to be furious with me instead of with himself. Trust me. I'm doing him a favor."

"That's the kind of arithmetic that starts wars."

"That's the kind of arithmetic that *ends* them, if you do the sums right."

* * *

The festival was in six days.

The Festival of Ashes—old harvest rite, older than the occupation, older than the guild if you believed the ballads. The whole city turned out for it every year, occupation or no. Even the inquisitors tolerated it; Vane's clerks had ruled it a "cultural observance of no doctrinal significance," which Sera considered the single funniest sentence ever produced by the Ministry of Truth, and she'd read their pamphlets.

Six days to move an uprising that was supposed to take a month.

She spent the next morning doing sums. The cell had forty-one people it could trust, which sounded impressive until you remembered the usurper had four thousand soldiers in the beet fields and a sealed gate. Forty-one. She'd once led a cell of two hundred, back before the purges, back when the Lantern had been a movement instead of a rumor with good handwriting.

But forty-one was enough, if the city itself decided to rise. And the city would rise—if it saw a miracle.

Not a small one. Not a fever broken or a blight greened, not the little mercies that had been leaking through the binding all autumn like light under a door. The city needed the door thrown open. It needed to see the sky split and know that the old gods were loose and angry and *on their side*, and it needed to see it in a crowd of ten thousand witnesses so that no clerk's report could ever file it away.

And it needed to happen before Vane's patience ran out, because Vane's patience was a resource too, and Sera had watched him spend it carefully, like a man counting coins he intended to collect on with interest.

She gathered the inner circle in the chapel that evening: Corvin, old Piet with his smuggler's maps, the twins Bren and Lotta who could pick any lock in the free cities and had, between them, been arrested eleven times, and Marta, who ran the fishmongers' network and scared Sera more than any inquisitor ever had.

"The festival," Sera said. "We take the Choir to the festival."

Nobody gasped. She'd hired better than that.

"The rite needs a vessel," Piet said. "The guild records are clear. Words alone don't open the binding. You need blood."

"We have a vessel."

"Hazel won't—"

"Hazel doesn't have to know until the moment comes." Sera laid the copied verse on the table. "The words are here. The rite is here. All it needs is a vessel's blood and a vessel's voice, and I have watched Hazel Eriksen for three weeks, and I can tell you exactly what he will do when ten thousand people are on their knees and a god is coming through the sky."

Corvin spoke quietly. "You'll force him."

"I'll *ask* him," Sera said. "In front of everyone. With the whole city watching. And if he says no—" She met Corvin's eyes and didn't flinch, because flinching was for people who still had the luxury. "—then I'll say the words myself and bleed on the page and find out how much of the rite works without the blood it wants. Magda's notes say the guild trained lay-singers once. The words remember. The binding remembers. It's been waiting three hundred years for someone to knock."

"And if it works," Marta said, in her flat fish-voice, "and the gods come through angry—what then? You've seen the prayers. You've seen what's leaking through a *closed* door. What comes through an open one?"

Sera looked down at the copied verse, at Corvin's neat, careful letters, and told the circle the truth she'd been carrying since Magda's deathbed: "Then we aim them. That's the whole point. The guild bound the gods so mortals could hold the reins. The usurper wants those reins for his empire. Sera's little rebellion wants them for the free cities. And Hazel—" she paused, and something in her chest pulled, honest for half a heartbeat before she folded it away again, "—Hazel wants to cut the reins loose and pray nothing bolts. But the gods are *straining*, Marta. You've felt it. Every answered prayer is them pushing at the door. Somebody is going to open it. The only question is whether it's opened by a man who wants to free them and run, or by people who intend to stay and hold."

"And you've decided it should be you," Corvin said.

"I've decided it should be *us*." She gathered the page back. "That's the difference between me and Vane, and the difference between me and Hazel, and if it's not enough of a difference, then we deserve what's coming either way."

* * *

She went to see Hazel that night.

Not to tell him—gods, no. To sit with him. That was the part Corvin would never understand, the part she couldn't put in a plan or a map: that she liked him. That in three weeks he had become the closest thing to a friend the Lantern Cell had, with his terrible jokes about the quality of resistance stew and his habit of charming the hinges on squeaky doors without noticing he was doing it, and that she was about to spend him like a coin.

He was in the loft, mending a luck-knot by candlelight, because apparently the man couldn't stop being a hedge-charmer even while the world ended. He looked up when she came in and smiled, tired and real.

"You've got the face," he said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided and you're waiting for me to catch up."

Sera laughed, and it hurt on the way out. "Corvin says I got it from Magda."

"He's right. She had it too. It's a guild trait. Comes with the candle stubs." He tied off the knot and set it down. "Sera. I need a few more days. I know the city's starving and the army's at the gates, and I know what I'm asking. But I have to be sure. If I do this—whatever I do—I have to do it with my eyes open. Magda spent my whole life lying to me about what I am. I won't act on another person's version of the truth."

And there it was, laid out in front of her like an open door: *tell him.* One sentence. *The rite is copied. The festival is the place. I'm sorry.* One sentence, and he'd be furious, and then he'd be in it with her, eyes open, the way he wanted, and whatever came after they'd face it as—

As what? As the last two people in Vellsmark who understood what was at stake, holding a weapon that could end the war or end the world, and trusting each other with it?

She'd trusted people before. The Lantern Cell's first leader had been a man named Aldric, and Sera had trusted him with everything, and when the purges came Aldric had traded six names to the inquisition to save his own skin, and hers had been first on the list. She'd spent two years in a Ministry cellar learning what trust cost at retail.

"I understand," she said. "Take the time you need."

He smiled, relieved, and something in him relaxed, and she thought: *he trusts me.* And she thought: *Aldric trusted no one, and I trust everyone, and both of us got people killed, so perhaps the lesson is that trust is just a bet with better manners.*

"I'll bring you supper tomorrow," she said. "The good barley. Corvin's been hoarding it."

"You're a saint."

"Don't tell the inquisitors."

She climbed down the ladder and stood in the yard a long moment, looking up at the stars over the sealed city. Somewhere in the beet fields, four thousand men were sleeping under the usurper's banners. Somewhere in the Ministry, Vane was reading guild records by lamplight, patient as a spider. Somewhere in the binding, older than all of it, three gods were pushing at a door.

Six days.

She'd burn the truth to save the cause. She'd known that about herself for years, since the cellar, since Aldric, since the night she'd decided that a person who wanted to be good should probably never be put in charge of anything. But the free cities hadn't left anyone else in charge. That was the joke of it. That was the whole rotten joke: the honest people were dead or hiding, and the ones left standing were the ones willing to carry the knife.

"Forgive me, hedge-charmer," she said quietly, to no one, to the stars, to a man mending luck-knots in a loft above her. "I'm about to make you the most important person in the world, and I'm not even going to ask."

Behind her, in the chapel, the copied verse lay on the table where she'd left it, and the candle stubs guttered, and the words waited—three hundred years old, patient as the gods themselves, remembering.

The festival was in six days.

And Sera Voss, who had once sworn she'd never become Aldric, went inside to plan the miracle.
