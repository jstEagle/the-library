## 17. Festival of Ashes

### Hazel

The ash came down like snow that had given up on being beautiful.

Hazel stood at the edge of the festival square and watched it drift through the lantern light—grey flakes from the bonfires the inquisitors had made of the guild quarter three nights ago. Children caught it on their tongues until their mothers pulled them away. Somewhere a fiddler was playing the harvest tune, the one about the fox and the turnip, and the crowd sang along in voices too thin to be cheerful.

"They're going to do it tonight," Corvin said beside him. He'd found Hazel an hour ago, breathless, smelling of lamp oil and panic. "Sera moved everything up. The rite's not for the closing ceremony—it's during the lighting of the pyre. Half the city will be watching."

"Then we have minutes."

"We have less than that." Corvin gripped his arm. "Hazel. Listen to me. When she starts—if she starts—you can't let her finish. You know what those words do. You've *heard* them."

He had. In the dark of the Lantern Cell's loft, alone, he had sung the first verse once more just to be sure of what he already knew: the gods were straining against the binding like dogs against a chain, and Sera meant to slip the leash only far enough to point them at Vane's army. A weapon. She'd dressed it up as liberation, but he'd seen her face when she thought no one watched. She loved the cause more than she loved the truth, and the truth was that the Choir had never been a song at all. It had been a cage, and his family had been its lock-smiths for eleven generations.

"I'm not going to stop her," Hazel said quietly.

Corvin turned. "You're going to *help* her?"

"No." He watched the ash fall. "I'm going to end it. All of it. The binding, the Choir, the whole rotten inheritance." He looked at Corvin then, and saw the fear there, and felt a strange tenderness for this man who had dragged him out of a market square and never quite let go since. "If I don't come back from it—"

"Don't."

"—tell Ilse her son owes me a luck-knot," Hazel finished. "She'll understand. It's a long story and mostly her fault."

Corvin laughed despite himself, a cracked, wet sound. "That's your farewell? A debt?"

"It's the only kind I'm good at collecting." Hazel straightened his collar, checked that the old guild ring was hidden under his glove, and started toward the great pyre at the center of the square, where the festival lights were being lit one by one, and where Sera Voss stood in a borrowed priestess's robe with stolen words folded against her heart.

* * *

### Sera

The robe itched. That was the thing nobody warned you about miracles—the wool.

Sera climbed the pyre steps with the copied verse pressed flat beneath her palm, and the crowd below her was so vast it seemed less like a gathering than a tide. Ten thousand souls. Fifteen. Every face in Vellsmark that still remembered what freedom smelled like, drawn out by the one night the occupiers permitted assembly. Above the rooftops, the inquisitors' watchtowers burned their cold white lamps, and she could see the grey coats massing at the square's four corners, patient as spiders.

Let them come, she thought. Let them see what ten thousand voices sound like when they remember they have throats.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with detached interest, the way you notice weather. Aldric's hands had shaken too, at the end, before the inquisitors took him. She had sworn on his grave that she would never become him—that she would never trade truth for victory—and here she stood in stolen vestments with a lie folded against her heart, about to open a cage she had no right to open, because the alternative was watching the free cities burn slowly for another generation.

*Forgive me,* she thought again, though whether she addressed Hazel or Aldric or the gods themselves, she couldn't say.

The fiddler finished. The mayor—a hollow man, a collaborator with a fine coat—began the traditional blessing, droning about grain and gratitude while the ash drifted down. Sera counted the beats of it. She had rehearsed this. Forty seconds of blessing, then the pyre-lighting, then the moment when every eye in the square turned to fire and no one would notice a woman on the platform speaking words older than the empire.

Thirty seconds.

Twenty.

In the corner of her vision, a figure pushed through the crowd toward the platform—broad-shouldered, gloved, moving with terrible purpose. Hazel. Of course Hazel. He walked like a man going to his own funeral and had probably planned it that way.

Ten seconds.

The mayor raised the torch.

Sera stepped forward, took the verse from beneath her palm, and began to speak.

### Vane

Inquisitor-General Vane did not attend festivals. He attended outcomes.

From the watchtower above the granary, he observed the square through a spyglass of brass and bone, and he noted, with the mild satisfaction of a man whose ledgers always balanced, that everything was proceeding according to prediction. The crowd: present. The resistance leadership: identifiable by their habit of standing too near exits. The vessel Eriksen: visible, approaching the platform, which was interesting, because Vane's clerks had placed him in the Lantern Cell loft as of midnight.

And the woman Voss, in a priestess's robe, opening her mouth—

"Sir," said Clerk Brandt beside him. "The words she's speaking. We have them in the guild records. Section nine. The—"

"The opening of the Choir," Vane said. "Yes."

He lowered the spyglass. Below, the woman's voice carried across ten thousand people, and it should have been lost in the wind and the murmur, but it wasn't; it threaded through the crowd the way water threads through cloth, and here and there people stopped talking, tilted their heads, listened as if hearing something underneath the words.

Vane had read every surviving page of the guild archives twice. He knew what the Choir was. He knew what it had been built to hold. And he knew—he had known since the sealed gates, since the starving city, since the first impossible prayer answered—that somewhere in that crowd was the last knot of the binding, and that the woman on the platform was about to pull it without understanding what she held.

"General?" Brandt's voice had gone thin. "Should we move? Disperse the crowd? Arrest her?"

Vane considered. The grey coats at the corners numbered six hundred. The crowd numbered fifteen thousand. If he dispersed it now, the vessel would vanish into the warrens and Vane would spend another winter hunting him. But if the rite completed—

"Wait," he said.

"Sir, if she opens it, and the vessel doesn't intervene—"

"Then the usurper gets nothing, the rebellion gets nothing, and the gods get everything, which is the single outcome worse than all others." Vane raised the spyglass again, found the broad-shouldered figure climbing toward the platform, and allowed himself something almost like hope. "No, Brandt. We wait for the hedge-charmer. He has never once done what anyone expected of him. It is his only virtue, and tonight it may save us all."

Below, the sky flickered—not lightning, not yet, but the memory of lightning, a bruise-colored shimmer along the clouds.

"Ah," Vane said softly. "There it is."

* * *

### Hazel

He reached the platform as Sera's voice rose into the second stanza, and the world had already begun to go wrong in small, precise ways.

The bonfire flames had gone still—not dying, but frozen, each one a painted tongue of orange. The ash hung in the air without falling. And the crowd had stopped being a crowd; fifteen thousand people stood motionless as figures in a tapestry, faces upturned, eyes reflecting a light that wasn't coming from any pyre.

"Sera." His voice sounded strange, flattened, as though the square itself were holding its breath. "Stop."

She didn't stop. Her eyes were closed, her face transfigured, and tears ran freely down it, and Hazel understood with a lurch that she wasn't performing anymore. The words were carrying her. Stolen guild words, copied from a dead man's ledger, and they had teeth, and they had her now the way they'd had eleven generations of Okonkwo-blooded charmers before him.

He grabbed her shoulders. Her eyes opened—still seeing him, thank every merciful thing—and for one heartbeat she wavered.

"Hazel," she whispered. "It's *real*. Oh, gods, it's real, I can feel them, they're—"

"Furious," he said. "They're furious, Sera. They've been furious for three hundred years. Listen to me. I can end this. Not aim it. *End* it. But you have to let go of the verse, right now, before it takes the choice away from both of us."

Something moved behind her eyes—loyalty, conviction, grief, the whole terrible architecture of a woman who had buried too many friends to believe in half-measures. For a moment he thought she would yield.

Then the third stanza arrived, and it arrived without her.

The words lifted off the page in her hands—lifted, actually lifted, ink rising off paper like a flock of black birds—and the sky split.

Not parted. *Split*, the way a seam splits, the way a wound splits, a jagged line of gold-white tearing across the clouds from horizon to horizon, and through it came a sound that was not thunder and was not singing and was not anything the mortal ear had been built for. The frozen flames all bent sideways at once. The ash began to rise instead of fall. And in the crowd below, fifteen thousand people fell to their knees in a single wave, like wheat before wind, and began—to Hazel's lasting horror—to sing.

They didn't know the words. Their mouths made them anyway.

"*Hazel.*" Sera clutched his arm, and all the certainty had drained out of her face, leaving something young and terrified. "What did I do? What did I *do*?"

"What you meant to," he said gently. "Just aimed wrong." He pulled off his glove. The guild ring gleamed on his finger, eleven generations of quiet, careful servitude, and it was hot as a coal. "Get everyone off this platform. Get yourself off it. Whatever happens next—"

"You said you could end it."

"I can." He looked up at the split sky, at the vast attention pressing down through it, and felt the last of his fear burn away and leave something clean behind. "But someone has to hold the door open while it closes. That's the part of the job description they left out."

Sera stared at him. And then—because she was, whatever else, a woman who understood sacrifice when it stood in front of her wearing a hedger's coat—she kissed his forehead, whispered "*forgive me*" a third time, and went.

Hazel stood alone on the pyre platform beneath the torn sky, raised his ringed hand, and began, very quietly, to sing the unbinding.

* * *

### Vane

Through the spyglass, Vane watched the hedge-charmer begin to sing, and for the first time in nineteen years of service, Inquisitor-General Vane prayed.

He was not certain to whom. That, he reflected distantly, seemed suddenly to be the entire problem.
