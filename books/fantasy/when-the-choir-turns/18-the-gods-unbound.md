## 18. The Gods Unbound

The festival square had become a wound in the world.

Hazel came through the smoke at a dead run, Corvin a half-step behind him, both of them coughing on ash that tasted of burnt sugar and something older—something like the inside of a temple that had been closed for three hundred years. Above the harvest stalls, above the maypole with its ribbons still fluttering absurdly in the updraft, the sky had split. Not torn open like a storm breaks; that would have been almost comforting. This was a seam, precise and deliberate, as though someone had taken a needle to the fabric of evening and pulled.

Sera had pulled it. Sera, with stolen guild words and a festival crowd of ten thousand souls who had come for roasted apples and fiddle music and had instead been given the end of the world's long silence.

"Hazel." Corvin grabbed his sleeve. "The rite's heart—it's the maypole, isn't it? She's standing at the—"

"I see her."

He did see her, and the sight of her stopped his breath for a moment that he could not afford. Sera stood at the base of the maypole with her arms raised and her voice doing something a human voice should not be able to do—carrying over the screaming without effort, threading through the crowd like a hook through fish. She had stolen the opening verse from the guild's hidden archive, and she was singing it wrong. Not badly wrong. *Deliberately* wrong. She had taken the words that were meant to be sung by nine voices in harmony and was bending them into a command, a lever, a key jammed into a lock by someone who wanted the door open but did not care what came through it.

And the gods were coming through it.

Hazel felt them the way you feel a flood coming through the soles of your feet before you hear the water—a pressure, a trembling, a vast displaced weight moving toward a hole. Three centuries of imprisonment, and Sera had cracked the cage without asking the prisoners a single question about what they would do with their freedom.

*Or whether they should have it at all,* said a small, treacherous voice in him that sounded, annoyingly, like Magda.

He ran faster.

* * *

The crowd was the worst part. Not the inquisitors—though Vane's grey coats had flooded the square from the north gate, and somewhere in the crush of them Hazel had glimpsed the Inquisitor-General himself, pale and still as a heron, watching the sky with an expression that was not fear but *accounting*. Not the resistance fighters, either, who were holding the square's edges with pikes and desperation and, in one case that Hazel would remember for the rest of his life, a rolling pin.

It was the crowd. The ordinary people. The woman clutching a sack of pears to her chest like a child because she'd paid good coin for them and by all the dead gods she was not leaving them. The two boys who had been dancing a reel when the sky split and were now dancing still, because their feet didn't know what else to do. An old man on a bench, unhurried, eating a candied apple, watching the heavens tear open with the mild interest of a man who had decided that if this was how it ended, he was at least going to finish his apple.

Hazel loved them. That was the thing no one had understood—not Sera, not Vane, not Magda in her long dying. He loved them, stupid and stubborn and pear-clutching, and he could not save them by giving the gods to any of the people who wanted to hold the leash.

He could only cut the leash out of the world entirely. And he could only do it from the inside.

* * *

He reached the maypole as the first god arrived.

It did not descend. That was the horror of it—it *arrived*, the way a thought arrives, suddenly present in a place it had not been a moment before, except the place was the entire square and the thought was the size of a mountain range. The crowd near the pole fell to their knees in a spreading ring, like wheat before wind. Hazel felt the god's attention pass over him and snag.

It knew him. Of course it knew him. He was the last knot in the rope that had held it.

*VESSEL,* it said, and the word was not sound but pressure, and Hazel's teeth ached with it. *VESSEL. LITTLE KNOT. YOU KEPT US.*

"I did," Hazel said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's, which under the circumstances he decided to forgive himself for. "I didn't know I was keeping you. But yes. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

*SORRY,* said the god, and something in the vastness of it bent—grief, or laughter, or both; with the old ones he would learn there was rarely a difference. *SORRY IS A SMALL WORD FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS.*

"I know." He wiped his face with the back of his hand and found it wet. "I know it is. But I'm here to do something bigger than sorry, if you'll let me."

Sera's voice was rising toward its crescendo. The seam in the sky was widening. Through it Hazel could see—not light, not fire, but *presence*, the deep green pressure of the field-god, the cold bright patience of the river-mother, the drowned-and-raging thing that had once been the god of the harvest before mortals had decided harvests needed managing. They were all straining toward the crack Sera had made, and Sera stood beneath it with tears streaming down her face and an expression of such radiant, terrible conviction that Hazel's heart broke a little even as he prepared to undo everything she had built.

"Hazel!" She saw him then, and her face lit with triumph. "You came! I knew you'd come! Take my hand—we'll sing it together, the way it was meant to be sung, and the faithful will see, the whole city will see—"

"Sera." He said it gently. He wanted her to hear the gentleness, to understand in the last moments that this was not betrayal. "The way it was meant to be sung was the way that built the cage. I'm not here to sing the opening verse."

Her face went through several expressions in the space of a breath—confusion, dawning comprehension, fury. "Then you're here to stop me. After everything—after they *answered*, Hazel, after they came back for *us*—you'd close the door again? You'd keep them leashed?"

"No," Hazel said. "I'm here to let them off the leash. All the way off. Forever."

And he began the unbinding.

* * *

Magda had taught it to him in the last week of her life, in a voice gone thin as paper, with her hand gripping his so hard her knuckles went white. *It's not a song, boy, whatever the stories say. It's an untying. Nine knots, and you are the ninth, and the ninth knot holds all the others. To unbind the gods, you must unbind yourself—and there is no unbinding that leaves the binder whole.*

He had asked her, in one of the last lucid hours, whether it would kill him.

She had looked at him for a long time with her milky eyes. *It will take everything the guild ever put into your blood,* she had said. *Everything you inherited. Everything you are that isn't just Hazel. Whether what's left over is enough to be a man—that's not a question I can answer. Nobody's ever come back to tell us.*

So. There it was.

The unbinding was not words, in the end. The guild had dressed it in words for three centuries because mortals trust what they can write down, but at its heart it was simpler and far more terrible: it was *letting go*. It was opening his hands—his blood, his name, the nine generations of Eriksens and Okonkwos and Vellsmark-born vessels who had carried the Choir like a burden of water across a desert—and pouring it all out.

He sang anyway. Because Sera was singing, and because the gods deserved to hear it done properly at least once, and because a man should make some noise when he ends the world's oldest prison.

The first knot was the easiest: his mother's lullaby, the one she'd sung without knowing it was a binding, the one that had kept the river-mother quiet in his blood since before he could walk. He let it go and felt her go with it—a cold bright presence sliding out of him like a stone from a boot, and in the last instant before she left, she touched the inside of his skull with something that felt, unmistakably, like gratitude.

*Oh,* Hazel thought, blinking through tears. *Oh, they can be grateful. Nobody told me they could be grateful.*

The second knot was his grandmother's. The third was a name he had never known, a woman dead two hundred years whose stubbornness still lived in his marrow. Each knot he released took something with it—the charm-craft went first, the little warm cleverness in his fingers that had tied luck-knots and brewed fever-salves, and he felt it leave the way you feel a tooth leave, a socket suddenly empty and aching. Then the deeper things: the guild's discipline, the vessel's endurance, the strange second heartbeat he had carried all his life without knowing it was not his own.

The gods poured through the widening gaps in him like water through a broken dam—not into the sky, not through Sera's crude seam, but *through him*, through the proper door, the door built for them by his blood and now opened by his choice. The square filled with presence. The maypole's ribbons stood straight out in a wind that touched nothing else. The old man on the bench dropped his candied apple. The woman with the pears sat down hard in the ash and began, incongruously, to laugh—the wild, relieved laughter of someone who has finally seen the thing she'd been dreading and found it, whatever else it was, *real*.

And the gods—vast, wounded, three centuries furious—stopped.

They hung in the air above Vellsmark like weather deciding what to become, and every soul in that square felt them listening. Not to Sera's stolen verse. Not to Vane's grey ranks, who had frozen mid-advance with their crossbows half-raised. Not to the resistance's pikes, or the maypole, or the burning stalls.

To Hazel. To the small, emptied, kneeling figure at the rite's heart, who had just given them back their names.

*VESSEL,* said the harvest-god, and its voice was gentler now, which was somehow worse. *YOU GAVE WHAT WAS TAKEN FROM US. WHAT DO YOU ASK?*

Hazel knelt in the ash. He was aware, distantly, of Corvin reaching him—of a familiar hand on his shoulder, gripping hard, of Corvin saying his name in a voice that shook. He was aware of Sera on her knees ten feet away, her face wet, her stolen verse forgotten, watching the liberation she had wanted arrive in a shape she had never imagined.

He was aware that he felt—nothing. Not numbness. *Emptiness.* The Choir was gone. The knots were gone. The warm cleverness in his fingers was gone. What remained was a thin, ordinary, mortal thread of a man, kneeling in festival ash, and it was the strangest sensation of his life to discover that the thread was still *him*.

"Nothing," he said. His voice came out rusted. "I don't ask for anything. That was rather the point."

*NOTHING,* the river-mother repeated, and there was wonder in it. *THREE HUNDRED YEARS, AND THE LAST OF OUR KEEPERS ASKS FOR NOTHING.*

"He asks for one thing," Corvin said, and his hand tightened on Hazel's shoulder. "He asks you to leave them alone." He gestured at the square—at the woman with the pears, the boys, the old man, the whole stubborn, pear-hoarding, apple-eating, dancing-in-the-ash multitude of Vellsmark. "All of them. Every city. No more vessels, no more bindings, no more answers to prayers that come with a price. Just—go. Be free somewhere we can't reach and can't be reached by. That's his ask. That's the only thing he's ever wanted."

The gods were silent for a long moment. The seam in the sky shimmered.

And then the harvest-god did something that no guild record, no inquisitor's interrogation, no resistance prophecy had ever predicted.

It laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of a prisoner walking out of a cell into sunlight and finding, on the far side of the wall, a field of the same wheat that had grown outside his childhood window. *YES,* it said. *YES, LITTLE KNOT. THAT IS WHAT WE CHOOSE. WE WERE GODS BEFORE MORTALS NEEDED US TO BE WEAPONS. WE REMEMBER HOW.*

The presence gathered itself, all of them together, the whole wounded chorus of the old world—and Hazel felt them consider the square one last time, consider *him* one last time, and then they poured upward through the seam like light returning to a lamp, and the sky closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting, and Vellsmark was alone under the stars for the first time in three hundred years.

* * *

The silence afterward was total.

Ten thousand people, and not one of them made a sound. The fires crackled. A ribbon fluttered down from the maypole and landed on Hazel's knee. Somewhere a baby cried, then thought better of it.

Hazel tried to stand and discovered his legs had resigned their commission. Corvin caught him before he hit the ash, lowering him down with the careful tenderness of a man handling something that might still break.

"Hey," Corvin said. His face was filthy, his coat was torn, and he was smiling like an idiot. "Hey. You're alive. You're—Hazel, you're *alive*."

"Am I?" Hazel flexed his fingers. They were just fingers. He reached, out of lifelong habit, for the little warm cleverness, and found nothing there—no charm, no craft, no Choir. Just hands. Just Hazel. He started to laugh, and the laugh turned into something else halfway out, and for a while he simply sat in the ash with Corvin's arm around his shoulders and wept like a man at his own funeral, which in every way that mattered, he was.

Sera came to him slowly, across the ash, and knelt. Her face was wrecked. Her cause—the Choir aimed at the usurper, the gods as a weapon of liberation—lay around her in the form of a maypole with no god behind it and a crowd with no miracle to follow.

"You let them go," she said. It was not an accusation. It was barely even a question. It was a woman reading the bill for something she had ordered without understanding the menu.

"They were never ours to keep," Hazel said. "That was the whole lie, Sera. Three hundred years of it. We called it service and it was a cage, and every prayer we answered through it was a hostage negotiation." He looked up at the seamless sky. "I think they'll be kinder to us now than we ever were to them."

Sera was quiet a long time. Then she said, in a very small voice, "What do we do now?"

And that was when the shouting started from the north gate—a runner, grey-coated, pushing through the crowd toward the still figure of the Inquisitor-General, and even from thirty feet away Hazel could read the shape of the news in Vane's posture as he turned to receive it: rigid, then very still, then—strangely, terribly—*relaxed*.

Because the word the runner brought, the word that would travel through the square and the city and the whole chain of the free cities before dawn, was from the front. The usurper's armies had stopped. All of them. Every soldier of the imperial bloodline had felt the Choir go out of the world like a lamp going out, and the war machine, suddenly and completely, had nothing left to run on.

Vane turned, slowly, and looked across the square—at the crowd, at Sera, at the ash—and found Hazel's eyes.

He did not look defeated. He did not look furious. He looked like a man standing in the ruins of a very long plan, doing arithmetic, and arriving—for the first time in his patient, heron-still life—at an answer he had never once considered.

He began, unhurried, to walk toward them.
