## 20. Aftermath in Vellsmark

The first thing Vellsmark did after the sky closed was argue about what to call it.

Not the war, not the miracle—those had no names yet, and perhaps never would—but the festival itself. The Festival of Ashes, some said, because of the grey rain that fell for three days afterward, soft as sifted flour, settling on rooftops and shoulders and the upturned faces of people who stood in the streets looking at a heaven that had gone quiet. Others called it the Unbinding, though that word sat wrong in the mouth, too grand for something that had felt, to those who witnessed it, like a door being opened from the inside. The priests of the usurper's rite, what few remained after their general's armies turned home, called it blasphemy and were ignored with the particular politeness Vellsmark reserved for opinions it found tiresome.

Ilse Brenner called it nothing. She called it *that day*, the way you might refer to a fever or a funeral, and she went on calling it that through the whole of the mourning month while the city buried its dead and argued over its living.

She was washing floors when the bells rang for the last time. The inquisitors' barracks had been emptied a week past—the grey coats marched out through the west gate with their records and their shackles and their terrible patience, ordered back to the capital by an empire suddenly unsure of itself—and Ilse had stayed on anyway, scrubbing the empty rooms out of habit and need, because the pay was honest and because there was a strange comfort in cleaning a place where no one suffered anymore. She was on her knees in the old interrogation room, working at a stain that would not come out no matter how she prayed at it, when the bells began.

She stood in the doorway with her rag dripping and listened. Nine bells, then silence. Then nine again. She counted them the way she counted everything now—her son's breaths in the night, the coins in her apron pocket, the days since that young man with the kind eyes had sold her a luck-knot and changed the shape of the world without meaning to.

*Hazel.* That was his name. She'd learned it afterward, from the whispers that moved through the market like weather. Hazel Eriksen, hedge-charmer, vessel of the Choir, the man who unbound the gods. They said it in the taverns the way you'd say the name of a storm or a saint, and every time Ilse heard it, something in her chest pulled tight, because she knew something the whisperers didn't.

She knew he'd been nobody first. Just a boy at a stall with salves and knots and a smile he wore like a coat against the cold. She knew that before he was a legend, he'd been a person, and that she was the one who had handed him to the fire.

* * *

Tomas was well. That was the thing Ilse came back to, over and over, the stone she built her days on: *Tomas is well.* The fever that should have taken him had broken in a single night, impossibly, miraculously, and he had grown like a weed since, all elbows and appetite, running through the rebuilt market with the other children as if death had never so much as glanced at him. He didn't remember much of his sickness. He remembered the charm—a knotted cord, brown wool, smelling faintly of rosemary—and he remembered that his mother had cried, which embarrassed him deeply and which he mentioned whenever he wanted to win an argument.

"Mother," he said one evening, mouth full of bread, "the other boys say the gods walked in the square during the festival. Marta says she saw one, all made of river water."

"The other boys," Ilse said, "say a great many things."

"But did they? Walk?"

Ilse looked at him across the table—at this child she had bargained with heaven for, this boy whose life had been the first domino in a row that toppled an empire's faith—and she told him the truth, or the part of it she could carry.

"I don't know what I saw, love. I saw light. I saw people on their knees. And then I saw a young man standing in the middle of it all, and he looked—" She stopped. She had tried to describe it before, to Corvin, to Sera, to anyone who asked, and the words always failed her. "He looked like someone setting down a weight so heavy that everyone around him had forgotten it was heavy at all."

"Was he a god?"

"No." The answer came fast and sure, faster than she expected. "No, he was a man. That was the whole point of him, I think."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a nine-year-old deciding whether dessert was imminent, then went back to his bread, and Ilse sat in the candlelight feeling the grief move through her again—not sharp anymore, not the raw thing of those first weeks, but a slow tide, familiar now, almost companionable. Grief braided with peace. That was what the midwife called it, when Ilse finally admitted she couldn't sleep. *You're grieving a man who isn't dead,* the old woman said, *and grateful to a miracle that cost too much. Both things are true. Hold them both.*

So she held them. She washed floors and mended clothes and queued for bread in a city learning to feed itself again, and she held both things, and some mornings the holding was nearly easy.

* * *

She went to find Sera on a grey Tuesday, because she had heard the Lantern Cell was ending and she wanted—I don't know what I want, she told herself, walking the wet streets—I suppose I want to see what it costs to be certain.

Sera received her in the cellar off Tanner's Row that had been the Cell's heart, a room of trestle tables and candle stubs and maps of a city that no longer needed liberating. Most of the benches were already gone. A handful of couriers moved through the space quietly, carrying out crates, and the whole place had the air of a household after a funeral—still functional, still fed, but hollowed.

Sera herself sat alone at the head table, and Ilse, who had seen her only twice before and always at a distance, was struck by how small she seemed. Not physically—she was still the woman who had stared down inquisitors and out-argued generals—but small in the way a house is small after the furniture is carried out. There was less of her than there used to be.

"You're the mother," Sera said. "The one from the market."

"Ilse. Yes."

"The one whose prayer started it." There was no accusation in it. If anything, there was something like envy, worn smooth by months of handling. "One prayer. One fevered child. And the whole binding came apart." Sera laughed, short and dry. "Do you know how many prayers I've said? How many plans? I had two hundred people ready to die in that square. I had the words memorized. And the gods came back for a woman's son and a charm-seller's kindness, and everything I built was—" She stopped herself. Pressed her palms flat on the table. "I'm sorry. You came here for something. Ask it."

"I heard you're disbanding the Cell."

"I am." Sera looked around the room, at the candle stubs and bare walls. "We were going to free the free cities. That was the phrase we used. *Free.* And then I stood in that square with stolen holy words in my mouth, and I watched the sky split, and I understood that I hadn't been planning a liberation at all. I'd been planning a theft. I wanted the gods bound and aimed, like a crossbow. Like Vane did. We just disagreed about who got to hold the weapon." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she steadied it. "A man died in that square rather than let any of us hold it. So yes. The Cell is done. The lantern goes out. Some of us will keep working—food, shelter, the boring miracles. But no more Choirs. No more vessels. No more gods on strings."

"He's alive," Ilse said. It wasn't a question; she needed it said aloud anyway. "They say he's alive."

"He's alive." Sera said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "I've seen him. Once. In the market, of all places. He didn't know me—or he pretended not to, which may be kinder. He looked..." She searched for the word and, finding none adequate, gave Ilse a look that said *you know*, and Ilse did know, because she'd seen it too, in the moment before the light took him: a man standing in the ashes of everything he was, wondering what was left.

"Will you tell him?" Ilse asked. "What you told me?"

"No." Sera shook her head. "He paid enough. More than any of us. Let him have his quiet." She smiled then, and it was the first real smile Ilse had seen from her, tired and crooked and human. "Besides, I suspect he knows. People like him always know. That was the whole trouble with him."

* * *

The night market came back the way green comes back to a burned field—patchy at first, then stubbornly, then all at once. By the first frost, the lanes behind the chandler's hall were crowded again, stalls lit with oil lamps, the smell of fried dough and tallow and woodsmoke thick enough to chew. The proscription was repealed; charms hung openly from hooks now, luck-knots and fever-salves and birth-blessings, and half the sellers were frauds and the other half were frauds with better marketing, and nobody minded, because the city had learned the difference between magic and hope and decided it could use both.

Ilse went on a Thursday, with Tomas trailing her, ostensibly to buy candles but really because she couldn't stop herself. She told herself she wouldn't look for him. She looked for him.

She found the stall by accident, third lane down, between a pickle-cart and a woman selling secondhand boots—and for a moment she couldn't breathe, because it was exactly as it had been, the same folded cloth, the same little jars, the same neat handwriting on the price cards. Except the jars were different. She looked closer. Fever-salve, yes, but ordinary fever-salve, beeswax and comfrey, the kind any herb-wife made. Luck-knots, but plain ones, brown wool with nothing in them at all. She knew—knew in her bones, the way she'd known her son's fever was breaking—that there was nothing in any of it. No craft. No gift. Just a young man selling remedies.

And he looked different. Thinner. Greyer around the eyes, as if the color had been drawn out of him along with everything else. His hands moved with the same careful competence, wrapping a knot for a fishwife, counting change for a carter, but the smile—he wore it still, but it sat differently now, less like a coat and more like a scar. Polite. Distant. The smile of a man greeting customers across a wide and quiet river.

He didn't recognize her. Of course he didn't. She was one of a hundred mothers who'd bought his charms, one face among thousands in a market he'd worked for years. She stood at the edge of the lamplight with Tomas tugging her sleeve and watched him serve a queue of strangers, and she thought: *I could tell him. I could say, you saved my son, you saved us all, I'm the reason they hunted you.* And she imagined his face changing—recognition, gratitude, or worse, forgiveness—and found she couldn't do it. Forgiveness would be a burden laid on him, and he had carried enough burdens to fill a cathedral.

Instead she bought a candle and a plain knot of brown wool, paying with exact coins, and when he handed her the change his fingers brushed hers, brief and warm and utterly ordinary, and he said, "Cold night. Mind the ice on the bridge," in the voice of a man who meant nothing more by it than exactly that.

"Thank you," Ilse said. "For—for the knot."

"They're good knots," he said mildly. "Hold well."

And that was all. She walked away with Tomas skipping beside her and the worthless, precious knot in her fist, and halfway down the lane she had to stop and press her hand over her mouth because the crying came up out of her like a tide, grief and relief tangled past separating, and Tomas, bewildered, put his arms around her waist and said, "Mother, it's only a knot," and she laughed through it, wet and helpless, and said, "Yes, love. It's only a knot. The best one I ever bought."

Behind them, under his little lamp, the charm-seller wrapped another customer's purchase and hummed something tuneless and low, and if it resembled, very faintly, very distantly, the opening bars of a song no one sang anymore, neither he nor anyone else remarked upon it.

But Ilse, blowing her nose on the walk home, thought she caught it—just a thread of it, carried on the cold air—and she stopped, listening, her heart doing something complicated in her chest.

Because it wasn't a lament. That was the thing. Everyone assumed a man who lost the gods would sing laments. But the tune drifting from the third stall in the night market was light. Almost glad. The sound of a man testing a room to see if it echoed—and finding, to his own surprise, that it did.
