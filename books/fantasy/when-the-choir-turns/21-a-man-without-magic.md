## 21. A Man Without Magic

The fever had been gone for eleven days, and Tam still demanded to be checked twice daily.

"Again," he said, holding out his arm with the gravity of a magistrate presenting evidence.

Hazel pressed two fingers to the boy's wrist and counted. Sixty beats, steady as a millwheel. He'd counted it every morning and every evening for a week and a half, and it had not once varied, because Tam was seven years old and healthy as a spring lamb, and both of them knew it.

"Well?" Tam demanded.

"Terrible," Hazel said. "Wasting away. I give you until supper."

Tam giggled and pulled his arm back. "You said that yesterday."

"And you've clearly fooled me again." Hazel set down the little brass instrument Corvin had procured from somewhere—a physician's pulse-glass, real glass, real brass, the kind of thing that before the war would have cost more than Hazel made in a season. Now half the inquisitors' instruments sat in pawnshops, their owners fled or worse, and Vellsmark's markets had grown strange and generous in the way of cities after sieges. "Your mother says you ate three rolls this morning."

"Four."

"Four rolls. Yes. Definitely dying."

Ilse laughed from the doorway, where she stood with a basket of mending she was using as an excuse not to come in. She did that sometimes—stood at thresholds, watching Hazel with her son, wearing an expression he'd learned not to name too precisely. Gratitude, probably. But gratitude with weight behind it, the kind that wanted to become something more and didn't know what.

"He checks his own wrist when he thinks I'm not looking," she said. "He counts his own heartbeats."

"That's sensible," Hazel said. "I taught him that."

"You taught him that so he'd stop waking you at dawn."

"I taught him that for *medicine*, Ilse. Everything else is coincidence."

She came in properly then, set the basket down, and looked around the room—the rented room above the chandler's shop on Tanner's Row, one window, one bed that Tam had claimed on the first night and refused to surrender, a table crowded with jars of rendered fat, dried willow bark, honey, comfrey, and a mortar and pestle that Hazel had bought with actual coins like an ordinary tradesman buying ordinary tools.

It was, he reflected nearly every day, the most remarkable collection of objects he had ever owned. Every single thing in it was exactly what it appeared to be.

* * *

Three weeks after the sky split open over the harvest festival, Vellsmark was still learning how to be itself again.

The gates had opened on the fourth day. Nobody had ordered it; the soldiers simply stopped guarding them, the way men stop singing a song they've forgotten the words to. The usurper's army sat outside the walls for another week, eating its own supply trains, waiting for something that never came—and then, one grey morning, it turned around and went home, and no one could say why, and everyone could feel why, the way you can feel weather changing through a closed window.

The gods were gone. Everyone agreed on that much. The priests of the Nine Names held services in empty churches and called it humility; the Lantern Cell's old meeting houses stood shuttered; and in the night market, where Hazel had once sold luck-knots to people who needed them to work, the charm-sellers had quietly changed their stock to buttons and bootlaces and candied ginger. Nobody said why that either. It was just understood, the way a city understands things, without anyone having to be brave enough to say it first.

Hazel had gone back to the market himself, out of habit more than hope, and stood for an hour among the stalls feeling like a man returned to a house where all the furniture had been sold. Then a woman had asked him for a fever-salve—just a salve, just tallow and beeswax and willow, nothing knotted into it, nothing sung over it—and he had made her one, and she had paid him two coppers, and he had walked home lighter than he'd walked in years.

That was the whole of it now. Tallow and beeswax and willow. It worked slowly, imperfectly, the way medicine was supposed to work. Children got better because their bodies fought, and the medicine helped the bodies fight, and if a mother prayed over the salve before she rubbed it on, the prayer went nowhere at all, and the child got better anyway.

Hazel found he could live with that. He was surprised, some nights, by how easily he lived with it.

* * *

"Show me the knot again," Tam said. "The one from before."

They were sitting on the chandler's steps in the late afternoon sun, and Hazel had a length of cord in his hands, because Tam had asked, and because Hazel had learned that there were questions you answered and questions you deflected, and Tam's questions had a way of sliding between the categories.

"It doesn't do anything," Hazel said. "You know that."

"I know. But show me anyway."

So Hazel showed him. A simple three-loop hitch, the kind sailors used, the kind any child might learn for skipping rope or tying bundles. His fingers moved through it without thought, and the cord lay dead and ordinary in his palm, and nothing in the world leaned toward it, and nothing in him reached back.

Before, the knots had been the smallest part of it—the visible tip of something vast and inherited, thirty generations of Choir-blood compressed into a hedge-charmer's sleight of hand. He had spent three years making sure the tip stayed hidden. He had never once, in all that time, wondered what it would be to have only the tip. To have the knot be the whole of the magic: cord, fingers, patience, done.

"Like this?" Tam said, mangling it.

"Worse than that, even. Here—" He guided the small hands through it. "Thumb under. No, your other thumb. There. That's a proper knot. It'll hold a bundle, a boat, anything you ask of it."

"Could it hold a god?"

Hazel kept his face pleasant. "No. Nothing holds a god. That was the mistake everybody made, for three hundred years. They thought things could hold gods. Knots, bloodlines, empires, prayers." He tied off Tam's lopsided loop and untied it again so the boy could practice. "Turns out gods are like cats. You can put them in a basket, but don't fool yourself about who's in charge."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he gave all theological matters, which was considerable. "Did it hurt? When they went?"

The question arrived sideways, the way children's questions did, aimed at something Hazel hadn't said aloud to anyone. When they went. The night of the festival, the unbinding, the vast wounded attention pouring through him and out into the world and away—he remembered it as light remembers a struck bell, which is to say: he remembered being the bell. And then silence. Not emptiness, exactly. More like a room after the furniture is carried out, where you keep seeing the shapes things used to make against the wall.

"A little," he said. "But it was the right kind of hurt. Like pulling a splinter."

"From the whole world?"

"Something like that."

Tam nodded, satisfied, and went back to murdering the knot, and Hazel sat in the sun and let the lie and the truth sit together comfortably, because they were mostly the same thing these days.

* * *

Corvin found him at the market on Thursday, as Corvin found him everywhere, with the punctuality of a man who claimed to be busy and was transparently not.

"Hazel Eriksen," Corvin announced, dropping onto the stool beside Hazel's stall as though he owned it, which he had been doing for two months and which Hazel had stopped objecting to after the first week. "Purveyor of salves. Enemy of fevers. Friend to children and widows and, according to Mrs. Brandt on Cooper Lane, the finest man in Vellsmark, a title I note you have done nothing to earn and less to deserve."

"Mrs. Brandt says that about anyone who fixes her roof."

"I fixed her roof in October. She calls me 'that nice young man' and then complains about my guttering." Corvin picked up a jar of cough syrup, read the label Hazel had written on it in careful plain script—*honey, thyme, lemon. Dose: a spoonful. It will taste bad. That means it's working.*—and snorted. "You've started writing instructions. Honest ones. 'It will taste bad.' Do you know how radical that is? Half the city used to sell miracles. Now you're selling honesty at two coppers the jar."

"Somebody has to corner the market."

"Mm." Corvin set the jar down and was quiet for a moment, which for Corvin was roughly equivalent to another man delivering a speech. Hazel had learned to wait him out. "Sera came by the cell house last night."

Hazel's hands didn't stop working. Wrapping the jar, tying the string, the ordinary motions. "And?"

"She's leaving. Going south, to her sister's, near Larenholt. She disbanded the Lantern Cell formally—read it out, gave back the ledgers, the works. Some of them cried. Some of them argued. Marta argued for four hours, but Marta argues with weather." Corvin turned the jar around, studying the label as if it were written in a language he was learning. "She asked about you. Whether you were well. Whether you'd want word sent."

"What did you tell her?"

"Told her you were well. Told her you'd want to know she asked." He paused. "Didn't tell her you'd write back. Figured that part was yours to decide."

Hazel tied off the string and set the finished jar in the row with the others. Twelve jars. A good morning's honest work. Somewhere in the middle of the war he had imagined that peace would feel like triumph, and instead it felt like this: twelve jars, a string, a friend who talked too much, and a letter he should write and didn't know how to begin.

"She believed it," Hazel said. "Right up to the end. She believed the gods coming back meant liberation. I think some part of her still believes it, even now, even after—" He stopped. The festival was not a thing they discussed in detail. Sera's stolen rite, the sky splitting, the crowd screaming, Hazel walking into the heart of it with a song that wasn't a weapon—it lived in the city's memory like a scar lives in skin, touched only at the edges. "I hope she finds something south of here that asks less of her."

"So do I." Corvin stretched, cracked his neck. "Anyway. That's the news. Cell's done, Sera's gone, Marta's opened a bakery, which everyone agrees is the most frightening development of the year. Oh—and the council voted yesterday. Officially. No proscription."

Hazel looked up. "What?"

"The magical proscription. Repealed. Unanimous, which in Vellsmark politics means two people abstained and one stormed out, but still." Corvin grinned, and there was something underneath the grin, something careful. "There's nothing left to proscribe, of course. Whatever the guild had, whatever the vessels carried—it's gone. All of it. Everywhere. The usurper's court tried a working in the capital last month, some old binding rite out of captured records, and by all accounts nothing happened at all. Just men in robes reading words at an empty room." He shrugged. "Magic's over, Hazel. Formally, legally, actually over. Which means—" and here the careful thing under the grin surfaced fully, and Corvin looked, for once, like a man asking rather than telling, "—which means nobody's going to come asking you for anything ever again. No inquisitors. No cells. No bloodline. So."

"So."

"So what will you do?"

* * *

It was, Hazel realized, the first time anyone had asked him that question and meant it.

Vane had asked him something like it, in that cold carpeted room with the sealed windows—but Vane's question had been *what are you*, and the answer had already been decided before Hazel opened his mouth. Sera had asked it too, in her way—*what will you become for us*—and the answer had been drafted before he arrived. Even Magda, gentle dying Magda, had asked it as inheritance: *what will you carry*. Every question of his life had been a form with the answers pre-filled, and he had spent forty years signing his name at the bottom.

Corvin sat on the stool with a jar of cough syrup in his hands and waited, actually waited, for an answer that didn't exist yet.

"I don't know," Hazel said, and heard how strange and clean the words sounded. "Isn't that something. Forty-four years old and I don't know what I'll do."

"You could stay," Corvin said, too casually. "Here. The room above the chandler's. The market. Mrs. Brandt's roof needs looking at again, and I'm told the guttering man is unreliable."

"The guttering man is you."

"Which is why the roof needs looking at." Corvin set the jar down. "I'm not—look. I'm not asking anything. I'm a courier with no letters to carry, in a city with no secrets left, and I've discovered I like knowing where you are in the mornings. That's all. That's the entire offer. Take it or leave it, but take it or leave it honestly."

Hazel looked at him. Corvin, who had watched him work magic in a fleeing crowd and said nothing to anyone. Corvin, who had broken him out of an inquisitor's wagon with a stolen key and a lie about orders. Corvin, who had held his arm across half of Vellsmark on the worst night of the century and never once asked what it cost.

"I'll stay," Hazel said.

"Yes?"

"For the winter, certainly. The chandler's a decent landlord, Tam needs someone to check his pulse twice daily or he'll sulk, and somebody has to keep the honest-syrup trade alive before the charlatans regroup." He paused. "And past the winter, we'll see. I find I like 'we'll see.' I've never had it before. My whole life was mapped before my grandmother was born, and now I've got 'we'll see,' and it turns out to be the finest thing anyone's ever given me."

Corvin smiled—not the market grin, the other one, the rare one. "We'll see," he agreed, as though sealing a contract.

* * *

That evening, Hazel climbed the hill above the city.

It was becoming a habit, though he told no one, because it required no explanation and he was out of practice with explanations that weren't lies. From the ridge you could see all of Vellsmark laid out below: the market square lighting its lamps, the harbor cranes folded up for the night, smoke rising straight from a thousand chimneys into windless air. Somewhere down there, Ilse was getting Tam to eat his supper, and Tam was counting his own heartbeats between bites, and Mrs. Brandt was complaining about gutters, and the whole enormous ordinary machinery of the city was turning without gods in it, without bindings, without a single knot holding anything but rope.

His hands ached faintly, the way they always did now at dusk. The healers Corvin had found had names for it—nerve-deep fatigue, the body remembering a weight it no longer carried. Hazel had his own name for it, which he kept private: it felt like the ghost of a ring worn for forty years, finally removed. The finger remembers. The finger heals.

Below, a lamp flickered in a window he couldn't see clearly, and he thought—idly, the way you think anything at the end of a long day—that somewhere in that city, someone was almost certainly praying. People prayed the way they hummed; you couldn't stamp it out, and maybe shouldn't. A mother over a fevered child. A sailor before a voyage. Someone whispering thanks into the dark for no reason they could name.

And the prayer went nowhere. That was the truth he had bought with everything he was. The gods were beyond reach, past the edge of the world's hearing, and no voice rose high enough, and no bloodline carried far enough, and the silence where the divine had been was permanent, total, final—

Except.

Hazel stopped on the path.

For half a heartbeat—less, less than the space between one lamplight and the next—something had stirred. Not an answer. Not a voice. Nothing so grand as any of it, nothing that would bend a knee or split a sky. More like warmth from a hearth in a house you're passing on a cold street: faint, brief, gone before you can turn your head. Felt in the hollow where the Choir used to live, the emptied room of him, the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was shut.

Then it was gone, and the evening was only the evening, and the city was only the city, and Hazel stood very still on the hill with his heart going sixty steady beats, counting.

He thought of Tam, checking his own pulse twice a day, needing to know the ordinary rhythm was still there. He thought of knots that held nothing but rope. He thought of Magda saying *never pray aloud*, and of how he hadn't, and of how whatever that warmth had been, it hadn't come from him.

A bird called somewhere down the slope. A dog barked twice and thought better of it. The lamps of Vellsmark burned on, small and stubborn, and the wind moved the grass, and nothing in the world required him to explain himself.

"We'll see," Hazel said aloud, to no one, to the hill, to whatever faint and gentle thing might or might not have been listening.

And he smiled, and put his hands in his pockets, and walked home in the dark like any other man.
