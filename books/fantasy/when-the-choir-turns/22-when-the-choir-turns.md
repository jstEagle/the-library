## 22. When the Choir Turns

The hill above Vellsmark had no name that anyone remembered, which Hazel considered a mercy. Everything else in his life had been named—the guild, the bloodline, the Choir, the war—and names had a way of becoming chains if you let them sit long enough. An unnamed hill was just a hill. You could walk up it without owing anyone an explanation.

He climbed it on the first morning of true autumn, when the wind came off the sea with a bite in it and the gorse had gone to seed in great pale clouds. Below him the city woke in its usual fashion: chimneys first, then the harbor bells, then the argument between the fishmongers on Cordwainer's Row that had apparently been running since before the occupation and showed every sign of outlasting them all. He could hear old Piet calling someone a thief over the price of herring, and the someone—a woman by the sound of her—giving as good as she got. Three years ago those voices had been muffled behind sealed gates and ration lines. Now they were just loud. It was, Hazel thought, the most beautiful sound a city could make.

His knees complained about the climb. That was new. Not the magic leaving him—he'd expected the hollow feeling, the way the world had gone quiet as a room after a party—but the ordinary wear and tear of an ordinary body. He was thirty-four years old and he ached like a man of sixty, because for eleven years he had spent power the way other men spent coin, carelessly and without ever checking the ledger. The gods' gift had papered over all of it. Now the bill had come due, and he paid it gladly, one stiff joint at a time.

Mostly gladly.

* * *

He still dreamed about the singing.

Not often anymore. In the first weeks after the festival he had woken every night with the taste of it in his mouth, that vast chord pouring through him and out of him, taking everything he was and everything the guild had hoarded for three hundred years. He had dreamed of the gods going—felt them go, the way you feel a great ship pass in the night, the water rocking long after the light is gone. Vast, wounded, listening. And then, at the last, something that might have been gratitude, or might have simply been the absence of rage, which from beings that old was probably the same thing.

Now the dreams came maybe once a month, and they were quieter. In them he stood on this hill, or one very like it, and heard nothing at all, and in the dream the nothing felt like rest.

Corvin said that was healing. Corvin said a lot of things with the confidence of a man who had once smuggled coded prayers inside fish barrels and never quite lost the habit of believing his own deliveries. *You didn't lose your magic, Hazel,* he liked to say. *You lost their magic. There's a difference.* Hazel usually let him have it. It was easier than explaining that he had checked, thoroughly, in the way a man checks an empty purse—not once but a dozen times, half hoping, half dreading—and found nothing there but himself.

Himself turned out to be enough for salves. That had surprised him more than anything. Feverfew and willow bark and honey and clean linen, the same things his grandmother had used before the guild ever put a knot in his palm, and it turned out they worked. Not miracles. Work. Ilse's boy Tam had come down with a summer ague in the second month after the unbinding, and Ilse had appeared on Hazel's doorstep with the child burning hot in her arms and a look on her face that asked nothing and feared everything, and Hazel had done what any herbwise man would do: cool cloths, bitter tea, and sitting up through the worst two nights telling Tam increasingly elaborate lies about the habits of harbor seals until the fever broke on its own schedule, the way fevers do.

Ilse had wept anyway. "It worked," she kept saying, as though ordinary medicine were the miracle. And perhaps, Hazel thought, from where she stood, it was. She had set a hunt on him with one grateful sentence to an inquisitor's clerk, and she had spent two years since trying to pay it back in small dangerous coins—barrack gossip, gate rotations, the location of Vane's records—and now she came to him with a sick child and called his willow bark a wonder. Some debts don't cancel. They just change shape until both people can carry them.

Tam was fine, incidentally. He had decided Hazel was the most interesting man in Vellsmark, mostly because Hazel knew the seal lies, and had appointed himself an apprentice. Hazel had explained carefully that there was nothing to apprentice to. Tam had nodded with the grave skepticism of an eight-year-old and shown up again the next week with a basket of weeds he'd pulled from the churchyard, asking which ones were medicine. Hazel had told him. It seemed harmless. It was, in fact, just knowledge, and knowledge was the one inheritance he intended to keep passing down.

* * *

Sera had disbanded the Lantern Cell in the spring.

He'd heard it secondhand, then thirdhand, then finally from Corvin, who delivered the news the way he delivered everything, folded twice and slightly damp. She had stood up in the wrecked granary where the cell kept its meetings and told them it was over—not because the cause was wrong, she said, but because she had nearly fed the whole city to a rite she didn't fully understand, and a leader who would burn truth for victory would eventually burn the city too. Then she had walked out before anyone could argue, which those who knew her agreed was the most Sera thing she had ever done.

Some of the cell had scattered. Some had stayed in Vellsmark and become, of all things, citizens—running a soup kitchen off Cordwainer's Row, organizing the rebuilding crews, arguing with the new council about grain tariffs with the same ferocity they'd once reserved for smuggling fugitives. Rebellion, it turned out, could be composted into governance if you were patient. Corvin had joined the council himself, to everyone's astonishment including his own. "I'm very good at logistics," he'd said defensively, when Hazel laughed. "And I look trustworthy in a sash."

"You absolutely do not."

"The sash does the work," Corvin said. "I just stand inside it."

Vane was dead—had died in the chaos of the festival, trampled or cut down depending on whose account you believed, though the version Hazel privately favored was the one where the Inquisitor-General of the free cities met his end not in glory but in the undignified crush of a crowd panicking at a split sky. The usurper's armies had stalled at the borders afterward, waiting for a miracle that never came, and then had simply stopped coming at all. Empires, Hazel had learned, run on certainty the way mills run on water, and the usurper's certainty had been divine right, and when the divinity drained out of the right there was nothing left to turn the wheel. The court had begun eating itself within the year. The free cities hadn't even had to fight. They'd just had to wait, and rebuild, and argue about herring.

* * *

So: the hill, on an autumn morning, for no reason except that his legs wanted walking and the city below wanted watching.

He reached the top as the sun cleared the headland and laid a long gold road across the harbor water. Gulls wheeled and swore. Somewhere behind him a lark went up, spiraling its small relentless song into the blue. Hazel sat down on the flat stone that generations of shepherds had probably also sat on, and breathed, and did nothing at all, which remained his favorite activity and the one skill no guild had ever taught him.

That was when he heard the prayer.

Not heard exactly. It wasn't sound. It was more like warmth on the back of the neck—a faint pressure at the edge of attention, the ghost of a sensation he'd spent a lifetime learning to notice and two years learning to ignore. Someone below, somewhere in the waking sprawl of Vellsmark, was praying. Not aloud. Not to any god, because there were none left within reach; he had made sure of that, at the cost of everything, and he would make sure of it again. Just—a person, talking to the morning. A woman, he thought, though he couldn't have said how he knew. Asking for something small. Strength, maybe, or luck, or the nerve to say a difficult thing to someone she loved. The kind of prayer people send up without expecting change, the way you toss a coin in a fountain mostly for the wish's own sake.

And something answered.

Hazel went very still.

It was not the old gods. He knew their weight the way you know the weight of a storm front—vast, ancient, indifferent as weather. This was nothing like that. This was small. Faint as a candle seen across a valley. Gentle in a way the bound gods had never once been gentle, not in three centuries of captivity, not in the fury of their release. It moved toward that woman's small prayer the way a hand moves toward a fallen bird—carefully, without appetite, without agenda. It touched whatever she needed touched. And then it withdrew, unhurried, and was gone, and the morning continued exactly as it had been.

Hazel sat on his stone for a long time.

He ran through every explanation he owned. Echo of the unbinding, lingering in the world's fabric. His own imagination, grieving for the music. Some residue of the Choir, a last knot working loose. He held each one up to the light and found them all wanting, and what remained, when the explanations were spent, was simply this: something new. Something that had not existed before—or had existed always, so deep beneath the old gods and the guild and the binding that no one had ever thought to look—that had stirred in the space they left behind. Not a god who could be bound. Not a power that could be aimed. Perhaps not even a *who*. Just a tendency in the world, the way water tends downhill. A leaning-toward. Answering, when it chose, and only ever gently.

The guild would have wanted to catch it. Knot it, name it, put it in a verse and hold the verse. The resistance would have wanted to march under it. Vane would have wanted to build an empire on it. Hazel sat in the autumn sun and understood, with a clarity that felt like the last payment on a very long debt, that the only way to keep such a thing safe was the way he was keeping it now: by knowing it and telling no one. By letting it stay unnamed. By being, himself, the only vessel it would ever have—one tired man on an unnamed hill, keeping a secret the size of a candle flame.

He thought about Tam, and the basket of churchyard weeds. He thought about Ilse's face when the fever broke. He thought about Corvin in his ridiculous sash, and Sera walking out of the granary into an ordinary life, and the fishmongers shouting, and the lark still going up and up as if the sky owed it money.

The world, he realized, was doing what he had done. It was choosing.

He smiled. It started small and got away from him, the way honest smiles do, until he was grinning like a fool at the gulls, and a shepherd passing on the lower path with his dog gave him the wide berth traditionally afforded to madmen and philosophers, which in Vellsmark were considered the same profession.

Then Hazel Eriksen—hedge-charmer, last of the Nine Names, unmaker of gods, teller of seal lies—got up off his stone, brushed the grass from his coat, and started back down toward the city, because there was salve to make and a boy expecting him and herring to be argued over, and a life, his own, finally and entirely his, waiting at the bottom of the hill.

Whatever had answered the woman's prayer stayed where it was: faint, new, and free.

He told no one.

But as he walked, whistling, he found he was listening—just a little, just in case—for the next small voice the world might choose to answer.
