## 1. Charms for the Faithless

The trouble with selling luck is that you have to believe in it a little, or the customers can tell.

I'd learned that my second year behind the counter of the shop on Candlemaker's Row — not *my* shop, never say *my*, because ownership leaves a trail and trails get followed — when a fishwife with hands like knotted rope asked me straight out whether the charm I was selling her would actually work. I gave her the smile I'd practiced in a cracked mirror until it stopped feeling like wearing someone else's face. *Madam, if it works, you'll never know it did. If it doesn't, you've lost three coppers and gained a story.* She laughed. She bought two.

Fifteen years in Vell, and that was still the whole of my trade: stories priced at three coppers, with a small cold weight of something else folded inside.

The city itself had been built for forgetting. Vell sat where the river Maren went fat and slow before the sea, all soot and slate and steeples, a place that had once hummed — literally hummed, though nobody under forty remembered — with the low harmonic of working magic. Now the Wardens walked the streets with tuning forks instead of swords, and every bell in every tower was cast from deaf metal, and children were taught their letters with the same lesson drilled beneath them: *magic is a debt, and debts are collected.* There were still charmsellers, of course. There are always sellers of anything people need badly enough to lie to themselves about. But we were licensed, inspected, and required by statute to sell nothing but brass, glass, and confidence.

Which is why, on the morning the widow came in, I was careful to be holding nothing but confidence.

* * *

She arrived just after the ninth bell, which was late enough that I'd already sold a fever-charm to a plasterer's apprentice and talked a drunk out of buying what he clearly wanted, which was an excuse. The bell over my door had a dull clapper — Warden regulation — so her entrance was announced only by the weather: a gust of wet autumn air and the smell of river mud.

She was perhaps fifty, dressed in good black gone grey at the seams, the kind of mourning that had been worn past fashion into habit. Her eyes did the thing I'd learned to read across a counter the way other men read weather over water: they moved to the shelves, then away, then back, hungry and ashamed of it.

"Madam," I said, "you look like a woman deciding between confession and purchase. I should warn you the confession is free but the wine's terrible."

That startled a ghost of a laugh out of her. Good. Fear makes buyers stingy; laughter loosens the purse strings. This too I had practiced.

"I'm told," she said, carefully, the way you'd step onto ice, "that you sell... luck."

"I sell luck the way a ferryman sells the far shore. I can't promise the crossing, only the boat."

"And the boat?"

I gestured her toward the counter. Under the glass, arranged on velvet gone bald in patches, lay my inventory: knucklebones polished smooth, horseshoe nails bent into rings, coins from countries that no longer existed, and — in the center, where the light through the dirty window fell best — a small brass locket on a leather cord, its surface worked with a pattern of interlocking waves.

The pattern was not decoration. The pattern was a binding sigil in the old coastal style, worn down by my own file until it read as mere ornament to any Warden's eye, but underneath — underneath it held a thread of the real thing, a single held note of working magic, the way a coal holds heat long after the fire's dead. I'd made it myself, eleven years ago, in the last hour of the last night I ever let myself practice openly, and I had never dared make another.

It was the last true thing I owned besides my name, and my name wasn't even mine anymore.

"This one," she said, touching the glass above the locket without asking, the way people always do, as if wanting could pass through solid objects.

"Ah." I lifted it out and let it catch the light. "The Wave-Knot. Popular with sailors' wives, mothers of the reckless, anyone who loves someone the sea has opinions about."

"My son," she said. "He fishes the Maren mouth. The water's been..." She stopped, started again. "Three boats lost this season. He won't stop going out. He says luck is for people who can't read weather."

"Your son sounds like a fool," I said pleasantly, "and also correct."

Her chin came up. For a moment she wasn't a nervous widow; she was somebody's mother, armed. "Then why should I buy your charm?"

"Because reading weather tells you what the sky intends. It says nothing about what the water remembers." I turned the locket over in my fingers, and here is where I must confess to the sin at the heart of my trade, the small daily treason I committed so routinely I barely felt it as treason anymore: I let the magic wake.

It isn't dramatic. That's the first thing they don't tell you about outlawed craft — that real magic, the old kind, the kind I was born to, doesn't flash or burn. It *hums*. It sits in the fingers like a struck tuning fork pressed to bone, and if you're careful, if you've spent fifteen years practicing care, you can hold it there, contained, invisible, while your face does the work of a common tradesman.

The locket warmed against my palm. Through the sigil I felt the little held note stir, recognize me, lean toward my skin like a plant toward light. Fifteen years since anything living in that way had touched me, and still my body reacted like a man hearing his mother's voice in a crowd — a full-body flinch of *home*, immediately strangled.

"Feel that?" I said. "Warmth. Brass does that in a warm room, of course. Coincidence."

"Of course," she said, and her hand came up slowly, and when her fingers closed over the locket beside mine, I let the note settle into the metal properly, seating it home the way you seat a keel, and her breath caught — just slightly, just once.

"It's warm," she whispered.

"Brass," I said again, but softer now, because there was no fooling her and we both knew it, and this was the moment that mattered, the moment I lived for and hated: the moment of complicity. Two criminals across a counter, agreeing without words.

"How much?" she said.

"For you? Twelve silver."

"That's robbery."

"That's *luck*, madam. Robbery would be charging you what it's worth." Which was everything I had left of a certain life, and not for sale at any price, which is why twelve silver was exactly right — high enough to honor it, low enough that I could bear to let it go.

She counted it out from a purse she kept knotted inside her sleeve, coin by coin, and I watched her hands and thought, not for the first time, that grief has its own economy, its own exchange rates. Twelve silver against one son. Cheap. I'd have paid more.

"Wear it under your clothes," I told her as I wrapped it. "Never speak of it. If a Warden asks, it was your husband's, and you keep it for sentiment, and sentiment is legal." I tied the string of the paper packet with a little flourish, because even contraband deserves presentation. "And madam — the charm doesn't make the water spare him. It tips things. A rope that holds one hour longer. A squall that waits till he's ashore. Luck is small mercies, stacked."

"Small mercies," she repeated, and something in her face crumpled and reassembled itself, and I understood that I had just sold her not a charm but permission to hope, which is the most dangerous commodity in Vell and the only one I dealt in.

At the door she paused. "You didn't ask his name."

"The charm doesn't need it."

"No." She looked back at me, and her eyes were sharp suddenly, sharper than nervous widows' eyes ought to be. "But you didn't ask *mine* either. Most tradesmen would."

"Most tradesmen remember faces," I said. "I prefer to forget them. It's kinder all around."

She considered that, nodded once like a woman closing a ledger, and went out into the wet street. The bell over the door gave its mute click. I stood there a moment with twelve silver in my palm and the ghost-warmth of the locket fading from my fingers, and I felt the familiar shape of the day settle over me: the ache, low and constant, like a tooth you've stopped noticing until you bite down.

* * *

Here is what I did with my afternoons, in the fifteenth year of my disappearance: I drank tea. I mended the shop's awning. I argued with the fishmonger about the honesty of his scales. I was, by every measurement the Wardens cared about, the most boring man on Candlemaker's Row.

I say *man*. Let the record show I was careful about that too. Silas Almeida, Realmwright of the Third Circle, had been many things before the Proscription — tall in the old chronicles' engravings, silver-tongued in the court records, and, in the final accounting, a traitor whose signature sat on documents that unmade the world. Silas of Candlemaker's Row was none of those things. Silas of Candlemaker's Row was medium, forgettable, charming in the harmless way of shopkeepers, and possessed of hands that trembled only when absolutely no one was watching.

Because here is the thing about hiding: you cannot hide from yourself. Every night I locked the shutters and sat in the back room among my forbidden tools — the pens cut from reeds that grew nowhere anymore, the inks ground from stones that had names, the half-finished maps of a world that had been declared fictional by statute — and I let my hands shake. Fifteen years of shaking, saved up, spent nightly, alone, like a miser counting coins he can never spend.

I missed it the way you miss a language. Not the power — well. Yes, the power, I'm honest enough in my own head to admit the power. But mostly the *speech*. Magic is a conversation. The world talks; you talk back. And I had spent fifteen years in a room where the world was gagged, and I had done the gagging, and some nights the silence was so complete I could hear my own heart apologizing.

You want to know what I did, back then, the thing I fled from? You'll get it eventually. Everyone gets everyone's sins eventually. But that afternoon I knew only this much: that I had helped write the law that made my own hands illegal, and that I had run not from justice but from the particular justice of being right too late.

So: tea. Awning. Fishmonger. Boring.

Until the knock came at the back door, which never happened, because nobody knew there was a back door except the man who'd built the shelf in front of it, and he was me, fifteen years ago, and I had paid him very well to forget.

* * *

Actually, no. Let me be precise, because precision was my trade before charm-selling was, and because the moments before a life changes never announce themselves honestly. They come disguised as errands.

It was dusk. I was trimming the lamp wicks, which I did every dusk, both because the shop needed it and because fire is the closest legal thing to magic and I rationed myself a small dose daily. The street outside had gone blue-grey. Somewhere a Warden's patrol passed, boots in rhythm, and I did what I always did when they passed: I went still, the way prey goes still, and listened to my own heartbeat count them by.

Nine pairs of boots. Past. Gone.

And then, from the front of the shop, in the space where the doorbell should have rung and didn't: a sound. Wet. Heavy. The sound of a body negotiating with gravity and losing.

I found him just inside the threshold, on his knees, one hand braced against my counter as if it were a rail on a pitching deck. He was soaked through with more than rain — there was dark spreading under his coat, too much dark, the wrong color for rain. His other hand was pressed flat against his chest, and tucked beneath it, held there like a letter he'd sworn to deliver, was a fold of heavy paper.

His eyes found mine. They were clear, which frightened me more than the blood. Dying men with clear eyes have decided something.

"Shop's closed," I heard myself say, because panic in me has always expressed itself as retail.

"Silas." My name. Not the shop name, not the safe name — the other one, the buried one, spoken in a voice going rough at the edges. "Almeida."

Every hair on my arms stood up and turned to face the door. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of careful boredom, and the name had come walking in off the street wearing blood.

"You're mistaken," I said. "My name is—"

"*Finish it.*" He dragged the folded paper from his coat and pushed it across the counter toward me, and his hand left a smear on the wood, and he looked at me with those terribly clear eyes and said, quieter, almost gentle, the way you'd instruct a child in something you hoped they'd never need: "Finish the map, Realmwright. Before the Cantor finishes the song."

I want to tell you I asked the sensible questions. Who are you, what song, what map, how do you know my name, shall I fetch a surgeon, a priest, a Warden. I want to tell you I was cautious, because caution had kept me alive for fifteen years and I owed it everything.

What I actually did was take the paper. My hands did it without consulting me — the hands of a cartographer, which never entirely die, they only sleep — and the moment my fingers closed on that fold, I knew.

Ink has a smell. Ordinary ink smells of oak gall and iron. But there is another ink, older, that smells faintly of copper and rain and something underneath like a struck bell, and I had ground that ink with my own hands in another life, and the smell of it rose off that folded paper and went through me like a knife finding the gap between ribs.

Blood-ink. Realmwright's ink. Forbidden twice over — once by the law I helped write, and once by the law of supply, because the last source of it had died with a country that no longer appeared on maps.

The stranger smiled. It was a terrible smile, the smile of a man whose accounts are finally balanced. "Knew you'd know," he whispered. "Knew you'd—" And then the clarity went out of his eyes all at once, like a lamp pinched, and he slid down the counter, and the shop on Candlemaker's Row was very quiet, and I was kneeling in a stranger's blood holding a map that smelled of my childhood, and somewhere out in the blue-grey evening the Wardens' boots were coming back around the block.

I did the only sensible thing.

I bolted the door.
