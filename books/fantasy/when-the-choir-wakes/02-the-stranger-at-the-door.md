## 2. The Stranger at the Door

There is an art to closing a shop in Vell, and I had perfected it over fifteen years the way other men perfect a golf swing or a lie: with patience, repetition, and a healthy fear of consequences.

The trick was to look unremarkable while doing it. Shutters down at half past the evening bell, not before — early closers attracted attention, and attention was the one commodity I never stocked. Lamp trimmed low but not *too* low, because a charm-seller who skimped on light was either poor or hiding something, and both invited questions. And always, always, a final sweep of the counter for anything that might be mistaken for the real thing.

I was doing that sweep when the door banged open hard enough to rattle the protective charms I'd hung there — the fake ones, brass and glass and honest fraud — and a man fell through it.

Not walked. Fell, in the way that furniture falls, all angles and surrender. He caught himself on the doorframe, left a smear of something dark on the wood, and made it three more steps into my shop before his legs filed their resignation.

I should tell you what I did next, because what I did next was nothing, and I have thought about that nothing every day since.

I stood behind my counter with a dust cloth in my hand like a man interrupted mid-prayer, and I watched him bleed on my floorboards, and every instinct I'd spent fifteen years sharpening told me the same thing: *not your problem, Silas. Not your name. Not your door.* In Vell, trouble was like rain — you let it fall on someone else's roof and thanked the Wardens you had eaves.

"Shop's closed," I said, because apparently my survival instincts had a sense of humor.

He looked up at me from the floor. He was older than I'd first taken him for, fifty or sixty, with grey going white at the temples and a face built for laughing that hadn't laughed in a long time. His coat was good wool once, now dark with wet that I very much hoped was rain. It wasn't rain. Rain doesn't smell like copper.

"You're the cartographer," he said.

My dust cloth stopped moving.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, in the flat, pleasant, forgettable voice I'd practiced until it fit me like a glove fits a hand — snug, seamless, easy to take off. "I sell charms. Luck, mostly. Love, if you're desperate. Protection from creditors, if you're very desperate."

Something like a smile crossed his face, which was an impressive feat given that he appeared to be dying on the floor of a stranger's shop at half past the evening bell. "Almeida," he said. "Silas Almeida."

And there it was. My name, spoken aloud in this room for the first time in fifteen years, landing on me like a dropped stone. You spend long enough being nobody and you forget that your actual name is a key — that somewhere out there are locks shaped exactly like it, waiting.

"You've got the wrong shop," I said. "Wrong city, probably."

"No." He dragged himself up onto one elbow, and I saw then the wound beneath the coat — a long tear across his ribs, deep enough that I looked away from it on principle, the way you look away from a debt. "No, I haven't. I've been looking for you for eleven years, Almeida. Eleven years. Do you know how well a man can hide? You hide better than anyone I ever hunted."

"*Hunted*," I repeated. The word did not improve my mood.

"The Wardens' word. Mine too, once. Doesn't matter now." He fumbled inside his coat, and I confess my hand went under the counter to where I kept a knife, because a dying man reaching into his coat in your shop is either handing you a treasure or a reason. He came out with neither. He came out with paper.

Folded paper, small as a prayer book, creased soft with handling. He held it out to me across four feet of bloodied floorboard, and his arm shook with the weight of it, which told me more about the paper than any description could have. Men do not tremble holding letters. Men tremble holding things that matter.

"Take it," he said.

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it." A cough bent him nearly double; when he straightened, there was red on his lips and something bright and terrible in his eyes. "That's rather the point of it. Take it, cartographer. *Finish it.*"

"I'm not—"

"You are." He said it simply, without accusation, the way you'd tell a man he had his hat on backwards. "You've been hiding it so long you think the hiding is who you are. But your hands know. Look at your hands."

Against every rule I had, I looked at my hands.

They were steady. That was the horror of it. Fifteen years of selling false charms and keeping my gift folded up small as a moth in winter, and my hands were *steady*, and they wanted the paper. I could feel the wanting in them like a tide against a seawall.

"Don't," I said, to him or to them, I'm still not sure.

"It's Realmwright work," he said. "Blood-ink. The last of it. Maybe the only of it, now, except—" and he looked at me with those terrible bright eyes, and finished: "except yours."

I took the map.

I want to be clear that I intended, even in that moment, to throw it in the river. I took it the way you take a live coal off a child — to dispose of it safely, at arm's length, with maximum caution. That is the story I told myself as my fingers closed around the folded paper and something in my chest turned over like a sleeper recognizing home.

It was warm. That was the first thing. Paper shouldn't be warm. This was warm the way a hand is warm, and it pulsed — faintly, slowly, once every few heartbeats — against my palm.

And the ink. Even folded, even in the bad lamplight, I could see the edge of a line where it seeped through the crease, and I knew that color. You know your own handwriting the way you know your own cough in a crowd. Blood-ink was the first thing they taught us, before they taught us anything else: *the realm remembers what is written in life.* I had been nine years old, kneeling at my grandmother's workbench, watching her open her own thumb with a bone stylus and draw a coastline that moved.

Fifteen years since I'd seen that color. Fifteen years since anyone alive could make it.

Except, apparently, whoever had made this map. Except, possibly, me.

"Who are you?" I asked. My voice came out strange. Smaller.

"Aldous's man," he said, and the name meant nothing to me then, though it would come to mean everything. "Once. Before the singing started." He tried to rise and failed, sliding back down against my counter, leaving another dark streak on the wood. I remember thinking, absurdly, *that will never scrub out,* as if the stain were the problem. As if any of it would ever scrub out.

"You're dying," I said. "I should fetch a physicker."

"They'd ask why a charm-seller has blood on his floor." He smiled again, and this time it cost him visibly. "You'll not fetch anyone, Almeida. You'll stand there and be careful, because that's what you do. Careful. It's all right. I didn't come here to be saved."

"Why did you come here?"

"To finish it." He pressed his back against the counter and looked up at me, and for a moment the pain in his face cleared like cloud off a mountain, and underneath it was something I recognized from mirrors: exhaustion. The specific, bone-deep tiredness of a person who has been carrying a secret too heavy for too long. "Eleven years I carried it. She was supposed to carry it after me, but they found us, and she ran east, and I ran here, because the map said here. It says a lot of things. It's been saying them louder lately." He tapped the paper in my hand with one bloody finger. "It wants its maker."

"I'm not its maker."

"No. But you're the same trade." His breath was going ragged now, each one a negotiation. "Listen. Listen, because I haven't much time and you haven't much choice, whatever you think. There's a country on the other side of the fold. Cael Sorrow. The maps call it unmapped, and they're right, and they're wrong — it's mapped fine, it's just that the mapping sings, and the song has gone wrong. There's a thing there wearing a crown. It calls itself the Cantor. It's eating the sound out of the world, Almeida. Villages. Names. People. It sang a whole harbor quiet last spring and now the water there doesn't move."

"That's a story," I said. My mouth was dry. "A good one. You should write it down."

"I did." He nodded at the map. "In blood. It's all true, and worse, and the only man who can read the rest of it is a Realmwright, and the only Realmwrights left are dead or hiding in charm shops in Vell pretending to sell luck to widows."

The bell above my door chose that moment to sway, though no draft had touched it, and give one soft, clear note. Neither of us looked at it. We were both looking at the map, which had grown, I would swear to this day, slightly warmer.

"How did you find me?" I asked. "Truly."

"The map led me. It's been leading me for eleven years, wrong turn after wrong turn, and every wrong turn was right, because it wasn't taking me anywhere. It was taking me to *you*. It knows its own." His eyes were losing focus, the bright terrible thing in them guttering like my lamp. "Finish it. Find the waypoints. Amberglass first. And Almeida — when you meet the others, and you will, because that map collects people the way gutters collect rain — tell them Merrick sent them, and that he was sorry about the floor."

"Merrick—"

But he was already going. I saw it happen the way you see dusk happen: gradually, then all at once. His head tipped back against my counter. His breathing stretched out between one silence and the next, longer and longer, like a singer holding a final note, and I knelt beside him — I don't remember deciding to kneel — and put two fingers to his throat because some part of me, the part that predates the hiding, still believed in doing things properly.

There was a pulse. Then there was a pause that lasted too long. Then there was nothing at all, and the shop was very quiet, and the map in my hand beat once, softly, like a second heart agreeing to carry on alone.

* * *

Here is what I did in the hour that followed, in order:

I checked the street. Empty. Evening fog coming off the canal, lamps lit, a drunk singing three streets over — the ordinary music of Vell, indifferent to the extraordinary corpse on my floor.

I locked the door. Turned the sign. Drew the bolt, and then, for the first time in fifteen years, drew the *other* bolt, the iron one I'd sworn I'd never need, the one shaped like a knot with no beginning.

I sat down on the floor across from Merrick — he had a name now, and the name made it worse — and I held the map and I did not open it.

Understand: opening it felt like a door I couldn't close. Everything in me that had survived fifteen years by staying shut said *leave it folded, leave him where he lies, fetch the Wardens in the morning and let them sort it, let them hang you if they must, but do not open that paper, because the last time you touched work like this you were nine years old and the world still had magic in it, and you have not been that person in a very long time.*

But my hands were steady. And the map was warm. And somewhere under the fear, under the fifteen years of careful nothing, a girl-shaped absence in my chest — no, not a girl, a child with ink-stained fingers and a grandmother's voice saying *the realm remembers, little wren, the realm remembers what we write in life* — that absence was leaning toward the paper like a plant toward light.

I opened it.

The creases fell apart along old wounds, and the lamplight caught the ink, and I stopped breathing.

Coastlines drawn in blood-ink, moving — actually *moving*, slow as continental drift, the shorelines breathing in and out. Mountains rendered in strokes so fine they might have been etched with a needle. And in the margins, in a cramped, slanting hand I knew — God help me, I *knew* — annotations. Tide-notes. Waypoint-marks in the old cipher. A sketch of a tower with a bell. And at the top corner, where a cartographer signs their work, a symbol instead of a name: a circle broken by a single vertical line, open at the bottom like a cupped hand.

The mark of the Realmwrights. My grandmother's mark. *My* mark, the one I hadn't drawn since I was twenty-two years old and running.

But it wasn't my hand. The writing was Merrick's — he'd learned it, copied it, kept the forms alive the way a chorister keeps a dead composer's notation. Someone had taught him. Someone had believed there would be a reader.

And there, in the lower margin, in that same borrowed hand, a line I read three times before it made sense, and then read a fourth time hoping it would stop:

*The last page wants the maker's blood. Forgive me. Finish it.*

The lamp flickered. Outside, the drunk had stopped singing, or wandered off, or been silenced — in Vell you learned not to wonder which. The map lay open across my knees, warm as a sleeping animal, its coastlines breathing, its margins full of a dead man's faithful copy of a craft I'd buried alive.

And beneath the waypoint-marks, beneath the tower and the tides and the cipher, in fresh blood-ink that had not been there when Merrick pressed the paper into my hands — I would swear this, I will always swear this — a new line was drying.

It pointed east. Toward the canal. Toward the thin place I'd spent fifteen years pretending not to feel, the ache in the air like a healed bone before rain.

And beneath it, in handwriting that was not Merrick's, and was not mine, and was older than both of us:

*Hurry.*

I am a careful man. I have been a careful man for fifteen years, and careful men do not follow maps written in blood by strangers who die on their floors.

I reached for my coat.
