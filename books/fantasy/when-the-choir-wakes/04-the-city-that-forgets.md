## 4. The City That Forgets

I buried the stranger's boots before dawn.

Not the whole body — the Wardens would have wanted the whole body, and the Wardens were already coming; I could hear them two streets over, their boots striking the cobbles in that particular cadence the city used when it wanted you to know it was thinking about you. Just the boots. They were good boots, oiled leather, salt-stained at the seams, and they were also evidence, and I had learned fifteen years ago that evidence is simply a story someone else is planning to tell about you.

The rest of him I'd laid out on my worktable under a sheet and told myself he was a mannequin. A large, cold, inconveniently dead mannequin who had pressed a folded map into my hands with his last living strength and said *finish it* in a voice like a door closing.

I did not finish it. I put it in the flour tin.

This is the kind of decision that seems wise at three in the morning and insane by nine, which is roughly when the knocking started.

* * *

Vell has a way of forgetting things, and I had always assumed this was a mercy. The city forgets debts, forgets grudges, forgets the names of hanged men within a season. It forgot magic so thoroughly that most people under forty genuinely believed charms were superstition — little tin birds and knotted red thread sold to the gullible — and honestly, that was the finest piece of enchantment I ever worked: convincing an entire district that my shop sold nothing but lies. Fifteen years of selling truth disguised as fraud. The faithless widow from chapter one of my life here bought her luck charm believing it was a placebo, and it worked anyway, because belief is a door and it doesn't much matter which side you knock on.

But there are two kinds of forgetting. There is the forgetting that lets a city sleep, and there is the forgetting that happens because something ate the memory, and when the Wardens came up Saltrow Lane that morning, I understood that Vell's forgetting had a budget, and someone had decided to spend it.

They went door to door. Not searching — *interviewing*. That was the polite word painted on the side of their wagon, in letters that seemed to drink the light around them: WARDENS OF THE PEACEFUL RECORD. I'd never paid them much attention before. Peaceful Record. In Vell, records were peaceful the way graves are peaceful.

The knock on my door came at half past nine, and it was not the Warden knock. It was two sharp raps and a scrape, and I knew that knock, because it belonged to Brann Oduya, who lived in the narrow house beside mine with a boat on a trailer she maintained better than most people maintain children.

"Silas." Her voice through the wood, low. "Don't open yet."

Which is, of course, the single fastest way to make me want to open a door. But I'd heard the Wagon stop at the corner, and I pressed my eye to the gap in the shutters instead, and there she was on my step: sixty-ish, built like a mooring post, grey braid coiled at her neck, wearing her ferry coat with the collar up. Behind her, at the mouth of the lane, four Wardens in dove-grey stood talking to the chandler, and the chandler was shaking his head in a way that made my stomach drop, because Tam the chandler had bought a fever-charm off me two winters ago and owed me either money or silence, and I could no longer remember which.

"They're asking after a stranger," Brann said, without turning around. "Big fellow. Foreign accent. Seen him?" She pitched it loud enough to carry, conversational, bored. "Everyone's seen everyone. That's the trouble with cities."

One of the Wardens looked up. Even across the distance I felt the look land on Brann's back like a hand. She didn't flinch. She scratched her nose, spat expertly into the gutter — a woman who understood the grammar of unremarkability as fluently as I did, though where mine was a performance hers seemed constitutional — and ambled toward them with her hands in her pockets.

I should have stayed at the shutter. Any sensible fugitive stays at the shutter. Instead I watched, because I am not any sensible fugitive, I am a coward with curiosity, which is worse.

What happened next took maybe ninety seconds and rearranged something in my chest that I'm still putting back.

Brann reached the Wardens and did not wait to be questioned. She questioned *them*. "You're the fourth wagon this morning," she said. "Fourth. Man dies on Ferry Street two nights back and now the whole district's turned out like it's market day. Who died? Anyone say?"

The tall one — sergeant's knots on his shoulder, face like a closed ledger — said, "That's not public information."

"No," Brann agreed pleasantly. "It never is. That's how I know it matters." She jerked a thumb at the lane behind her. "You'll want the lodging houses. Sailors' rest, above the net-mender's. Stranger'd go there, if he was a stranger. Nobody strange sleeps on Saltrow Lane. We're all too tired."

And the thing — the *thing* — was that it was true. Nobody strange slept on Saltrow Lane except me, and I had spent fifteen years making sure that even my strangeness was boring. But she said it with such flat, salt-cured certainty that the sergeant actually wrote it down. *Lodging houses above the net-mender's.* He thanked her. He touched his hat brim, which Wardens do not do for civilians, and the wagon rolled on toward the harbor, and Brann stood in the emptying lane with her hands in her pockets until it was fully gone, and only then did she turn and look directly at my shuttered window.

She couldn't have seen me. The gap was two fingers wide and I was standing back in the dark.

She looked anyway. Then she climbed my steps and knocked again, the same two raps and a scrape, and said, conversationally, to the door:

"I'm coming in. Put the kettle on or don't, but don't point anything at me."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Brann Oduya before that morning: she ran a ferry across the Vellwater, the slow flat channel that loops the city's southern edge like a moat nobody remembers building. She'd lived next door eleven years. We exchanged weather. Once, when my roof leaked, she appeared on it with tar and a hammer before I'd finished cursing at the ceiling, fixed it, refused payment, and said, "You owe me a favor, and I collect favors slow, so don't die." I had filed this under neighborliness and thought no more about it. This is what I do with kindnesses. I file them. Filing is safer than feeling.

Here is what I learned about her between half past nine and ten o'clock, sitting in my own kitchen while she drank my tea and looked around my shop with eyes that missed nothing, which is the worst quality a pair of eyes can have when you are hiding a corpse and a blood-inked map:

She noticed the flour tin.

She didn't say anything about it. She just looked at it a beat too long — the tin on the high shelf, the one I'd wedged shut with a splint of pine because the map inside it had been doing something all night, a faint pressure against the lid, like a heartbeat, like a tide deciding — and then she looked at me, and then she looked at the sheeted shape on the worktable in the back room, visible through the doorway, unmistakably the length and breadth of a dead man.

"You've got a body," she said.

"I've got a situation."

"It's a body, Silas."

"It's a *situation* that currently resembles a body, yes."

She set down her cup with the deliberate care of a woman deciding not to throw it. "Two nights ago. Ferry Street. I found the blood myself, mopped the boards before the Watch came, because blood on a ferry dock is bad for business and worse for ghosts. Big man, foreign, throat opened clean. Professional work." She said it evenly, but her thumb was moving along the rim of her cup, small circles, the way hands move when they're holding something down. "I've been asking quiet questions since. You want to know what I found out? Nothing. Nobody saw anything. Nobody knows anything. In a district where everybody knows everything." Her eyes came up. "That's not normal, Silas. That's *arranged*. Somebody arranged this city's forgetting, and somebody else bled out on my dock, and this morning the Wardens came asking questions with answers already written, and you—" she gestured at the sheeted shape, at the flour tin, at all of me, "—are standing in the middle of it looking like a man reading his own arrest warrant."

"I'm a charm-seller," I said. "I sell tin birds."

"You sell tin birds that work," she said, "and we both know it, and I've known it for eleven years, and I never once said a word, and you can sit there and decide right now whether that was kindness or patience."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, gulls argued over the harbor's leavings. My heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing.

"Patience," I said finally.

"Mm." She nodded slowly. "Eleven years of patience. Do you know why?"

I said I didn't.

"Because my brother disappeared," Brann said, "nineteen years ago, and the last place anyone admitted seeing him was a charm-shop in the Tanners' Quarter, run by a woman who swore up and down she sold nothing but tin and thread, and the Wardens burned the shop and the woman both inside a week, and nobody remembered her name by spring. Not even the people who'd bought from her. The city forgot her *on schedule*. And I promised myself that if I ever lived next to another one — another quiet person selling quiet miracles — I would keep my mouth shut and my eyes open, and when the day came that the wagons rolled, I'd be on the step." She drank her tea. "So. Here I am. On the step."

I want to be able to tell you that I said something gracious. What I actually said was: "Your brother. What was his name?"

"Tomas."

"And the Wardens—"

"Took the record. All of it. There's a building on Candler's Row where they keep what the city forgets, and you can't read it, you can't buy it, you can't even stand near it without your thoughts going smooth." Her jaw tightened. "Nineteen years, and I don't even know if he's in there. A name in a vault. Maybe just a smudge where a name used to be."

*A building where they keep what the city forgets.*

I sat very still, because pieces were moving in my head, old pieces, fifteen years old, and they were clicking together in an order I did not like. When I had helped write the Accords — when I had been young and certain and Realmwright enough to believe that law could hold magic like a jar holds fire — I had drafted clauses. Containment. Sequestration. *Records of proscribed practice to be held in trust by the Office of the Peaceful Record.* I had written those words. I had written them believing they meant ledgers and licenses. I had not imagined a vault full of erased brothers. Or perhaps I had, distantly, in the way you imagine every terrible thing your law might become, and I had signed anyway, because I was twenty-six and certain, and then I had run, because running was easier than watching.

"Ferry Street," I said. "The man who died. Did anyone claim him?"

"Not yet. No papers. Boat's missing, or was never his." She paused. "Except—" and here she hesitated, and I learned that Brann Oduya hesitating was more alarming than most people shouting, "—the night-watchman on the south stairs says he saw a girl. Young. Dark-haired. Standing at the rail where the blood was, two nights back, just standing, looking at the water. Wouldn't answer him. Gone when he came back around."

A girl. At the site of the murder. Looking at the water.

I thought of the map in the flour tin, the pressure of it against the lid, patient as water. I thought of the handwriting in its margins — my own childhood hand, looping and precise, the hand I'd had before I taught myself to write like a clerk, before I sanded myself down into someone unremarkable. Someone had kept that map. Someone had known what I was. Someone had carried it across whatever distance separates dying strangers from charm-shops in Vell, and pressed it into my hands, and said *finish it*, and died with my name — my real name, the one I hadn't spoken aloud in fifteen years — presumably somewhere in the folds.

And now a girl stood where he fell, looking at the water, and wouldn't speak.

"The Wardens will come back," Brann said. "Sergeant Closed-Ledger writes everything down, and what he writes goes to Candler's Row, and what goes to Candler's Row gets *kept*. You understand? Every statement I made this morning is now a record. 'Nobody strange sleeps on Saltrow Lane.' If they find out that's false, that's not just suspicion, that's a *false statement to the Peaceful Record*, and in Vell that's a door that locks from the outside." She stood, buttoning her coat. "So here's what happens now. The body leaves tonight. My boat, the low tide run, out past the breakwater. I know deep water. Don't argue — arguing wastes tide. The map stays with you or comes with me, your choice, but if it stays, it goes somewhere better than a flour tin, because tins get found, Silas. Tins get found by everyone eventually. That's practically their purpose."

"Why?" I said. It came out rougher than I meant. "Why are you doing this? You met me properly ten minutes ago. Eleven years of weather-talk and one roof, and you're offering to ferry a corpse."

Brann stopped at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at me, and for a moment all the salt and iron in her face shifted and I saw the thing underneath, which was simply a person who had waited nineteen years for someone to be on *her* step, and had decided, in the absence of anyone arriving, to become the person who arrives.

"Because somebody should have done it for Tomas," she said. "And because you looked at me through that shutter like a man deciding whether to hope, and I know that look. I wore it for years." She pulled the door open. "Tide turns at moonrise. Have him ready."

Then she was gone, down the steps and into the lane, and I stood in my kitchen holding a cooling kettle and understanding, with the slow vertiginous clarity of a man on a dock watching his boat untie itself, that my fifteen years of careful anonymity had just acquired a witness. Not a betrayer. Worse. A *believer*.

* * *

I did not have him ready by moonrise. I want to be honest about that, because the version of this story where I am competent is a lie I tell in shops.

What happened was this: I went to the flour tin at dusk to move the map, as agreed, and the moment my fingers closed on the tin the pressure inside changed. Not stronger. *Directed.* All night it had pushed like a sleeping animal shifting; now it pushed like a compass needle finding north, and north was me. I set the tin on the table and it rocked, gently, deliberately, until the seam faced my chest.

"Don't," I told it. Out loud. To a tin.

I opened it — because I am weak, and because part of me had been waiting all day for an excuse — and the map lay in its folds, and the blood-ink had moved. I am a cartographer; I know what ink does. Ink dries. Ink fades. Ink does not *travel*, creeping overnight along the fibers of the paper like frost along a window, redrawing coastlines that had been blank when the stranger pressed it into my hands. New lines. Fine ones. And in the margin, in that looping childhood hand that was mine and not mine, fresh words had surfaced like something rising through dark water:

*Saltrow. She watches. Let her.*

I read it four times. *Let her.* As if the map had been in the room that morning. As if the map had approved of Brann Oduya the way a current approves of a boat.

And underneath, smaller, newer, the ink still wet enough to shine:

*Tide turns at moonrise. So do we.*

I wrapped the map in oilcloth with hands that would not quite steady, and I did not put it back in the tin. I put it inside my shirt, against my skin, where I could feel it — faint, patient, insistent — pulling. Not hard. Not yet. The way a tide pulls at the ankles of someone standing just deep enough to pretend they're still on the shore.

At moonrise, Brann's knock came — two raps and a scrape — and together, without much talk, because talk would have made it real and neither of us could afford real, we carried the stranger down my back stairs and along the water-stair to her boat, and the river took him the way rivers take everything, without comment. Brann said a few words over the side. I didn't catch them. They weren't for me.

We walked back up the lane together, and at the boundary between our two doorsteps — the exact stone where eleven years of weather-talk had lived — she stopped.

"That girl," she said. "The one on the south stairs. Watch-woman says she's been back every night since. Same spot. Doesn't beg, doesn't speak, doesn't leave till dawn." Brann's eyes were on the dark line of the harbor. "Tonight I'm going to go stand near her. Not ask. Just stand. Sometimes that's how you get answers — you make yourself available to them." She glanced at me. "You could come. Or you could lock your door and go back to being furniture. Your call, cartographer."

She used the word lightly. Casually. A word tossed like a fish to a gull.

I had never told her I was a cartographer.

The map against my chest pulsed once, warm as a struck match, and far away — or not far away at all, possibly directly beneath the flagstones of Saltrow Lane — something vast and patient turned over in its sleep, and began, very slowly, to pull.

"I'll come," I said.

Of course I said I'll come. The tide had already decided.
