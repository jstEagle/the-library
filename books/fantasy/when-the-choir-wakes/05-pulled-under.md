## 5. Pulled Under

I did not sleep. I want that on the record, in some ledger somewhere, because everything that happened afterward has been blamed on my judgment, and a man who hasn't slept deserves at least one line of defense.

Brann left near midnight. She stood in my doorway with her collar up against the drizzle and said, "Dawn, then. The east stair. If you're not there, I'll assume you've come to your senses, and I'll be quietly disappointed in you for it."

"Disappointed?"

"You strike me as a man who's spent fifteen years being sensible," she said. "It'd be a shame to break the streak over a dead stranger's laundry list."

Then she was gone, boots ringing on the cobbles of Saltrow Lane, and I was alone with the map.

I should describe what I did with those hours, because it will matter later — how I laid it out on my worktable under three lamps, weighted at the corners with charm-stones, and told myself I was only *studying* it. That's the cartographer's oldest lie. We don't study maps. Maps study us. Any map worth its ink is a mirror with geography drawn on top, and this one had been showing me my own handwriting since the moment I unrolled it: the cramped rightward slant of a boy who learned his letters from a mother who wrote with her left hand and made him copy her anyway.

*Finish it,* the stranger had said. And in the margin, in my own hand, aged fifteen years but unmistakably mine:

*The fold opens where the water forgets its name.*

I hadn't written that. I would have remembered writing that. But it was mine — the way a scar is yours even if you can't recall the fall.

So I sat with the lamps burning low and the rain ticking at the shutters, and I did what any sensible man does when he suspects he's already caught: I packed a bag. Rope, flint, dried fish, the good knife, a spare shirt, and — because I am apparently incapable of learning — six charms I had no intention of selling to anyone. Luck, warding, quiet feet, true north, warm hands, and one I'd never been able to classify, bought off a dying hedge-witch in another life, which she'd called *a door that remembers being open.*

I wrapped them all in oilcloth like contraband, which is exactly what they were.

And then, an hour before dawn, the tide came in.

* * *

It started as a sound. Or rather, as the absence of one — the rain against the shutters stopped mid-drop, each bead of water hanging in the air outside my window like beads on a string someone had pinched closed. The lamp flames stood up straight and still, as if listening.

The map lifted off the table.

Not dramatically. Not with thunder. It rose the way dust rises when you smooth a blanket — gently, inevitably — and hung there in the lamplight, and every line on it began to move. Rivers crawled. Coastlines breathed. In the margins, my stolen handwriting darkened from brown-black to a red so deep it was nearly sound.

I grabbed for it. I want to say I hesitated, that some wiser Silas stood at the edge of that moment weighing his choices, but the truth is my hand shot out before my fear finished loading, because fifteen years of hiding is also fifteen years of wanting, and wanting moves faster than fear.

The paper met my palm and *knew* me.

That is the only word. It knew me the way a lock knows its key, the way a wound knows salt. Something in the ink recognized something in my blood, and the recognition went through me like cold water down a well — and then the room folded.

I don't have better language for it. I'm a cartographer; folding space is supposed to be my trade, and I have never once found honest words for what it feels like from the inside. Imagine a page being turned, except you are a word on it. Imagine the crease passing through you. My shop — the counter, the jars of luck, the false bottom where I kept my real self — all of it swung away like a door closing seen from very far off, and I had time for exactly one coherent thought, which was: *the latch, I didn't lock the latch, Brann will think—*

And then: *Brann.*

Because there was a second weight in the fold with me. A fist knotted in my coat collar, hauling backward with the strength of a woman who pulls boats for a living, and a voice shouting words that arrived shredded, syllables strewn behind us like cargo lost overboard:

"—told you — dawn — you absolute — *don't you dare let go of me—*"

"I'm not letting go!" I shouted back, though whether she heard it or merely felt the shape of it through the fold, I couldn't say. Her grip said enough. She wasn't holding on to me. She was holding on to the world she knew, and I happened to be the last piece of it within reach.

The crease passed through us both.

* * *

Falling, if you can call it that, lasted somewhere between a heartbeat and a season. There was no up. There was the sensation of being read — of passing across some vast attention the way a finger passes down a column of figures — and everywhere around us, in the black between folds, other pages. Other maps. I saw them the way you see things in the instant before waking: a coastline stitched in gold thread, a mountain range that was also a spine, a sea rendered entirely in small careful handwriting, wave after wave after wave, each one named.

Every mapmaker dreams of the place where the master copies are kept. Every mapmaker also knows it isn't real.

I stopped dreaming about it fifteen years ago. I remember being embarrassed about that, even mid-fall.

Then the fold spat us out, and we landed — actually landed, knees and palms and dignity scattered across wet grass — under a sky I did not know.

* * *

Here is what I noticed first, and I offer it in the order my senses offered it to me, because the order matters.

First: silence. Total, physical silence, the kind Vell only manages inside Warden-sealed vaults. But it wasn't empty silence. It had texture. Weight. It lay on the skin like snowfall.

Second: the light. Dawn-colored, but coming from everywhere and nowhere, a grey-gold suffusion with no sun in it, as though the sky itself were a lamp behind paper.

Third: the grass. It was moving. Not with wind — there was no wind — but rippling outward from where we'd landed in slow concentric rings, the way water rings when a stone goes in. As if our arrival were still traveling through the ground.

Fourth, and last, and worst: the sound of our own breathing. It echoed. Not off walls — there were no walls, just that pale endless sky and the rolling dark-green country below us, hills folded into valleys folded into distant blue mountains — but off the *air itself*, as if the atmosphere here were a struck bell that hadn't yet decided to stop ringing.

"Silas." Brann's voice came out flat and strange, stripped of its harbor-side rasp. She was on her knees in the grass, one hand pressed flat to the ground as if checking whether the world owed her money. "Silas, tell me what this is."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Cael Sorrow," I said, and the name left my lips and rolled away downhill, audible long past the point where it should have faded, growing softer and softer but never quite gone, until minutes later I could still hear the ghost of my own voice saying *sorrow, sorrow, sorrow* out over the valleys like a bell tolling for a drowned town.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have." I got up. My legs reported in late and unreliable. "There are stories. Hedge-witch stories, smuggler stories, the kind of thing you hear at three in the morning from people who've had too much to drink and too little to lose. A realm next to ours. Folded against it. Where sound is—" I gestured helplessly at the air, at the lingering ghost of my own voice. "Where sound is *real*. Where it has weight. Substance. Where a word spoken carelessly can outlive the speaker by a hundred years."

Brann looked at me the way she'd looked at the map: sharp, measuring, deciding how much of me to believe.

"And you believed them."

"I *made* some of them up," I admitted. "To sell. *Tales of the Folded Realm, two coppers.* People love a world they can't get to. It's the same reason they buy luck charms." I looked down at my hands. They were shaking, and not from the fall. "I never thought — you don't write a story and expect it to write back."

But it had. Someone had taken my childhood craft, my forbidden blood-ink, and written a door into the world I'd invented to sell to drunkards — or perhaps I had invented nothing at all, perhaps the stories were memories leaking through the fold, perhaps Cael Sorrow had been humming itself into my imagination for years the way a neighbor's music comes through a wall. I genuinely could not tell anymore. That frightened me more than the falling had.

"Look," Brann said.

She was pointing at the sky. At first I thought she meant the sourceless light, but no — she meant the *snow*.

It was falling. Fine and slow and grey-white, drifting down out of that pale luminous nothing, and it was not snow. I caught a flake on my sleeve and leaned close, and it was a fleck of — hush. That's the only word I have. A little curl of solidified quiet, delicate as ash, melting into the wool without wetness. All around us it settled on the grass, on the stones, on the shoulders of the hills, and wherever it landed, the land grew *quieter*, visibly quieter, the way frost kills a garden leaf by leaf.

"It falls in the silent places," I heard myself say. "Or — no. It makes them." I crouched and touched the ground. Under my palm, faint as a pulse through a wrist, I felt it: a hum. Deep, vast, patient. The whole country was *humming*, some note too low for ears, held the way a singer holds a breath. "Something's keeping this place alive with sound. And the silence is trying to take it back."

"Poetic," Brann said. "Useless, but poetic." She stood, brushed grey hush-flakes off her knees, and turned a slow circle, taking inventory the way a ferrywoman takes inventory — current, cargo, weather, exits. "No road. No river. No smoke from any chimney that I can see. And a dead man's map that throws people across worlds." She fixed me with a look that would have stripped barnacles. "You're going to tell me you know what happens next."

I took out the map.

It had changed. Of course it had changed. The coastlines had stilled, the rivers slept, but new ink had surfaced while we fell — or perhaps it had always been there, waiting for the right light, the way words appear on paper held to a flame. A route now ran across the western hills in that deep blood-red, fine as a hair, stitching from the hollow where we'd landed toward a mark on the far side of the mountains. Beside the mark, in my own fifteen-year-old handwriting:

*Amberglass. Where the binding was written. Bring the chorus.*

Bring the chorus. I read it three times. There was no chorus. There was me, a ferrywoman who hated me slightly more with each passing minute, and whatever waited in the country below.

"Well?" Brann said.

"There's a destination," I said. "Across the mountains. Amberglass." I hesitated on the next part, then decided lying to her was a luxury I could no longer afford. "It says to bring a chorus."

"A chorus." She said it the way she might say *a second moon*. "Of what? We haven't got a choir, Silas. We haven't got a *village*. We haven't got anything but you, me, and a piece of paper with opinions."

As if summoned — and in this country, I was beginning to suspect, things were summoned constantly, carelessly, by every stray word — a sound came up out of the valleys below.

Singing. Distant, many-voiced, threaded together like rope: dozens of voices humming a single sustained chord, rising and falling with the contours of the land, and as I listened I understood that it wasn't performance. It was maintenance. The singers were holding their world together the way a crew bails a boat — steadily, wearily, without pause — and the grey hush-snow fell thickest wherever the song faltered.

Brann and I stood shoulder to shoulder on that nameless hill, two smuggled objects from a world where magic was a crime, and listened to an entire countryside sing itself into continuing.

"We should go down," I said.

"Should we?" Brann asked. But she was already walking.

* * *

We followed the red thread of the route downhill through grass that whispered back our footsteps half a beat late, as if the ground were learning our rhythm and rehearsing it. The hush-snow thickened in the hollows. Twice we crossed patches where it lay deep and undisturbed, whole fields of fallen silence, and in those places our voices died an arm's length from our lips — I watched Brann shout and see the shout simply stop, a visible ripple of sound that hit the white drift and sank into it like a stone into a pond. After the second field we walked through them quickly and touching shoulders, and neither of us remarked on it, because remarking on it would have required speech, and speech felt suddenly precious.

Near the bottom of the valley, the singing resolved into a village.

It sat beside a river that ran silver and slow, maybe forty houses with roofs the color of slate and doors painted every color doors can be painted, and from every house, every window, every doorway, the humming poured out and braided together over the rooftops into one great patient chord. People worked as they sang — a woman mending a net, a man splitting wood, children chasing a dog between the gardens — all of them humming, all of them *holding*, and none of them stopping, not for breath, not for greeting, not for anything.

They saw us from a long way off. Word traveled down the chord faster than we walked — I could feel it pass through the harmony, a ripple of altered notes, curiosity moving through the village like wind through wheat — and by the time we reached the first garden gate, the entire population had turned to look at us without a single one of them ceasing to sing.

A small figure detached from the rest and came toward us along the fence line. A child — a girl, eight or nine perhaps, dark-haired, gap-toothed, with the ferociously direct stare of someone who has never once been taught to be afraid of strangers. She stopped an arm's length away, tilted her head, and studied us with the frank appraisal of a buyer at a fish market.

Then she spoke — sang, rather, pitching her words neatly into the village chord as if slotting a coin into a groove:

"You fell out of the sky."

"We did," I said.

"Everyone falls eventually," she said, with the serene authority of the very young. "Most people just do it slower." She looked me up and down, and then her eyes went to the satchel where the map rode against my ribs, and something flickered across her face — recognition, or hunger, or grief; at that age all three wear the same expression. "You've got paper that hums," she said. "I can hear it from here. It's humming *your* name."

Behind me, Brann went very still.

The girl stuck out her hand with the brisk formality of a harbormaster.

"I'm Wren," she said. "You'd better come meet Elder Sef. She's been waiting for a humming paper since before I was born." She paused, and her gap-toothed grin turned, for just a moment, into something older and sadder than her face should have been able to hold.

"She says you're late."
