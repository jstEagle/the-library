## 6. A Country Made of Song

I want it on the record that I did not scream when we fell through the world. I made a sound, yes — a short, dignified noise, like a gull being stepped on — but screaming implies a loss of control I refuse to admit to.

Silas screamed. Properly. Both hands over their mouth afterward, as if they could stuff it back in.

We landed — and I use *landed* generously; it was more a question of where the ground decided to catch us — in a field of grass that hummed.

Not rustled. Hummed. A low, steady note underfoot, like the whole meadow was a struck bell that had forgotten to stop ringing. When I sat up, my palms pressed flat against the earth, the note climbed into my bones and set my teeth buzzing.

"Tell me you felt that," Silas said. They were on their knees, patting the ground like a man checking whether his horse was still breathing.

"I felt it. I don't like it."

"Good," they said faintly. "Neither do I. That means we're both sane."

I took stock, because taking stock is what I do instead of panicking. Sky: wrong. It was the color of deep water at dusk, violet shading to green near the horizon, with no sun anywhere and yet light coming from everywhere, soft and sourceless, like the world had been lit from inside its own skin. Hills: rolling, gentle, dotted with trees whose leaves shivered without wind. Air: sweet, faintly metallic, the taste of a coin held in the mouth. Companions: one terrified mapmaker, one ferrywoman with wet knees and a rapidly souring temper.

And the humming. Everywhere. Not just the grass. The air itself carried a tone so constant I'd already stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat until someone reminds you it's there.

"Where are we?" I asked.

Silas didn't answer right away. They'd pulled the map from their coat — the dead stranger's map, the blood-inked thing I'd told them to burn, twice, and been ignored, twice — and were staring at it with an expression I can only describe as *homesick and horrified at the same time*.

"Cael Sorrow," they said.

"You know this place?"

"No." Their thumb moved along the map's edge, tracing a line of ink that seemed, I swear on my ferry license, to move under their touch. "But the map does."

Wonderful. We'd been kidnapped by stationery with opinions.

* * *

We walked because standing still felt worse. The hills rolled toward a valley where smoke rose from chimneys — real smoke, ordinary smoke, the first comforting thing I'd seen since the floor of Silas's shop had turned into a mouth and swallowed us.

The village appeared around a bend: two dozen houses of pale timber and turf roofs, a well, a lane, laundry strung between doors. Ordinary, except for the sound.

They were all humming. Every soul in the street. An old man splitting wood hummed between strokes. Two women hauling water hummed in harmony, one low, one high, weaving around each other like rope. Children running past hummed without breaking stride. Nobody sang words. Just tones, sustained, unbroken, each person carrying their own note the way you or I might carry a bundle — carefully, constantly, like putting it down would cost them something.

"They're holding something," Silas whispered.

"What do you mean, holding?"

"Their shapes." They said it the way you'd say *the roof is leaking*, matter-of-fact and dreadful. "Look at the edges of things here. Look at the fence posts."

I looked. The fence posts nearest us were slightly — soft. Not rotten. Soft, the way a candle flame is soft, their outlines wavering if I stared too long. And when the old woodcutter paused too long between strokes — three breaths, maybe four — his outline wavered too. His shoulders went vague as smoke. Then he caught his note again, hummed it back up from somewhere deep, and snapped into focus like a picture settling.

I have ferried drunks, smugglers, one escaped murderer, and a woman who insisted she was her own twin sister. I thought I'd seen everything a body could see. I stood in that lane and watched a man hold himself together with a tune, and I understood that everything I'd seen before was nothing at all.

"Don't stop," Silas breathed. "Whatever happens — don't let yourself go quiet. Keep making noise. Talk, hum, sing, I don't care. Just don't be silent."

"Why?"

"Because I think silence is heavier here than it is at home. I think it *lands* on you."

A door opened. A woman stepped out, flour to her elbows, and saw us — two strangers, mute with shock, standing in her lane like a pair of dropped parcels.

Her note faltered.

So did mine. Because hers faltered, and hearing it falter made something in my chest lurch, and for one horrible second I felt my own edges go strange — a coldness at the fingertips, a sense of being *less outlined* than before —

Then she recovered, sweeping her hum back up, and gestured sharply at us, urgent, mouthing something.

"Hum," Silas translated, already doing it — badly, a wavering drone like a wasp in a jar. "She's saying hum. *Hum.*"

I hummed. I am not musical. My humming sounded like a barge engine with grievances. But the coldness left my fingers, and the woman's face eased, and she waved us toward her door with the brisk authority of someone who has fed lost travelers before and expects to again.

Later, over bread that tasted faintly of honey and static, she told us her name was Maren, and her daughter translated, because Maren spoke little and mostly in notes anyway. Her daughter was called Pell, age maybe nineteen, with her mother's broad hands and a look in her eyes like someone who'd stopped being surprised by anything some years ago and missed it.

"You came through a fold," Pell said. "Through the thin place. People do, sometimes. Rarely. Mostly they don't survive the falling."

"We nearly didn't," I said.

"Most don't." She said it without drama, refilling our cups. "You're lucky. Or marked. Hard to say which, here."

"And the humming?" Silas asked. "Why does everyone—"

Pell looked at her mother. Maren hummed a low phrase, sad and round, and Pell listened to it the way you'd listen to a long story.

"Because of the Cantor," Pell said. "Before him, we sang freely. Songs with words. Songs about weather and love and grudges. Now—" she shrugged, a gesture that contained a war's worth of grief, "—now any song with words belongs to him. He hears them. So we hold ourselves with wordless notes instead. A hum isn't a song. A hum is just... staying."

"Staying what?" I asked.

"Staying *here*." She tapped her own sternum. "You've seen what happens when someone goes quiet too long."

I had. I'd watched an old man almost come apart like wet paper.

"How long," Silas said quietly, "before quiet takes you? Completely?"

Pell's answer was a pause. The pause was the answer.

Maren touched my arm then — my arm specifically, though she'd had a whole table of strangers to choose from — and hummed a rising phrase, questioning, and pointed at Silas, then at the map poking out of their coat, then out the window toward the hills eastward.

"She wants to know if you're the ones the map called," Pell translated reluctantly.

Silas went very still. "Called?"

"There's a story," Pell said. "Older than me. That a map would come to Cael Sorrow someday, carried by a stranger with ink in their blood, and that the map would walk them across the country to—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "It's a children's story. Hope-stories. We tell them so the young ones don't lie down and go quiet."

"What happens in the story?" Silas asked.

"The king wakes up," Pell said simply. "And the singing comes back."

* * *

We stayed the night in Maren's house, because night here is a strange animal — the violet sky darkens but never fully, and a hush rolls across the land like fog, and in that hush every household hums louder, together, a whole village braided into one chord to keep the dark from pressing in.

I lay on a pallet by the fire, listening to that chord, and I will confess something I told no one: it was the safest I had felt in years. Fifteen years I'd lived beside Silas Almeida — neighborly, wary, keeping my own counsel the way harbor folk do — and never once had I heard a whole street make a single sound together. In Vell we kept our voices down because voices drew Wardens. Here they raised theirs because silence was the Warden.

Different jailers. Same cell.

I was still chewing on that when I noticed the child.

She was sitting cross-legged at the foot of the stairs, watching us. Small — seven, eight years old. Brown-skinned, hair cropped close, wearing a coat clearly cut for someone twice her size with the sleeves rolled six times. She wasn't humming. That was the first alarming thing. Every other soul in the house was holding their note even in sleep, a soft unconscious drone, but this child sat in perfect silence and looked entirely, insolently fine.

The second alarming thing was that she was looking at Silas the way pilgrims look at relics.

"You're the ink-blood," she said.

Silas startled awake. "I'm the what?"

"Ink-blood. From the story." She scooted closer on her knees, utterly without fear, which in my experience is either sainthood or a warning sign. "The map-carrier. Pell told the story at hearth-night and I remembered every word. You have ink in your blood and the map chose you and you're going to wake the king."

"That's a story," Silas said weakly.

"So are you, then." She said it with devastating simplicity. "Stories get true. That's how it works here. Things that get sung enough become real. That's why the Cantor sings so loud."

I sat up. "Who are you, little one? Where's your mother?"

"Wren." She said it like a bird name, which I suppose it was. "My mother's gone. Gone-quiet. Three winters ago." No tears. Just the flat recitation of a child who had done her grieving early and thoroughly. "I don't hum much anymore. Everyone says I should. But quiet doesn't take me. I've tried. I sat in the silent hollow behind the mill for a whole afternoon once, just to see, and nothing happened except I got cold." She looked at Silas with fierce, terrible hope. "Pell says people like me used to exist before the Cantor. People who could be quiet and stay themselves. She says that's why the story needs an ink-blood. Because the king can only be woken by someone who knows both — the song and the silence."

Silas looked at me over the child's head. Their face did something complicated — the look of a person who has just realized the exit they'd been walking toward is actually another trapdoor.

"Wren," they said gently. "We're not staying. We're going to find the way home. That's all."

"The map doesn't go home," Wren said. "Maps go forward. That's what makes them maps."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I decided immediately that I liked her, which was inconvenient, because liking things in my experience is how you end up responsible for them.

"Go to sleep, sprout," I said. "It's hearth-dark. Even stories rest."

She gave me a look of pure ferrywoman-grade skepticism — honestly, she could have worked my route — but she lay down by the fire, curled small in her enormous coat, and within minutes she was asleep. Truly asleep. Silent. And nothing happened to her. Her edges stayed crisp as cut paper while the whole room hummed around her.

I lay awake a long time after that, watching her breathe, and feeling something settle in my chest that I recognized from long ago and had hoped never to feel again, because it always cost me.

It was the feeling of deciding someone was mine to look after.

* * *

In the morning, Maren fed us again and packed us food we hadn't asked for — hard bread, dried fruit, a twist of salt — pressing the bundle into my arms rather than Silas's, having apparently decided I was the reliable one. Flattering. Accurate.

Pell walked us to the edge of the village, where the lane climbed toward the eastern hills.

"The drowned archive is that way," she said. "Amberglass. If the map's pulling you, that's where it'll pull. Everyone knows the story-way goes through Amberglass." She hesitated. "There are places on that road where the Cantor's listeners sit. Hush-wards. If you pass one, whatever you do, don't speak above a whisper, and don't stop humming till you're past."

"Noted," I said. "And thank you. For the bread, the beds, the warnings."

Pell shrugged. "You're the story-people. If you wake the king, my mother gets her songs back." Her voice cracked, just once, on *songs*. She covered it fast. "Besides, you've got Wren to look after now."

"We haven't got—" I started.

At which point Wren stepped out from behind Maren's rain barrel, where she had been hiding with all the skill of a cat behind a curtain, wearing a pack roughly the size of her torso and an expression of iron resolve.

"I'm coming," she announced. "I know the road to Amberglass. I know where the hush-wards sit. I know which berries lie about being ripe. And I don't hum, so I can walk quiet, and quiet walks faster."

"No," said Silas.

"Yes," said Wren.

"Absolutely not," I said, "you are a *child*—"

"I'm the fastest thing in this village and the only one who isn't scared of the hollows." She planted her feet. "Also, the story says the ink-blood travels with a keeper and a singer and a boatman. You're the keeper." She pointed at me, then at Silas. "That's the ink-blood. We just need the boatman and the singer, and I bet we find them on the road, because that's how stories work."

"I am not a character in a bedtime tale," I said with as much dignity as a woman can manage while arguing with someone who comes up to her elbow.

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "You're worse. You're real."

Behind me, Silas made a sound that was suspiciously close to a laugh, quickly disguised as a cough.

I looked at the child. I looked at the eastern hills, where a drowned archive waited at the bottom of somebody's bad idea. I looked at Silas, who had gotten us into this and who owed me, by my current accounting, approximately one lifetime of favors plus interest.

"If she trips," I told Silas, "she rides on your shoulders. If she cries, you fix it. If she starves, I'm feeding her from your share. And if anything with more teeth than a fox comes down that road, you stand between it and her, mapmaker, or so help me I'll fold you back through that thin place myself."

Silas held up their hands in surrender. "Agreed. All of it."

Wren grinned like sunrise and started up the hill without waiting for us, because of course she did.

I followed, because that was becoming a pattern in my life — following smaller, braver creatures into trouble — and behind us Maren began to hum. Not a farewell. Something older. Pell had told me the night before that the villages had a walking-song, forbidden now like everything else with words, but the hum of it remained, passed mother to daughter, and mothers sang it when their children left for the road so that the road would remember them.

I didn't understand the words. There weren't any. But halfway up the hill I found I was humming it too, my barge-engine drone fitting clumsily into Maren's melody, and Wren glanced back at me with an expression far too old for her face, and said:

"That's the one. That's the song the choir wakes to. You'll see."

Ahead, the hills rolled east toward water I couldn't yet see, and somewhere beneath that water, a library waited with our names — or at least Silas's — already written in it.

I hummed louder. It was that kind of morning.
