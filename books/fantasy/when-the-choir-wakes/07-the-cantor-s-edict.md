## 7. The Cantor's Edict

I've been on the water long enough to know that weather doesn't warn you honestly. It gives you a pretty sky and a light wind, and then it drowns you. So when I woke in Elder Maren's guest loft to birdsong — actual birdsong, sweet and unhurried, like nothing in this realm had ever gone wrong — I didn't trust it for a second.

"Silas," I said, elbowing the lump of blankets beside me. "Up."

The lump made a sound like a hinge that had been oiled with regret.

"Birds are singing."

"So?"

"So nothing sings for free here. You told me that yourself. Everything hums because they have to." I sat up. The loft window looked out over Thistlefen's low rooftops, all of them steaming gently in the morning damp, and beyond them the fen itself, silver and flat as a coin someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up. "Birds singing like that means either the world's ended or it's about to."

Silas surfaced, hair doing something architecturally impossible, and squinted at me with the expression of a man who had been hoping to be left alone by the universe for at least another hour. Fifteen years of hiding will do that to a face. It carves grooves where the flinching goes.

"You're cheerful this morning," they croaked.

"I'm not cheerful. I'm correct. There's a difference."

Downstairs, Maren was already up, which didn't surprise me. Elders sleep like cats — lightly, and with one ear open for trouble. She had bread out and tea that smelled like pine needles and honey, and she was standing at her door looking east with her arms folded, which is the universal posture of women who have seen something coming.

"There's a herald on the road from Corvenne," she said without turning around. "White cloak. Silver bell. Been walking since before dawn, and the whole fen's gone quiet around him. Not silent-quiet. Listening-quiet."

Silas set down their cup so carefully it might have been made of eggshell. "The Cantor's sending edicts now."

"The Cantor's been sending edicts for years," Maren said. "This one's different. My granddaughter heard the last one read in Low Hum. Before, the law was: sing sanctioned songs, at sanctioned hours, in sanctioned company." Her jaw did something tight. "The girl says this one's shorter."

* * *

The whole village gathered in the square by midmorning, because when a man in a white cloak rings a silver bell, you come look. That's not cowardice or curiosity. That's just what bells are *for*. They're an argument your body loses before your mind gets a vote.

I stood at the back with Silas and Wren, and I'll tell you now that keeping Wren at the back was like anchoring a boat with a ribbon. The child vibrated. She'd taken to us with the terrifying total commitment of the young, and no amount of my scowling had dislodged her. She held my hand, technically. In practice, I was holding hers down.

The herald was younger than his cloak. That was the first thing I noticed — a soft-faced boy, maybe twenty, with the hollowed eyes of someone who'd been made to memorize things he didn't understand. He unrolled a scroll with hands that shook only slightly, and when he spoke, his voice came out strange. Not his voice. Polished. Like a stone worn smooth by other people's tongues.

"By decree of the Cantor, Voice of the Realm, Keeper of the King's Peace—" he paused there, and I saw his throat work, "—all song is hereby gathered into the One Song. All unsanctioned melody, humming, whistle, chant, and lullaby is forbidden. What is sung apart is sung against. What is sung against will be answered."

He said it flat, the way you recite something you've been made to say so many times the meaning has rotted out of it. But the village heard every word. I watched them hear it. Old men whose hands stilled on their canes. Mothers glancing at their children the way you glance at a candle in a draft.

Because here's the thing about Cael Sorrow that took me three days to understand and will take you one sentence: these people hum to hold themselves together. Literally. Their shapes, their memories, their places in the world — all of it is stitched with sound. A fisherman hums his own name into the water so the water remembers him. A grandmother hums her house warm. Take away their song and you don't punish them. You *unmake* them, thread by thread.

Elder Maren knew it too. She stepped forward out of the crowd with the unhurried gait of a woman who has buried a husband, two floods, and most of her patience, and she said, clear as a bell herself:

"And who decides what's sanctioned?"

The herald's eyes flicked up. For half a heartbeat he was just a tired boy on a long road. Then the polish came back down over him like a lid.

"The Cantor decides."

"The Cantor isn't from here." Maren's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It got *lower*. "No one elected it. No one crowned it. It wears our king's crown and sings through our king's throat, and now it tells grandmothers they may not hum their grandchildren to sleep. I've lived seventy-one years in this fen. I have sung the same rising-song every dawn since I could stand. I will sing it tomorrow."

"Maren—" Silas breathed beside me, and I felt them start to move forward, and I caught their arm, because some part of me already knew how this went. Knew it the way you know a river's got a hole in the bottom before you see the whirlpool. Some knowledge arrives in the body first.

The herald sighed. And I want to be fair to that boy, if he's still alive somewhere, because what he did next he did gently. He reached into his cloak and drew out a small silver tuning fork, and he said, almost kindly:

"Elder. Please don't make me do this."

"Then don't," Maren said.

He struck the fork against his palm.

I have heard silence before. Real silence — a becalmed sea, a snowed-in night, the inside of grief. This wasn't that. This was silence with *intent*. It came off the tuning fork like cold water off an oar, a ring of pure quiet that spread across the square, and where it touched, sound died. Not faded. Died. The fen went mute. The wind went mute. And Maren—

Maren was mid-breath, drawing air to sing her rising-song, and the silence took the song out of her before it ever left her lips.

She didn't scream. Screaming is sound. She opened her mouth and nothing came, and then she *opened*, is the only way I can say it — her edges went soft, then translucent, then gone, like breath on glass. The humming that always lived under her skin, the constant low note every soul in this realm carries, simply stopped. And a person, it turns out, is mostly that note. The rest is arrangement.

Her shawl fell. That's what hit the ground. Just the shawl, folding down onto the cobbles in a heap, still holding the shape of shoulders for a moment before it settled.

The square made no sound at all. Two hundred people, and not one of them could manage even a gasp, because the silence hadn't finished its work — it hung there, patient, tasting the air for anything that dared vibrate.

Wren's hand crushed mine hard enough to grind bone. She was shaking. She didn't cry, though. I remember being proud of that later, and ashamed of being proud, because a child shouldn't have to learn not to cry at a thing like that.

The herald rolled up his scroll. His hands were shaking properly now, and his face had gone the color of porridge, and he looked out at all of us and said, in his own cracked young voice, barely above a whisper:

"The Cantor thanks you for your attention."

Then he walked on west, toward the next village, ringing his little bell, and the silence peeled off the square behind him like frost off a warming pane, and the world's sounds crept back in — wind, water, a dog barking somewhere with the frantic confusion of an animal that has lost its master's whistle mid-note.

Nobody moved for a long time.

* * *

We found Maren's granddaughter in the crowd afterward. A girl of maybe sixteen, named Tansy, with her grandmother's same stubborn chin, standing over the shawl. When I reached her she was picking it up. Folding it. Neat corners. The way you fold a flag.

"We should bury it," she said. Her voice was steady in the way ice is steady. "Or keep it. I don't— does anyone know which? Has anyone been unmade before and come back?"

Silas crouched beside her, and I watched them struggle with it — the answer, the kindness, the truth. Fifteen years of selling charms to people who needed comfort taught them how to lie gently. But they didn't.

"No one comes back," Silas said quietly. "Not from that kind of silence."

Tansy nodded once, twice, three times, like she was driving a nail home in each nod. Then she looked up at the road west, where the herald had gone, and her chin did the stubborn thing.

"He cried," she said. "Did you see? He cried while he did it. He's not a monster. He's a *tool*." She said the word like she was spitting out a fishbone. "The monster's in Corvenne, wearing the king's face and singing the king's voice, and everyone keeps waiting for someone else to go stop it."

"Not everyone," Wren said.

We all turned. The child had climbed onto the well's low wall, and she stood there with her fists on her hips, seven years old and furious, and I swear on my ferry license the whole ruined square leaned toward her.

"My da said the map would find the cartographer," she announced, "and the cartographer would find the way, and the way would need people loud enough to walk it." She pointed a small finger at Silas. "You're the cartographer. I'm the loud enough." Then the finger swung to me, and I felt it land like a brand. "And you're the one who keeps saying we should keep our heads down."

"I am," I admitted. "It's served me beautifully my entire life."

"Well, it's a stupid plan now." She jumped down off the wall, landed light as a bird, and took Silas's hand. "Grandmother Maren hummed me a song yesterday. About the tide coming home. If nobody sings it anymore, it's gone forever, and that's the same as her dying twice." Her voice wobbled on *twice* and steadied. "I'm not letting the Cantor kill anybody twice."

There was a silence after that. A real one, the honest kind, full of people deciding things.

Tansy broke it. She walked over and pressed the folded shawl into Silas's arms. "You're going to Corvenne. Everyone knows it — strangers asking about the capital, carrying maps. Take this. When you fix it, bring it back, and I'll teach you the tide song so somebody outside this square knows it too." Her eyes were wet and her voice wasn't. "That's not a request. Grandmother never made requests either."

Silas took the shawl like it weighed forty pounds. I saw their thumb move over the fabric, once, the way you touch a wound to check it's real.

"We'll bring it back," they said. "On my life."

"Don't promise your life," Tansy said. "Around here, things collect on promises like that." And she turned and walked back into the crowd, straight-backed, seventeen kinds of broken and none of them showing.

* * *

We left Thistlefen that afternoon, because staying felt like standing in a doorway during a rockfall. Maren's neighbors pressed food on us — bread, smoked eel, a jar of honey — the fierce hospitality of people who cannot fight and refuse to do nothing. Nobody spoke much. Grief had made the whole village quiet in a new way, a chosen way, and I understood suddenly why the folk of this realm hummed even at chores and funerals and arguments. The humming wasn't habit. It was defiance. Every note was a middle finger aimed at the dark.

I thought about that a lot as we walked the raised causeway west, the fen breathing around us, Wren riding on my shoulders because her legs were short and her fury was long.

"You're quiet," Silas said, falling into step beside me. "Quieter than usual, which for you is practically a funeral."

"I'm doing arithmetic," I said.

"What kind?"

"The kind where I add up what that thing in Corvenne did today, and divide it by the number of days it's been sitting on that throne, and try not to drown in the answer." I kept my eyes on the causeway. Easier to talk to a road than a face. "I ran a ferry, back home. You know that much. What you don't know is why I'm good at it. I'm good at it because I learned early that most disasters are just small warnings nobody counted. A rope fraying. A sky going green. A stranger bleeding out in a charm-shop doorway." I glanced at them. "You were a warning, Silas. The day you landed on me, I should've run the other direction. Instead I'm walking toward the biggest disaster on the map, carrying a child and a dead woman's shawl."

"Do you want to turn back?" Silas asked, and there was something raw under the question. Something braced. Fifteen years of expecting people to leave will put that brace in a voice.

I let the question sit while I stepped over a gap in the planking. Then: "My mother used to say the sea doesn't ask if you're ready. It asks if you're aboard." I shrugged. "I'm aboard. Complaining the whole way, but aboard. There's a difference between those too."

Silas smiled — small, crooked, real — and for a second the fear fell off their face and I saw whatever they'd looked like before the hiding started. Younger. Steadier. Someone worth following, maybe, or at least worth walking beside.

Behind us, Wren started to hum.

Low at first, barely a thread — and I tensed, every muscle screaming *no, child, not after this morning* — but then I heard what she was humming. Four notes, rising, falling, rising again. The shape of a tide coming home. Maren's song. The one that was supposed to be gone forever.

She'd learned it in one day. One hearing. And she sang it soft and steady into the evening air, not loud enough to carry past the reeds, but loud enough to carry past the silence, and Silas stopped walking, and I stopped walking, and the three of us stood on a causeway in a doomed country while a seven-year-old committed her first act of treason.

"That's resolve," I said finally, my voice rougher than I intended. "That right there. That's what it looks like before it gets people killed or saves the world, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"It usually can't tell either," Silas said. They unrolled the map a few inches, careful as handling a sleeping animal, and I saw the ink shift — actually shift, crawling like something alive, a new line darkening along the road west. Toward Corvenne. Toward a drowned archive called Amberglass, if the marks meant what I thought they meant.

The map wanted us moving.

The child wanted us singing.

And somewhere ahead, in a tower wearing a stolen crown, something with a king's throat and a composer's hunger lifted its head — I'd have sworn it — and listened.

"West, then," I said, and shouldered the pack, and led the way into the dark.
