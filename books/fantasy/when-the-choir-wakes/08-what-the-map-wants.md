## 8. What the Map Wants

The map had been watching me for two days. I want to be clear about that, because "watching" is not a word cartographers use lightly. Maps are passive things — ink and vellum and the vanity of whoever drew them. They do not breathe. They do not shift their weight when you look away. And they absolutely do not rearrange themselves overnight like a cat finding a warmer spot on the bed.

But when I unrolled it on Brann's kitchen table for the fourth morning running, the coastline had moved.

Not far. A few finger-widths west, maybe. Enough.

"Stop doing that," I told it.

From the doorway, Brann watched me argue with a piece of paper with the expression of a woman recalculating how much trouble she was in. She'd been doing a lot of that lately. Two days ago she'd been a ferrywoman with a leaky boat and a fixed route; now she was an unwilling co-conspirator in whatever this was, sleeping on her own floor because she didn't trust me alone with the thing that had dragged us both through a fold in the world.

"It moved again," she said. Not a question. She'd started noticing before I did, which stung more than I let show.

"The eastern shore." I tapped the spot where a bay had been yesterday and was now open water. "Or there is no eastern shore, and it's inventing one."

"Which would be worse?"

"For a map? Inventing geography is forgery. Forging *toward* something—" I turned the vellum toward the window light. The paper was old, older than it had any right to be, and the blood-ink had gone brown as dried roses, but under the right angle you could still see the faint wet gleam of it, like something alive just under the skin of the page. "For a map to forge toward something means it wants to arrive somewhere."

"And where does it want to arrive?"

"That," I said, "is the question I've been avoiding for two days."

Brann pulled out a chair — the scrape of it loud in the small room, loud everywhere now, since we'd come back from Cael Sorrow with our ears still tuned to a country where sound had weight. She sat across from me and folded her arms, which was her way of saying *we're doing this now*.

"Silas."

"Brann."

"You've been avoiding questions since the night that man bled out on your shop floor. I covered for you with the Wardens. I let you sleep in my house. I followed you through a hole in the air." Her eyes were the grey of harbor water before weather. "So here's what's going to happen. You're going to tell me what the map wants, or I'm going to take it down to the pier and give it to the tide, and you can explain to your dead stranger why his dying wish smells like fish."

I looked at the map. The map, I could have sworn, looked back.

*Finish it,* he'd whispered. Blood on his lips, blood already soaking into my floorboards, blood drying into lines on vellum that I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting — because it *was* my own handwriting, or close enough to make my stomach drop through the floor. Fifteen years of being nobody, of selling luck charms with the magic sanded off them, of keeping my hands steady and my name small. And some stranger had walked out of nowhere knowing exactly who I used to be.

"I found the margins," I said finally.

"What margins?"

I turned the map over. On the reverse, in the corner, faint as breath on glass: annotations. Surveyor's notes. Depth soundings written in a shorthand only three people in the world had ever used, and two of them were dead.

"This is Realmwright notation," I said. "It's how we marked places that couldn't be mapped by ordinary means. Places that moved, or lied, or ate surveyors." I traced one line without quite touching it. "This says *Amberglass*. With a depth reading."

"Amberglass," Brann repeated. "That's a story. Grandmothers scare children with Amberglass. The drowned archive. Library at the bottom of the Sighing Strait, sunk with all its books, and if you dive there the books read *you*."

"Grandmothers are frequently more accurate than historians." I kept my voice level, which took work. "The archive was real. It was where the binding rites were written. Where the old law of magic was drafted, before anyone thought to outlaw the people who wrote it." I heard my own voice go dry and careful, the way it did when I got too close to myself. "If the Cantor's song has a source text, it'll be there."

"So the map wants to take us to the place where all this started."

"The map wants to take us to the place where all this can be *undone*, maybe. Or finished. Those might be the same word, in the original notation." I sat back. My hands, traitors that they were, had gone cold. "There's a waypoint forming. Look."

And there was. Along the western edge of the strait, where yesterday there'd been blank vellum, a mark was surfacing the way a drowned body surfaces — slowly, reluctantly, and never as gently as the stories promise. It wasn't drawn so much as *remembered*: a spiral sigil, the old sign for a repository, with depth-lines spiraling down beneath it like a wound closing in reverse.

Below it, in handwriting I knew better than my own reflection:

*What was written can be unwritten. Bring the hand that wrote.*

Brann read it twice. Then she looked up at me, and I watched her arrive at the conclusion I'd been circling for two days, because Brann arrived at things fast; it was one of the first things I'd liked about her, back when liking people was still something I permitted myself.

"'Bring the hand that wrote,'" she said. "That's you."

"That's someone like me."

"Don't." She said it quietly, without heat, which made it worse. "Don't do the thing where you become vague. I've seen you sell charms, Silas. I know exactly how good you are at vague. Save it for customers."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Outside, Vell went about its morning — carts rattling, gulls arguing, the Wardens' boots somewhere down the street doing their patient knocking door to door. They were still looking for the stranger's killer. They were not, yet, looking for me. There was a difference between those two things and I was living inside it.

"The man who died in my shop," I said. "He knew the notation. He knew *me*, or knew of me. Which means somebody, somewhere, has been keeping a list of Realmwrights. And I'm the last entry on it." I flattened my palms on the table, on either side of the map, careful not to touch it. When I touched it, it touched back. "Whatever it wants, it wants me specifically. That's either a rescue or a trap, and in my experience the interesting ones are both."

*

We argued about it for most of the afternoon, which is to say Brann argued and I performed the role of a man being argued with while secretly agreeing with everything she said.

"You're not going back through that fold," she said, for the third time. "You saw what it did to us. My teeth rang for a day. My *teeth*, Silas."

"Yes."

"And the last time you went near that realm, a man died getting the map to you. Whatever's hunting him might be hunting the map. Which means it's hunting you."

"Also yes."

"So we burn it."

"We don't burn it." I said it faster than I meant to, and Brann's eyebrows went up, and I realized I'd answered from somewhere below thought. From the place where the map had gotten into me already, the way a tide gets into sand. I tried again, slower. "If the Cantor is singing Cael Sorrow silent — if whole villages have to hum just to keep their shapes — then somewhere in that drowned library is the song sheet. The original. And if nobody ever reads it, nobody ever stops it. Burning the map doesn't put out the fire, Brann. It just leaves the building burning with no one sent for."

She stared at me for a long moment. "You've already decided."

"I decided fifteen years ago, when I ran. Everything since has just been me pretending the decision hasn't come due." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as cheap tin. "You cover for a stranger with the Wardens, you follow him through a fold in the world, and this is what you get. A middle-aged charm-seller with debts."

"You're not what I expected," she said, which from Brann was practically a love letter. "Fine. We go. But we go *prepared*, and we go together, and if the map tries anything clever—"

"It will try something clever."

"—then I drown it. I'm serious. I've drowned bigger things than a map."

I believed her. It was one of the comforts of knowing Brann: she was entirely serious about drowning things.

*

Wren found us at dusk.

I want to say she appeared, but that gives her too much mystery and not enough credit. Wren didn't appear; Wren *arrived*, the way weather arrives, suddenly and with total confidence that she belonged there. One moment Brann's kitchen held two adults and a haunted map; the next, the door banged open and a girl of perhaps eleven stood in it with salt wind in her hair and a look on her face that said she'd been walking since morning and had enjoyed every minute of it.

"You're the charm-seller," she announced, pointing at me.

"I'm closed."

"You're the one the humming comes from." She marched in without invitation, dropped onto the third chair like she owned it, and studied the map upside-down with the frank greed of a child reading someone's letters. "I heard it from the shore. Everyone did. The whole point's been humming since you came back — old Nan says the point remembers what touches it. So." She looked up. Bright eyes, quick as a bird's. "Where are we going?"

"*We* aren't going anywhere," Brann said. "How did you even find this house?"

"I asked the tide." Wren said it the way other children said *I asked my mum*. "It likes me. Also I followed the smell of your boat tar, it's very strong." She leaned over the map, squinting at the spiral sigil, and went suddenly, completely still — a stillness so total it was louder than her arrival had been. "That's Amberglass."

Brann and I exchanged a look over her head.

"You know it?" I asked carefully.

"Everyone knows it. But I know the *way*." She tapped the strait's western shore, tapping precisely, tapping like someone who had done this before. "The Sighing Strait isn't crossed by boat, not really. The water sings you off course unless you know which silences to steer by. My mother knew. I know." She lifted her chin. "You need a guide. I'm a guide."

"You're a child," Brann said.

"I'm eleven and a half."

"You're a child," Brann repeated, with the flat finality of a woman dropping an anchor, "and you are going home."

Something crossed Wren's face then — quick, gone before I could name it, but I caught it because I'd spent fifteen years catching exactly that look in mirrors. It was the look of someone who had no home to be sent to. Who had learned to make *arriving* into a substitute for belonging.

"The map said bring the hand that wrote," she said, quieter now, and her eyes came to mine, and they were not a child's eyes in that moment, or not only. "Are you the hand?"

The room went very quiet. Even the city outside seemed to lean in. On the table, the map lay between us, and I felt it listening — felt it the way you feel a held breath.

"I was," I said. "Once."

"Then it wants you." Wren nodded, satisfied, as if that settled everything. "And it needs me, because you can't get to Amberglass without steering by silence, and you two are the loudest quiet people I've ever met." She stuck out her hand across the table, small and certain, palm up. "Wren. Guide. Half price for friends of the tide."

I looked at Brann. Brann looked at me, and her face said *absolutely not* in several languages. But underneath it, in smaller print, was the other thing — the thing I was beginning to suspect she'd carried since the day she'd covered for me with the Wardens: *and yet*.

Because here is what I knew, sitting in that kitchen with a haunted map and a ferrywoman and a child who talked to tides: the map had chosen its company the way rivers choose their course. Not kindly. Not cruelly. Just inevitably. The stranger had died to put it in my hands. It had pulled Brann through a fold without asking. And now it had sent a girl who knew the way to the one place where everything began.

You can fight a tide. Sailors do it all the time, and lose, and are remembered fondly. Or you can raise sail.

"We leave at slack water tomorrow," I heard myself say, and felt the map warm slightly under my hand, like a held breath released. "And Wren — if you're guiding, you do exactly what I say when I say it."

"No promises," said Wren, shaking my hand with the grip of a dockworker twice her size.

Outside, the light was failing, and far off — so far I might have imagined it, though I knew I hadn't — the sea began, very softly, to sigh.
