## 9. The Ferryman's Debt

There are three kinds of passengers who come to the Sighing Strait. The first kind pay their fare and keep their mouths shut, and I love them like brothers. The second kind haggle, which is tiresome but honest — haggling is just lying with arithmetic, and I respect a fellow liar. The third kind arrive at dusk with no money, no plan, and a look in their eyes that says the world has recently ended for them personally.

The woman and the child were the third kind. The map-seller was something new.

I'd been mending a hole in my boat's hull with tar and optimism when they came down the shingle path — the tall one walking like her knees were negotiating separately from the rest of her, the small one skipping, and behind them both a figure I took at first for a ghost, because nobody that pale and that careful had any business being outdoors.

"Ovid," said the tall one, reading it off the sign I'd nailed to a post years ago. *OVID. PASSAGE. FAIR RATES. NO QUESTIONS.* "You're the ferryman."

"I'm the sign's owner, anyway. Close enough." I spat the tar off my fingers. "Fare's two silver or one favor of equivalent nuisance. No questions means no questions, so don't tell me your names, your troubles, or where you're going unless you're paying extra for the privilege."

"We need to cross to Amberglass," said the pale one.

"That'll be three silver."

"You said fair rates."

"I did. And crossing to Amberglass isn't fair on anyone. It's a drowned archive full of things that used to be books and are now mostly opinions. Four silver."

The tall one — Brann, though she hadn't told me yet, because I hadn't asked, because I'm a professional — folded her arms. "We have none of your silver."

"Then we're done here, and it's been a genuine pleasure, which is more than most people get from me."

"Corvin said you'd help us."

And there it was. The name dropped into the conversation like a stone into still water, and I felt it go all the way down.

* * *

Let me tell you about Corvin, since apparently somebody was going to make me.

Corvin Ashgrave was the only man I ever knew who could out-wait me. That's not a small thing. Waiting is my trade. A ferryman who can't wait drowns; a ferryman who waits too long starves; the whole art is knowing which tide forgives which mistake. Corvin waited better than anyone. He once sat outside my hut for two days through a storm without knocking, because he'd promised he wouldn't disturb me before dawn, and when I finally opened the door he was soaked through and smiling and holding a bottle of plum wine he couldn't afford.

He owed me nothing. That was the arrangement. I owed him.

It started as a joke between us, the ledger. Every time he pulled me out of trouble — and he pulled me out of trouble the way other men pull weeds, casually, without breaking stride — I'd say *that goes in the book*, and he'd say *what book*, and I'd say *the book*, and neither of us ever wrote anything down. Fifteen years of favors accumulating interest in an account that existed nowhere except in the space between us when we were both pretending not to count.

He had a daughter. Wren. He used to bring her down to the strait when she was small enough to fit inside a rowboat's shadow, and she'd sit on my mooring post and hum, and the fish would come up to listen, and I'd pretend to be annoyed about the noise while quietly catching more fish than I'd caught in my life. When the Cantor's first purge came through the coast villages, Corvin sent her away ahead of it. I know because I ferried him partway to wherever he was sending her, and he didn't talk the whole crossing, and I didn't ask, because no questions means no questions, even when the question is eating you alive from the inside out.

That was the last time I saw him until tonight, when his name came out of a stranger's mouth on my own beach.

"He's dead," said the child. She said it simply, the way children say terrible things, because they haven't yet learned the adult trick of wrapping horror in cushioning words. "He gave Silas the map and then he died. I'm his daughter. I'm Wren."

I looked at her. She had his chin. She had his habit of looking straight at you when she said hard things, so you couldn't look anywhere else either.

"The map-seller," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me, because I'd have bet good silver it wouldn't. "The one standing behind you looking like a drowned clerk."

Silas raised one hand in something adjacent to a wave. Up close they were worse than pale — they had the particular grayness of someone who has been recently swallowed by geography and spat back out, which, I would learn, was roughly accurate.

"Corvin said you'd help us," Brann repeated, and there was iron under it now. She'd seen the name land. She was watching what I did with it.

"Corvin says a lot of things. Said. Said a lot of things." I picked up my tar brush and examined it as if it were suddenly fascinating. "Here's my problem. Corvin also told me, fifteen years ago, that he'd stay out of trouble. Corvin told me the Cantor's song was just weather, and weather passes. Corvin told me—" I stopped. The tar brush was not fascinating. Nothing was fascinating. "What do you want with Amberglass?"

"That's a question," said Brann. "No questions, remember? Your rule."

"I'm waiving it. Waiving costs extra."

"We can't pay extra."

"Everyone says that right up until they find something to pay with." I looked at the three of them properly then. A ferrywoman with soldier's shoulders and a stranger's patience. A child carrying her father's death like a lantern. And a charm-seller — I knew the type, Vell was full of them, half-charlatan, quarter-magician, quarter something else entirely — holding a folded paper the way people hold things that hold them back.

I should have turned them away. Any sensible man would have. The strait was rising, Amberglass was a graveyard with pretensions, and the Cantor's heralds had been through the coast villages twice that month asking after unsanctioned song, and here was a child who hummed in her sleep — I could hear it already, faint, stubborn, like a kettle that refused to go quiet.

But Corvin Ashgrave had sat outside my door in the rain for two days rather than break a promise, and somewhere in the last hour or the last year or the last fifteen minutes, I had apparently decided that debt doesn't die with the debtor. It transfers. That's what debt does. It's the only thing in this world that reliably survives its owner.

"Four silver," I said. "Or one favor of equivalent nuisance, payable by whoever survives this fool errand. I'll take payment in advance in the form of information: what's in Amberglass that's worth drowning for?"

Silas and Brann exchanged a look — the quick married-couple look of people who've known each other four days and already argue like it's forty years — and then Silas unfolded the map.

I am not a learned man. I can read tide, wind, weather, faces, and the specific silence a person makes when they're lying, and beyond that I've never needed much. But I knew what I was looking at anyway. Everyone in Cael Sorrow knows what Realmwright ink looks like, even if they've never seen it: it doesn't sit on the page, it *breathes*. The lines moved when I wasn't watching them directly. The coastline drew itself a little further every time I blinked, patient as erosion.

"That's illegal," I said.

"Yes," said Silas.

"That's very illegal."

"Yes."

"And you're showing it to a stranger on a beach."

"You're not a stranger," Wren said. "You're the favor. Papa wrote you on the margin."

She pointed, and there it was, in a hand I'd have known anywhere, in ink that was not ink at all: *If all else fails, Ovid owes me. He'll hate it. He'll come.*

"He's not wrong," I said, "on either count," and heard myself sigh the sigh of a man signing his own arrest warrant. "Fine. Fine. One crossing to Amberglass, one return if there's anyone left to return, and in exchange you lot owe me a favor of equivalent nuisance, to be named later, probably at the worst possible moment, because that's how nuisances work. Do we have terms?"

Brann put out her hand. Her grip was exactly as strong as I'd guessed. "Terms."

"Don't shake on it too hard, you'll wake the strait. It's temperamental." I turned toward my boat — the *Second Thought*, because she was always slower than my first one and twice as reliable — and began untying the mooring line, mostly so I wouldn't have to look at any of them while my face did whatever it was doing.

"Papa said you'd pretend to be annoyed," Wren offered helpfully.

"I'm not pretending."

"You are. Your ears went red."

"My ears are red because of the wind."

"There isn't any wind."

I liked her immediately, which was inconvenient, and I made a private note to be careful about that. People you like are people you lose. I've kept the list short for exactly that reason. It currently contained a dead man and a mooring post.

* * *

The Sighing Strait earns its name honestly. The water there doesn't move like water; it moves like breath — long inhale, long exhale, and between them a sound that gets into your chest and rearranges the furniture. Sailors say it's the realm remembering something it lost. I say it's the realm clearing its throat before it says something rude. Either way, you cross at slack tide or you don't cross at all, and slack tide was two hours off, so we sat on the shingle and I taught them the only seamanship lesson that matters: how to wait without fidgeting.

Brann learned it fastest. Of course she did — ferrywoman, river-born, hands that knew rope. She sat with her forearms on her knees and watched the water the way you watch a sleeping animal, respectful and ready. Wren lasted four minutes before she started humming, and I opened my mouth to tell her to stop, and then closed it again, because the fish were coming up. Old habits. Corvin's daughter humming on my shore, fish rising in rings around her, and me sitting there like a man being shown a photograph of his own past and told to guess the year.

"So," I said, to fill the silence before it filled itself. "Amberglass. Since we're all going to drown together, you may as well tell me what we're drowning for."

Silas told me. Not everything — I could see the seams where they were leaving things out, and I let it go, because everyone leaves things out, myself emphatically included — but the shape of it. The binding. The king wearing the Cantor like a second skin. An archive under the water where the original rite was written, and a map that wanted to be finished, and a price at the end of it that Silas mentioned quickly and lightly, the way you mention a splinter.

I didn't catch the price. I want that on the record. Whatever came later, whatever was said about what I knew and when — I didn't catch it. I was watching the water.

"A rite to unbind a king," I said instead. "Written by scholars who've been dead longer than I've been disappointing my mother. In a library that's been underwater since before the purge. And your plan is to walk in and read it."

"Swim, mostly," said Brann.

"Swim. Wonderful. Do any of you swim?"

"I do," said Brann.

"I float," said Wren, "if someone holds my hair."

Both of them looked at Silas.

"I'm a cartographer," Silas said, with the dignity of a cat being shown a bath. "I draw water. I don't enter it."

"This is going beautifully," I said. "Two hours to slack tide, one swimmer, one floater, and one man who draws water. We'll be legends. Brief ones."

Wren laughed — a real laugh, sudden and bright, and it hit me sideways, because it was Corvin's laugh, pitched up an octave, the exact same shape of it, and for a second the strait went quiet as if it had heard it too and was being polite.

I stood up abruptly and went to check the mooring line I had already checked.

* * *

We crossed at slack, and it was ugly, and I'll spare you the details except to say that the Second Thought handled it better than any of us deserved and that Wren held my hair — metaphorically speaking — the entire way, chattering questions at me with the tireless appetite of the young: Was the strait always like this, had I really met her father, what was Amberglass like before it drowned, why did I live alone, did I have a family, why did I stop talking when she said that—

"Wren," Brann said gently.

"It's fine," I lied, in the grand tradition of my profession. "It's fine. Ask your questions. Questions are free tonight. Special promotion."

She asked whether I'd known her father long, and I said fifteen years, give or take. She asked what he was like, and I said, "Stubborn. Patient. Generous in ways he thought nobody noticed, which was the point of the design." She asked if he ever talked about her, and I said, "Constantly, and badly, the way men talk about the best things they have — like they're afraid saying it plainly will jinx it." She nodded and looked out at the black water for a while, and I pretended to steer, and the strait sighed beneath us, and nobody said anything true for a mile or two, which is sometimes the kindest thing a crew can do for each other.

Amberglass rose out of the sea near midnight — or what passes for midnight in a realm where the sky hums instead of darkening properly. You see the top of it first: a tower, or the crown of one, tilted like a drunk leaning on a wall. Then the rest, descending in flooded terraces, windows glowing faintly gold under the waterline, because the archive preserved light the way it preserved everything else — stubbornly, and slightly wrong.

"There it is," Silas said softly, and took out the map, and the map's ink stirred like something waking up.

"One condition," I said, shipping oars. "When this is over — whatever 'over' means — you three come back to my beach, and we settle accounts proper. Silver, favor, story, whatever. Nobody walks off owing anybody. Debts that don't get settled turn into ghosts, and I've got enough ghosts."

Brann studied me across the gunwale. She had the look of a woman cataloguing everything I wasn't saying and filing it for later, and I found I minded less than usual.

"Settled," she said.

"Good." I threw the grapple toward the drowned terrace below the tower, and it caught with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and far below us, down in the gold-lit dark, something in the archive shifted — pages, maybe, or attention.

"Now," I said, "everyone remember which one of you can actually swim, and try to look surprised when it matters."

Wren was already humming. The fish were already coming up. And I was already in it — up to the neck, past the neck, in over my head in every sense the language offers — because a dead man had written my name in magic ink, and the worst part, the part I'd deny under torture, was that some tired old corner of me had been waiting fifteen years for exactly this kind of trouble to come down the shingle path and ask, politely, for passage.
