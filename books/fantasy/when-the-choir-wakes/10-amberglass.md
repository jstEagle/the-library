## 10. Amberglass

The drowned archive did not look drowned. That was the first lie it told me.

I had expected silt and ruin — the way a city looks after a flood takes it, all soft edges and surrendered furniture. Instead Amberglass rose out of the shallow sea like a held breath: towers of amber-colored stone that had never learned to fall, streets swept clean by currents that moved with the courtesy of servants, light filtering down from the surface in long gold columns that turned the whole place into the inside of a lantern.

"It's beautiful," Wren said, and her voice came out strange — flattened, rounded at the corners, as though the water were handling each word gently before passing it along.

"That's how you know to be careful," Ovid said. He poled his ferry through a street that had once been an avenue of merchants' houses, shopfronts still bearing their signs in a script I half-recognized from my old work. "Beautiful places are where people stop watching the exits."

"You're a delight," Brann said from the bow. She'd said little since we'd struck our bargain with him, but she watched everything — the currents, the towers, me — with the expression of a woman counting her money in a crowd.

"He's not wrong," I admitted. "Places this lovely usually have teeth."

"Everything here has teeth," Ovid said cheerfully. "The question is whether they're pointed at you."

The map, folded against my chest inside its oilcloth, had been warm for two days now. Not fever-warm. Body-warm. Like something sleeping against my ribs, dreaming in ink. As we drifted deeper into Amberglass it grew warmer still, and when I pressed a hand to it I could feel a faint pulse, patient as a tide.

It wanted me to read what was down here. It had always wanted that, I suspected, from the moment a dying man bled it into being on some floor far above the waterline.

* * *

We moored the ferry at what had been the archive's great doors — twin slabs of amber stone carved with musicians, their instruments raised, their mouths open in eternal song. The water ended at the threshold. Not drained, not parted; simply *ended*, as if someone had drawn a line on the world and written *no* beneath it. Beyond the doors stretched dry halls lit by the same golden columns, falling from nowhere, casting no shadows.

"Thin place," Brann murmured. "Like the one that spat us into Cael Sorrow."

"Worse," Ovid said. He stayed with the boat. "I'll be here. If you're not back by the time the light changes color twice, I'm leaving, and I want you to know that in advance so you can't guilt me about it later."

"Noted," I said.

"And if you die in there—" He paused, and for a moment the cynicism slipped and something older showed underneath, something tired. "Finish your business quickly. The dead make poor company."

Wren squeezed past us both before anyone could stop her, which was how she entered most rooms and, I suspected, would eventually enter most legends. Brann followed, hand on the knife at her hip, and I went last, because someone had to be the coward who checked the rear.

Inside, the archive breathed.

There was no other word for it. The halls expanded and contracted around us in slow rhythm, and the air tasted of resin and old paper and, faintly, of salt — the memory of the sea that had been kept out rather than kept away. Shelves lined every wall, rising beyond sight into the golden dark, and every shelf was full. Books, scrolls, tablets of pressed glass, cylinders of wax scored with grooves. A library the size of a nation, drowned in light instead of water.

"The original binding," I said quietly. "The stranger's map led here. So somewhere in all this—"

"It'll find us," Wren said. "It found everything else."

She was right, which was becoming a habit of hers I didn't entirely trust. We hadn't walked twenty paces before the warmth against my chest flared, and when I drew the map out, it unfolded itself — not to any page I'd seen before, but to a blank expanse that began, even as we watched, to fill with lines. Ink bloomed across the paper like frost forming in reverse, drawing a route through the archive's halls: left at the hall of winds, down the stair of nine names, through the gallery of unfinished works.

"It's showing off," Brann said flatly.

"It's hurrying," I said. And I didn't say the other thing, the thing I'd been not-saying since the map first pulled us under: that it hurried the way a wound hurries toward a bandage, or a secret toward confession. Something down here had been waiting fifteen years for a Realmwright to arrive. Possibly longer.

* * *

The stair of nine names descended in a spiral, each step carved with a word in the old realm-script. I read them as we went, because reading them was the only prayer I knew anymore.

*Listen. Remember. Bind. Hold. Endure. Mourn. Mend. Begin. Again.*

Nine steps. Nine words. And the last one — *again* — was worn smooth, polished by centuries of feet or fingers or grief.

At the bottom waited a single room, round as a bell, and in the center of the room stood a lectern of black glass, and on the lectern lay a book bound in white leather gone ivory with age.

The map stopped pulling. Just — stopped. The warmth faded to nothing, and the paper hung in my hands as ordinary as any scrap, as if to say: *you're here now. This part is yours.*

I approached the book the way you approach a sleeping animal. Brann and Wren flanked me without being asked, and I noticed, distantly, that they'd done it naturally, the way people do who have begun to think of themselves as a unit. I filed that away in the small warm room I kept in my chest for things I couldn't afford to examine yet.

The book bore no title. Its cover bore only a seal I recognized — because I'd spent fifteen years trying to forget it. The seal of the Court of Cael Sorrow. The crown, the harp, and beneath them, the motto of a kingdom that had believed music was a form of government:

*What is sung is held.*

"I studied this," I heard myself say. My voice sounded wrong in the round room, too loud and too small at once. "When I was young. Before. Every Realmwright did. The binding rites of Cael Sorrow were considered — they were considered the masterworks. The proof of what the craft could do. We copied them. We taught them."

"You helped write the outlawing," Brann said. Not cruelly. Just placing the fact on the table between us, where facts belong.

"After," I said. "After I learned what the masterworks cost." I opened the book.

* * *

The hand that had written it was beautiful. That was the worst part. I know beautiful hands; I was trained to judge them, trained to produce them. This was a hand that loved its letters, that gave each one room to breathe, that understood a page was a piece of music made visible. The margins carried sketches — a crown, a sickbed, a man's face rendered over and over in quick, worried strokes, each attempt more exhausted than the last.

And the face was familiar. Not because I'd met him. Because I'd seen him yesterday, lucid for one terrible moment at a tower window, mouthing a word I hadn't been able to read.

*Hurry,* Brann had said he mouthed. She'd seen it too.

"This is Aldous," I said. "The king. This is his court composer's hand."

"How do you know?" Wren asked.

"Because composers write the way this page writes." I turned pages with hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake. "Cartographers write to record. Chroniclers write to argue. Composers write to *hold* — every line placed so the whole stays standing. Look." I tilted the page toward them. Around the central text of the rite ran a second text, smaller, woven between the lines like a vine around a trellis. "That's not commentary. That's a counter-melody. Whoever wrote this built the spell the way you build a fugue — every element answering another, nothing allowed to stand alone."

Brann leaned closer. "Read it. The main part. Out loud."

So I did. And I will set down here, as plainly as I can, what the original binding says, because I have read many terrible documents in my life — outlawries and death warrants and the cold arithmetic of treaties — and none of them ever frightened me the way this beautiful page did.

*The king is dying,* it began. *The physicians have failed. The priests have failed. I have not failed yet, and I will not.*

*Aldous took an arrow at the ford of Sorrel, and the wound has gone to poison, and by tomorrow's dusk he will be dead unless death is given somewhere else to go.*

*I am Maestro Vey Corvane, First Composer of the Court, and I have loved this king since we were boys stealing plums from the orchard below the west wall. I tell you this so you understand that what follows is not ambition. There is no ambition in it. There is only a plum tree, and a boy laughing with juice on his chin, and my refusal — my absolute refusal — to sing at his funeral.*

*The rite is mine. I designed it alone, in secret, because he would never have permitted it, and because the Council of Song would have called it heresy, and they would have been right. It is heresy. It takes two souls meant for separate deaths and binds them into one continuing life. It requires consent — true consent, freely given, from both — and it requires that the binding be sung, wholly, by both parties, until neither voice can be told from the other.*

*He has consented. God forgive me, he begged me for it, with the poison already in his eyes, and I could not refuse him. I have never once been able to refuse him anything. That is either the best or the worst fact about me, and I no longer have time to learn which.*

Then the hand changed. Not degraded — changed, the way a voice changes when the singer stops performing and starts meaning it. The letters came faster, crowded, the margins filling with those worried sketches of the same face.

*It worked. By every law of the craft, it worked. His wound closed. His heart steadied. He woke and asked for breakfast and complained about the porridge, and I sat in the corner of his chamber and laughed until I cried, and for six days we were the happiest men in any realm.*

*On the seventh day I understood what I had done.*

*A binding holds only while it is sung. I knew this. I wrote this. But I had imagined — I don't know what I had imagined. That the song would become unnecessary. That love would take over where craft left off. But the rite does not run on love. It runs on voice. One of us must hold the melody at all times, without rest, without end, or the binding tears and both souls tear with it.*

*He cannot sing it. The poison damaged his throat beyond mending; the binding saved his life but could not save the instrument. So it falls to me. All of it. Forever.*

*I have not slept in nine days. I do not know if I sleep anymore at all. When I stop singing — even for a moment — I feel him start to slip, and the feeling is worse than drowning, worse than fire, and so I sing, and sing, and sing.*

*Last night he looked at me across the council chamber with eyes that were half his own and half something else, something vast and frightened that lives in the space where our voices meet, and he said: Vey, you may put me down now. And I said: I can't. And he said: I know. And then he smiled at me, the plum-tree smile, and sang the harmony part as well as he could, in that ruined whisper, to help me carry it.*

*I am writing this so that whoever finds it understands. Whatever wears the crown in the years to come — whatever the histories call it, monster or god or Cantor — remember: it began as a man who loved his king and could not stop singing. It began as two boys under a plum tree. It began as a mercy.*

*Every horror begins as a mercy. That is how you recognize them, in the end. They are the mercies that refused to stop.*

The page ended there. The next page was blank. The next hundred were blank. The book simply stopped, mid-urgency, a man setting down his pen to draw breath he no longer needed, and never picking it up again.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. In the round room, the golden light fell and fell and cast no shadows, and the archive breathed its slow breath around us, and I stood with my hand flat on the page as if I could keep the dead composer company through his own handwriting.

"A tragedy," Wren said finally. She said it without drama, the way children state enormous things, and it landed harder than any speech could have. "It's not a monster. It's just — sad. It's the saddest thing I ever heard."

"Yes," I said.

"We came here to kill it," Brann said slowly. "Or unmake it. Or whatever the rite does."

"We came here to unbind it," I corrected. "Which may amount to the same thing. Which may kill them both. The rite cuts both ways — that's why it needs blood for ink. Unbinding isn't surgery. It's amputation." I closed the book with more gentleness than any book of spells deserves. "But now we know what we'd be cutting apart. Two men. One of whom has been singing without pause for — how long? Centuries? Long enough to forget there was ever a plum tree?"

"They forgot," Brann said. "Or it forgot. The king part." Her jaw was tight. "You saw him at the window. Lucid for a moment. *Hurry.* He's still in there, under all that singing. Drowning in his own rescue."

"And the other one," Wren said, "the composer — he's still in there too. Singing. He never got to stop." She looked up at me, and her eyes were bright and furious and about eight years old and about eight hundred years old, both at once. "Silas. What if we don't cut? What if there's another way? You're a Realmwright. You *write*. Can't you — fix the song? Change it? Make it a song somebody chose?"

I opened my mouth to give the responsible answer. The answer about the limits of the craft, the dangers of improvisation, the reasons the old masters forbade exactly what she was suggesting. Fifteen years of hiding had made me very good at responsible answers; they were load-bearing walls in the architecture of my caution.

But the book lay closed on the lectern with its beautiful grieving hand, and the ninth step upstairs said *again*, worn smooth by everyone who had ever climbed back up to try once more, and I thought: *every horror begins as a mercy.* And perhaps — perhaps — every mending begins as heresy.

"Maybe," I said. "Maybe there's another way. I don't know yet. I need to study the rite. All of it. Every variation, every margin note—" I stopped.

Because the margins of the final page had changed.

They had been empty when I closed the book. I was certain of it. I am a cartographer; certainty about surfaces is my entire profession. But there, in fresh ink, in a hand I knew better than my own heartbeat — my own childhood handwriting, the same hand that haunted the stranger's map — new words had appeared, as if the page had merely been waiting for me to finish reading before it finished speaking:

*Now you understand. Now come and finish it. The rite is not the ending, cartographer. It is the door. Bring your blood. Bring your friends. Bring the child — she is the verse you have not met yet.*

Beneath the words, drawn small and precise, was a route. Out of Amberglass, across the Sighing Strait, through the Road of Teeth, to the capital.

To Corvenne. To the tower window. To the king.

Brann read it over my shoulder. Then she looked at me, and her voice was very quiet.

"Silas. Who's been writing to you in your own handwriting?"
