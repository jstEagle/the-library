## 11. Fifteen Years of Silence

Brann waited until the fire had burned down to coals before she said anything, which was how I knew it would be bad. Brann was a woman who did her talking the way she did her ferrying — at speed, in bad weather, and without asking permission — but she'd been quiet all evening, watching me across Wren's sleeping shape with an expression I recognized from my own mirror: the look of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"Say it," I said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since Amberglass. You've been looking at me like I owe you money."

She poked the fire with a stick that wasn't really a stick — everything here was a little more than it pretended to be — and sent up a scatter of sparks that hummed as they died. In Cael Sorrow even embers sang. It should have been beautiful. Mostly it was.

"You read that binding," she said. "In the archive. The original one."

"I read a lot of things in the archive. It's an archive. That's sort of the point of them."

"Don't." Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "Don't do the thing where you talk until I forget what I asked. I watched you read it, Silas. I watched your face. And then I watched you touch the margin of that page the way you'd touch an old scar, and I thought: *they know this handwriting.* Not the words. The *hand*."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird called three notes and waited, the way birds did here, as if conversation were a courtesy you extended to strangers.

"So," Brann said. "Whose handwriting is it?"

I could have lied. Fifteen years of practice; lying was the one craft I'd kept sharp while everything else went soft. I sold charms to the faithless in Vell by being exactly unremarkable enough, by keeping my hands still and my history folded up small as a pressed flower. I had a whole liturgy of deflections. *I'm just a charm-seller. I copied that style from a book. Realmwrights are dead, everyone knows it.*

But Wren was asleep with her mouth open and her stolen whistle-song clutched in one fist, and Ovid was supposedly on watch though his snoring suggested otherwise, and Brann was looking at me with those sharp ferrywoman's eyes that had already decided, somewhere between the drowned archive and this cold campfire, that whatever I said next would be the truth or nothing.

"It's mine," I said.

The word came out smaller than I meant it to. Fifteen years is a long time to keep a sentence in your chest. It comes out dusty.

Brann didn't move. "Yours."

"The binding. The original text. Part of it. The marginalia, the annotations, the — there were four of us who drafted the final version, and the third clause is mostly mine." I heard myself talking fast now, doing the thing she'd told me not to do, and made myself stop. "I wrote part of the law that outlawed magic. That's whose handwriting it is."

* * *

You should understand something about how magic was outlawed, because the histories get it wrong, and because I was there, and because getting it wrong lets people like me sleep.

It wasn't a purge, at first. It was a conference. There were chairs, and water pitchers, and a woman named Chancellor Vere who cried when she gave the opening address because her brother had been killed by a working gone wrong — a weather-binding that slipped its anchors and drowned a valley. Everyone in that hall had lost someone, or feared to. Grief makes a terrible drafting committee, but it makes a very motivated one.

I was twenty-two and I was the best young hand in the cartographers' guild, which in those days meant I was a Realmwright — one of the ones who could draw a border and make it true. We thought we were so clever. We thought the problem was *unlicensed* magic, *unbound* magic, magic without oversight, and that if we wrote the law carefully enough — if we drew the boundaries precisely enough — we could cut away the rot and keep the garden.

Four of us wrote the operative clauses. I wrote the third. The third clause was the elegant one, the one everyone praised. It didn't ban magic outright; it *severed the connection* between the realms, on the theory that most wild workings drew their power through the thin places, and if you closed the doors, the fire would starve.

Elegant. Contained. Reversible, we told ourselves. A door can be reopened.

We hadn't understood that the realms weren't two rooms with a door between them. They were one body holding its breath. When I inked the severance — my blood in the ink, because that's how Realmwright work goes, that's always how it goes — I felt something tear on the far side of the world. I felt it in my teeth. And then the reports started coming in from the other realm, the one we'd cut off, the one nobody in the capital thought about because it was far away and poor and full of people who *sang*, for pity's sake, who built their whole lives out of song the way we built ours out of stone.

Cael Sorrow began to die quietly. And the man who noticed first — a court composer over here, though I wouldn't learn his name for fifteen years — tried to hold his king together with a fusion rite, and made the Cantor, and made all of this.

So no. I didn't outlaw magic single-handedly. But I drew the line. My hand. My blood. And when the consequences came home — when the guild started asking why the thin places were screaming, when the first refugees from the fold-crossings arrived with stories nobody wanted printed — I ran. Changed my name, burned my tools, took up a trade small enough to hide behind. Charms. Little ones. Luck and mending and finding-lost-things, nothing that would show on any warden's instruments, sold to people too desperate to ask where the luck came from.

Fifteen years of being almost no one. And then a stranger bled out on my floor and handed me a map in my own handwriting, and here I am.

* * *

Brann listened to all of it without interrupting, which cost her something; I could see the interruptions queuing up behind her teeth.

When I finished, she fed the fire another stick and said, "Ovid owes the dead stranger a favor. What do you owe him?"

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. Answer the question."

I looked at my hands. Charm-seller's hands, calloused from pretending to be ordinary. "Everything," I said. "I owe him everything. I owe this whole realm — every village that has to hum itself into shape every morning, every name the Cantor stole, that elder they unmade in front of us—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "I owe them the third clause, Brann. I owe them the door I closed."

"And the map?"

"What about it?"

"The stranger. The dying man. He found *you*. Out of every charm-shop in every city in Vell, he walked into yours, bleeding, and put a Realmwright map in a Realmwright's hands and said *finish it*. You think that's chance?"

"I think the world has a sense of humor and it's cruel."

"I think," Brann said slowly, "that he knew. However long he searched, however much it cost him — and it cost him plenty, look at the state he was in — he knew who you were. Which means someone's known for fifteen years. Which means your hiding never worked as well as you thought." She shrugged, brutal and gentle at once, the way she did everything. "Which means you can stop paying rent on it."

"That's not how shame works."

"How does it work, then? You tell me. You're the expert. Fifteen years of practice."

I laughed, or something near it. "It works by never stopping. You carry it. You get good at carrying it. You become very quiet and very careful and you sell luck to widows and you tell yourself that's penance, selling charms below cost, and some nights you almost believe it."

"And is it?"

"Is it what?"

"Penance." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the grey in her cropped hair. "Because from where I sit, it looks like hiding. Penance would have been staying. Fixing it. You had the skill to reopen the door, Silas. You *drew* it shut. One hand closes, the same hand opens. Instead you ran and let a composer over here try to patch your wound with a ritual, and now a king's been wearing a monster for fifteen years and a girl's father died running from city to city looking for the only person alive who could undo it."

Every word landed. She'd aimed them well; Brann missed nothing on the water and missed less on land.

"Yes," I said. "All of it. Yes."

"Good." She sat back. "Then we're done with the worst part."

"We are?"

"You confessed. You didn't talk your way out, you didn't blame the guild, you didn't turn it into a lecture about Chancellor Vere and her dead brother. You said *my hand, my blood, my fault*." She poked the fire again. "I've ferried enough guilty men to know the difference between the ones drowning in it and the ones swimming. You've been drowning. Tonight you swam a stroke or two."

"Brann—"

"I'm not done." She held up a hand. "Here's what I know about debts, because I grew up on a ferry and debt is the water we float on. A debt you run from grows. A debt you face gets *smaller*. Not paid — smaller. Because once you're facing it, other people can help carry it, and that's the part you've never let anyone do. Fifteen years, Silas. Fifteen years hauling it alone because you figured you deserved the weight." She looked at me straight. "You probably do deserve it. That's not the point. The point is whether the realms get fixed, and they don't get fixed by you drowning politely in Vell."

Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured a half-bar of her lullaby, the way children here breathed song even unconscious, holding her own shape together in dreams. We both went quiet until she settled.

"I helped write the law that made this possible," I whispered. "The Cantor. All of it. Doesn't that make you want to walk away?"

"Want?" Brann considered it honestly, which I'd always liked about her even when it hurt. "First day, maybe. When I saw that elder come apart into silence. I wanted to walk away from everything, including you." She shook her head. "But walking away doesn't bring anyone back, and neither does drowning. So." She reached across the fire and gripped my wrist — hard, ferrywoman's grip, the grip that pulls people out of water. "Here's my terms. No more hiding. Not from the Cantor, not from the map, and not from us. Whatever the rite costs, whatever the last page says, you tell us. All of it. Because I've seen what happens to people who carry secrets into a current, and the current always finds out."

Something in my chest went cold and then warm, in that order. The last page. The blood-ink. The price I'd read alone in the archive and folded away where I kept everything else.

"All of it," I said. "I promise."

She held my wrist a moment longer, then let go and lay back against her pack like a woman off shift. "Good. Sleep, then. Tomorrow we figure out what the map wants next."

I didn't sleep. I sat by the coals and turned my hands over and thought about doors — how one hand closes them, how the same hand opens them, how a door is only ever a line drawn in the right place by someone willing to stand there and hold it.

Across the dead fire, Brann's breathing slowed into something like trust. Behind her, Wren slept humming. And in my satchel, the map shifted against my ribs like a living thing, impatient, pulling — and as I laid my palm flat against the leather to still it, fresh lines surfaced under my fingers, darkening into view like ink dropped in water.

A new waypoint. And beneath it, in handwriting that was mine and yet somehow older, a single instruction:

*Bring the chorus. One hand cannot hold what four voices broke.*

I stared at it a long time. Then I woke Brann, because I had promised her no more hiding, and promises made over dying fires are the only kind I'd ever kept.
