## 12. The Unbinding Rite

The drowned archive of Amberglass did not, in the strictest sense, drown. It had simply been lowered into the sea the way a body is lowered into a grave — with ceremony, with intention, and with no expectation that anyone would come back for it.

We spent three days there. Brann counted them like a woman counting coins she didn't trust. Wren counted them like a child counting festival days. I counted them the way I count everything now: against how much time I have left that I don't know about.

The archive's shelves rose out of green water in tiers, and the books had not rotted. They had *changed*. Paper made from reeds grown in song-rich soil keeps its shape underwater; ink made the old way — ground pearl, ash, and a drop of the scribe's own blood — does not run. It waits. Every book down there was waiting.

"You're doing the face," Brann said on the second morning.

"What face?"

"The one where you've read something and you're deciding whether the rest of us need to know it. You get very polite. It's terrifying."

"I'm always polite."

"Exactly my point." She wrung out her sleeve — everything in Amberglass was damp, even the dry things, as if the sea had left its opinion behind — and looked at me with those ferrywoman's eyes that took soundings of people the way her hands took soundings of water. "Silas. What did you find?"

"A shelf," I said. "A promising shelf."

It was even true. The trouble with being a liar of long practice is that you learn to hide truths inside truths, the way a smuggler hides contraband inside legitimate cargo. I had gotten so good at it that sometimes I couldn't find the honest goods myself.

* * *

The shelf was in the third tier, past a collapsed gallery where amber had flooded in through a broken window and set whole rows of books in honey-colored stone, their spines visible forever, unreadable forever. A library of ghosts. I tried not to think about what that might be an allegory for.

The book I wanted had no title on its spine. It had a sigil instead: two interlocking circles, broken at the point where they crossed. I knew the sigil. I had drawn it myself, once, fifteen years ago, in a margin, in a moment of academic vanity, before I understood what the circles were for.

Binding notation. The grammar of fusing one soul to another.

I should explain something about Realmwright work, since apparently I'm the last practitioner and therefore the only person left who can explain anything about it. Magic, before it was outlawed in the living realms, was never really spells. That's the story they tell children and Wardens. In truth it was *notation*. Reality is a text, and most people read it without knowing they're reading. A Realmwright could write in the margins. Could amend. Could — if arrogant enough, and I was arrogant enough — compose.

The Cantor composes too. I'd seen its work already: villages humming themselves into coherence, an elder unmade into silence, a king wearing his own death like a mask. The Cantor writes loud. Realmwrights write small. But we use the same alphabet, and that fact sat in my chest all through the second day like a swallowed stone.

I found the rite near dusk of the second day, in a section of the book whose pages had been deliberately stuck together with resin. Whoever hid it had used a lot of resin. Whoever hid it had wanted it found by someone patient, or desperate, or both. I warmed the page against my palm for an hour until the resin softened, and peeled it open like skin off milk.

*The Rite of Unbinding,* said the header, in a hand steadier than mine.

Below it, forty lines of notation. Below that, in smaller script, the instructions — conditions, preparations, warnings. I read them twice. Then I read the last line a third time, because the first two times I had read it, some part of me had refused to translate it from the old script into meaning, the way you can look at a word you know and see only shapes.

*The rite requires ink of the maker's making: let the cartographer who would loose the binding open a vein, and let the map be finished in that ink, for a binding written in blood may only be answered in kind.*

There it was. Plain as a tax notice.

A cartographer's blood as ink. Not a drop. Not a symbolic smear. The instructions were specific in the way old ritualists are specific when they want to make sure nobody cheats: *the vein opened must not close until the last line is sung.* Which meant hours. Which meant emptying.

Which meant me.

Because of course it meant me. The dead stranger hadn't pressed that map into my hands out of a hundred thousand possible hands by accident. The map had pulled me under because it recognized its author's craft in my fingers. The whole journey had been addressed, sealed, and delivered. I was not the courier of this rite. I was its component.

* * *

I climbed up to the archive's highest gallery that night, where the water had never reached and the air smelled of dust and cold amber, and I sat with the book in my lap and did not go back down.

This is the part where I'm supposed to say I considered telling them. And I did consider it. I considered it thoroughly, the way you consider jumping from a great height — you stand at the edge, you appreciate the view, you note how easy it would be, and then you step back and say nothing to anyone about how much of your attention the edge holds.

Brann would have taken the book out of my hands. She'd have done it gently, which would have been worse. She'd have said something practical like *we'll find another way*, and then she'd have spent the rest of the journey looking at me the way you look at someone standing on that edge, and every plan we made after that would bend around the shape of my dying, and I couldn't — 

Wren would have cried. No. Wren wouldn't have cried. Wren would have gone quiet, which from Wren is a catastrophe, a weather event, a silence in a country where silence means someone has been erased. I'd seen what the Cantor's silence did to faces. I wasn't going to put that face on her.

And Ovid — Ovid would have done arithmetic. He'd have stood there with his ferryman's ledger open in his eyes and calculated whether one cartographer balanced against a king and a realm and a child, and he'd have arrived at yes, and he'd have hated himself for arriving at yes, and I'd have watched him hate himself, and somehow that was the worst outcome of all. You can survive being sacrificed. What you can't survive is watching the people you love do the math and get the right answer.

So. There it is. The honest inventory of a coward's reasoning, laid out as carefully as I lay out coastlines.

Except it isn't cowardice, is it? That's the lie I told myself on the gallery floor, wrapped in my coat, listening to the sea press patiently against the walls. Cowardice would be refusing the rite. This was worse. This was accepting it and hiding it, which meant I'd decided my life was forfeit but my *agency* wasn't, and there's a name for people who make that trade, and the name isn't hero.

Fifteen years ago I helped write magic out of the law. I sat in a room with reasonable men and drew the boundaries on a map, and the boundaries became wards, and the wards became prisons, and I told myself it was for order, for safety, for the greater peace of people who couldn't defend themselves against the strong. And then I ran, because the same law I'd drafted had a clause about practitioners, and I had ink-stained fingers, and I valued my skin over my signature.

And now here was the bill coming due at last, with interest. The realms I'd helped sever, asking to be rejoined. The craft I'd abandoned, demanding to be used. The blood I'd been hoarding for fifteen years, itemized at last.

It almost felt fair. That was the seduction of it. Somewhere underneath the fear there was a small, vile voice saying *this balances the ledger, Silas, this makes you clean.* I lay in the dark and listened to that voice and understood, finally, why people walk willingly into sacrifices. Not for glory. For absolution. Dying is easy compared to living with what you've done, and here was a way to do both at once, elegantly, with notation.

I hated the voice. I listened to it anyway. That's the thing about self-condemnation — it fits so well you forget you're wearing it.

* * *

On the third morning I came down with the book and a face I'd practiced.

"Found it?" Brann asked.

"Found it." I set the book on a dry crate between us. Wren was turning a page of something with her finger, careful as a surgeon, and Ovid was pretending not to care, which was his way of caring intensely. "The rite's intact. Forty lines of binding notation, plus the performance conditions. It's real. It will work."

Ovid narrowed his eyes. "But."

"No but. Conditions are conditions. We need the chorus — well, we'll need singers, plural, to hold the lines while the notation takes. One voice can't carry a forty-line unbinding; the strain would—" I stopped myself from saying *kill them*, and rerouted. "—would tear the singer apart. So we find voices. Low Hum, maybe, or wherever the rebels are singing these days. And we need the map itself present, finished to the final waypoint, because the rite answers the original binding through it. Same ink, same hand, same grammar."

"And the ink?" Brann asked.

"The map's already inked," I said, and showed her the blood-dark lines, and let her believe the answer was complete.

She looked at me a moment too long. Ferrywomen know about cargo that doesn't match its manifest. But the tide was going out — literally; Amberglass exhaled on a schedule, the water withdrawing down the stairwells in long silver sheets — and Wren spotted something glittering on a newly exposed shelf and shouted, and the moment broke the way moments do when a twelve-year-old wants you to come look at a thing.

I let it break. I have never been so grateful to a child for finding a shiny object.

That afternoon we began the ascent out of the archive. Ovid went first, testing each stair with his boat-hook, grumbling about stairs in general and submerged stairs in particular. Wren went second, cataloguing everything she'd seen into a running monologue that Brann called "the inventory" and encouraged shamelessly. Brann went third. I went last, which was my choice, and I told myself it was so I could watch the others' footing, and I let myself keep telling myself that.

At the top, where the sea gave way to grey daylight and the Sighing Strait stretched out ahead of us toward the capital, I paused with the book strapped to my back and looked down once more into the green dark.

Somewhere down there, in a resin-sealed page, the last line waited for me. *Let the vein opened not close until the last line is sung.*

I flexed my right hand. The palm that would open. It seemed absurdly ordinary — a palm, a lifeline, a scar from a kitchen burn in Vell, years ago, when I still pretended to be nothing more than a charm-seller with steady-enough hands.

"Silas!" Wren, from the headland above, silhouetted against a sky the color of pewter. "Come *on*. The tide's coming back and Ovid says the strait gets rude."

"Coming," I said.

And I climbed up out of Amberglass carrying the rite, the map, and the price of both, and none of them looked at my hand, and I made sure of it.

Behind us, far below, the sea began to close over the shelves again — patient, thorough, keeping its secrets the way I kept mine.

One of us, at least, would hold its breath for as long as it needed to.
