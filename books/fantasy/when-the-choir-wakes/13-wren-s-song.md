## 13. Wren's Song

I have ferried the living, the dead, and on two memorable occasions something that insisted it was neither, and I can tell you this: nothing on the Sighing Strait frightens me half so much as a child asking a direct question.

"Ovid," Wren said that evening, while we made camp in the lee of a leaning stone that Silas swore was marked on no map and Brann swore was watching her, "why do you never sleep?"

"I sleep," I said.

"You sit with your eyes shut and your hands on your oar-stick and you listen."

"That *is* sleeping, where I come from."

"Where do you come from?"

"The Strait," I said, which was true in the way that a coffin is true to a man buried in it.

She considered this with the grave attention she gave everything — knots, beetles, my lies — and then let it go, which is more mercy than most adults have ever shown me. She went back to helping Brann lay the fire, chattering about the way the light came through the stone, and I sat with my back against my pack and did what I always did when the sun went down: I kept watch over nothing, because there was nothing left worth keeping watch over, and I had not yet learned how to stop.

Three nights out from Amberglass. The drowned archive still clung to us like river-smell — Silas kept touching their sleeve as if checking for mildew, and kept finding instead the folded map, and each time they found it their face did a small complicated thing I pretended not to see. We had a rite now. A rite with a price written into its last line that Silas thought they were hiding from us, and which I had already read upside down over their shoulder two nights ago, because I am a ferryman and reading things upside down is half my trade. The other half is knowing when people are lying, and Silas lied the way amateurs row upstream: with tremendous effort and no progress.

But that is a grief for another chapter of my life. This one belongs to the song.

* * *

The trouble with camping in Cael Sorrow, for those of you fortunate enough never to have done it, is that sound has weight here. Not metaphorically — actually. A shout lands on the air like a stone dropped in shallow water. A scream leaves a dent. And at night, when the wind comes down off the hills carrying all the sounds the day has shed, sleeping in the open means sleeping under weather made of noise. Brann called it "the night-market." She said it fondly. Brann says most terrible things fondly; it is her single worst quality and the reason I tolerate her.

Silas had been managing it with charms — small ones, quick ones, the kind a person makes without thinking, which should have told me everything about them years before it did. They'd hum a note into their cupped palm, press it flat, and set the little pressed silence on the ground around our camp like plates at a table. It worked. It also cost them something; by morning they were grey as old rope, and they hid it worse than they hid the rite.

On the third night, Wren watched them do it, frowned the frown of a seven-year-old confronting amateur work, and said:

"That's not how you're supposed to do it."

"No?" Silas said, mild. They were very good at mild. Fifteen years of practice, I'd have bet my boat.

"You're spending your own voice. That's why you're tired." She knelt and picked up one of the pressed silences, turning it in her fingers like a coin. "Mama said a song shared costs nothing. That's the whole point of a lullaby. You give it away and everyone who hears it helps hold it up while they sleep."

Brann looked up from the fire. "A lullaby that keeps watch?"

"A lullaby that *keeps*," Wren said, with the particular exasperation children reserve for adults failing to grasp the obvious. "It tucks the camp in. Nothing with bad intentions can cross it, because bad intentions sound wrong inside it and the song won't match them. Mama used to sing it over the whole village before the—" She stopped. Just stopped, mid-word, the way a bird stops when it hits glass.

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, the night-market murmured its wares.

"We'd like to hear it," Silas said quietly. "If you want to sing it."

Wren looked at each of us in turn — Brann, big and still by the flames; Silas with their careful face; me, I suppose, doing whatever my face does, which is less. Then she nodded once, businesslike, the way you nod before hauling on a rope, and began.

* * *

I want to be honest with you, since honesty is cheap at this distance: I had decided, somewhere between Amberglass and the third night out, exactly how much of my heart I was willing to risk on this expedition. The figure was small. I keep good ledgers. My daughter taught me arithmetic before she could write her own name, so that I would never be able to cheat myself without knowing it, which she announced was the point.

Her name was Lark.

There. It's out. It took eleven chapters' worth of walking and one child's lullaby, but there it is, sitting on the page where anyone can read it, and isn't that just like her — making me spend what I meant to hoard.

Lark was nine when the Cantor's first purge came through the river towns. This was years ago, before the edicts got polite, back when the Wardens of Song still worked with fire rather than paperwork. They came to our landing at dusk and asked who had been singing unsanctioned songs, and the whole village stood silent as stones, because we are a river people and we know when silence is the only boat that floats. And then Lark — nine years old, fearless the way only children and fools are fearless, and she was both — stepped forward and asked them why singing was a crime, in a clear voice, in front of everyone.

They took her voice first. There is an instrument for it; I heard it once, a sound like a door closing in a room you're standing in. And a child without a voice, in those days, did not stay a child long. The Cantor's law held that the voiceless were unfinished, unheld, liable to come apart. There was a place they took such people. There was no place they brought them back from.

I burned my ferry that winter. Did I mention that? The *Kindly Tide*, forty years of my father's line and mine, and I fed her to the flames on the shingle below the house, because a ferryman whose cargo includes his daughter's empty body does not need a ferry anymore; he needs a hole in the water shaped like a life. When the smoke cleared I bought a smaller boat with someone else's money and started calling myself cynical, which is cheaper than calling myself what I was.

All of which is to say: when Wren opened her mouth that night and sang the first three notes of a lullaby every mother on the southern rivers sang, I knew within a heartbeat that I was going to come apart, and I began, calmly and professionally, looking for somewhere to put the pieces.

* * *

The song itself — I'll tell you what I can, though songs on paper are like maps of water; accurate and useless.

It starts low, almost a hum, the way a mother talks to herself when she thinks no one's listening. Then it rises in a pattern that doesn't repeat so much as *return*, coming back to the same warm note the way a boat comes back to its mooring, and each time it returns it brings something new with it — a little run of notes like rain on a roof, a phrase that dips and lifts like someone breathing beside you. Wren sang it the way children sing, which is to say completely: no performance in it, no audience, just the song happening to her the way weather happens.

And the camp changed.

I don't have better words, and I've had days to look for them. The pressed silences Silas had laid out — those little coins of quiet — began to glow faintly, or perhaps they didn't glow, perhaps they simply became noticeable, the way a thing becomes noticeable when it stops being alone. The night-market wind reached the edge of our little ring of light and went around it, the way water goes around a stone. Inside the ring the air felt the way a harbor feels at dawn: held, deliberate, safe. Brann's shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time since I'd met her. Silas closed their eyes and their grey tiredness lifted like fog off a channel, and I understood that the song was doing for them what their charms had been doing at cost — being paid for, this time, by four voices instead of one.

Because that was the trick of it, the thing Wren had tried to explain and I hadn't grasped until I felt it: the lullaby wanted company. It left gaps. Little rests, little open doors in the melody, and if you breathed into them — you didn't even have to sing, just breathe, just let yourself be part of the holding — the song carried you too. It was a boat, is what it was. A boat built out of a bedtime song, and every sleeper aboard was crew.

I breathed into the gaps. I told myself it was tactical. Rest is a resource; I keep good ledgers.

And then Wren, in one of the rests, turned toward me — children always know, God help us all, children always know exactly where the wound is — and sang the second verse directly at me, the verse about the watcher at the water, the one that goes:

*Sleep, the river's keeping count,*
*sleep, the far shore isn't far,*
*and whoever waits there, waiting,*
*knows the way you are.*

My daughter used to sing that verse to me when I came home late off the tide. She'd stand on the shingle with her arms crossed like a tiny harbormaster and sing it at the boat until I laughed, and then she'd pretend she hadn't, because she was nine and dignity mattered.

I am fifty-one years old. I have been stabbed twice, capsized four times, and once spent a full day adrift sharing a skiff with a corpse and a crate of pickled eels, and I have never once cried in front of a living soul.

I cried in front of three.

* * *

Here is what I remember of it, in the order I remember it, which is not chronological: the fire, very bright. Wren's voice, still going, unhurried, wrapping around me like a mooring line. Brann's hand, enormous, landing on my shoulder with the delicacy of a woman setting down something breakable. Silas not looking at me, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done — giving me the dark side of their attention, the privacy of being ignored. And the song holding all of it, holding *me*, the way it held the camp, the way it had held villages before the purge, the way it was apparently built to hold exactly this: grief, out loud, in company, without the world ending.

That's the whole secret of the lullaby, I think, now that I've had time to turn it over. It's not a ward against monsters. It's a ward against the thing grief does when it's carried alone, which is eat the carrier. Sung solo, it's just a pretty tune. Sung together, it's a hull, and you can put anything in it.

Wren finished. The last note settled over the camp like snowfall. Nobody spoke.

Then Brann, because Brann cannot leave a silence unfilled any more than she can leave a stranger unhelped, said gruffly, "Your mama taught you well," and Wren said, "She said songs are just boats with the shape taken out," which is either nonsense or the wisest thing I've ever heard, and I started laughing, wetly, horribly, helplessly, and couldn't stop for a good minute, and nobody minded.

"Lark," I said finally. Because it seemed suddenly obscene that they didn't know, that I'd hauled the name around like ballast all these years with no one to show it to. "My daughter. Her name was Lark. She'd have liked you." I looked at Wren. "She'd have liked you entirely too much. You'd have been unbearable together."

Wren crawled over and sat against my side, matter-of-fact as a cat claiming a warm spot, and said, "You can hold the song with us whenever you want. That's how it works. The more people who know it, the stronger it gets. Mama said that's why the Cantor hates lullabies most of all."

"Why's that?" Brann asked.

"Because you can't arrest a song once everybody knows it," Wren said, as if reporting the weather. "It's already everywhere. It's already in everybody. He can take the singers but the song just — waits in them. Like seeds."

Silas had gone very still by the fire. I watched them look at Wren — really look, the way a cartographer looks at terrain that has just refused to match the map — and I saw something kindle behind their eyes that I would learn, later, to call hope, and to fear, because hope in a Realmwright is a dangerous instrument. But that night I only saw a tired person hearing, for the first time in fifteen years, a reason to be tired.

"Sing it again," Silas said. "Teach me the second verse properly. If we're going to Corvenne—" they glanced at the dark beyond the firelight, toward a capital none of us could see and all of us could feel, humming faintly on the horizon like a struck bell that wouldn't finish dying away— "we should arrive knowing how to hold each other up."

So we sang it again. All of us, badly, except Wren, who conducted us with a stick and infinite patience, correcting Brann's timing ("you're rushing, ferries rush, lullabies don't") and Silas's precision ("it's not supposed to be *right*, it's supposed to be *together*") and me — me she didn't correct at all. She just leaned a little harder against my side on the second verse, and let me sing the watcher-at-the-water lines alone, and the camp held us both up, and the night-market wind went around and around outside our little harbor of light, finding no purchase, carrying nothing away.

We slept. Actually slept — all four of us, deep and dreamless and guarded, tucked in by a song older than the Cantor's crown. For the first time since I burned my ferry, I woke in the morning without having counted anything.

* * *

It was Silas who ended it, near midday, with the map spread flat on a boulder and all of us crowded round. They'd gone quiet in the way they go quiet before saying something they've rehearsed, and I braced, because I knew — I'd known since Amberglass — what was coming off that page eventually.

"The next waypoint," Silas said, tracing a line of blood-dark ink that seemed, even now, even dry, to move slightly under their finger, "isn't a place. Not exactly." Their finger stopped. "It's a road. The Road of Teeth. And according to the map's margins—" a pause, the careful face flickering— "the Cantor's enforcers patrol it. The mute ones. In numbers."

Brann cracked her knuckles, which is her way of saying *good*. Wren, practical as tide-tables, asked how far the road was from water, in case we needed a boat that didn't exist. And I stood there with a lullaby lodged in my chest like a splinter of warmth, thinking about seeds, and songs that wait in people, and how the Cantor had built an empire on the theory that a silenced voice stays silent forever —

— and how my daughter's voice had just proved him wrong, nine years after he stole it, sung back to me out of the mouth of a stranger's child on a road I didn't want to walk.

"Well," I said, and picked up my pack. "Let's go teach the mute ones a song."
