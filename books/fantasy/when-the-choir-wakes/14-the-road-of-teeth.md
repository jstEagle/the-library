## 14. The Road of Teeth

I want it on the record, before anything else happens, that I did not want to be on this road.

The Road of Teeth runs north from the last of the humming villages toward Corvenne, and it is called the Road of Teeth for reasons that become obvious about a mile in and then never stop being obvious. The hills on either side are white stone, worn into ridges and cusps by wind that has been chewing at them since before the Cantor learned his first note. Walking between them is like walking down the throat of something enormous and patient. The locals — the ones who still talk, anyway — say the road was named by a ferryman who owed money everywhere, which is the kind of historical detail I find personally slanderous and therefore believe completely.

Silas walked at the front with the map open across both palms, the way you'd carry a sleeping animal. Brann walked behind them with her oar over her shoulder like she was bored, which is Brann's version of standing watch. Wren skipped. Actually skipped, heel to toe, humming under her breath in that threadbare way she had, half a melody held together with spit and stubbornness.

"You're going to wear a hole in the ground," I told her.

"Good," she said. "Then there'll be two holes and they can be friends."

There was no arguing with that. There was no arguing with any of it, really. That's the thing nobody tells you about traveling with people: eventually you stop arguing, not because they're right, but because arguing has started to feel like something you'd miss.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a hill that looked, from certain angles, disturbingly like a molar. Silas built the fire small — smaller than warmth strictly required — because the map had been twitching all afternoon, its ink crawling like ants disturbed under a stone.

"It knows we're close to something," Silas said, holding it near the light. "Or something's close to us."

"Comforting," I said. "Which is it?"

"I don't think maps make that distinction."

Brann spat into the fire, an old ferrywoman's benediction meaning roughly *may whatever comes be slower than me*. "Then we sleep in shifts," she said. "Ovid, you take first."

"Why me?"

"Because you complain loudest when you're tired, and if you sleep now you'll have nothing to complain about later, and then what would we even listen to?"

Wren laughed so hard she fell off the rock she was sitting on. I want to state clearly that I did not smile. There were witnesses. There is no proof.

So I took first watch, which suited me fine. I've always liked the hours when everyone else is asleep and the world admits, quietly, how strange it is. In Cael Sorrow the strangeness is louder than most places. Sound has weight here — real weight, weight you can lean against — and at night, with the fire down to embers, I could hear the silence coming off the hills like cold rolling off ice. It pooled in the low places. It sat on the road like fog.

And underneath the silence, faint as a heartbeat through a hull, something else. A rhythm. Regular. Patient.

I told myself it was my own pulse. I am very good at telling myself things. It's how I've stayed alive this long: I once convinced myself a sinking boat was merely enthusiastic about becoming a submarine.

But the rhythm didn't match mine. Mine was fast, because I was scared. This one was slow. Unhurried. Like footsteps. Like something walking toward us at the pace of a creature that already knew where we were.

I woke Brann without a word. She came up out of sleep the way she does everything — instantly, silently, and with one hand already closed around the nearest heavy object. I pointed east, where the silence was thickest.

She listened for ten seconds. Then she put her mouth next to my ear and whispered, so quiet it was barely breath:

"How many?"

"That's the problem," I breathed back. "It sounds like one. But it's too steady. Nothing that big walks that evenly unless it isn't breathing."

Her eyes went flat and bright, the way water goes before it freezes. She touched Wren's shoulder, and the girl surfaced from sleep without a sound — a child raised in a country where sound is currency learns not to spend it carelessly — and then Silas, whose hand went straight to the map.

"They've found us," Silas whispered.

"The mute ones?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. Children shouldn't be able to do that. Somebody should file a complaint.

"The enforcers," Silas said. "The Cantor takes singers who break the edict and he — takes the song out. All of it. What's left walks and obeys." They swallowed. "They hunt by listening for sound. Any sound. Even heartbeats, the old stories say, if they get close enough."

"Wonderful," I said. "A monster that finds you by the fact that you're alive. You know, in my line of work we call that a client."

Nobody laughed. Tough crowd.

* * *

Here is what I know about ambushes, having survived several and caused at least two: the trap is never the dangerous part. The trap is just furniture. What kills you is your own surprise — the half-second where your body insists this cannot be happening while the world cheerfully demonstrates otherwise. Everything I did in the next few minutes, I did in that half-second, stretched out by terror into something almost roomy.

They came up the road from the east, out of the pooled silence, and I will tell you honestly that my eyes took longer than my ears to accept them. They looked like people. That was the worst of it. Tall, gray-cloaked figures, five of them, moving with that terrible even stride, and their faces —

Their faces were the faces of people who had been asked a question once, a long time ago, and were still waiting for permission to answer.

No mouths moving. No breath fogging the cold air. And around each of them, a bubble of that dead silence, so complete that where they walked, the sound of their own footsteps simply wasn't. They moved through the world like holes cut out of it.

"They can't hear," Silas breathed beside me. We'd killed the fire with sand and pressed ourselves against the molar-hill's flank. "That's the mercy in it. The Cantor hollows them out past hearing. They track vibration. Movement. Song, most of all — song carries for miles here, it — "

"Silas," Brann whispered. "The point. Get to the point."

"The point is they felt Wren's humming. Yesterday. On the road. It carried."

Everyone looked at Wren. Wren, to her eternal credit, looked less guilty than thoughtful, which in a nine-year-old is either wisdom or trouble, and in my experience the two arrive in the same coat.

"So we don't sing," Brann said. "We don't hum. We don't breathe loud. We go around, quiet as drowned men, and we're gone before — "

The nearest figure stopped.

Stopped dead, mid-stride, in the middle of the road, and turned its head toward our hill with the smooth, jointless patience of a weathervane finding wind.

"— or they've already heard my heartbeat," Brann finished, "in which case, run."

We ran.

* * *

I have ferried soldiers, smugglers, one king in disguise, and a bride fleeing her own wedding, and I will tell you the truth about flight: it is not dignified. It is lungs and luck and the animal part of you making decisions your mind ratifies afterward. We crashed downslope through scrub and white stone, the five gray shapes fanning out above us with that unhurried stride, and the horrible thing — the thing I still think about — was that they weren't chasing. Chasing implies urgency. They were *converging*, the way water converges on a drain, because they knew exactly where we were and they knew exactly where we could go, and neither of those facts included anywhere.

The Road of Teeth narrowed ahead into a defile between two great cusps of stone. Brann saw it the same moment I did, and I saw her do the arithmetic — narrow ground means no escape, narrow ground means cornered — and then I watched her do the thing that makes Brann Brann.

She reversed the sum.

"Narrow's good," she snarled, more to herself than to us, and grabbed Wren by the collar. "Narrow means they come one at a time. Narrow means a wall at our backs. Ovid! The oar!"

I threw it. She caught it without looking, which I intend to bring up in every story I tell for the rest of my life.

We hit the defile and turned, backs to the stone, and the first of them came around the cusp with its arms slightly open, the way you'd approach something you intended to pick up. Up close its face was worse — weathered like old wood, eyes the color of skimmed milk, and that mouth, closed and quiet and waiting.

Brann hit it with the oar across the knees.

I heard the crack of bone. What I did not hear — what none of us heard, though all five of us flinched hard enough to bruise ourselves on the air — was any sound from it. No cry. No grunt. It went down like a felled tree, silent from impact to ground, and the silence where its pain should have been was somehow louder than screaming.

"They don't feel it," Silas said. Their voice had gone thin. "Ovid, they don't — hurting them doesn't stop them, they'll just — "

The second one came. Then the third, stepping over its kneeling fellow without a glance. Brann swung again; the oar splintered on a raised arm; a gray hand closed on her wrist with a grip like a mooring cleat, and I did the only thing left in my inventory, which was to throw myself bodily onto the third one's back and hang on like debt.

It didn't stagger. Of course it didn't. It simply reached back, unhurried, fingers finding my collar, and began — patiently, terribly — to peel me off like a barnacle.

And in that moment, wedged against its shoulder, my ear inches from its hood, I heard it.

Not sound. The absence arranged into shape. Inside each of these creatures, beneath the hollow, there was a residue — a single note, worn down to a nub, repeating over and over like a word someone can't stop saying. The note they'd been singing when the Cantor took them. The last thing they'd ever said.

This one's was high and sweet. A child's note.

I know that note. I have that note. My daughter's last song ended on nearly the same pitch, and I have spent nine years keeping it in a locked room in my chest, and in that half-second hanging off the enforcer's back, the locked room blew open and I understood — with a clarity that felt like drowning — that every one of these gray things was somebody's Wren. Somebody's daughter, emptied out and sent walking.

"Hold them," Brann was shouting. "Just hold them, thirty seconds, I need thirty seconds — "

For what? I had time to wonder. And then I heard it.

A whistle.

Thin, sharp, three notes rising — stolen notes, lifted months ago from a Warden's patrol-call outside Amberglass, notes Wren had practiced under her breath for days until Silas made her stop and she'd only pretended to. Three notes that meant, in the grammar of the Cantor's own patrols: *all clear, nothing here, move along.*

She stood at the mouth of the defile with her chin up and her small fists at her sides, and she whistled the enemy's own language at them, clear as a bell across the stones.

The hands holding me stopped.

All of them. Five gray heads turned toward her, milk-pale eyes fixing on that small defiant figure, and for three heartbeats the whole ambush hung there, suspended, a machine waiting on an instruction it had been built to obey.

Wren whistled the signal again. Then — and this is the part I will carry to my grave, the part I'll tell wrong on purpose in taverns because the true version is too much — she added a fourth note. One note of her own. A little upward flick, sweet and insolent, stitched onto the end of the stolen call like a signature. Like a girl signing her work.

*Move along,* said the whistle. *Nothing here.*

*Nothing here except me,* said the fourth note. *And I'm not afraid of you.*

The enforcers turned. All five, in that same smooth weathervane motion, and walked away up the Road of Teeth, unhurried, obedient, empty, until the pooled silence of them drained eastward and the night's ordinary sounds crept back in — wind, stone, my own ragged breathing, and Brann laughing, actually laughing, low and disbelieving, with blood on her split knuckles and her broken oar in pieces at her feet.

* * *

We didn't sleep again that night. We sat in the defile with our backs against the stone and passed a skin of cider around, and nobody spoke for a long time, which after a night like that is its own kind of conversation.

It was Brann who finally broke it, and she broke it looking at Wren.

"That fourth note," she said. "You made it up."

Wren shrugged, in the way of children pretending shrugs cost nothing. "It needed an ending. The Warden's whistle just — stops. It sounded unfinished." Another shrug. "Everything should get an ending."

I looked away and studied the stars and thought about locked rooms and nubs of songs and how the difference between an enforcer and a child might be, in the end, exactly one invented note. Something in my chest was doing work I hadn't authorized. Grief, maybe. Or its opposite. With me the two share plumbing.

Silas had the map out again, turning it toward the ember-light, and I watched their face change.

"What," Brann said.

"The ink." Silas tilted it toward us. Where the road ahead had been blank for weeks — a stretch of unmapped white that had driven our cartographer quietly mad — new lines were surfacing, dark and sure, drawing themselves like something writing through the paper from behind. The Road of Teeth. The defile. And beyond it, far to the north, a shape rising out of the blankness in tall, crooked spires.

Corvenne. The capital. The Cantor's seat.

And below the city's name, in handwriting I recognized — because I'd seen it on a dying man's lips and in a child's stubborn face — fresh letters surfacing one by one:

*You are five now. The song needs five.*

We looked at each other. Four of us, plus a map that counted.

"Well," said Brann, and cracked her knuckles, which is her way of saying *good*. "Let's go give him a sixth."
