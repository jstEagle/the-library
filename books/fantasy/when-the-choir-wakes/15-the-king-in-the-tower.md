## 15. The King in the Tower

Corvenne announced itself the way a funeral announces itself: with bells.

Not ringing — that would have been too honest. These bells *hummed*, a low sustained note that rolled across the plain for miles before we could see the walls, and I felt it in my teeth the way you feel a storm coming through a bad filling. Brann felt it somewhere worse. She'd been quiet since dawn, and quiet-for-Brann was its own kind of weather warning.

"That's not a bell," she said finally. "That's a leash."

"It's a chord," Wren said. She was riding on Ovid's cart with her legs swinging over the tailboard like this was an outing to a fair, which was either courage or the particular blindness of eleven-year-olds, and I had long since stopped trying to determine which. "The whole city's holding one note. Listen — nobody's singing over it."

I listened. She was right, and the rightness of it made my skin crawl. Every village we'd passed since Low Hum had hummed constantly — people holding their shapes together with little threads of song, mending their fences with melody, arguing in harmony. But Corvenne was silent except for that one note, and silence in Cael Sorrow wasn't emptiness. It was weight. It fell on us as we crested the last hill like snow falling upward, pressing against my ears, my eyes, the soft places behind my sternum.

"Everyone in that city is being held," I said. "Like specimens under glass."

"Cheerful," said Ovid, flicking his reins. "And here I'd hoped the capital would be festive."

The map was warm against my chest. It had been warm for two days now, ever since we'd crossed the border stones into what the old margins called *the Cantor's Reach*, and it had developed the disconcerting habit of tugging — gently, insistently, like a dog on a lead who has smelled home. I'd taken to wearing it inside my coat, partly to keep it hidden from the road's occasional patrols of Wardens in their grey livery, and partly because I didn't trust myself not to look at it every ten minutes. A map that wants something is a dangerous companion. I speak with some authority on the subject, having been one for most of my life.

* * *

We came down to the river gate at dusk, when the light does cities the favor of making them beautiful whether they deserve it or not. Corvenne deserved it, once. You could see the bones of it — the great arched bridges, the terraced gardens climbing toward the citadel, the towers with their singing-stones still set into the cornices like teeth in an old jaw. But everything was grey at the edges, worn smooth, as though the city itself had been sanded down by that single held note. The gardens grew in perfect, joyless rows. The people in the streets moved without speaking, mouths closed, throats working faintly as they hummed the sanctioned chord beneath their breath. Even the gulls were silent. Gulls. Silent. I have seen many wrong things in two worlds, and that is high on the list.

"No market noise," Brann murmured. "No arguments. No children shrieking." Her hand found Wren's shoulder and stayed there, which was becoming a habit, and which neither of them seemed inclined to examine.

"They're allowed to hum," Wren said. "Just not anything else. Papa said—" She stopped. We all let her stop. Her father, the stranger who had bled out on my shop floor with a map pressed between us like a covenant, had said a great many things, apparently, and each one surfaced now like a body in a pond. "Papa said the king used to sing with the people. From the tower. On festival days, everyone in Corvenne would sing back up at him, and he'd sing again, louder, and it would go all night."

"And now?" Ovid asked, though his voice suggested he already knew.

"Now there's just the one note."

We found lodging of a sort in a caravanserai near the river gate, run by a woman with a face like a closed ledger who took Ovid's coin and asked no questions, which in Corvenne was less hospitality than survival strategy. The room was small and smelled of tallow and wet wool. We ate bread and hard cheese and did not talk much, because talking meant raising our voices above the chord, and raising your voice in Corvenne felt like shouting in a sickroom.

It was Wren who couldn't bear it. Of course it was Wren.

"I want to see the tower," she said. "Tonight. Before the Wardens change shift — Papa said they change at moonrise and the streets go empty for a quarter hour."

"Wren—" Brann began.

"You came all this way to see him." She looked at each of us in turn, and her chin was set in a way that I recognized with a sinking feeling, because I'd last seen it on her father's face as he died. "He's been in there fifteen years. Fifteen years humming someone else's note. And we're going to walk past his own front door?"

Silence, of the ordinary kind, which after the city's kind felt almost luxurious.

"She's right," I said, and heard Brann exhale through her nose in the manner of a woman cataloguing grievances for later use. "We should see it. All of it. If we're going to do what the map says, I need to know what we're walking into, and maps lie about palaces. Palaces are where maps go to flatter themselves."

"A cartographer insulting cartography," Ovid said. "There's hope for you yet."

* * *

The quarter hour after moonrise was exactly as Wren promised: the Wardens of Song changed watch, and the streets emptied as if a hand had wiped them clean. We went up through the terraces in the dark, four shadows among shadows, and the chord grew stronger as we climbed until it wasn't sound anymore but pressure, a hand laid flat against the whole city's chest.

The citadel rose above the last terrace like a held breath. Its walls were pale stone shot through with veins of something darker — singing-stone, the same mineral the villages used to hold their songs, but here it was black, dead, drinking light instead of giving it. And rising from the heart of it stood the tower: tall, slender, crowned with an open gallery where, if Wren's stories were true, a king had once sung to his people all night long.

Now the gallery windows glowed with a cold white light, and the chord poured out of them like smoke.

We crouched in the shadow of a garden wall — the joyless garden, roses trained into geometries no rose would choose — and looked up. This close, I could see figures moving behind the gallery's windows. Slender shapes, robed, swaying slightly, in time with the note. The Cantor's choir, or its wardens, or its instruments; I didn't know which word fit and suspected none of them did.

And then, in the highest window — smaller than the others, set apart, half-hidden behind a screen of blackened stone —

A face.

I saw him the way you see a face in a crowd you've been told not to hope about: first as shape, then as detail, then all at once as a person, and the world reorganizes itself around the fact of him. He was gaunt. That was the first thing. Fifteen years of whatever was happening in that tower had carved him down to architecture — cheekbones like buttresses, a throat corded with the effort of holding a note that was not his own. His hair had gone white at the temples though his face was not old enough to earn it. He wore no crown. Whatever the Cantor wore publicly, the man himself wore a plain grey robe, and his hands were folded in his lap like a man at his own funeral who has decided to be polite about it.

His eyes were open. And they were *looking at us.*

I want to be precise about this moment, because everything afterward depends on it, and precision is the only craft I've ever truly owned. There was no reason he should have seen us. We were four shadows against a black wall, forty feet below and across a garden, on a moonless night in a city that had forgotten how to make noise. But his gaze came down and settled on us with the terrible accuracy of a lighthouse, and I understood — with the whole-body certainty of a man who has spent his life reading terrain — that this was not accident. Something in him had been waiting at that window the way a drowning man waits at a surface. Not hoping. Hoping takes practice. Just *waiting*, the way stone waits.

His lips moved.

No sound came out. Of course no sound came out; the chord owned every vibration in that tower, and whatever was left of King Aldous lived in the cracks between notes. But I read his mouth the way I'd learned to read contour lines — patiently, hungrily, line by line.

*hur*

Then again, slower, deliberate, each syllable shaped like a man carving his name into a cell wall:

*hur-ry.*

Brann's breath caught beside me. Wren made a small sound, half gasp, half sob, and clapped both hands over her own mouth — the first time in all our weeks together I had known her to silence herself voluntarily. Ovid went very still, in the way of a man whose cynicism has just been presented with a bill it cannot dispute.

And then the lucidity went out of the king's eyes like a lamp guttering. I watched it happen — watched the man recede and something else surface in him, something smoother, his features settling into an expression of mild, vacant contentment that was so profoundly *wrong* on a human face that my stomach turned over. His head tilted. His lips began to move again, shaping the chord, humming along with the thing that wore his crown. His hands unfolded and rose slightly, conducting nothing, or conducting everything.

"Hurry," Brann whispered. Her voice shook. Brann, who had faced mute enforcers on the Road of Teeth with a boat hook and a blasphemy, and whose hands had not shaken then. "Did you — tell me you saw—"

"I saw."

"He *saw* us. He knew. For a second he was *in there*—"

"For a second," I said, "he was the only real thing in this entire city."

Wren lowered her hands. Her face was wet and furious, an expression I was beginning to understand was simply her face, and she stared up at the window where the king swayed gently, conducting his own imprisonment.

"That's what Papa died for," she said. Flatly. Not a question. "Getting the map to us. So we'd hurry."

The words landed in the middle of our little group of shadows and sat there, heavy as an anchor. I thought of the stranger bleeding out across my threshold, spending his last breath on two words addressed to a charm-seller he'd never met. *Finish it.* I had assumed he meant the map. Standing in the dark below that tower, watching a king conduct his own cage, I understood he had meant something larger and much more expensive.

"We should go," Ovid said quietly. "Moonrise quarter's nearly spent, and the new watch comes down the terrace road. I've smuggled past enough Wardens to know the difference between bold and bait."

Nobody moved.

"Ovid's right," Brann said, and took Wren's shoulder again. "We go. We plan. We come back." She said it like a promise, and like a threat, and knowing Brann it was both.

We withdrew through the gardens, low and fast, and I went last, because I am a coward in the ways that matter and honest enough to admit it. At the edge of the wall I looked back once.

The king was still at the window. Still swaying. Still humming. But his head had turned, very slightly, tracking us as we slipped away into the dark — and though his eyes were vacant again, though the Cantor's chord had clearly reclaimed whatever territory it had briefly lost, his hands had stopped conducting.

They were folded in his lap again. Patient. Waiting.

* * *

Back in the caravanserai room, we lit no lamps. We sat in the dark around the table like conspirators, which was what we were, and the chord thrummed through the floorboards and up through the chair legs into our spines, so that even sitting still we were all of us vibrating slightly, tuned to the enemy's pitch.

"The rite needs the palace," Brann said at last. "Amberglass gave us the words. The words need the binding's anchor, and the anchor's in that tower. So we go back in. Through the front door, the river gates, or a hole in the wall — but we go in."

"With what army?" Ovid asked. "Four of us. One stolen whistle-song, one boat hook, one map with opinions, and one—" he gestured at me with the bread knife, not unkindly, "—walking war crime."

"One chorus," Wren said.

We all looked at her.

"Low Hum," she said. "The rebels. They've been teaching songs for years — Papa said so, before he left. Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe, by now, if the villages passed them on like he hoped." Her eyes were bright and terrible in the dark. "You don't need an army. You need everyone singing the same thing at the same time. That's what an army *is*, in this country."

The chord pulsed through the floor, patient, vast, certain of itself. And against my chest, under my coat, the map warmed — and pulled.

Not gently, this time. Not the dog-on-a-lead tug I'd grown used to. This was a current, a riptide, the map straining toward the tower like iron toward a lodestone, and through my shirt I could feel the ink shifting, rearranging itself, pages I hadn't opened stirring like sleeping birds. I pressed my palm flat against it and felt, distinctly, unmistakably, a rhythm underneath the warmth.

Two beats. Then three. Repeating.

Hurry. Hurry.

Either the map had learned to speak, or the king's urgency had soaked into its ink through forty feet of stone and fifteen years of silence, and I honestly did not know which possibility frightened me more.

"Silas?" Brann's voice, sharp. "Your face is doing something."

"My face," I said, "is doing arithmetic." I kept my hand on the map and my voice steady, which was the hardest piece of charm-work I'd done in fifteen years. "How fast can a message reach Low Hum?"

Ovid smiled, slow and crooked, and for the first time since I'd met him there was nothing wry in it at all.

"Ferryman's tide turns at midnight," he said. "If we're quick, we can catch it going out."
