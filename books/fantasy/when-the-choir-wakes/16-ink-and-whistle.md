## 16. Ink and Whistle

I want it on the record that I said we shouldn't go to the market.

Not in those words, exactly. What I actually said was, "Wren, if you so much as hum in that square I will carry you out of there by your ankles," and she'd looked at me with those enormous amber eyes and said, "I know how to be quiet, Brann. I've been quiet my whole life."

That's the thing about children. They lie the way rivers lie — sincerely, and with no idea they're doing it.

We'd been in Corvenne two days. Two days of pressing ourselves against walls while the city went about its business with its throat clamped shut. Corvenne was worse than the villages. In Low Hum, people still risked a note under their breath, a tapped rhythm on a table leg, the small rebellions of a people who hadn't yet been fully broken. But here, in the shadow of the palace, silence wasn't just law. It was fashion. It was piety. People walked through the streets with their lips pressed white, and when a cart rattled over cobblestones too loudly, they flinched like it was blasphemy.

Silas had gone to find Ovid's contact — a woman named Hess who ran a dye shop and owed the ferryman more favors than he'd admit. That left me with Wren, which was either an honor or a punishment, depending on the hour.

"We just need thread," Wren had said. "And salt. And you said my boots are falling apart, and you're right, they are, because you keep making me walk everywhere."

"I make you walk everywhere because walking is quiet."

"Everything's quiet here," she'd muttered. "That's the whole problem."

So we went to the market. And I want to be fair to myself: I watched her. I watched her the way a hawk watches a field mouse, except the mouse was smarter than my hawk, because somewhere between the fishmonger and the cloth stall, Wren heard something.

Later, Silas would piece it together from what witnesses stammered out before the Wardens cleared the square. A street sweeper. An old man, sweeping the flagstones near the fountain, and as he swept he was humming. Barely humming. Three notes, maybe four, worn down to almost nothing, the way a stone gets worn by water. He probably didn't even know he was doing it. Old men forget. Old men have been humming since before the Edict, and some songs live deeper than fear.

But Wren knew the song.

It was a lullaby. *Her* lullaby — or close enough to it, one of the hundred small variations that mothers in the river country used to sing, back before singing got you taken. The song she'd taught us on the road outside Amberglass, the one that let us sleep safe in the open with the dark listening. The song Ovid had cried to, though he'd deny it until his dying day and possibly after it.

She heard three notes of her mother's music rising out of a stranger's broom-handle, and she did what any child would do.

She answered.

* *

I was haggling over bootlaces when the sound hit me.

You have to understand what sound means in Cael Sorrow. It has weight. It has color. In the villages, a held note could keep a person's shape together; under the Cantor's law, an unsanctioned song could unmake them. So when a child's voice lifted above the market stalls — clear, bright, *unafraid*, carrying a melody that half the crowd must have known in their bones — it was like someone struck a bell inside a church where bells had been forbidden for fifteen years.

Every head turned. Every face went through the same terrible sequence: recognition, longing, terror. I saw an old woman mouth the words without letting any air past her teeth. I saw a man grab his son's shoulder hard enough to bruise. I saw the whole market remember, all at once, what it had lost.

And then I saw Wren, standing on the lip of the fountain with her ruined boots and her chin up, singing the second verse like she had every right to it, like the Edict was a rumor she couldn't be bothered to believe.

My blood went cold and then it went hot, and I was moving before I'd decided to move, shoving through the crowd, screaming her name in my head because I knew better than to scream it out loud—

The Wardens of Song came fast. They always do. They wear grey, and their faces are wrapped, and they don't shout, because shouting is sound and they've made their peace with that particular hypocrisy. They move like weather. One moment the market was a market; the next, six grey shapes were converging on the fountain, and the crowd was peeling away from Wren like flesh from a wound, everyone suddenly very interested in their own feet.

I got within ten paces. I'll tell you that now, because I need you to know I tried. Ten paces, and a Warden turned and looked at me — not at my face, at my *throat*, the way you'd look at a weapon — and something in the way it stood told me that ten paces was exactly as far as I was going to get, unless I wanted to be carried off beside her.

A hand closed on my arm. Hess, the dyer, indigo-stained to the elbow, her voice a breath against my ear: "Not here. Not now. You'll die for nothing and she'll still be taken."

I let her hold me. That's the sentence I've been chewing on ever since. *I let her hold me.* Fifteen years I ferried people across the Sighing Strait, and I never once let anything hold me that I didn't choose — and there I stood, held, watching a girl I'd known for three weeks get bound at the wrists with grey cord and led away, and the worst part, the part that keeps me up at night, is that she didn't cry out.

She looked for me. Over the shoulders of the Wardens, across the emptied square, her eyes swept the crowd once, twice — and found mine. And she lifted her chin, the same stubborn angle she'd used on the fountain, and she *smiled*. Like to say: *don't you dare look like that, Brann. This isn't how my story goes.*

Then the grey swallowed her, and the old sweeper was face-down on the flagstones with a Warden's knee in his back, and the market resumed around the wound in it, haggling and flinching and pretending nothing had happened, because pretending is the only song Corvenne knows anymore.

* *

Silas found me sitting on the steps of the dye shop, and I must have looked bad, because they sat down without a word and let the silence do its work. Silas is good at silence. It's the one thing about them that fits this realm.

"They took her," I said.

"I know." Their voice was flat, but their hands weren't. They were turning the map's folded edge over and over, worrying it like a scab. "Hess sent word. The Wardens' processional goes to the palace. There's a holding place beneath it — Ovid called it the Quiet Cells, back when—"

"When he still talked about his daughter." I heard my own voice come out like a blade and didn't care. "Yes. I remember."

Silas flinched. Good, some ugly part of me said. Flinch. We should all be flinching.

For a while neither of us spoke. The dye shop smelled of mordant and wet wool, and somewhere down the street a crier was announcing tomorrow's grain prices in the toneless official drone that passed, in Corvenne, for public speech.

"It's my fault," Silas said finally.

"Don't." 

"If I'd been faster with the map, if we'd gone straight to the palace instead of waiting for Hess's contact, if I hadn't needed another day to—"

"*Don't.*" I stood up, and my voice cracked out louder than I meant it to, loud enough that a passerby flinched and hurried on. I lowered it to a hiss. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to stand there and make it yours. I was *there*, Silas. I was ten paces away. I watched her sing and I watched them take her and I did nothing, because a woman I met yesterday squeezed my arm and I *let her*. So no. This one's mine. You can have the guilt about everything else — gods know you've got a warehouse full — but this one is mine."

Silas looked at me for a long moment. Under the grime and the careful nothing-expression, something moved in their face — not quite gratitude. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone finding out their shame has company.

"The plan hasn't changed," they said quietly. "The palace. The rite. We were always going in."

"Now we're going in angry."

"Is that worse?"

I thought about it honestly, the way I think about most things: slowly, and with an eye toward the tide. "Angry people make mistakes," I said. "But angry people also don't stop. I've been afraid since the day we fell into this world. Fear makes you careful, and careful was fine when it was just us staying alive. Careful doesn't get Wren back."

* *

Ovid arrived at dusk, and he brought the map of himself with him.

That's how I've come to think of Ovid — a man who is, underneath the cynicism and the ledger-keeping and the relentless arithmetic of debts, a map of every loss he's ever refused to look at directly. He came into the dye shop's back room smelling of the strait and cheap spirits, took one look at my face, and said, "Who died?"

"Worse. They took Wren."

Something happened to Ovid's face then. It was quick — a shutter slamming, a door closing on a room I wasn't meant to see — but I've spent years reading passengers, and I saw it. Behind the shutters, a man went pale.

"The market," he said. Not a question.

"She sang." My voice caught on it. "An old man was humming her lullaby and she *answered*, Ovid. She stood on a fountain and sang her mother's song in the middle of the capital, and I was ten paces away and I did *nothing*."

"You did the smart thing."

"The smart thing." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Is that what you told yourself? When they took your Mara? That you'd done the smart thing, staying in the boat, keeping your head down, surviving?"

The room went very still. Silas froze mid-motion, the map half-raised. I knew I'd crossed a line the moment I said it — I'd promised myself I'd never use that girl's name as a weapon — but grief makes a blunt instrument of everything, and I was done being careful.

Ovid set down his satchel. Slowly. The way a man sets down something heavy when he's decided not to throw it.

"Yes," he said. "That is exactly what I told myself. Every day for nine years. And do you know what it bought me?" He spread his hands, palms up, the ferryman's gesture, the *see for yourself* gesture. "A boat. A ledger. A very fine collection of debts owed to me by people who are all, without exception, still alive, which is more than I can say for anyone I've loved." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. It went quieter and quieter, like water going down. "So yes, Brann. Be angry. Anger is the only honest currency left in this country. Just make sure you spend it on something, because I hoarded mine for nine years and it bought me nothing but interest."

He turned to Silas. "The processional takes the Wardens' Road to the River Gate. From there, down. The Quiet Cells are below the waterline — the Cantor likes its prisoners where their voices can't carry, though that's superstition; stone is stone. There's an access. Old ferry-tunnel, pre-Cantor, from when the palace drew its water from the Amal. My people cut it. Sealed it after the first purge, but seals are just doors that haven't met the right bribe."

"You know someone at the River Gate," Silas said.

"I know everyone at the River Gate. I've smuggled half of them brandy and the other half their mistresses." Ovid pulled out his ledger — that cursed, beloved book — and began, with terrible calm, to write. "Here's what we'll need. Here's what it costs. Here's who owes me enough to look away, and here's who'll need to be unconscious instead." He glanced up, and for one unguarded moment the cynic's mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was just a father, nine years drowned. "They kept her alive, Brann. The ones they take — they don't kill them. They *keep* them. I used to think that was mercy."

"And now?" Silas asked.

"Now I think the Cantor collects voices the way I collect debts." He snapped the ledger shut. "And I intend to call in every favor I have."

* *

We planned until the candles drowned in their own wax.

I'll give the details short shrift, because plans are like boats: the drawing of them is pleasant, and the water is always different. The shape of it was this — Ovid's tunnel, Ovid's bribes, Ovid's list of names recited in a voice gone flat with purpose. Silas bent over the map for hours, tracing routes that shifted under their finger like eels, muttering about waypoints and thin places, and twice I caught them looking at the page where the palace was drawn and touching it gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you hoped wouldn't wake.

Me, I sharpened things. It's what I'm for. Brann the ferrywoman, Brann the practical one, Brann who keeps the rope coiled and the knife keen while the clever ones think. I sharpened and I listened and I burned.

Because here's what I learned about rage, that night, in a dyer's back room that smelled of indigo and defeat: rage is just love with nowhere to go. Fifteen years I'd kept my head down in Vell, ferrying, surviving, minding my own heart the way you mind a purse in a pickpocket's crowd. And in one afternoon, a child with ruined boots had taught me that a heart you never spend is just weight. She'd sung in a silent square because some songs are bigger than fear. And if she could stand on that fountain with the whole grey machine of Corvenne coming down on her, then I could walk into the machine itself and take her back out.

I owe her that lesson. I intend to pay it forward with interest — Ovid's word, but it fits.

Near midnight, Silas looked up from the map, and their face in the candlelight was the face of someone who has stopped asking whether a thing is wise and started asking only whether it's possible.

"There's a waypoint," they said. "Inside the palace. The map wants us there anyway — it's been pulling toward Corvenne since Amberglass. If the tunnel fails, if the bribes fail, the map may still find us a door." They hesitated. "There's something else. The ink's changed. Since the market. Look."

I leaned over. The blood-dark lines of the map had shifted — I'd seen them crawl before, but this was different. New markings bloomed around the palace's footprint, fine and furious, like veins standing up in a clenched fist. And at the center, where the throne room should be, a single word had surfaced in handwriting I recognized as Silas's own, though they swore they hadn't written it:

*Find her first.*

"Maps don't give orders," I said.

"This one does." Silas closed their eyes briefly. "It's Realmwright work. It remembers what it's for. And right now—" their voice roughened— "right now it agrees with you."

Outside, Corvenne lay silent under its curfew, a city holding its breath. Somewhere beneath the waterline, in a cell built to swallow sound, a girl who sang anyway was waiting to see who'd come for her.

I coiled the rope. I checked the blade. I put out the candle.

"Hold on, Wren," I said to the dark, in the loudest voice I own, which is the kind nobody hears. "We're coming. And we're bringing the whole song with us."
