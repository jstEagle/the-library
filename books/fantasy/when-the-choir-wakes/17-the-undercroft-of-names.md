## 17. The Undercroft of Names

I want it on the record that I never intended to go back to Corvenne. The city and I had an arrangement: I stayed out of its canals, it stayed out of my nightmares. A fair trade, by ferryman's standards.

But here's what nobody tells you about grief — it keeps a ledger too. And mine had a line item that read *Corvenne, unpaid*, in handwriting I hadn't looked at in nine years.

So when Silas asked — not asked, exactly; Silas did that thing where they stared at the map until the map stared back and then said "we need a way in" in the tone of someone who has already decided you're going to provide one — I told them about Marrow.

"Marrow," Brann repeated. She was sharpening a boat knife against a stone with the steady rhythm of a woman who found violence meditative. "That's a name or a threat."

"Both, if you're lucky. He's a warden of the palace cellars. Third ring down. We ran cargo together before the Quieting laws — him below, me above. He owes me for a night in Sallowport I will never describe to you, partly because it's disgusting and partly because he'd kill me."

"And he'll just let us walk into the Cantor's own cellar," Silas said, "because of one disgusting night."

"He'll let us walk in because I'm going to pay him." I patted the satchel at my hip. Pearl-dust, three vials of Amberglass water, and a compass that pointed at whatever you loved most, which I kept mostly as a joke because for me it just spun. "Smugglers don't do favors, cartographer. We do transactions with sentimentality attached, like barnacles on a hull."

Wren, who had been quiet all day in the way that worried me most — Wren's silences were never empty, they were full, like a held breath — looked up from whittling nothing in particular. "Is your friend a good man?"

I considered lying to her. I'd gotten good at it over the years; grief makes excellent practice. But she had her father's eyes, and her father had once caught me cheating at cards and simply turned his chair so I couldn't see his hand, which was somehow worse than any accusation.

"No," I said. "He's a tired man who does small kindnesses and hates himself for them. It's the best kind of man available these days."

* * *

Corvenne at night was a city holding its tongue. You could feel it from the river — the whole capital humming at a frequency just below hearing, the Cantor's sanctioned song laid over everything like frost on glass. The legitimate bells rang it. The streetlamps flickered in time with it. Even the rats, I swear, moved in rhythm.

We came up through the drainage sluices on the south bank, which smelled like every bad decision I've ever made distilled into liquid form. Marrow was waiting at the third grate, exactly where I'd left word, a lantern hooded to a coin's width of light and his face above it wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Ovid." He said my name like it cost him something. "You look terrible."

"You look employed. Time changes us all."

His eyes went past me to the others — Brann coiled and watchful, Silas with the map wrapped against their chest like a sick child, Wren half-hidden behind them. His gaze snagged on Wren and something crossed his face, quick as a fish under ice.

"Three strangers and a child," he said. "Into the undercroft. Do you know what they'd do to me?"

"Do you know what I know about Sallowport?" I said pleasantly.

Marrow closed his eyes. Somewhere above us, faint through forty feet of stone, the sanctioned song shifted key, and we all felt it in our teeth. "Two hours," he said. "The night wardens change at the fourth bell. After that I never saw you, and if you're caught, you tell them you bribed me, and I'll deny it so convincingly they'll believe *you* were the liar." He paused. "Why the undercroft? Nobody goes to the undercroft. There's nothing down there but—"

He stopped. He knew what was down there. Everyone in Corvenne knew, the way you know there's a body under your floorboards: you don't talk about it, but you walk carefully.

"The names," Silas said quietly.

Marrow looked at them for a long moment. Then he unhooked the lantern and handed it to me, which in warden's language is either a blessing or a surrender. "Fourth bell," he said again, and was gone into the dark like a man escaping his own conscience.

* * *

The stairs down were cut from black stone that drank the lantern light and gave back nothing. I counted steps out of habit — two hundred and six, then a landing, then another hundred and twelve — because counting is what ferrymen do instead of praying.

Brann walked behind me. I could hear her breathing, slow and deliberate, the way she breathed during the Road of Teeth ambush, turning fear into fuel. Silas was ahead, map-hand pressed to the wall, reading the stone the way other people read signs. And Wren —

Wren was humming.

Barely. Under her breath. The lullaby, the one she'd taught us on the moor, the one that had opened Ovid like a locked box nobody had the key to anymore. I put my hand on her shoulder without thinking and she stopped, and I hated myself for it, and she understood anyway, because children understand complicity better than adults do. They've had less time to dress it up.

"Sorry," she whispered.

"Don't be sorry. Just—" I searched for words that weren't lies. "Down here, quiet isn't safety. Quiet is what they want. So we give them silence instead. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

I didn't have an answer ready. That should have been my first warning.

The vault door was iron, banded with bronze, and carved across its face in script older than the Cantor's reign were words I recognized from Amberglass: *WHAT IS NAMED IS HELD.* Silas touched the letters and flinched like the metal had bitten them.

"It's a Realmwright seal," they said. Their voice had gone strange — flat, careful, the voice of someone standing on their own grave. "Old work. This door was built to keep things safe. Then someone turned it around."

"Turned it around how?" Brann asked.

"A lock doesn't care what it keeps in or what it keeps out," Silas said. "It only cares about keeping. Someone taught this door to keep *people*."

I've broken into a lot of places. Warehouses, manors, one memorable temple whose god turned out to be asleep rather than absent. I know the sound a building makes when it's wrong — the creak of a trap about to spring, the hush of a guard shifting weight. The undercroft made no sound at all. That was the wrong thing. Buildings breathe. This one was holding its breath.

Silas set their palm flat against the seal and spoke a word I didn't catch, soft and syllable-less, more shape than sound. The bronze bands glowed briefly, the color of old honey, and the door swung inward without a whisper.

Inside, the dark was total. Then Silas raised the lantern, and I stopped being a man who joked about anything ever again.

* * *

Shelves. Floor to ceiling, row upon row, receding into blackness farther than the lantern could reach. And on the shelves — jars. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Glass, sealed with wax, each one glowing with a faint inner light like a firefly drowning in honey, and inside each jar, written on slips of paper that were not paper, curled and drifting as though suspended in water:

Names.

I knew it the way you know your own name, which is to say instantly and helplessly. The light wasn't light. It was *identity* — the particular hum of a person, bottled. Each jar held someone's name, torn out of them, and the name was still alive in there, still turning over in its sleep like something dreaming of being spoken.

"Oh," said Brann, in a very small voice. "Oh, no."

Wren stepped forward and I grabbed for her and missed, and she stood in the aisle with her face tilted up, and the nearest jars brightened. All along the shelf, dozens of them, warming toward her like coals toward breath.

"They can hear her," Silas whispered. They had gone gray. "They can hear singing. They remember it."

"How many?" Brann asked, and her voice was the ferrywoman's voice, the one that took soundings. "How many are down here?"

Silas walked the aisle. Counted. Walked another. I watched them do the arithmetic and watched it break something in them, because Silas was a cartographer and cartographers believe that everything can be measured, and some measurements are a kind of wound.

"Hundreds of thousands," they said finally. "Maybe more. Every purge since the first. Every village the Cantor silenced. Every elder unmade, every child taken off the rolls." They touched a jar gently, the way you touch a sleeping stranger you don't want to wake. "This isn't a trophy room. It's a larder. The Cantor is keeping them. Keeping them *for* something."

"For what?" I asked, though I already suspected, and my suspicion tasted like the Sighing Strait at flood tide.

"A song needs voices," Silas said. "Even a terrible one."

I found myself walking the rows without deciding to. My feet knew the way, the way feet know the way to a grave you've visited too often. And I told myself I was scouting, cataloguing exits, being the professional I claimed to be. And then I saw the label on a jar at shoulder height, in the Cantor's elegant looping hand, and the world went narrow as a keyhole.

*Mirelle Ovid.*

My daughter's name. My daughter's actual name, in a jar, glowing softly, turning over in its sleep.

Here is what I know about that moment, because I have gone over it the way you go over a wreck looking for survivors: I did not cry. What happened instead was worse and simpler. I reached out and picked up the jar, and it was warm, warm as a sleeping child's forehead, and the light inside it pressed against the glass toward my hands, and somewhere in the deep administrative machinery of my heart a ledger closed with a sound like a door.

Nine years I'd told myself she was dead. Dead was clean. Dead was a stone with a name on it and weather doing its patient work. But she wasn't dead. She was *held*. Her name was in a cellar under the palace, filed between a cooper from Duskwater and a grandmother from Low Hum, and she was — wherever she was — walking around with a hole where her self used to be, humming the Cantor's song, maybe, smiling the sanctioned smile, and I had spent nine years mourning a girl who was only missing.

"Ovid." Brann's hand on my arm. When had she come around the shelf? Her face was wet; she'd been crying quietly for some time, moving down the rows, and I realized she'd been reading labels too. Everyone in Cael Sorrow had someone in here. That was the design. That was the *point*. "Ovid, put it back. If it's missed—"

"If it's missed," I said, and my voice came out level, which surprised us both, "then they check. And if they check, they notice a gap. And a gap means someone came down here, which means Marrow hangs, and the trail leads up and out and to four travelers who were never here." I turned the jar in my hands. The light inside leaned toward me like a plant toward sun. "But if I take it—"

"You can't carry her name out of here like contraband," Silas said gently. "A name isn't a thing, Ovid. It's a thread. Cut it loose from the vault and it frays. The rite — the unbinding — that's what returns them. All of them, at once, or none."

"All of them at once," I repeated. Hundreds of thousands of jars. One rite. One cartographer with a map that wanted blood. "You're telling me the only way to get her back is to overthrow a god."

"I'm telling you that's been the job since chapter one," Silas said, and even now, even here, they managed to make it almost funny, which is the Realmwright gift nobody writes ballads about: making the unbearable briefly portable.

Wren appeared at my elbow. She didn't say anything. She just put her hand on the glass beside mine, and Mirelle's name flared — bright, bright, brighter than any jar in the row — and in the flare I heard, or imagined I heard, or refuse to this day to categorize, a single clear note. Not the lullaby. Something else. Something that sounded like a little girl laughing at a ferryman getting splashed.

Wren looked up at me. "She knows we're here," she said.

And that was when the lights began to go out.

Not all at once. In a wave, starting from the far end of the vault, row by row, jars dimming like windows going dark at curfew. Something had noticed us. Something was coming down the aisles, and it was not Marrow, because Marrow would have called out, and it was not a warden, because wardens have footsteps, and whatever this was had neither voice nor steps but a *pressure*, a silence with intent, rolling toward us through the stacks like cold water into a hold.

"Back to the door," Brann breathed. "Now. Slow."

We moved. I put Mirelle's jar back on its shelf with both hands, the way you lay down a sleeping child, and I made a promise to it in the oldest language I know, which is the language of debts: *I'm coming back with the tide.*

Behind us, in the darkening vault, something began to count our heartbeats.

* * *

We made the door. We made the stairs. We made two hundred and six steps and a landing and a hundred and twelve more, and Marrow was waiting at the grate with his hooded lantern and his face like a man watching his own funeral procession, and above us, muffled through stone, the fourth bell began to ring.

It got through four strokes.

On the fifth, the bell changed. The note bent — warped mid-swing into something flat and wrong, a sound like a throat closing — and every lamp in the sluice tunnel guttered at once, and Marrow said, in a voice stripped of everything but terror:

"That's not the change-bell. That's *summons*. They know something's in the palace." He looked at me, and beneath the fear was the tired man doing his small kindness one last time. "Sluice gate's unlocked. Go. Don't run — running echoes. Walk like you belong to the song."

We walked. Four abreast through the dripping dark, unhurried, hearts hammering in a rhythm I prayed was our own. Behind us, far below, I could still feel the vault — those hundreds of thousands of small warm lights, waiting in the dark for a voice worth waking to.

At the mouth of the sluice, where the canal water breathed its cold river smell, Silas stopped. The map had come unwrapped from their chest without their seeming to notice, and it lay open across their palms, and in the space where the next waypoint should have been, fresh ink was surfacing. Rising through the parchment like a bruise.

Not a place this time.

Words.

Silas read them once, silently. Then aloud, in a voice with no wryness left in it at all:

"*Thank you for visiting my collection. Please come upstairs. I keep better light for guests.*"

The canal water around us went perfectly, unnaturally still.

Somewhere above, in the palace of the Cantor, a door we hadn't chosen was already opening.
