## 18. Audience with the Cantor

They did not drag us. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that frightened me most.

You can brace against a rope. You can spit at a man with a spear. But four Wardens of Song in grey lacquered armor walked us through the palace of Corvenne at a courteous pace, hands never touching us, and it was somehow worse — as if we were guests. As if the whole thing had been arranged.

The palace corridors were lined with bells. Hundreds of them, thousands, hanging from iron frames along every wall: great bronze cathedral bells no bigger than my fist, tiny silver things like thimbles, cracked ones, ones that had been melted and recast so many times they'd lost their original shape entirely. None of them rang. The air in Corvenne was thick with a hum I could feel in my back teeth, one vast sustained note that never resolved, and every bell hung silent inside it like a bird frozen mid-flight.

"They're not decoration," Brann murmured beside me. Her wrists were bound in front of her with cord that shimmered faintly, and she kept flexing her fingers against it, testing. "It's a warning. Look at the small ones."

I looked. The smallest bells were newest — their metal still bright. The largest were ancient, green with patina, some of them older than Vell itself.

"It's a graveyard," I said quietly. "Every bell is someone who used to sing."

Ovid, walking behind us with the particular slouch of a man conserving his energy for an escape he hadn't planned yet, snorted. "Cheerful place. I once ferried a nobleman who hung paintings of his dead rivals in his foyer. Same energy. Less bronze."

Wren said nothing at all. They'd taken her whistle-song out of her — I didn't know how, I didn't want to know how — and she walked between Brann and me with her chin up and her eyes fixed forward, and every few steps her lips moved silently, shaping notes no one could hear. Keeping herself together the way the villagers did. Humming without sound.

I wanted to take her hand. I didn't. There were four armed Wardens watching, and something else watching too, something whose attention I could feel on the back of my neck like sunlight through glass — warm, total, and utterly without mercy.

The throne room doors were white stone, thirty feet tall, carved with a single line of notation that spiraled inward like a nautilus shell. As we approached, the great note in the air shifted key — subtly, smoothly, the way a tide turns — and the doors swung open without being touched.

"Right," Ovid said under his breath. "If anyone asks, I'm a hostage with valuable maritime knowledge."

"You're a smuggler," Brann said.

"A *logistically creative* ferryman—"

"*Quiet,*" I breathed, because we had crossed the threshold, and the sound died.

Not faded. Died. One step past the doors, the world's hum cut off like a severed string, and the silence that replaced it was not absence but presence — a pressure, a weight, snow falling upward into my ears. I had felt silence like this only once before, fifteen years ago, standing over the body of the last other Realmwright, and I had sworn never to feel it again.

The throne room was vast and empty and lit by windows of colored glass that threw no color, only pale washed light, as if even the spectrum had been asked to keep its voice down. And at the far end, on a dais beneath a crown-shaped canopy, sat the Cantor.

I had expected a monster. Everyone expects a monster. What sat on the throne was a figure in robes the grey of old ash, crowned with the iron circlet of Cael Sorrow's kings, and where its face should have been there was — I struggled for the word, and the word was *movement*. Its face moved constantly, features flowing like water disturbed by wind, mouth opening and closing around sounds that never emerged, eyes that were sometimes two eyes and sometimes one and sometimes none. It wore King Aldous's crown the way a coat is worn by a hook.

And it was singing. I realized that with a lurch. The great note that filled all of Corvenne, the hum that held the city's shapes together or ground them down — it came from here. From that mouth that made no sound. The Cantor sang in a register below hearing, or above it, or in some register that had never been given a name, and everything in Cael Sorrow lived inside its song the way fish live inside water.

We were made to stand — not kneel; interesting, that — twenty feet from the dais. The Wardens withdrew to the walls. The doors closed behind us with a click that I felt rather than heard.

Then the Cantor looked at me.

Its face settled. That was the only way I can describe it — the flowing features stilled, gathered themselves, and became for one moment something almost human: a long face, hollow-cheeked, with eyes the brown of dried ink. Eyes that had once laughed, maybe. Eyes that had once belonged to a court composer named — I didn't know the name yet. I would learn it later, in a cell, in the dark. In that moment I only knew that the thing on the throne was looking at me with recognition, and recognition is the most dangerous thing in the world when you have spent fifteen years buying your anonymity one charm at a time.

"Cartographer," the Cantor said.

Its voice arrived inside my skull rather than my ears, which meant I couldn't pretend I hadn't heard it, which meant everyone saw me flinch. Brann's shoulder pressed against mine. Solid. Warm. *Here.*

"You write in blood-ink," the Cantor continued, and its voice was — gods help me — *beautiful*. Rich and layered, a chord given speech. "You fold space into paper. You bind the unmappable with a word and a wound." It tilted its head, and the crown caught the colorless light. "There are perhaps three people alive in either realm who could have carried that map as far as you have carried it. Two are dead. I killed one myself, years ago, and I remember regretting it."

"We're honored," Ovid said dryly, "to be in such distinguished company."

The Cantor's gaze slid to him and something passed through the room — a pressure, a flattening, the feeling of a hand pressing down on the top of your head — and Ovid's mouth snapped shut and stayed shut. When the attention returned to me, it came gentler. Almost tender.

"Fifteen years," it said. "Selling luck charms to widows in Vell. Do you know what I find remarkable about that? Not the hiding. Hiding is easy; fear does most of the work. What I find remarkable is that you *kept the craft*. All those years, pretending to be nothing, and your hands never forgot. You trembled every time you sold a charm, because real magic ran through your fingers and you let it. You could have burned it out of yourself. You chose to keep it alive in secret, like a coal under ash." The almost-human face smiled. It was the worst thing I had seen yet, worse than the unmaking of the village elder, because it was warm. "That is not the behavior of a coward, cartographer. That is the behavior of a person waiting to be asked."

"I wasn't waiting," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I was hiding. There's a difference."

"Is there?" The Cantor rose from the throne, and its robes moved wrong, pooling and lifting as if the fabric itself were listening. It descended the dais steps slowly, unhurried, and stopped ten feet away — close enough that I could see that its skin, where the sleeves fell back, was marked all over with notation. Inked. Scarred in. A lifetime of music written directly onto flesh. "I have watched you since the stranger died in your shop. Did you think I wouldn't feel it? A Realmwright's map waking up after fifteen years? It rang through both realms like a struck bell. Every Warden in Corvenne felt it. I let you walk. Do you know why?"

"Because you enjoy a dramatic entrance," Brann muttered.

The Cantor ignored her, which told me more about its hierarchy of threats than anything else it might have said. It raised one marked hand, and the air above its palm folded — folded the way my maps folded, the way space folded when I worked — and there, hovering, was a copy of the stranger's map. My map now. Rendered in light, every blood-inked line glowing softly, and around its edges, in margins I had studied by candlelight until my eyes ached, the handwriting that was mine. My childhood handwriting. *Proof someone knew who I am.*

"I know what this is," the Cantor said softly. "I commissioned its beginning, long ago, from a woman who thought she was mapping trade routes. I know every waypoint. Amberglass. The Sighing Strait. The Road of Teeth." Its ink-brown eyes came back to mine. "And I know what the last page requires. Don't I, cartographer?"

My stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, through whatever bedrock Cael Sorrow rested on.

Because I hadn't told them. Brann, Ovid, Wren — none of them knew what the final page demanded, what the rite cost, what I had been quietly planning to pay alone since the night in Amberglass when I'd read the binding's last line by drowned candlelight and then folded the page closed and lied with my face. Fifteen years of practice lying with my face. I'd thought I was good at it.

But the Cantor watched me now with those terrible warm eyes, and beside me Brann went very still, and I understood — with the slow, sick clarity of a man watching a dam crack — that the thing on the throne had just told them for me.

"The map wants blood," the Cantor said, gently, gently. "A cartographer's blood, as ink. Your blood, Silas Almeida. The last Realmwright. You have been walking toward your own sacrifice since the day you opened that map, and these people—" it gestured, gracious, inclusive, monstrous, "—these dear people have been walking beside you without knowing they were escorting you to the altar. Have you told them? No. Of course not. You hide tremors in your fingers. You would hide this too, all the way to the end."

"Silas." Brann's voice. Low. Not a question. A door opening.

"Not now," I said.

"Now seems fine," said Ovid.

"*Not now.*"

But Wren had turned to look up at me, and her eyes were wide and wet and furious, and there was no version of *not now* that survived that look. She knew. Children always know. She reached out and grabbed two fistfuls of my coat, hard, as if she could hold me to the world by main force, and she didn't say anything at all, because they had taken her song and she refused to speak in a room built for speaking, and it was the loudest silence I have ever heard.

The Cantor watched us with something that in any other creature I would have called satisfaction.

"This is why I am offering you a place at my side," it said. "Hear me fully before you refuse — and you will refuse, you're all refusing lately, it's become quite fashionable — but hear me. I do not want your death. Death is waste. I have spent fifteen years learning exactly how much of this realm can be held together by one voice, and the answer is: most of it, but not enough, and not forever. The Great Quieting approaches whether I will it or not. The song frays. The edges of Cael Sorrow are already going soft — villages unmade, coastlines forgotten, whole valleys sliding into the blank places where nothing holds its shape." It spread its marked hands. "I need a second voice. A co-author. Someone who can *write* — not sing, writing is better, writing lasts — someone who can take the burden of holding a reality and split it down the middle. You, Silas Almeida. Finish the map. Not the way the dead stranger intended. Finish it *my* way. Be my scribe, and no one needs to bleed. Not you. Not the king. Not the child clutching your coat. I will even reopen the folds between realms — you could visit Vell whenever you liked. Charm-seller on weekends. Scribe of a kingdom the rest of the time."

"And if I say no?" I asked.

The warmth left its face all at once, like a lamp blown out.

"Then the Quieting comes nine days early," the Cantor said, "and I begin with the child."

The silence after that had teeth.

I want to be honest, because honesty is the one luxury I allowed myself in that room: for three full seconds, I considered it. Three seconds in which I stood in the colored non-light of that throne room and weighed a child's life against my freedom, my secrets, my carefully rationed half-lived life, and found the scales moving in directions that shamed me. That's what temptation actually looks like, in case you wondered. Not a devil with a contract. Just a tired person and an easy exit and a very reasonable voice explaining that everyone lives, isn't that nice, no one has to bleed.

Then Wren's fists tightened in my coat, and Brann's shoulder leaned into mine — deliberately now, a choice, a weight she was giving me instead of taking — and somewhere behind us Ovid exhaled the way he did before doing something stupid and loyal.

"No," I said.

The Cantor's face began to flow again.

"I've heard the offer," I said, and my voice shook, and I let it shake, because I was done hiding tremors in my fingers. "And I'll tell you what I told the Wardens of Vell fifteen years ago, right before I ran: magic isn't a crown and it isn't a cage. It's a *hand*. Open or closed. Yours has been closed so long you've grown into the fist." I lifted my chin. "I'm a cartographer. I don't finish other people's maps. I draw new ones."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — the Cantor looked at me with something that was not anger. It looked almost like grief. Like a composer hearing a phrase they'd always wanted to write and knowing it would never be theirs.

"Pity," it said.

It snapped its fingers — a soundless snap, a snap made of silence — and the floor opened.

Not dramatically. Politely. A section of pale stone simply rotated, smooth as a page turning, revealing stairs going down into dark, and the Wardens of Song took our arms at last — the first time they'd touched us — and guided us toward them with the same courtesy they'd shown all along. Brann fought. Of course she fought; it took two Wardens and a pressure in the air like deep water to move her, and she broke one of their visors with her bound hands on the way, and I loved her for it in a way I decided I'd examine later, if there was a later. Ovid went with the resigned grace of a man who had clearly already counted the exits and found them wanting. Wren screamed without sound, mouth wide, and the Wardens flinched from her anyway, as though soundless screaming were worse than the loud kind.

They put us in separate cells. I heard Brann's voice going down one corridor and Wren's silence going down another, and then a third corridor, colder, older, hewn from rock that predated the palace, and my Warden stopped before a door of black iron banded with notation.

"One courtesy," the Warden said — and the voice was muffled, strange, as if speaking through cloth. "The Cantor does not listen to this wing. It cannot bear to." The door swung open. "Sleep well, cartographer. It's quieter than you deserve."

The door shut. The lock turned. And in the darkness of my cell, alone for the first time since a dying stranger bled out on my shop floor, I put my back against the cold stone and let myself shake.

*I cannot bear to,* the Warden had said. *It cannot bear to.*

The Cantor, who filled a realm with song, afraid of a little quiet.

I sat in the dark and turned that over, the way I'd turn an unfamiliar coastline over in my hands, looking for the harbor. And that was when, through the wall to my left — thin stone, old stone, stone that had been listening longer than any of us had been alive — I heard breathing.

Slow. Measured. Deliberately audible.

Someone in the next cell was breathing *at* me. Someone who wanted me to know they were there.

And then, softer than breath, in a voice roughened by years of disuse but carrying underneath it the unmistakable cadence of a man who had once commanded a kingdom — a voice I had seen mouthing *hurry* from a tower window — came three words:

"So. You're the cartographer."
