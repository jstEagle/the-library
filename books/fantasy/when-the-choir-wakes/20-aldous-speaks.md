## 20. Aldous Speaks

The first thing you must understand about being a king is that it is mostly waiting.

I do not say this bitterly. Well — I say a little of it bitterly; I am not a saint, and my confessor died eleven years ago, though I have lately begun to suspect he simply walked out of the song and no one noticed, which is its own kind of commentary on my reign. But it is true. Kingship is a long corridor of other people's urgencies, and you stand at the end of it holding a scepter and nodding, waiting for the moment when the corridor empties and you can think your own thoughts again.

I had been waiting for fifteen years.

The Cantor does not sleep. This is the first thing to know about my cellmate, if cellmate is the word for the thing that lives in your bones and sings through your teeth. It does not sleep, and so neither do I, not properly. There are depths of the song where I go down like a diver and come up hours later with salt in my mouth and no memory of the surface, but that is not sleep. Sleep is a country. I have been exiled from it.

But silence — silence is different. The Cantor cannot hear silence. I discovered this the way one discovers most useful things: by accident, in despair, at three in the morning, metaphorically speaking, since there is no morning here, only the endless amber noon of the Cantor's attention. A gap had opened in the song — some administrative matter in the eastern provinces, a village that had hummed out of turn, the great machine of the realm grinding through its corrections — and in that gap, for perhaps four breaths, there was nothing.

Nothing. Do you know what nothing sounds like after fifteen years? It sounds like snowfall. It sounds like your mother's kitchen. It sounds like every unimportant afternoon you ever wasted and did not know you were wasting, and I stood in my four breaths of it and wept like a child, silently, because even my grief had learned to be quiet.

That was the day I understood the shape of my prison. Not the walls. The walls are stone and stone is honest. The prison was this: the Cantor filled every space, and it could not perceive the spaces it did not fill. Silence was invisible to it. Silence was the one door it had never thought to lock, because to lock a door you must first believe the door exists.

And then, last night — or what I call night, the hour when the Cantor's song runs low and repetitive, like a musician checking tuning — someone knocked on that door.

* * *

They came to me as lines scratched through the wall, in the old grammar of cartographers, which I confess I have not read since my tutors despaired of me at fourteen. My father believed a king should know maps the way a shepherd knows hills. I believed a king should know wine the way a shepherd knows hills, and our disagreements on this point were legendary. But the grammar came back to me the way a language comes back when you are drowning in it: slowly, then all at once.

*I think I may have an idea,* the lines said. *But I'm going to need a choir.*

I laughed. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever archive survives us: the King of Cael Sorrow, prisoner in his own palace, laughed at a stranger's handwriting through a wall, and it was the first honest sound I had made in fifteen years. The Cantor stirred above us — I felt it the way you feel a change in weather, a pressure behind the eyes — and I smoothed my face into the blankness it preferred, the dull mask of the Hollow King, and waited for the song to close over the moment.

It did. And in the next gap, I answered.

*Who are you?*

The reply took a while. Whoever they were, they wrote carefully, the way people write when they are deciding how much truth a wall can be trusted with.

*A cartographer.*

I considered this. In Cael Sorrow, cartography had been the first art to die. The Cantor had burned the maps in its second year — publicly, in the Palace Square, while the heralds sang the burning into law — because a map is an argument that places exist apart from the singer of them, and the Cantor could not tolerate the argument. So either this was a liar, or a ghost, or something that had crossed the fold from the other side of the world, where — if the old stories held — the Realmwrights still worked their forbidden ink.

*You're a long way from home,* I wrote back, and felt rather than heard the pause on the other side of the stones.

*You have no idea,* they wrote. And then, after a moment: *Your majesty.*

No one had called me that in fifteen years except the Cantor, and the Cantor said it the way a puppeteer says it to the wood. From a stranger, through a wall, in scratches, it went through me like a blade finding the seam of armor. I put my forehead against the cold stone and let myself shake for exactly as long as the silence lasted, and when the song rose again I was the Hollow King once more, smooth-faced, empty-eyed, singing along.

A king's dignity, my father used to say, is not in never kneeling. It is in knowing which kneelings are yours to choose.

I had been kneeling for fifteen years without choosing it. I intended, if it killed me — and I was beginning to suspect it might — to make my next kneeling a choice.

* * *

We learned each other's silences the way lovers learn each other's breathing.

There were gaps in the song, and each gap had its own character. The deep ones came when the Cantor turned its attention to the borders, where the realm frayed into static and needed constant mending; those lasted longest, sometimes a hundred breaths, and in them we could almost converse. The shallow ones came randomly, like fish rising, and in those we managed only a line apiece. We developed shorthand. We developed, gods help me, jokes — the desperate gallows humor of two prisoners separated by a wall, which is a genre with a long and undistinguished history and in which I discovered I was apparently fluent.

*Tell me about yourself,* I wrote, in one of the deep silences. *The real answer. Not the one you'd give a customs officer.*

The scratching was slow. Hesitant. When it came, it came in pieces:

*Silas Almeida. Charm-seller. Vell. Fifteen years selling luck to people who don't believe in it.*

*And before that?*

A longer hesitation. Long enough that I thought the silence would close and I would get nothing. Then:

*Before that I was someone who shouldn't exist anymore.*

Ah, I thought. One of mine, then. Or one of my world's. I knew that tone. It was the tone of everyone the Cantor's purges had scattered — the hedge-singers, the bell-keepers, the women who sang the rivers clean. Guilt wears the same coat in every country.

*In Cael Sorrow,* I wrote, *that describes roughly half the population. You'll have to be more specific.*

The laugh came back through the stone as a single scratched line that might have been *ha* or might have been a hiccup, and I counted it a victory.

Then, in the next deep silence, they told me. The Realmwrights. The outlawing. The ink that had signed the prohibition — and I will be honest, because a man talking to a wall has no one left to perform for: when I read those words, my first feeling was anger. Fifteen years I had sat in this cage while the world outside forgot its own name, and one of the architects of the forgetting was scratching apologies through my wall.

My second feeling arrived more slowly, the way shame always does, dressed as honesty: I had begged for the binding. I had lain in my sickbed with the fever eating me and ordered my court composer to save me by any means, and the means had been a ritual older than my dynasty, and I had signed the consent with a hand too weak to hold the pen properly. Whatever Silas Almeida had done to the world, I had done this to myself. There is a particular dignity available only to kings who admit this, and I decided — lying on my pallet in the dark, listening to the thing in my blood sing — to avail myself of it.

*You helped write the law that ended magic,* I wrote, when the silence next allowed. *I helped write the prayer that started this. Between us we've made rather a mess of two worlds.*

The reply came fast, almost fierce: *You didn't know what it would become.*

*Neither did you.* I let that sit. Then: *The difference between us, Master Almeida, is that you ran for fifteen years and I couldn't. One of us should stop running now. I suggest you, since I'm somewhat constrained.*

*Ha,* they wrote. Definitely *ha*, that time.

* * *

Here is what I told them, in the silences, over what I estimate was three days — time moves strangely under the song, and I measure it now in gaps rather than hours.

I told them about the fever. About the winter it came, the year the grey sickness came up the river valleys and took my queen first and then turned for me, and the physicians stood around my bed doing the thing physicians do, which is arranging their faces. Maestro Corvane — he had a name once, remember that, he had a name and a family and hands that shook when he was nervous, which was always — Maestro Corvane came to me on the ninth night and knelt and said, "Your majesty, there is an old rite. It is not sanctioned. It may not work. It will cost—" and stopped, because he was kind, and kindness makes cowards of us at the worst moments.

"Cost what?" I said. "Corvane, I am dying. Name the price."

He named it. He named it badly, half of it, the way frightened men name prices — the binding of his music to my life, the fusion of his song to my breath, so that as long as he sang I would live. What he did not name — what perhaps he did not know, or perhaps knew and could not say — was that the rite did not bind his song to my life. It bound him to me entirely. All of him. His grief, his terror, his loneliness, his love of his work, his fear that his work was all he was. Everything he was went into the vessel of me, and everything I was went into the keeping of his song, and the ritual, which had been designed — I learned this much from the drowned archive's fragments, which reached me even here, smuggled in the Cantor's own memory like worms in a ship's timber — the ritual had been designed for two willing participants of equal strength standing in balance.

We were not in balance. He was terrified and grieving and half in love with the idea of saving me, and I was arrogant enough to think a king's consent covered the difference.

So when the fever broke — when I woke on the twelfth morning whole and hungry and weeping without knowing why — there was something else awake in me too. Something that had been a man and was now a chord. And it was afraid. Gods, it was afraid, the way only a man who has dissolved can be afraid, and it did the only thing it knew how to do: it sang. It sang to hold its own shape together, and the singing leaked into me, and through me into the court, and the court into the city, and within a season the whole realm was humming whether it wished to or not, and within two years the humming had rules, and within five the rules had teeth.

"I begged for the binding," I told Silas, in scratches, in the deepest silence we had yet found, the one where the Cantor chased some rebel frequency out past the Salt Fens and forgot us entirely. "Remember that, whatever else you learn. I asked for this. He gave more than I asked, but I asked. If you hate anyone, hate me first."

The answer took a long time. When it came, it was one line, and I have kept it the way one keeps a coin from a dead friend's pocket:

*Hating you first seems inefficient. I'd rather get you out.*

* * *

There is a thing I must set down here, because if these scratches are ever read, the reader deserves the whole of it, and because saying it to a wall is the only rehearsal I will get.

I told Silas to unbind us even if it killed me.

I meant it. Let no one romanticize this into nobility — nobility is what cowards call a decision made quickly. I meant it the way a man with a stone in his boot means *stop the carriage*. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of wearing my own face as a mask over someone else's panic, of watching my kingdom learn to whisper, of attending my own courts as a hollow figure who nodded when the Cantor nodded. I have watched children born who have never heard unsanctioned sound. I have watched old women die humming the Cantor's scales because they had forgotten there were others. I have watched my realm become an instrument, and instruments do not grieve, and a realm that cannot grieve is already dead and merely rude about it.

If the price of ending that is my life, then the arithmetic is simple, and I am a king, and arithmetic is the one subject my tutors never despaired of.

But I will confess to the wall — to Silas, to whoever comes after — that when I wrote those words, *even if it kills me*, my hand hesitated. Because there is a version of freedom that arrives too late to be used, and I have had fifteen years to imagine using mine. I want to walk in the gardens. I want to hear a market argue. I want to sit in a tavern and listen to bad musicians play worse songs and drink something the color of garnets and complain about it. I want, in short, the unimportant afternoons. I spent forty years trading them away for the crown and told myself it was duty, and now I would trade the crown back for one of them and tell myself that was duty too, and both times I would be lying, and the lie is the most human thing left in me.

I did not write any of that. A king's dignity, etcetera. But Silas wrote back something that made me suspect the wall had heard anyway:

*Noted. But for the record, your majesty — I've spent fifteen years hiding from my own life, and I can tell you from professional experience: dying isn't the only way to stop being a prisoner. Sometimes it's just the laziest option.*

*I'll keep it in reserve,* I wrote.

*Do that,* they wrote. *We may need reserves.*

* * *

On what I believe was the fourth day, the silences changed.

I felt it before I understood it — a new texture in the gaps, a faint undertone beneath the Cantor's song, like a second instrument joining very far off. At first I feared the Cantor had found us, had heard the scratching, had begun some new counter-melody of punishment. I pressed my ear to the wall — uselessly; the threat was everywhere and nowhere — and waited for the song to turn toward us with that terrible attentive sweetness it uses before it unmakes something.

It did not turn. Instead, the gaps grew longer. Richer. And in them, faint as memory, I began to hear — humming. Many voices. Far away, out past the fens, out in the villages and the roads, ordinary people humming the small illegal tunes the Cantor had spent fifteen years stamping out, and the Cantor straining toward them and finding — nothing it could name. Silence shaped like song. A country of quiet learning, note by note, to make noise.

Someone was teaching them. Someone with a cartographer's patience and a heretic's pen.

Through the wall, in the old grammar, I wrote: *What have you done?*

The reply came in three lines, unhurried, and I have read them a hundred times since, and I expect I will read them a hundred more:

*Started a choir. Your majesty — the map says the rite needs a chorus to hold it. I need to know everything you remember about the night of the binding. Every word Corvane spoke. Every note. Can you give me that?*

I lay back on my pallet. Above me the Cantor sang its vast, anxious, deaf song, chasing harmonics across a kingdom that was quietly refusing to be flat. Somewhere beyond the wall, a charm-seller who should not exist was assembling an army of hummers to break a king out of his own body.

Every word, they asked. Every note.

I closed my eyes, and for the first time in fifteen years I reached deliberately, willingly, down into the place where the Cantor lived in me — down past the terror, past the fused and frantic chord of a man who had never stopped singing alone — and I began, in the silence where the Cantor could not follow, to transcribe.

*Your majesty,* the wall had written once, and I had laughed.

Well. Let the record show that when the choir wakes, it will find the king already singing.
