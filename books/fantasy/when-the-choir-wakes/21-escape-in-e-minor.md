## 21. Escape in E Minor

I want it on the record that I did not want to be a hero.

In Vell I was a ferrywoman. I moved people and cargo from one bank to another, took their coin, kept my mouth shut, and went home to a room that smelled of rope and river. That is an honest trade. Nobody writes songs about ferrywomen, which is exactly how we like it.

But here's what fifteen years on the water teaches you: every crossing has a moment where you commit. The point where the current takes hold and turning back costs more than going on. You learn to feel for it with your whole body — in the drag of the oar, the pull against your palms. And you learn that once you're past it, hesitation kills faster than the river does.

Somewhere between Wren's cell and the undercroft stairs, we passed that point. So when Ovid said, *this plan is insane*, I said, "Good. Sane plans are what they'll expect."

He gave me the look he gives everyone — the one that says he's known a hundred braver fools than me and buried most of them — and then he started counting bells.

* * *

Here is what three days in a cell will teach you about Corvenne Palace: it breathes.

Not alive, exactly. But the Cantor's song runs through the stone like blood through a body, and blood has a rhythm. Every hour, on the hour, the great bell in the Lantern Tower swings and sounds one clean note, and for the length of that note — eleven heartbeats, I counted, because counting is free and hope isn't — the whole palace's sound-wards flex around the bell's resonance like a fist relaxing.

Ovid knew this before I did. Of course he did. He'd spent twenty years smuggling through places built by men who thought walls were enough, and he had a smuggler's ear for the moments buildings forget to pay attention.

"The bell's tuned to E," he told us through the wall of our cell, in the flat voice he used for facts he didn't want to feel anything about. "Old bell. Older than the Cantor. It won't be retuned because retuning it would mean admitting something in this palace predates the great and glorious song. Pride. Every tyrant's soft spot."

"So during the note," Silas said slowly — and I could hear them working it out, the cartographer's mind laying down bearings, "the wards ring at E too. Everything resonates."

"Everything resonates," Ovid agreed. "Which means everything is busy being itself and not watching the doors. We have eleven heartbeats per bell to move through a gap that shouldn't exist. After that, the fist closes."

"And how many bells do we need?"

Silence. The kind of silence that does arithmetic badly.

"Four," Ovid said finally. "Cell block to the undercroft stair, undercroft to the service gallery, gallery past the Wardens' post, post to the gatehouse. Four gaps. Forty-four heartbeats of grace, spread over four hours, assuming nobody sneezes."

"Assuming," I said.

"Assuming," he agreed, "nobody sneezes."

* * *

The problem — well, one of the problems, the list was long and growing — was Wren.

She wasn't in our cell. They'd taken her after the market, after she stood on a fountain's edge and sang three lines of a harvest round loud enough to crack a teacup two streets away, and they'd put her somewhere else. Somewhere the Cantor kept the ones it hadn't yet decided what to do with. Ovid's contacts called it the Quiet Room, and even the smugglers said the name quietly.

Three days, and no word whether she was still herself. Three days of Silas wearing grooves into the flagstones with their pacing, of me lying awake listening to the palace hum its terrible lullaby, of Ovid sharpening a spoon into a weapon and pretending it was a hobby.

Then, on the third night, between the midnight bell and the one after, I heard it.

A whistle. Thin, high, threading up through stone and silence like water finding a crack. Four notes. Then a pause. Then the same four notes again.

I sat up so fast I cracked my head on the low ceiling, and I didn't care, because I knew those notes. Every kid on the Vell waterfront knew them. It was the dockhand's signal — *all clear, come alongside* — except Wren had never worked a dock in her life. She'd learned it from me, two weeks ago, on the road, as a joke. A way to call each other across a crowded market.

She'd changed the key, though. She was whistling it in E.

"She's telling us where she is," Silas breathed beside me, and their hand found my shoulder in the dark, gripping hard enough to bruise. "Brann. She's telling us she can hear the bells. She's *counting with us*."

"Not broken," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I meant it to. "They haven't broken her."

"Yet," Ovid said, from his corner, not unkindly. Which was why we couldn't wait any longer.

* * *

We went at the second bell past midnight.

Eleven heartbeats. Let me tell you what eleven heartbeats feels like when you spend them running through a corridor that has spent three days humming at you, trying to wear your name smooth. It feels like nothing. It feels like diving off the pier as a girl and surfacing on the other side of the mooring posts, gasping, astonished, alive.

The cell door wasn't locked — the Cantor didn't believe in locks, believed in song, and the song held doors shut the way frost holds a pond. But when the bell struck, the frost went thin. Ovid hit the door with his shoulder on the fourth heartbeat and we spilled through into a corridor lit by lamps that burned without flickering, because flickering was movement and movement made noise and noise belonged to the Cantor alone.

Second gap: the undercroft stair. A sentry stood there — one of the mute enforcers, gray-robed, face like a closed book. He didn't see us. That was the horror of the enforcers; they saw only what the song told them to see, and during the bell's note, the song was looking inward, admiring itself. We pressed into an alcove full of ceremonial trumpets — silent trumpets, muzzled with wax, which tells you everything about this place — and held our breath while the eleventh heartbeat passed and the fist closed around the corridor again, an inch from our noses.

Then we waited. An hour. In the dark, among the smothered trumpets, while the palace sang.

It was Silas who spoke first, barely a breath: "When we're out. When this is done. I'm going to map every room in this palace and then burn the map."

"You don't burn maps," I whispered back.

"I know," they said. "That's why it's a threat."

And despite everything — the fear sitting on my chest like a wet cloak, the memory of the elder in that village coming apart into quiet — I laughed. Just a little. Just enough. That's the thing about found family that nobody warns you about: they make you laugh in the exact places where laughing should be impossible, and somehow that's how you know it's real.

Third bell. Third gap. The service gallery ran above the great hall, and below us, through the grate, we could see them — the court, rows of them, standing in the dark and humming the Cantor's harmony with their eyes open and empty, keeping themselves together the way the villagers did, but wrong, forced, a choir of hostages. Among them, near the dais, a figure in white and gold, swaying like a reed, singing a line above all the others that no one else could have carried.

The Cantor. In person, or whatever passed for person here.

I looked at Silas. Silas looked back, and their face in the lamplight was doing something complicated — fear and fury and, horribly, pity, all fighting for the same ground.

"Later," I mouthed.

"Later," they mouthed back, and we moved.

* * *

The fourth gap was where it went wrong, because there is always a place where it goes wrong, and if a plan doesn't have one, the plan is lying to you.

The Wardens' post sat between the gallery and the gatehouse, and unlike the sentries, the Wardens weren't deaf to the world during the bell. They were the Cantor's own instrument, tuned sharp, and they'd posted two of them at the stairhead with orders that apparently included *stand there looking inevitable*.

We had one heartbeat of decision. Maybe less.

Ovid took it.

"I'll draw them," he said, already moving, already pulling the spoon-knife from his sleeve, and there was no time to argue because arguing takes words and words take time and we had neither. "Gatehouse is left at the bottom. Don't wait for me. Ferryman's rule — the boat leaves when the boat leaves."

"Ovid—" Silas started.

"Daughter's waiting," he said, and grinned, that awful crooked grin he wore like armor, and stepped out of the alcove into the open, and knocked over a lamp stand with a crash that rang through the whole gallery like a blasphemy.

The Wardens turned. The song *shrieked* — I felt it in my teeth, a frequency like a scream played backwards — and both of them moved toward him, and Ovid met the first one with the spoon-knife and pure spite, and the second one raised a hand and *sang*, one note, a note with a hook in it—

I grabbed Silas. Silas grabbed me. We ran.

Down the stair, left at the bottom, the gatehouse yawning ahead, and behind us the sound of Ovid's fight getting quieter in a way I hated, in a way that sounded like an ending. My legs wanted to turn around. Fifteen years of instinct said *you go back for your people, that's the whole law, everything else is commentary.*

But the boat leaves when the boat leaves. His rule. His stupid, self-protective, grief-forged rule, and I honored it the way you honor anything a man like Ovid builds: swearing the entire time.

The gatehouse ward thinned with the last of the bell's dying note, and we slipped through it like water through a closing hand, out onto gravel, out under open sky, out of the palace that sang.

* * *

Wren was waiting.

Of course she was. She'd whistled herself hoarse guiding us — four notes at every bell, adjusting when we drifted, patient as a lighthouse — and when we came around the corner of the stable wall she launched herself at me hard enough to knock the air out, arms around my ribs, face buried in my coat.

"You came," she said into the wool. "You actually — I knew it, but I *knew* it, you know? Both kinds of knowing."

"Yeah, well." My voice betrayed me, cracking like a boy's. I put my hand on her head anyway, her hair still smelling of smoke and the road. "Dockhand's signal. All clear, come alongside. You came alongside."

"Where's Ovid?" she asked, pulling back, and her eyes went from my face to Silas's, and she knew before either of us said it. Kids always know first. They haven't learned to arrange their faces yet.

"He bought us the door," Silas said quietly.

"Is he—"

"He was fighting when we left." I crouched to her level, because some things you say eye to eye or not at all. "He's the most stubborn man I've ever shared a boat with, and I once ferried a tax collector who argued about the fare for a full mile. If anyone can lose two Wardens and live out of pure contrariness, it's him."

"But if he doesn't," Wren said, because she was that kind of child, the kind who looked straight down the well, "then we go back for him. Right? That's the rule. You said — on the road, you said—"

"I know what I said." I looked at Silas over her head. Silas looked at me. And whatever passed between us needed no whistle, no key, no bell.

"We go back for him," I said. "But first we get you out of the city, because if they've lost a king's prisoners *and* a songbird in one night, every street in Corvenne will be a throat tomorrow. Low Hum. Rebels, hidden songs, people who owe Silas a debt and me a grudge. Two days' walk if the weather's kind."

"It won't be," Silas said.

"It's never kind," I said. "That's why we walk fast."

* * *

We made the aqueduct road by grey dawn, three shadows moving along the spine of the city's old waterline, and it was there — with Corvenne still hunched and humming behind us, its towers crowned in morning mist like a head bowed in false prayer — that we heard the bells change.

All of them. At once. Not the hour-note, not the clean E, but a chord, vast and wrong, rolling out over the rooftops, and beneath it — faint, at the edge of hearing, or maybe only at the edge of mine — a single voice raised in something that was almost laughter.

Silas stopped dead. Their face had gone the color of the mist.

"That's not an alarm," they said. "Alarms are simple. That's... a composition." Their hand went to the map at their breast, the blood-inked thing that had dragged us all into this world, and I watched it tremble against their chest, tugging toward the palace like a compass needle finding north. "Brann. Something's happened in there. Something big."

Behind us, the chord swelled, and under it, threaded thin as wire, I thought — I *chose* to think — I caught a scrap of melody that didn't belong to the Cantor at all. Four notes. Dockhand's signal. All clear, come alongside.

Or maybe just a man whistling in the dark because whistling in the dark is the last defiance anybody owns.

"Walk fast," I said again, and took Wren's hand, and we went.
