## 22. What Blood Costs

Ovid bled the whole way to Low Hum.

He insisted it was nothing — "a scratch," he said, in the tone of a man who has ferried enough dying passengers to know exactly what a scratch looks like from the inside — but the wound in his side had opinions of its own, and they were loud ones. Brann carried him half the last mile with Wren running ahead like a small furious scout, and I brought up the rear holding a map that hummed against my chest like a cat that knows it's won something.

The Road of Teeth spat us out at dusk into a valley that didn't believe in silence. That was the first thing I noticed about Low Hum: you could feel it before you saw it, a vibration through the soles of your boots, as if the ground itself were humming a note too low for ears and just high enough for bones. Then the village resolved out of the gloom — low stone houses with turf roofs, smoke rising crooked from chimneys, and everywhere, everywhere, people humming. A woman pulling water from a well hummed. Two boys mending a net hummed in harmony, badly. An old man on a doorstep hummed a tune I recognized, absurdly, as a Vell street-cry for roasted chestnuts, transposed into something that sounded like grief and supper at the same time.

"They're not hiding it," Brann said, setting Ovid down against a wall. Her voice was raw from the escape. "After Corvenne, after the Wardens — they're just... doing it out loud."

"That's rather the point of a rebellion," Ovid managed, gray-faced. "You do the forbidden thing where the tyrant can see. It's called a statement."

"It's called getting everyone killed," Brann snapped, and then winced, because he was bleeding on her boots.

A door opened. A broad woman with iron-gray braids and forearms like dock rope filled the doorway, took in the four of us — the wounded ferryman, the tall ferrywoman, the child, and me clutching my own ribs like a man holding a bag of stolen coins — and said, "You'll be the ones from Amberglass, then. Maren sent word ahead by moth." She jerked her chin at Ovid. "Bring him in. And someone tell the humming to settle down, we've got work."

Her name was Hesta, and she ran Low Hum the way a keel runs a boat: invisibly, structurally, so that everything else stayed upright. Within an hour Ovid was laid out on a pallet being stitched by a woman who hummed while she worked — "it keeps the flesh listening," she told him, and he looked at her with the expression of a man falling reluctantly in love — and Wren had been fed, adopted by three separate grandmothers, and was already teaching their children a clapping game that was definitely, definitely a coded rebel signal, because everything in this village was.

And I sat by the fire with the map open on my knees, reading the last page for the hundredth time, and tried to find a way to say it that wouldn't sound like a confession.

There wasn't one. There never is. Confessions are like maps in that respect: the honest version is always ugly, and the pretty version is always a lie.

* * *

They gathered around the fire past midnight. Hesta came, and the stitcher-woman whose name was Pell, and a handful of others who drifted close under the pretense of banking coals. Brann sat across from me with her arms crossed, which was her way of saying *talk*. Wren sat pressed against my side, drowsy and warm, which was her way of saying the same thing without any threat whatsoever. Ovid lay propped on his elbow, pale as paper, refusing to be anywhere else.

"The rite," Brann said. "You've been chewing on that map since the archive. You read the final page in the cell and went quiet, and you haven't been loud since, and Silas, you are many irritating things but you have never once been quiet when you could be clever. So. Say it."

I looked into the fire. Fire is very useful for this. It gives your eyes somewhere to go that isn't other people's faces.

"The unbinding requires ink," I said. "Not ordinary ink. The binding was written in blood — Aldous's blood, freely given, mixed with the Cantor's song. To tear a binding written in blood, you write the counter-verse in blood. The rite's final line specifies the scribe's own. A cartographer's blood as ink. My blood." I turned the page toward them, because apparently I wanted to make it worse, wanted them to see the old Realmwright script sitting there patient as a trap. "*Let the hand that drew the bounds pay the price of the bound.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the village hummed on, oblivious.

"No," Brann said.

"I haven't actually proposed anything yet—"

"You were going to propose walking into Corvenne alone, cutting your palm open, and bleeding yourself dry on the palace floor while we all stood around watching." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Brann; her anger went down, not up, like water finding the lowest room in a house. "I've watched you do the math since the archive. Every time we plan, you plan around yourself. Routes you can take alone. Fallbacks where you're the fallback. I thought you were being arrogant. You were being arithmetic."

"Brann—"

"How much?" Ovid said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He was watching me with those flat ferryman's eyes that had seen every kind of passenger and trusted none of them, and I understood, suddenly, that he wasn't asking out of morbid curiosity. He was asking because he'd spent thirty years pricing passages across the Sighing Strait, and he knew that every crossing costs something, and the only unforgivable sin in his trade is lying about the fare.

"All of it," I said. "That's how the original binding worked. Aldous gave his life-blood to save himself; the Cantor gave its whole voice. The rite balances the ledger. To unmake a binding that cost everything, the counter-verse has to cost—" I made myself say it plainly, because they deserved plain. "It has to cost everything. Not a cut. Not a scar. The ink takes until there's no more ink."

Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Small, sticky with honey from supper, gripping hard.

"No," Brann said again, and stood up, and began to pace, which meant the water had found the cellar. "No. Absolutely not. We did not cross a strait, rob an archive, break into a palace, get arrested, escape in — what was it, E minor—"

"E minor," Wren confirmed solemnly.

"—and lose half our dignity to a bell tower so that you could die politely in a throne room. That's not a plan, Silas. That's a funeral with extra steps."

"It's the only plan the map allows."

"Then the map can learn some manners."

I laughed — I couldn't help it, one short bark of a laugh that hurt my ribs — and immediately hated myself for it, because laughing was easier than saying the next part, and the next part was the actual confession, the one underneath the arithmetic.

"There's more," I said. "You should know what you're arguing against. The reason the rite demands a cartographer isn't cruelty. It's symmetry. Bindings want balance. Aldous gave his life to keep living; the Cantor gave its voice to keep singing. The counter-verse needs a gift of equal weight from someone who *chooses* it. And the map—" I put my hand flat on the parchment, on the blood-dark lines I'd spent fifteen years pretending I hadn't helped invent. "The map was drawn by a Realmwright. Drawn *for* a Realmwright. It's been steering us since the day it swallowed us. Every waypoint, every turn — it wasn't guiding us to the king. It was guiding me to the inkwell. The stranger who died in my shop didn't hand me a quest. He handed me a bill, and my name was already on it."

Silence, real silence, the kind Cael Sorrow makes fall like snow. Even the village seemed to hold its hum.

"So that's it," Brann said at last. "Fifteen years of hiding, and the hiding was pointless anyway. The world knew where you were the whole time."

"Apparently."

"And you were going to let us walk you to the altar and never say a word."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because I'm a coward. Because I've been a coward so long it fits me like a coat tailored to my shoulders. Because if I told you, you'd try to stop me, and I couldn't bear both the dying and the disappointing. Because some traitorous part of me has wanted to stop running for fifteen years and this was the first exit that came with absolution attached.

What I said was: "Because it would have worked."

Pell, the stitcher, spoke for the first time. She was older than Hesta, with hands that moved even while the rest of her was still, worrying a thread. "My mother used to say the old rites were honest that way," she said. "One life buys one miracle. Clean accounting. But my mother also said the old rites were written by people who'd forgotten something." She looked at me. "You're a Realmwright. What did they forget?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing*, to say *the craft is exact, the price is fixed*, and then I stopped, because I was looking at the fire and the fire was looking back, and behind me I heard it — faint, off-key, absolutely unmistakable.

Wren was humming.

She wasn't performing it. She was half-asleep against my side, humming the lullaby she'd taught us on the road, the one that kept the night safe, the one that had made Ovid cry into his collar and pretend it was wind. And as she hummed, the map on my knees *shifted*. Not much. A tremor, like a sleeping animal dreaming. The blood-lines of the final page shivered, and for half a heartbeat the old script flickered, and underneath it — beneath the Realmwright's careful, terrible symmetry — I saw something else. A margin note. In handwriting I knew better than my own face, because it *was* my own face, fifteen years younger:

*If one voice is not enough, ask whether the song was ever meant to be sung alone.*

I had written that. I had written it as a young fool with too much talent and too little fear, a marginal joke, a private heresy against my own teachers — and someone had copied it onto this map, or the map had remembered it, or the map had been waiting fifteen years for me to be desperate enough to read my own footnotes.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out strange. "Hum it again."

She blinked awake, suspicious. "Why?"

"Humor the cartographer."

She hummed. The map shivered again, stronger this time, and the flicker lasted longer — the old line *let the hand that drew the bounds* wavering like a reflection in disturbed water, and beneath it, ghostly, a different phrase assembling itself out of possibility: *let the voices that walked together...*

"What is it?" Brann crouched beside me. "What are you seeing?"

"A loophole," I said. "Or maybe not a loophole. Maybe the front door, and I've been staring at the servants' entrance for fifteen years." I looked up at all of them — Brann with her arms still crossed but her eyes wide, Ovid propped up and forgetting to look bored, Pell with her thread stilled, Hesta filling the doorway like a mountain that had decided to listen. "The rite demands a cartographer's blood because the old rites assumed a cartographer works alone. One hand, one map, one price. But I haven't worked alone since the day this thing ate us. And the binding itself — the original binding — was two gifts fused into one prison. King and Cantor. Life and voice. It's a duet locked in a single body. You don't unmake a duet with a solo."

"You're babbling," Ovid said. "It's the most hopeful I've seen you. Continue."

"If the counter-verse needs a gift of equal weight," I said slowly, feeling my way along the edge of the idea the way you test river ice, "then weight doesn't have to come from depth. It can come from breadth. One life, given entirely — or many lives, each giving a piece, each holding a line of the verse. Blood to start the ink, yes, mine, a cut, a beginning — but the *carrying* of it, the sustaining, the part that costs everything? Split it. Share it. A chorus instead of a single ink."

"That's not how magic works," Hesta said flatly.

"No," I agreed. "That's how magic *worked*. Fifteen years ago. Before it was outlawed, before the Cantor, before anyone thought to ask whether the rules were laws or just habits with good posture." I turned to Wren. "The lullaby you taught us. Where did you learn it?"

"My da," she said. "He said it was older than the Cantor. He said it was a *weaving* song — everybody sings a different part and the parts hold each other up. He said that's why the Cantor banned singing first and everything else second." Her chin lifted, and there was the dead stranger in her face, sudden and sharp. "He said one singer can always be silenced. But a choir has to be silenced one throat at a time, and that takes forever, and tyrants are lazy."

The fire cracked. Outside, Low Hum hummed on, hundreds of small defiant notes, and for the first time I heard them properly — not as background, not as atmosphere, but as infrastructure. A realm holding itself together, one throat at a time, right under the Cantor's deafened nose.

"It won't be easy," I said. "The rite will fight us. Bindings hate improvisation. And I don't know if it will hold — a chorus is only as strong as its weakest voice, and the Cantor will aim for the weakest voice first, and we'll be standing in its throne room when it does."

"Then we rehearse," Brann said.

Everyone looked at her.

"We rehearse," she repeated, in the voice she used on passengers who claimed they couldn't possibly row another stroke and then rowed another stroke. "You heard the woman — Pell's mother. The old rites forgot something, and Silas just remembered it. So we don't walk into Corvenne with a theory and crossed fingers. We practice. Here. With these people." She swept an arm at the village, at the humming houses, at the whole stubborn valley. "Hesta. How many people in Low Hum can hold a tune?"

Hesta smiled, slow and dangerous, like a drawbridge lowering. "All of them, dear. That's rather why we're still here."

"Then starting tomorrow, they learn the rite."

Ovid groaned and dropped back onto his pallet. "I've been stabbed, conscripted, and now I'm joining a choir. My daughter would find this hilarious." He said it lightly, the way he always said her name, lightly, lightly, the lightness worn thin as old sailcloth. Then he raised one finger at the ceiling. "But I want it noted that I called this. Back on the strait. I said the map liked this one, and nobody listened."

"You said the map *wanted* something," Brann said. "You didn't say what."

"Nobody ever listens to the ferryman. It's traditional." He closed his eyes. "Wake me when the treason rehearsals start."

Wren tugged my sleeve. When I looked down, she was studying me with that unnervingly adult scrutiny she deployed at random intervals, like weather. "You're still scared," she said. Not a question.

"Enormously."

"But less than before."

I considered it honestly, the way I'd consider a coastline before charting it. "Yes. Less than before. I don't know if that's wisdom or exhaustion."

"It's us," she said, with the serene authority of a nine-year-old delivering a verdict, and burrowed into my side, and was asleep within four breaths, humming faintly even in her dreams, holding her shape together, holding all of ours.

Brann stayed after the others drifted off. She banked the fire, sat back down, and looked at me across the coals for a long moment.

"For the record," she said, "if the chorus fails — if it comes down to you and the ink and no other way — I want you to know that I will tie you to a ferry post myself. I've hauled bigger cargo than you, and I've done it in worse weather."

"Noted."

"And Silas?" The firelight caught her face, and underneath the flint there was something older and softer, the thing she usually kept below the waterline. "Thank you for telling us. Finally. It only took a death sentence."

"I work best under deadline."

"I know," she said. "It's one of your many irritating things." She stood, stretched until her spine crackled, and paused at the edge of the firelight. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we start writing a new song. And this time—" a ghost of a grin, there and gone, "—you're not writing it alone."

Outside, the valley hummed. Inside, the map lay open on my knees, and the final page had gone quiet — not silent, never silent, but quiet, the way a held breath is quiet. Waiting. On the parchment, faint as a watermark, the new words were still there, half-formed, patient:

*let the voices that walked together*

I fell asleep with my hand over them, and dreamed, for the first time in fifteen years, in harmony.

* * *

Morning came to Low Hum the way morning comes everywhere in Cael Sorrow: as a sound. A bell in the village square — a real bell, cracked and patched, tuned deliberately flat so the Cantor's listeners would dismiss it as broken — rang the rebels' waking note, and the village answered it house by house, voice by voice, until the whole valley was a chord.

Hesta stood me on a cart in the square, in front of perhaps sixty people — farmers, fishers, two defected Wardens of Song with their mute-masks hanging from their belts like shed skins, and a grandmother who informed me she'd sung in Corvenne's cathedral choir before the purge and would thank me not to teach her to breathe.

"People of Low Hum," Hesta announced, with the gravity of a woman introducing a new well, "this is the cartographer. He's going to get us all killed, but pleasantly."

"Rewritten," I corrected. "We're going to rewrite a song. The old rite says one blood must pay for the unbinding. I think the old rite was wrong — wrong the way a map is wrong when the country changes underneath it. I think the price was never meant to be paid by one throat. I think it was meant to be shared, and we forgot, and forgetting is the Cantor's favorite weapon." I held up the map, and let them see the flickering words. "So I need singers. Not good ones. Steady ones. Who's willing to be ink?"

The grandmother raised her hand first.

By noon we had a chorus. By evening we had a key — E minor, naturally; the palace bell had opinions. And by full dark, when Wren climbed onto the cart beside me and taught sixty frightened, stubborn, magnificent people the first line of a weaving song older than the Cantor, I understood at last what the dead stranger had meant when he pressed the map into my hands and whispered *finish it.*

He hadn't meant the map.

He'd meant the verse. And the verse needed every voice it could get — because in nine days, unless we sang louder than the silence, there would be no voices left to sing.
