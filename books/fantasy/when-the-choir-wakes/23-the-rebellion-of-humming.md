## 23. The Rebellion of Humming

The rebels of Low Hum did not look like rebels. They looked like a fishing village that had misplaced its fish.

There were forty-one of them when we arrived, and I counted twice because the first count seemed absurd. Forty-one people, gathered in a root cellar beneath a tanner's shed, lit by lamps shuttered with oiled cloth so no glow escaped upward. Forty-one faces turned toward me — the stranger their scouts had smuggled in over three nights, wrapped in fish tarps, because apparently that was the safest way to travel in a country where sound was contraband and I was, technically, the most wanted piece of paper in Cael Sorrow given legs.

"You're the Realmwright," said a woman at the front. Not a question. She had forearms like mooring rope and the particular patience of someone who had spent her life arguing with weather.

"I'm a charm-seller," I said, on fifteen years of reflex.

Brann elbowed me hard enough to relocate a rib. "They're the Realmwright," she confirmed.

The woman nodded slowly, as if I were a shipment she'd been promised and found slightly damaged. "Maren," she said. "I keep the songs. What's left of them." She held out a hand, and I saw that her palm was inked — not blood-ink, nothing so dramatic, just ordinary lampblack, worked into the creases until it would never wash out. Dozens of tiny marks. A notation system I didn't recognize.

"What are those?"

"Where the words used to go." She closed her fist around them, almost shy. "The Cantor took the singing. It couldn't take what we wrote on ourselves."

I have spent most of my life around maps, and I know the difference between a map and a territory, between a record and the thing recorded. But standing in that cellar looking at Maren's hands, I understood for the first time that a person can be an archive. That you can burn every library and still lose nothing, if the librarians refuse to forget.

"Show me," I said.

And forty-one people, in a root cellar under a tanner's shed, began to hum.

* * *

Here is what I knew about teaching before that week: nothing. Here is what I knew after: that it is harder than cartography and pays worse, and that I loved it with a ferocity that frightened me.

The problem was structural, and I will confess it took me two days to see it, which by my standards is practically clairvoyance. The rite — the unbinding rite from Amberglass, the one whose final line I had been carrying around like a stone sewn into my coat — required sustained harmonic structure. Seven voices in canon, holding intervals that ordinary human throats find unnatural, while the ink does its work. One voice to lead, six to hold the frame, and the whole thing balanced on a knife-edge of resonance for the length of time it takes to speak a king free.

One voice. Six voices. I had written the numbers down in my notes without thinking about them, the way you write *here be dragons* on the edge of a chart without expecting to meet any.

Forty-one singers. And not one of them able to hold a fifth for more than eight seconds.

"It's not their fault," Wren said, when I complained about it that first evening, sitting on the tanner's fence with my head in my hands like a man auditing his own funeral arrangements. "Nobody's sung together since the purge. They hum alone. In cellars. In whispers. You can't learn to carry somebody else's line if you've never heard anybody else."

She was eleven years old and correcting me, and the worst part was that she was right, which is becoming a pattern I intend to speak to someone about.

"So we teach them to listen," I said.

"We teach them to be brave," Wren said. "Listening comes after."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I gave her the last of my dried plums as payment for the lesson, and she accepted them with the gravity of a merchant closing a lucrative deal.

* * *

We started at dawn, because Low Hum's rebels worked the smokehouses and the nets by day and could only steal hours from either end of it. Dawn in that village came up gray and salt-smelling, gulls arguing overhead in the one language the Cantor had never thought to outlaw, because birdsong flattered its own.

Maren arranged them in a crescent on the shingle below the tide line, where the sea would eat our sound before it traveled. I stood at the center feeling like a fraud in a very specific flavor: not the old fraud of Vell, selling luck to the faithless, but a new one — a man asking broken people to do an impossible thing and calling it hope.

"First exercise," I said. "I'm going to sing a note. When you've found it, hold it. Don't harmonize. Don't embellish. Just — be a note."

A fisherman named Col raised his hand. "What if we're bad at it?"

"Then you're a bad note for a minute and the sea forgives you."

That got the first laugh I'd heard in Low Hum, thin and startled, like something escaping a trap. I decided then that laughter counted as rehearsal too.

I sang a low G, the note I'd been humming to myself since Amberglass, the one the rite's opening line sits on like a keel sits in water. And forty-one voices — cracked, shy, scattered across three different keys — reached for it.

It was terrible. It was genuinely, historically terrible. Somewhere in the second row a woman was producing a tone that I can only describe as *seagull with opinions*. But underneath the wreckage, something happened that made the hair on my arms stand up: they leaned toward each other. Physically. Shoulders tilting, heads cocking, whole bodies angling toward their neighbors like plants toward light, hunting for the shared pitch the way you'd hunt for a handhold on a cliff.

"There," I said, and stopped singing, and let them have it — thirty seconds of rough, wobbling, magnificent unison, forty-one strangers becoming one instrument that didn't yet know its own range.

When it finally collapsed into coughing and grins, Maren looked at me with her mooring-rope arms crossed and said, "Again."

We did it again. And again. By the third time, nobody asked what if we're bad at it.

* * *

The days found a shape, and I want to record the shape here because I suspect it matters more than any single event of that week.

Dawn: unison work on the shingle. Midmorning: the singers went to their nets and smokehouses, humming their lines under their breath as they worked, which was illegal and which the whole village conspired not to notice — the tanner's boy covering the practice with rhythmic hammering, the fishwives timing their market cries to mask the harmonies drifting from the gutting tables. Low Hum had been breaking the Cantor's law for years, I realized; they'd simply been doing it in pieces small enough to deny. We weren't teaching them rebellion. We were teaching their rebellion to stand up straight.

Afternoon: theory, in the root cellar, for the six who would hold the frame. This was the hard part, and I'll be honest about how badly I handled it at first. I taught them the way I'd been taught — precision, correction, the cold mathematics of interval — and I watched them shrink. These were people who had lost parents and children to the silencing, who sang now with the specific terror of those who know exactly what happens when the Wardens of Song hear you. Precision was a luxury. What they needed was permission.

It was Ovid, of all people, who found the way in. He sat in on one disastrous afternoon session, ostensibly mending a net, actually eavesdropping, and finally put down his needlework and said:

"You're teaching them like it's a cargo run. Like there's a right way to load and everything else spills." He limped — still limped, the wound from the Road of Teeth healing slow and ugly — to the front of the cellar. "My daughter couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. Couldn't find a fifth if you drew her a map." His voice did something complicated on the word *map*. "But when she sang along wrong, she sang along *with* you. That's the whole trick. Nobody holds the frame alone. You just agree to fall in the same direction."

Then he taught them a round. A stupid tavern round, four parts, the kind of thing ferry crews sing to keep rhythm at the oars. And inside an hour, six terrified rebels were weaving lines around each other, laughing when they collided, steering back in.

I stood at the back of my own classroom, thoroughly upstaged, and wrote in my notebook: *the map is not the territory; the score is not the song.* Underlined it twice. Felt, for the first time since a dying stranger bled onto my shop floor, like I might actually know what I was doing.

* * *

On the fourth evening, Brann found me on the seawall after dark, where I'd gone to check my figures and instead been checking my fears, which take up roughly the same amount of space.

"The frame six held the canon for ninety seconds today," she said, sitting down beside me. Her shoulder against mine, warm through the salt-stiff wool. "Ninety seconds. You need what, four minutes?"

"Four minutes and eleven seconds. At tempo."

"So you're a tenth of the way there." She said it cheerfully, which is either a gift or a cruelty depending on the day. "Silas. Look at me. Four days ago these people couldn't sing in the same key. Whatever you're doing in that cellar, it's working."

"It's not me doing it."

"No," she agreed, easily, and I felt the ground shift under the argument I'd been preparing. "It's all of them. That's rather the point, isn't it? That's why it's going to work when your blood alone wouldn't have."

I hadn't told her that part in those words yet. I'd told them all about the rite's price back on the road — the cartographer's blood, the single life the old binding demanded — and they had refused it, loudly, in shifts, for days. Wren had proposed the chorus. But hearing Brann say it plainly, sitting on a seawall with the tide going out — *that's why it's going to work* — made it real in a way the arguments hadn't. The mathematics of it. One blood cannot hold four minutes. Forty-one voices, maybe. Maybe.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Brann said, watching the water. "Vell. The district. Everyone humming their little private charms, everyone hiding their little private magic, and none of us ever — " She waved a hand. "Fifteen years I ferried that river next to you. Fifteen years. And I never once heard you sing."

"You never once asked."

"I'm asking now."

So I sang for her — quietly, there on the wall, the rite's opening line, the low G and the rising fifth above it — and she listened the way she does everything, completely, without blinking, and when I finished she said, "Right. Tomorrow I'm joining the frame six. Ovid's round was good but he counts like a drunk."

"You don't have the ear for canon."

"I have the stubbornness for it. Ear can be learned. Stubbornness you're born with." She stood, pulled me up by the wrist. "Come on. Wren's teaching the little ones a new verse and she'll sulk for a week if you miss it."

* * *

I should say something about what the singing did to the village itself, because I was a cartographer long enough to know that places change as well as people.

By the sixth day, Low Hum hummed. Not loudly — never loudly, the discipline of years doesn't break that fast — but constantly, in layers, a weave of sound running under the whole village like groundwater. The smokehouse boys had a working chant. The net-menders had rounds. Children skipped to tunes their mothers had only ever dared to think. And the strange thing, the thing I kept trying to map and failing, was that the village got *more solid* as it got louder. People stood straighter. Doors stayed unlatched. Old Halvard, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since his brother was silenced at the edict, laughed at full volume over the gutting tables and then looked around, astonished, as if someone else had done it.

Sound has weight in Cael Sorrow. I'd known that since the day the map swallowed us. But I had understood it as threat — the Cantor's song pressing down, silence falling like snow. I had not understood it as *mortar*. That a place, held in enough voices, becomes difficult to unmake.

"They're not learning the rite," Maren said to me on the sixth night, watching her villagers from the cellar door. "They're becoming it. You understand that, yes? Whatever happens at Corvenne — win or lose — this village is already free. It's been free since the first dawn on the shingle. The Cantor just hasn't been told."

"That's the plan," I said. "We tell it. Loudly. In about a week."

She smiled, and her inked palm flashed in the lamplight, all those little marks where the words used to go. "Good," she said. "I've been wanting to tell it for nine years."

* * *

It was near midnight when the scout came in.

I was in the root cellar with the frame six, running the canon by lamplight — we'd reached two minutes, thirty seconds, and the seagull woman had achieved a tone I could only call *transcendent seagull* — when the tanner's boy slid down the ladder two rungs at a time, white-faced, and didn't bother with the whisper-code.

"Boat," he said. "Coming up the coast road. Wardens' lanterns. Blue ones."

The cellar went silent in the old way — the hunted way, the way I remembered from Vell, bodies remembering danger faster than minds do. Then Maren stood, and something in the set of her shoulders changed the room's silence into something else. Readiness, maybe. Or refusal.

"How many?" she asked.

"Six riders. Maybe more behind. Moving slow." The boy swallowed. "And ma'am — they're stopping at every house on the headland. Marking doors."

Marking doors. I knew that protocol from the archives at Amberglass, from the dry accounts of the first purge that read like weather reports. Marked doors meant assessed households. Assessed households meant lists. Lists meant that somewhere in the Cantor's palace, a clerk was writing down the names of everyone in Low Hum who would need silencing when the time came.

Ovid appeared at my elbow. He'd gone very still, in the way he goes still when the tide turns against him. "They're not raiding," he said quietly. "Raiding's loud. This is surveying." He looked at me, and for once there was no wry deflection anywhere in his face. "Silas. The Cantor doesn't survey villages it plans to leave alone. It surveys villages it plans to erase."

Above us, faint through the earth, we heard the first blue lantern pass along the headland road. And beneath the fear in the cellar, underneath it, steady as a keel — forty-one throats, waking in the dark, beginning to hum.
