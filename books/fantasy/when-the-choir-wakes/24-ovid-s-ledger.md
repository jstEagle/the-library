## 24. Ovid's Ledger

I have been stabbed before. Twice by customs men, once by a bride whose father I'd shortchanged on the dowry crossing, and once — and I maintain this was an accident — by myself, in the dark, with my own boat hook. So when I say I know how to lie still while a body knits itself back together, you can take it as professional testimony.

Lying still in Low Hum, however, turned out to be the hardest work of my career.

The rebels had given me a cot in what had been a smokehouse before the war on song made everything provisional. The walls were hung with drying herbs that smelled like a grandmother's kitchen, and every few hours someone came by to hum at me. Not talk. Hum. A low, round note, held for as long as breath allowed, then passed off to whoever stood next. They called it *keeping the room*. I called it insufferable, right up until the third night, when the pain got loud enough that I understood: they were giving me something to hold onto that wasn't the hurt.

I didn't thank them. I'm not a monster. But I didn't tell them to stop, either, which from me is roughly the same thing.

The wound itself was honest work — a mute enforcer's blade, caught under my ribs on the Road of Teeth because I'd turned to shove Wren clear instead of minding my own business, which is the last time I'll ever do anything of the kind. The village healer, a broad woman named Sefa who hummed even while she stitched, informed me cheerfully that another inch and she'd have been sewing shut a memory instead of a man.

"Another inch," I said, "and you'd have saved yourself good thread."

She laughed. Everyone here laughs at death jokes. It's not gallows humor exactly; it's more that they've decided fear doesn't get a vote.

* * *

On the sixth day, Silas came in carrying a slate, some chalk, and the expression of a person about to ask a favor they've already decided you owe.

"Ovid," they said. "You've been inside Corvenne."

"Many times. Usually through doors that don't officially exist."

"The palace."

"Once or twice." I folded my arms behind my head, which cost me something around the fourth rib, and kept my face pleasant anyway. Pain is just information. You learn to file it. "Smuggling run, three years back. The client wanted a case of southern wine delivered to a lord who wasn't supposed to be drinking it. I delivered the wine and a great deal of my own opinion about his taste."

Silas set the slate across my knees. "We need to know where the Cantor keeps its wards."

"And there it is." I looked at them for a long moment. They had that quality I'd noticed since Amberglass — the one that made people tell them things. Not charm. Something quieter. A person who listens like your words are going on a map, and who treats what you hand over as though it has weight. Dangerous, in a smuggler's opinion. The best cargo-masters all had it. "You know I don't do favors for free."

"I know," Silas said. "Name your price."

And there was the trouble, wasn't it. Because I'd spent six days on this cot doing arithmetic I hadn't agreed to do, and the sum kept coming out wrong. My price, historically, was coin, or passage, or silence — things that let me walk away clean. And lately every price I could think of sounded small. Petty. Like pricing a lifeboat by the plank.

"You know what a tide table is," I said instead.

Silas blinked. "A chart of when the water rises and falls."

"A chart of *when*," I said, "and how far, and where the pull comes from. Moon, mostly. The strait doesn't move because it wants to. It moves because something big and far away is leaning on it, and if you know the leaner, you know the water." I tapped the slate. "Sound's the same. Your palace full of singing tyrants — those wards aren't walls. They're tides. Somebody set them, somebody winds them, and they rise and fall on a schedule, same as any water. I watched the guards change their humming when I was inside. Thought it was ritual. It was bookkeeping."

Silas sat down slowly on the edge of the cot. "You memorized the schedule."

"I memorize everything. It's a curse of the profession. You spend enough years reading water, you stop being able to look at anything without asking when it turns." I picked up the chalk. It felt strange in my fingers, heavier than it should have. Chalk for maps, now. My daughter had used chalk. She'd drawn boats on the inside of our door until she was seven, and I'd let her, because paper cost money and doors were free.

I drew.

I started with the shape of the palace as I remembered it from the outside — the crown of Corvenne, all spires and buttresses, built like a fist closed around a throat, which I suppose was the architectural point. Then the undercroft we'd come up through, Ossa's old smuggling routes still good after all these years, bless his crooked heart. Then, working inward, the thing I'd been turning over on this cot for six days because I couldn't sleep past the pain and the humming both:

The wards.

"They hang them in layers," I said, talking because talking kept the chalk moving. "Like current. Surface layer, easy to feel — that's the market noise, the heralds, the sanctioned songs. Loud on purpose. It covers the second layer, which is where the real work lives. The enforcers' patrol-hum. It runs in counterpoint to the surface noise — you'd never hear it unless you knew to listen for the gap between the two. Third layer's the deep one. That's around the throne room and the tower cells. Dead quiet, but not empty quiet. Pressurized. Like water over a wreck." I marked each layer in different strokes — dotted, hatched, solid. Old habit. Charts are lies you make precise enough to be useful.

"How do you know the deep layer's pressurized?" Silas asked quietly.

"Because I've crossed a pressure line before, and I know what it does to a hull." I kept my eyes on the slate. "When we were dragged in front of the Cantor — you remember the door. How the sound died at the threshold. That's not silence. Silence is nothing. That was weight. Something holding the noise *down*, the way deep water holds a body down. You cross it wrong, it crushes whatever you brought with you. Voices. Names. Maybe nerve." I shrugged, and paid for it. "Maybe souls. I wasn't eager to test which."

Silas was silent a moment. Then: "When does it turn?"

"There's my cartographer." I allowed myself one grim smile. "Tides turn. Even pressurized ones. When I was in the palace, the changing of the resonant bell — the big one in the west tower, the one Brann timed our escape to—"

"She said the bell's tone dropped for almost a minute. Long enough to move."

"Long enough to move *if you already knew*, which she didn't, which means either she's luckier than any woman alive or she listened the way I listen and hasn't admitted it yet." I chalked a circle around the west tower. "That bell is the keel of the whole arrangement. Every ward in the palace keys off its pitch. When it swings low, the layers slacken — not break, slacken, like a rope going soft between waves. Ninety seconds, maybe less. Once per day at dawn bell, once at dusk. Everything else hangs off that rhythm. Patrols, ward renewals, the feeding of the undercroft vault—" I stopped.

"The vault," Silas said. "The names."

"The names get *renewed*," I said. "I saw the clerks. Little gray men with tuning forks, going down into the undercroft on the dusk bell, coming up two hours later looking hungry. Whatever the Cantor does with the stolen names, it isn't storage. It's maintenance. Things down there need feeding, or they fade." I met Silas's eyes. "Which means your silenced villagers aren't gone. They're being kept. Badly."

Silas's hand went to the map-case at their belt — the dead man's map, the blood-inked thing that had eaten us whole. I'd seen them touch it like that before, the way you touch a scar to check it's still yours.

"That's why the rite needs the names," they said. "Not just the king. The chorus needs voices, and half the voices we need are locked in that vault."

"Well," I said. "Then it's convenient that I've drawn you a chart."

* * *

They took the slate away and came back with Brann, and then Wren, and then half the rebel council of Low Hum crowded into my smokehouse smelling of herbs and urgency, and I lay there propped on my elbows like a captain holding court, which — fine. Yes. I enjoyed it. A man's allowed one vanity per near-death experience.

Brann studied the chart with her arms crossed and her jaw set, the way she studies everything, like the information owes her money. "The dusk bell," she said. "Ninety seconds. That's how we got out. I heard the pitch drop and I moved. Didn't know why it worked. Just knew."

"You listened," I said.

"I ferried the Vell crossing for twenty years. You learn when a current changes under you." She glanced at me, and for a moment we were two professionals acknowledging each other across a crowded room. It was almost warm. I distrusted it immediately.

Wren had climbed onto the foot of my cot, which nobody had authorized, and was tracing the dotted lines with one finger. "This is like Papa's charts," she said. "He did the little dashes too. For the places he hadn't been yet."

The room went quiet in that way rooms do around Wren sometimes — everyone recalibrating, remembering whose daughter she was and what she'd inherited. The stranger who'd died in Silas's shop. The map. The word *finish it*.

"He dashed ahead," Wren said, matter-of-fact, "so you'd know where to be careful."

"Yes," I said, before anyone could make it a moment. "That's exactly what dashes are for." My voice came out rougher than intended. I coughed, professionally. "Anyway. Careful is the theme of today's lesson."

Sefa the healer pushed through to change my dressing, muttering that councils were bad for fevers, and while she worked I watched the others bend over my chart — Silas measuring distances with a thumb, Brann arguing patrol timings with a rebel who'd once been a palace laundress, Wren adding, uninvited and unchallenged, a small drawing of a fish in the corner of the undercroft, labeled *guard eel? ask Ovid.*

There was no guard eel. I told her so. She wrote *no eel (confirmed)* beneath it, gravely, and I had to turn my face to the wall for a moment on the pretext of resting.

Here is the thing I had not said out loud, and would not, and am only admitting now because a ledger is a private document and this chapter is mine:

I had been keeping accounts since the day my daughter was taken. Not the official kind. The other kind. Debts. The Cantor owed me a girl with chalk on her fingers, and interest accrued daily, and I had spent years telling myself the ledger was closed because no collection was possible. Cynicism is just grief wearing a work apron. You carry it because it's lighter than the alternative.

But lying on that cot, watching my tide-chart of the enemy's house pass from hand to hand among people who intended to *use* it — watching Silas treat my bitterness as navigation — the ledger opened. Just a crack. Enough to see the balance wasn't zero. Enough to remember that debts can be collected, if you're willing to sail into the weather.

"I want it noted," I announced to the room, "that I'm not joining your rebellion. I'm recovering. Slowly. In this bed. For weeks, probably."

"Of course," Silas said, not looking up from the chart.

"This is convalescence."

"Naturally."

"I simply happen to convalesce most effectively when consulted on matters of infiltration, timing, and tide."

Brann snorted. Wren grinned at me with her whole face, which should be regulated somehow. And Silas finally looked up, and gave me the smallest nod — the nod of one professional to another, of a person who maps hidden things acknowledging a person who reads hidden water — and said, "Then rest well, Ovid. We'll need the dusk bell, the undercroft clerks' schedule, and everything you remember about the deep layer. Take your time."

Take your time. As if I had any. As if the Great Quieting weren't coming — I'd heard the whispers moving through Low Hum that evening, the scouts' reports of villages going silent on the horizon, the Cantor winding up toward something enormous. As if the water weren't already leaning.

* * *

That night, when the room-keepers came for their shift, I hummed with them.

Just the one note. Low, round, held as long as my breath allowed, passed on to the next. I told myself it was for the pain, which was true. I told myself it meant nothing, which was not.

My daughter used to sing while she drew. Off-key, always, gloriously off-key, boats and fish and a door covered in chalk. Somewhere under Corvenne, in a vault of little gray men and tuning forks, there might be a name in a jar that used to belong to her. Might. The healer says hope is bad for fevers too.

But I've read water my whole life, and I know a turning when I feel one.

Two days later, the scouts came running in from the east ridge, white-faced, and the whole village gathered, and the news went through the crowd like cold iron: the Cantor had begun. Nine days. The Great Quieting. Villages falling silent one by one behind us like lamps going out along a shore road.

Nine days to collect on an old debt.

I got out of bed.
