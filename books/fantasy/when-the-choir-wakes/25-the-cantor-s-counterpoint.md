## 25. The Cantor's Counterpoint

The first sign was the birds.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when the histories get written, if they ever do, and if I'm the one writing them, which seems increasingly likely and increasingly terrifying — people will want to believe the Great Quieting announced itself with thunder, with trumpets, with some appropriately operatic flourish from a villain who called himself the Cantor. It didn't. It began with the birds going quiet over Low Hum, one valley at a time, like someone walking down a row of candles pinching them out.

I noticed it at dawn on the fourth day of rehearsal, standing on the ridge above the village with a cup of barley tea going cold in my hands, and I knew what it meant before I knew why I knew. Fifteen years of hiding teaches you to read silence the way sailors read weather. Silence isn't nothing. Silence has a direction.

"Silas." Brann came up the path behind me, boots crunching on frost. She'd taken to sleeping three hours a night since Ovid's wound, as though vigilance were a debt she could pay down by installments. "The miller's wife says the thrushes didn't sing this morning."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I mean I've noticed, and I have a bad feeling about it, and I'd like ten minutes with the map before anyone panics."

Brann looked at me the way she always did when I said something like that — the look of a woman doing arithmetic on how much of my confidence was real and how much was paint. She'd gotten faster at the calculation lately. The answer kept coming back: less than I claimed.

"The map," she said. "Right. The map that eats people. Let's go look at the map."

---

The map lived now in the meeting house, pinned flat under glass because Wren had learned — by nearly losing an eyebrow — that it pulled harder when it could see skin. It had changed again overnight. Maps do that here. That was the first thing Cael Sorrow had taught me: paper is not dead in this realm, it is only very patient, and ink remembers everything you've ever asked of it.

The western reaches of the map had gone grey.

Not blank — blank would have been honest. Grey, the way a page goes when something has been written on it and then written over, layer upon layer until the words beneath drown. The village of Cinderfell, where we'd slept two weeks ago, was a smudge. The Sighing Strait was a smear of ash-colored nothing. And spreading eastward from each grey place, slow as frost across a window, crept a fine tracery of lines that my fingers recognized before my mind caught up.

Notation. Musical notation, drawn in a hand I knew intimately, because I'd spent three days in a drowned archive reading it, and four hours in a cell listening to its owner breathe through stone.

"It's a counterpoint," I said.

Brann stared at me. "It's a mess."

"It's a counterpoint," I repeated, and heard my own voice go thin. "You write a melody, and then you write a second line against it — note answering note, voice against voice. It's the oldest trick in composition. You don't silence a song by stopping it. Stopping leaves a hole, and holes echo. You silence it by singing *against* it, so precisely, so completely, that there's no room left for anything else." My finger hovered over the grey without touching. "He's not erasing Cael Sorrow, Brann. He's covering it. Note by note. Village by village. Every sound in the realm gets an anti-sound, and when the last one lands—"

"—there's no room for us," Brann finished. She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but her hand had gone to the knife at her belt, which was her version of crossing herself.

Wren was already at the edge of the glass, small fingers spread, reading the notation upside down the way children read, which is sometimes the right way. "Nine days," she said.

"What?"

She tapped the margin, where a line of figures curled in that terrible elegant hand. "Rests. Whole rests, half rests — he's counting them out. Look." She counted under her breath, quick and sure; the girl had been raised on forbidden songs the way other children were raised on prayers. "Nine bars of rest at the top, and then the same figure repeats smaller, and smaller again. He's written a countdown into his own music." She looked up at me, and her eyes were too old, the way they'd been too old since the day she told me whose daughter she was. "Nine days, Silas. Then the whole song lands at once."

* * *

We called the village together in the meeting house that evening, which in Low Hum meant everyone, because Low Hum was four hundred souls and no concept of delegation. They packed the benches and the floor and the window ledges, humming low to hold their shapes — everyone here hummed constantly, a habit that had once seemed eerie to me and now sounded like home — and I stood at the front with the map behind me and told them the truth, or most of it.

"The Cantor knows we're here," I said. "He knows what we're building. And he's begun the Great Quieting."

I explained the counterpoint. I explained the nine days. I watched faces do what faces do — the miller's wife pressing her hand to her mouth, old Tam Voss going still in a way that made the humming around him falter, young people looking at older people to learn how afraid to be. In Vell I'd have called this crowd hopeless. In Vell I'd have sold them charms and slipped out the back. But Low Hum had been quietly breaking the law for a generation, teaching their children songs in root cellars, and hopelessness was a luxury they'd never been able to afford.

A woman near the back — Maren, the blacksmith, forearms like dock rope — raised her hand as if this were a town meeting about drainage. "So we've got nine days to finish learning a rite that took your archive a century to write."

"Yes."

"And the rite failed last time we tried it."

"It failed," I said, "because I was trying to complete the wrong song. We're fixing that."

"With what? You said yourself the original score is—" She stopped, diplomatic.

"Is a binding," I said. "Yes. A ritual built to tear one man away from another, written by frightened people in a drowning library. It nearly unmade Dellan when we tried it, and it would have worked, and working would have killed Aldous and left the Cantor loose with all that power and none of the restraint. So no. We're not finishing the old song." I put my hand flat on the table, on the map's living edge, and felt it lean toward me like a plant toward light. "We're writing a new one. Which is either the worst idea I've ever had or the only idea I've ever had, and after forty years of cartography I can tell you those are usually the same idea."

That got a laugh — small, ragged, real. You learn things selling charms to the faithless. You learn that people don't buy luck, they buy permission to feel lucky, and that a laugh in a frightened room is worth more than any charm I ever soldered.

Maren wasn't laughing, quite, but she nodded slowly. "Nine days," she said. "Then we'd best get back to work."

And that was it. That was the whole war council. Four hundred people filed out into the cold to rehearse, because the rebellion of Low Hum had exactly one weapon and they intended to sharpen it.

Brann stayed behind. So did Wren. So, propped in the corner on a chair dragged from someone's kitchen, pale as tallow and furious about it, sat Ovid.

"Well," said Ovid. "That went swimmingly. Nine days. Wonderful. I once moved a cargo of cursed brandy through a blockade in nine days, but that cargo didn't have to *sing*."

"How's the shoulder?" Brann asked, in the tone of someone changing the subject on purpose.

"Ruined. The healer says the mute's blade nicked something that doesn't like being nicked. I've told her to stop saying 'nicked.'" He shifted, winced, covered it with a sneer he'd clearly practiced in a mirror. "Here's my question, cartographer. Say we pull off your miracle. Say your chorus sings the Cantor's counterpoint into oblivion and the king wakes up and everyone lives. What happens to the Quieting already sung? Those grey places on your map. Cinderfell. Half the strait. Is that reversible, or are we saving what's left and calling it victory?"

I hadn't wanted to think about that. I thought about it now, because Ovid had a gift for making you think about things, and I suspected it was grief wearing a cynic's coat.

"Ink remembers," I said. "Everything written can be rewritten. That's the whole terror of my craft and the whole hope of it. If we win — if the Cantor's voice stops holding the counterpoint — the grey comes unstuck. Slowly. Like ice coming off a river. But it comes unstuck."

"And if we lose?"

"Then in nine days Cael Sorrow is the quietest graveyard in creation, and then he turns to our world, because the fold between realms is already thin and getting thinner, and Vell won't even know what's killing it."

Ovid looked at me for a long moment. Under the sneer, under the ferryman's professional detachment, something moved — the thing I'd seen once, badly, the night Wren's lullaby cracked him open like a knot in wet wood. His daughter had been among the first silenced. He'd ferried survivors out of the purge and never once, in all the years since, let himself say her name aloud.

"Nine days," he said finally. "Then I suppose I'd better finish mapping those sound-wards. Fetch me the charcoal. And someone get me a drink that isn't the healer's swamp water, or I'll do it drunk out of spite."

"That's the spirit," said Brann dryly.

"It is literally the only spirit available in this village. Your distiller should be tried for crimes."

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat up with the map instead, alone in the meeting house with one candle, doing the thing I am actually for — the thing I'd run from for fifteen years, the thing that had gotten strangers killed and kings bound and a composer fused screaming to his monarch. Mapping.

Because here is what nobody tells you about being a Realmwright: the gift isn't drawing. Anyone can draw. The gift is *listening*. A true map isn't a picture of a place; it's a conversation with it. You ask the land what it is, and if you're humble enough, and your blood-ink is honest enough, the land answers. And tonight, with the Cantor's counterpoint crawling across the west like frost, I laid my palm at the map's clean eastern edge and asked Low Hum what it was.

The answer came up through my arm like a struck bell.

Not words. Never words. A chord — hundreds of voices braided, the whole village's constant humming rendered in ink, and threaded through it, distinct as a signature, four voices I knew better than my own heartbeat. Brann's steady drone, a keel through water. Wren's bright thread, quicksilver, unbreakable. Ovid's low reluctant baritone, carrying grief the way a hull carries ballast. And mine underneath, wavering, fifteen years out of practice, but *there*.

And beneath all of it, faint, almost lost, a fifth voice. Far away. A king's voice, worn down to a whisper, keeping time.

"Oh," I said aloud, to the empty room. "Oh, you clever, stubborn man. You're singing too."

Aldous. Bound in his tower, wrapped in the Cantor's song, held under for years — and still, in whatever cracks the binding couldn't fill, keeping the beat. The true king, marking time for a rescue he couldn't know was coming, the way a prisoner marks days on a wall. The counterpoint couldn't cover him because he wasn't singing *against* anything. He was singing *with*. The Cantor's great silencing spell had a flaw the size of a heartbeat, and the flaw was the man it was built to contain.

I sat back. My hands were shaking. Not with fear, for once.

The Cantor had made one mistake, and it was the mistake every tyrant makes, the mistake I myself had made long ago when I helped write magic out of law: he believed that silence was the absence of song, when really silence is just a song waiting for enough voices.

Nine days. Nine bars of rest.

I blew out the candle, and in the dark the map glowed faintly along its living edges, and somewhere far to the west a bird tried to sing and found the notes already taken — but here, in the east, in a village that hummed in its sleep, the countermelody was already rising.

At dawn, Wren found me still at the table. She looked at the new lines I'd inked through the night — the chorus's chord, the king's buried pulse, the shape of a song that had never been sung — and then at me, and she smiled the way she did when she'd already known and was only being polite.

"Told you," she said. "Nine days is plenty. If everybody sings."

Outside, Low Hum woke humming, four hundred strong, and the frost on the windows began, very softly, to melt.
