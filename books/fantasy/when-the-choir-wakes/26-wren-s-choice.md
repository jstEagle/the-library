## 26. Wren's Choice

I had been carrying my father's death for three years before I ever learned he was dead.

That sounds like a riddle, and in Low Hum we liked riddles — they were one of the few things the Cantor's Wardens couldn't confiscate. But it's true. Papa left in the autumn of the second purge, when I was nine, with a rolled oilskin under his arm and a kiss on my forehead and a lie about a trading run to the Sighing Strait. Mama knew better. Mama always knew better. She stood in the doorway with her arms folded tight across her chest like she was holding herself shut, and she watched him go down the lane until the fog took him, and then she went inside and made supper as if nothing had happened, because that was how you survived in Cael Sorrow: you hummed through the hole where the truth used to be.

He didn't come back that winter. Or the next. And every year on the first frost, Mama would set out his bowl at the table anyway, and fill it, and we'd eat around it, and neither of us said anything, because saying something would have made the bowl real, and the bowl being real meant he wasn't coming home.

So when the stranger died in a charm-shop in another world entirely — when he bled out on the floorboards pressing a map into a foreigner's hands with his last breath — I didn't know. I couldn't know. All I knew was what arrived six months later, folded into a loaf of black bread by a smuggler who owed Mama a favor: a scrap of paper in Papa's handwriting, water-stained and hurried, that said only:

*If I don't come back, find the cartographer. The map knows her. Tell Wren the last page is hers.*

I read it forty times. I showed it to no one. Because here is the thing about being nine-going-on-twelve in a country where singing wrong gets you unmade: you learn very early which truths are load-bearing. You hold them quietly, or they bring the whole house down.

*The map knows her.* Not him. Her. Which meant Papa hadn't just been running from the Wardens all those years ago. He'd been *looking*. He'd crossed the fold between worlds somehow — I still don't know how, and maybe I never will — and found the one person alive who could finish what he'd started, and then he'd come back to Cael Sorrow to fetch me, and somewhere between there and here the Cantor's enforcers had caught up with him, and he had chosen, with his final breath, to give the map to a stranger rather than let it be taken.

Or rather: to give it to a stranger rather than carry it home himself. Because he knew if he carried it, they'd take it off his body. But a dead man handing a map to an unremarkable charm-seller in an unmagic city? That was a hiding place even the Cantor couldn't sing open.

He hid it inside his own death. Papa always did have a flair for the dramatic. I get it from him, probably.

* * *

I told them on the fourth night after Ovid's ledger was finished, in the back room of the tanner's house in Low Hum, with the Great Quieting already four days old and the silence creeping toward us from the east like frost across a windowpane.

I'd been waiting for the right moment, which is a coward's way of saying I'd been waiting for a moment that would never come, and finally Brann called me on it. She has a way of doing that. She'll look at you sideways while she's mending a net or sharpening a knife, not even watching you directly, and say, "You've got something behind your teeth, little bird. Spit it out before it goes bad."

"I know who the dead man was," I said.

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet in Cael Sorrow — not empty, but *attentive*, like the walls were leaning in. Sound has weight here; everyone knows that. What fewer people remember is that silence does too. It pressed down on us, and I felt it, and I kept talking anyway, because I'd promised myself I would.

"He was my father."

Silas's hands stopped moving over the map. They'd been moving constantly for days — tracing routes, checking elevations, muttering about sound-wards — and the sudden stillness of them frightened me more than anything I'd said. Silas looked up at me with those gray cartographer's eyes that see everything and admit to nothing, and I watched them do the arithmetic. The stranger. The shop. The blood on the floorboards. The child who had attached herself to them in a village square and refused, with the full stubbornness of her small body, to be left behind.

"You were nine," Silas said. "When he left."

"Three years ago, yes." I sat down on the flour barrel, because my legs had opinions about standing. "Mama got a note six months later. Smuggled. It said to find the cartographer, that the map knows her, that the last page is mine." I pulled the scrap from inside my shirt, where it had lived against my skin since the day it came, soft as cloth now from handling, and I put it on the table between us.

Silas picked it up the way you pick up something that might be sleeping. I watched them read it. I watched them read it again. And then I watched something happen in their face that I'd never seen before — a crack, right down the middle, the practiced charm-seller's calm splitting open along a fault line fifteen years deep.

"The margins," Silas said hoarsely. "On the map. The handwriting in the margins. I thought—" They stopped. Started again. "It matched my own childhood hand. I thought someone had stolen pages from my old workbooks. I thought the Cantor had gotten into my things, that it was bait, a trap—"

"It's not your handwriting," I said. "It's *copied* from yours. Papa studied Realmwright script for years. He had fragments of your early maps — traded half our winter stores for them, Mama says. He taught himself to write like you so the map would trust whoever held it." I swallowed. "It was supposed to be me holding it. That's why it pulled you instead — you're the Realmwright, the map wanted its maker — but the *last page*, the part that isn't drawn yet? That was always meant for me. He wrote it in the note. *Tell Wren the last page is hers.*"

Ovid whistled low, which cost him something — his ribs were still strapped from the Road of Teeth — and then said, in the tone of a man rearranging furniture in a burning house, "Well. That explains why the little bird navigates by instinct and lies fluently under pressure. Family traits."

"Ovid," Brann warned.

"What? I'm *celebrating* the revelation. This is my celebrating voice."

But his eyes weren't joking. None of ours were. Because underneath the shock of it, underneath the grief, there was a colder thing settling over the room, and I could feel everyone feeling it: the Great Quieting was eating the country behind us, and the child who had been tagging along on the quest — the child they had been *protecting* — had known things they hadn't. Had been sent. Had been *chosen*, by a dead man, for this.

Brann crouched in front of me, forearms on her knees, the way she did when she wanted you to look at her properly. "Wren. Your father sent you to find Silas. Did he send you to Corvenne?"

"No," I said honestly. "He sent me to find the cartographer. Everything after that is mine."

* * *

They argued about it, of course. They argued about it for two days, in the low murmuring way adults argue when they think the child can't hear, which is insulting, because children are the best eavesdroppers in any world and everyone over twenty seems to have forgotten being one.

Brann wanted me sent south to the coast villages, out of the Quieting's path, with a trusted runner. "She's twelve," I heard her say through the wall. "Twelve, Silas. In three days we march on a palace whose throne room kills sound. Whatever her father intended, he's dead, and dead men don't get a vote on whether their daughters walk into the Cantor's throat."

"She's also the only person alive who can read the marginalia," Silas said. "And she's been humming harmony with the chorus since the second rehearsal. Brann, I hate it too. But sending her away doesn't protect her. There's nowhere left to send her *to*. The Quieting doesn't stop at the palace gates. If we fail, the whole realm goes silent, and she goes with it. At least here she's — " a long pause — "she's with us. And the rite needs every voice that can hold one."

"And if we fail *with her beside us*?"

"Then none of it matters anyway."

That silenced Brann for a while. When she spoke again her voice had gone rough. "You've gotten good at saying terrible things calmly. Fifteen years of practice?"

"Fifteen years of practice," Silas agreed, and there was something in it that wasn't quite a joke, and I heard Brann's chair scrape, and then quieter, almost lost under the house-hum of Low Hum's evening song: "I won't lose her, Brann. I've spent fifteen years losing everything by keeping it at arm's length. I'm done. She stays, or I don't sleep again, and frankly I barely sleep as it is."

I lay in my corner of the loft with my heart going like a drum-line and thought: *oh.* Thought: *they love me. These ridiculous people — a fugitive ferrywoman, a cynical boatman with cracked ribs, a charm-seller who flinches at kindness — they love me, and they were going to break themselves trying to give me away to safety, and nobody asked me.*

Which was, when you got right down to it, the entire problem.

* * *

I called the meeting myself. Third night. Tannery back room, same barrel, same faces, plus a dozen of the chorus elders who'd earned the right to listen. I stood on the flour barrel so I'd be taller than everyone, which is a trick I recommend to anyone short and serious.

"You've been deciding for me," I said. "All of you. Since the village square. Brann decided I'd stay close where she could watch me. Silas decided I'd learn the chorus parts but not the rite's center. Ovid decided I was 'cargo with opinions.'" A ripple of uncomfortable laughter; Ovid raised his hand in partial surrender. "And I let you, because I was scared, and because it was easier to be the mascot than the messenger. But I'm done letting you. So here's the truth, all of it, and then you can decide — except you can't, actually, because the deciding part is mine. That's what inheritance means. Papa gave me the last page. Not Silas. Not the rebellion. Me."

I told them about the bread-loaf note. About the bowl Mama set out every first-frost. About the years of learning Papa's copied script without knowing why, because Mama made me practice it every week — "in case," she said, and never said in case of what — so that when the map finally came to me, my hand would fit it. About sitting in the dark of the loft in the first village, listening to Silas turn the map over and over, hearing them call the margins a mystery, and saying *nothing*, because I was afraid that if I spoke, they'd send me away, and the map was the closest thing to my father I had left in any world.

"That's the confession," I said. My voice cracked on the word and I let it. "I chose the map over telling you the truth. I'd choose it again. Now you know what I am. So decide what I am to *you*."

The silence afterward had weight, the way it does here. Brann looked at Silas. Silas looked at the note in their hands, turning it over like a page of the map itself, and I saw their thumb trace the watermark — Papa's mark, the little gull-over-wave he signed his smuggling manifests with — and something in their shoulders came undone.

"When you first grabbed my sleeve in the square," Silas said slowly, "and wouldn't let go — I assumed you were a stray. Hungry, bold, orphaned by the purges like half the children in this realm. I told myself Brann would find you a place and that would be that." They looked up. "You weren't a stray. You were the heir. Walking beside me the whole time, reading my father-craft in the margins, and I never — I never even *asked* your surname."

"Halloway," I said. "Wren Halloway. Papa was Tomas Halloway. He signed his work T. Halloway, but on the map he wrote *the Cartographer's Hand*, because he said a map shouldn't belong to a man, it should belong to the craft." I jumped down off the barrel and crossed to Silas and held out my hand, palm up, the way you offer a blade hilt-first. "He was wrong about one thing. Maps don't belong to the craft either. They belong to whoever walks them. So. Walk it with me?"

Silas took my hand. Their palm was ink-stained and scarred from the cut they'd made at the failed rehearsal, and it closed around mine careful as a birdcatcher's.

"Together," they said. "Last page and all."

* * *

We worked until dawn, the two of us, spread out on the tannery floor with the map unrolled and weighted at the corners with ink pots and a heel of bread. Silas showed me the rite's architecture — the chord-structure of the unbinding, the places where the original binding had fused king to composer like two trees grown into each other — and I showed them the marginalia they'd been staring past for weeks. Papa's notes weren't warnings, the way Silas had feared. They were *instructions*. Waypoints written for a reader who hadn't been born yet. *Here the corridor sings in thirds — mind the flat. Here the ward tastes fear; bring a joke. Here, daughter, rest, and eat something, and know that I loved you enough to plan for the version of you I'd never meet.*

That last one undid me a little. Silas pretended not to notice, and passed me the bread, and that was its own kind of kindness.

Near the end, as the sky outside the shuttered window went the color of pewter, Silas paused over the map's blank final quarter — the region beyond Corvenne, the unmapped country — and tapped it once.

"This is yours," they said. "When this is over. Whatever comes after. The last page is yours to draw, Wren. I'll teach you the craft — the real craft, not the outlawed scraps — but I won't put pen to it. A map should belong to whoever walks it."

I looked at the blank space for a long time. Somewhere below us, Low Hum was waking, a thousand throats taking up the morning round, holding the town together against the creeping silence, and I thought about my father walking out of the fog with a rolled map under his arm, and my mother setting out his bowl, and the stranger dying in a far city whispering *finish it* to a person he'd never met, and how all of it — all of it — had been aimed at this: a girl and a cartographer and a blank page, at dawn, in a town that refused to be quiet.

"All right," I said. "But I'm drawing the gull-and-wave in the corner. Family mark."

"Wouldn't dream of stopping you."

Then Brann's fist hit the door — three sharp knocks, the alarm-rhythm — and her voice came through the wood, tight and fast: "Scout's back from the east road. The Quieting's moved. Silas — it's taken Millbrook. Whole town, gone silent overnight. We've got five days, maybe less, and the road to Corvenne runs straight through what's left of it."

Silas and I looked at each other over the map — over my father's handwriting, over the blank last page, over the route to the capital that now ran through a town where nobody would ever hum again.

I rolled up my sleeves.

"Then we'd better walk fast," I said.
