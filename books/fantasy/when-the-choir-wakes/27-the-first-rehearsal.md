## 27. The First Rehearsal

The trouble with miracles, I've learned, is that everyone wants one on a schedule.

Low Hum's meeting hall had been a granary before the Cantor's edicts emptied it of grain and hope in equal measure. Now it held forty-two singers arranged in the rough crescent I'd chalked on the floorboards — farmers, ferry-hands, two retired Wardens of Song who'd defected with their tuning forks still in their pockets like smuggled knives, and old Maren Hale, who claimed she'd once sung at Aldous's court and could prove it by holding a note until your ears apologized.

Forty-two voices. One rite. One cartographer's blood to bind them all together.

What could possibly go wrong.

"Again," I said, and Maren Hale cracked her knuckles like she was going to fight someone, which, in a sense, she was.

* * *

Let me back up, because I've been avoiding this part the way I used to avoid my own reflection during the Vell years — thoroughly, and with excellent excuses.

The rite from Amberglass is beautiful. That's the trap of it. Old bindings always are; nobody writes an ugly ritual, because ugly rituals don't get performed. The unbinding rite is written as a single sustained line — one voice carrying the melody while the ink does its work, the singer's breath and the cartographer's blood braided into a cord that pulls king free of Cantor without tearing either of them loose from the world.

One voice. That was the design flaw we'd all agreed to ignore, because the alternative was admitting that no single throat in Cael Sorrow could hold that line for the eleven minutes the rite demanded. Not even Maren Hale. Especially not Maren Hale, who was sixty-eight and smoked a pipe like a chimney with opinions.

So: forty-two singers, sharing the load. A relay. Each voice taking up the line as the last one tired, passing the melody hand to hand like a bucket brigade against a fire.

I'd spent six days teaching them the line. Six days of humming in a granary that smelled of dust and old barley, of drawing the notation on slate because paper was rationed, of Wren translating my Realmwright shorthand into plain speech for people who'd never seen a staff in their lives. And they were good. Genuinely good. There's something about people who've been forbidden to sing for fifteen years — when you finally give them permission, they don't hold back. They sing like each note might be their last meal.

Which, as it turned out, was closer to the truth than any of us wanted.

* * *

The rehearsal began at dusk, because the rite required "the hour between lights," which is a phrase the archive used freely and never bothered to define. We decided dusk was close enough. Rituals are mostly confidence anyway; I say that as a professional.

Brann stood at the door with her arms crossed, doing her ferrywoman's assessment of the room the way she assessed currents — looking for the undertow. Ovid sat on a grain sack near the back, his wounded side propped against the wall, pretending to be bored, which is how he paid attention. Wren perched on the chalked crescent itself, right at the point where the first voice would stand, because she had appointed herself my assistant and no one had been fool enough to argue.

"Remember," I told them, "if you feel the line pulling — if it starts to feel less like singing and more like being sung — let go. Pass it off. Nobody holds on past their strength. That's not failure, that's the whole design."

Maren Hale nodded solemnly and then winked at me, which should have been my first warning. People who wink before dangerous work are either liars or heroes, and both kinds get you killed.

We began.

I won't pretend I can describe what forty-two forbidden voices sound like when they find a line together. I'm a cartographer; I deal in distances. But there's a moment in any chorus — I'd read about it, never heard it — where the individual voices stop being countable and become a single weather system. We hit that moment maybe eight bars in. The granary walls hummed. Dust rose off the beams like it was trying to listen. Somewhere outside, I'd swear the frogs went quiet out of respect.

And then the rite woke up.

Here's what the archive didn't say, or said so quietly I'd skimmed past it: the binding at Amberglass wasn't just a song. It was a *hunger*. The original composer hadn't written a melody; he'd written a mouth. The line we were singing was shaped exactly like the thing it was meant to undo — the same architecture, the same pull, the same appetite for a voice to fill it. Singing the unbinding was, note for note, an act of offering yourself to be bound.

The first three singers felt it and passed the line off cleanly, just as trained. The fourth hesitated half a beat too long. By the seventh, the melody had stopped moving down the crescent and started *pooling*, gathering around one spot like water finding a crack in a hull.

It found Tam Broadhurst.

Tam was nineteen, a miller's son with a voice like warm honey and the specific courage of someone who has never yet been seriously hurt. He took the line because it came to him, and he held it because he was strong, and he kept holding it because the song told him he was the only one who could — and the song, I realized with a horror that arrived about four seconds too late, was telling all of them that. It was designed to isolate. To make every other voice feel unnecessary. That's how the original binding had worked on Aldous: not by force, but by convincing the world that one voice was enough, that no one else need carry anything, that everyone else could rest.

The rest of the chorus fell silent. Not all at once — in ones and twos, like candles going out. And Tam stood alone in the middle of the crescent, singing a line that was now visibly *drinking him*. I don't mean metaphorically. The sound coming out of him was getting thinner, paler, and where it passed through the air it left faint gray streaks, like frost forming on glass in the shape of a scream.

"Tam." My voice didn't carry. Nothing carried; the song had eaten all the room's acoustics for itself. "TAM."

Wren got there first. Of course she did. She got her hands on his arm and pulled, and the song pulled back, and for one endless moment my entire found family was a tug-of-war rope with a nineteen-year-old in the middle.

Then Brann did the only sensible thing anyone did that night. She picked up the iron poker from the hearth and smashed it against the granary's great bell-hook, and the clang tore through the melody like a rip through sailcloth.

The song died. Tam folded up like a dropped marionette. Brann caught him before the floorboards could.

In the ringing silence afterward, I looked at the gray streaks hanging in the air where his voice had been, and I understood — with the particular clarity that only arrives after you've nearly gotten someone killed — that I had made a category error.

* * *

They laid Tam on sacks by the hearth. His color came back slowly, like tide returning over flats. Wren sat with his head in her lap and hummed the lullaby — her own, the safe one, the one with no hooks in it — and gradually his breathing remembered its own rhythm.

Ovid crouched beside me where I sat staring at my notes like they'd personally betrayed me. Which, to be fair, they had. I'd copied them faithfully from a book written by a man who bound himself to a dying king and called it mercy.

"Go on, then," Ovid said. "Deliver the eulogy for your plan. I'll wait."

"The rite works," I said. "That's the problem. It worked exactly as designed. It's designed to take everything from one person and call it devotion."

"Mm." He turned his pipe over in his fingers — unlit; the rebels' tobacco ration had gone the way of most things. "You know what I hauled across the Sighing Strait for thirty years? Everything. Grain, coffins, brides, fugitives. Never once met cargo that improved by putting it all in one boat." He tapped the cold pipe against his knee. "You built a fleet, cartographer. Then you sailed them all in single file behind one hull and acted surprised when the lead ship started taking water."

"I didn't *design* it that way. The archive—"

"The archive was written by the man who became the Cantor." Ovid's voice stayed mild, which with him meant furious. "You're using his sheet music and wondering why it sounds like him."

I wanted to argue. I want you to understand how much I wanted to argue — fifteen years of hiding teaches you to defend even your worst ideas like family. But he was right, and worse, he was right in a way I'd already half-seen and walked past, because walking past inconvenient truths was the finest skill I'd ever developed.

The rite couldn't be fixed. It could only be replaced.

* * *

Brann found me an hour later at the big slate, where I'd wiped my careful transcription clean and was staring at the empty black rectangle like it owed me money.

"Tam's sleeping," she said. "He'll keep his voice. Mostly. He says—" She stopped, and I watched her decide whether to soften it, and watched her decide against it, because Brann has never softened anything in her life including butter. "He says it felt wonderful. Right up until it didn't. Says the song made him feel chosen."

"That's how it gets you."

"That's how *he* got Aldous." She leaned against the wall beside me, shoulder almost touching mine. In Brann's vocabulary, almost touching is an embrace. "So. New plan?"

"New plan," I said, and picked up the chalk, and my hand shook, and I let it shake, because apparently I'm done pretending my hands are steady when they aren't. "Except I don't have one. Every text I know — every binding, every unbinding, every rite in Amberglass — is built on the same bone. One voice, one sacrifice, one vessel. The whole discipline assumes power concentrates. That's the founding principle of Realmwork. You gather, you focus, you pour it through a single point and spend a single person."

"And if the principle's wrong?"

"Then the discipline is wrong. Then everything I was taught—" I laughed, and it came out ragged. "Brann, I helped outlaw magic because I believed the texts. Believed power was a blade that needed controlling, and better it sit in one trained hand than loose in the world. Fifteen years running from my own signature, and the whole time I was running from an idea."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the village hummed its evening rounds — Low Hum's nightly practice, forty small unsanctioned songs drifting through the dark like lanterns.

"When I was learning the strait," she said eventually, "my instructor told me never to fight a crosscurrent head-on. You can't overpower water. You *join* it. Find where the currents already agree to go, and put your boat there." She nodded at the blank slate. "Maybe songs are water."

Maybe songs are water. I have been paid, praised, hunted, and nearly executed for my mind, and the best idea I ever received came from a ferrywoman leaning against a wall smelling of woodsmoke.

Because here's what I saw, the moment she said it. The rite failed tonight not because forty-two voices were too many, but because I'd asked them to sing *in relay* — one at a time, in the old shape, a chain of soloists. What if the shape itself was the error? What if the line didn't need to be *carried* at all? What if it could be *held* — communally, the way a roof is held, no beam bearing the whole weight? The Cantor's binding isolated because isolation was its nature. So the unbinding shouldn't mirror it. It should *contradict* it. Not one voice poured through one wound, but many voices woven into something no single throat could contain or be contained by.

Not completing the map. Rewriting it.

I grabbed the chalk.

"What are you doing?" Brann asked.

"Cartography." I drew the crescent of singers, and then I drew lines between every voice and every other voice — not a chain, a *web*, a lattice dense enough that no node could fail alone. "The old map shows where the binding is. I don't need to walk to it. I need to draw a new country on top of it. A song with no center. A chord so wide that when the Cantor listens for a single throat to swallow—" I tapped the web, "—there isn't one. There's everybody."

"You're inventing a rite. Tonight. From scratch."

"I'm inventing a *map*," I said, "tonight, from scratch. The rite is just what happens when people walk on it together." And then, because honesty has become a habit I can't break since the archive: "It might not work. It has literally never been done. The last person who tried anything this new was the Cantor, and look how that went."

Brann considered this with the gravity she gave weather reports. "Well," she said. "At least you're consistent."

* * *

I worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. The lattice grew under my chalk — forty-two points, and then, when I counted the village's absent singers, the sick, the scared, the ones who'd fled to the hills, more points ghosted in beyond the crescent, because a web doesn't stop at whoever showed up tonight. At the center, where the old rite had placed its single sacrifice, I drew nothing. An absence. A door left open on purpose.

Wren appeared sometime past midnight, barefoot, dragging a blanket, trailing sleep like a scarf. She studied the slate with her head tipped, the way she studied everything, as though the world were a puzzle box she intended to open with her teeth.

"It looks like a spider," she said critically.

"It looks like a *web*."

"Spiders eat things in webs." She pointed at the empty center. "Who goes there?"

"No one. That's the point. No one goes there. Everyone holds the edges."

Wren frowned at it for a long time. Then she reached up, took the chalk from my hand, and in the empty center she wrote a single word in her round, stubborn child's hand:

*everybody.*

"There," she said. "Now it's finished."

I looked at that word for a long time. Long enough that she got bored and went back to bed, which is the privilege of nine-year-olds.

Because she was right, and she was wrong, and both at once. The center wasn't finished — nothing was finished, the ink wasn't mixed, the Cantor's Great Quieting was grinding toward us day by day like a glacier with a grudge. But the *shape* of it, the true shape, the thing I'd failed to see through six days of rehearsals and one nearly-fatal performance:

The old song had a hole in the middle shaped like a person. The new one had a hole in the middle shaped like *everyone*.

I picked up the chalk again. Outside, Low Hum hummed. And somewhere north, in a palace where sound went to die, the Cantor was listening — I could feel it the way you feel a storm front — listening for one voice, one throat, one sacrifice.

Let it listen.

By morning, I was going to teach forty-two people to sing a song with no throat at all.
