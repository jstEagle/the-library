## 28. Brann's Shore

The thin place opened on a Tuesday, because of course it did. Vell had never once done me the courtesy of drama on a convenient day.

Silas found it first — or rather, the map found it and Silas found the map doing it, which is how most things happened in our little expedition. We'd been camped in the ruins of an old listening-post on the road back from Amberglass, and I'd gone to fetch water from a stream that hummed when you touched it, and when I came back Silas was standing at the edge of camp holding the folded map like it had bitten them.

"It's showing a door," they said.

"Maps don't show doors."

"This one does." They turned it toward me. There, inked in that same blood-dark brown I'd learned to stop flinching at, was a small arch drawn in the margin of Cael Sorrow's western coast, with a line of Silas's cramped handwriting beneath it: *thin place — Vell side, the ferry stairs, low tide.*

I knew those stairs. I'd climbed them every working morning for eleven years, my boots ringing on wet stone while the harbor fog came in off the Sighing Strait like something that wanted its name back.

"That's my dock," I said.

"I know."

"That's *my* dock, Silas."

"I know," they said again, gentler now, the way you talk to someone standing on ice. "And the tide's low tonight. And we need your hands for what comes next, because mine shake too much for fine work anymore." They looked down at their palm, at the thin white scar the rite's first attempt had left there, and didn't finish the thought. They didn't have to. We all carried the memory of that night in Low Hum — the chorus fraying, Maren the weaver nearly coming apart at the seams of herself before Silas caught her song and held it. We needed tools. Real ones. The kind a Realmwright keeps hidden under the floorboards of a charm-shop in a city that hangs people for less.

"You want me to go back," I said.

"I want you to *choose* to go back," Silas said. "There's a difference. There always was, with you."

Ovid, who had been mending a net by the fire with the concentration of a man pretending not to listen, spoke without looking up. "Take the whistle-song. And take this." He tossed me a stub of chalk. "Mark the door behind you. Thin places drift. I've lost three cargoes and one brother-in-law to drifting doors, and I liked two of the three."

Wren was asleep with her head on her father's old map-case, and I was glad of it. Some doors you don't let children watch you walk through.

* * *

The crossing felt like being swallowed politely.

One moment I was standing in cold grass under a sky the color of a bruise healing; the next there was salt fog in my throat and stone under my boots and the particular gray light of Vell at low tide, which is to say light that has given up on ambition. The ferry stairs rose above me, slick and familiar, and the harbor spread out below with its boats knocking against their moorings like impatient teeth.

Eleven days. That's how long I'd been gone, realm-time. In Vell it might be different — time ran strange through thin places, Ovid said, like water through a cracked jug — but my body didn't care about metaphysics. My body knew these stairs. My knees knew them. Eleven years of hauling nets and ferrying dockworkers and smuggling a little on the side, because a woman's got to eat, and all of it lived in my legs as I climbed.

That was the thing nobody tells you about leaving a life. You think you leave it behind. You don't. You carry it in your knees.

The Wardens were still sweeping the Lantern District — I could see their lanterns moving between the rooftops, slow and methodical, hunting whoever killed the stranger who died in Silas's shop. Hunting Silas, probably, or whatever story they'd built around the death. I kept to the shadowed side of the street out of habit, hood up, gait changed. A ferrywoman walks one way. A woman who doesn't want to be known walks another. I'd learned both walks young.

The shop was exactly as Silas had left it: shutters closed, sign swinging — *ALMEIDA'S CHARMS & CURIOS*, the paint peeling on the ampersand — and a Warden's notice pasted to the door, the wax seal unbroken.

*PREMISES SEALED PENDING INQUIRY. ENTRY BY AUTHORIZED PERSONS ONLY.*

I stood there a moment, reading it twice, feeling something complicated move through my chest. Sealed pending inquiry. Like a life could be put on hold the way you'd put a hold on cargo. Like someone might come back and pay the duty and collect.

I went around the back, where the window latch was broken because Silas had been meaning to fix it for fifteen years, and let myself into the dark.

* * *

The shop smelled the way it always had — beeswax and dried lavender and underneath it, so faint you'd miss it if you weren't looking, the ozone tang of real magic kept banked like a fire in a stove. I'd stood in this room a hundred times buying nothing, chatting, keeping an eye on my odd neighbor, and I'd never once smelled it. Now it hit me like a wave and I understood how much I hadn't known, and how much Silas must have carried, alone, in this exact room, for fifteen years.

No wonder they'd grabbed onto a dying stranger's map like a drowning man grabs a rope. No wonder they'd been half-terrified and half-relieved when it pulled us under.

The floorboards near the hearth were loose — third board from the left, if I remembered Silas's drunken confession right, the one night they'd had too much plum wine after the widow Hesk's funeral and talked more than they should have. I pried it up. Beneath: a wooden box, iron-banded, locked with a mechanism that wasn't a lock at all but a puzzle of interlocking brass rings that shifted when you breathed on them wrong.

I sat back on my heels and swore.

"Fine," I told the empty shop. "Riddles. Of course. Why would a cartographer use a key like a normal person."

It took me the better part of an hour. The rings wanted pressure in a pattern, and the pattern, when I finally saw it, was a coastline — I traced it with a fingertip and the rings fell open like a flower, and I laughed out loud in the dark, alone, which is either madness or joy and is often both. A coastline. The man had locked his life's work behind a shoreline. He'd been homesick for a world he'd helped outlaw, and he'd built the missing of it into his own front door.

Inside the box: pens of quill-feather and something darker that might have been storm-petrel bone. Three inkpots, sealed with wax stamped with a sigil I recognized now from the map's margins — the Realmwright's mark, a compass rose with one arm bent like a walking stick. A folding rule of graduated brass. A burnished case of needles fine as eyelashes. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a journal with a cracked spine, and I almost didn't open it, because some things you shouldn't read even when they're handed to you by a floorboard.

I opened it.

The early pages were maps in miniature — coastlines, river systems, mountain ranges rendered in that impossible blood-ink, each one annotated in a younger hand than the one I knew, quick and sure and full of itself. *Realmwright survey, Year 12 of the Accord.* The Accord. The law that made magic contraband. The law Silas had helped write, and then run from, and then spent fifteen years hiding under a charm-seller's apron.

But it was the later pages that stopped my breath. Because somewhere in the middle of the journal, the handwriting changed. It got smaller. Careful. Afraid. And the maps stopped being surveys and started being *hiding places* — routes out of cities, blind spots in Warden patrols, the locations of other thin places, marked and dated across years. Fifteen years of a man mapping his own cage so precisely he could see its bars.

And on the last written page, undated, just four words:

*I should have stayed.*

I closed the journal and sat in the dark of the sealed shop and thought about the version of Silas who'd written that. The one who'd signed the Accord believing order was mercy, and watched what order did, and run, and hated himself in ink for a decade and a half. The one who'd stood in this room selling fake luck to widows and keeping one trembling finger's worth of real magic warm in his palm like a coal he couldn't put down and couldn't dare to use.

Then I thought about the Silas I knew now. The one who'd stood in front of the whole village of Low Hum and said *no* — not to the sacrifice, not at first, but eventually, gloriously, to the whole idea that one person's blood could buy what only many voices could build. The one who was, at this moment, back in Cael Sorrow teaching a chorus of fishermen and weavers to sing a binding apart.

Same man. Same handwriting, underneath. Just a different map.

"Should have stayed," I said aloud, to him, across the fold. "Liar. You should have *left sooner.*"

* * *

I was wrapping the tools in my spare shirt when the bell over the shop door rang.

I went still as ballast. Footsteps — two sets, unhurried, the particular scuff of boots worn by men who walk for a living. Wardens' cadence. Through the gap in the shutters I saw their lantern-light swing across the street, and heard one of them say, low, "...sealed since the Almeida business. Kell wants a second look at the stock. Half these charm-sellers are running the real thing, you ask me."

The second voice grunted. "Almeida. The one that vanished? Dockside gossip says the ferrywoman next door went missing same week. Brann, her name was. Ran the morning crossing."

My own name, said in a lantern-lit street by strangers, landed in my stomach like a dropped anchor.

"Missing or dead?"

"Missing. Left her boat tied up. Sister's been asking after her at the market." A pause, and then, with the flat cruelty of bureaucracy: "Likely debt. Or a man. They always leave something tied up."

They tried the door. The seal flared faintly blue against the wood — Warden-wax, honest work, and for once I blessed the institution I'd spent my life dodging — and the taller one muttered something about coming back with a writ, and their footsteps moved off down toward the harbor stairs, arguing about supper.

I stood in the dark with my heart going like a drum in a parade and realized two things at once.

First: my sister Mara had been asking after me. Mara, who I'd left a note for that said *gone on a long charter, don't wait up*, because I am a coward and she has never once believed a word I say. She'd be worried. She'd be furious. She'd be at my boat right now, probably, sitting in it out of pure spite, waiting for me to come explain myself.

Second: they'd said *the ferrywoman next door went missing same week.* Which meant that in Vell's ledger, Brann of the morning crossing was now a loose end. A question with no answer. And unanswered questions in Vell had a way of becoming warrants.

I could stay. I could go find Mara, sit in my boat, tell her everything — the fold, the singing country, the king in the tower, the child with the whistle — and watch her face do the thing it does when she decides whether to believe me. I could come back to my stairs tomorrow and the day after, haul my nets, take my fares, live the life I'd built out of the wreckage of the first one.

Or I could close this door.

I found myself back at the hearth, looking at the gap where the floorboard had been. Empty now. The box in my arms, the journal against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Here's what I knew that the Wardens didn't, that Mara didn't, that Brann-of-the-morning-crossing hadn't known eleven days ago: that there was a country on the other side of the tide where sound had weight and silence fell like snow, and the people there held themselves together with humming, and a girl had taught us a lullaby that kept the dark off, and a cynical ferryman had wept for his daughter, and a disgraced king had mouthed *hurry* from a tower window, and a man who'd spent fifteen years apologizing to the air was finally, finally writing himself back into the world.

You can't know a thing like that and go back to hauling fares across a gray harbor. Not really. You can do it. People do it all the time. But you'll always be two women, and the one below decks will be humming songs that don't exist here, and eventually the crew will notice.

I went out the back way, past the broken latch Silas never fixed, and stood a moment in the alley with the box under my arm.

"Mara," I said to the fog, knowing she couldn't hear me, "when this is done, I'm coming back, and you're going to throw something at my head, and then I'm going to tell you the best story you've ever heard. So keep my boat dry."

It wasn't good-bye. I've made enough good-byes to know the difference. It was a *later* — and later is a promise, and promises are just maps you draw for other people.

* * *

The tide was turning when I reached the ferry stairs. The thin place shimmered at their base like heat off summer stone, and beyond it I could see the listening-post's cold grass, and firelight, and three figures — one small and bouncing, one large and pretending not to bounce, one sitting very still with a map across their knees.

Wren saw me first. She shouted something that crossed the fold as pure melody, no words attached, and it went through me like warm water.

I took one last look at Vell — the harbor, the stairs, the gray light giving up on ambition, the whole dear damp stubborn city that had taught me both my walks — and I found I wasn't sad. I was a door, and doors aren't sad. Doors are just honest about which way they open.

I stepped through with the box in my arms and the journal against my heart.

Silas met me at the edge of the grass, eyes going straight to the oilcloth bundle, hands rising toward it and stopping short, the way you reach for something you're afraid will be taken away.

"You got it," they said. "All of it?"

"All of it." I unwrapped the bundle enough to show the inkpots, the pens, the brass rule catching the firelight. Then, before I could lose my nerve, I pulled out the journal and held it out. "And this. Your survey books. From before."

Silas went very still. "Where—"

"Third board from the left. Coastline lock. Took me an hour, you absolute menace." I pushed it into their hands. "I read the last page."

Their face did something complicated — the flinch of a man shown his own worst sentence. "*I should have stayed,*" they said quietly. "Yes. I wrote that the night before the stranger came. I'd decided that was the end of it. Growing old in the shop. Dying unremarkable."

"Well," I said, "it's a good thing you're a lousy cartographer of your own future."

Behind them, Wren was already asking what was in the box, and Ovid was already telling her not to touch, and the fire was already being fed, and somewhere east of us, nine days were burning down toward Corvenne like a fuse.

Silas opened the journal to a blank page, uncapped a pen of storm-petrel bone, and looked up at me with something new in their face — not gratitude exactly. Recognition. The look of a man realizing the map was never meant to be finished alone.

"Brann," they said. "I need to show you what the last page wants. And then I need your help drawing something that's never been drawn."

Far off, past the hills, the horizon flickered — once, twice — like a great throat clearing before a song. Or a silencing.

We had eight days left, and I'd brought the ink.
