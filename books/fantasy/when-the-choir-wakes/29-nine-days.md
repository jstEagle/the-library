## 29. Nine Days

I have ferried the dead, the drunk, and the dead-drunk across the Sighing Strait for thirty years, and I will tell you what I learned from all of them: nobody ever believes the water is going to rise until it's at their chin.

The Great Quieting began on a Tuesday.

I know because Tuesdays were my daughter Maren's market days, and I have never once in eleven years managed to forget one. She'd come down to the boat with her skirts hiked up and her arms full of eels she'd sworn were for supper but were mostly for naming. She named everything. Eels, gulls, the third plank from the bow, which she called Gerald. When the Cantor's purge came through our village and took every voice that wouldn't kneel, it took hers mid-sentence — she was arguing with me about the price of mussels, of all things, and then her mouth was moving and nothing was coming out, and then not even her mouth was moving, and then —

Well. You know how silence works here. It doesn't leave a body. It leaves a *shape* of a body, humming faintly at the edges where the song used to hold it together, like a coat still warm after the man's gone out of it.

So: Tuesday. The day the sky over Corvenne went the color of a held breath, and the first of the villages behind us stopped singing.

* * *

We'd been marching two days out of Low Hum when we heard it happen to Thornhollow.

You don't hear a village go silent, is the thing. That's the whole obscenity of it. You hear everything else get louder — your own boots, your own breath, the creak of Brann's pack straps, Wren's whistle-song going soft and automatic the way she did when she was scared and would've bitten anyone who said so. And underneath all that, where there should have been the far-off hum of a living place, there was nothing. A hole in the world's music, clean-edged, like a tooth knocked out.

Silas felt it first. They stopped dead in the middle of the column with their hand pressed flat against the map case at their chest, and I watched the color go out of their face by degrees, like a lamp running down.

"Thornhollow," they said. Just that. Their voice had that flat quality I'd learned meant they were holding something shut behind their teeth.

"How far?" Brann asked. She'd come up alongside them without seeming to hurry, which was her trick; Brann never hurried anywhere, she simply arrived before everyone else.

"Half a day back."

Brann looked at Silas, and Silas looked at the road ahead, toward Corvenne, where the sky was doing its held-breath trick, and I saw the arithmetic run behind both their faces. Half a day back. Nine days left. A chorus of two hundred hummers and farmers and one disgraced ferryman, marching on a palace that ate sound for breakfast.

"We keep walking," Silas said.

Nobody argued. That's the part I want written down somewhere, if anybody ever writes this down: nobody argued, and I think it's because we all understood, in the wordless way crowds understand things, that if we turned around even once, we'd turn around forever. Grief is a current. You cross it or you drown in it, but you can't stand in it.

Wren took Silas's hand. Didn't say anything. Just took it, the way you'd take hold of a rope in bad water, and kept walking, and Silas let her, and I looked away because some things aren't mine to see.

* * *

By the third day we could count the silences.

They came in the night mostly, or what passed for night under that bruised sky — each one a new hole punched in the realm's hum, and the old woman Vess, who'd been Low Hum's songkeeper since before the Cantor had a name worth spitting, taught us to feel for them the way sailors feel for weather in their knees.

"Hold your own note," she told us, walking the column with her crooked stick tapping time. "Hold it steady, and when a silence opens, you'll feel it pull at you. Like a draft under a door. That's it wanting you. Don't answer it. Don't fill it. Just hold."

I held my note. Badly. I'm a ferryman, not a choirboy; my singing voice has been described as "a structural concern." But I held it, and on the third night I felt the draft come through — cold, patient, *interested* — and I understood for the first time what we were actually marching against. Not an army. Armies you can fight. This was a hunger with a crown on it, going door to door through the whole country, drinking every voice that wouldn't hide.

And every morning the map told Silas which villages hadn't hidden fast enough.

That was the worst ritual of it, worse than the nights. Each dawn Silas would unroll the map — my map now too, I supposed, though I still thought of it as the dead man's, as Maren's killer's, though I'd stopped saying that out loud somewhere around the Road of Teeth — and read off the names. Not names of places anymore. Names of *absences*. Harrow Fen, gone. The Two Sisters, gone. Little Saltwick, where I'd once run contraband brandy to a customs officer's wife and gotten paid in kisses and a warning about Wardens, gone so thoroughly that the map showed the coastline continuing smooth and unbroken, as if the town had never been inked at all.

The map was forgetting them. That's what blood-ink does, Silas explained once, very quietly, to no one in particular: it remembers what matters, and the Cantor was teaching the world that these places didn't matter.

"So we write them back," Wren said.

She'd taken to carrying a stub of charcoal and copying the lost names onto her forearm, one under another, until she ran out of arm and started on Brann's, who grumbled about it and rolled up her sleeve to make room. By the fifth day Brann's forearm looked like a tally-keeper's ledger and she guarded it like one.

"It's not much," Wren said, catching me watching. Her chin was up in that way of hers that dared you to disagree. "But they're not forgotten while somebody's carrying them."

"No," I said. "It's not much."

It was everything. It was exactly the kind of not-much that the Cantor couldn't abide, and I think — I *know*, later, when it mattered — that those charcoal names on two forearms did more damage to that thing than any army could have.

* * *

On the sixth night we made camp in the ruins of a waystation on the King's Road, and I couldn't sleep, so I went and sat with the horses. Not horses. Long-necked gray brutes the rebels called marsh-geldings, ugly as sin and twice as stubborn, and the only creatures I'd met in this whole cursed country whose company I preferred to most people's.

Ovid, talking to horses. My daughter would have laughed herself sick.

She'd have laughed, and then she'd have sat down next to me and asked the question everybody else was too polite to ask, which was: *why are you here, Da?*

Because here's the truth I'd been hauling around like ballast since Low Hum: I didn't join this march for the realm. I didn't join it for Aldous, whoever he was when he was a king and not a prisoner mouthing *hurry* from a window. I joined it because the dead stranger with the map — Wren's father, though I didn't know that yet when I signed on — because he'd once done me a kindness I never repaid, and because his killers wore the same face as my daughter's killers, and because a man can only ferry other people's griefs across so many straits before he has to admit the one he's been standing in himself.

I owed a debt. That was the story I told. Debts are clean. Debts have ledgers and terms and an end.

But sitting there in the dark with the marsh-geldings breathing their cabbage breath on my neck, I finally admitted what the ledger didn't show: I wasn't here to pay anything. I was here because Maren used to sing while she worked, badly, constantly, songs she made up about eels and tides and her idiot father, and the last thing the Cantor took from the world was the sound of her, and I wanted — with a greed that shamed me more than cowardice ever had — I wanted to be there when it gave something back.

Footsteps behind me. Brann, of course. The woman moved quieter than gossip and appeared at exactly the moments you'd decided you were alone.

"Can't sleep either?" she said, settling onto the mounting block like she owned it.

"I sleep fine. I just prefer the company of animals."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. In the dark her forearm caught the firelight from camp — the ledger of names. "Saltwick," she said. "You knew someone there?"

"A customs officer's wife. Adela. Terrible singer. Sang anyway, every market day, loud enough to scare fish." I scratched the nearest gelding between its ears. "She paid me in information and once in a pie. Best pie I ever ate. Probably the only reason I survived my thirties."

"She's on my arm now."

"I know. I saw."

Brann nodded slowly, looking at the firelight. "Silas thinks we're going to lose," she said.

That landed like a hook under the ribs. "Do they, now."

"They haven't said it. But they do that thing with the map where they check the distances twice, and they only do that when they already know the answer's wrong." She turned to look at me, and Brann's eyes in firelight were the eyes of a woman who'd spent her life reading water. "Nine days. Two hundred singers. One gate. You've run tides your whole life, Ovid. Tell me straight, the way you'd tell a fare: do we make the crossing?"

I thought about lying. It's practically my trade.

"The tide's against us," I said. "Running hard. Every village that falls is weight in the hull." I let that sit, because Brann deserved the whole truth, not the comfortable half. "But I've crossed against tide my whole life. There's a trick to it. You don't fight the water. You find the seam — the place where the currents argue — and you slip through in the argument. The Cantor's strong because everything answers it, everything obeys the key it sets. So we don't obey. We sing in the cracks. We sing *wrong*, on purpose, gloriously wrong, until the whole song trips over its own feet."

Brann stared at me. Then she laughed — short, rough, real. "Silas is going to hate that."

"Silas is going to love it and hate that they love it. Same thing."

She got up to go, then paused. "Adela's pie," she said. "When this is done, if she's — if any of them come back from the undercroft — you'll introduce me."

"If any of them come back," I said, "I'll buy the pies."

* * *

Day seven, the sky over Corvenne began to move.

Not clouds. The silence itself, rolling out from the capital in visible rings, the way rain shows you where it's falling on a far hillside. We stood on a ridge above the King's Road and watched a ring of quiet sweep across the valley below, through a farming hamlet whose name I never learned, and I watched the people come out of their houses and stand in their yards with their mouths open, humming, fighting, holding their notes the way Vess had taught us — and I watched the ring pass over them and take three of them. Three shapes left standing in the yard, humming at the edges, empty in the middle.

Two hundred voices behind me rose up on that ridge, ragged and terrified and absolutely refusing, and the ring hesitated. Actually hesitated, like a dog meeting a fence it hadn't expected, and slid around us and went on.

Silas stood at the front of the column with the map open in both hands, and I swear on Gerald the plank, the map was *glowing* — faint, gold, the ink stirring like something waking up.

"It knows where we're going," Silas said. Their voice shook. "It's been to the throne room. It remembers the way in."

"Then it can lead," Brann said, "and we follow, and we sing the whole cursed way."

And that's what we did. Two hundred souls on the King's Road, marching on the seat of a god-shaped hunger, singing Wren's lullaby at the top of our lungs — the one about the moon keeping watch, the one that had made a hardened smuggler cry into his soup not three weeks past — and behind us the villages fell one by one into silence, and their names went onto our arms in charcoal, and ahead of us the rings of quiet came faster and faster, nine days becoming eight becoming seven, and I marched in the middle of it all thinking about eels named Gerald and pies bought with secrets and a girl who sang badly about the tide,

and for the first time in eleven years, I wasn't marching away from her grave.

I was marching toward getting her back.

On the morning of the eighth day, the road crested a long hill, and there it was: Corvenne, black-walled and crowned in silence, its towers rising out of the held-breath sky like organ pipes waiting for a hand.

And every bell in every tower began, silently, to swing.
