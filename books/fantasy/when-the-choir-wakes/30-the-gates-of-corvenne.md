## 30. The Gates of Corvenne

I have crossed the Sighing Strait in a storm with a hole in the hull and a passenger who insisted on singing to calm the horses, and I have watched an old woman be unmade into silence by a herald's decree, and I have carried Wren's weight in my arms through the Road of Teeth while mute things with too many joints came out of the fog for us. I am not easily impressed.

But I had never heard four hundred people hum at once, and I will tell you honestly: it rearranged something in my chest that I don't think is going back.

We came down off the hills two hours before dawn, the whole ragged procession of Low Hum spilling along the old salt road like a river deciding, after years of being dammed, to remember which way was downhill. Silas walked at the front with the map open across their forearms, and I walked beside them because that was where I'd decided I belonged, and nobody — least of all me — had yet found the nerve to argue.

"Tell me again what happens when we reach the walls," I said, for the fourth time. Repetition steadies me. It's a ferrywoman's habit. You say the route out loud until the water believes it too.

"The palace is warded in sound," Silas said. "The Cantor built the wards itself, back when it still thought of them as protection rather than a cage. Every wall of Corvenne sings a note — a low one, constant, below what most ears catch. That's why the city feels heavy when you enter it. That's why people whisper there without knowing why." They turned a page. In the dark, the blood-ink caught what little light there was and held it, like it was proud of itself. "The wards are tuned to silence. To *one* song. They were never built to withstand many."

"And if they were?"

Silas's mouth did the thing it does when it wants to be a smile but hasn't finished the paperwork. "Then we'll improvise. I'm told ferrywomen are good at improvising."

"I'm told cartographers are good at lying about how much they know."

"That too."

Behind us, the humming rose and fell in waves. Silas had spent nine days teaching it — not a song, exactly, not at first. A chord. A shape made of voices, each person holding their own small piece of it, the way a bridge holds its own small piece of weight. Old Marta from Low Hum who hadn't sung since her brother was silenced. Twin brothers who communicated mostly in elbows. Wren, moving up and down the column all day like a small ferocious nurse, correcting pitch with a glare that could curdle milk. And Ovid at the rear with his wounded side strapped tight, refusing the cart, mapping the sound-tides of Corvenne from memory onto scraps of paper he handed forward like a man dealing cards in a game he expected to lose.

"You're doing the thing," I said to Silas now.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at the map instead of at the four hundred people trusting you."

"I'm looking at the map *for* them," Silas said with dignity. "It's called leadership."

"It's called hiding. I've done it for fifteen years; I know the posture."

That shut them up for a hundred paces or so, which for Silas is roughly a season.

* * *

Corvenne announced itself before we saw it — as a pressure, a flatness in the air, the way your ears feel before a storm breaks or after a door has been shut on a room you didn't know you were in. The humming behind us thinned as people felt it. Some of them faltered. I heard a woman near the back stop entirely, and then start again, alone, her single thread of sound small and stubborn as a candle in wind.

"Don't stop," I said, turning to walk backward along the column, the way I used to talk nervous passengers across the strait in bad weather. "Whatever you feel coming off those walls — that's not the world ending. That's just the world holding its breath. Breathe for it."

Wren appeared at my elbow. She'd braided her hair back with cord, the way her father used to, she'd told us once, and then gone very quiet, and none of us had been brave enough or stupid enough to say anything about it.

"They're scared," she said.

"Of course they are. Scared and walking anyway is the entire job description."

She considered this. "Brann?"

"Mm?"

"When we get inside — if we get inside — I want to find the undercroft first. Before anything else." Her jaw set. "The names. My father's name might be in there. Somebody should put them back."

"Somebody will," I said, and put my hand on her head briefly, which she tolerated the way cats tolerate affection: as a tax paid to the lesser species.

Ahead, the road crested, and there was Corvenne.

I'd seen it once, years ago, from the water — a grey crown of towers on its hill, beautiful the way a well-made lock is beautiful. Now it sat under a sky going bruise-colored with the first hint of dawn, and I could see the wards. Not see them, precisely. But the air above the walls shimmered the way water shimmers over a stone in summer, bending everything behind it slightly wrong, and when the wind moved I could hear it — that low note, vast and patient, the palace's one long held breath.

Against it, our four hundred voices sounded like nothing. Like a joke.

"Silas," I said quietly. "Tell me the plan works."

"No," Silas said. "Plans don't work. People work. Plans just give them somewhere to stand." They folded the map with hands that were almost steady. "Get everyone into position. When the bell changes, we sing."

* * *

Ovid found us at the base of the outer wall, in the shadow of a drainage arch that smelled the way you'd expect. He was grey around the mouth and holding his side, and he was grinning, which with Ovid is never a good sign.

"Well," he said. "Bad news and worse news."

"Give us the bad news first," Silas said.

"There isn't any bad news. That was the worse news. The bad news is that my friend Tamsin is still on the gate watch tonight, which means either she got my message or she didn't, and if she didn't, we're about to find out in front of several hundred witnesses." He leaned against the arch and breathed carefully. "Tamsin Ferrow. We ran cargo together, back before either of us had principles. She took the warden's coin when I took the ferryman's oar. She's kept her head down fifteen years, same as somebody else I could mention—" he looked pointedly at Silas, "—and the only reason she'll open that gate is if she believes, truly believes, that this isn't a suicide parade. So the question is: can your choir convince a professional cynic in about ninety seconds?"

"What do you suggest?" Silas asked.

"Volume," said Ovid. "And grief. Wardens have seen volume. Grief they haven't seen in numbers."

* * *

The bell changed at first light.

I don't know how to describe the Great Quieting except that it was a sound like the opposite of sound — a flattening, a pressing-down, as though the whole sky had decided to lie on top of us. Behind us, on the road we'd come by, villages went silent one after another, and I felt each one go like a hand closing around my throat. Somewhere back there was Low Hum. Somewhere back there was Marta's village, and the twins' forge, and every kitchen and field where people had learned, over nine impossible days, to hold a note together.

The humming behind me wavered.

Then Wren stepped forward, alone, to the front of the column, in full view of the walls, and began to sing her father's lullaby.

Not loud. That was the terrible thing — she sang it softly, the way you'd sing it to a child afraid of the dark, and the Great Quieting reached for her and couldn't quite take hold, because the lullaby wasn't a defiance, it was a *remembering*, and the Cantor's silence had no answer for that. Her voice threaded out thin and clear across the killing ground between the road and the gate.

And one by one, the others joined her.

Marta first, her cracked old voice finding the second line. Then the twins. Then dozens, then hundreds, the chord rising out of the crowd the way water rises in a well when you finally dig deep enough — not performed, not projected, just *held*, four hundred people standing in the open under a hostile sky, holding a note the way you hold a drowning stranger: with everything you have, and past the point of sense.

The wards bent.

I watched it happen. The shimmer above the walls rippled, and the great low note beneath the city stuttered — actually stuttered, like a singer losing their place — and Silas made a sound beside me that was half laugh and half prayer.

"It's working," they said. "It's actually— Brann, look at the seams. Look at the mortar lines. The wards run along the stonework, and the stonework is starting to—"

The wall hummed back.

That's the only word for it. The whole eastern face of Corvenne picked up the chord, stone remembering it had once been part of a mountain, and the note beneath the city fractured into a dozen discordant pieces, and somewhere high on the battlements a warden's horn tried to sound and produced instead a sad, flat wheeze, like the instrument had opinions about whose side it was on.

"NOW," bellowed Ovid, and hammered on the drainage arch with the flat of his hand, three times, pause, three times — some old smuggler's knock that his hands remembered better than his face did.

For a long moment, nothing.

The choir held. The walls groaned. Somewhere inside the palace, I imagined, the Cantor was waking to the fact that its perfect silent kingdom had developed a heartbeat, and I confess I hoped it choked on its morning scale.

Then, above the gate, a lantern lit. Went out. Lit twice more.

"Tamsin," Ovid breathed. "That's her signal. That's — ha. HA. Fifteen years of keeping her head down and she still remembers the tide-code." He was laughing and crying at the same time, which on Ovid's face looked less like joy than like a storm trying to decide which direction to blow. "The winch is on the inner side. She'll need time — there are six wardens on the gate watch and at least two of them are true believers—"

The gates began to open.

Not all at once. An inch. A foot. The groan of iron and oak, slow as a ship coming off the mud, and through the widening gap I saw a broad woman in warden grey with her back braced against the bar, hauling with her whole body, and beyond her the dark throat of the gate passage, and beyond *that* — the city, waking, windows lighting one by one as the sound poured in over the walls like a tide over a sill.

People came out of their houses.

That's what I wasn't ready for. I'd prepared myself for wardens, for arrows, for the gate being a trap. I had not prepared myself for doors opening along the street inside, and ordinary people stepping out in their nightclothes, listening — really listening, some of them for the first time in fifteen years — and then opening their mouths and adding their voices. A baker. Two children on a doorstep. An old man leaning out a window, singing badly and loudly and with what sounded like decades of arrears to make up.

The few became the many right there in front of me, and the many became a flood, and the flood came down the street toward the gate like it had been waiting fifteen years for someone to give it permission.

"Go," Silas said. Their eyes were wet and their voice was steady and I loved them furiously and unsentimentally, the way you love a keel. "All of you — through. Wren, stay with Brann. Ovid—"

"Leading the way," Ovid said, already limping toward the gap. "Tamsin owes me a drink and an argument, in whichever order survival permits."

We went through the gates of Corvenne in the grey light of the first morning of the rest of the realm, four hundred rebels and one stolen city, and the sound-wards overhead tore like old cloth, strand by strand, note by note, and fell.

Tamsin Ferrow let the last of us through, then swung the bar back into place behind us with a crash that rang like a verdict. She looked at Ovid. She looked at Silas. She looked at the choir filling her street shoulder to shoulder, still humming, tears running freely down faces that had forgotten they were allowed.

"So," she said, in the accent of someone who had rehearsed being unimpressed and failed. "You're the cartographer."

"I'm the cartographer," Silas said.

"Heard you can rewrite songs." She jerked her chin toward the palace on its hill above the city, where every window was dark and the air around the towers had begun, very faintly, to tremble. "Good. Because whatever's sitting on that throne just stopped singing — and in my experience, that's the moment you most need to know what it's about to do instead."
