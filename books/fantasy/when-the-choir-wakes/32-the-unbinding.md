## 32. The Unbinding

There is a particular kind of fear that arrives not as a scream but as a very tidy voice that says, *well, this is where you die, and honestly you've had a good run.* I had been hearing that voice since dawn. By the time we stood at the doors of the throne room, it had stopped being a voice and become more of a roommate.

The throne room of Corvenne was built to hold ten thousand voices and held none. Sound died at the threshold — I felt it go out of me like warmth leaving my hands on a winter morning. My own footsteps made no noise. Brann's boots, which could wake a garrison, made no noise. Even Wren's breathing, which I had come to think of as the truest sound in either world, was gone — just the small motion of her shoulders rising and falling, like a bird deciding whether to fly.

Ahead, on a dais of black glass, the Cantor sat wearing Aldous's crown and Aldous's face and none of Aldous's patience.

And around us, pressed into the galleries and balconies and every shadowed corner the palace offered, stood the chorus of Low Hum. Two hundred souls who had spent years humming forbidden songs into their soup and under their blankets, who had walked nine days through a country going quiet behind them, who were about to do the single most illegal thing in Cael Sorrow's history.

They couldn't hum yet. Not here. Not until the rite began, because the throne room ate sound, and if they gave it their voices too early it would simply drink them down and hand them back as silence.

So we stood there in absolute quiet, two hundred people pretending to be furniture, while the Cantor looked at us with the king's grey eyes and smiled with the king's mouth, and I thought: *you have got to be kidding me.*

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about dying, because it turned out to be relevant.

I knew that fifteen years ago I had signed my name to a document that outlawed magic across three realms, and then I had run, and running had worked so well that I'd nearly forgotten I was anyone at all. Charm-seller. Quiet man. Nobody. I had gotten so good at being nobody that being somebody again felt like wearing someone else's coat.

And now the map — my map, our map, the blood-inked thing that had swallowed me whole and dragged four strangers into each other's lives — lay open on the floor before the dais, its final page blank except for one line written in a hand I recognized as my own younger, stupider self:

*A cartographer's blood must be the ink.*

I had hidden that line from them for weeks. Then, in Low Hum, I hadn't. And instead of letting me walk to Corvenne alone to bleed out politely, they had done something far worse.

They had refused.

"You don't get to be the ink," Brann had said, arms crossed, in the tone she used on passengers who tried to haggle mid-flood. "You're the cartographer. The map needs your hand, not your life."

"It says blood—"

"It says *a* cartographer," Ovid had corrected, from his sickbed, with the smugness of a man whose ribs were broken but whose logic wasn't. "And last I checked, there are two hundred people in this village who've learned to read a chart. Amend the rite. You're the Realmwright. Rewrite it."

Wren had simply looked at me — twelve years old, fatherless twice over now, carrying her dead father's whistle-song like a lantern — and said, "We finish it together or not at all. That's what he meant by *finish it*. He didn't mean you. He never meant just you."

So I had rewritten the rite. Three nights without sleep, ink-stained to the elbow, redrawing the binding's architecture so that instead of one vein feeding one line, the song would be carried by many voices and anchored by one hand. A chorus instead of a sacrifice. It was either the greatest work of my career or the most elaborate way ever devised to get two hundred people arrested.

Possibly both.

* * *

The Cantor rose from the throne.

Even without sound, I could feel it — the pressure of its attention, like deep water. When it spoke, the words arrived inside my skull rather than my ears, which is a sensation I recommend to no one:

*Silas Almeida. Last of the Realmwrights. You brought an army of humming peasants to my door.*

"An army of *singers*," I said. My voice came out flat and dead in the silence, and I hated how small it sounded. "There's a difference."

*I offered you a place at my side. Twice. I am nothing if not generous.*

"You unmade an elder in front of children," Brann said. Her voice didn't echo — nothing echoed here — but it landed anyway, solid as a mooring post. "Generous isn't the word."

*The elder sang against the edict,* the Cantor said, with the weary patience of someone explaining arithmetic to a child. *Unsanctioned song unravels the realm. I hold it together. Every note I permit, I permit because I have measured its cost. Do you know what I carry? Do you know how long I have sung alone?*

And there it was — the tragedy underneath the monster, showing itself for half a heartbeat before the crown settled back down over it. Fifteen years ago, a court composer had fused himself to a dying king to save him, and had never once, in all the years since, stopped singing. Alone. In the dark. Holding up a whole country with his throat.

I pitied it. That was the worst part. I pitied it even as it prepared to flatten everything I loved into silence.

"I know," I said. "That's why we're not here to kill you."

The Cantor went very still. The king's face did something complicated.

*Then what—*

"We're here to give you a duet," said Wren.

* * *

It started, as so many dangerous things do, with a child humming.

Wren stepped forward — past me, past Brann's outstretched hand, straight into the dead air of the throne room — and began the lullaby. The one she'd taught us on the road, the one that let us sleep safe in the open, the one Ovid had wept to because his daughter had sung it before the first purge took her.

In the throne room, the hum made no sound. But it made *weight*. I saw it settle over the black glass floor like frost. I saw the Cantor flinch, because the lullaby was unsanctioned, unmeasured, unaccounted-for — and because somewhere underneath fifteen years of enforced harmony, some part of it remembered being a composer hearing a new melody for the first time.

Then Brann joined her. Then Ovid, white-faced, hand pressed to his bandaged ribs, singing through the pain because he'd insisted and nobody was fool enough to argue. Then the galleries, section by section, like lamps being lit — two hundred people humming in perfect defiance, pouring their voices into a room designed to swallow them, and the room *couldn't*, because there were too many, because the rite I'd rewritten turned each voice into a thread and wove the threads into a net, and the net caught the song before the silence could eat it.

The throne room filled with sound for the first time in fifteen years.

It was not beautiful, exactly. Two hundred untrained singers is not a choir, it's a weather event. There were cracked notes and wandering harmonies and one elderly woman from Low Hum who was, generously, in a different key entirely. But it was *alive*, and it was theirs, and when the Cantor reached out to smother it, the song bent and held, the way a reed bends and holds.

"Now," Brann breathed beside me. "The page, Silas. Now."

I knelt at the map.

The final page waited, blank as snowfall. Around its edges, faintly, I could see the other pages — the drowned archive of Amberglass, the Road of Teeth, the Sighing Strait, the village where Wren had attached herself to us like a burr and never let go. The map remembered everywhere we'd been. It was waiting to remember where we were going.

I drew the knife along my palm.

Pain, bright and simple and honest — the first uncomplicated thing in days. Blood welled up, dark, and I pressed my hand to the page, and the blood-ink woke.

Here is what nobody tells you about Realmwright work: the ink doesn't take orders. It takes *intent*. You don't write with your hand; you write with whatever you actually believe, down at the bottom, beneath the performance. For fifteen years my intent had been *hide*. The ink had obeyed, and I had vanished into a charm-shop in Vell.

Now I knelt in a stolen throne room, bleeding onto the last page of my life's work, and my intent was this: *let them carry it. I can't hold this alone. I was never meant to hold anything alone.*

The line went down like a river finding its bed.

* * *

The binding tore the way old cloth tears — slowly at first, with a sound like a held breath finally released, and then all at once.

I felt it through the floor. Through the map. Through the scar tissue of fifteen careful, cowardly years. Somewhere above us and below us and threaded through the whole fabric of Cael Sorrow, the fusion that bound a king to his composer — the ritual gone wrong, the saving that became a prison — began to come apart at the seams.

The Cantor screamed.

Not with the king's voice. With its own — a voice I had never heard, high and strange and terrified, the voice of a man who had been singing solo in the dark for fifteen years and had just felt another pair of hands touch the music.

*What are you doing—* it cried, staggering, the crown slipping sideways. *The binding holds the realm together, if you tear it the realm—*

"—was never meant to be held by one throat," I said, and my voice carried now, carried on the chorus, on the net of two hundred threads, and for the first time in this room my words sounded like they belonged to someone. "You were dying, and he was dying, and someone fused you together to save one life, and it worked, and it was a prison. We're not tearing the realm. We're tearing the *cell*."

The song swelled. I could feel the rite moving through me now, through the cut in my palm, through the ink crawling across the page in lines I barely recognized as mine — because they weren't only mine anymore. Brann's steadiness was in them. Ovid's tide-knowledge, his maps of currents invisible to everyone else. Wren's lullaby, braided through the whole structure like a spine. Two hundred villagers' stubborn, off-key, magnificent refusal to be silenced.

One blood cannot carry this. I had known it, and lied about knowing it, and nearly died of the lie.

But a family — a found, improbable, argumentative family of a ferrywoman and a cynic and a child and two hundred humming rebels —

*a family can.*

The binding ripped.

Light came out of it — not the white light of annihilation but the gold of late afternoon through amber, the color of the archive where I'd first read the truth. The Cantor was thrown back against the throne, and the crown fell, and rolled, and rang against the black glass with a clear bright note that the throne room did not swallow, because the throne room had forgotten how.

And in the space where the Cantor had been — where the king-and-composer had been, fused and furious and afraid — something else was emerging. Something smaller. Something *two*.

Brann's hand found my shoulder. Wren's found my other one. Ovid, wheezing, put his over both of ours, and I understood that I was kneeling in a puddle of my own blood on the floor of a tyrant's hall, and that I had never in my life felt less alone.

The light gathered. The song held its breath.

And the thing that had worn the king's face for fifteen years opened its eyes — *both* pairs of them — and looked at us, and began, in a voice cracking with disuse and terror and something dangerously close to relief, to sing a note that was not alone.
