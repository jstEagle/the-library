## 33. The Cantor Unmasked

I had not heard my own name spoken aloud in fifteen years.

That is a strange thing to write, and stranger to have lived. You would think a man would miss his own name the way he misses a hand — constantly, usefully. But I did not miss it. I had forgotten it the way you forget the taste of water when you have been drowning for a long time. The Cantor's song filled every space where my name used to live. It sang through my mouth at audiences. It sang through my hands as I signed edicts I never read. It sang through my eyes while I watched, from somewhere very far inside myself, like a man pressed against the far side of a mirror.

And then Silas Almeida cut their palm open on the floor of my throne room and said, in effect: *let him out.*

The binding tore.

I want to describe what that felt like, and I find I cannot do it honestly without admitting it felt like being born. There is no dignified way to be born. There is screaming, and there is light where you expected none, and there is the sudden appalling fact of your own weight. Fifteen years of song fell away from me all at once, and I collapsed onto the marble of my own throne room like a coat dropped from a hook, and the first thing I thought — the first coherent thought I had owned in a decade and a half — was:

*Gods, this floor is cold. I chose this marble myself. Why did I choose this marble?*

Kings are vain about strange things.

* * *

But I am getting ahead of myself, and a man who has been silent for fifteen years may be forgiven for wanting to tell things in order.

You should understand what the throne room was like while the rite began. The Cantor had built it that way deliberately — sound died at the doors. Not muffled. *Died.* A word spoken inside those walls simply stopped existing, halfway between the lips and the ear, like a bird flown into glass. The Cantor liked it that way. It said — and I remember this, because even fused into its song I remembered everything, which is its own particular torture — it said that silence was the only honest acoustics. That every other room lied to you with echo.

It was lying, of course. It wanted silence because silence was the only thing it could not sing over. But I will come back to that.

Silas stood before the throne with blood running down their wrist, and behind them — arranged in something between a choir formation and a defensive line, which told me everything about how they had learned to survive the last nine days — stood the others. Brann the ferrywoman, arms crossed like a woman daring the universe to try something. Ovid, bandaged, leaning on a smuggler's crowbar as though it were a walking stick he had merely borrowed from history. And Wren. Small Wren, with her father's map folded somewhere against her heart, singing a note so steady it made the marble hum.

And around them, filling the gallery, the rebels of Low Hum. Hundreds of them. Humming.

The binding had been woven from one voice. It came apart under many. I felt each thread snap — not painfully, exactly. More like the moment when a cramp releases and you realize how long you had been clenched.

The Cantor screamed.

I do not mean it cried out. I mean it produced a sound that was a scream the way a hurricane is a breeze — technically accurate, catastrophically insufficient. Every thread of the binding it clutched and rewove and clutched again, and Silas's chorus answered each repair by simply singing louder, and I lay on my cold beautiful marble and watched my captor begin to come apart.

Here is what I saw. Here is the thing I have not told anyone yet, because until this chapter there was no one to tell.

The Cantor unmasked is small.

* * *

You expect — I expected, even from inside it — something vast. A shadow with a crown. A mouth where the king should be. The heralds had built it that way in effigy, all teeth and resonance, and the Wardens of Song wore masks tall enough to make grown villagers faint. Fear wants architecture. The Cantor understood this better than anyone; it had spent fifteen years building itself a body out of other people's terror.

But bindings go both ways, you see. That is the cruelty of them, and the mercy. It held me, and I held it. When the threads tore loose, what spilled out of the shape it had worn was not a monster.

It was a man on his knees.

A thin man. Grey at the temples, though his face was young-ish in the way of people who have not slept properly in years. He wore court dress of a fashion thirty years out of date — I recognized it, gods help me, I had complimented him on it once, at a midwinter concert, back when I still gave compliments instead of edicts. His hands were a composer's hands: long, scarred at the fingertips, moving constantly, fretting silent chords against his own knees.

He was crying. Not weeping — crying the way a kettle cries, involuntarily, a pressure finding its only exit.

"No," he was saying. "No, no, no, you don't understand, if it comes apart now, if it comes apart *now*, then it was all—"

"Then it was all what?" Silas asked. Their voice was rough. They had cut their own hand open for this; I could see the blood drying black along their fingers, Realmwright's ink, the last of it. "Finish the sentence."

The man looked up at them, and his face did something terrible. It hoped.

"Then it was all for nothing," he whispered. "Fifteen years. If I stop singing, then he dies anyway, and it was all for nothing."

And I — lying on my own floor, half-free, watching this creature who had eaten my life — I felt pity.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because I certainly had to. Pity. For the thing that wore me. I had imagined this moment for fifteen years, in the quiet cells of myself where it could not hear: I imagined rage, revenge, the satisfying crunch of a crown breaking. What I got instead was a frightened man in outdated clothes, kneeling in front of a cartographer, terrified that if the music stopped, the dying would resume.

Because that is what happened, in case Amberglass's archive left it ambiguous. Let me tell it plainly, since I was the one dying on the table.

* * *

Thirty years ago — no. Let me count. The binding was fifteen years ago, and before that there were years of the illness, so: twenty years ago, I was a young king with a fever no physician could name, and a court composer named Maesin Vael whom I trusted more than I trusted my ministers, my priests, or my own brothers. Maesin wrote music the way rain writes on windows — effortlessly, endlessly, a little sadly. He ate dinner at my table twice a week. He was shy. He laughed at my jokes a half-second after everyone else, because he was always hearing some inner music and had to surface from it first.

I loved him. Not romantically — or not only, or not in any way either of us had time to examine — but the way you love the person who makes the silence bearable.

When the physicians gave me the winter, I went to him. This is the part I begged him to keep secret, and he kept it, and keeping it destroyed him. I went to him because he knew the old rites — the pre-Realmwright rites, the ones the archives call *crude* now, the ones that work by fusion rather than craft. I asked him to bind my dying into the music. To put whatever was killing me into a song and sing it away from me. I asked him to save my life, and I offered him anything in the kingdom as payment.

He refused. Twice. Three times. I was a king; I was not accustomed to refusal; I was also, I remind you, dying, and dying men are tyrants of a very specific and unforgivable kind. On the fourth request I did not ask. I invoked. There are words a king can say that are not quite commands and not quite prayers, and I said them, and Maesin — loyal, exhausted, weeping — began the rite.

It was meant to bind my death into the song.

What it actually bound was *me*. Because the rite required two voices, one for the flesh and one for the death, and Maesin had no second singer, and so he used himself. He poured himself into the binding to hold it closed, intending — I know this now, I have had fifteen years inside his head to learn it — intending to hold it for an hour. An evening. Until the fever broke.

The fever did not break. The binding needed a living king and a living singer, joined, forever, or both would die. And Maesin Vael discovered, alone, at three in the morning, in a room full of candles guttering in the draft of his own mistake, that he had traded his whole self for one hour of my survival — and that the hour was never going to end.

So he sang. He sang to keep the binding taut. He sang to keep me breathing. He sang for fifteen years, and the singing hollowed him, and the hollow filled with panic, and the panic learned to sing too, and somewhere in the fourth or fifth year the man stopped existing and the function remained. The function needed control — a loose thread anywhere and the whole binding might unravel and the king would die and it would all have been for nothing. So the function silenced the villages whose bells interfered. The function took the names of singers whose harmonies clashed. The function built a throne room where nothing could interrupt it, ever, and sat inside my body wearing my crown, singing, always singing, alone, alone, alone —

Do you understand? It was not hunger for power. Power was just the only tool left in its hands. It was the terror of a man alone in the dark with a candle he cannot let go out, and everyone who comes near it looks, to him, like wind.

* * *

"You could have told someone," Silas said. Their chorus had gone quiet, holding the torn edges of the binding open, and the throne room was full of that dead acoustic silence, and Maesin Vael knelt in the middle of it like a man whose orchestra has stopped mid-note. "Fifteen years. Someone would have helped you finish it differently."

"Who?" And here his voice cracked into something almost angry, almost alive. "Who? The Realmwrights were gone — hunted out, outlawed, by *your* laws, cartographer, don't look at me like that, I read the archives, I know whose hand signed the Proscription—" Silas flinched. I watched them flinch, and I confess it did not displease me; shame shared is a kind of company. "There was no one. There was never anyone. There was only the song, and if the song stopped—"

"The song didn't save me," I said.

My voice. My actual voice, coming out of my actual throat, for the first time in fifteen years. It came out as a ruin — cracked, rusted, barely mine — and every head in the throne room turned, and Maesin Vael went absolutely still, the way a wild animal goes still.

"It didn't save me," I said again, stronger. "Maesin. Look at me. Look at what you were protecting." I pushed myself up. My legs shook. Fifteen years of being carried will do that; I recommend against it. "You sang my death away into yourself and kept me alive, yes. Alive. In a cell. Behind my own eyes. Do you know what I was doing in there, all those years? I was listening to you. That was my whole existence. Listening to the song that was eating my kingdom, sung with my mouth, and knowing — knowing — that somewhere inside it was my friend, screaming."

"I was saving you," he whispered.

"You were drowning with me," I said, "and calling it swimming."

* * *

I will not pretend that landed cleanly. Grief does not land; it floods. He made a sound then that I will hear for the rest of my life, however long the restored years grant me — a sound with no music in it at all, which for him must have been the most frightening sound in the world. He curled forward over his scarred hands, and the great shape he had worn — the crown-shadow, the resonant dread — sloughed off him entirely, pooling on the marble like a costume after a play, and underneath it he was just a tired man who had been singing alone for fifteen years because stopping meant admitting the song had failed.

Brann shifted like she wanted to go to him. Ovid's hand on her arm stopped her — gently, I noticed. Even cynics learn tenderness eventually, given enough funerals.

It was Wren who moved.

Of course it was Wren. She walked across the throne room floor — past me, past Silas, past the pooled shadow-crown — and she sat down on the cold marble in front of the most feared entity in two realms, cross-legged, the way children sit, and she said:

"You can stop now. We've got him."

And Maesin Vael lifted his head, and looked at this child who had lost her father to his purge, and whatever he saw in her face undid the last knot in him. The binding's final thread — the one no rite could cut, the one made of his own belief that he was still necessary — snapped with a sound like a struck bell.

He sobbed. Openly, horribly, humanly, in front of four hundred rebels, and not one of them laughed, and not one of them turned away. The Low Hum folk know what fifteen years of forced silence looks like. They simply waited, the way you wait at a bedside.

Silas crouched beside him. I saw them hesitate — I saw the whole war of it cross their face, the memory of the edict, the elder unmade in the village square, the daughter taken in the market, the map that demanded their blood — and then I saw them choose. They put their inked, bleeding hand on Maesin's shoulder.

"We're going to need a composer," Silas said. "For the new song. If you're willing."

* * *

That is where we stand tonight. The Cantor is unmasked, and it is a man, and the man is wrecked and grieving and — this is the miracle, the one I intend to guard jealously for the rest of my reign — willing. Aldous lives, though I write this propped up in a bed I do not recognize in a wing of my own palace, being fed soup by a ferrywoman who shows me no deference whatsoever and whom I already consider one of the finest people in either realm.

But the binding is torn, not healed. Two men who were one flesh for fifteen years do not separate without consequences, and I can feel Maesin's heartbeat faintly under my own, a second pulse looking for its measure. The rite is unfinished. The song is unsung. And tomorrow, Silas says, we find out whether a king, a broken composer, a cartographer, and a chorus of humming rebels can write a duet where neither voice is consumed.

Wren thinks we can. Wren, as usual, is probably right.

I only hope I remember how to sing.
