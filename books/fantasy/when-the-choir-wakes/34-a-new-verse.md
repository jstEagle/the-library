## 34. A New Verse

The binding tore the way old cloth tears — all at once, and with a sound like a scream held for fifteen years finally let go.

I felt it through my cut palm first. The ink on my blood was no longer mine alone; it had been passed hand to hand through forty throats, hummed into something that no single body could have carried, and now it moved through the throne room of Corvenne like a river finding its old bed. The chorus stood in a crescent around the dais — Brann at one horn of it, her voice low and stubborn as tide; Ovid at the other, singing through gritted teeth and a bandaged side; Wren dead center, thirteen years old and holding the melody the way she held everything, as if it were hers by right and always had been.

And between them, unraveling, the Cantor.

I want to be honest about what I saw, because history — if there is any history after this — will want to make it simple. It was not simple. It was not a monster shedding its skin. It was two people coming apart from each other, and it was the worst thing I have ever watched.

The Cantor's form lifted off the throne like smoke rising off a doused fire. Underneath it, King Aldous knelt where he'd been held for fifteen years, gaunt as winter, his crown sitting on his head like a bird's nest abandoned by everything that once lived in it. And above him, wound through him, threaded into the marrow of him — the composer. I never learned its true name in that moment; perhaps there wasn't one left. There was only a shape made of every note it had ever sung alone, and it was *screaming*, because the rite was doing exactly what it was written to do: unmaking the fusion, separating king from song, and the song had nowhere to go.

That was the flaw in the binding. That was the flaw in the *unbinding*. I saw it the way you see a cliff edge — all at once, when it's already under your feet.

The original ritual, the one in Amberglass, had been written to save a dying man. It had failed at mercy and succeeded at mathematics: it bound the composer to Aldous to keep his heart beating, and it never asked what would happen to the composer. Fifteen years of singing alone inside another man's skull. Fifteen years of being the only voice in a room that had no walls. And our rite — my rite, drafted in a drowned archive by a cartographer who thought in lines and borders — proposed to fix that by cutting the song free and letting it dissolve.

Unbinding. Unmaking. The same word wearing different clothes.

The Cantor knew it. Even torn half-loose, even shrieking, it knew, and it turned what remained of its attention on me with an expression I will carry to whatever grave I'm owed. Not hatred. *Recognition.* One scribe to another. *You wrote this,* it said, without sound, without breath, in the language of ink itself. *You know what your own hand does.*

And God help me, I did.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a Realmwright: the gift was never drawing maps. Maps are just the excuse. The gift is seeing what a thing *is* and writing down what it could *be instead*, and having reality, grudgingly, agree.

I had spent fifteen years pretending I didn't have it. I had spent the last nine days using it in increments — a ward rewritten here, a sound-ward folded there — telling myself I was only completing the map, only finishing what the dying stranger started, only following instructions. The map's final page demanded a cartographer's blood, and I had opened my palm on the throne room floor believing that was the whole price: blood, ink, rite, done. A clean transaction. I am very good at believing in clean transactions. I ran a charm shop for fifteen years selling luck to people who needed permission more than luck.

But the rite was tearing, and the tear had edges, and edges are just lines waiting to be redrawn.

"Silas." Brann's voice, strained, holding her note against the pull of the unbinding like a woman leaning into a gale. She didn't break her singing to speak — she spoke *inside* it, the way ferrywomen talk over water. "It's coming apart wrong."

"I know."

"The king—"

"I know." Aldous had begun to fade at the edges too. That was the arithmetic no one had said aloud: the binding had held them together so long that unstitching it threatened to take the king's living thread with it. Save one, lose both. The archive's rite had been written by optimists, and optimism, in my experience, is just cowardice with better handwriting.

Wren's voice wavered — not failing, never failing, but bending toward me, asking without asking. Ovid's eyes found mine over her head, and for once there was no deflection in them, no ledger, no wry arithmetic about payment and debt. Just the question everyone in that crescent was too disciplined to sing: *what do we do?*

And the Cantor looked at me with its terrible recognition, and King Aldous — lucid, present, kneeling in his own ruin — looked at me with something worse than hope, and I understood that I had maybe four bars of music to decide what kind of writer I was.

The map was in my left hand. My blood was still wet on my right.

I did the unthinkable. Which is to say: I stopped following the map.

* * *

You have to understand what a transgression this was. Realmwright work has laws — I helped write some of them, back in the years I don't discuss, back before I fled to Vell and became a seller of harmless charms. The first law is that you do not rewrite what is already written. You complete it. You correct errors, you amend boundaries, but the underlying text is sacred, because reality is a document and documents need integrity or they fall apart. Every Realmwright for a thousand years had agreed on this. It was the one rule that kept us from becoming gods, or worse, editors.

The binding on Aldous was written text. Sacred, ancient, sealed in a drowned archive, paid for in a king's dying consent.

And it was *wrong*. It had been wrong from the first bar. It had taken a terrified composer and a dying king and fused them without asking either what they'd want after the crisis passed. It had assumed salvation meant preservation. It had been written — I could see it now, reading the tear's raw edges the way you read the grain of split wood — by someone who loved the king and feared the composer, and fear makes terrible prose.

So I broke the law. I put my bleeding palm flat against the air above the dais, against the tear itself, and I began to write.

Not unbinding. *Rewriting.*

The first thing I changed was the verb. The old rite said *separate*. I struck it — struck it out of the living text, which feels like reaching into a river and moving its source — and wrote instead *harmonize*. Two voices, one song, neither consumed. A duet. The word went in like a blade going into bread, easy, as if the text had been waiting fifteen years for someone to fix it.

The Cantor screamed again, but the quality of the scream changed. Have you ever heard a chord resolve after hours of dissonance? It was that, if the dissonance had lasted a decade and a half and the resolution was happening inside a person's soul.

"It hurts," it said — aloud this time, in a voice like a boy's, like a young court composer's, the voice it must have had before the ritual, before the crown, before the long solitary madness of singing inside someone else. "What are you doing, it *hurts*—"

"I know," I said. "Stay with me. This part always hurts. It's the old notes leaving."

Because that was the truth of it, the truth I was gambling four lives and a kingdom on: the Cantor's cruelty hadn't been malice. It had been *arrangement*. A composer locked in a duet where the other voice couldn't answer, forced to carry both melodies alone, driven mad by the silence where harmony should have been. The Great Quieting, the stolen names, the edicts, the unmade elder in that village — all of it was a madman's solution to loneliness. If you cannot have harmony, flatten the world until nothing contradicts you.

I was offering it the thing it had been trying to seize by force: someone to sing *with*.

King Aldous raised his head. His eyes — lucid, that terrible lucidity he'd shown me at the window, the look of a man drowning who can see the surface — fixed on mine.

"Cartographer," he said. His voice was gravel and dignity. "What are you writing?"

"A new verse," I said. "Yours and its. If you'll consent. I'm done doing things to kings without asking."

He laughed. It was a ruined sound, half cough, and it was the most kingly thing I heard all day. "Fifteen years I have begged the gods for silence," he said. "And now you offer me music."

"Not silence," I said. "Never silence again. But not captivity either. A duet means you can stop singing whenever you want, and it will still be there when you start again. Can you — " and here my voice did something embarrassing, cracked like a first-year apprentice's — "can you forgive it? What it took?"

The throne room held its breath. Forty singers held their note. The Cantor hung above its king, trembling, waiting for the sentence.

Aldous looked up at the thing that had worn his crown, eaten his name, silenced his country. And I watched him do the hardest arithmetic a human being can do — harder than sacrifice, harder than war — which is to count the cost of a crime and still see the criminal as a person.

"You were afraid," Aldous said at last. "As was I. We were afraid together, badly." He extended one skeletal hand upward. "Sing with me properly, Maestro. And then get out of my government."

* * *

I won't pretend I composed it alone. That's the lie the ballads will tell, if there are ballads — the lone Realmwright, the heroic pen, the singular genius. Here is what actually happened: I wrote the frame, the bare structure, two lines braided into one — and then I reached back to the chorus, to the crescent of stubborn, grieving, magnificent people behind me, and I asked for their voices, and they gave them.

Brann gave the verse the shape of a tide — in and out, never forcing, patient as water finding a harbor. Ovid gave it a minor turn, a grief-note that refused to resolve cheaply, because he had lost a daughter to this thing's first purge and the new song had to hold that loss or it would be a lie. Wren — Wren gave it the lullaby, the one she'd taught us on the open road, the one that let exhausted travelers sleep safe in hostile country. She threaded it through the duet like gold wire through dark cloth, and suddenly the whole structure had somewhere soft to land.

And Low Hum's rebels, and the villages behind us, and every throat that had ever hummed a forbidden note in a cellar or a field — I felt them through the map, through the thin places, a hundred distant voices adding themselves because the new verse had room. That was the difference, I think. That was the whole revolution in one line of theory. The old binding had been written for one king and one composer, a closed system, a private transaction. The new verse was written *open*. Anyone could join. Nothing in it required anyone to disappear.

The Cantor came down out of the air like snowfall. Not falling — *settling*. It poured back into Aldous, but differently now, the way a river settles into a shared bed rather than a flood. And Aldous — Aldous *straightened*. Fifteen years fell off him like a coat. Color came into his face. He rose from the floor of his own throne room under his own power, and when he breathed, the breath had music in it, and the music had *company*.

For one moment, the two of them sang together — actually together, actually answered — and I heard what Cael Sorrow had been trying to be since before the binding. The sound went through the walls of the palace, through the cracked sound-wards, out over a city that had forgotten what unsanctioned joy sounded like. Somewhere below, I knew, people were stopping in the streets. Somewhere below, people were starting to hum along.

Then it was done, and the throne room was just a room, dusty and battle-scarred and smelling of ozone and my own blood, and King Aldous stood in it looking like a man waking from a fever, and the Cantor's voice — small, exhausted, sane — said from somewhere inside him:

"*Is it finished?*"

"No," I said, and looked down at the map in my hands, and felt the grin crack across my face like ice on a spring river. "Now it's finished. Now we start the real work."

Because the map had changed. I felt it before I saw it — the weight shifting, the pages stirring like something breathing. When I looked down, the blood-ink lines were running, reflowing, redrawing themselves across Cael Sorrow. Borders dissolving. Blank spaces filling in. The unmapped realm mapping *itself*, now that nothing was flattening it into silence — and in the corner of the final page, in handwriting that was mine and also older than mine, new words were surfacing as though rising through water:

*One realm restored. One remains. The fold opens both ways, cartographer.*

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a second line of ink, still wet:

*They are waiting to hear from you.*

Brann leaned over my shoulder, sweat-soaked and hoarse and grinning like a pirate. "What's it say?"

I closed the map gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"It says," I told her, "that we're not done. And that somebody's been holding the door open for us this whole time."

Behind us, King Aldous laughed — a real laugh now, doubled, harmonized, alive — and outside, for the first time in fifteen years, the city of Corvenne began to sing back.
