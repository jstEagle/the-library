## 35. After the Music

The first thing I did as a king again was ask for a chair.

Not the throne — the throne had spent thirty years as a perch for something that sang through my mouth, and I suspected it held opinions about me that would take time to soften. I asked for a plain wooden chair set beside the throne, and I sat in it while the court filed back into the throne room of Corvenne, blinking like people emerging from a long illness into a world they no longer trusted to be real.

Which, to be fair, was exactly what we all were.

The sound came back before the light did. That's the thing about the Great Quieting breaking — it didn't break all at once, like a spell snapping. It broke the way ice breaks on a spring river: a crack here, a groan there, then everywhere at once, and suddenly the whole realm was talking. The guards in the corridor were arguing about whose watch it had been when everything went strange. A chamberlain was weeping openly into his sleeve and insisting he was fine. Somewhere below us, in the undercroft of names, the vault doors stood open, and the stolen names were going home.

I felt every one of them leave. Like birds lifting off a wire.

"You're staring at the floor," said Brann, who had appointed herself the person who told me uncomfortable truths, apparently by right of conquest. She'd crossed realms on a folding map and broken into my palace; she'd earned the title.

"I'm listening," I said.

"To what?"

"The quiet." I looked up. "It's different now. It used to be a held breath. Now it's just... a rest between notes. There's a difference."

Brann considered this with the expression of a woman deciding whether the madman on the chair was worth humoring. "There's a ferrywoman in Vell who'd say you're overthinking it."

"There's a ferrywoman standing in front of me saying it now."

"That one agrees with the other one." But she almost smiled, and in the weeks since the throne room, almost-smiles from Brann were currency worth more than anything in my treasury.

* * *

The Cantor came to court on the ninth day after.

I insisted on it. Half the council wanted the creature locked in the deepest cell, and the other half wanted it executed before breakfast, and I understood both halves because I had spent three decades fused to the thing and had wanted both those things myself, at volume, from inside my own skull.

But here is what nobody else knew, because nobody else had been where I had been: I knew what it sounded like when it was alone. I knew the shape of its terror. Maestro Edevane — that had been its name, before the binding, before the ritual I begged for on my deathbed, before it took the crown along with the fever — Edevane had been afraid of silence the way drowning men are afraid of open water. And for thirty years, I had been the only shore it had.

You do not execute your shore. Not even when the shore has been singing your voice into a prison for half your life.

So on the ninth day, the doors opened, and the Cantor walked in — diminished, yes. The great layered harmonies it had worn like robes were gone; what remained was a tall figure in grey, its face still flickering between mine and something older and wearier, its hands — a composer's hands, ink-stained at the nails — folded in front of it like a schoolboy called before the headmaster.

The court went silent. Real silence, chosen silence, which is the only kind that ever frightened it.

"Your Majesty," it said. My voice. It still had my voice, though rougher now, unpracticed, like a door left shut too long. "I have come to be sentenced."

I let the silence sit. Let it learn what chosen silence felt like from this side of it.

"Maestro Edevane," I said at last, and I watched it flinch at its own name, the way a man flinches at sunlight after years in a cellar. "Thirty years ago, I lay dying of the grey fever, and I asked my court composer to save my life. You did. You also took my kingdom, my voice, my face, and the names of everyone who ever disagreed with you. I could list the crimes until winter. I suspect the ledger already exists somewhere in this palace, kept by someone with better handwriting than mine."

"It does," said Ovid from the back of the room, and several courtiers jumped. The ferryman had a gift for being exactly where he wasn't invited. "I've seen it. Very thorough. Depressing reading."

"Thank you, Ovid." I turned back to the Cantor. "Here is what will happen instead of execution. You will compose. That is the sentence. You will compose for this court, under guard, under watch, for the rest of your very long life — and every piece you write will carry the names of the silenced in it. All of them. You will put them back into the music, note by note, until Cael Sorrow has heard every name you took so many times that no one can forget them again. When the last name has been sung, we will discuss whether you have earned anything more than that. Perhaps you'll have earned an audience that doesn't flinch. Do not expect forgiveness. Forgiveness is not mine to give. It belongs to the silenced, and they are only now learning to speak."

The Cantor — Edevane — stood very still. Then it bowed, low, the bow of a man accepting not mercy but work, which is sometimes the heavier sentence.

"And if I cannot?" it asked quietly. "If the music won't come? Thirty years, Aldous. I only ever learned to sing alone."

From the gallery above, a small voice said, "That's not true."

Wren leaned over the railing, nine years old and utterly without reverence, the way only nine-year-olds and cats can be.

"You sang with us," she said. "In the throne room. During the rite. You thought nobody noticed, but I notice everything about songs. You took the third verse and you made it a duet. You didn't know how to stop singing alone because nobody ever asked you to sing *with*. So." She shrugged, devastatingly casual. "Somebody's asking now."

The Cantor looked up at her. And for the first time since the binding tore loose, its face stopped flickering between my face and its own, and settled into something that belonged to neither of us — something small, and startled, and beginning, very cautiously, to hope.

"I don't remember how," it said.

"Then you'll practice," said Wren. "Like everybody."

* * *

The names came home slowly, the way tide comes in.

Ovid managed the undercroft recovery, because of course he did — the man claimed to be a cynical mercenary motivated entirely by payment, and then spent fourteen hours a day sorting stolen souls out of a vault and refused, absolutely refused, to discuss why. He kept a ledger. He read each name aloud before releasing it, in a flat ferryman's voice, as if reciting cargo manifests, but I saw him once, late, thinking himself alone, press his palm flat against an empty shelf where a name had been and stay there a long time.

Her name was Lira. His daughter's name was Lira. He never said it aloud in daylight. But on the night her name left the vault — the last of the first purge, the oldest shelf, the dust thickest — the whole palace heard him laugh once, wetly, in the dark, and then go very quiet, and in the morning he was at his ledger again with red eyes and a new column headed *owed*, and he worked down that column for the rest of his life, as far as I know, without ever finishing it. Some debts you keep paying on purpose.

The returned were strange, at first. People who had been unmade into silence came back with gaps in them, like books with pages torn out, and the healers of Low Hum taught the court that you do not fill another person's missing pages for them. You sit beside them. You hum, softly, whatever they seem to need hummed. You let them find their own words.

The village elder from chapter seven of this disaster — Elder Sef, the first to resist the Edict, the first to be unmade in front of witnesses — came back on the twelfth day, reassembled out of a name and a great deal of Wren's stubborn humming. The first thing Sef said upon opening their eyes was: "Did the herald at least sing off-key? Tell me the herald sang off-key. I refuse to have been martyred by good pitch."

The whole infirmary laughed until someone cried, and then everyone cried, and then Sef demanded tea, and that, I am told, is how restoration actually works. Not trumpets. Tea, and terrible jokes, and the slow return of appetite.

As for me — I resumed the throne, such as it was. I kept my wooden chair beside it. I found that ruling after thirty years of captivity was less like riding a bicycle and more like trying to remember a language you dreamed in: the shapes came back, but the fluency took seasons. I gave bad orders and corrected them. I apologized to servants whose names I should have known and made a public ceremony of learning them. I reinstated the old custom of the Open Door, where any subject could bring a grievance to the king directly, and on the first day forty-three people came, and the forty-third was a farmer who simply wanted to tell me, at length, about drainage, and I listened to every word about drainage as though it were scripture, because it was the first ordinary complaint I had heard in thirty years and I nearly wept with gratitude for it.

Brann ran my security, having annexed the position. She interviewed every guard twice and dismissed eleven of them, and when I asked her by what authority, she said, "The authority of having broken into your palace, Your Majesty. If I can do it, your gates are decorative." I could not argue. I had tried arguing with her before. It was like arguing with weather.

And Silas —

Silas Almeida, last Realmwright, cartographer, charm-seller, the person whose blood had started the ink and whose refusal had rewritten the song — Silas avoided me. That was the strange part. Everyone else in the realm wanted something from the king: petitions, pardons, patronage, an audience to complain about drainage. Silas, who had saved my life and my kingdom and had every right to ask for anything, asked for nothing, and made themselves scarce with the practiced skill of fifteen years' hiding.

I caught them once, in the map room, three weeks after. They stood before the great table where Cael Sorrow spread out in vellum and ink, and they were not looking at the mapped parts. They were looking at the edges. The blank margins where the country fell away into the unknown.

"You're avoiding me," I said.

Silas didn't startle. Realmwrights, I was learning, don't startle; they recalibrate. "Kings say that like it's a problem."

"It is a problem. I owe you a debt I can't repay and you won't let me try. It keeps me up at night."

"Then take up drainage." They traced the margin with one finger. "I'm not avoiding you, Majesty. I'm avoiding being *looked at*. There's a difference. Fifteen years of practice." A pause. "Though it's getting worse at sticking."

"Why?"

They were quiet a moment. Then: "Because Wren asked me yesterday why the map has a blank spot in the northeast, and I told her it was 'to be walked together,' and she said, 'With who?' and I said, 'Whoever wants to,' and she said, 'I'm asking who YOU want,' and I realized—" Silas's hand closed, briefly, around nothing. "I realized I've spent so long being no one that I don't know how to answer questions about wanting things. And kings ask those questions. So I avoid kings."

I sat down on the edge of the map table, which made Silas wince — cartographers and their maps — and I said, "Then here is a question you can't avoid, because I'm the king and I'm asking it formally. What do you want, Silas Almeida?"

They looked at me for a long time. And I watched the fifteen years of practiced anonymity fight the nine days of found family, and I watched the family win, barely, the way dawn wins over night — not all at once, but inevitably.

"I want to finish the map," Silas said. "All of it. Every edge. And then I want to draw a new one, of places that don't exist yet, so people can go find them." A breath. "I want to stay."

"Then stay," I said. "We'll discuss terms. I'm told I'm generous when I'm kept up at night."

* * *

On the fortieth day, the court gathered to hear the Cantor's first composition.

The hall was full — fuller than any coronation. The returned sat in the front rows, holding each other's hands. Sef brought tea. Ovid stood at the back by the doors, arms crossed, claiming he was only there to check the exits, fooling no one. Brann stood beside me. Wren sat on the steps of the dais because chairs were, in her opinion, for people with less important ears.

Edevane walked to the center of the hall in plain grey, carrying a single instrument — a slender bowed thing I remembered from before, from the years when the court composer had been a man named Edevane who laughed at his own puns and feared the king's disapproval more than death. It raised the bow. It hesitated.

"I have written a name into every measure," it said. "I will not get through all of them tonight. Or this year. Possibly not this decade. But I have been instructed—" and here its eyes flicked to Wren, who nodded once, magnanimous as an empress— "to practice singing *with*. So. If anyone knows the third verse of Wren's lullaby, you may join in at the bridge. I have left room."

And it began.

The first notes were the name of Elder Sef. The next, the name of a fisherman from the Sighing Strait. Then a child from Amberglass, then a Warden who had once been kind, then hundreds upon hundreds, each one given its own phrase, its own small architecture of sound, so that the hall filled gradually with the dead and the silenced walking back into the world through the only door their jailer could build for them: music.

At the bridge, Wren began to hum. Then Sef. Then the returned, raggedly, then the whole hall, then — I am not ashamed — me, a king humming a child's lullaby off-key in his own throne room, and beside me Brann humming too, and at the back Ovid's lips moving around a name he finally, finally said out loud.

*Lira.*

The song carried it gently, the way water carries a leaf, and set it down among all the others, and none of them were alone anymore.

Afterward, in the emptied hall, Edevane stood amid the fading resonance with its head bowed, and I approached it, and for a moment neither of us spoke — two men who had shared a skull for thirty years, learning the etiquette of separate bodies.

"You left room," I said. "In the music. For others."

"It was harder than writing alone," it admitted. "I kept wanting to fill the space. Silence kept... beckoning." Its hands trembled slightly on the bow. "She said to leave room. So I left room."

"She's usually right," I said. "It's insufferable."

Something happened to its face then that I think might have been the ancestor of a smile — some distant relative, three generations removed, visiting briefly. "Aldous. The mapmaker's apprentice came to me yesterday. Wren. She asked me to teach her composition." It looked at its hands. "I said I would. I don't know if I should. Everything I touch—"

"Everything you touched, you touched alone," I said. "That's over. Teach her. Leave room. We'll all be watching, Maestro. That's rather the point."

It bowed. And as it straightened, its gaze went past me to the high windows of the hall, where dawn was coming up over Corvenne, and it said, very quietly, mostly to itself:

"There's a passage I keep hearing. In the mornings. From the east walls. Something the whole city hums at dawn — I didn't write it. Nobody wrote it. It's just... there now. In the stones." It frowned, the frown of a composer confronted with music that had escaped its authorship entirely. "Someone should write it down."

I smiled, and turned toward the doors, toward the morning, toward the east walls where — I knew, because I had heard it too, every dawn since the rite — the whole realm was singing a song that belonged to no one and everyone, and where a certain cartographer had taken to standing at first light with ink-stained fingers and a page that drew the future instead of the past.

"Someone already is," I said.
