## 36. What Silas Owes

The trouble with saving a kingdom, I discovered three days after the Cantor fell silent, is that everyone insists on thanking you for it.

I had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of being overlooked. In Vell I could stand in a crowded market and be mistaken for furniture. I had cultivated a face that invited no questions and a voice that ended conversations before they began. It was, if I'm honest, my second-greatest work — after the map that unmade a tyrant, which I suppose makes it a distant second.

And now here was King Aldous of Cael Sorrow, restored and scrubbed and wearing an actual crown instead of a parasite with delusions of grandeur, standing in his rebuilt audience hall and looking directly at me like I was someone worth looking at.

It was unbearable.

"Silas Almeida," he said, and my name in a king's mouth sounded like a summons. "Realmwright. Cartographer. The hand that rewrote the song." He smiled, and it was a good smile, tired at the edges the way all his smiles were now, but good. "The realm owes you a debt it cannot count."

"It really can't," I agreed. "I've seen your ledgers. Ovid showed me. Your accounting practices are a scandal."

Somewhere behind me, Wren snorted. Brann made the low sound she made when she wanted to laugh but felt a queen-in-waiting-adjacent dignity was required of her, which was not actually a role she held, but she'd been practicing anyway.

Aldous didn't laugh. He did something worse. He went solemn.

"Which is why," he said, "I am prepared to offer you a title. Lands. A place at court. Whatever shape of home you want, Silas — the realm will build it for you."

* * *

Let me tell you what happened inside me when he said *home*, because it happened fast, the way a trapdoor happens.

Fifteen years is a long time to practice leaving. You get good at it. You learn to keep your possessions few and your attachments fewer, to love places from a distance and people from further. You learn that belonging is just the interval between arrivals of bad news — that every home you've ever had has ended with Wardens at the door or blood on a map or a friend's face going carefully blank as they realize what you are. I had been the last Realmwright, the architect of magic's outlawing, the coward who wrote the law and then ran from it. Every kindness anyone ever offered me came stamped with an expiration date: *valid until they find out.*

They'd found out, of course. Brann had found out in a drowned archive and forgiven me, which still didn't feel entirely legal. And yet the old arithmetic ran on regardless, ticking away beneath my ribs like a second heart: *nothing stays, nothing holds, go before you're sent.*

"Your Majesty is generous," I heard myself say, "but—"

"Don't," Brann said.

I turned. She was leaning against a pillar with her arms crossed, ferrywoman's shoulders set at the angle she used for passengers who were about to do something stupid near water.

"Don't what?" I said. "I haven't finished refusing yet."

"That's exactly what I mean. You've got the whole refusal loaded and ready. I can see it. You do this thing where you thank people and start backing toward the door like the room's on fire."

"The room isn't on fire."

"The room is never on fire, Silas. That's the problem. There's never a fire, there's never a reason, there's just you, backing out of every good thing that's ever happened to you like good things are a species of bear."

"That's — I don't —" I looked at Aldous for support, which was a tactical error, because Aldous was watching us with the expression of a man who had spent years trapped inside another mind and had developed, consequently, infinite patience for human nonsense. He wasn't going to help. He was going to *wait*. Kings should not be allowed to have patience. It's an abuse of power.

Wren tugged my sleeve.

She was eleven, and she had inherited her father's gift for maps and her mother's — whoever her mother had been — talent for cutting straight through me as though I were tissue paper. She looked up at me with those serious dark eyes and said, "You promised."

"I promised a lot of things. I'm a cartographer. Promising is most of the job."

"You promised the blank page would be walked together." She pointed at my satchel, where the map lived now — the new one, the one whose final page drew forward instead of back, the one with a whole region left empty and labeled in my own hand: *to be walked together.* "You can't walk together if you leave."

"That's — Wren, that's not —"

"It's exactly what it says. You wrote it down. Writing things down means they're true. That's what you told me about maps."

"I said writing things down means they're *accurate*, which is different, and also I was talking about coastlines—"

"Silas." Brann pushed off the pillar. Her voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. Quiet Brann was the one who had cornered me in Amberglass with the truth of who I was and refused to let me drown in it. Quiet Brann was the most dangerous thing on two legs in either realm. "Say the real reason."

"There isn't a real reason. There's a perfectly reasonable set of reasons. I'm a foreigner. I'm a criminal by the laws of my own country. I have no courtly skills whatsoever. I once sold a man a charm that made his chickens singe their own feathers, and if that gets out it'll be a diplomatic incident—"

"Silas."

"—and frankly the architecture here is far too vertical, I have a documented fear of towers after recent events—"

"*Silas.*"

I stopped. Because she'd crossed the distance between us without my noticing, which was a thing she could do, and taken my hands, which was a thing she almost never did, and her palms were rough as rope and warm as anything, and I ran out of words the way a tide runs out of sea.

"Say the real reason," she said again, gently.

And the terrible thing, the genuinely unforgivable thing about people who love you, is that they make lying feel like more work than truth.

* * *

Here is the real reason, which I said out loud, eventually, in an audience hall with a king and a child and a ferrywoman listening, because apparently I had lost all control of my own life:

"I don't know how to stay."

That was it. That was the whole shameful inventory. Fifteen years of craft and cunning and survival, and underneath it all, a person who had simply forgotten the mechanics of remaining. Staying meant unpacking. Staying meant letting people learn your habits, your nightmares, the way you took your tea and the sound you made when you slept badly. Staying meant being knowable, and being knowable meant being losable, and I had done my losing already, thank you, I had done a lifetime's worth, I had lost a whole profession and a whole world and every version of myself I'd ever tried to be.

"If I take a title," I said, "then I'm somebody here. If I'm somebody here, then I can be grieved here. Do you understand? Everything I've ever belonged to, I've broken or it's broken me, and I am — I am so tired, Brann. I'm tired in a way sleep doesn't touch. And staying feels like volunteering for it all over again."

The silence afterward had weight. Sound has weight in Cael Sorrow; I've mentioned that. But this silence had a different kind — the kind that comes when you've said a true thing in front of witnesses and nobody rushes to fix it.

It was Aldous who spoke first.

"When I begged for the binding," he said, "I was dying, and I was alone, and I thought those were the same condition. I have since learned otherwise." He came down from the dais steps — he did that now, he walked among people, it was making the courtiers frantic — until he stood close enough that I could see the faint silver threading in his eyes where the Cantor's song had lived. "Alone was the disease, Silas. Not the cure. I wore its cure for twenty years and it nearly ate the world."

"That's very poetic," I managed, "but I'm not a king, I'm a—"

"A cartographer," he said. "Yes. So tell me what you told the chorus, the night before the gates. Tell me what a map is for."

I looked at the floor. The floor offered no assistance.

"A map," I said, because he was going to wait me out, kings and their cursed patience, "is a promise that a place can be known. That it can be held. That nothing in it is unmappable, nothing is beyond care. It's — it's a way of saying *you exist, you matter, you are here*, to every road and river and—"

I stopped.

"Oh," I said.

"Yes," said Aldous, dry as old vellum.

"You absolute — you did that on purpose."

"I'm told I can be manipulative. Twenty years fused to a composer; some of it rubs off." His smile went soft at the corners. "You have spent your life promising places that they matter, Silas Almeida. I am asking you, for once, to let a place make the same promise to you."

* * *

I wish I could tell you I surrendered gracefully. I want that on the record. History will presumably record whatever the court scribes decide to record, and court scribes flatter everyone, so let history believe I accepted with dignity and a moving speech.

What actually happened is that I stood there in the middle of the audience hall and cried, briefly and furiously, like a man being handed something he'd stopped letting himself want, and then I said, "I need to think about it," which in the language of people like me means *yes, but let me panic privately first.*

Brann rolled her eyes. Wren grinned like she'd won a bet, which possibly she had; she'd been running wagers with the kitchen staff for days on when I'd crack.

I took my panic to the walls.

Corvenne at dusk was learning to be loud again. After the Great Quieting — after villages gone snow-silent and a city holding its breath — sound itself felt like a celebration here. Bells that had hung mute for years were being re-hung and rung, badly, joyfully. Somewhere below, the market district was rehearsing. Actually rehearsing — the chorus from Low Hum had stayed on as something between a militia and a music school, and you could hear them working through the new song, the duet, the verse I'd stitched between king and composer. They got it wrong in a dozen places. They kept going anyway. That was the whole doctrine of the rebellion of humming, and apparently it was contagious, because I found myself humming along before I noticed, and then noticing, and then stopping, and then starting again on purpose.

"You're doing it wrong," said Wren, materializing beside me on the battlements the way she did, soundless as a cat, smug as one too.

"I invented half that harmony."

"And you're still doing it wrong. You always drop the third. Brann says you drop the third when you're thinking too hard."

"Brann says a lot of things."

"She does." Wren leaned her elbows on the stone and looked out at the city with me. For a while neither of us talked, which was its own kind of music, these days. Then: "My dad used to hum when he worked. On his maps. I forgot that until the other day. The Cantor took the memory along with everything else, and now it's back, and it turns out remembering him hurts better than not remembering him did." She shrugged, eleven years old and ancient. "That's what staying is, I think. Choosing the hurt-better kind."

I looked down at her, this child who had walked into my shop-shaped life and rearranged all the furniture. "When did you get wise?"

"Amberglass. They have books."

"They have drowned books."

"Drowned books are just books with better stories." She bumped her shoulder against my arm. "Stay, Silas. Not for the king. For the blank page. Nobody else knows how to walk it."

* * *

Brann found me later, or I found her — the distinction had stopped mattering somewhere around the third realm-saving. She was at the harbor wall below the city, watching Ovid's new ferry line load up, arms crossed against the evening wind, and she didn't look at me when I sat down beside her, which was her way of giving me room.

"I nearly refused today," I said.

"I know. I was there."

"No, I mean — I had it done. The refusal was built. Load-bearing. And then you and Wren knocked it down in under a minute, and I've been trying to work out why it was so easy for you and so impossible for me."

Brann considered the harbor. A gull screamed something editorial. "Because you think belonging is a debt," she said finally. "You've thought that since I met you. Every kindness anybody does you, you start calculating how to repay it before you've even said thank you. You can't accept a home because a home is a debt you can never finish paying, and unfinished debts terrify you more than loneliness does."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "That's — hm."

"Your face right now," she said, "is the face of a man whose own ferry has been stolen and sailed better than he ever sailed it."

"Whose ferry have you stolen?"

"Yours. Years ago. You just weren't watching the harbor." She turned to me then, and the wind pulled at her hair, and she had that expression — the one from the archive, the one that had said *I know what you did and I'm still here.* "So here's what you owe, Silas. Since you insist on keeping accounts. You owe this realm a map. All of it. Every road, every shore, every village the Cantor silenced and every thin place between worlds. No one gets unmapped again — not here, not anywhere. That's the debt. That's the whole ledger. Can you pay it?"

The question went into me like a keel into water.

Because that — that I could do. That I *wanted* to do, had wanted since the moment the dying stranger pressed his blood-inked map into my hands and whispered *finish it.* I had thought *finish it* meant finish the binding, complete the rite, end the song. But maps don't end. Maps begin. Every finished map is just the first draft of the next one.

"Not alone," I said.

"No," Brann agreed. "Not alone. That's rather the point of the last month, yes?"

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, at dusk on a harbor wall in a world that wasn't mine, except that maybe it was, except that maybe it had been since the tide of a dead man's map pulled me under. "You're a terrible influence, Brann."

"I'm a ferrywoman. I influence people across." She stood, and offered me her hand, which was ridiculous, we were both sitting on the same wall, but I took it anyway. "Come on. The king's waiting for your answer, and Wren's bet closes at sundown."

"What did she bet?"

"On you staying? A week of desserts." Brann grinned. "On you running? Nothing. Nobody bet on you running, Silas. Not even you."

* * *

I gave Aldous my answer the next morning, in the audience hall, with the whole court watching and Wren visibly calculating dessert futures.

I declined the title. Lands, I declined. A place at court, I declined — courts and I had a history, and the history was mostly me forging documents.

But a home —

A home, I said, I would take. On one condition: not given, but tasked. If the realm wanted to repay its debt, it could do it by owing me one in return. Make me useful. Give me the whole unmapped country — the borders the Cantor erased, the villages gone quiet, the roads that no longer appeared on any chart in existence. Let me walk all of it, with a ferrywoman who knew every current and a girl who knew every story and a cynical boatman who owed me approximately nothing and pretended that meant something. Let me draw Cael Sorrow as it truly was, so that no one in it would ever again be unmapped, unnamed, unsung.

Aldous listened to all of it with that maddening patience, and when I finished he said only: "Then we will need an oath."

"An oath," I repeated.

"A cartographer's oath. Sworn before the court and the chorus both. Tomorrow at dawn, on the walls, where the realm can hear it." He was smiling now, openly, the tired edges gone soft. "Unless the great Silas Almeida has objections to swearing oaths as well?"

I had, in fact, seventeen objections, and Wren kicked my ankle before I could enumerate them.

"No objections," I said.

"Liar," whispered Wren.

But I stayed. That night, in the rooms the palace had given me — rooms with my maps spread across every surface, rooms that smelled of ink and salt and, faintly, of home whether I consented to it or not — I sat up late with the map across my knees, and I inked a single word onto the blank region's border, small, where only I would know to look.

*Ours.*

Tomorrow there would be an oath. Tomorrow I would swear, in front of a kingdom, that I belonged to it and it to me, and every coward I had ever been would be screaming, and I would swear anyway.

I dipped my pen. The dawn was coming. For the first time in fifteen years, I wasn't planning how to be gone before it arrived.
