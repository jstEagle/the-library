## 37. The Cartographer's Oath

I had sworn oaths before. That was the trouble with oaths — they came cheap when you were young and certain, and they collected interest for the rest of your life.

The first one I'd ever taken was at nineteen, ink still wet on my certification from the Guild of Realmwrights, my hand raised before a hall of elders whose names I can no longer recall and whose faces I have spent fifteen years trying to forget. *To map truly. To bind the wild places into legible order. To serve the law of the realms above all things.* I had said the words with a straight back and a full heart, and within the decade those same words had helped outlaw magic in an entire world and left me selling luck charms to widows who deserved better than counterfeit fortune.

So you'll forgive me if, standing in the great hall of Corvenne with a king's oath-ring warm in my palm, my first instinct was to run.

"You're doing the thing," Brann said beside me. She'd appointed herself my keeper somewhere around the Road of Teeth and had never formally resigned the position. "The thing where your face goes very calm and your feet start calculating exits."

"I don't have a thing."

"You have several things. This is the worst one." She nodded toward the doors at the far end of the hall, through which a crowd was gathering — not courtiers, thank every listening god, but people. Villagers. Rebels from Low Hum with soot still under their fingernails. Ferryfolk. A delegation of Amberglass archivists dripping lake water on the marble because no one had thought to tell them that palaces had floors you weren't supposed to flood. "They didn't come to watch a ceremony, Silas. They came to watch you stop hiding."

"That's much worse."

"I know," she said, and there was something underneath her dryness, something soft as silt, and I loved her for it. "Do it anyway."

* * *

The hall had been rebuilt around its silence. That sounds like poetry but it's really just architecture: after the Cantor's fall, the throne room's deadening wards had been peeled away layer by layer, and what remained was a space with acoustics so honest it felt indecent. Every footfall rang. Every breath carried. When King Aldous spoke from the dais — and he insisted on speaking, though his voice was still a thin reed of the thing it must once have been — the whole room heard him the way you hear a friend across a kitchen table.

He had refused a grander setting. "Coronation halls are for crowns," he'd told me the night before, sitting wrapped in three blankets in a room that still smelled faintly of the cell he'd been kept in, because he'd asked to be moved back into it until the repairs were done. He said it made him feel like himself. I understood that more than I let on. "This is not a coronation. It's a hiring."

"A hiring," I repeated.

"An appointment. An employment. Whatever word lets you pretend this is smaller than it is." His eyes had gone lucid and wry, and for a moment I saw the man the Cantor had sung over for thirty years — the man who had begged, dying, for someone to carry half his weight, and who had watched that mercy become a prison for them both. "You taught me something in that cell, cartographer. You told me silence wasn't empty. You said it was just a page nobody had written on yet."

"I say a lot of things when I'm terrified."

"Yes," Aldous said. "That's how I know which ones are true."

* * *

Now he stood on the dais in plain clothes — he'd been firm about that too; the crown sat on a cushion beside him like a guest who'd overstayed — and the hall hushed itself without being asked.

Wren found my hand. She'd grown two inches since the market, or perhaps she'd simply stopped trying to be small. Her father's map hung in a frame behind the dais now, all but the last page, and she looked at it the way you look at a parent's grave: with grief and argument in equal measure.

"He would have hated this," she whispered. "All these people watching. Papa hated crowds."

"He'd have loved the part where everyone has to listen to me talk about maps."

"No," Wren said, with the merciless precision of twelve-year-olds everywhere, "he'd have fallen asleep. But he'd have been *proud* asleep. There's a difference."

Ovid cleared his throat from the front row. He was walking without the cane now, mostly, and pretending he hadn't noticed the difference, and every morning he checked the ledger of names recovered from the undercroft against his memory of the drowned and the silenced, crossing off the found ones with a ferryman's pencil worn to a stub. He'd crossed off four hundred and eleven names. He kept the stub in his breast pocket like a relic.

"If he stalls," Ovid announced to the hall at large, "the traditional remedy is throwing bread. I've seen it done at weddings. Very dignified."

"Ovid," Brann said.

"I'm establishing precedent. Someone has to."

Aldous raised a hand, and even that small motion carried in the honest air, and the hall went quiet enough that I could hear the banners moving against their poles.

"Silas Almeida," he said. "Fifteen years ago, you took an oath under the old law. I'm told you've spent every year since paying its debts." He paused. "I'm also told you're terrible at accepting compliments, so I'll keep this brief."

A ripple of laughter. I felt my ears go hot. Somewhere in the crowd, Brann made a sound she would later deny was a snort.

"The old oath bound mapping to law," Aldous said. "And law, we learned, can be written by fear. The Cantor sang one song and called it order. The Guild drew one map and called it truth. Both were lies of the same shape — the lie that a world is finished, and needs only to be enforced." He picked up the oath-ring. It was old iron, unadorned, the kind ferry captains wore. "So I am not asking you to swear the old oath again. I'm asking you to write a new one. Out loud. In front of everyone. And then live inside it, which is the harder part."

"And if I refuse?" I asked, because someone had to, and it has always been my curse to be that someone.

"Then you refuse," Aldous said simply. "And you stay anyway, because you already have. The oath isn't what binds you here, Silas. We both know that. The oath is just the moment you admit it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about claiming an identity: it feels less like putting on a coat and more like taking one off. Fifteen years of charm-selling Silas, of lowered eyes and mumbled names and magic hidden in tremors — I had built that person plank by plank, and I had believed, right up until the stranger bled out on my shop floor, that the planks were load-bearing.

They weren't. They never had been. Underneath was just me, holding a pen, terrified of the blank page.

Aldous held out the ring.

I did not take it yet. Instead I turned to face the hall — the rebels and the archivists, the ferryfolk and the villagers, Brann with her arms crossed and her eyes suspiciously bright, Wren gripping my hand hard enough to grind bone, Ovid wearing the expression of a man determined to look bored and failing — and I spoke.

Not the old words. New ones, rough-hewn, mine.

"I swore once to map truly," I said, and my voice carried whether I wanted it to or not. "And I used that truth as a weapon. I drew borders for men who feared what they couldn't name, and then I outlawed the naming, and then I ran. For fifteen years I sold false charms and called it penance, and penance it was not, because penance requires facing what you did, and I faced nothing. I hid."

Nobody moved. In Cael Sorrow, I was learning, people knew how to listen. It might have been the most valuable thing this realm had ever taught me.

"So hear the new oath," I said. "I swear to map *everything*. Not to tame it. Not to order it. To walk it, and listen to it, and set down what it says about itself. I swear that no place in Cael Sorrow will ever again be unmapped — because unmapped means unclaimed by anyone's care, and unclaimed is where the Cantors grow. I swear that no person will be erased from the record — not the silenced, not the exiled, not the ones who fled across the fold like cowards with good handwriting." My voice cracked on that last part. I let it. "I swear the map will belong to the realm, not the realm to the map. And I swear—" and here I looked down at Wren, who was staring up at me with her father's stubborn chin, "—that the last page stays open. Always. Because a finished map is a dead map, and I have buried enough things."

Silence. The honest kind, the kind that holds a room instead of hollowing it.

Then Aldous slid the ring onto my finger, and it fit — of course it fit; I suspected he'd had it measured weeks ago, the quietly scheming man — and the hall did not cheer so much as *hum*, low and collective, the way Cael Sorrow expressed everything, and the sound went through the floor and up through my boots and settled somewhere beneath my sternum like a second heartbeat.

"There," Brann murmured. "Done. No bread required."

"I'm almost disappointed."

"Don't be. Ovid brought some anyway. He's eating it."

* * *

Afterward — after the hum and the handshakes and the archivist who shook my hand so enthusiastically that lake water flew off us both — Wren cornered me on the balcony overlooking the eastern ranges, where the mountains went blue and endless into the distance, all of it waiting to be walked, all of it waiting to be named by its own consent.

"So," she said, swinging her legs over the balustrade with the total disregard for mortality unique to children and cats. "When do we start?"

"*We?*"

"You said 'to be walked together' is going on the map. I heard you. Everyone heard you. You said it in front of a king." She grinned at me, gap-toothed and merciless. "Besides, Papa's map has a region on it he never let anyone see. Bottom corner. He always covered it with his thumb when I peeked."

Something cold and bright moved through me. "Show me."

She unfolded the old map against the balustrade, careful with its ancient folds, and pointed to the far southwest — a stretch of parchment that was, impossibly, *blank*. Not faded. Blank. And as my fingers hovered over it, the blood-ink stirred, faint as a pulse, and I could have sworn — could have *sworn* — that the blankness shifted, the way a sleeper shifts under blankets.

"Well," I said, when I trusted my voice. "It seems the map isn't finished with us either."

Wren smiled like sunrise over a country finally allowed to sing. "When do we leave?"

I looked out at the blue mountains, at the realm that had swallowed me and handed me back to myself, at the ring on my finger and the family at my back and the page that stayed open, always.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight, we sleep. Tomorrow, we walk."
