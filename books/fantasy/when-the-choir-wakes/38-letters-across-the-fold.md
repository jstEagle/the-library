## 38. Letters Across the Fold

The trouble with two worlds is that you can be late in both of them at once.

I learned this on a Tuesday — or what passed for a Tuesday in Cael Sorrow, where the days were named after tides and the tides had opinions. I was due to ferry a wool merchant from Vell's dockside to the thin place off Gullspit Rock by midmorning, and I was also due at Wren's letter-reading in Amberglass by the same midmorning, and the fold between the realms, generous as it had become since Silas rewrote it, still made you choose which side of yourself to stand on while you crossed.

I chose the ferry. The merchant paid better than family did, and family, unlike merchants, forgave.

"Mind the gunwale," I told him as we slid out of Vell harbor. The fog was doing its usual trick of pretending the city didn't exist beyond three boat-lengths, which suited me fine. Fifteen years I'd lived in that city and it had never once pretended anything for my benefit.

He was one of the new kind of passenger, the ones who'd heard rumors. Since the fold reopened — since Silas stood in a throne room full of dead sound and sang a king and a monster into a duet — there'd been traffic. Nothing official. No treaties, no customs men, no Wardens with their little brass whistles. Just people, trickling through the thin places like water finding a crack in a wall: traders with salt they swore came from nowhere, pilgrims who wanted to hum in a country where humming meant something, and the occasional scholar with more curiosity than sense.

This merchant was the third kind wearing the first kind's coat.

"Heard there's a city over there," he said, "where the buildings are grown, not built."

"There is."

"And the sea sings back when you shout at it."

"It does. Don't shout at it. It remembers."

He laughed, the way people laugh at ferrywomen who might be joking. I let him. The truth was stranger than any joke I could tell, and jokes kept the questions short.

We rounded Gullspit as the tide turned, and there it was: the fold. From the outside it looked like nothing much — a shimmer in the air above the water, the way heat shimmers over a summer road, except this shimmer hung in November fog and went down into the sea as well as up. I'd ferried through it maybe forty times now, and every time it took my breath somewhere behind my ribs and held it until the other side.

"Steady now," I said, and put the bow into the light.

Crossing feels like being read. That's the only way I've ever found to say it. Like something turns the pages of you quickly, checking for damage, checking for contraband, checking — I've always suspected — for whether you're the same person who went through last time. I'm not. Nobody is. The fold knows it and doesn't hold it against you.

On the far side, the water was Amberglass Sound, and the drowned archive rose out of it like a chandelier someone had sunk on purpose, which, in fairness, was roughly what it was. Light came up through the glass in long amber columns. And on the archive's lowest terrace, ankle-deep in the shallows, sat a girl of eleven with her sleeves rolled up and ink to her elbows, surrounded by floating pages she was bullying into order.

"You're late," Wren said, without looking up.

"I'm a businesswoman. Businesswomen are never late. They arrive when the tide does."

"The tide was here an hour ago." She finally looked up, and grinned, and the grin undid me the way it always did — because her father had that same grin, the stranger who bled out on Silas's shop floor with a map in his hands and a daughter he'd never see grow up, and every time Wren smiled like that I got to watch a dead man be alive again for half a second. It was the best and worst thing about her, and she didn't even know she did it.

I tied off at the terrace and climbed down. "Letter?"

"From Silas." She held it out, then pulled it back an inch, the way she did, making me earn it. "You have to hear the postscript."

"Wren."

"They wrote it in the margin. In the old hand." She said *the old hand* the way scholars said it, which was funny, because she'd learned it from Silas, who'd invented half the excuses for why nobody should learn it. "You know what that means."

"It means your teacher is showing off."

"It means it's not just a letter. It's a chart." She smoothed the page flat on her knee, careful of the wet, and read aloud:

*Brann — Tide's low here too. Corvenne's walls at dawn are worth the early rising; the whole city hums the morning verse and the stone carries it out to sea. You'd hate how early it starts and love everything after. Ovid says his new line will beat mine to the Sighing Strait by spring, which is a lie he tells himself nightly, and I've told him so in the margin of his own ledger, which he has forgiven, or hasn't, hard to say. Wren — the third shelf of the west stacks is mislabeled. Fix it. Consider it your examination. — S.*

Wren stopped. "That's not the postscript."

"No," I said, because I'd caught it too, caught it the way you catch a fisherman's lie — not by the words but by the shape of them. "Read the margin."

She tilted the page toward the amber light. And there, running up the edge of the paper in a slanted, looping script — the Realmwright's hand, the hand I'd spent fifteen years next door to without once seeing, because Silas hid things the way eels hide, in plain sight and perfectly still — was a second message:

*P.S. There's a thin place opening near Low Hum that neither of us has walked. It doesn't lead anywhere on my map yet. Come look at it with me before I name it.*

Silence, for a moment. The good kind — the kind that had taken a war to make safe.

"She wants us to go exploring," Wren said.

"She wants company while she's scared of a blank spot on a map. Same thing."

"When do we leave?"

I looked out across the sound, at the light coming up through the drowned library, at the pages drifting around my feet like lazy fish, at this impossible life I had fallen into sideways through a folding of space while trying to stop a neighbor from getting arrested. Somewhere behind me, through the fold, Vell was waking up: gulls, fishwives, the Warden bells that no longer hunted anyone but still rang like they hadn't gotten the news. Two worlds. One woman. Not enough mornings.

"Tomorrow's tide," I said. "Tonight we read the rest of the letter properly, and you fix that shelf, and I drink whatever passes for tea in this place without complaining, which you know is a sacrifice."

Wren laughed and folded the letter into her satchel, into the pocket where she kept her father's map — the finished one, the one Silas had inked the last page of on Corvenne's walls at dawn while ten thousand voices held the note. She touched the satchel flap once, lightly, the way you touch a door you're not ready to open, and then she was up and moving, bossing the floating pages back into their shelves, and I followed her into the amber dark of the archive, thinking:

Tomorrow's tide. A blank place on a map. A friend who wrote in margins because some things were too true to say in the middle of the page.

It was, I reflected, hauling myself up the terrace steps after her, a long way from covering for a charm-seller during a Warden sweep and expecting nothing but trouble in return.

But then, I'd never expected anything else. I'd just hoped, quietly, in the way ferrywomen hope — against the current, against the schedule, against all evidence.

And for once, the evidence had come around to my way of thinking.

* * *

Ovid met us at the Sighing Strait two days later, which was itself a miracle, since Ovid meeting anyone anywhere on time would have been grounds for canonization in the old days.

His new ferry line ran three boats now — the *Ledger*, the *Last Word*, and a leaky thing called the *Second Thought* that he swore was his best vessel precisely because it forced passengers to commit. He'd painted all three a deep, unrepentant black, "because grief is a color and I own it," and he stood at the strait's edge in a coat almost as long as his history of complaints, watching our boat come in with the expression of a man who has been waiting exactly long enough to be annoyed about it.

"You're late," he said.

"So I'm told. It's becoming a theme."

"Tides don't wait, ferrywoman."

"Neither do letters, and yet yours took four days to reach Amberglass."

"My letters travel at the speed of grievance. It's a slower current." He waved us ashore with a jerk of his chin, and I saw — because I'd learned to see it, these months, the way you learn to read weather — the thing under the wryness. His hands. He kept them busy when he talked, coiling rope, straightening rope, coiling it wrong on purpose so he'd have something to fix. Since the night Wren's lullaby had cracked him open on the road and he'd wept for his daughter, he'd been busier than ever. Busy hands, busy mouth, busy boats. A man building a fleet between himself and the quiet.

Except now and then, the quiet got through anyway. And when it did, he didn't run anymore. He just stood in it, jaw set, and let it pass through him like weather. I'd seen him do it at the memorial in Low Hum, standing at the wall of names with his hat off, saying nothing, for the length of a full tide. The rebels had started calling it Ovid's Minute, though it lasted an hour, though he'd deny to his grave that it happened at all.

"How is the line?" I asked, nodding at the boats.

"Profitable, which disgusts me. Popular, which disgusts me more. Half of Vell wants to come see the singing country and half of the singing country wants to see what all the fuss is about in Vell, and I am the poor fool in the middle charging both directions." He squinted at me. "You're going to ask me for something. I can tell. You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face of a woman about to say *Ovid, we need a favor* and pretend it's a compliment."

I glanced at Wren, who was already halfway up the *Second Thought*'s rigging because of course she was, and then back at him. "There's a thin place near Low Hum. New one. Off Silas's map."

Something shifted in his face — small, quick, gone. "Off the map," he repeated.

"That's what I said."

"Nothing's off her map anymore. She redrew the world. I watched her do it." He coiled the rope. Uncoiled it. "When?"

"Tomorrow's tide, if the wind agrees."

"The wind never agrees. The wind takes bribes." He threw the coil down and looked out at the strait, where the water sighed against the rocks in long slow breaths, the sound that gave the crossing its name, the sound that had carried his daughter's name once, on the day the Cantor's purge reached his village and took everyone who wouldn't stop singing. He'd told us that on the road, in the dark, in the flat voice of a man reading someone else's inventory. He'd never told us twice. He didn't need to. Some ledgers you keep in your head.

"I'll take you as far as Low Hum," he said. "After that—" He shrugged, elaborate, theatrical, entirely unconvincing. "After that I suppose I'll just happen to be sailing in the same direction, because that's where the current goes, and I'm a slave to the current, everyone knows this about me."

"Wouldn't dream of suggesting otherwise."

"Good. Because if you suggested otherwise I'd have to charge you for sentiment, and my rates for that are criminal." He turned and bellowed up the rigging — "Wren! Out of my lines before you reorganize them!" — and she laughed and slid down like it was a game, like it was easy, like falling was just another way of arriving, and I watched the two of them argue about knots, and thought: *there it is.* The family. Not the one any of us were born to. The one you build, plank by plank, out of the people who show up when the tide turns bad.

Ovid caught me looking and scowled. "Stop that."

"Stop what?"

"Being pleased at me. It's unsettling."

"Too late. I've been pleased at you for months. You'll live."

He muttered something about mutinies and fare refunds and stalked off to yell at his crew, and Wren bumped her shoulder against mine, and above us the strait sighed, and somewhere past the headland, in a country that had been unmapped and unloved and unsung for fifteen years, a blank space waited on a map with our names practically written on it already.

* * *

We reached Low Hum at dusk, and Silas was waiting at the village gate, which meant Silas had known we were coming, which meant Silas had felt it — the new thin place, the pull of the unnamed — the way they felt everything now, with the Realmwright's senses wide open and no more hiding left to do.

Fifteen years I'd lived beside them and never once seen their face do what it did when they spotted us coming up the road. It was a small thing. A loosening. Like a knot deciding, after years of holding, that it was allowed to be untied.

"You got the letter," Silas said.

"We got the margin," Wren said.

"Ah." Silas rubbed the back of their neck, embarrassed, and I marveled — not for the first time — at how a person could rewrite reality and still flinch when caught being sincere. "I wasn't sure how to say it in the middle of the page."

"You could try *hello, I missed you*," I said. "Words exist. I've checked."

"I did write that."

"In the margin."

"Important things go in margins." They smiled then, fully, and gestured past the gate, up the slope behind the village, where the evening light lay strange and silvery on the hillside — bent, somehow, focused, the way light bends through water or glass. "Come on. Before the tide turns. It opens best at slack water, and I want—"

They stopped. Started again, quieter.

"I want to walk it with you first. Before I map it. That's the rule now. Nothing gets named until somebody's stood in it together."

Wren was already through the gate. Ovid was pretending to inspect the fence. And I fell into step beside Silas on the climb, our boots quiet on the turf, the village humming its evening songs below us — dozens of small voices braided together, holding each other's shapes the way they'd always done, except now nobody was punished for it, and the songs had gotten longer, and sillier, and free.

"So," I said. "A blank spot."

"A blank spot." Silas's eyes had that light in them, the dangerous one, the cartographer's hunger. "It wasn't there last month. The fold is still settling — rewriting ripples outward, apparently, for seasons. New thin places keep opening like—" they waved a hand, searching, "—like scars healing into doors."

"And nobody's walked this one."

"Not yet."

"Could be anything in there."

"Could be." They glanced at me sidelong, and there was the wryness, back where it lived. "Could be nothing. Could be another realm. Could be a very disappointed rock formation. The map won't say until it's drawn, and it won't draw until it's walked, and it won't walk itself."

"No," I agreed. "It needs a cartographer."

"It needs," Silas said, and here their voice did the thing it did rarely, the unguarded thing, the thing that had taken a throne room and a chorus and a cut palm to teach it, "its people."

Ahead of us, Wren crested the hill and shouted back, breathless, pointing at the air above the ridge — where the twilight was folding, gently, like a page being turned.

"Come *on*," she yelled. "It's opening!"

We ran. All four of us, up the last stretch of the hill, Ovid grumbling, Silas laughing — actually laughing, open-throated, a sound I intended to bring up whenever convenient for the rest of my natural life — and the thin place unfurled before us like a held breath finally let go, showing nothing yet, showing everything possible, a doorway with no room behind it except the one we'd carry in.

Silas paused at the threshold, quill already in hand, and looked back at me.

"No telling what's on the other side," they said.

"Never is," I said. "That's the whole point of doors."

And we stepped through together, into the blank page, four names walking into a space that had no name yet — which was, I thought as the light closed behind us, exactly how a family begins.
