## 39. When the Choir Wakes

I have been awake since the third bell, which is to say I never really slept. Fifteen years of sleeping like a man with one ear cocked toward the door will do that to you; it takes longer than a season of peace to unlearn it. But this morning I didn't mind the waking. This morning the whole city was already humming.

I stood at my window in the Cartographer's House — *my* house, a phrase I still test in my mouth the way you'd test ice before putting your weight on it — and watched Corvenne wake in layers. First the walls themselves, where the watch had lit their lamps against the last of the dark. Then the rooftops, where pigeons cooed something that sounded suspiciously like the second verse of the new song, though I refuse to believe the birds learned it from us. Then the streets, filling with people who were not going anywhere in particular, who were simply walking toward the walls because that was where everyone else was walking, and because today was the day.

Today we would finish the map.

The ink was already mixed. It sat on my desk in a bowl of Amberglass glass — Ovid had insisted on glass from the archive itself, "for sentiment," he said, "and also because I stole it fair and square" — and it was not red anymore. That had been the great discovery of the winter, the thing Wren had seen when none of us older, duller fools could: that the rite did not want blood. It wanted *voice*. The old Realmwrights had used blood because they worked alone, because they were proud, because no one had ever thought to ask whether a map could be drawn by more than one hand.

The ink in the bowl now was pale gold, shot through with threads of every color the chorus had given it. Brann's contribution was a grey-blue like the Sighing Strait at dawn. Wren's was a bright reckless green. Ovid's, when he finally admitted he'd sung anything at all, turned out to be the exact brown of river mud, which he claimed was an insult until I pointed out that rivers are how everything gets where it's going.

A knock at my door. Two sharp, one soft. Brann's knock; she'd invented it herself and drilled me on it until I stopped answering doors for anyone who knocked any other way.

"You're up," she said, looking me over with the frank appraisal of a woman who has ferried enough passengers to know which ones are lying about being ready. "Good. Wren's been pacing the wall walk since midnight. She'll wear a groove in the stone."

"She's excited."

"She's *terrified*," Brann said, "which is the same thing in her, and don't pretend you can't tell the difference anymore." She paused in the doorway, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for things she meant. "You ready? Actually ready?"

I looked at the bowl of ink. At the rolled map beside it, fat as a sleeping cat, fifteen years of hiding stitched through its early pages and six months of belonging through its later ones. At my own hands, steady for the first time since Vell.

"No," I said honestly. "But I've done my best work unready."

Brann snorted. "That's the most honest thing you've said all year. Come on, then. The realm's waiting on you, and it hates waiting."

* * *

The eastern wall of Corvenne at dawn is the kind of view that makes a cartographer dangerous. The city falls away below you in its rings and terraces, the palace behind you wearing its new scaffolding like a patient convalescent, and beyond the gates the whole of Cael Sorrow rolls out to the horizon — the Road of Teeth green again now that nothing mute patrols it, the Sighing Strait glittering, the far hills of Low Hum hazed blue with distance and, if you knew where to look, with the faint golden shimmer of wards rebuilt by humming instead of fear.

And the people. Saints, the people. They lined the wall walk three deep. They filled the battlements and spilled down the outer stair and stood in the fields beyond the gate, and every one of them was singing. Not loudly. Not in unison, exactly — the new song wasn't built for unison, that was rather the point of it — but in parts, in rounds, in the loose braided harmony that Silas Almeida, last of the Realmwrights and first Cartographer of Cael Sorrow, had spent a winter teaching to anyone who'd hold still long enough.

Wren found me before I'd taken ten steps along the wall. She's grown half a head since autumn, which she announces monthly as though I might have missed it, and she walked backward in front of me so she could talk while I walked, which is a habit she learned from Ovid and should be ashamed of.

"You're late."

"I'm punctual. Everyone else is early."

"The page is set out. The light's perfect. Ovid says if you wait much longer the tide of the dawn will turn and the ink won't take, and then he gets to say *I told you* for another full year, and Brann says she'll push him off the wall if he does, and I say let them fight, it's very entertaining—"

"Wren." She stopped walking backward. I crouched a little, the way I do, so we were eye to eye. Her father's eyes. I still see him sometimes, in the corner of the shop in Vell, pressing the folded map into my hands, saying *finish it* — and for a long time I thought he'd meant the map. Now I think he'd meant all of it. "It's going to hold. You know that, yes? Whatever happens with the ink, the song holds. We've proven it."

She bit her lip. "What if my part goes wrong?"

"Then two hundred people will carry it for you until you find your note again. That's the entire design. That's what your father died carrying, and what you finished carrying, and what—" I tapped her sternum gently, "—you keep carrying better than any of us. There is no wrong note in this song, Wren. There are only notes waiting for the rest of us to catch up."

She considered this with the gravity of a twelve-year-old deciding whether to be comforted or offended, chose comfort, and seized my hand. "Come *on*, then."

* * *

They'd set the table on the highest bastion, the one facing east, where the first light would hit. The map lay open across it, weighted at the corners with four stones that each of us had carried from somewhere that mattered: a stone from Vell's harbor, a stone from the drowned shelves of Amberglass, a stone from the shore where Ovid's daughter's name had come home to him, a plain gray pebble Wren had picked up on the Road of Teeth and refused to explain.

One page remained blank. The last page. The one the old binding had reserved for a cartographer's blood.

Ovid leaned against the parapet, arms crossed, watching me approach with the expression of a man pretending not to be nervous about someone else's big moment. His wound had healed crooked, and he favored his left side in cold weather, and he complained about both constantly, which was how we all knew he was happy.

"About time," he said. "Do you know how long I've been standing here? There are tides turning all over the place. Metaphorical ones. Very stressful."

"Sit down, Ovid."

"I never sit down. Sitting down is how you get attached to places." But he straightened up, and his voice lost its wry edge for exactly one sentence. "Fifteen years ago somebody ran from a world that didn't want them. Today that same stubborn fool draws a page that tells a whole country where it's going. If you'd told me that man existed, I'd have charged you double for the crossing." He clapped me on the shoulder, carefully, on the good side. "Make it a good line, cartographer. I'm in it."

Brann stood on my other side, and she said nothing at all, which from Brann was a speech. She just nodded once, ferrywoman to passenger, the nod that means *I've got the tiller; step aboard.*

I dipped the pen.

Here is what I had expected, all those months ago when the rite's price was blood and I had resolved to pay it alone: trembling. Terror. The old loneliness rising up to meet me one final time. What I got instead was the hum of two hundred voices at my back, and Wren's hand in mine for the first stroke, and the strange calm of a man who has finally stopped being the only one holding the rope.

The pen touched the page, and the ink *sang*.

I mean that literally. The gold rose off the nib in threads that caught the dawn and harmonized with the chorus behind me, and where they landed, the blank page began to fill — not with the past, as every map I'd ever made had done, not with coastlines already walked and borders already bled over, but with lines that moved. Rivers that hadn't yet changed course shifted under the ink, considering. A road appeared where no road was, faint as breath on glass, running from Corvenne's east gate out across the plains toward a valley I did not know. Villages sketched themselves in soft outline, some standing, some not yet built, waiting to see which way the world decided to go.

"Aldous said it would do that," Wren whispered. "Draw forward. He said the old maps were obituaries and yours could be a birth announcement."

"Your king," I said, steadying my hand, "has a flair for phrasing."

I drew the coastline of the Sighing Strait, and Ovid made a sound low in his throat when the ferry routes appeared on it — his routes, the new line, the one he'd sworn up and down was just a business venture. I drew the hills above Low Hum, and heard behind me the rebels' chord swell with something that was not quite grief and not quite triumph. I drew Amberglass, dry now, its drowned shelves breathing air and lamplight, and somewhere in the crowd a young archivist — Wren's rival, her friend, her future — cheered.

And then the pen slowed, because there was one region left, high on the page where the mountains began, and my hand remembered what I had promised myself I would do.

I left it blank.

Not empty — there's a difference, and every Realmwright knows it. I wrote across it, in my own hand, in ink that shimmered: *to be walked together.*

Behind me, the chorus reached the verse where all the parts come home, and the sound went up off the wall and out over the fields like weather, like sunrise given a voice, and I felt it land in the wet ink and set it, felt the page accept the song the way soil accepts rain, and the map was done.

Fifteen years ago I had pressed my palm flat against a closing door between worlds and chosen silence, chosen smallness, chosen a life half-lived in a city that forgot things on purpose. I stood now on a wall with ink on my fingers and a whole realm singing through me, and I understood at last that the door had never closed. It had only been waiting for enough voices to open it.

* * *

We stayed on the wall until the sun cleared the hills. Aldous came, eventually — kings are always eventually — and stood beside me at the parapet without ceremony, which is the thing I like best about him. The Cantor was with him, as it always was now: diminished, watchful, sane, its composer's hands folded, listening to the crowd with the expression of a man hearing music he once believed only he could make.

"It holds," Aldous said quietly, nodding at the map. "You can hear it holding."

"That's the design."

"Mm." He was silent a moment. "When I was bound, there were years — decades — when I couldn't tell where I ended and it began. And in the worst of it, I used to dream of a room with a table in it, and a lamp, and someone bent over a map, drawing a world where I was free." He glanced at me sideways, and his eyes had that terrible lucid warmth I'd seen through the tower window, the morning everything changed. "I thought it was only a dream. Kings are rarely lucky enough to have their dreams walk in through the front door."

"Don't make it strange, Your Majesty."

"Too late. I've already signed the proclamation." He smiled, and beside him the Cantor made a sound that in its old life would have been a held breath and was now, unmistakably, a laugh.

Below us, Wren had organized — there is no other word for it — a contingent of children, and was leading them in the round, badly and joyfully, correcting their mistakes with the authority of someone who had personally survived worse. Brann stood at the edge of them with her arms crossed, ferociously pretending not to sing, singing anyway. Ovid had found the wine.

I looked down at my hands. Ink-stained, scarred at the palm where the old rite had asked its price and the new one had asked only trust. Hands that had hidden in pockets for fifteen years, wrapped around charms for the faithless, trembling at every knock.

They were steady now. They were home.

The sun came fully up. The choir woke, all of it, everywhere, the whole singing country of Cael Sorrow greeting the day — and far away, past the hills, past the strait, past the thin places reopening one careful fold at a time, I thought I heard, very faintly, another world beginning to hum along.

There was so much left to draw. Thank every saint there ever was: there was so much left to draw.

I picked up my pen.
