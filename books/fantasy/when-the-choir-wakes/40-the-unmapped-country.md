## 40. The Unmapped Country

The map took a year to finish, and then it wasn't finished at all, and that turned out to be the whole point.

I should explain. I'm Wren — Wren Almeida-Sorrow, if you're being formal, which nobody in Corvenne ever is, because formality died somewhere around the time the Cantor started crying in the throne room and the king let him. I was eleven when my father folded himself into a stranger's shop in another world and died with the word *finish it* in his mouth. I was twelve when I learned why. I am thirteen now, and I have opinions about maps.

Here is the first one: maps are liars. Not on purpose. They lie the way portraits lie, by pretending a thing can be caught and held still. My father knew that better than anyone, and he made them anyway, because a good lie about where you are is still a kind of home.

Here is the second opinion: Silas Almeida makes the only honest maps in two worlds, and they do it by leaving holes in them.

* * *

I found the hole on a Tuesday, which is a day that exists now mostly because Ovid insisted the ferry schedule needed one more slow morning. The Great Hall of Amberglass had been dry for three seasons — Aldous had ordered the waters turned aside, gently, the way you'd move a blanket off someone sleeping — and the archive smelled the way it always had, like old paper and drowned things remembering they were paper. Sunlight came through the new glass ceiling in long amber bars. Somewhere below us, scholars were arguing about whether the binding ritual could be taught to children, and whether it *should* be, and whether either question was any of their business. (It was not. It was mine.)

Silas was at the master table with their sleeves rolled up, ink to the elbows, doing the thing they did when they thought no one was watching: talking to the map. Low, under their breath, the way you'd talk to a horse or a stubborn door.

"You're crowding the coast," they murmured. "Give it room. It'll grow into itself."

"It's a map," I said. "It doesn't grow."

Silas didn't jump. Fifteen years of selling charms to nervous widows teaches you not to jump when a child materializes at your elbow; I think it was the first useful thing they ever learned in Vell, and Brann says so every chance she gets.

"Everything grows," Silas said. "That's the first rule of cartography and the last one too. A map that can't change is just a grave with labels."

I came around the table and looked. And there it was.

The map of Cael Sorrow covered the whole of the great sheet — the Sighing Strait rendered in silver-blue, the Road of Teeth with its little warning marks like filed-down fangs, Corvenne drawn in careful detail down to the resonant bell tower, Low Hum with its crooked chimneys, even the thin place over Vell marked with a small ferry glyph that Brann had personally demanded be drawn flattered. Every realm-walked mile of it, inked in Silas's hand, in the blood-ink formula my father had carried across the fold in his own veins.

Except for one region.

In the northeast, past the last surveyed ridge, where the country climbed toward mountains nobody had names for yet, there was a space. Blank. Not empty-blank — deliberate-blank, the way a held breath is blank. And across it, in small, even letters:

*To be walked together.*

"Why?" I asked.

Silas set down the pen. They do that now, I've noticed. When I ask a real question, they stop working. It took them most of a year to learn it, and Brann had to say something sharp about it twice, but they learned.

"Because we haven't been there."

"So map it anyway. You've mapped places you haven't been your whole life. That's what a cartographer *is.* You told me. Inference, testimony, educated guessing—"

"I did." Silas looked at the blank space the way some people look at old scars. "For fifteen years I drew worlds from other people's descriptions and called it knowledge. Before that—" they stopped, the way they still sometimes stop, at the edge of the confession they made in the dark outside Amberglass, the one about helping write the outlawing, about running from it. "Before that I drew lines around places I'd never stood and laws fell on people I never met. Lines are powerful, Wren. A line on a map tells everyone who comes after: *this is known. This is settled. Nothing here needs your courage.*"

"And this does?"

"This needs everything." They tapped the blank space once, lightly, like knocking on a door. "Every mountain in there, every valley, every river — none of it exists yet as far as any map is concerned. Which means anyone who goes there goes as a discoverer. Not a tourist. Not a tax collector. Not an army following a surveyor's certainty." A small, wry tilt of the mouth. "Blankness is the most defensible border I've ever drawn."

I stared at it. I was twelve and I wanted the map finished the way I'd wanted my father to come home — completely, immediately, and without understanding what finished would actually cost.

"But it's incomplete," I said. "You spent a year. People will see the gap and think you failed."

"People will see the gap," Silas said, "and the ones worth knowing will ask about it. And I'll tell them the truth, which is that the best places are the ones you don't find alone."

* * *

I want to tell you about the year, because the year is the answer to the question I asked, and I didn't know it yet that Tuesday in Amberglass.

After the singing — after the throne room, after the Cantor came apart into a terrified man named Maestro Caelen Voss who had been holding one note against the silence for thirty years because he thought if he stopped, the king would die, and he was right, he'd been right the whole time — after all of that, the realms woke up. That's the part the ballads get wrong, by the way. There are ballads now. Brann has heard four of them and hates all of them, chiefly because in three of them she is described as "rugged" and in the fourth she is described as "comely," and she says both are slander.

The realms waking meant the thin places thinned further, and then opened, and then became doors. Brann runs the ferry between Vell and the Sorrow shore now — a proper ferry, with a schedule and a fare box and a painted sign that says BRANN'S CROSSING, NO REFUNDS, NO WHINING. She goes back and forth like a stitch through cloth, sewing the two worlds together one crossing at a time. She still keeps her rooms in Vell. She says a person needs somewhere to be grumpy in private. But her boat sleeps in Cael Sorrow water, and her best laugh lives here, and I don't think she knows that yet, though everyone else does.

Ovid got his ledger back. That sounds small. It wasn't. The Wardens of Song had confiscated it during the purges — every ferryman's ledger, every record of crossings and debts and favors owed, because the Cantor understood something terrible and true: that a ledger is a memory, and memory is a song you sing to yourself so you stay yourself. Ovid's ledger had his daughter's name in it. Ilse. Ferry fare, one crossing, paid in full, age nine. He showed me once, in the winter, with the fire low. He didn't cry that time. He said, "She paid her fare. Nobody can say she didn't pay her fare." And then he went out and checked his mooring lines in the dark for an hour, which is how Ovid cries.

He runs the new line now — the long route, along the coast, connecting the villages that the old regime had let fall off every map. He calls it a business. He charges almost nothing. Brann teases him about it and he tells her sentiment is bad for margins, and then he ferries a widow across for free and dares her to mention it.

And Aldous sits the throne, and Caelen Voss composes under watch, which is a phrase that sounds sinister and isn't. The watch is Aldous himself, most days, sitting in the composer's gallery with his chin on his fist, listening. They argue. Everyone says so. The king and his former captor, arguing about tempo in front of the whole court, and the court has learned to love it, because the arguments sound exactly like what they are: two men who were fused together for thirty years learning to be separate, and finding that separate doesn't have to mean apart. Last month Caelen wrote a piece called *Duet for One Room.* Aldous conducted it himself. Halfway through, they swapped. Nobody planned it. The music didn't stumble at all.

And Silas —

Silas stayed.

That was the hard part, though you'd never know it to watch them now, elbow-deep in ink, scolding a coastline. Aldous offered them a title in the first week, and Silas nearly walked into the sea rather than accept it. I watched it happen. Brann and I cornered them in the guest wing of the palace, and Brann said, "You've run from every place that ever tried to keep you, and I'm telling you as a ferrywoman: the current only goes one direction eventually," and I said — I was twelve, and furious, and right — "If you leave, take me with you, because I'm not finishing this map alone either."

They stayed. They swore the Cartographer's Oath on the walls of Corvenne at dawn, with the whole city humming below, and their voice cracked on the words *so that no one is unmapped again,* and nobody laughed, because half the city's names had come home from the undercroft vaults and everyone knew exactly what unmapped meant.

But staying, it turns out, is not a thing you do once. It's a thing you do every morning. And every morning, Silas Almeida chooses this world, this hall, this table, this ink. Some mornings it costs them visibly. Most mornings lately, it doesn't cost anything at all, and I think that's what belonging actually is — not the absence of the choice, but the choice getting easier.

* * *

"Show me the blank part," I said, a week after the Tuesday. We were on the wall at dawn, because that's where Silas thinks best, and because the dawn chorus had become a kind of civic religion that neither of us minded attending. Below us, Corvenne was waking into the new song — the one Silas had written with a chorus of rebels and a king and a broken composer and, though Silas denies it, a stolen whistle-song from a girl who'd learned it from her father before the world broke. The harmonies rose up the stones like warmth.

"Which blank part? There are seven."

"That's cheating. There's one big one."

Silas leaned on the parapet. Up close, in this light, you could see the years on them — the gray coming in at the temples, the fine scar across the palm where the first cut of the rite had been made. They kept that scar unhealed-looking on purpose, I think. A reminder of what blood buys.

"My father walked toward those mountains," I said. "Before he came to find you. His journal mentions a pass. He never named it."

Silas was quiet for a while. The song swelled underneath us.

"He left it for you," they said finally. "Did you know that? The map, the errand, all of it. He could have gone to any charm-seller in Vell. He went to the one Realmwright alive, and he sent his daughter after it, because he knew — the way fathers know — that the ending would need more than one pair of hands." They glanced at me sideways. "He was right. It needed yours most of all."

"Don't say things like that before breakfast."

"It's true."

"It's heavy before breakfast."

Silas laughed — really laughed, the unguarded one, the one that took two years to come back — and bumped my shoulder with theirs. "Then here's something lighter. I've decided the blank region gets a name after all. Just not yet. Not until we've walked it."

"*We,*" I repeated.

"Well. I assumed." They suddenly looked uncertain, which on Silas is a rare weather and worth savoring. "Unless you'd rather—"

"I'm coming," I said. "Obviously. I'm coming, and I'm bringing the whistle, and I'm bringing father's journal, and you're bringing the ink, and we're going to find whatever's in those mountains and put it on the map *together*, which means when people ask whose discovery it is, the answer is going to be insufferable."

"The answer," Silas said solemnly, "is going to be *ours.*"

Below us, the choir reached the verse that everyone loved, the one Caelen wrote and Aldous argued into its final shape, the one that goes: *what was silenced, sing; what was sundered, bind anew; what is unknown, walk toward.* The whole city hummed it. The stones hummed it. Somewhere out past the harbor, I'd swear the sea picked it up and carried it toward the strait, toward the thin places, toward Vell, where Brann was probably swearing at a tourist and Ovid was probably waiving a fare, and the song would find them too, because songs that mean it don't respect borders. That was sort of the entire point.

I looked northeast, past the last inked ridge, to the white space waiting there like a held breath.

"When do we leave?" I asked.

Silas smiled, and rolled up the map, and said, "At first light."

It was already first light. It had been, for a year. It felt like it always would be.

We went down the stairs together, into the singing city, toward the country that hadn't been found yet — the one we weren't going to find alone.
