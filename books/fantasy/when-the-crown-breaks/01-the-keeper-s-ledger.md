## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The ledger smelled of tallow and old winters, which was how Lena knew it was nearly full.

She sat at her desk in the keeper's house — a stone room with one window, one door, one chair, and a view of the Hollow Gate that no architect had intended but that she would not have traded for a palace. The gate itself was half a mile up the trail: a seam in the mountain where two cliffs leaned together like conspirators, sealed over with worked stone so old the masons' marks had worn to whispers. Nobody alive remembered who built it. The Ravensworths remembered why, which was better, and worse.

Lena dipped her pen. The ink was cold enough to drag.

*Autumn, third week,* she wrote. *Frost on the north face by midmorning. Goats came down early this year; Tam Weller says his ewes are restless, but Tam Weller says that every autumn and every autumn it means nothing.*

She paused, listening. Wind in the pines, the tick of the stove, nothing else. At the Hollow Gate, *nothing else* was the whole job. Three hundred years of Ravensworths had kept that nothing else intact, and the ledger on her desk held their handwriting going back further than any kingdom she'd sworn nothing to. She was the ninth Lena. Her mother had been the eighth. Somewhere in the back pages, in faded iron-gall brown, her grandmother had recorded a summer so quiet she'd written *nothing stirring* for sixty-one consecutive days, and Lena had once counted them out of boredom and then felt ashamed, as if she'd caught her grandmother being human.

*Nothing stirring,* she wrote, and set down the pen, and did not yet believe it, because believing it was not her job either. Checking it was.

* * *

The ritual took the rest of the morning, and she performed it the way her mother had taught her: alone, unhurried, and talking to the stone.

"Morning," she said to the outer seal, pressing her palm flat against granite cold enough to bite. "Still ugly. Good."

The outer seal was a ring of seven stones set into the cliff face, each carved with a word in a language that predated Aldmere, predated the Thornfell clans, predated probably the mountains themselves if you believed the oldest letters in the archive box under her bed. She touched each stone in order, west to east, and at each one she listened — not with her ears, though her ears were good, but lower down, somewhere behind her ribs, where the Ravensworths had always kept their second sense. Her grandmother's letters called it *the deep ear*. Her mother had just called it *listening*, the way you'd call breathing breathing.

Stone one: silence. Stone two: silence. Stone three—

A flicker. Not sound, not quite. More like the memory of warmth in a stone that had been cold for three centuries. Lena crouched, pressed both hands flat, and waited through forty heartbeats until it resolved into nothing — or into something so small that nothing was the honest word for it. A settling. Frost heave. The mountain shifting its weight the way old men shift in chairs.

"Frost heave," she told the stone, and wrote it in her head for the evening entry, and moved on.

Stones four through seven gave her clean silence, and the inner seal — the true seal, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall that only thawed for her because her blood had asked it to, generations ago, in words nobody spoke anymore — gave her what it always gave her: a stillness so complete it rang. Standing before the inner seal was like standing inside a held breath. Most people couldn't have borne it. Lena had built her whole life in that breath and called it home.

She finished the circuit, marked the seals with a smear of ash-and-oil compound that smelled like a chapel having a bad day, and walked back down through the pines feeling the particular satisfaction she never said aloud: *all quiet, all mine, all well.*

That was when she saw the smoke from Dara Voss's camp, and decided, as she decided most weeks, not to see it.

* * *

The smugglers used the Shepherd's Cut, a goat track that wound around the eastern shoulder of the mountain and let them move goods between Aldmere and the clan holdings without troubling any customs house. Lena could have reported them. There was even a form for it, in theory, buried in the same royal statutes that assigned her a stipend of twelve crowns a year and had done so without increase since her grandmother's time. In practice, the nearest tax assessor was four days' ride away, and Dara Voss paid her tolls in news, and news was the only currency that spent well up here.

So Lena filled her water flask at the stream below the camp, taking slightly too long about it, and Dara appeared out of the pines with the timing of a woman who had grown up learning exactly which footsteps meant business and which meant an excuse.

"Keeper." Dara Voss was thirty-something and weathered like a fence post, with a scar through one eyebrow she updated the story of whenever the audience changed. Today, Lena suspected, it had been acquired fighting a river pirate. Last month it had been a jealous husband. "Come to arrest me?"

"Heavens, no. I'm filling my flask."

"You've been filling your flask for eleven minutes."

"It's a small flask."

Dara grinned and jerked her head toward the fire. "Then you'll want something warm in it. Sit. We've got tea that's only mostly bark."

The camp was six bodies and a string of sure-footed ponies, and they treated Lena with the careful courtesy of people who understood that the quiet woman from the mountain was best left unprovoked. That suited her. Being left unprovoked was, if she were honest, her chief talent. She took the offered tin cup and sat on a rock at the edge of the firelight, present but not *of* the group, the way she'd been present-but-not-of everything her entire life.

"Gossip first or goods?" Dara said, settling across the fire.

"Gossip. I don't need anything you're carrying."

"Pity. I've got Aldmerian wool that would keep you warm through a glacier."

"I have a stove."

"One day, Keeper Ravensworth, I will sell you something, and I will frame the coin."

Lena sipped the tea. It was, as promised, mostly bark, with a ghost of honey. "What's moving?"

"Nothing much. Clan wool south, salt north, a little brandy for the Weller wedding if Tam ever gets around to having one." Dara fed a stick to the fire. "But there's talk out of the valley villages. Shrine-talk."

Lena kept her face still. "Shrines."

"The old ones. You know — the mossy lumps with the worn-out names that grandmothers still leave bread on when nobody's watching. Old Mother Cattrell in Millbrook swears the shrine behind her byre lit up green last month. Green light, midnight, the works. Her grandson says she was drunk. Her grandson also says that, and I quote, 'the bread was gone in the morning, and we don't have foxes.'"

"What do you say?"

"I say old women see things, and I say foxes are cleverer than we give them credit for." Dara looked at her over the cup, and her eyes had gone from wry to watchful, quick as a card turning. "But I also say you just went very still, and you're the stillest person I know, so still is saying something."

Lena made herself shrug. "Old shrines do odd things in autumn. Frost in the stones, light refracting—"

"Keeper." Dara's voice dropped, gentle in a way that was somehow more unnerving than sharp. "I run these roads because I mind my own business professionally. So believe me when I tell you I'm only asking because if something's wrong on this mountain, it'll be wrong on my road next, and I'd rather hear it from you than from a landslide." She paused. "Also, you look like a woman who hasn't spoken to anyone but goats in a fortnight, and I'm feeling charitable."

It was, Lena reflected, deeply irritating to be known. She drank her tea and considered how much truth a smuggler could safely hold, and settled on the amount she always settled on: almost none, delivered dry.

"There are stories attached to this mountain," she said. "Old ones. My family keeps them so nobody else has to. If a shrine lights up, it's weather in the stones, or a trick of the eyes, or a fox with expensive taste. If it happens again—" she met Dara's eyes, "—I'll handle it. That's the whole of my office. Things stir, I settle them. It's dull work. Be grateful."

"And if it's not dull work someday?"

"Then I'll be busy, and you'll be rich, because everyone will be buying wool to wrap their frightened families in." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "Thank you for the tea. It was terrible."

"Come back Thursday. I'll have terrible stew."

"I'll bring the bread," Lena said, and was halfway down the trail before she realized she'd promised to come back, and further along before she realized she hadn't minded.

* * *

Evening found her at the desk again, lamp trimmed low, the ledger open to its last clean page. Outside, the frost was coming down hard and bright, and the stars over the pass stood out like nailheads. She listened with her deep ear out of habit, found the familiar ringing stillness, and relaxed by one degree, which for Lena was a festival.

Her mother had died at the gate fourteen years ago — a fall, a fever, a winter that took her slowly while Lena, nineteen and terrified, learned the rites from a dying woman's instructions. What Lena remembered most wasn't the grief. It was the last lucid afternoon, when her mother had gripped her wrist and said, *You'll be invisible, my girl, and it will feel like a small life, and you must never mistake that for a wasted one.* Lena had built forty years — no, twenty-six years, she corrected herself, she was thirty-five, the winters blurred — she had built twenty-six years on that sentence, and most days the sentence held.

Today it held. She picked up the pen.

*Third week of autumn, continued. Seals checked, all seven outer stones sound; inner seal rings true. Compound renewed. Weather turning hard; expect snow within the fortnight. Smuggler traffic normal, route unchanged, tolls paid in gossip (see: shrine rumors, valley villages — likely frost-light or foxes; will monitor).*

She hesitated, pen hovering. Then, because the ledger deserved honesty even when the world didn't:

*I am content. Let the record show it.*

And beneath that, because three hundred years of Ravensworths had ended every seasonal entry the same way, and because ritual was the spine of a solitary life:

*Nothing stirring.*

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and banked the stove. Through the window, the Hollow Gate stood black against the starfield, patient as it had been for three hundred years, and Lena went to bed in the held breath of the mountain and slept the sleep of a woman whose work was done.

Forty miles downriver, in a shepherd's hut in the village of Millbrook, a dying girl opened her eyes and asked for bread.
