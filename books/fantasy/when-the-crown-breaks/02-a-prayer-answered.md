## 2. A Prayer Answered

The fever had taken three days to climb and four nights to settle in, and by the fifth morning Tam Weller had stopped pretending he wasn't counting his daughter's breaths. He counted them the way he counted sheep — automatically, hopelessly, with the certain knowledge that some would be lost.

Maren was eleven. She'd been strong as a foal all her life, the kind of child who raced the dogs down the scree and came home bleeding and laughing. Now she lay on the truckle bed by the hearth looking like something already half-given back to the earth: skin gone grey-yellow, lips cracked, a sweat that soaked the linens and smelled wrong, sweetish, like fruit rotting in a barrel. Old Mother Quill had come up from the village two days ago, looked at the girl, looked at Tam, and said nothing at all, which from Mother Quill was a sermon.

"There's a tincture," she'd said finally. "Bitter enough to turn milk. It buys time. That's all it does."

"How much time?"

"Enough to say what needs saying."

So Tam said it. He sat by the bed through the nights and told Maren stories she'd heard a hundred times — the one about the ram that butted the tax collector into the creek, the one about her mother, who had died birthing her and whom Tam described so often now that he sometimes caught himself inventing details just to have something new. He told her about the spring lambs coming, how he'd save her the first one, the runt of the lot, because she always loved the runts best.

She didn't open her eyes. Her breath went shallow, then shallower, then there were gaps between the breaths long enough for Tam's heart to fall through.

It was somewhere past midnight when he gave up on everything else. He wasn't a praying man. His father hadn't been, nor his father before him; the Weller family faith had been worn away generations ago like a coin passed through too many hands, until what remained was a habit of muttering at the sky when the weather turned foul. The chapel in Hollowmere village kept a priest out of politeness more than piety, and everyone knew the old gods were gone — gone or dead or never real at all, depending on which grandfather you asked. Three hundred years since anyone had burned an offering. Three hundred years since the names stopped being spoken, and the names themselves had gone with them, worn off the world like lettering off a gravestone.

But Tam stood at his daughter's bedside in the small hours, holding her hand that weighed nothing, and he did the only thing left that a man can do when he has nothing left. He prayed. Not to anything in particular. To whatever might be listening.

"Take me instead," he said. "I'm used up anyway. Take the old stock and let the young grow." And then, because it felt absurd to bargain without a name to bargain with, he added the only holy word he knew, the one carved over the chapel door where nobody read it anymore, worn nearly smooth:

"Hollow One. If you're there. If you ever were."

The fire settled. The wind outside the stone croft changed direction, or seemed to. And Maren breathed — a full breath, deep, the kind she hadn't managed in three days — and then another, and then she coughed, a great racking cough, and turned her head, and opened her eyes.

They were clear. That was the thing Tam noticed first and could not stop noticing after: her eyes were clear, no fever-glaze, no dullness, just Maren looking at him with the flat irritation of a child woken early.

"Papa," she said. "You're crying on my blanket."

He laughed. It came out of him like something breaking. He gathered her up, this girl who weighed nothing yesterday and suddenly weighed herself again, warm and solid and complaining, and he held her until she squirmed free, and then he held her some more, and she let him, which she hadn't done since she was six.

By dawn the sweat had dried. By noon she was asking for food — actual food, bread and cheese and the last of the honey — and eating it with an appetite that made Tam weep a second time, privately, into the water bucket. Mother Quill arrived, felt the girl's brow, listened to her chest, and stood there for a long moment with her lips pressed thin.

"What did you do?" she asked.

"Nothing. I—" Tam stopped. He thought about the midnight, the wind changing, the name worn smooth on the chapel door. "I prayed," he said, and felt foolish saying it aloud in daylight.

Mother Quill looked at him for a long time. She was older than the village well and most of its opinions, and she had a way of looking at you that made you feel she was reading a ledger with your name in it.

"To who?" she said.

"I don't know. The old — the Hollow One. Whatever's left."

The old woman crossed herself, which was the new fashion, and then, after a pause, touched the doorframe with two fingers, which was the old one. She didn't say anything else. But she walked down to the village faster than Tam had ever seen her walk, and by evening the story was everywhere, growing in the telling: the Weller girl raised from her deathbed, the shepherd's prayer answered, the god in the mountain awake and listening.

Tam didn't think much about any of that. He was busy watching his daughter sleep — really sleep, easy and deep, color in her cheeks — and telling himself the fever had broken on its own, that prayers were just words, that the world didn't work that way anymore.

But three nights later, when Maren was well enough to sit up and spin wool and complain about the boredom of convalescence, Tam found himself standing at the edge of the high pasture at dusk, looking up at Thornfell, at the black shoulder of the mountain where the pass narrowed to a cut of darkness no shepherd drove sheep. There was a place up there the old people had named and then stopped naming. Everyone in the valley knew where it was and agreed, without ever discussing it, not to look at it directly.

The mountain looked back at him, patient as stone.

And Tam Weller, whose daughter should be dead, took off his cap to it — just briefly, just a dip of the head, the way you acknowledge a landlord — and went inside, and barred the door, and told himself the shiver down his spine was only the cold.

* * *

Lena Ravensworth climbed to the shrine two days after the story reached her, because the story reached her fast — the valley was a bowl and gossip was water, it pooled everywhere and seeped through everything, and even a keeper who spoke to perhaps six people a season heard things eventually. A smuggler had mentioned it, actually, tossing the news across the counter of the waystation like a coin he didn't want: "Your mountain's working miracles, Keeper. Shepherd's girl in Hollowmere. Dead and back. They're saying the Hollow One's woken up."

"They're saying wrong," Lena had said, and made change for his brandy, and smiled the smile she kept for exactly such moments, and then walked straight back to the gate and checked every seal twice.

Now she stood in the shrine itself, high on the eastern flank of Thornfell, in a hollow of stone that most maps marked as nothing at all. It was a small place, low-ceilinged, formed where two slabs of mountain rock leaned together and someone, long ago, had smoothed the floor and cut a channel for offerings. The channel was dry. It had been dry for three hundred years, because the Ravensworths had seen to it — not by destroying the shrines, which would have been noticed, but by letting them fill with leaves and silence, by letting the names erode, by doing the slow patient work of making a god forgettable.

Lena crouched and put her palm flat against the offering channel.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm — the shrine faced north, and the shadow of the peak lay across it, and the air in the hollow was cold enough to fog her breath. Warm from underneath. Warm the way a stone is warm when something vast and slow has been lying against the other side of it for a very long time, and has recently shifted.

"Oh no," she said quietly, to no one, in the empty shrine. "No, no, no."

She'd known, of course. She'd known the moment the smuggler said the word miracle, the way you know a storm is coming from the ache in an old break. But knowing a thing in the abstract and kneeling in the wet leaves with your hand on warm stone are different countries, and the road between them is short and ugly.

Because here was the arithmetic her family had carried for eleven generations, scratched into ledgers and drilled into daughters: the Hollow Gate did not merely seal a door. It sealed a pressure. Behind the gate was the gods' country, whole and alive and pressing against the border the way floodwater presses against a dam, and the seals — her seals, the Ravensworth seals, renewed each season in blood and salt and words she was sworn never to write down — held that pressure back. When the seals were sound, the old gods slept their long sleep behind the stone, unreachable, unanswering. Prayers went nowhere. That was the point. That had always been the point.

An answered prayer meant a leak.

A leak meant the pressure was finding cracks, seeping through in threads and trickles, pooling in the low places — in shrines, in dreams, in the ears of desperate men at midnight. And a leak, her grandmother's letters said, in handwriting that grew shakier toward the end, is how it begins. Not with the gate breaking. Gates don't break. Gates erode. First a trickle answers a prayer, then a stream heals a dying child, then a river finds a king asleep, and by the time anyone thinks to check the dam, the dam is a memory and the valley is underwater.

Lena sat back on her heels and made herself look at the shrine properly, the way she'd been trained to look, the way her mother had trained her before the mountain took her mother, and her grandmother before that.

The moss on the north wall had died. All of it, in a neat oval, as if something beneath the stone had exhaled. In the center of the oval, faint as breath on glass, was a mark. She scraped the moss residue away with her thumbnail and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

It was a sigil. She knew it. Every Ravensworth knew it, though none of them had ever been taught why. It was the sign her family chalked on their own doors at midwinter, the sign pressed into the wax of the gate-seals, the sign that meant, in a language older than Aldmere, older than the kingdom, older than writing: *bound here*. The mark of the prisoner.

Except this sigil wasn't the binding-mark. It was the other one. The mirror of it. The one that appeared in exactly one place in all her family's records, drawn once, in her grandmother's tightest and most frightened hand, with a single line beneath it:

*If this sign appears anywhere in the mortal country, the vessel has begun to call. Come to me at once. Do not wait for the season. Do not pack.*

*— R.*

Lena sat in the cold shrine for a long time, listening to the mountain breathe under her knees.

The vessel. She knew that word too, the way you know a word from a nightmare — perfectly, without context. It surfaced in the oldest ledger, the first one, the one written in a hand so fierce it tore the page: *the crown is the vessel, the crown is the cage, let no Ravensworth forget what sits upon the throne and why.* Eleven generations of keepers had read that line and understood it the way children understand thunder: something to do with the king, something dangerous, something not our business. The crown was the crown. Kings wore it. The Ravensworths kept the gate. The two facts lived in separate rooms and were never introduced.

But the sigil on the shrine wall didn't care about separate rooms.

She copied it into her ledger — the keeper's ledger, the plain black book that recorded weather and seal-condition and the comings and goings of smugglers, the book whose final entry for the autumn season still read *nothing stirring* in her own neat hand, written, she now realized with a cold twist in her gut, roughly around the hour a shepherd knelt by his daughter's bed and called a dead god by name. Beneath the sigil she wrote the date, the location, the temperature of the stone. Then she sat with her pen hovering, wanting to write *impossible*, and wrote instead, because her training was better than her fear:

*Answered prayer confirmed. Shrine active. Sign of calling present. Pressure finding paths. Seal integrity: unknown. Unknown is the problem. I have been checking the gate and not the mountain. I have been checking the lock and not the walls.*

She closed the ledger and stayed crouched a moment longer, and it was there, in the dead-moss smell and the warm stone, that the dread finally found its true shape. Not *the gate is failing* — gates failed, that was the job, that was what the seasons of renewal were for. The true shape was worse and quieter: *it isn't pushing at the gate at all. It's going around. It learned.* Three hundred years of patience, and somewhere in that patience the prisoner had stopped throwing itself against the door and started looking for windows. A prayer here. A shepherd's desperation there. A thread of warmth through a mile of granite. Small leaks, deniable leaks, the kind a lone keeper checking one gate on a schedule could miss forever.

Her family's oldest failure, her grandmother had called it — not the binding itself, but the arrogance of it. *We built a cage and assumed the prisoner would stay stupid,* the letter said. *Nothing stays stupid forever. Not even gods. Especially gods.*

Lena stood. Her knees ached. Outside the shrine the afternoon light was going long and gold across the valley, and down in Hollowmere she could see smoke rising from the Wellers' croft, ordinary smoke, hearth-smoke, and she thought about the girl who should be dead sitting up in it, spinning wool, alive because something under the mountain had heard a tired man bargaining and decided to spend a little of itself on kindness.

That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and would sit there for weeks: it had been kind. The leak was kind. Whatever came through was spending its strength on healing children, and she was going to have to be the woman who sealed the crack and let the next dying child die, because the alternative was written in her grandmother's shaking hand and ended with a king.

She slung her pack and started down, taking the keeper's path, the hidden one, moving through the bracken the way her family had moved for eleven generations — unseen, unthanked, on time.

Halfway down she stopped. Below her, at the treeline, a figure was climbing up toward the shrine: a villager, by the clothes, walking slowly, carrying something bundled in cloth. Offerings. The first offerings in three hundred years, and tomorrow there would be more, and the day after, and every candle lit and every loaf laid on warm stone would widen the crack by a hair, and she couldn't guard them all, couldn't stand watch over every shrine on every flank of a mountain range, alone, the way she had always been alone, the way the job required—

The thought snagged on something. *Alone. The way the job requires.* She looked at the figure below her, and at the vast dark shoulder of Thornfell above, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the smuggler's coin-tossed gossip: one keeper, forty miles of border, a patient enemy, and a valley full of people already praying.

She needed help. The realization landed like a diagnosis. And there was exactly one person in the mountains who knew every path, every cave, every smuggler's cut through the stone — a person she'd spent seven years pretending not to see, buying brandy from with a polite frozen smile.

Lena Ravensworth stood on the mountainside in the failing light, dreading the conversation ahead more than she dreaded the god.

Then she turned downhill, toward the waystation, toward Dara Voss, and walked fast before she could change her mind.
