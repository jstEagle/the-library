## 3. What the Smugglers Saw

The Gilded Goat sat where the Thornfell road bent toward the lowlands, close enough to the border stones that its patrons kept one eye on the door and the other on their purses. Lena had been coming here for eleven years, always on the same pretext — a keeper's errand, provisions for the mountain hut, nothing worth remembering — and in all that time she had never once given her true name.

Tonight she gave it.

Not aloud. Not yet. But she counted it as she pushed through the door into smoke and lamplight and the smell of fried onions, counted it like a coin she was about to spend, because what she'd seen at the shepherd's shrine had emptied her of every other currency she had.

*Answered prayers.* Three hundred years of silence, and then a dead girl breathing in her father's arms, and a shrine older than any living memory burning with light that had no business existing this side of the Hollow Gate.

Lena needed help. The thought arrived like a stone dropped down a well — she heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom.

She found Dara Voss in the corner booth she always took, back to the wall, boots up on the bench opposite, dealing cards to two men who were losing with the particular patience of people who knew better than to stop. Dara was small and quick-boned, with a face built for forgetting — brown hair going iron at the temples, eyes the color of wet slate — and a voice that carried exactly as far as she wanted it to.

"Keeper." She didn't look up from the cards. "You're standing in my light."

"You don't need light to cheat."

"Cheating is an art form. It requires *illumination*." She flicked a card at the man across from her. "Fold, Tam. I'm begging you. Save your daughter's dowry." Tam folded. His companion lasted two more hands and his dignity, and then the booth was theirs, and Dara finally looked up, and something in her face shifted — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The kind merchants give to customers who pay in coin instead of questions.

"You look terrible," Dara said. "Even by your standards, which is saying something, because your standards are a cave."

"I need information."

"You need a bath and a meal and possibly a priest. But sure. Information first." She nudged the bench opposite with her boot. "Sit. You're making my regulars nervous. They think you're here about the tolls."

"Aren't you?"

"The tolls are paid. In full. In advance. In *coin*, which I know you prefer to corpses." Dara swept the cards together and produced, from somewhere in her coat, a bottle of amber liquor and two cups that had probably been clean within the last month. "So. What has the mountain's favorite ghost so rattled she's buying drinks in public?"

Lena sat. "Who says I'm buying?"

"You walked past the bar. You never walk past the bar. You stand at the bar like a fence post and drink exactly one cup and leave. Tonight you came straight to me." Dara poured two measures without asking. "That's a woman with a purse open and a problem closed around her throat. Drink."

Lena drank. It was better than she expected, which meant it was worse than Dara claimed it was, which meant Dara had stolen it from someone important. "There was a healing," she said. "In Lowmere. A girl — fevered, dying. Her father prayed at the old shrine on the fell. The one nobody's tended since my grandmother's time."

"And?"

"And she woke up whole. No fever. No scar. The fever had been in her lungs, Dara. I've seen lung-fever take strong men in a day."

Dara turned her cup slowly on the table, once, twice. When she spoke again, the performance had drained out of her voice, leaving something flatter and more careful underneath. "I heard that story yesterday. Thought it was village nonsense. They love a miracle in Lowmere. Gives them something to talk about besides sheep."

"It wasn't nonsense. I saw the shrine. The votive stone was lit."

"Lit how? Candles?"

"Candles go out, Dara. This didn't. And there were prayers carved into it that I have only ever seen in one place." Lena kept her voice level. "On the gate itself."

For a long moment the tavern noise filled the silence between them — dice, argument, a fiddle tuning badly in the corner. Then Dara leaned forward, elbows on the table, and said, quietly, "How much for the whole story?"

"Nothing. If you tell me what you saw in the high pass."

Something flickered behind Dara's eyes — surprise, quickly filed away. "Who told you I saw anything?"

"No one. That's why I'm asking. Your crew came down the west road four days early, bought winter stores you won't need until the thaw, and your helmsman — the big one, Orla — hasn't stopped looking north since. Smugglers run from soldiers and tax men. You're running from weather, or you're running from something else." Lena turned her cup the way Dara had turned hers. "And last night, from the ridge above my hut, I watched lights move along the Widow's Stair. Where nothing lives. Where nothing *can* live. So either you saw them too, or you've gotten very religious very fast, and I've known you eleven years, Dara Voss. You'd sell your own shadow before you'd pray to anything."

Dara laughed — a short, genuine bark that turned heads at two tables — and then killed it just as fast. "Gods, I've missed you. Do you know how exhausting it is, trading with people who *talk*?" She refilled both cups. "All right. Keeper's privilege. But this goes nowhere, because if my crew hears I told the ghost of Thornfell our business, they'll mutiny, and I'll deserve it."

"I keep secrets for a living."

"That's what worries me. People who keep secrets for a living are always looking for new ones to fill the shelf." Dara drank, set the cup down, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, pitched under the fiddle. "We were three days up the pass. Running wool over the Stair — quiet work, easy work, we've done it thirty times. Second night, camped at the Hollow Shelf, just under the snowline. Orla wakes me near midnight. Says there's a light on the ridge above us."

"There's no trail on that ridge."

"There's no *anything* on that ridge. That's the point. Rockfall country. The old maps mark it cursed, and the old maps are usually wrong about everything except the parts that get you killed." Dara's fingers had gone still on the table. "But there it was. A light. Moving. Not torchlight — torchlight jumps, gutters, throws shadows. This was steady. White, with a kind of gold in the middle of it, like a coal wrapped in silk. It moved along the ridge the way a person walks. Paused where a person would pause. Looked down at us — I'd swear it on whatever you like — and then went on, north, toward the gate, and up over the shoulder of the mountain where even goats don't go."

"How long?"

"Half an hour. Maybe more. We broke camp in the dark and ran the whole way down, and I'm not ashamed to say it, because Orla's been on these roads since she was twelve and she was crying." Dara met Lena's eyes. "Here's the part I haven't told anyone. There wasn't one light. There were three. Two walking, and one behind them, higher up, keeping pace. Like a procession."

Lena's hands had gone cold around her cup. *Procession.* The word belonged to the rites, to the old liturgy her grandmother's letters used, to worship conducted along the high ways of the gods' country. Worship did not happen on this side of the gate. Worship had been *forbidden* on this side of the gate, by blood and seal and three hundred years of Ravensworth vigilance.

"When did the shrine light up?" Dara asked. "Exactly."

"Four nights ago."

"We saw the lights five nights ago." Dara let that sit. "Whatever's walking your mountain, Keeper, it got there before the miracles started. The healing isn't the beginning. It's the *spill*. Something upstream is overflowing."

Lena stared at her. Eleven years of trading pleasantries and contraband tolls, and she had never once suspected that Dara Voss thought in watersheds.

* * *

They talked until the fiddler gave up and the tavern thinned to drunks and diehards. Dara laid out her knowledge the way she laid out cards — quick, precise, every piece face-up but held loosely, ready to snatch back if the price changed. Lights on the Widow's Stair. A week earlier, strange tides in the mountain streams, running clear when they should have run white with melt-silt. A hunter who swore he'd heard singing above the snowline and come down vowing never to go up again; everyone had laughed at him, and now nobody was laughing.

"And you?" Dara said, eventually. "What does the great ledger say? Because I know you keep one. Everyone knows. Half the reason the tolls get paid on time is that nobody wants to find out what you write down."

Lena hesitated. It was an old reflex, older than the tolls, older than the tavern — the reflex of a woman whose entire life was a sealed door. But the shrine stone had burned in her mind for four nights, and the lights had walked a ridge where nothing should walk, and she was one person with one pair of eyes and a mountain that had begun, very quietly, to move.

"My family built the gate," she said. "Or — sealed it. Centuries ago. Before Aldmere was Aldmere. There's a god on the other side of it, or something the old books call a god, and the seals exist to keep it there. My line has kept those seals for thirteen generations. Alone. On purpose." She turned the cup. "If prayers are being answered on this side of the gate, then the gate is leaking. And if the gate is leaking, then either the seals are failing—"

"—or somebody opened them," Dara finished. She was quiet a moment. "Thirteen generations alone. That's not a job, that's a sentence."

"It's a duty."

"It's a cell with a view." Dara said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Then she shrugged, brisk again, transactional. "Right. Here's my offer, and I'll only make it once, so listen. I know every road on this mountain — the honest ones, the dishonest ones, and the ones that don't officially exist. You know the gate and whatever book-learning comes with it. You can't watch the whole of Thornfell by yourself. I can't fight whatever's up there with knives and charm. But together—" she spread her hands, "—we might at least see it coming."

"Why?" Lena asked. "Truly. What's your price?"

Dara considered her for a moment, and for once didn't smile. "My crew saw something on that ridge that unmade thirty years of knowing what the world is. I don't sleep well. I'd like to sleep well again. That means finding out what it was, and either killing it or getting paid enough to move to the coast." She raised her cup. "Also, you're the only conversation I've enjoyed in a decade, which is pathetic for both of us, but there it is."

It was, Lena realized, the closest thing to friendship anyone had offered her since her mother died, and it was dressed up as a business deal, and she found — to her mild horror — that she wanted to say yes.

"One condition," she said. "Nobody learns what we're doing. Not your crew, not the villagers, not the king's men if any come asking."

"Paranoid."

"Careful."

"Same thing with worse posture." Dara drank. "Done. We leave tomorrow, up the east road, before dawn. Wear something that isn't gray. You look like a headstone."

* * *

Lena was halfway home through the cold dark, lantern swinging, breath steaming, when she saw the rider on the switchback below her hut — a courier's cloak, a king's badge catching the moonlight, a satchel heavy with wax-sealed paper.

She stood very still among the pines and watched him hammer on her door, call her title to the empty mountainside — *Keeper! Keeper Ravensworth!* — and wait, stamping, in the cold.

No one had called her by that name aloud in eleven years. No one from the capital had climbed this road in her entire tenure. The crown did not remember the Hollow Gate existed. The crown had *paid good money* to forget.

The rider knocked again. Somewhere far above, past the snowline, past the Widow's Stair, the mountain held its breath — and Lena, with a smuggler's bargain warm in her pocket and dread rising in her throat like floodwater, stepped out of the trees to answer a summons she already knew she could not refuse.
