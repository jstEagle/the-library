## 4. The King Who Speaks in Two Voices

The summons came on a Tuesday, riding up the mountain in the saddlebag of a courier who complained the whole way about the cold.

Lena read it twice by the light of the gate-lamp, standing at her post with the wind pulling at her hood. The parchment was heavy stock, sealed in green wax with the crowned stag of Aldmere. *His Majesty King Aldric III requests the presence of the Keeper of the Hollow Gate at court, to render report upon disturbances of a mountainous nature. Travel expenses provided. An escort will await at Thornfell village.*

*Disturbances of a mountainous nature.* She almost laughed. Somewhere in the capital, a clerk had earned his wage smoothing her whole world into bureaucratic language — the answered prayers, the burning shrines, the lights Dara Voss had seen walking the high pass where nothing should live. All of it filed under *disturbances*, like a rockslide or a wolf problem.

But beneath the clerk's careful phrasing was the thing that made her go cold: the summons was signed not by a minister but by the king himself. And kings did not write to gatekeepers. Kings did not know gatekeepers existed. That was rather the point of gatekeepers.

She looked up at the Hollow Gate behind her — the seam of black stone in the mountain's face, older than any kingdom, older than the word *kingdom* — and felt the familiar weight of her office settle onto her shoulders like a coat she'd outgrown.

"Nothing stirring," she told the ledger that night, and hated how the words sounded now. Like a lie she'd been telling so long it had worn smooth.

She left the gate in the care of the rites themselves — the seals were woven into the stone, not into her attendance; her family had made sure of that centuries ago, though no one remembered why anymore — and rode down through three days of rain to find the king's escort waiting exactly where the letter promised, which frightened her more than anything else. Someone in Aldmere knew the mountain's roads as well as she did.

The capital took two weeks to reach and smelled like a hundred thousand people deciding what to do with their sewage. Lena had been there once before, at nineteen, summoned by a previous administration to confirm that yes, the seals were intact, no, there was nothing to worry about, and could someone please show her the way back to the mountains. She had hated every minute of it. She hated it again now, but differently — because now she knew what leaked when gates leaked, and this city was downstream of everything.

They gave her a room in the guest wing of the palace, which was warmer than anywhere she'd ever slept and twice as uncomfortable. A servant brought her a bath. Another brought her clothes that fit, which meant someone had described her to a tailor, which meant someone had been watching her longer than the summons suggested.

At dusk, a chamberlain with a voice like oiled hinges informed her that His Majesty would receive her after supper.

"Informal audience," he said. "The King prefers... candor, in matters concerning the northern provinces."

"The northern provinces," Lena repeated.

"The mountains," the chamberlain said, with the faintest flicker of distaste, as if the mountains were a personal failing of hers.

* * *

The king received her in a small room, not a throne room — a study lined with maps, lit by a fire that crackled too evenly, as if it had been rehearsed. And for a moment, seeing him across the room, Lena thought: *he looks tired.* That was all. A man in his forties with grey coming in early at the temples, dressed simply, standing by the window with a cup of wine he wasn't drinking.

"Keeper Ravensworth," he said, and his voice was ordinary. Warm, even. "Thank you for coming. I know you don't leave your post lightly."

"No one asks me to," Lena said. "It seemed rude to refuse the first invitation in my life."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "Sit, please. Wine?"

"I'd rather stand."

"So would I, honestly." He set the cup down. "Then let's be quick, because I suspect neither of us enjoys this."

He asked her questions, and they were good questions — better than she expected. Not *are there disturbances* but *when did the shrines relight, in what order, moving toward the gate or away from it?* He knew about the shepherd's daughter. He knew about the lights in the high pass. He knew things he should not have known unless someone was reporting from inside the valley itself, and Lena filed that away with a cold, professional anger she'd examine later.

And then a knock came at the door, and a young courtier leaned in — golden-haired, silk-shouldered, the kind of beautiful that takes effort — and said, "Your Majesty, forgive the interruption. Lord Cassel insists the grain tariffs cannot wait until morning."

"Tell Cassel the tariffs will keep," the king said, in his own warm, tired voice. "I'm speaking with—"

He stopped.

Lena saw it happen. Saw the stillness come over him like frost crossing water, starting at the eyes and working outward. For three heartbeats he did not move at all, and the fire crackled its rehearsed crackle, and the courtier waited with the patience of a man who had seen his king pause before.

When the king spoke again, it was still the king's mouth. Still the king's voice, mostly.

"Tell Lord Cassel," he said, "that the tariffs are the least of what cannot wait."

But underneath the words — underneath, the way bedrock sits under soil — there was a second voice. Lena felt it more than heard it, the way she felt the seals through the soles of her boots: something vast and slow and patient, a depth answering in the same breath as the surface. It didn't shout. It didn't need to. It spoke the way the mountain speaks, which is to say continuously, whether or not anyone is listening.

The courtier bowed and withdrew, closing the door softly, having noticed nothing. Or having noticed everything and decided, wisely, to notice nothing.

The frost receded from the king's eyes. He looked at Lena, and his face was his own again, and he was afraid. She could see it plainly now — had perhaps been seeing it since she walked in and misread it as tiredness. It was the fear of a man who has been shouting underwater for a long time.

"You heard it," he said quietly.

"Your Majesty—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Please. I've spent four months listening to myself say things I don't remember deciding to say. You're the first person in this palace who looked at me when it happened instead of away. So don't give me the courtesy voice. Tell me what wears the crown."

Lena stood very still. Every instinct she owned said *report nothing, reveal nothing, get back to the mountain.* But the instinct had also said *nothing stirring,* hours before something stirred, and she was done trusting it.

"It's not supposed to be possible," she said. "The crown is a vessel. It was made to hold something. If it's holding something and that something is speaking through you—"

"Then the vessel is leaking."

"Or full past its capacity. Or—" She stopped. "Your Majesty, do you know what's in it?"

He crossed to the window. Below, the capital glittered, ten thousand lamps, a hundred thousand lives, none of them aware their king was a door with something knocking on the other side.

"I know it's old," he said. "I know it's patient. I know it talks to me at night, when the crown is heaviest, and it doesn't threaten. That's the worst part, Keeper. It never threatens. It *commiserates.* It tells me how lonely the throne is, how heavy, how no one has ever truly spoken to me — and it says these things in a voice like my mother's, if my mother had been a cathedral." He turned. "It offers to help. It has been offering to help since the day I was crowned. And some nights, God forgive me, I want to let it."

The fire crackled. Lena's hands were steady, because her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that wanted to shake.

"What does it want?" she asked.

"To go home," the king said simply. "That's what it claims. It says it was bound here against its will, by people who feared it, and that it only wants the door opened." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen yet, because it was a kind smile, an honest one, his. "It sounds reasonable, Keeper. That's what I keep coming back to. Everything it says sounds reasonable. Am I a king being worn like a coat, or am I a jailer who's finally listening to the prisoner's side of the story? I genuinely no longer know."

*Neither do I,* Lena thought, and said nothing.

* * *

Aldric watched the keeper leave, and the moment the door closed behind her, he sat down on the floor.

Not in a chair. On the floor, beside the fire, with his back against the leg of the map table, because sitting on floors was something Aldric did and kings did not, and this was one of the small territories he defended. They could have his voice for sentences at a time. They could have his hands, sometimes, when he woke with ink on his fingers and no memory of writing. They could not have the floor.

*I know you heard her,* he thought, deliberately, the way you'd speak into a well.

*She is interesting,* the other said. It didn't use his skull's voice for thoughts between them; it used something deeper, pressure without sound. *She has stone in her. Old stone.*

"She's a person."

*Everything is a person, at this scale. You are a person. I am being very polite about it.*

"You're wearing me."

*I am sharing you. There is a difference, and the difference is consent, and we will return to the subject of consent presently.* A pause, vast and unhurried. *You fought me today. In front of the courtier. You gave the tariff answer twice.*

"I did."

*It cost you. I felt it. You are stronger than your father was. Your father lasted eleven days before he stopped fighting altogether and simply became a pleasant room I lived in.* The pressure shifted, and Aldric recognized the tone even through the strangeness — it was admiration, or the shape admiration leaves when it passes through something that hasn't felt it in three hundred years. *I respect that you fight. I want you to understand that. When this is over, whatever remains of you will know that you fought well.*

"When what's over?"

*When the door opens.*

"I won't open it."

*You already are,* the other said gently. *Every day you sit the throne, the crown drinks a little deeper. You are not the lock, Aldric. You are the key turning. Slowly. Beautifully. I could not have designed a better king, and believe me, I helped design several.*

Aldric pressed his palms against the cold floorboards and counted his breaths, the way his old nurse had taught him, the way he'd taught himself again in the dark months. Four in. Six out. The panic was a wave; waves passed.

There was one thing the other didn't know. One thing he'd found three weeks ago, at three in the morning, in a burst of clarity that might have been his own last free act or might have been bait — he couldn't tell anymore, and that uncertainty was its own torture. He'd written it in his private cipher, in the margin of a book of orchard almanacs where no one would ever look, and he'd hidden the book in the one place in the palace the other never went, because the other found grief boring.

*You're quiet,* he observed.

*I am patient. It is my defining virtue. Ask anyone who bound me.*

He got up off the floor, because the king was expected at supper, and kings did not sit on floors. He washed his face. He put the crown on — it had grown heavier again, the way it always did after he resisted, a jeweled hand tightening by degrees — and he looked at himself in the mirror and practiced the smile.

In the glass, for half a second, his reflection smiled back a fraction late.

*Good,* said the thing beneath his voice, warm as a cathedral. *Let's go meet your court.*

Down in the guest wing, in a narrow room that smelled of unfamiliar soap, Lena Ravensworth sat awake with her grandmother's name turning over in her mind like a stone in a stream. *Ravensworth.* The king had said it like a question. The chamberlain had flinched at it, just slightly, when he announced her.

Someone in this palace knew what her blood had done.

She decided, lying in the dark, that tomorrow she would find the archive.
