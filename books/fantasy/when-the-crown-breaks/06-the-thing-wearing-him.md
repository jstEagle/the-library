## 6. The Thing Wearing Him

The king's private study smelled of ink and cold iron.

Lena had been shown up three flights of stairs by a servant who would not meet her eyes, past guards who did not ask her name — they already knew it, which was its own kind of warning. Now she stood on a rug older than the kingdom, watching Aldric of Aldmere pour wine with hands that trembled only slightly, and told herself the trembling was a man's exhaustion and nothing more.

"Keeper Ravensworth." He set the second cup before her without asking if she wanted it. "You've come a long way from your mountain."

"You asked me to."

"I did." He sat across from her, and for a moment he was only a tired man in his forties with grey coming in early at the temples. "Your report was thin. *Nothing stirring.* Three words about the most important door in my kingdom."

"The gate has been quiet for six years. Before that, quiet for sixty. My family kept records going back—"

"I know what your family kept." He drank. Set the cup down too hard. "I know exactly what your family kept, Lena. That's why you're here."

Something moved behind his eyes when he said her name. Not a flicker. A settling, like water finding its level.

She kept her voice level. "Then perhaps Your Majesty will tell me why prayers are being answered in villages that haven't prayed in three hundred years. Why shrines that were stone yesterday are warm today. Why shepherds' daughters wake up healed with no mark on them at all."

"Would you believe me if I said I don't know?"

"No."

"Good." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for one heartbeat his face went slack — not peaceful, but *vacant*, like a house with the furniture still in it and no one home. Then he came back, and his smile was apologetic and human and wrong. "I admire that about you Ravensworths. You never learned how to be lied to politely."

"Your Majesty—"

"There is something in the crown, Keeper." He said it fast, like a man diving into cold water. "There has been since the night they put it on me at my coronation. It doesn't speak often. When it does, I hear it from the inside, like a voice through a wall. And lately—" his hand closed around the armrest until the wood creaked "—lately the wall is getting thinner."

Lena's mouth had gone dry. She made herself pick up the wine and drink, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. "You're telling me you're possessed."

"I'm telling you I'm *occupied*. There's a difference. Possession means it drives. Occupation means it waits." His eyes found hers, and this time what looked out of them was patient as bedrock. "It has gotten very good at waiting."

* * *

Aldric felt her fear the way he'd learned to feel weather — pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper rain. He hated that he could feel it. He hated more that part of him, the part that was still just a king with a headache and a kingdom, wanted to comfort her, and the other part let him, because comfort was a lever.

*Don't,* he thought at the thing beneath his thoughts. *Not her.*

*Not yet,* it agreed, and he couldn't tell whose caution that was.

He had fought it the first year. He remembered that much clearly — the nights spent biting through leather straps so he wouldn't answer questions in a voice that wasn't his, the letters he wrote and burned, the priest he dismissed before the man could look too closely at how the king prayed. He had fought it the way men fight drowning, and he had lost the way men lose drowning: gradually, then completely, then in small daily surrenders that didn't feel like surrender because he was still walking, still ruling, still signing his own name.

But his name was a cage too now. He understood that. The crown held the god, and the god wore the king, and somewhere underneath both of them there was still an Aldric who counted his councilors' faces each morning to make sure they were all still his.

Two of them weren't anymore. He'd stopped being able to tell which two.

So he did what drowning men do. He left messages. Small ones, deniable ones, in the margins of documents only a Keeper would think to read. *The seals weaken from the inside.* *Ask the archive for Ravensworth letters, 3rd shelf, false backs.* He had written those himself, fighting through fog thick as wool, and he did not know if they were his own idea or bait the god had let him take.

That was the worst of it. Not the voice. The not-knowing which thoughts were his.

Now the Keeper sat across from him, sharp-eyed and frightened and trying so hard not to show it, and the god rose in him like a tide deciding to come in, and Aldric braced against the back of his own skull and *watched*.

*Let me speak to her,* the god said. Not in words. In certainty.

*You'll frighten her.*

*I will interest her. Fear comes later, and fear is useful.*

And Aldric, who had learned that resistance cost him days he could not afford, did the arithmetic every prisoner eventually does, and stepped back from the front of his own mind, and let the tide come in.

* * *

Lena saw the change happen and had no word for it except *depth*.

The king had been sitting maybe four feet away. Now he seemed further, the way a well seems deeper than it is wide. When he spoke again, his lips moved correctly, his voice was his voice, but the cadence had changed — slower, spaced out, each word placed like a stone in a stream.

"You have guarded the gate your whole life, Lena Ravensworth."

"Yes."

"And your mother before you. And hers." He turned the wine cup slowly, not drinking. "Do you know what they were guarding? Truly? Not the story. The truth under the story."

"A prison," Lena said. "For a god."

"Yes." Something like pleasure moved through the room, a warmth with no source. "Say my name."

"I don't know your name."

"Vael." He said it gently, the way you'd say the name of someone you loved. "They took my name and made it a lock. They took my longing and made it a chain. I offered your ancestors peace, little keeper. I offered to leave the mortal world entirely, to close the way myself, to unmake my own hunger. And they heard *god* and thought *trap*, and they built the crown instead, and they bound me into gold because gold endures and gods do not."

The fire cracked. Lena realized she had risen halfway out of her chair without deciding to.

"You're wearing their king."

"I am *living* in him, as I lived in nothing for three hundred years." Vael-Aldric looked up, and the king's eyes were wet. That undid her worse than anything — that the god could weep with a borrowed face and mean it. "Do you know what it is to be conscious in the dark? Not sleeping. Conscious. For centuries. To feel prayers arrive like light through a crack and be unable to move toward even one of them? A child dying in a shepherd's hut called my name because her father read it off old stone, and I *heard* her, and I could do nothing, nothing, nothing — until the wall thinned enough to push a little healing through. One miracle. My first act in three hundred years, and it cost me a decade of stored strength, and your ledger would call it *something stirring*."

Lena's throat hurt. She sat back down because her legs told her to.

"You want the gate opened," she said.

"I want the gate *opened*, yes. But hear what I am not saying." He set down the cup and folded the king's hands, an oddly humble gesture. "I am not asking to walk into your world. I know what I am. I know what my kind became when we walked freely among you — your oldest stories remember, even if your priests burned the versions with endings. I am asking for passage. Home. The country behind the gate is mine as much as theirs. Let me return to it, and I will seal the way behind me better than any Ravensworth ever could, because I will be on the *other side* of the lock."

"And the king?"

The pause was one heartbeat too long.

"The king has been generous," Vael said. "The king will be released."

"And I should trust that because—"

"Because I am offering you a bargain, Keeper, and bargains can be inspected." He leaned forward, and the warmth in the room gathered toward him like moths. "Open the gate willingly. Walk me to the threshold yourself. Do this, and I will give you anything within my power. Ask, and it is yours."

"Anything."

"Anything."

And here was the trap of it, the terrible soft center of it: Lena had spent her whole life wanting almost nothing, because wanting was dangerous when you lived alone above a door at the top of the world. But the wanting was there anyway, packed down under thirty years of duty like seed under snow, and Vael's voice found it the way water finds a crack.

*A life where someone knows your name,* something in her whispered. *A mother's voice. A table with two chairs used.*

"I could give you her," Vael said quietly, and Lena's blood went to ice, because she hadn't said it aloud. "Memories are only doors, little keeper. I have been locked behind doors a long time. I know how they open."

"My mother is dead."

"Death is a wall. I have lived behind walls." The king's face was gentle, so gentle. "I am not offering you a trick. I am offering you the truth: that you have been alone since you were nine years old, and you have called it duty because the alternative was calling it what it is."

"*Stop.*"

"You guard a door no one thanks you for. You trade kindness with smugglers and pretend it's commerce. You write *nothing stirring* in a book no one reads, because if anyone read it, they might ask why you chose a life with no one in it—"

"I said *stop*." Her voice cracked on the last word, and the silence after it was enormous.

Vael regarded her with the king's tired eyes, and for a moment — she would swear this later, in the dark, hating herself — for a moment he looked *lonely*. Not monstrous. Lonely the way she was lonely, vast and patient and utterly without company.

"I have been alone longer than your family has existed," he said softly. "I only wished for us not to be."

* * *

Inside, Aldric screamed.

He had heard all of it — the bargain, the offer, the god reaching for the woman's grief with hands made of sympathy — and he threw himself against the walls of his own mind like a man shoulder-charging a door. *Leave her alone. LEAVE HER ALONE.*

*Why?* The god's attention barely flickered. *Because you care whether she says yes?*

*Because she'll say yes, you bastard, she's alone and grieving and you're offering her the dead — that's not a bargain, that's a knife—*

*It is both,* Vael said, with something like regret. *Most true things are.*

And then Aldric felt it — the god's grip loosening, not from mercy but from focus, all that vast patience narrowing onto the Keeper like a lens onto dry grass. This was the moment. He knew it in his bones. If the woman said yes, the crown would drink her consent and the wall between worlds would come down like a dam, and whatever Vael truly intended — passage home or a kingdom of flesh — would begin with her hand on the door.

He could not stop it from the inside. He had tried for twelve years.

But he could make noise.

Aldric gathered everything that was still his — the memory of his daughter's laugh, the weight of his dead wife's hand, the stubborn animal fact of his own name — and he slammed it all against the inside of his skull, and the king's body shuddered once, visibly, on the chair.

The god paused. *What are you doing?*

*Being loud.*

The cup on the table rattled. Aldric's right hand spasmed, dragged itself across the wood, and knocked the wine over — deliberately, clumsily, a drunkard's motion — and red spread across the report Lena had brought, bleeding through *nothing stirring* until the words drowned.

The Keeper flinched back. The spell — whatever web of warmth and loneliness the god had spun around the room — tore a little. She looked at the ruined page, then at the king, and Aldric poured everything he had left into his eyes, into the muscles of his face, into one raw silent scream:

*Run. Don't answer him. RUN.*

* * *

Lena stood in the doorway of the king's study with her heart slamming and the corridor cold at her back, and she made herself say the words plainly, because plainness was armor.

"No."

Vael tilted the king's head. "No?"

"I don't open gates for voices in the dark. I don't make bargains with things that wear people. And I especially don't make them with something that offers me my dead mother like a coin." Her voice shook and she let it. "Whatever you were before they caged you — whatever they took from you, however long you waited — the answer is no. Find another door."

"There is no other door."

"Then wait," Lena said, "longer."

She walked out. Her legs held. Barely.

Behind her, faint through the closing door, she heard the king's voice — his real voice, ragged, human, half a sob and half a laugh:

"*Well answered, Keeper.*"

* * *

She made it four steps down the corridor before she had to put her hand on the wall.

The trouble was not fear. Fear she knew how to carry; she'd carried it up and down the Thornfell passes since childhood, the way other women carried market baskets. The trouble was that the god had been *right*. Every word of it. She had chosen a life with no one in it, and she had dressed the choosing in duty, and some traitor part of her had leaned toward that warm voice like a plant toward a window — not because she believed it, but because it was the first thing in thirty years that had looked at her loneliness and called it by its name.

*Alone since you were nine years old.*

Her grandmother's letters were in the satchel under her cloak, and suddenly she needed them — needed the archive, the third shelf, the false backs, needed facts and ink and the dry safety of history before the warmth of that voice followed her out into the hall and settled into her bones.

She was halfway to the archive stairs when she passed a window overlooking the palace courtyard, and stopped.

Below, torches. Horses being saddled — not two, not ten, but forty, soldiers moving with the unhurried purpose of men following standing orders. A captain checked a list. On it, she was almost certain, though the angle was bad and the light worse, she read a single line before the page turned:

*The Keeper of the Hollow Gate — detain and convey to the crown.*

Convey. To the crown. Permanently.

Lena stepped back from the window into shadow, and her mind, trained by decades of ledgers, laid the entries side by side and drew the only line that connected them: the king had summoned her here, and the thing wearing the king could not afford to let the one person who knew how to break bindings walk back out of the capital.

Unless.

Unless the summons itself had been the drowning man's message. Unless Aldric had called her here knowing what would happen, betting his last free hours that a Keeper would rather run than rot.

Either way, the arithmetic was simple. She had perhaps an hour, less if the captain's list included the guest wing.

She needed horses, a pass, and a guide who knew every road the soldiers did — and there was exactly one person in this city who fit, and she owed her a great deal of money and a greater apology.

Lena pulled her hood up and headed for the docks, moving like smoke, like her mother's daughter, like a woman who had just discovered that invisibility had a price and the bill had finally come due.
