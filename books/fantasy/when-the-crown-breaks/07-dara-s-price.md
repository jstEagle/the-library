## 7. Dara's Price

Dara Voss poured the tea like she was pouring an insult.

"Keeper's blend," she said, sliding the chipped cup across the table of her cabin above the river bend. "Pine needles and regret. Drink it before I change my mind about the whole conversation."

Lena drank it. It tasted exactly as advertised.

The smuggler's den sat tucked into a fold of the lower Thornfell foothills, half boathouse, half counting room, all of it smelling of tar and wet wool and the particular sweetness of goods that had never met a tax collector's ledger. Three of Dara's crew played cards in the corner with the studied indifference of people who had been told to listen without looking like they were listening.

"You want to know what we saw in the high pass," Dara said. She dropped into the chair opposite, boots up on a crate stamped with a wine merchant's seal that had nothing to do with wine. "That's not information, Keeper. That's a confession. Anything moving up there that isn't you means I've been running cargo under something's nose for years, and I don't love being reminded I'm not the scariest thing on my own mountain."

"You already told me there were lights."

"I told you there were lights." Dara turned her cup slowly on its saucer, a nervous habit she'd deny under torture. "I didn't tell you they were walking. Lights don't walk, Lena. Lanterns swing. Torches bob. These moved like someone carrying them had all the time in the world and no interest whatsoever in being seen by anyone who mattered." Her eyes came up, sharp and gray as slate. "And then last week, one stopped. Right at the edge of the tree line. And it looked down at the valley. At us. For the better part of an hour."

"What did you do?"

"What any sensible woman does when something ancient stares at her village." Dara shrugged. "I raised my cup to it. Figured if it was going to eat me, it could do it while I was having a drink."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "You're impossible."

"I'm alive. The two overlap more than people think." Dara set the cup down and leaned forward, and the humor drained out of her face the way water drains from a cracked bowl — slow, then all at once. "Now here's what I actually came here to say, since you're too proud to say it first. You've been climbing that mountain alone for eleven years. Checking your seals, writing your little ledger, pretending you're the only soul who cares whether the world stays stitched together. And now something up there is *awake*, and the king's men are asking questions in every tavern from here to the capital road, and you're still planning to do this alone."

"I'm not alone. I have you selling me information at extortionate rates."

"That's business." Dara waved a hand. "I mean the other thing. The walking-into-the-high-pass-in-winter thing. You're going to do it. I can see it sitting behind your eyes like a stone behind ice. So let's skip to the part where I tell you my price."

Lena set down the cup. "Name it."

"Take me with you."

The cards in the corner went quiet. One of the crew — Ferro, the big one with the burned jaw — looked over and then very carefully looked away again.

"Dara—"

"No, hear it before you refuse it, because you were going to refuse it, I watched you start." Dara stood and paced to the window, where the river ran silver-gray toward the valley. "I know these passes better than the goats do. Every goat path, every collapsed mine shaft, every smuggler's cut the king's maps have never heard of. You need three days to reach the high pass by the pilgrim road. I can get you there in one and a half, and the king's soldiers will still be arguing about which side of the mountain to climb. That's worth something. That's worth a great deal, if the stories out of the capital are even half true."

"What stories?"

Dara turned. "That the king has recalled his Warden-Captains. That there's a company of forty riding north under sealed orders. That their orders concern the Keeper of the Hollow Gate specifically." She let that sit. "That they're not to bring back a report, Lena. They're to bring back *you*. Permanently. Whatever that word means when a crown uses it."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed at the river.

"They can't," Lena said, and heard how thin it sounded. "The Keeper serves the crown. It's in the founding charters. My line swore—"

"Your line swore a lot of things three hundred years ago. Kings break oaths faster than they make them." Dara crossed back and crouched in front of Lena's chair, close enough that Lena could see the fine scar through her left eyebrow, the one she'd never explained and Lena had never asked about. "Something answered a prayer in Shepherd Bram's field. Something is walking in your pass. And now soldiers are coming for the one person who understands what either of those things means. You don't put those pieces together and stay calm. Nobody could. So be scared with me for a minute, and then let's talk routes."

Lena looked at her hands. They were steady. Eleven years of ritual had trained steadiness into them the way water trains stone. But underneath, in the place where she kept the things she didn't write in the ledger, something was moving — the same cold current she'd felt at the shepherd's shrine, at the king's court, in the archive among her grandmother's letters.

*They are not bringing you home. They are bringing you to the crown.*

"The high pass," Lena said quietly. "By the smuggler's cut."

Dara grinned, and it transformed her whole face, ten years younger and twice as dangerous. "Knew you'd see reason."

"My price is that you turn back at the snowline. I go to the gate alone. That's not negotiable. It's not stubbornness, it's—" She searched for a word that wasn't *atonement*, wasn't *inheritance*, wasn't *the only thing I know how to be*. "It's the office."

"Fine," Dara said instantly.

Lena blinked. "Fine?"

"Fine. Snowline. Alone. I'll pretend to believe you'll hold to it, you pretend to believe you will, and we'll both enjoy the theater." Dara stuck out her hand. "We leave at first light. Pack for cold. And bring that ledger — if we're going to die on my mountain, I want a written record so I can haunt you properly."

Lena took the hand. It was warm, calloused, certain.

She hadn't shaken hands with anyone in four years.

* * *

They left in the blue hour before dawn, when the river fog lay thick enough to hide a company of soldiers, which was rather the point. Dara led, moving through the mist with the unbothered economy of a woman walking her own kitchen, and Lena followed with her pack, her rites-book, and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her spine.

The first day was all lower slopes: alder scrub, scree, the abandoned charcoal camps that Dara read like other people read letters. *"Ferro camped here in autumn. Lazy fool, banked his fire wrong."* *"This trail's been used since the thaw — heavy boots, marching order."* She read the mountain the way Lena read seals, and Lena found herself watching, cataloguing, admiring despite herself.

"You stare like a customs officer," Dara said over her shoulder.

"You notice like a criminal."

"Best compliment I've had all year." She paused at a fork where both paths looked identical — gray stone, gray lichen, gray sky — and chose right without breaking stride. "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the cabin. You've got a question. You keep opening your mouth and closing it like a fish. Ask."

Lena considered lying. It would have been easy; she was out of practice at conversation but well-practiced at silence. Instead: "Why? Genuinely. You run cargo. You avoid attention the way I do. Every instinct you have says hide, and instead you're walking a wanted woman toward the most watched mountain in Aldmere. What do you get?"

Dara walked on for twenty paces before answering. Long enough that Lena thought she wouldn't.

"When I was fourteen," Dara said, "my father's boat went down off the Teeth in a storm that every fisherman in the delta said couldn't be sailed. He sailed it anyway because the harbor master owed him money and the harbor master said no debt survives a drowned man. Everyone told him to wait. He didn't wait, because waiting meant trusting other people to do their part, and he'd been burned enough times that he trusted arithmetic instead." She stepped over a fallen birch, held a branch aside for Lena without looking. "He was right about the storm. He was wrong about everything else. The harbor master paid his debts to the widow, mind. Very honorable. Sent flowers."

"Dara—"

"So I learned young: being right alone is just a slower way of drowning." She glanced back, and her expression was lighter than her words. "You're the most alone person I've ever met, Keeper, and I once spent a winter with a hermit who argued with his own beard. You're alone like it's a discipline. Like you're training for something. And I keep thinking — training for what? Who's going to be there at the end of all that practice?" She faced forward again. "So that's what I get. I get to find out what happens when somebody stops practicing."

Lena had no answer. She put her head down and climbed, and the mountain rose around them, and somewhere below, faint as a memory, she thought she felt the ground hum — or imagined it, or feared it.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a hollow beneath an overhang, sheltered from the wind that came off the high snowfields in long white breaths. Dara built a fire small enough to hide in the rocks and cooked trout she'd pulled from a stream with a speed that suggested witchcraft. They ate. The stars came out hard and close, the way they only did at altitude.

"Tell me about the god," Dara said.

Lena fed a twig to the flames. "What makes you think there's a god?"

"Because I've seen bandits, wolves, avalanches, and one very memorable bear, and none of them made lights that *stare*. Because the shrine in Bram's field burned cold, not hot — Ferro touched it and swears his fingers went numb for a day. And because you, who have lied to tax collectors and priests and me with equal fluency, went pale as milk when I mentioned the king's soldiers." Dara hugged her knees, firelight catching the planes of her face. "So. The god. Start wherever it doesn't hurt too much, work your way in."

So Lena started with the gate. The Hollow Gate, the seam in the world her family had guarded since before Aldmere had kings — the door between the mortal country and the country of the old gods, sealed shut by Ravensworth hands. She told Dara about the seals older than any kingdom, the rituals half rite and half habit, the ledger with its seasons of *nothing stirring*. She told her about the shepherd's daughter waking healed. About the king answering a courtier twice, once in his own voice and once in something vast and patient underneath, like a lake speaking through a man's mouth.

"And in the archive," Lena said, "I found my grandmother's letters. There was a god named Vael. Three hundred years ago it came to the border and asked — bargained — for passage into flesh. A body. A life. And the Ravensworths of that day gave it a prison instead. They bound it into the crown itself. The king's crown, Dara. The throne of Aldmere has been a cage for three centuries, and every coronation has been a new lock, and nobody — not the priests, not the archivists, not the kings themselves — knew." She stared into the fire. "Except my grandmother. And she died knowing, and passed the knowing down like a disease, and here I am."

Dara was quiet for a long moment. "And the soldiers coming for you. That's the cage defending itself."

"That's the cage defending itself."

"Huh." Dara poked the fire. "My father would have liked you. Terrible combination — right and doomed."

Lena laughed. It startled her; the sound came out rusty, half-forgotten, like a door opening in a house she'd thought empty. Dara looked pleased, insufferably so.

"Your turn," Lena said. "Fair's fair. The eyebrow scar."

"Ah." Dara touched it reflexively. "Boring story. Smuggler dispute, knife, I won, mostly."

"Not the scar. The other thing. Why you live up here instead of the coast. You're a river-and-sea woman; I can tell by how you walk on rock, like it's insulting you personally. So why the mountains?"

Dara was silent long enough that the fire had to be fed again. When she spoke, her voice had lost its performance entirely, and Lena understood she was hearing the real one for the first time.

"I had a partner. Years ago. Not crew — partner. We ran the delta together, and she was better at it than me, funnier too, and one winter she took a route I begged her not to take because the ice was wrong and I could feel it was wrong, and she laughed at me and went anyway, because I'd been cautious one time too many and she wanted to prove a point." Dara's jaw worked. "The ice was right where I said. She went through near Miller's Bend. They found her pack downstream in spring. Just the pack."

"Dara—"

"So when I saw those lights staring down at my valley, and then heard the king's men were asking after a woman who knows why — I did the arithmetic." She looked at Lena across the fire, and her eyes were bright and hard and honest. "Every path I've taken alone since Miller's Bend has been a way of not standing next to anybody. And I'm tired of it. So that's my price, Keeper. Not coin. Not favors. I guide you through my passes, and in exchange I stop doing the arithmetic alone. That's the whole deal. That's everything."

The wind moved through the high pines. Below them, far away, the valley villages showed as scatterings of ember-light, small and human and unaware.

Lena found that her throat had gone tight. Eleven years of solitude, and she had built a whole architecture around it — duty, inheritance, atonement, the office — and this smuggler with her chipped teacups and her dead partner had walked through it in two days flat, simply by refusing to leave.

"I don't know how to do this," Lena said finally. "Have — company. I check seals. I write in a book. I'm very good at being unseen."

"Yeah, I noticed. You apologize when you take up space. It's unbearable." Dara tossed her a second trout, wrapped in leaves. "Eat. Unlearn it slowly. We've got a day and a half."

They sat together as the fire burned down, shoulders almost touching, and Lena discovered that silence shared was a different substance entirely from silence kept. It had warmth in it. It had weight and shape, like a stone you could hold.

She was nearly asleep when Dara spoke again, low, not really a question:

"Lena. Your seals. If something's leaking through — if the gate's cracking — how long before it's not a leak anymore?"

Lena opened her eyes. High above them, past the black teeth of the ridgeline, the snowfields caught the starlight — and for a heartbeat, no longer, one of the high peaks seemed to glow from within, a pale pulse beneath the ice, like a heart beating under skin.

Then it was gone, and she might have dreamed it, except Dara had gone rigid beside her, staring at the same spot.

"I don't know," Lena said. "But I think we're about to find out."

The mountain hummed again — unmistakable this time, deep and patient, a sound felt in the teeth rather than heard — and far to the south, down in the dark of the valley, a shrine light flared gold against the night.
