## 9. Soldiers at the Pass

The snowline came at dusk, a white seam drawn across the mountain like the gods had taken a knife to it.

Lena felt the soldiers before she saw them — a wrongness in the quiet, the particular silence of men trying not to make noise. Twenty years of watching the Hollow Gate had taught her every sound the Thornfell made: the tick of freezing stone, the wind combing through the pines, the small industrious lives of things that lived above the snow. None of that was happening now.

"Dara," she breathed.

"I know." Dara Voss had gone still in the way of her kind — not frozen but *coiled*, one hand drifting to the knife at her hip as if greeting an old friend. Her eyes moved along the ridgeline, reading it like a ledger. "Six. Maybe eight. Above us, on the shelf rock. They've been tracking us since the ford."

"Since the ford." Lena's stomach dropped. "That's two days."

"That's two days of me being very charming and you being very oblivious, so yes." Dara's grin was thin and didn't reach her eyes. "They let us get ahead of them because they wanted the high ground. Soldiers always want the high ground. It's the first thing they teach and the last thing they learn to stop doing."

Below them, the valley was going blue with evening. Somewhere down there were the shrines, still glowing faintly since the earthquake, little wounds of light in the dark hills. Somewhere beyond that was the capital, and a king walking north at the head of an army, wearing his own body like an ill-fitting coat.

And somewhere up here, between Lena and the gate she had spent her whole life guarding, were six or eight men with orders to bring the Keeper to the crown permanently.

She had read those orders herself, three nights ago, in a courier's satchel Dara had lifted off a dead messenger without breaking stride. *Detain the Ravensworth woman. By any means necessary. The King's will.* The seal on the letter had been Aldric's own — pressed deep, slightly crooked, the way he always pressed it when his hand wasn't entirely his own.

"They won't kill me," Lena said. "They need me alive."

"They need you *delivered*," Dara corrected. "There's a difference, and it usually shows up around the ribs." She turned, and for once there was nothing wry in her face at all. "There's a road. A real one, not this goat track. My father cut it when I was nine, running brandy past the toll-keepers. It goes over the shoulder of the peak instead of through the pass. Steep, narrow, and no soldier who wasn't born on this mountain would find it in the dark."

"Then we take it."

"It's not wide enough for two abreast. And there's a stretch—" Dara stopped. Lena watched her decide whether to lie, and watched her choose not to, and something about that choice lodged under Lena's ribs like a splinter. "There's a stretch where you climb with your back to open air. If they catch us on it, we're done."

"Then we don't get caught on it."

Dara looked at her for a long moment. Snow had started falling, fat slow flakes that caught the last grey light. "You know," she said, "most people who hire me lie to themselves quieter than that."

"I'm not hiring you anymore."

"No," Dara agreed softly. "You're not."

* * *

They almost made it.

The shelf road was exactly what Dara had promised — a ledge hacked out of living rock, barely a hand's width wider than a boot, angling up across the mountain's shoulder where the wind came off the ice fields hard enough to lean against. Lena went first because Dara insisted, and because Dara had spent two days teaching her the smuggler's walk: weight low, steps short, never trust a stone until you've insulted it.

Lena had been climbing these mountains since before she could write. But she had climbed them as a keeper — by the safe paths, the pilgrim paths, the paths her mother had walked and her grandmother before her. This was different. This was a path built by people who had needed the mountain to keep their secrets, and it hummed under her boots with a lawlessness she found, shamefully, exhilarating.

Which is why she heard the shout too late.

"*Keeper!*"

The voice rolled down from the rocks above — a sergeant's voice, trained to carry. "Lena Ravensworth! In the name of King Aldric, halt!"

She didn't halt. Of course she didn't halt. She heard the scramble of boots on scree above, saw the first crossbowman swing out onto the ledge thirty yards back the way they'd come, and did the arithmetic every keeper learns young: distance, footing, time.

What she didn't calculate — what she had never learned to calculate, because no one had ever stood between her and harm before — was Dara.

The bolt came fast and flat in the bad light. Lena saw it the way you see things in nightmares, clearly and uselessly, already arrived. And then Dara was there, which made no sense, because Dara had been behind her, and Dara was moving — stepping *into* the arrow's line, shoulder turning, one arm sweeping Lena toward the rock face —

The sound it made was small. That was the worst part. All of Dara's noise and swagger and terrible laughter, and the end of it was a sound like a fist hitting wet cloth.

They went down together. The ledge took Dara's knees; the wall took Lena's palms; the world tilted and steadied. Dara slid half a foot before Lena caught her collar and hauled her back from the edge, and then they were crouched against the stone with the wind screaming past and Dara's breath coming in short, shocked gasps against Lena's neck.

"Bolt's in my side," Dara said, conversational, as if reporting weather. "Don't look. You'll make a face."

"You absolute *idiot*—"

"Language, Keeper." A laugh that ended in a cough. When Lena pulled her hand away from Dara's ribs it came back black in the twilight. "Still with me. Mostly. Go."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're *carrying* me, actually, if you can manage it, which will be humiliating for us both." Dara's teeth were gritted, her eyes bright and furious with pain. "There's a hollow. Fifty feet up, past the bend. Old cache site. My father's. Get me there and you can pull this thing out properly instead of snapping it off like a coward."

Above them, boots on stone. Voices. The sergeant shouting something about the ledge, about the drop, about taking them alive.

Lena got Dara's arm over her shoulders and stood.

* * *

Fifty feet had never been so far.

Dara was tall and solidly built, all rope muscle and stubbornness, and even with most of her weight carried on the good side she dragged at Lena like an anchor. The ledge narrowed. The wind found every seam in their clothing. Twice Dara's boots skidded and twice Lena's grip was the only thing between her friend and the long dark fall into the valley, and both times Dara laughed — a thin, wheezing, awful laugh — and said something like "*Told you it was humiliating*" or "*If you drop me, haunt someone else*."

Lena didn't answer. She was counting steps. It was easier than thinking.

Because here was the thing she could not stop thinking, the thought that kept surfacing like a drowned body: *she stepped in front of me.*

No one had ever done that. No one had ever had the chance. Lena Ravensworth had arranged her entire life so that no one would ever be close enough to bleed for her — invisible, unthanked, a shadow at a door — and she had told herself for twenty years that this was duty, this was discipline, this was the price of vigilance. That her invisibility protected people. That a keeper with no attachments was a keeper with no weaknesses.

And in one evening on a ledge in the snow, a smuggler with a crooked grin had proven the whole architecture of her life to be a beautiful, useless lie. Invisibility hadn't protected Dara. Invisibility had just meant that when the blade finally came, it came for someone standing where Lena should have been standing.

They rounded the bend. There it was — a fissure in the rock face, chest-high, screened by a curtain of frost-stiffened scrub. Dara tapped the stone twice with her knuckles, some old smuggler's knock, and Lena hauled her through.

Inside was a space barely big enough for two bodies, dry, floored with old sacking gone stiff with age. Shelves cut into the wall held the ghosts of caches: empty bottles, a rusted lantern, a tin of tinder gone soft with damp. Someone had scratched names into the rock by the entrance, dozens of them, generations of them. Dara's father's mark was there, and beside it, small and jagged, a child's attempt at *DV*.

Lena laid her down on the sacking and lit the lantern with hands that shook only after the flame caught.

"All right," she said. "Show me."

* * *

The bolt had gone in below the last rib, angled up and back. Not the heart. Not the gut. Dara, who knew wounds the way Lena knew seals, pronounced it "annoyingly survivable" while sweat stood on her upper lip and her jokes came slower and slower, like a clock winding down.

Lena had stitched goats, once, badly, as a girl. She had set a broken finger on herself at nineteen. Nothing had prepared her for putting her knee on a friend's shoulder and drawing steel out of her, and nothing would have made it bearable except that Dara talked her through it — steady, profane, precise — until the bolt came free with a sound Lena would hear for the rest of her life, and blood welled, and she packed it and bound it and stitched it with thread boiled in the last of their wine.

Dara passed out twice during the stitching. Both times she woke up mid-sentence, finishing whatever joke she'd been telling, which was somehow worse than screaming.

When it was done, Lena sat back against the rock and discovered she was crying.

"Oh, don't," Dara mumbled, eyes closed, grey-faced, insufferable even at death's door. "You'll fog the lantern."

"Why?" Lena's voice cracked on it. "Why did you step in front? I'm the one they want. It was my arrow. It should have—"

"Don't finish that sentence, it's terrible craftsmanship." Dara opened one eye. The lantern light made her look younger, and more tired, and more honest than Lena had ever seen her. "You want the truth? The ugly truth?"

"Yes."

"I've run cargo for twenty years. Brandy, silk, secrets, once a live bear, don't ask. Every job, every road, every deal — it was transactional. Somebody pays, somebody carries, everybody walks away richer and alone. That was the whole shape of my life and I told myself I liked it fine." She took a careful breath, testing the stitches. "Then a grim little woman in a grey cloak buys me drinks and asks about lights in the high pass, and looks at me like I'm the first person she's spoken to in a year. And I thought — oh no. Oh, *no*. I know that look. I've *been* that look."

"Dara—"

"So when the arrow came, I didn't think. That's the truth of it. Twenty years of thinking and I didn't think at all. My legs decided before I did." Her mouth twisted, half smile, half grimace. "Turns out underneath all the transactional warmth, I apparently care whether you live. Deeply inconvenient. I'd like to file a complaint."

Lena laughed, and the laugh broke into something else, and she put her forehead against her knees and let it come — quietly, the way she did everything, twenty years of practice at feeling things where no one could see.

Invisibility protected no one. It had never protected anyone. It had just meant that the people who chose her — a smuggler who shared her fire, a king who wrote warnings in his own shaking hand, a god whose loneliness sounded like hers — had to stand unprotected in front of everything aimed at her.

That was over. Whatever came next, that was over.

"Listen to me," Lena said, when she could speak again. She wiped her face and looked at Dara, and made herself say the rest of it plainly, because Dara deserved plainness. "I have spent my whole life being no one, and I told myself it was holy. It wasn't. It was fear with better posture. You bled tonight because I let myself believe that staying unseen was the same as keeping people safe. So here's my vow, and I mean it the way my family means vows: I am done hiding. We go up the mountain, we reach the gate, and I end this — the crown, the god, all of it. And when it's done, I'm going to stop being a door and start being a person, and you're going to help me, because you're terrible at letting me do otherwise."

Dara stared at her. Then, weakly: "Was that a speech? Did I get stabbed for a speech?"

"Yes."

"Good speech," she admitted. "Bit long. Seven out of ten." Her eyes were closing. "Wake me when the soldiers give up. Or don't. Either way."

Within minutes she was asleep — truly asleep, the deep graceless sleep of the genuinely exhausted — one hand curled near the knife she hadn't let go of even while Lena stitched her.

Lena sat watch at the fissure's mouth, the lantern shuttered to a slit, and listened to the mountain.

The soldiers worked the ledge below for an hour, calling to each other, probing. Then the cold did what cold does to discipline, and their voices drew back toward the pass, toward a fire Lena couldn't see. They'd regroup. They'd report. And somewhere south of here, a king in a crown that was eating him alive would learn that his men had lost the Keeper on a smuggler's road, and the thing wearing him would grow impatient, and impatient things make mistakes.

Above her, past the shoulder of the peak, the Hollow Gate waited under its new wound — the crack in the outer seal, the shrines burning in the valley like signal fires. Two days' climb, maybe less, if she pushed.

She looked at Dara's sleeping face, at the blood soaking through the bandage, and understood with terrible clarity that she couldn't push. Dara couldn't be carried up a mountain with a torn side. She needed warmth, rest, days — none of which they had.

Unless.

Lena remembered something then, half-buried under twenty years of rites and ledgers: her mother's voice, telling her about the old ways, the pre-Ravensworth ways. *Before we were wardens, we were guests,* her mother used to say. *The mountain has rooms the gods kept for travelers. There's one below the snowline, east-facing, where the stone remembers kindness.*

A cave-shrine. A place where the border between worlds wore thin — where, if the stories were true, a sleeper might be watched over by something vast and patient.

Something imprisoned.

Lena looked at her friend, and at the dark, and made her choice.

"Dara," she whispered, though the other woman couldn't hear her. "I hope you're right about the roads. Because I know a shortcut, and it runs straight through the enemy's house."

She banked the lantern, settled the sacking around Dara's shoulders, and began planning how to move a wounded smuggler up a haunted mountain before dawn.
