## 11. What Duty Costs

The cave-shrine was older than the kingdom, and it smelled like it.

Lena had found it by accident on the second night of their climb — a hollow in the mountainside where the stone curved inward like a cupped hand, its walls carved with figures whose faces had worn smooth centuries ago. Offerings still littered the alcoves at the back: clay cups gone to dust, a child's wooden bird, coins from mints that no longer existed. Whoever had worshipped here had stopped a long time ago. The mountain hadn't noticed.

She'd built the fire small, tucked behind a fall of rock so its light wouldn't leak down the slope. Dara lay wrapped in both their bedrolls near the flames, and she was burning too — not with fever's heat exactly, but with something that moved under her skin like weather.

"You're doing the thing again," Dara said.

Lena looked up from the poultice she was grinding. "What thing."

"The thinking thing. Where you go somewhere inside your head and lock the door." Dara's eyes were half-open, glassy in the firelight. Her side was bandaged three layers deep, and every breath cost her something she was trying not to show. "I've watched you do it for two weeks straight. It's like watching someone stand in front of a door they own but refuse to enter."

"You have a fever."

"I have a fever *and* eyes." Dara shifted, winced, kept talking anyway — she always kept talking anyway; Lena was beginning to understand that silence physically pained her. "Ask me what I saw in the high pass."

"You told me. Lights."

"I told you lights. I didn't tell you what kind." Dara stared at the ceiling of the shrine, at the smooth-faced carvings above them. "They moved like people walking. But slow. Slow like honey off a spoon. And there were more of them every night, and they all walked toward one place, and you know what place that was, Keeper. You knew before I said it."

"The gate."

"The gate." Dara laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent her double. When it passed she lay back, pale, and her voice came out quieter. "You know what I thought, the first time I saw them? I thought, *someone should tell the keeper.* And then I thought — who even is the keeper? Does anyone know her name? Does she have a house? A family? A favorite drink?" She turned her head, and her eyes found Lena's with the unnerving directness of the very ill. "Why do you do it?"

"Do what."

"*This.*" Dara gestured weakly at the cave, the bandages, the whole bleeding length of their flight. "Guard a door nobody thanked you for. In a country that forgot the door existed. You've spent your whole life up there alone, haven't you? Alone enough that buying drinks for a smuggler counts as an evening out." She smiled, but there was no edge in it. "Tell me why. I'm feverish. I'll forget anything embarrassing by morning."

Lena went back to grinding the poultice. The mortar scraped against stone, a small dry sound in the dark.

"Dara—"

"You don't have to. Obviously." Dara closed her eyes. "But we might die tomorrow. The king's soldiers are coming up the north road, and the god is coming up the north road wearing the king, and you've got a plan that involves trusting a prisoner, which — no offense — is the plan of a woman who has never once in her life asked anyone for help." She opened one eye. "So. We might die tomorrow. Tell me something true."

The mortar stopped scraping.

Lena sat with it for a long moment. Below them, faint through a mile of stone, she could feel the shrines glowing — not see them, nothing so crude as sight, but feel them the way you feel a storm building behind a wall. The whole valley was waking up. And somewhere in the capital or on the road between here and it, Aldric was walking north with something vast and patient riding his bones.

"Her name was Maren," Lena said.

Dara's eye stayed open now.

"My mother. She was the keeper before me. And her mother before her, back eleven generations to the Binding itself." Lena set the mortar down. Her hands didn't quite know what to do without it. "We kept the ledger. We checked the seals. We lived in a hut at the tree line that leaked in spring and froze in autumn, and once a season we walked the whole circuit of the outer marks, and nobody — not the villages, not the crown, not the gods themselves — ever once acknowledged that we existed. That was the design. My grandmother wrote about it. *A keeper who is known is a keeper who can be bribed, threatened, or killed. A keeper who is forgotten is a keeper the world cannot reach.*"

"And you believed that."

"I *lived* it." The words came out harder than she meant. She softened them with effort. "I believed it because my mother did. Because she'd say it while we patched the roof, or salted meat, or walked the circuit in snow to our knees. *Invisible is safe. Invisible is strong.* She made it sound like a prayer."

Dara said nothing. For once in her life, Dara Voss said absolutely nothing, and waited.

"She died when I was nineteen," Lena said. "Not dramatically. That's the thing everyone gets wrong about duty — they think it takes you in some grand moment, some battle. Mine took her in winter, on the routine circuit, checking a seal that had held for four hundred years and would have held another four hundred without her. Ice on the ledge. She fell maybe twenty feet. I found her because she didn't come back to the hut by dark, which she always did, always, to the minute, because routine was the whole architecture of her life." Lena heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice she used for ledger entries. "I carried her back down. It took most of the night. And the whole time I carried her I kept thinking: if one person in one village had known her name, had wondered where she was, had come looking — she might have lain on that ice for an hour instead of six. She might have lived."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, a shrine flared brighter, and the light of it came up through the stone and touched the carved walls of the cave like dawn through cloth.

"So I made a vow," Lena said. "At her grave. I vowed I would matter. Not be loved, not be known — *matter*. There's a difference. Being known means someone can take you away from the work. Matter means the work holds because you hold it." She looked at her hands. "And the only way I could figure to matter without being known was to become the thing I guarded. To be a seal myself. Closed, silent, holding. Eleven generations of Ravensworths, and I was going to be the best one. The one who never cracked."

"That's the loneliest thing I've ever heard," Dara said quietly. "And I once sailed with a man who married his boat."

Lena laughed — a short, startled sound, gone as fast as it came, but real. Then it collapsed into something else, something that had been waiting behind her sternum for nineteen years, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"I used to talk to her," she said. "After. At the grave. Every week for years. Telling her the ledger entries, like she could hear them. *Nothing stirring, Mother. Seals hold. Nothing stirring.*" Her voice frayed. "Do you know how many entries I've written in thirty years? Thousands. Thousands of days of *nothing stirring*, and I told myself each one was a victory. Each empty day was the work succeeding. But sitting here, right now, I can't remember a single day that I'd want back. Not one. I can't remember a sunrise that wasn't just weather. I can't remember the last time someone said my name before this month." She lowered her hand and looked at Dara, and there was nothing left in her face to hide behind. "You asked what duty made of my life. It made a very good lock with nobody inside the house."

Dara reached over, slow with pain, and gripped Lena's wrist. Her hand was hot with fever but steady.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the smugglers had a name for you. Did you know that? Not your name — they never learned it. They called you the Gray Lady of Thornfell. Half of them thought you were a ghost. The other half thought you were a ghost you shouldn't cross." She grinned, weakly. "I bought a lot of drinks proving you weren't a ghost. Best coin I ever spent."

"Why?" Lena asked. It came out almost angry. "Why did you care? Why does any of this—" she gestured at the bandage, the fire, the two of them, "—why are you *here*? You could have taken your crew and your roads and been a hundred miles away before the crown ever looked at this mountain."

Dara was quiet for a moment. The fever moved behind her eyes.

"Because I know what it is," she said finally, "to be good at something nobody sees. I run roads that officially don't exist, carrying goods that officially aren't there, for people who officially never met me. Twenty years of being excellent at nothing anyone can admit to." She shrugged, and paid for it with a wince. "Difference between us, Keeper: I never pretended it didn't cost anything. I know exactly what I traded. Crews that scattered. A woman in Corrow Bay who stopped writing. A name I can't use in three ports." She looked at Lena steadily. "You built yourself into a seal and called it strength. I built myself into a road and called it freedom. Same stone. Different lie."

Lena stared at her. *Same stone.* The words landed somewhere deep, next to everything Vael had shown her in the dream — the prison and the home cut from the same rock, the cage and the keep indistinguishable from inside.

"Duty," Lena said slowly, testing the word like a loose tooth. "I thought it was the opposite of belonging. I thought you had to choose. Hold the door, or hold a life. Guard the world, or be in it." She shook her head. "My mother chose. Her mother chose. Eleven women choosing the same loneliness and calling it devotion."

"And now?"

"Now I'm starting to think they were just afraid," Lena said. "All of us. Afraid that if we let anyone in, we'd find out we wanted to stay. That we'd choose the warm room over the cold door. So we made the cold door holy, and called the fear a vow." She laughed, low and unsteady. "Vael offered me anything. Anything at all, for the gate. And do you know what frightened me? Not the power. Not the bargain. What frightened me was that for one moment — one — I wanted to say yes, because he *saw* me. Thirty years invisible, and the first thing that truly saw me was a god in a cage. That's how starved I was. That's what duty fed me instead of meals."

Dara squeezed her wrist once, hard, and let go.

"Well," she said. "For the record. I see you. Stubborn, self-flagellating, terrible at campfire conversation, carries wounded smugglers up mountains without being asked." She settled deeper into the bedrolls, her eyelids sinking. "Terrible trade, honestly. Terrible rates. But somebody should've thanked you, so consider it done. Thank you, Gray Lady. Now sleep. If we die tomorrow I want to die having won an argument."

Within minutes her breathing went long and heavy, fevered but even, and the fire burned down to a red heart, and the carved faces on the walls watched with their smooth erased expressions, the way they had watched for a thousand years while the world forgot them.

Lena did not sleep.

She sat with her grandmother's final letter open on her knee, though she no longer needed to read it. *Only at the gate. Only a Ravensworth. Only with consent.* Three conditions, and the third one impossible — unless the prisoner wanted out badly enough to take a different door than the one he'd been clawing at for three hundred years.

*He saw me,* she had told Dara. But that wasn't the whole truth, and lying to herself had gotten eleven generations of women killed by inches. The whole truth was worse and better: in the dream, beneath the mountain, bound in his own name, Vael had looked at her the way she had looked at the Hollow Gate her entire life. From the inside of a sealed thing. Wanting out. Wanting, more than out, to be *answered*.

Three hundred years of prayers bouncing off a locked door. She knew exactly what that did to a soul.

Below her feet, through the warm dark stone, the shrines were burning brighter. And far to the south, moving up the north road faster than any army had a right to march, the crown was coming home to its prison — and Lena understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever happened at the gate in the next two days, it would begin with a single question she had to ask a god:

*What would you have chosen, if anyone had ever asked?*

The fire guttered. Dara murmured in her sleep. And on the north road, unseen in the dark, the first torches of the royal vanguard crested the last ridge before the snowline.
