## 12. The Crown's Hunger

Aldric woke inside his own body the way a drowning man surfaces: too briefly, and already sinking.

He had learned the geography of his captivity. There was the surface — the thin bright skin of himself where he could watch through his own eyes — and beneath it the vast cold weight of Vael, patient as sediment. Most days he surfaced for seconds. Sometimes minutes. Once, in the third month, he had held on long enough to weep into his wine cup while the thing that wore him smiled at a visiting ambassador and discussed grain tariffs.

Today he surfaced because Vael wanted him to.

*Look,* said the god, not in words but in pressure, in the slow turning of Aldric's head toward the window. *See what we have built.*

Below the palace, in the dawn-grey square, soldiers were forming ranks. Not the palace guard — the army. Columns of them, banners furled, supply wagons creaking, farriers shouting at mules. Enough men to take a mountain.

*We march north,* Vael said, and the words moved Aldric's mouth like a puppeteer testing strings. *To our gate.*

*Your gate,* Aldric thought, and hated how small the thought felt, a candle against an ocean current. *It was never yours. She keeps it. The Ravensworth woman keeps it, and she will die before—*

*Yes,* Vael agreed pleasantly. *She may well die. That is why we bring so many soldiers.*

The horror of it was the calm. Aldric had expected, in the first weeks, madness — a god rampaging through his skull, screaming prophecies, burning him from within. Instead there was only this: vast, unhurried attention, like something at the bottom of a lake watching light move across the surface. Vael had waited three hundred years in a cage of stone and crown. It could wait out one king's resistance the way winter waits out a leaf.

But today — today something had changed. Aldric could feel it even through the drowning. The weight was *lighter*. Eager. Somewhere north, in the Thornfell mountains, something had cracked, and the god inside him had felt it the way a man feels a door open in a house he'd forgotten he owned.

*The seal weakens,* Vael said, savoring it. *Come. We should not keep our appointment.*

And Aldric sank.

* * *

He came up again in the council chamber, three days later, and knew immediately what day it was because the room smelled of fear.

His council sat along the curved table — Chancellor Oren with his ink-stained fingers pressed flat to the wood, Marshal Cade rigid in her chair, Lady Maren of the Exchequer looking at the king with the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want to finish. They were afraid of him. He could see it in the way no one met his eyes, in the way Oren's papers were arranged with obsessive neatness, as if order on the table might hold off disorder in the world.

*I have called you here,* Vael said through Aldric's mouth, *to thank you for your service.*

Aldric fought. God, how he fought. He threw everything he had against the current — memories of his father teaching him to hold a sword, of his mother's laugh, of the first time he'd stood in this room at nineteen and been terrified and proud in equal measure. *I am the king. I am Aldric of Aldmere. This is my voice, my mouth, my—*

The god barely noticed. It was like struggling against deep water; effort meant nothing, only direction mattered, and Vael had all the direction there was.

*The crown requires no counsel,* the king's voice said, mild and terrible. *The crown requires obedience, and you have given it. You are relieved. Go to your estates. Speak of this to no one.*

"Your Majesty," Chancellor Oren began, "the northern levies are not mustered, the roads through the Thornfell passes are—"

"*You are relieved.*"

Something moved in the air of the chamber — not wind, not sound, but pressure, the way the air feels before lightning. Every candle flame bent sideways. Lady Maren made a small sound and put her hand over her mouth.

They went. One by one, bowing, backing away, faces grey. Marshal Cade was last. At the door she stopped, turned, and looked at her king for a long moment — and Aldric, surfacing by sheer desperation, poured every ounce of himself into his eyes. *Cade. Cade, see me. See me, you old warhorse, you've known me since I was twelve, look at me—*

She frowned. Just slightly. A crease between her brows, the ghost of a question.

Then she bowed and was gone, and Vael closed around him like water closing over a stone.

*That one is dangerous,* the god observed, almost idly. *We will remember her.*

*Please,* Aldric thought, and it wasn't even strategy anymore, just the naked animal plea of a drowning man. *Please. Whatever you are, whatever they did to you — I never wronged you. I didn't build your cage. Take my years, take my throne, but let me warn them—*

*Warn them?* And then Vael did something it had never done before. It laughed. Not aloud — inside, in the deep places, a sound like glaciers calving, like continents settling. *Little king. Do you know what I was, before the Ravensworths wrote their name into gold?*

Images flooded him, unasked. A country of standing stones under alien stars. Voices like choirs. A presence so vast that human worship was to it what a single raindrop is to a river — welcome, noticed, insufficient. And then hunger. Not wickedness, just hunger, the enormous impersonal appetite of a thing that had lived too long in too small a place, pressing against the membrane between worlds until it thinned.

*I asked,* Vael said. *That is what your histories will never record. I asked. I offered peace, partnership, a border both sides could honor. And the Ravensworths answered with a forge and a chain.* The pressure tightened, and Aldric felt his own hands clench on the arms of the throne hard enough to whiten the knuckles. *I do not hate them. Hatred is a human economy and I do not trade in it. But I am done asking.*

*So you'll take a king's body instead,* Aldric managed. *That's your peace. Wearing me like a coat.*

*A vessel,* Vael corrected. *The crown was built to hold me. Very well. Then the crown shall walk to its own door, and I shall pour myself through the crack these clever mortals made, and I shall go home.* A pause, and when it spoke again there was something underneath the vastness that sounded almost — almost — tired. *You misunderstand me if you think I want your world, little king. I want out of it. There is a difference.*

For one vertiginous moment Aldric almost pitied it. Then he remembered the shepherd's daughter in the valley below the gate, healed by a leaking god; remembered the lights in the high pass; remembered the Keeper's report he had read with his own eyes a lifetime ago, in a hand that wrote *nothing stirring* hours before everything stirred. Whatever Vael wanted, its wanting broke things. That was enough.

*Let me write to her,* he said suddenly. *The Ravensworth woman. Let me warn her — she can prepare, she can meet us with rites instead of soldiers, fewer people die. Isn't that what you want? Fewer deaths?*

Silence. Long enough that he dared hope.

*No,* Vael said gently. *But you may try.*

And it let him rise.

* * *

The study was dark except for one candle. His hand shook as he dipped the pen — not from weakness but from the effort of holding the surface, like treading water with lead boots. He had perhaps minutes. Maybe less. He wrote fast, ugly, no salutation because he didn't know her name beyond *the Keeper*, no courtesies because there was no time.

*To the Keeper of the Hollow Gate,*

*If this reaches you, I am not writing it. Understand that first. The king who signs below is a prisoner in his own body and has been since midwinter. The crown is the cage and the key both — it holds a god named Vael, and Vael has learned to wear me, and now it marches north with four thousand soldiers to open your gate by force.*

*Do not come to the capital. Do not trust any summons signed with my seal. The Ravensworth letters in the archive — the binding — read them, if you haven't. Your grandmother knew more than the histories say.*

*Cade suspects. If you can reach her, reach her. She watched me leave this room and something in her face said she saw me, just for a second, underneath.*

*I don't know how much longer I can surface. It lets me sometimes. I think it enjoys watching me hope. But hear this, Keeper, whoever you are: I am still in here. I have not stopped fighting. Every step those soldiers march north, some part of me is dragging at the leash, and when the moment comes — at the gate, at the crown, wherever it must be — I will spend whatever I have left to buy you time.*

*Tell my people I did not abandon them.*

*Burn this letter.*

*Aldric*

He stared at the signature. His own name, in his own hand, and it looked like a stranger's — like a message scratched on the inside of a coffin lid.

*Done?* Vael asked, from everywhere and nowhere, amused. *Shall I post it for you?*

Aldric's heart slammed. *You said I could try.*

*I said you may try. Trying is not the same as succeeding.* The pressure rose, gentle as a tide. *But I confess a curiosity, little king. Three hundred years in the dark makes one curious about hope. Let us see what your hope weighs.*

The candle guttered. Aldric felt his hand move — not his will, Vael's — sliding the letter beneath the blotter, squaring it neatly, precisely, in plain view of anyone who entered the study. Then his body rose, snuffed the candle, and walked out into the corridor, leaving the warning sitting in the dark like bait.

*No—* he thought, clawing, *no, someone will find it, someone loyal, Oren keeps the keys—*

*Oren,* Vael said, *has already been relieved of his keys.*

And Aldric understood, with a lurching cold clarity, that the god had never intended to stop him. It had let him write the letter the way a cat lets a mouse run — to see where it goes, who picks it up, whether the Keeper would come. The letter wasn't a warning. It was a lure. Vael wanted the Ravensworth at the gate. The binding could be broken only by her blood, in that place, and the god had decided to arrange all three conditions itself.

*You're using me as bait,* Aldric thought, despairing.

*We are walking to an appointment,* Vael replied. *All of us. Even you.*

* * *

The army moved north through the spring mud, and Aldric drowned by degrees.

He learned to measure his surfacings in landmarks. He rose once outside Greywater Ford and saw his reflection in the river — a stranger's face wearing his frown, crowned in gold that seemed, in certain lights, to ripple like the surface of deep water. He rose once at a night camp and heard the soldiers singing a marching song about home, and the sound of it hurt somewhere Vael couldn't reach, and he clung to the hurt because it was his.

Mostly he sank. And in the sinking he discovered the worst thing of all: the god was lonely, and loneliness was contagious.

Three hundred years under a mountain. Three hundred years hearing prayers trickle down through stone like water through a roof — first thousands, then hundreds, then a handful of shepherds who half-remembered the old names, then nothing. Vael didn't speak of it. But Aldric felt it in the god's depths the way you feel weather coming: an ache so old and so large it had stopped being an emotion and become a landscape. When Vael wore his body and accepted the bows of his soldiers, some starved part of it drank the reverence like a man drinking after a desert crossing.

*This is how it starts,* Aldric told himself, in the dark. *First you pity it. Then you understand it. Then you agree with it.* He recited names to stay anchored. His mother. His father. Cade. The cook Marta who'd slipped him honeycakes when he was eight. The village schoolmaster who'd told him kingship was just stewardship with better clothes. Names, names, a rope of names, and he climbed it every time he surfaced, hand over hand.

On the ninth day, near the snowline, he surfaced during a halt and found Marshal Cade standing beside his horse.

She hadn't been relieved. He saw that now — Vael had kept her close, the way you keep a tool you might need or a threat you haven't solved. Her face was weathered and unreadable, and she was looking at him with that same faint crease between her brows.

"Your Majesty," she said. "The scouts report the pass is held by no one. But there's a woman ahead of us on the road. Smuggler's colors. Traveling with a companion." A pause. "Moving fast."

Through the drowning, Aldric felt Vael's attention sharpen to a point. *The Keeper,* the god breathed, and the eagerness in it was almost human, almost heartbreaking. *She comes to us.*

"She rides toward the gate?" Aldric heard himself ask.

"Toward it, Majesty. Or away from us along the same road. Hard to say." Cade's eyes held his a moment too long. "Orders?"

And Aldric — surfacing, fighting, spending everything he had left like a man emptying his purse in a burning house — forced his mouth to shape different words than the ones Vael chose.

"Let them run," he said.

Vael struck him down instantly, a hammer of cold, and the world went white. But he'd felt it — the fraction of a heartbeat where his tongue had been his own, where the god's grip had slipped on wet stone. Cade's eyes had widened. Just slightly. Just enough.

As he sank, he heard her voice above him, flat and careful, giving orders to the column:

"We make camp. The pass will keep till morning."

* * *

That night, in the prison of his own skull, Aldric dreamed — or surfaced so deep it amounted to dreaming — of a door.

It stood alone in a mountainside of his mind, older than kingdoms, carved with names worn smooth by centuries of touching hands. And before it, small and fierce and utterly alone, stood a woman with a ledger under her arm, checking seals by lantern-light, writing *nothing stirring* in a book no one would ever read.

*There she is,* Vael murmured, from the darkness behind his eyes, with something that in anything else would have been tenderness. *Our keeper. Our key. Sleep, little king. Tomorrow we knock.*

And far to the north, under snow and stone, something ancient felt the crown coming, and began — very slowly, very patiently — to pull.
