## 13. Unbinding Rites

The cave-shrine had a name once. Lena could feel it in the walls, the way you feel an old scar under new skin — something carved and then deliberately sanded away. The villagers of Thornfell's lower valleys had scrubbed Vael's name from every stone three centuries ago, but they'd missed this place, tucked behind a waterfall that froze half the year, because no one had thought to look where nothing lived.

She'd been at the letters for two days.

Dara slept by the fire, fevered, her wound wrapped in boiled linen Lena changed every few hours with hands that did not shake because shaking was a luxury. The smuggler talked in her sleep — fragments, mostly: *left at the cairn, don't trust the ice bridge, m'father owed me better* — and each fragment made Lena like her more, which was its own kind of danger.

But she couldn't think about Dara now. She couldn't think about the soldiers at the snowline, or the king marching north with something vast and patient wearing his voice, or the earthquake that had split the outer seal like a struck bell. She could only think about paper.

Her grandmother's final letter was spread across her knees, weighted at the corners with four smooth stones from the shrine floor. It was the last of eleven letters, and it was written in cipher — not a complex one, because Elspeth Ravensworth had never trusted complexity, only repetition. Lena had cracked the substitution key on the first night, working by tallow light while Dara breathed shallowly beside her. The hard part wasn't the code. The hard part was what the code said.

*If you are reading this, child, then the gate is failing, and you are the last of us, and I am sorry for what we made you carry.*

Lena read it again. And again. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, water fell against stone in a rhythm like slow breathing.

*You will have learned by now what the crown is. You will have felt it through the gate the way one feels a storm coming through a shut window. So I will tell you only what you do not know, and then I will tell you the worst thing, and then I am done writing, because my hands are old and my courage was always smaller than my duty.*

*Three things unmake the Binding.*

*First: place. The crown was forged at the Hollow Gate, from ore pulled out of the mountain's own root, and it must be broken there. A binding vessel belongs to the ground that birthed it. Carry it elsewhere and it is only a pretty hat. Break it at the gate and it is a key turned backward.*

*Second: blood. Only a Ravensworth can break it. We made sure of that — we wrote ourselves into the spell so deeply that our name is load-bearing. This was vanity, I think, dressed as prudence. We wanted the prison to be ours forever. Forgive us. Forgive me. I was young and certain, which is the most dangerous thing a person can be.*

*Third — and here is the worst thing, child, so sit down if you are standing, though I suspect you have been standing your whole life —*

*Third: consent. The god must consent to be unbound.*

Lena stopped reading. Her breath had gone strange and thin.

She looked up at the shrine wall, at the faded carvings she'd spent two days mostly ignoring — processions of figures, a crowned man kneeling, a door with light spilling through its seams. Her eyes traced them now with new attention, the way you reread a sentence that has suddenly changed meaning.

*Understand what this means,* Elspeth wrote. *It does not mean the god must want freedom. Of course it wants freedom; everything bound wants freedom. It means the god must consent to be unbound *by us*, through *our* rite, on *our* terms. It must accept the shape of its release. And we gave it no shape worth accepting. We built a cage and called it mercy because the bars were gilded. Why would it consent? Why would anything?*

*I searched forty years for another way. There is none. I checked the arithmetic of it until my eyes failed. Three conditions, all required, and the third one impossible — unless.*

*Unless someone asks it properly.*

*Not as a jailer offering parole. Not as a priest offering doctrine. As one creature to another, with nothing held back, willing to hear the answer no. That is the only coin it will accept, because it is the only coin we never paid it. Every Ravensworth who tended that gate believed their vigilance was humility. It was not. Vigilance without speech is just fear wearing armor. I know. I wore it sixty years.*

*Someone must go to the god and ask it what it wants. And then — this is the part that will cost you — someone must believe the answer.*

Lena set the letter down.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, she was aware of a feeling she couldn't immediately name, and then she could: it was the feeling of a locked room in her mind being opened after years, and finding it exactly as terrible as she'd always suspected, and also finding a window in it she'd never known was there.

A way out existed.

For eleven chapters of her family's history — for eleven generations of Ravensworths keeping the ledger, checking the seals, dying at their posts — there had been no way out, only containment, only delay, only the long arithmetic of holding a door shut until the end of time. Her mother had died holding it. Her grandmother had died writing about holding it. Lena had built her entire self around the assumption that the door simply stayed shut, forever, and that her life was the price of that staying, and that this was fine, this was noble, this was enough.

And now a dead woman was telling her: *it opens. It was always meant to open. We just needed someone brave enough to knock.*

* * *

She worked through the rest of the night.

Not the letter — she had that memorized now, every clause, every apology, every bitter joke Elspeth had salted into the margins (*the king of my day was a fool; kings usually are; ask yours when you meet him, if he is still home behind his own eyes*). What she worked through was the rite itself, reconstructing it from fragments scattered across the other ten letters and the shrine carvings and the half-remembered emergency liturgies her mother had taught her as lullabies, back when Lena thought they were lullabies.

It was all there. That was the obscene thing. It had always been all there.

The breaking had to happen at the altar-stone inside the gate itself — the cracked one, the one the earthquake had already split, as if the mountain were impatient. The Ravensworth blood went on the crown in a specific pattern, seven points, matching the seven seals. And the words — the words were the oldest part, older than Aldmere, older than the kingdom's founding myth, a call-and-response in a language that predated language, where the speaker asked permission and waited, actually waited, for the answer.

Elspeth had transcribed the opening line phonetically, with pronunciation notes in the margins like a scholar annotating a foreign poem:

*"Vael-annur, keth-vara."* God-without-name, I come unarmed.

*I come unarmed.* Not "I come in authority." Not "I come as your keeper." Unarmed. Open-handed. The posture of someone who can be refused.

Lena said it aloud, once, quietly, testing the shapes. The syllables tasted like cold air and iron. The fire guttered — or maybe it didn't, maybe she imagined that, maybe she wanted it to gutter because some buried animal part of her believed that speaking to gods should have consequences.

"Vael-annur," she whispered. "Keth-vara."

Nothing answered. The mountain held its silence the way it always had.

But somewhere far below — below the snowline, below the roots, below the ledger of the world — something vast and patient shifted its weight, and every shrine in the valley flickered for the space of a heartbeat, and Lena sat very still in the dark and understood that she had just knocked.

* * *

Dara woke near dawn, fever-bright, and found Lena surrounded by papers like a woman building a nest out of guilt.

"You look terrible," Dara rasped. "No offense."

"Some taken."

"Good. Keep your standards." She tried to push herself upright, hissed, and settled for propping against the wall. Her eyes tracked over the letters, the stones, the charcoal notes. "So. Did the dead woman give you anything useful, or just more reasons to feel bad?"

"She gave me a way to end it."

Dara went still in the way soldiers and smugglers go still — total, economical, the stillness of someone recalculating everything. "End what, exactly."

"The binding. All of it. The crown, the prison, the whole three-hundred-year arrangement." Lena gathered the pages, squared them, made herself say it plainly because plainly was the only way it would survive saying. "The crown can be broken. At the gate, by my blood, with the god's consent."

"And the consent?"

"That's the impossible part. It has to agree. Willingly. To the terms of its own release." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Three hundred years ago my family offered it a cage and called it peace. Now I have to walk down to that gate and ask it to trust the people who built the cage."

Dara was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the frozen waterfall ticked and groaned as the morning warmed it.

"You've met it," she said finally. "Through the king. What's your read?"

"My read?"

"You're a reader of people, Keeper. Locked doors, sealed gates, men who won't tell you what they're carrying. You read them. So read this thing. Will it say yes?"

Lena thought about the throne room. About the second voice underneath the king's voice, vast and patient, and how it had spoken to her alone in that private audience — *you know what it is to keep watch over an empty country, keeper, you know what it is to be the only one home* — and how she had hated it, and how she had recognized it, and how those two things had felt identical.

"It's lonely," she said. "Lonelier than it is angry. Anger burns out. Loneliness compounds." She looked at her hands. "I think it might say yes. If someone asked it like it was real."

"And if it says no?"

"Then the king dies, the gate fails, and everything my family ever did was for nothing." She folded the letters away. "So I need it to say yes."

Dara studied her with fever-dimmed but unnervingly clear eyes. Then she grinned, wincing. "Well. Good news. I know a road to the gate that doesn't exist on any map, takes two days off the journey, and avoids every pass the army will be watching." She paused. "Bad news. I can't walk it yet, and you can't carry me and fight, and the king's soldiers move fast when their master starts pulling."

"How long do you need?"

"Two days. Maybe three." Dara's grin faded into something more honest. "Lena. Listen to me. When we get there — when you're standing in front of that thing with your little prayer and your open hands — remember that it's had three hundred years to practice sounding reasonable. My whole trade is knowing when a deal smells wrong. And every deal that smells wrong smells wrong the same way: too good, too easy, too much like exactly what you wanted to hear."

"I'll remember."

"No." Dara reached out, gripped Lena's wrist with surprising strength. "You'll remember *me*. Feverish, ugly, bleeding in a cave, telling you: the moment it offers you the thing you want most, that's the moment you check the price twice." Her grip tightened. "What do you want most, Keeper? Think carefully. Because it already knows, and it's already rehearsing."

Lena didn't answer. She didn't have to. They both knew what sat at the center of her, the wound she'd built her whole invisible life around: her mother, dying at the gate, and the vow Lena had made over that body — *I will matter, even if no one ever knows my name.*

The god knew. The god had heard her heart the way you hear weather through a wall.

Dara saw the answer land and let her wrist go. "Yeah," she said softly. "That's what I thought. Rest when you can. We leave tomorrow, whatever state I'm in."

* * *

Lena took the first watch, because she couldn't sleep, because sleep was where the god lived now.

She sat at the cave mouth with the letters in her lap and watched dawn come up over the Thornfells, gilding the peaks, lighting the high pass where the Hollow Gate waited — cracked, failing, and for the first time in three centuries, possible.

*Hope with teeth,* she thought. That was what this was. Not the clean hope of rescue but the desperate kind, the kind that requires walking toward the thing you fear and asking it for help and meaning it. The kind where the plan works only if the prisoner keeps faith with the jailer's daughter, and the jailer's daughter keeps faith with the prisoner, and neither of them has any reason to trust the other except that they are both, in their different ways, so tired.

She opened the ledger — her real one, the keeper's ledger, the book of seasons and seals and *nothing stirring*. She turned past all those years of entries to the first blank page.

For a long time she wrote nothing.

Then, in small careful script, the way her mother had taught her, the way Ravensworths had written for eleven generations:

*Day one of the unbinding. Conditions: place, secured. Blood, present. Consent — pending.*

*Going to ask.*

Below the high pass, faint as heat-shimmer, the shrines of the valley lit one by one, like windows going warm in a house where someone has finally come home.

And far to the north, moving faster than any army should move, the king rode toward his own door.
