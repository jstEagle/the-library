## 14. The Bargain Refused

The cave-shrine breathed.

Lena had noticed it the first night — a slow swell and fall in the air, as if the mountain behind the walls of stone were sleeping with its mouth slightly open. The old carvings along the ceiling moved when she wasn't looking directly at them. She'd stopped looking directly at them.

Dara slept by the fire, or something near sleep, her breathing shallow and wet. The wound in her side had gone from red to an ugly bruised purple at the edges, and the fever came in waves now, each one higher than the last. Lena had burned through half her store of yarrow and all of her comfrey. There was a village two days south that might have a healer. There was an army one day north that definitely did not.

So she did what Ravensworths did. She sat down with the dead woman's letters and she worked.

Her grandmother's final letter lay open on her knee, weighted flat with a river stone. *The crown can be broken only at the gate,* the old handwriting insisted, ink browned to the color of dried blood. *Only by our hand. Only with his consent freely given.* And beneath it, smaller, almost an apology: *I could not ask him for it. I hope you are braver than I was.*

Lena had read the line forty times. She was reading it again when the fire guttered sideways, against the draft, and the carvings on the ceiling began to move even while she watched.

"You're doing that on purpose," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in the shrine. "The flickering."

*It is how I speak,* said Vael, and the voice did not come through her ears so much as through the stone itself, up through her boots, through the bones of her sitting body. *Light is the only language I have left. Your grandmother's family saw to that.*

"I'm not my grandmother."

*No,* the god said, and there was something in the word — a pause, a tilt, like a head turning. *You are the one who dreams herself down here. That is rarer.*

She hadn't meant to come back. That was the truth she kept not saying out loud. Last night, exhausted past sense, she had closed her eyes for a moment and found herself standing in that other country again — the gray meadow under the colorless sun, the grass that bent away from her feet like water flinching from a hot iron. And he had been waiting, because of course he had been waiting; he had been waiting for three hundred years, what was one more night.

She should have woken herself. She knew the rites for it; her mother had taught her before she could write. Instead she had stood in the gray grass and listened, and hated herself for how much of the listening felt like rest.

"Say what you came to say," she told the ceiling. "You didn't pull me down here twice to talk about weather."

The fire bowed low. In its light, the shadows of the carvings stretched and gathered into a shape on the far wall — tall, wrong-angled, crowned in shadow rather than gold. Not a body. A suggestion of one. Vael had never shown her a face, and she suspected that was mercy, or strategy, or both.

*I have been thinking,* the god said, *about what you love.*

"That's a bad opening. Whatever follows it is going to be terrible."

*Your smuggler is dying.*

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Lena's hand closed on the river stone until the edges bit her palm. "Don't."

*I can mend her.* The voice was gentle now, and gentleness from something that vast was more frightening than anger. Anger she could refuse. *Flesh is nothing. I wore flesh once; I know its seams. I could close that wound the way you'd smooth a wrinkle from cloth. She would wake tomorrow hungry, complaining about your cooking, demanding payment for services rendered. She would live.*

Lena made herself breathe. In through the nose. Out slow. The way she'd been taught to stand at the gate when the wind screamed. "And the price?"

*Ah.* Something like appreciation moved through the stone. *You do know me.*

"I've read your file," she said. "Three hundred years of it. Every bargain you ever offered started reasonable and ended with someone's name signed in blood. So. The price."

The shape on the wall was quiet for a long moment. When it spoke again, the vastness underneath the voice had thinned, and what remained was almost — almost — human.

*Not your soul. Not your service. Nothing so crude. I want only this: break the crown entirely. Not the way your grandmother planned it, with her careful gate and her measured passage home. Break it here, in the world of men, where I am bound into it. Unmake the vessel and let all of me pour out into the country I was promised. And in exchange—*

He let the silence stretch. Lena realized her heart was hammering.

*In exchange, I will give you back your mother.*

*

The gray meadow again. She hadn't felt the transition — one blink she was in the shrine watching shadows, the next the colorless grass whispered around her knees and the sun hung in its sky like a coin too worn to spend.

And someone was walking toward her through the grass.

Lena knew the walk before she knew anything else. The slight favoring of the left leg, the old injury from the ice on the pass road. The way her hands moved when she talked, always talking, always narrating the world to a daughter who followed in her footprints. The gray light caught a face Lena had spent twenty years refusing to picture because picturing it hurt too much, and there it was anyway, whole, smiling, saying her name—

"Lena. You've gone and grown taller than me, you impossible child."

"Mama." It came out cracked. She was thirty-one years old and she said it like she was six.

Maren Ravensworth stopped an arm's length away, close enough to touch, and did not touch her, and Lena understood dimly that this was some courtesy of the dream, some rule, though every part of her strained forward like a dog against a rope. Her mother looked exactly as she had on the last morning. The braid coming loose at the temple. The ink stain on her right thumb from the ledger. The eyes that had looked at Lena once, bleeding out at the foot of the Hollow Gate, and said *keep it shut, keep it shut, keep—*

"You died for that door," Lena whispered. "You died and I took your place and nobody has thanked either of us in twenty years, and you want me to believe he can just — hand you back?"

"He can." Her mother's smile was warm and certain and completely wrong, because her mother had never been certain about anything except the gate. "He showed me. I'm here, aren't I? All he asks is that you stop being afraid of him. All he asks is what we should have given him three hundred years ago."

"This isn't you." But her voice shook, and her mother heard it shake, and reached out — almost touched her cheek, the air warm where fingers would have been — and said, softly:

"You used to fall asleep against my shoulder during the winter vigils. You'd pretend to be asleep so I'd carry you back down the path instead of making you walk. You weighed nothing. You smelled of woodsmoke and tallow. Do you remember?"

She remembered. Gods help her, she remembered everything, and the remembering was a knife sliding in sweetly, and somewhere very far away a part of her mind — the keeper's part, the cold ledger part — was writing entries in the margin: *too precise. too kind. knows exactly which wounds to press.* Because that was the thing about Vael. He had lived inside a king's skull. He had worn men. He knew how memory hooked into love, and love into need, and need into yes.

"Do you remember?" her mother asked again, and her face was beginning to change, subtly, the way the carvings changed when you weren't quite looking — the warmth staying fixed on the surface while something else moved underneath, patient and enormous and lonely.

"I remember," Lena said. "That's how I know you're fake."

The meadow shuddered.

*

She came back to herself with her cheek against cold stone and the fire nearly dead and Dara coughing across the cave, a deep wet sound that Lena felt in her own chest.

"Dara." She was moving before she decided to move, kneeling, pressing the back of her hand to the smuggler's forehead. Heat like a forge door. "Hey. Stay with me."

"Wasn't going anywhere." Dara's eyes opened to slits. Even fever-burned, the wit crawled out. "Roads are bad this time of year. Heard you arguing with somebody. Ghost? Or do you talk to yourself now that you've got no one better?"

"A ghost." Lena wet a cloth, laid it across her friend's brow. Friend. When had that word become true? Somewhere between the campfire and the snowline and the blade meant for her ribs, apparently. "Go back to sleep."

"Mm. Don't sell the boat." Dara drifted under again, muttering. Lena knelt there a moment longer than she needed to, watching her breathe, counting the breaths the way she counted seal-stones, because counting was how she kept panic at bay.

Then she stood, walked to the far wall, and put her palm flat against the oldest carving.

"I'm still here," she said. "Talk."

*You refused,* Vael said. No pretense of the meadow now. The voice came raw out of the stone, stripped of its costumes. *Or you will. I felt you decide.*

"I'm deciding now. Out loud, so you can't claim I was unclear." She kept her hand on the stone. It seemed important to touch what she was speaking to. "No."

*Lena—*

"She's dying. I know she's dying. I've known since the snowline, and I've done the arithmetic every hour since, and here's mine: if I break the crown your way, you don't go home. You flood out. Into a valley full of villages. Into a kingdom whose king you've already hollowed out once. Three hundred years of rage doesn't stay gentle just because it got what it wanted." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "You offered me my mother because you've been alone so long you think love is a lever. Maybe it is. But I've seen what's written on the shrines."

Silence. Then, carefully: *What do you mean, written?*

"The valley lights." She'd checked at dawn, before Dara woke, climbing to the shrine's mouth with her spyglass. Twelve shrines down the length of the Thornfell valley, old ones, pre-Binding, the ones her family had catalogued and dismissed for generations. For weeks they'd been igniting as the gate cracked — answering beacons, Vael's power leaking toward them. This morning four of them had been dark. Not extinguished. *Drained*. Burned down to cold ash like lamps left too long without oil. "Whatever you're doing to hold yourself together, to push at the crown, to make offers to girls in caves — it's eating the valley's grace. Those shrines were the old gods' gifts to mortals. Healing wells. Blessing-stones. They're going dark one by one, and the villages below them are going to start losing things they can't name. Good harvests. Easy births. Luck." She pressed her palm harder against the stone. "That's the price of your bargain. Not written in any contract. Written in the hills. I nearly said yes to you, and the cost was already glowing in the dark where I couldn't see it."

The silence went on so long she thought he'd withdrawn. Then the carvings flickered — a slow, weary pulse, like a man exhaling.

*You sound like her,* he said. *The first Ravensworth. She stood where you are standing. She also did arithmetic.*

"Good. Then you'll understand this part." Lena swallowed. Her throat was tight, and she let it be tight. "My mother is dead. She has been dead for twenty years, and I have carried her the way you carry your prison — everywhere, always, hurting. I would burn down every shrine in the valley to hear her say my name again. I want you to understand that. I want you to know I'm not refusing because I'm strong." Her voice finally broke, and she let it. "I'm refusing because I'm weak. Because if I take her back the false way, I'll never stop taking things the false way. And because—" she thought of Dara's shallow breathing, of Aldric's stubborn human fury inside his own skull, of a valley full of people who had never once thought to thank their invisible keeper and deserved to keep their luck anyway — "because love that costs other people everything isn't love. It's just appetite wearing its face. I know. I've been reading your correspondence."

Something moved through the stone that might have been laughter, if laughter could ache.

*Three hundred years,* Vael said, *and no one has refused me kindly before. It is a strange experience. I recommend it to no one.*

"There's another way." She crouched, pulling her grandmother's letter from her pack, smoothing it against her knee though he couldn't see it. "The gate opens. Fully. Freely. You walk home through it whole, the way you were promised before my family got clever and scared. The crown breaks as the key turns — my blood, your consent, at the threshold. Nobody floods anywhere. Nobody gets a body they stole. And the shrines—" she glanced toward the cave mouth, toward the dark valley beyond, "—the shrines refill. I don't know how I know that. I just do. Homecoming feeds the ground it crosses. Ask any exile."

*And if the gate cannot open,* the god said quietly. *If your king's soldiers reach it first. If the thing wearing Aldric tears it open from the outside, badly, the way a drowning man claws a door — what then, Keeper? What is your plan then?*

Lena looked at Dara, gray-faced and burning by the dead fire. She looked at the letter in her hands, at her grandmother's apology in faded ink. She looked at the mountain around her, which had been her whole life and was now, whether she willed it or not, a battlefield.

"Then I get there first," she said. "However fast I have to go. However much I have to carry."

She was packing the letter away when Dara stirred, and her eyes — clear for the first time in a day, fever-bright but focused — fixed on Lena's face.

"You were talking to it again," Dara rasped. "What did it offer you?"

Lena hesitated. Lied to smugglers for a living, practically. Couldn't lie to this one. "My mother."

Dara studied her for a long moment. Then she tried to sit up, failed, swore with impressive creativity, and settled for gripping Lena's wrist with a hand hot as a stove-lid.

"And you told it no," she said. "Gods. You told a god no over your own dead mother." A crooked, feverish grin. "Keeper, when I'm well — and I *am* going to be well, I refuse to die somewhere with this much bad art on the walls — remind me to teach you how to haggle. You'd have gotten a better deal."

Outside the cave, far to the north, the sky had begun to glow.

Not dawn. Dawn was hours off, and dawn didn't burn gold-black along the ridgeline like a wound opening, and dawn didn't make the old stones of the shrine hum in their sockets like struck bells.

Lena stepped to the cave mouth and looked north, and understood, with the cold clarity of the ledger, that the crown was coming to the gate — and that whatever happened next, it would happen at the threshold, with her name on it.

"We leave now," she said. "Can you ride?"

Dara, half-conscious, managed something that was technically a nod.

"Good," said Lena, and shouldered both their packs, and stepped into the dark.
