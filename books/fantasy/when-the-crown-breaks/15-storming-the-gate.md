## 15. Storming the Gate

**Lena**

She heard the army before she saw it — a sound like a river deciding to be a landslide.

Lena stood at the shoulder of the pass with the wind pulling her hair east and the Hollow Gate humming behind her like a struck bell that would not stop. Below, where the smugglers' road bent around the last spine of Thornfell, torchlight had begun to spill through the trees. Not a patrol's worth of light. Not even a company's. A thousand points of flame, moving in order, climbing.

"They're early," Dara said beside her. She had said it three times now, as if repetition might argue with reality. "Soldiers march slow. Soldiers complain about their boots. That is not slow."

"That's not soldiers," Lena said. "That's him."

The crown had come north. She could feel it the way you feel a storm change pressure in your teeth — a vast, patient attention pressing up through the mountain, through the stones, through her own bones. Vael was done waiting for gates to be opened *for* him. He had brought his own hands.

Dara spat over the edge of the path, which was, Lena had learned, how she prayed. "How long do we have?"

"Until they reach the standing stones." Lena did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. The stones ringed the gate at the head of the valley, her grandmother's geometry, each one a word in a sentence that held the world shut. If the army reached them before she finished the unbinding rites, there would be no rites, no gate, no argument. Just a god wearing a king, tearing the door off its hinges with an audience of a thousand swords.

"Then we'd better be rude hosts," Dara said, and grinned, and it was the same grin she'd worn over campfires and card games and the night she'd taken a blade meant for Lena at the snowline and laughed about the scar before she'd finished bleeding. Except now the scar was three days old and still angry-red under her collar, and the grin was doing more work than a grin should have to.

"Dara—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Whatever speech you've got drafted, save it. I've heard your speeches. They're terrible. You apologize like you're paying off a debt."

"I am."

"You're not. I chose this road. That's the whole point of me." She rolled her shoulders, checked the knife at her hip, then the axe on her back, then the second knife Lena hadn't known about until this morning. "Now tell me again what you need. Small words. I'm about to be very busy."

"I need ten minutes at the altar stone. Maybe twelve. The rite has to be spoken by a Ravensworth with the god's consent, and getting the consent is—" Lena stopped. "I need the army to be somewhere else for ten minutes."

"Somewhere else," Dara repeated. She looked down at the torch-river climbing toward them, all thousand points of it, and then back at Lena, and something in her face went quiet and clear, the way it did when she named a price and meant it. "You know what you're asking."

"I know what I'm asking."

"Good. Then don't ask. Tell me where to stand."

**Aldric**

*Left,* he thought. *Go left. There's a drop past the third cairn, the snow bridge is rotten, take them left.*

His legs went left.

He had learned, in the long drowning weeks since the capital, that he could not steer himself but he could lean. It was like leaning on a door — the god moved through him the way weather moves through a house, and Aldric could not stop the wind, but he could rattle the shutters. He could make every step cost something. He could leave marks.

Last night, while the thing that wore him slept its thin god-sleep, Aldric had found a scrap of charcoal and written on the inside of his own gauntlet, letter by agonizing letter, fighting his own fingers for every stroke: *SHE IS RAVENSWORTH. LET HER SPEAK.* He didn't know if anyone would ever read it. He didn't know if he would ever be anyone who could read it again. But the words were there, pressed into leather, a message in a bottle thrown from inside his own skull.

*You are persistent,* Vael said, from everywhere inside him. The voice was not unkind. That was the worst of it. It had never been unkind. *Most vessels go quiet within a month. You are still rattling your cup against the bars.*

*I'm not a vessel.* Aldric put everything he had into the thought, leaned on the door with his whole weight. *I'm a king. This is my body. These are my soldiers.*

*Your soldiers,* Vael agreed, and turned Aldric's head to look down the column of torches winding up the pass, a thousand men and women who had sworn their oaths to a face the god now wore. *They came when I called. They kneel when I ask. Tell me again whose they are.*

Aldric had no answer, so he did the only thing left to him. When the column reached the fork below the cairns, he leaned. His body hesitated — a hitch of half a heartbeat, a king's hesitation mistaken for caution — and the vanguard swung left, onto the rotten snow bridge, into the drop and the delay and the screaming of mules.

Half a heartbeat. It wasn't much. But he had watched Lena Ravensworth work, and he knew what she could do with less.

*Why?* Vael asked, genuinely curious, the way a child is curious about an insect's struggle. *You cannot win. Why do you spend yourself on delays?*

Because, Aldric thought, because I have knelt in a room full of people praying to me without knowing I couldn't hear them, because I have signed orders with a hand that wasn't mine and felt my own name going strange in my mouth, because somewhere down in that valley is a woman who is about to do the hardest thing anyone has ever asked of anyone, and she deserves every half-heartbeat I can steal.

He said none of it. He just leaned.

* * *

**Dara**

The standing stones were older than roads, which meant they were older than Dara's entire profession, which meant she felt professionally obligated to disrespect them.

She picked her ground the way she picked her fights: narrow, mean, and hers. The path between the two eastern monoliths was barely wide enough for three men abreast, and below it the slope fell away into scree that would eat a falling soldier whole. She wedged herself into the gap with her back to one stone, axe loose in her hand, and waited for the torches to arrive.

They arrived the way torches do — first a few, then all of them, pouring up out of the dark like the mountain had caught fire from underneath. And at the center of them, walking bareheaded through the wind with his crown catching the light like a wound, came the king.

Except it wasn't. Dara had drunk with enough liars to know one at fifty paces, and the thing wearing Aldric's face walked like a lie. Too smooth. Too patient. Its eyes moved across the stones, the altar, the sky, cataloguing, and when they passed over the gap where Dara stood, they paused — just a moment — as if it had read her name off the inside of her skull.

"Well," she said conversationally, to nobody. "Come on, then."

The vanguard came on. She let the first three reach her — close work was her religion — and opened the account with the axe butt to a knee, a boot to a jaw, a shoulder-check that sent the third man cartwheeling down the scree. The fourth was smarter; he came in low with a spear, and she took the shaft on her forearm and felt the bruise bloom and didn't care, because behind her, up the spiral of stones, she could hear Lena's voice starting the rite, high and steady and terrified, and every second Dara bought was a word in whatever sentence was holding the world together.

*Ten minutes,* Lena had said. *Maybe twelve.*

Dara looked at the hundred men between her and those twelve minutes and did the kind of arithmetic she was good at.

"Voss!" someone shouted from the ranks — a sergeant she'd sold brandy to in another life, a man who owed her money. "Stand down! Captain's orders! It's the Keeper's friend!"

"It is," Dara called back, cheerfully, spitting blood. "And I'm open for negotiation. First offer: go home. Second offer: come through this gap and find out why nobody robs the Thornfell road."

They chose the second offer. They always did. People were like that.

She fought the way water fights — badly, endlessly, and always arriving anyway. She gave ground by inches and made every inch cost a man's evening. Somewhere in the sixth minute a sword opened her side above the hip and she killed its owner almost politely and kept moving, because stopping was dying and she had a debt to service: a lonely keeper on a cold mountain who'd once bought her drinks and pretended it was business, who'd looked at Dara like she was the first person in thirty years to choose her company, who was up there right now betting the world on a god's good manners.

Twelve minutes. Dara Voss had never once delivered late.

In the ninth minute they stopped coming at her one at a time. In the tenth minute the shield wall formed. In the eleventh minute she was on one knee in the gap between the stones, laughing, because what else was there, and the shield wall came on, and she rose to meet it with the axe held in both hands and her back to the oldest stone on the mountain—

—and the world went white at the edges, and quiet, and very simple.

*Sorry, Lena,* she thought, with the last of her considerable style. *Running a bit behind schedule.*

* * *

**Lena**

The rite was a rope and she was hauling it hand over hand through burning air.

Her grandmother's letters had given her the words, but words were only the shape of the thing; the thing itself was consent, and consent had to be asked, and the god had to mean it, and the god was currently four hundred yards away tearing at the outer seal with the crowned king's bare hands.

She could see him from the altar stone. That was the horror of it — the clarity. The possessed king knelt at the gate itself, fingers dug into the crack her emergency rites had never fully closed, and where he touched it the old carvings glowed and guttered like coals under breath. With every pull, a shrine in the valley below went dark. She could feel them going out, one by one, little lamps of the old covenant snuffed in sequence, and beneath her feet the mountain groaned like a ship changing its mind about floating.

*"Vael,"* she spoke into the rite's rising cadence, *"bound beneath your name, I ask—"*

*You ask,* the god said, and it was everywhere, in the stones, in her teeth, in the voice of the man kneeling at the gate. *You ask. Do you know how long it has been since anyone asked me anything?*

Below, the battle broke against the standing stones like surf. She saw Dara's gap hold, saw it bend, saw the flash of axes too far away to count. She saw the shield wall form. She kept speaking.

*"—your freedom, freely given, passage home through the opened gate—"*

*A gate you will open,* Vael said. *With the crown broken. Yes. I have read your grandmother's letters too, little keeper. I have had three hundred years to read them. I know the terms.* The king's hands pulled, and a seam of light split the air above the altar, and every hair on Lena's arms stood up. *But understand what you offer. Freedom through your door is freedom on your sufferance. I have been a prisoner of Ravensworth mercy before.*

"Then don't take it," Lena shouted, abandoning the cadence, shouting plain words into the holy geometry. "Take nothing! Take the door I'm opening because it should never have been shut! Take the apology my family owes you! Take it or don't, but stop eating a king to get in!"

For one moment — one full, ringing moment — the pulling stopped.

The god considered her. She felt it the way a mouse feels an owl considering: total, weightless, absolute attention. And underneath the terror she felt the other thing she'd felt in the cave-shrine dream, the thing that had shaken her certainty down to its studs. The loneliness. Three hundred years of it, vast and patient, sounding terribly like her own.

*You would unmake your inheritance,* Vael said. *Your family built their name on my cage.*

"My family built their guilt on your cage," Lena said. "I'm the one holding the chisel."

Then the shield wall broke through.

She saw it happen the way you see disaster in dreams — clearly, slowly, helplessly. The gap between the eastern monoliths, Dara's gap, Dara's ground, folded inward. Men poured through. And Dara — Dara was down on one knee, red to the elbow, rising, always rising, meeting them with both hands on the axe and her grin doing its final, impossible work—

"DARA!"

Dara didn't look back. Of course she didn't. Looking back cost time, and time was the whole price, and she had never once delivered late. The last thing Lena saw of her was the axe swinging, and the shield wall staggering, and then the press of bodies closed over the gap like water, and Lena was running — down the spiral of stones, rite abandoned, chisel forgotten, everything forgotten except the arithmetic of a friend's blood against a god's patience—

Something hit her from the side. Hard. Kind. A soldier's shoulder-check, deliberate, throwing her clear of a spear thrust — and as she went down she saw the soldier's face under the helm, young, terrified, and his lips moving silently, shaping words over and over:

*SHE IS RAVENSWORTH. LET HER SPEAK.*

The king's handwriting. The king's order, passed mouth to mouth down a column of men who didn't know why they were following it, only that they were. Aldric. Still in there. Still leaning on the door.

Lena got up. Her ribs screamed. Above her, at the gate, the crowned figure had risen to its feet, and the light pouring from the seal's cracked seam lit its face from below, and it turned — slowly, terribly — to look at her across the battlefield, across Dara's fallen ground, across everything.

*Little keeper,* Vael said, through the king's mouth, aloud, so that a thousand soldiers heard it and knelt. *You have until the last shrine goes dark.*

Down in the valley, another lamp went out.

Lena ran for the altar stone, and began to speak.
