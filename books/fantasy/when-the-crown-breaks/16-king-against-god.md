## 16. King Against God

Aldric had learned, over the long months of his occupation, that the body was a country with borders.

Vael held the gates — the mouth, the hands, the eyes. Vael decided when the king's arm rose and what the king's voice said. But there were rooms in the country of Aldric that the god had never found. Small rooms. A memory of his mother's kitchen, bread and woodsmoke. The weight of a sleeping hound across his feet when he was nine. The particular gray of the sky over Aldmere on the morning he was crowned, when an old woman in the crowd had shouted *long may you be kind* instead of *long may you reign*, and he had carried those four words like a coin in his pocket ever since.

He kept his treasures there, in the small rooms, and when Vael reached for him he retreated into them and shut the doors.

It was not much of a war. He knew that. He was a man defending a house with sticks against something that had watched mountains rise. But a siege works both ways, and Aldric had discovered the one weapon Vael could not take from him: boredom.

Gods did not get bored.

*Again,* Vael said, in the vast patient voice that lived behind Aldric's eyes now, that had lived there so long Aldric sometimes dreamed in it. *The seal resists.*

"Because it's not yours," Aldric said. He said it inside, where only they could hear, because his mouth belonged to the god and his mouth was currently shouting orders at ten thousand men on a frozen mountainside. "You've been pulling at it for three days. It's not stone you're fighting. It's a promise. You can't shout a promise open."

*I have unmade promises older than your language.*

"And yet." Aldric let himself smile, somewhere deep where no one could see, because pettiness was also a room in his country and Vael couldn't burn it down. "Here we are."

Below him, spread along the pass like spilled ink, his army climbed through the standing stones toward the Hollow Gate. His army. He had to keep reminding himself of that word, because the men who followed the crown's orders were still his people, still farmers' sons and millers' daughters in armor, and every one of them was marching toward a door that should never open because their king had told them to. Because *he* had told them to, in a voice that was not his.

That was the arithmetic that kept him fighting. Not hope — he had made his peace with the likely absence of hope — but arithmetic. Every second Vael spent wrestling the seal was a second the Keeper had to work. The Ravensworth girl. The quiet one with ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at him in court as if she were counting the exits. She had come to the capital asking careful questions, and he had answered her twice — once with his tongue, once by leaving words where she might find them — and whether she had understood, whether she was even alive after the soldiers caught her party at the snowline, he did not know.

But he chose to believe it. Belief was another small room. He furnished it well.

*You struggle,* Vael observed. There was no anger in it. That was the worst part, the thing Aldric could never make his court understand in the letters he wrote at night with a hand that shook, hiding warnings in the margins of trade agreements: the god was not cruel. The god was *lonely*, and loneliness this old looked exactly like patience. *Why? Your body is comfortable. Your people will live under my peace. I offered you rest.*

"You offered me a coffin with good linens."

*I offered you what your crown was built to prevent. Union.*

Aldric felt the god reach for the seal again, and this time he did not retreat to the small rooms. This time he went forward.

It was hard to describe, even to himself, what possession felt like from the inside. The best he could manage was this: imagine your name is written on a slate, and every day someone rubs out a letter and writes their own beside it, until one morning you look at the slate and cannot remember which hand is yours. Vael had been rubbing for months. Aldric could feel the letters going. Some mornings he woke — if waking was the word — and could not recall the color of his own banner.

So he recited. He had a list, and he kept it in order, and he said it every time the slate blurred:

*Maren. My sister, who cheats at cards and thinks I don't know. The harbor bells at Wexford sound flat in fog; I promised the harbormaster I'd fix them and haven't. The cook, Ilse, puts too much honey in everything and I have never once corrected her. The gray sky. Long may you be kind. Long may you be kind. Long—*

*Stop.* And there, for the first time, something other than patience. A hairline crack of irritation, thin as frost on a window. *Why do you persist in this?*

"Because you can't rub out what keeps getting rewritten."

*I could end you.*

"No," Aldric said, with the calm of a man who has done the sums. "You can't. Not yet. The crown needs a living vessel — your own archives say so, the ones the Keeper found. Break me and you break your door handle. So you need me quiet, and I decline to be quiet."

Silence, then. Vast, offended silence, the silence of something that had not been contradicted since before the moon had a name.

And in that silence, Aldric did the thing he had been saving. The thing he had practiced at night, alone in his skull, while the god slept its lightless sleep.

He took back his right hand.

Not all of it. Not for long. But the crown's power flowed through the vessel, and a vessel, he had reasoned, is shaped like what it holds — and what it held, underneath everything, was still a king of Aldmere, crowned by acclamation, loved badly but genuinely by a small cold country that deserved better than a god wearing his face.

His hand moved. On the map table before him, amid the markers of his advancing regiments, his finger dragged one wooden piece sideways — a small motion, meaningless to any watching officer, but the signal agreed upon months ago in a smuggled note to General Corven: *if my hand ever moves without my leave, delay the third column. Delay it however you must. My life for the delay.*

Corven was three hundred miles away and would never know if it worked. But the third column would halt at the river ford, confused, awaiting orders, and Vael would feel the hesitation and not understand it, and confusion in an army was minutes, and minutes were what the Keeper needed.

*What did you do?* The god's attention snapped back inward, furious now, truly furious, and Aldric laughed — inside, in the last unburned room, laughing like a man throwing furniture at a flood.

"Bought you a lie you'll spend an hour untangling."

*You are nothing. A mayfly. A draft in a doorway.*

"I'm a king," Aldric said. "We're very good at wasting powerful people's time."

* * *

The battle, when it came up the mountain to meet them, arrived as sound first.

Aldric stood at the altar below the Hollow Gate — Vael stood, wore him, spent him — and heard, rolling up the pass, the clash of steel among the standing stones. The Keeper had come down from the high road with the smuggler woman, and the vanguard had met them, and then the whole frozen valley had become a single grinding argument about the future of the world.

Vael turned the king's head toward it, and Aldric felt the god's attention split — the seal, the battle, the small figure running through the smoke toward the altar — and in the splitting, he felt something he had waited months to feel.

Weakness. A seam.

A mind that old should not be dividable. But Vael was doing three things at once: holding a stolen body, tearing at a binding forged by blood, and watching a woman who frightened him more than any army. Gods were not used to being afraid. Fear made seams, and seams were doors, and Aldric had spent his whole reign learning doors.

He threw himself at the seam.

There was no technique. He was not a sorcerer; he was a middle-aged man who liked bad poetry and cheated at nothing except, possibly, taxes. What he had was weight — the accumulated mass of thirty-nine years of being specifically Aldric, of loving a specific gray sky and a specific cheating sister and a specific too-sweet kitchen — and he brought all of it down on the crack in the god's attention like a portcullis.

For three seconds, maybe four, he was in control of everything.

The body staggered. His hands — *his* hands — flew to the crown on his head, and he gripped it, and he screamed with a throat that was his again, screaming not words but a warning pitched to carry: *"KEEPER! IT FIGHTS YOU FROM THE INSIDE — BREAK IT WHILE I HOLD—"*

Then the world inverted.

Vael came down on him like winter coming down on a harvest. Not rage — removal. The god simply erased him from his own limbs, folded him up small, and put him away, and the last thing Aldric saw from the surface was the Keeper reaching the altar, dark-haired and desperate, her hands full of letters and fire.

But he had bought the seconds. He counted them even as he fell, even as the small rooms went dark one by one — the kitchen, the hound, the gray sky, *long may you be kind* — counting like a miser because counting was human and human was the one thing the god could not make him stop being.

Four seconds. Maybe five.

Somewhere beneath the ice of himself, in the deepest small room, Aldric arranged his remaining treasures in order and began, quietly, to recite.

Maren. The harbor bells. Ilse's honey. The gray sky.

Long may you be kind.

Long may you be kind.

Long—

* * *

Above him, far above, at the cracked altar where a keeper stood with a crown's fate in her hands, the mountain held its breath — and Lena Ravensworth heard, still ringing in her ears, a king's true voice giving her his last free seconds, and she did not waste one of them.
