## 18. The Empty Ledger

The woman who kept the Waystone Inn had no name she could remember, so the village gave her one.

They called her Wren, because she was small and quick and had arrived in Thornfell Hollow the way a wren arrives at a window—suddenly, without explanation, looking startled to find herself anywhere at all. She did not argue with it. A name was a coat someone handed you when you stepped off the road shivering; you put it on and stopped noticing it after a while.

She had been at the inn for five weeks now. She knew this because she counted, though she could not have said why counting mattered to her. She simply woke some mornings with the certainty that numbers were a kind of rope, and if she held on tight enough they would haul her back to whatever shore she'd been washed away from.

So far they had hauled her nowhere. But she kept counting.

* * *

The inn sat where the mountain road bent toward the valley, a low stone building with a slate roof and a sign carved with a waystone marker that no one living could explain. The old keeper—a man named Hobb, slow and kind and mostly deaf—had taken her in when she appeared at his door with no pack, no coin, and no story, only a look on her face like a woman standing at attention for a war that had already ended.

"You'll have fallen on your head," Hobb had decided, which was as good an explanation as any, and better than most. He fed her stew. She stayed to wash dishes. By the second week she was cooking. By the third she had reorganized his cellar, repaired two shutters, and fixed the drafty back door with a precision that made Hobb squint at her and say, "You've done soldier's work before, or something near it."

"I don't think so," she said.

But her hands thought otherwise. Her hands knew knots she couldn't name. Her hands woke before she did, some mornings, already moving through a sequence of small checks—latch, lintel, threshold, sill—that meant nothing and felt like everything. Once, half-asleep, she caught herself tracing a pattern on the tabletop over and over: three lines down, one across, three lines down again. A sigil of some kind. When she noticed what she was doing she pulled her hand back as if the wood had burned her.

There were other things. She flinched at the sound of bells. She could not walk past the old shrine at the edge of the village green without her chest going tight, though the shrine was nothing—a weathered stone bowl, moss-eaten, its carvings worn to suggestion. The children left wildflowers in it sometimes. Wren left nothing. Wren crossed the street.

And there were the faces.

That was the worst of it, or the best, depending on the hour. Faces she almost knew. Not the villagers—those were strangers, pleasantly, cleanly strange, faces with no weight behind them. It was faces that weren't there at all. A woman's laugh in the creak of the floorboards. A voice like gravel and smoke saying something wry she couldn't quite catch, always just at the edge of hearing, always gone when she turned. Some nights she dreamed of snow and blood and a hand letting go of hers, and she woke with tears already drying and no idea whose death she was mourning.

She kept a ledger. Hobb had given her a blank book from the inn's stores, thinking she might want to track the accounts, and she did track the accounts—flour, ale, lamp oil—but in the back pages she wrote other things. Fragments. *Snowline. Two voices. The crown.* Words that surfaced like drowned things and sank again. She wrote them down hoping that a page full of pieces would eventually become a picture.

It never did. The book just got heavier.

* * *

On the evening the stranger came down from the pass, Wren was hauling water and arguing with the sky.

Autumn was leaning into winter early this year, and the light came down the valley at a slant, gilding the standing stones on the far ridge—the ones the villagers still wouldn't name, though they'd started leaving bread and honey at their feet since the earth shook, weeks ago, in a tremor everyone swore had never happened before. The whole valley had changed since the shaking. Quieter, somehow, but a good quiet, like a house after crying stops. Even the shrine on the green seemed less like something to avoid. Wren still avoided it. Habits older than memory die hard.

The stranger came around the bend of the road on a tired gray horse, and Wren set down the water bucket and forgot, entirely, what she had been doing.

He was ordinary-looking, deliberately so—a traveler's cloak gone soft with wear, a week of beard, boots that had seen honest miles. But he sat his horse like a man who had been taught by masters, and when he pulled back his hood, her heart did something complicated and painful that she had no vocabulary for.

"Room for the night?" he asked. His voice was low and careful, a voice used to being listened to and trying very hard not to be.

"We've room," she heard herself say. "Stew's on. Ale's Hobb's own and tastes like regret, but it's warm."

Something moved across his face—quick, startled, quickly mastered—and for a moment he looked at her the way people look at a door they'd believed bricked over, finding it ajar. "Regret-flavored ale," he said. "Honest advertising."

"It's the only kind I serve." She frowned at herself. That wasn't her humor. Or—it was, apparently, coming out of her mouth with no origin she could trace, like the sigils on the table, like the knots in her hands. Everything about her was secondhand and she was only now beginning to notice the seams.

He dismounted and led the horse past her, and as he passed, close enough that she caught woodsmoke and cold stone on his cloak, he said, quietly, "You've mended the shutters."

She looked at the shutters. They were mended. She had mended them weeks ago, well, why would he know that—

"Villagers talk," he added, before she could ask. "I stopped at the mill downstream."

The mill was four miles away and closed at dusk. She let it go. There was a whole category of things, she was learning, that you let go when you had no ground to stand on.

* * *

He ate alone by the fire, and she found reasons to be near the fire.

Not reasons she was proud of. She refilled his cup twice when once would do. She wiped a clean table. She asked him his name and he paused a beat too long before saying, "Ald. Just Ald." And then, watching her face with an intensity that made her want to step backward, "Do I remind you of anyone?"

"No," she said. "Should you?"

"No." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, a smile doing labor it hadn't trained for. "No one. Forget it."

But he watched her all evening, sidelong, the way you watch a fire you're afraid will go out. And she watched him too, because there was something in the set of his shoulders that her body recognized and her mind refused. Grief, she thought. He carries grief the way I carry—I carry what?

She went to the ledger after he went up to bed. Sat with the book open on her knees while the fire burned down. In the back pages, under *snowline* and *two voices*, she wrote: *the king.*

She stared at it. She had no idea why she'd written it. There had been a king—everyone knew there was a king, King Aldric of Aldmere, though news traveled thin up here and thinner since the shaking. Something had happened in the capital. The miller's wife had mentioned it, soldiers coming north and then turning back, the crown doing something or other, the whole story arriving tattered like cloth through a thorn hedge. Wren had listened the way she listened to everything now: politely, from a great distance, as though it concerned a family she'd once known.

*Ald,* she wrote underneath. Then crossed it out. Then, annoyed at herself, un-crossed it, because ink was ink and lies were lies even to yourself.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. She heard the guest turn over, and heard—she was nearly sure—him murmur something in his sleep. A name. It sounded like *Lena.*

The name went through her like a bell through water.

She sat very still for a long time, hand pressed flat against the page, waiting to see what rose. Nothing rose. That was the cruelest arithmetic of her condition: the name meant everything and produced nothing, a key with every tooth intact and no lock anywhere in the world shaped to fit it.

*Lena,* she mouthed. It tasted like snow. It tasted like duty. It tasted like a door she had stood in front of for her whole life, and she did not know how she knew that, and she hated not knowing, and she was so tired of hating it.

She blew out the candle and let the dark have her.

* * *

In the morning he was in the common room early, and he asked, casually, terribly, whether she'd ever been to the Hollow Gate.

"The pass?" She set his breakfast down—eggs, bread, the black honey the valley made. "It's closed. Has been forever. There's nothing up there but stones and wind."

"There used to be a keeper," he said.

"Hobb says the keepers died out." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Old families, old duties. All finished now, he says. The gate doesn't need anyone anymore. Since—" She stopped. Since the shaking. Since the shrines lit up and dimmed again. Since whatever happened that everyone spoke of gently, the way you speak around a sickbed.

"Since the crown broke," Ald said.

The words landed in her like stones dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It never came. Only depth, and more depth, and somewhere far down, faintly, a light she couldn't reach.

"Yes," she said. "Since then."

He looked at his hands. When he looked up, his eyes were wet and furious about being wet, a man fighting a battle on two fronts and losing both. "Wren," he said—her borrowed name, in his careful voice, and it sounded wrong in his mouth, like a coat buttoned wrong. "If I told you a story—would you want to hear it? About the gate. About the keeper."

Every instinct she had—instincts with no memories attached, reflexes outliving their reasons—said *no*. Said *some doors stay shut because opening them costs more than the dark does.* She had paid something once. She was sure of it. She was sure the way you're sure of a scar's shape without remembering the wound.

But he was looking at her like that, with the whole ruined tenderness of the world in his face, and her hands had gone still on the table, and somewhere below the well's bottomless bottom, something turned over.

"What kind of story?" she asked.

"A true one." He hesitated. "It takes more than one telling. It might take—weeks. A lot of weeks. Every week, if you'd have me."

"That's a strange way to sell a story."

"I'm not selling it." Almost a smile. "I'm asking permission."

Outside, the morning lay gold along the ridge, and the standing stones held the light without giving any back, and the shrine on the green, she noticed—for the first time in five weeks—did not make her chest hurt at all. It made her chest feel *waited-on*. Like a hearth swept and laid, ready for a match.

"All right, Ald," she said, and did not know why the name fit her tongue like it had lived there before. "Tell me about the keeper."

His breath went out of him all at once, like a man surfacing. "Her name," he said, "was Lena Ravensworth. And she was the bravest person I ever—"

He stopped. Started again, gentler, the way you start a long road.

"One memory at a time," he said. "If that's all right."

Wren picked up her ledger, and held it, and nodded.
