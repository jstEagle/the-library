## 19. One Memory at a Time

The man with the kind eyes came back on the fourth day after his first visit, and then every week after that, always on the day the baker set out honey rolls.

Lena noticed that. She noticed everything about him, in the way a person notices weather — without meaning to, without being able to stop. He sat at the corner table by the window where the morning light came through amber and slow, and he ordered tea he barely drank, and he told her stories.

That was what he called them. Stories.

"You look like you need something to do while you wipe down tables," he'd said the second time, "and I have too many stories and no one to tell them to."

"Flatterer," she said, because it was easier than asking why her chest had gone tight when he walked through the door.

"I'm serious. There's a woman — a keeper — who lived alone on a mountain for eleven years. She kept a ledger of everything that happened there, and most seasons, nothing did. She wrote *nothing stirring* so many times she started signing it like a signature." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the saucer, a small precise habit she found herself watching for. "Do you want to hear about her?"

The name *keeper* landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, warm and strange, like a coal carried in cupped hands.

"All right," she said. "But if this is a courtship story, I should warn you I'm terrible at them. I don't remember ever having one."

Something crossed his face — quick, quickly mastered — and he said, "Neither does she. That's rather the point of her story."

* * *

He came on the eighth day of every week's turning, or close enough that Tam, the inn's boy, started calling him *the Thursday king* behind his hand, because he had a way of sitting that made even his plain gray coat look like ceremony.

She learned things about him in pieces, the way she was learning about herself: that his hands were scarred across both palms, old wounds healed badly; that he read aloud beautifully but stumbled over certain words as though they hurt his mouth; that when he laughed — and he made her laugh more than anyone she could remember, which wasn't saying much, since she couldn't remember — he pressed his knuckles briefly against his lips first, as if to hold it in and failing.

His stories were always the same story, told in pieces.

The keeper on the mountain. The smuggler captain named Dara Voss who drank like a fish and lied like a poet and once talked her way past three border garrisons with nothing but a forged permit and a barrel of pickled herring. The gate itself, black stone older than any kingdom, sealed by bloodlines and prayers.

"She didn't believe in the gods," Aldric said one afternoon, rain silvering the window behind him. "The keeper. She said a god was just a story people told when they were afraid of the dark, and she'd spent her whole life being the dark's neighbor, so she'd know."

"What changed her mind?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the rain filled the silence.

"She met one," he said finally. "And found out the dark was lonelier than she was."

Lena wiped the same clean spot on the table twice. "That's a sad story."

"It's not finished yet."

* * *

On the fifth visit, he brought a book.

Not a fine book — a battered journal with a cracked spine and a strap of leather tied around it, water-stained, salt-bleached, smelling faintly of tar and pipe smoke even now. He set it on the table between them with the care of a man laying down something breakable.

"This belonged to Dara Voss," he said. "The smuggler. She kept records of every run, every road, every debt — and, it turns out, every person. She wrote about your mountain. About the keeper."

"My mountain?" Lena laughed. "I've never climbed anything steeper than the church stairs."

"No," he agreed softly. "You haven't. Not anymore."

He untied the leather strap. His fingers weren't quite steady, and she pretended not to see.

"May I read some of it to you? Some of it's dull — cargo manifests, mostly. But some of it—" He stopped. Started again. "Some of it is the truest thing I own."

"Read me the truest part," she said, and then, hearing herself, felt her face go hot. "I mean — whatever you like. I'm not fussy. I don't have anything to compare it to."

"That," said Aldric, opening the journal, "is exactly why I'm reading it to you."

* * *

Dara Voss wrote the way she apparently lived: fast, sideways, all elbows and charm, sentences that doubled back on themselves and somehow arrived exactly where they meant to.

*"Day 40-something of knowing the mountain hermit,"* Aldric read, one finger tracing the faded ink. *"Traded her three casks of southern wine for a week's worth of silence about my route. She doesn't drink. She just likes owning things other people want. I respect that in a woman. Terrifying quality in a gatekeeper."*

Lena laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, surprising her from the inside. "I like her."

"So did everyone," Aldric said. "It was her worst flaw and her best armor." He turned the page. "There's more. Do you want—"

"Yes," she said. Too fast. She made herself slow down. "Yes, please."

*"She checked those seals every dawn,"* he read. *"Every single dawn, cold or storm, like the mountain would fall if she slept past sunrise. I asked her once what she was keeping in, and she said, 'Nothing, if I do my job.' Eleven years of dawns, Voss. And nobody in that whole valley knew her name. I asked if that bothered her and she looked at me like I'd asked if breathing bothered her. 'Names are for people who get to stay,' she said."*

The kitchen went very quiet around them. Somewhere a kettle ticked as it cooled.

Lena realized her hand had risen without her permission and was resting flat against her own sternum, as if holding something in place.

"Why does that feel familiar," she whispered. It wasn't quite a question. Questions go up at the end. This went down, down, into some cellar of herself she couldn't find the door to.

Aldric closed the journal gently, keeping his thumb in the page. "Because it's yours," he said. "It was yours. And I think — I hope — some part of you knows it still is."

* * *

He read to her every week after that.

He read the entry where Dara described the keeper teaching her an old mountain song to pass a snowed-in night, and how Dara sang it wrong on purpose just to watch her scowl. He read the entry where Dara admitted, in a rare unguarded line, *"She's the only honest thing on this whole mountain and I'd burn every road I know before I let anything reach her door."*

He read the last entries — the ones written in a shakier hand, after a blade meant for someone else had found her instead — and Lena had to sit down halfway through them, gripping the edge of a chair, her heart hammering at a danger she had no memory of and could not stop grieving.

"Did she live?" Lena asked. "Please tell me she lived."

Aldric was quiet long enough that the answer arrived before he spoke it.

"She lived," he said. "Scarred, furious, alive. She's on the roads again. She'll come back this way eventually — she always comes back." He hesitated. "When she does, she'll walk in here and say your name, and you won't know it. I wanted you to be ready for that. I wanted you to hear, first, how much you were to each other."

Lena looked at the journal, at the ink-stained pages full of a woman she had loved and could not recall loving, and something in her cracked open — not painfully, or not only painfully. More like ice going out of a river. Like movement returning to something frozen.

"Aldric." She used his name carefully, tasting it. "Why are you doing this? Coming every week. Reading to a stranger. Carrying a dead woman's— a scarred woman's journal up a mountain road. What is she to you? What am I?"

He set the book down. For once, the storyteller had no story ready.

"You were the bravest person I ever met," he said. "You gave up everything — everything — to save people who never knew your name. Including me. Especially me." His voice caught and steadied. "I can't give you back what you lost. I've tried. There's no rite, no prayer, no crown that returns memory. But a life isn't only what you remember. It's what's true. And everything in this book is true, and everything I can tell you is true, and if I tell it enough times—"

"Then maybe I'll fall in love with it twice," Lena finished quietly.

He stared at her.

She hadn't known she was going to say it. It had simply come up out of her, the way water finds a crack — and now that it was said, she found she didn't want to take it back. She looked at this man with the scarred palms and the careful grief, this stranger who knew her better than she knew herself, and she thought: *I don't remember you. But my hands remember wanting to hold onto something, and you're the first thing in a year that hasn't slipped away.*

"I don't know what we were," she said. "Before. I don't know if we were anything."

"We were friends," Aldric said. "We were becoming more. We ran out of time before we found out." A small, crooked smile. "I'm in no hurry now. I have stories for years."

"Years," she repeated.

"If you'll have them."

Outside, evening was coming down blue and soft over the village, smoke rising straight from the chimneys, the mountain standing dark and patient above it all. Lena looked at it — at the peak she'd lived beneath all this time without one flicker of recognition — and felt, for the first time, not absence but invitation. As if the mountain were waiting for her too. As if it had something to show her.

"There's a place," she said slowly, "up past the tree line. I dream about it. Black stones in a ring, and a door in the rock, and I'm always standing in front of it in the dreams, and I'm never afraid." She turned back to him. "Is that real? Is that the gate?"

Aldric's breath left him slowly.

"It's real," he said. "It's the Hollow Gate. You kept it. You opened it. And Lena—" He stopped himself, shook his head. "There's something else you should know. Something lives there now. Or *through* there. Something that knew you, that spoke to you, that—" His jaw worked. "The villagers leave offerings at the threshold now. They say it answers. They say it's *kind.*"

Kind. The word moved through her like a bell struck in an empty room.

"I want to see it," she heard herself say. "Not someday. Soon. Will you take me?"

He looked at her for a long moment — hope and fear fighting openly on his face, neither winning — and then he nodded once, solemn as an oath.

"Thursday," he said. "Wear good boots."
