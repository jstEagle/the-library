## 20. The Gate Stays Open

The path to the Hollow Gate had been a secret for as long as there had been Ravensworths to keep it. Now it was a road.

Lena felt the difference in her feet before her mind caught up. Her body remembered the old way — the switchback hidden behind the waterfall's shoulder, the ledge you had to take at a crouch, the false trail that dead-ended at a scree field to turn back anyone who didn't know. She had walked those paths half-blind with purpose for twenty years, and her legs still wanted them.

But the villagers had cut a new trail instead. Wide enough for two abreast, graded gently up the mountainside, marked with white stones painted by children who had clearly argued about the spacing. Someone had planted rowan saplings along it, their leaves gone copper with autumn. A woman coming down passed her with a basket of late apples and nodded the way neighbors nod — no fear, no curiosity, just the ordinary courtesy of one person going about her day to another.

*Good morning,* the nod said. *The mountain is quiet. You can go up if you like.*

Lena returned it and kept climbing, and the strangeness of it sat in her chest like a swallowed coal: warm, uncomfortable, impossible to spit out.

She still did not remember these people. That was the truth she carried up the mountain with her, heavier than any pack. The king had given her back her story over weeks of patient telling — the ledger, the smugglers, Dara Voss laughing around a campfire, the crown breaking in her hands like a struck bell. He had given her names and faces and the shape of a life. But memory given is not memory lived. She knew about these villagers the way she knew about the sea: accurately, secondhand, without ever having tasted salt.

They knew her, though. That was the part that undid her every time.

The innkeeper's wife had wept when Lena first came down from the capital, and Lena had stood in the doorway of her own inn — *her* inn, they insisted, though she had no memory of signing anything — while a stranger held her hands and said, "You don't know me, but I know you, and that will have to be enough until it isn't." It had been enough so far. It was more than she deserved, some cold practical corner of her mind whispered, and that corner sounded so much like her grandmother that Lena told it, gently, to be quiet.

The new trail curved past the first shrine.

That was another thing that had changed. In her keeping, the shrines had been secrets — tucked into hollows, warded, unmarked, tended at night by a woman who wanted no one to know they existed. Now they stood open beside the path like milestones. This one was a low stone basin, older than Aldmere, its carvings worn to suggestions. Offerings filled it: apples again, a twist of wool dyed madder-red, a child's carved horse with one ear chipped, a candle stub burning in a jar against the wind.

Not prayers, exactly. The villagers had been clear about that, almost shy about it. Not asking. Just — leaving something. Acknowledging that the world was wider than the valley and that the wide world had been locked out for three hundred years by people who meant well and were wrong.

*A god lives behind the mountain now,* the village had decided, in the loose democratic way villages decide things. *We should be good neighbors.*

Lena stopped at the basin and looked at the little horse with the chipped ear, and something in her ached without attaching itself to any particular memory. She had done this. Or someone like her had done this, a woman whose story she owned but could not feel. That woman had spent her whole life making sure no one ever left an offering here. That woman would have found this basin scandalous, dangerous, the thin end of the end of the world.

Lena picked up the apple that had rolled free of the pile and set it back among the others. Then she walked on.

* * *

The gate itself came into view all at once, the way it always had — the trail bent around a granite shoulder and there it was, filling the notch in the ridge like a door fills a wall.

Except it wasn't a door anymore. That was the thing she still hadn't gotten used to, the thing that made her breath catch every single time, and she suspected it always would.

The Hollow Gate had been built to look like nothing: a seam in the rock, sealed with wards laid down by nine generations of Ravensworths, invisible unless you knew to look. What stood there now was unmistakably a gate. The earthquake that had cracked the outer seal had never fully healed — couldn't, maybe, shouldn't — and where the stone had split, light came through. Not firelight, not sunlight. Something older and softer, the color of the sky ten minutes after sunset, pouring through the crack in slow pulses, like breathing.

The villagers had built around it instead of away from it. There was a paved approach now, and two stone benches, and a roofed alcove where offerings could sit out of the rain. A young man Lena half-recognized — the miller's son? — was sweeping the paving when she arrived, and he straightened and touched his forehead in the casual salute people had started giving her, half deference, half joke. *Keeper*, they called her sometimes, but they'd softened the word into something she could carry. Not an office. An inheritance they'd decided to share.

"Quiet morning," the young man offered.

"They're all quiet mornings now," Lena said.

"That's the miracle of it." He grinned, shouldered his broom, and headed down the trail, leaving her alone with the light.

Alone with the light. She laughed quietly at herself. For twenty years — so she was told — alone had been the whole architecture of her life. Alone at the gate, alone in the ledger, alone in a tower of duty with the door bricked over from the inside. And now she stood in an open place, on swept stone, before an unlocked border between worlds, and she was not alone at all. There was a boy's broom-marks drying in the dew. There were apples and red wool and a chipped wooden horse. There was a village below that knew her name and a king in the capital who wrote her letters she read four times each.

And there was what waited beyond the light.

Lena walked to the threshold and stopped, as she always stopped, one pace from the crack in the world. Close enough that the light washed over her boots and up her shins, warm as bathwater, smelling faintly of rain on hot stone. Beyond it the rock opened into a passage that was not geology — walls too smooth, angles too deliberate, descending into a glow that never quite resolved into a source. The gods' country. Vael's country. His road home, held open now by a bargain kept instead of a cage.

"I'm here," she said. Her voice sounded small in the notch of the mountain, and then it didn't sound small at all, because the mountain answered.

It was not a voice, exactly. It had never been a voice, even in the days when it wore a king and spoke through his mouth in court. It was more like standing near a very large bell after it has been struck — a presence in the air, a pressure that arranged itself into meaning. Fainter than it had been. Every week fainter, as he went further home, and every week gentler, as if distance were polishing him rather than erasing him.

***Keeper.***

"Not anymore," she said, the way she always said it. "Just Lena."

***Lena.*** The name settled over her like a shawl. ***You come often.***

"You say that like it surprises you."

***It does not surprise me. It pleases me. Those are different things.*** A pause, the light pulsing once, twice. ***I had keepers for three hundred years. None of them came to talk.***

"My family talked plenty. Just not to you."

***To their ledgers. To their god of duty. I read their handwriting through the stone, all those years. Very neat. Very afraid.*** The pressure shifted, and she felt — she was nearly sure she felt — something like wry humor move through it. ***Your hand is worse.***

"My hand is fine."

***It slants. When you are tired it slants left. The king showed me a letter once, before—*** The presence hesitated, and Lena understood that even a god could find a sentence he didn't want to finish. ***Before he understood why he wanted to show me.***

She looked at the light instead of answering. Aldric wrote her every week. Long letters, careful and unhurried, full of council squabbles and weather and small jokes, and always, somewhere in the middle, a paragraph that was not about anything at all — just him talking to her as if she were in the room, as if the page were a bench they shared. She had written back that she was learning the inn, learning the village, learning the names of things she'd supposedly known all her life. She had not yet written the truest thing, which was that she read his letters aloud to herself in the empty kitchen after closing, in a voice she imagined was hers, and that it was the only hour of the day she felt like one continuous person.

A god behind a door probably already knew. Gods behind doors had a way of knowing things.

"Why do you stay near the threshold?" she asked. "You're free. The passage is open. You could be home by now, however far home goes."

The light dimmed, then brightened, thinking. Or gathering. With Vael it was hard to tell the difference, and she had learned not to rush him. Three hundred years in a cage taught a being patience; three hundred years of watching your jailers' descendants taught you how slowly humans moved through their own hearts.

***Home is not a place I can arrive at quickly,*** he said at last. ***I was... poured out, when the crown broke. Spread along the passage like water finding its level. Some of me is already through. Some of me is still here, in the stone, in the shrines, in the apples your villagers leave.*** Another pulse of light, and this time she could have sworn it bent toward her, the way a person leans in. ***And some of me stays because staying is a choice now. Do you understand the difference? For three hundred years I could not leave. Now I can, and I choose to remain a while, at a doorway, in the company of people who leave me apples. No one has ever given me the chance to choose anything. I am savoring it.***

Lena's throat tightened. "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

***Is it? I find it the happiest.*** The vastness of him seemed to fold down, closer, kinder. ***You of all people should understand, Lena. You spent your life guarding a door because you were born to it. Now you bake bread in a village because you choose to. Which life did the door give you, and which did you give yourself?***

"That's not fair."

***No,*** he agreed, gently. ***Truth rarely is.***

She sat down on one of the benches then, because her knees had opinions, and pulled her cloak around her, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The light breathed. Somewhere below, a dog barked at nothing. Autumn wind moved through the rowans along the trail, and the copper leaves turned over all at once, showing their pale undersides, like a crowd glancing up.

***I want to thank you,*** Vael said finally. ***Properly. I have tried to find the words for six weeks and I cannot, so I will use yours instead. Your grandmother wrote, in the last letter — the one you found in the dark of the archive — that she hoped someday someone would stand at this gate without fear. She hoped it so hard it was almost a prayer, and prayers travel strangely. I think it reached me, all those years later, through the stone. I think it is the reason you were able to offer me the road instead of the knife. Fear builds cages. She knew. She built one anyway, and hated herself for it, and hoped.***

"She never came back here," Lena said. "After the Binding. My mother told me—" She stopped. Corrected. "I'm told my mother told me she never came back. Couldn't bear the sight of it."

***Grief makes cowards of the strong. I held her grief in the stone with me for fifty years. It was heavy, but it was honest, and honest things are easy to hold.*** The light pulsed, slow. ***You came back. Broken open, emptied out, carrying nothing of your own life — and you climbed this trail anyway, the first week, before the inn had a roof. Do you know what I thought, watching you stand where you stand now?***

"Tell me."

***I thought: there is the bravest creature in either world. She has lost everything she is, and she walks toward the door anyway. Not to guard it. Not to seal it. Just to see. Just to look at the wound she made and call it hers.*** The pressure of him receded slightly, respectfully, the way one steps back from someone crying. ***I was worshipped for a thousand years, Lena. Ten thousand voices, temples, blood and honey and song. None of it moved me like a woman with no memories standing at my door saying *I did this and I am sorry*.***

Lena discovered she was crying, which annoyed her, and let it happen anyway, which didn't.

"I don't remember doing it," she managed. "Breaking the crown. I know the shape of it. Aldric told me. Dara's journal describes it. But it's a story I know, not a thing I did. Sometimes I feel like a fraud sitting in that kitchen. Everyone thanks me for something I can't remember surviving."

***Then let me remember it for both of us,*** Vael said. ***I was there. I felt the binding come apart like a knot untied by kind hands. I felt you choose — and I will tell you what you chose, since the world has taken it from you and given you only the cost. You chose to send me home instead of taking your mother back. You were offered everything you had ever lost, whole and warm and real, and you looked at the price — the valley going dark, the shrines guttering, the world paying for your private happiness — and you said no. You said no kindly. That is the part I will keep until I am entirely elsewhere. Not the power of the refusal. The kindness of it.***

The wind came up the notch and moved through the gate-light, and where they mixed, the air shimmered like heat off summer stone. Lena wiped her face with her sleeve and laughed at herself, wetly.

"You're going to make the villagers think I'm strange, sitting up here crying at a crack in the mountain."

***They already think you're strange. They think all keepers are strange. It is a tradition now.*** The humor again, unmistakable, warm as the light. ***But come. Since you are here, and since I am still partly here — walk the threshold with me. Not through. Alongside. I want to show you something before I go further home.***

"What?"

***Patience, keeper.*** And the light brightened, inviting, and Lena rose from the bench and stepped close to the crack in the world, closer than she had ever let herself stand, close enough that the sunset-colored glow wrapped her shoulders like a hand.

* * *

She walked the threshold for what the shadows said was an hour, though it might have been ten minutes or a morning; time pooled strangely near the passage, and Vael admitted he'd never mastered local clocks even in his own country. He showed her the walls of the passage, where the old binding-sigils still scarred the stone like healed burns — he could not smooth them, he said, and had decided he didn't want to. Scars were testimony. Then he showed her deeper marks underneath, far older than the Ravensworths' work: the traces of his worshippers' original road, the one that had run openly between the worlds before the bargain curdled and the crown was forged.

***This was a market street once,*** he said, and the light flickered across shapes in the rock that might have been stalls, doorways, the ghosts of awnings. ***Mortals came through singing. We traded weather for stories. It ended badly, but it began well, and everyone remembers only the ending. I would like it remembered whole.***

"People could come through again?" Lena asked. "Someday?"

***Someday. Carefully. With consent on both sides of the stone — that word again, consent, I have grown fond of it.*** The light pulsed, and she felt him considering her, the vast attention narrowing to something almost personal. ***But that is a conversation for a world that has rested. For now, the gate stays open the width of a kindness and no wider. Apples pass through. Wool. Songs, if anyone sings loud enough. I am learning to be a neighbor instead of a god, and I find I prefer it.***

"A neighbor with a mountain."

***Every neighbor has a mountain of something.***

She laughed, and the sound echoed oddly down the passage, and something far inside the light echoed it back — not Vael, she thought, or not only Vael. Something younger. Something curious. Other presences, faint as fish under ice, moving in the deep country, aware now that the door stood open, wondering at the smell of apples.

Vael felt them too. The light around the threshold steadied, protective, paternal.

***They are coming,*** he said softly. ***Slowly. The young ones first, the way it always is. They will want to see the mortal side. Some of them will love it. Some of them will cause trouble, because the young always do, in every world.*** A pause, and then, with the gravity of a god handing over a weight: ***Someone will need to meet them at the door. Not to bar it. To welcome them, and teach them manners, and write down their names.***

Lena went very still. "Vael."

***I am only an old prisoner observing patterns,*** he said, in a tone that fooled neither of them. ***But I observe that the village below has already begun leaving offerings. I observe that the miller's boy sweeps the threshold without being asked. I observe that a certain woman climbs this trail every week and stands at my door talking to a crack in the mountain, and that the crack in the mountain talks back, and that neither of them considers this strange anymore.***

"The ledger burned," she said. "With the gate. Everything I wrote is ash."

***Then write a new one,*** said the god who had once bargained for a body and learned, at terrible cost, to want better things. ***Not of threats. I suspect you already know what belongs in it.***

Lena stood at the threshold a long moment, the light on her face, the wind at her back, the village smoke rising soft and gray below and the deep country glowing patient above, and understood that she was standing exactly on the line between them — and that for the first time in the history of her bloodline, the line was not a wall. It was a doorstep. And doorsteps, she was fairly sure, were meant to be sat on, with neighbors, for a long time.

"I'll need a new book," she said at last, and her voice didn't shake. "Heavy paper. It should last."

***Buy two,*** Vael said, and the light pulsed once, warmly, like a heartbeat, like laughter. ***One for the gate. And one for whatever else you decide to fill your days with — because you will have days, Lena, whole years of them, and I have watched you long enough to know you will not waste them being only a keeper.***

She walked down the new trail as the sun dropped behind the western peaks, past the shrine with its apples and its chipped wooden horse, past the rowans turning copper, past the white stones the children had painted. At the bend where the village first came into view — smoke, lamplight, the inn with its fresh roof and its lit kitchen window — she stopped, and took out the stub of pencil she carried everywhere now, and wrote on the back of her hand so she wouldn't forget:

*Two books.*

Below, the kitchen door opened, spilling gold light onto the lane, and someone stepped out into it — a figure in a traveler's coat, mud to the knee, looking up the mountain as if he'd been walking a long time and had finally, exactly, arrived.
