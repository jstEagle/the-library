## 1. The Shop of Small Mercies

The shop had no name above its door, only a small brass plate worn nearly blank by forty years of thumbs, and that suited Dominic Sterling fine. Names invited questions. Questions, in Vellharrow, were how the Ashwardens found their kindling.

He opened at half past the morning bell, as he always did, turning the key with a wrist that ached when it rained and pretending, as he always did, that it didn't. The bell above the door gave its tired little cough. Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and old paper and the faint mineral ghost of river clay — the smell of harmless things, deliberately accumulated over two decades until it had soaked into the very grain of the shelves.

Harmless things. That was the whole architecture of his life, built shelf by shelf.

There were luck-charms braided from horsehair that held nothing but patience. There were love-tokens of pressed violets under glass, their magic consisting entirely of what the buyer brought to them. There were birthing charms carved from alder, which worked because midwives believed they did, which was most of the way magic had ever actually worked, if he was honest, and he tried not to be honest about such things even in the privacy of his own skull. The Ashwardens could burn a man for a whispered cantrip; they could not burn him for selling hope at three coppers a twist.

Dominic hung his coat on the hook behind the counter, put the kettle on the little iron stove, and began the ritual of dusting.

He was careful with the dusting. That was the joke he never told anyone: the most dangerous object in the Shop of Small Mercies was a feather duster wielded by a man who knew exactly which trinkets to touch and which to leave sleeping. On the third shelf, behind the row of tin mirrors, sat a hair comb of black horn that hummed if you held it too long — not magic, precisely, but memory, and memory was worse. On the bottom shelf, wrapped in oilcloth and weighted with a river stone, lay a folded square of grey silk that he had not unwrapped in twenty years and would not unwrap today, or tomorrow, or ever, if he had any discipline left at all.

He dusted around it. He always dusted around it.

Outside the window, Vellharrow went about its morning in the flat grey light it did everything in. Fish-carts rattled down Cordwainer's Row. A pair of Ashwarden novices in their ash-grey cloaks walked the far pavement with the unhurried gait of young men who enjoyed being feared, and Dominic watched them pass with the mild, empty expression of a shopkeeper watching weather. Twenty years of practice — the ordinary kind, the survivable kind — had made his face into a locked room.

The kettle sang. He poured tea he didn't particularly want, because the ritual mattered more than the drinking. Rituals were load-bearing. Take one out and you found out too late which wall came down.

* * *

Business, that morning, was the usual trickle of small sorrows.

A widow bought a remembrance candle "for her husband's soul," and Dominic sold it to her without correcting her theology, because the candle was just tallow and the comfort was real and neither fact needed saying aloud. A young mason with mortar still in the creases of his knuckles bought a charm against falling, and Dominic wrapped it in brown paper and said, "Mind your knots all the same," and the mason laughed and said he would, and both of them understood the transaction completely: the charm was an excuse for the mason to be careful, and Dominic was paid three coppers for providing it.

An old woman came in wanting a charm to make her son write home from the garrison at Grey Ford. Dominic sold her ink instead, good ink, and a sheet of paper, and told her that letters answered letters more reliably than charms did. She'd looked at him sharply, then laughed, then bought the ink. Small mercies. Honest ones, mostly. He liked to think they balanced some ancient ledger somewhere, though he knew better than most that ledgers never balance. They only wait.

It was past noon when the light changed.

Not brightened — *changed*, the way it does when a cloud slides off something, except there were no clouds, and the thing the light slid off was standing in his doorway.

* * *

The child was small. Seven years old, perhaps eight, though there was something wrong with the estimate the moment Dominic made it, the way there is something wrong with a reflection in water that doesn't quite match the face making it. The child wore a coat too big and too fine for a beggar — dark wool, silver-threaded at the cuffs, cut in a style fifty years out of fashion — and stood in the doorway without knocking, without moving, without any of the shuffling hesitation of the poor. Just stood, and looked at him.

And did not speak.

"Shop's open," Dominic said. His voice came out steady. He was proud of that, later. "Come in, if you're coming."

The child came in. Crossed the floor with a strange formal precision, each step placed like a piece in a game whose rules Dominic had almost forgotten, and stopped before the counter, and looked up at him with eyes that were grey — not the grey of Vellharrow skies but the grey of slate before rain, of iron before the quench, of—

Of her.

The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way old grief does, wearing new clothes. Dominic set down the cloth he was holding with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not permit it.

"Are you lost?" he asked. "Where's your mother?"

The child tilted its head. And then it spoke — and the sound that came out was not a child's voice at all, but something older pitched through a child's throat, syllables like stones turned over in a deep stream, vowels that bent where common speech didn't bend, a rhythm that fell in threes:

*"Vael su-mereth. Vael tor-anne. Ith."*

The Old Tongue.

Twenty years fell on Dominic Sterling like a roof coming down. He heard it in a kitchen lit by a single green lamp, in a voice like smoke given manners, a woman with iron-grey eyes teaching him words that had no business existing in a city that burned people for lesser ones. *Say it again. Slower. Language is a door, Dominic, and every door swings both ways.* He heard her laugh. He heard the other thing, the night before the end, the bargain struck in whispers while he slept and woke poorer and alive and never told why.

The child watched him with slate-grey eyes and waited, patient as debt.

"I don't understand you," Dominic said, in Vellharrow common, in his shopkeeper's flat voice, in the locked-room tone. "I sell charms. Luck, love, safe childbirth. Nothing else. You want the alms-house, it's two streets east."

The child reached across the counter — small fingers, deliberate — and touched the oilcloth bundle on the bottom shelf. The one under the river stone. The one he dusted around.

And the grey silk beneath the cloth, silent for twenty years, began — softly, sweetly, horribly — to sing.
