## 2. A Child at the Door

The rain came to Vellharrow the way debt collectors did—without warning, without apology, and staying far longer than anyone wanted. Dominic Sterling watched it hammer the cobbles of Lamplighter's Row from behind the safety of his shop window, a cup of chicory cooling forgotten in his hand, and told himself, as he told himself every morning, that this was enough. This was good. This was the life he had chosen.

The Shop of Small Mercies opened at eight bells, though no one in Vellharrow expected punctuality from a trinket-monger. His wares sat in their dusty hierarchy along the shelves: luck-bringers near the door where superstitious hands could reach them without committing to anything, love-charms at eye level for the desperate and the hopeful alike, and—up high, where the light through the warped glass fell thinnest—the things he was proudest of and least able to explain. A brass compass that always pointed toward home. A mirror that showed the viewer five years younger. A music box that played a lullaby no one recognized but everyone, briefly, remembered.

None of them worked. That was the point. They were beautiful failures, harmless curiosities, the kind of magic that had been sanded down until nothing remained but wonder—and wonder, in Vellharrow, was legal.

Dominic had spent twenty years making certain of that distinction.

He was polishing a silver locket—heavy, ornate, engraved with roses that might have been thistles if you squinted—when the bell above his door gave its small, tired ring.

"Closed for lunch," he said without turning around. "Back at one."

No answer came. Only the sound of rain, louder now, and beneath it something else: the soft, deliberate pad of small feet on wet floorboards.

He turned.

A child stood just inside the doorway, dripping onto his welcome mat—a girl, perhaps seven or eight years old, though it was hard to say with any certainty. She was thin in the way that children get thin when meals are a rumor rather than a promise, wrapped in a man's coat three sizes too large, its hem dragging a dark wet trail across his floor. Her hair was black as spilled ink and cut short and unevenly, as if she'd done it herself with a knife. Water ran from her in rivulets and pooled around her bare feet, which were blue with cold and cut in two places.

But none of that was what made Dominic set down the locket with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a lock turning.

It was her eyes. They were not looking at him. They were not looking at the shop, or the charms, or the rain outside. They were fixed on the music box on the top shelf—the one that played the lullaby—with an expression he recognized because he had worn it himself, once, standing in a tower room while a woman with silver-threaded hair sang those same notes into the dark.

*Recognition.* The terrible, specific recognition of someone hearing a song they'd heard before they were born.

"You're bleeding on my floor," Dominic said, because it was easier than saying what he actually thought, which was *no, no, not again, not after twenty years.*

The child said nothing.

"Where's your mother? Your father? Someone must be missing you."

Nothing. Not a shake of the head, not a shrug. She simply stood there, water pooling, eyes on the shelf, and when Dominic took a step toward her she didn't flinch—which was wrong too, because children who wandered alone in Vellharrow flinched at everything, at raised voices and sudden movements and men who stepped toward them in empty shops.

She flinched at nothing. As if she had already survived the worst thing that could happen to her, and was merely waiting to see what came next.

"The Ashwarden's men sweep these streets on quarter-bells," he said, softer now. "If they find you here, they'll ask me questions I can't answer. Do you understand me?"

At that, something moved in her face—not fear, not defiance, but a kind of weary patience, the look of an adult trapped in a child's patience. And then she opened her mouth and spoke.

Or tried to. What came out was breath shaped into syllables, a language that had no business existing in a trinket shop on Lamplighter's Row in the city where the Practice was punishable by burning. It was low and liquid and old, older than Vellharrow, older than the Crown that had outlawed it, each word falling into the room like stones dropped into still water.

Dominic knew the language. He had dreamed in it for ten years. He had been sung to sleep in it, scolded in it, taught the names of stars in it by a woman whose voice had been the last beautiful thing he ever trusted.

And the words themselves—that was worse. Because he understood them, and what they said was:

*You kept it. You kept it safe.*

His hand found the counter's edge. "What did you say?"

The child tilted her head, and there was something almost gentle in the gesture, almost pitying. She spoke again, and this time the Old Tongue came slower, more carefully, as if she were choosing words from a very small store:

*The box. The song. You kept it safe all these years.*

"I don't know what you're talking about." His voice came out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "I don't know you. I don't know how you got in here, but the door is that way, and the Ashwarden pays copper for reports of vagrants, so I suggest—"

*Sereth,* she said, and the name went through him like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

No one alive knew that name. No one alive *could* know it. Sereth had died before the child standing in front of him was born—had died in a tower room that officially never existed, burned in effigy in a trial that officially never happened, erased so thoroughly from the records of Vellharrow that even Dominic sometimes wondered, in his worst hours, whether he had invented her entire.

But the child had said *Sereth* the way you say a word you've carried a long way. The way you say *home.*

"Get out," Dominic whispered.

She didn't move. Rain drummed on the roof. Somewhere down the street, a cart rattled past, and a vendor shouted about chestnuts, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, and Dominic Sterling stood in his shop of harmless things and felt twenty years of careful, deliberate forgetting begin to crack like river ice in spring.

The child crossed the floor—three steps, four, leaving wet footprints he would later scrub away on his knees and tell himself meant nothing—and stopped in front of him. This close, he could see that her eyes weren't brown or black as he'd first thought, but a deep grey, the grey of storm-light, the grey of—

*No,* he thought. *Don't. Don't you dare.*

She reached up and touched his hand where it gripped the counter. Her fingers were ice-cold. And when she spoke again, her voice was barely a thread, and the Old Tongue had fallen away entirely, and what she said was in plain, gutter-born Vellharrow speech, rough and simple:

"You promised her."

"I made no promises," Dominic said. "I don't make promises. Promises are debts, and debts are how they catch you."

The child looked up at him, and for a moment—just a moment, a flicker, gone as fast as it came—her face was not a child's face at all. Something old looked out through those storm-grey eyes, something patient and sorrowful and terribly familiar, and it regarded him the way Sereth had once regarded him across a candlelit table, when he'd asked her why she trusted him and she'd smiled and said, *Because you're the only man I've ever met who wants nothing.*

"You want nothing," the child said. "That's why it has to be you."

Then the flicker passed, and she swayed, and her knees buckled, and Dominic caught her before he had decided to—an arm around her back, her weight against his chest, light as a bundle of kindling and burning hot despite the cold of her hands. Fever. Of course. Bare feet in the rain, a coat that wasn't hers, days on the streets by the look of her. Any healer in Vellharrow would ask questions. Any neighbor who saw her would talk. And if the Ashwarden's men came asking about a mute child who spoke the outlawed tongue—

He should carry her to the almshouse on Cordwainer Street. That was the sensible thing. The safe thing. Drop her at the door, leave a coin, walk away, go back to his lockets and his luck-brings and his twenty years of harmless nothing.

Instead he found himself climbing the stairs to the rooms above his shop, the child against his shoulder, her breath coming quick and shallow against his neck. He laid her on the daybed in the corner, under the window where the rain made shifting patterns on the ceiling, and pulled the one good blanket over her, and stood there feeling like a man watching a fire start in a house he'd sworn was made of stone.

Her eyes half-opened. Storm-grey, fever-bright. And in the Old Tongue, slurred with heat, she murmured something he had to lean close to hear:

*The mountain remembers what the city burns.*

Then she slept—or fell into something like sleep—and Dominic sat down heavily on the floor beside the daybed, his back against the wall, and put his face in his hands.

Outside, the rain went on. Downstairs, the bell over the shop door rang once, twice, as customers let themselves in and waited, and eventually let themselves out again, and Dominic did not go down. He sat on the floor of his own rooms like a man under arrest, listening to a fevered child breathe, and doing arithmetic he had forbidden himself for twenty years.

Because he knew what she was. He had known since the moment she'd spoken—known it in the place where he kept the knowledge of what he'd lost, the sealed room in himself he never opened. The Old Tongue wasn't learned; it was inherited, carried in the blood like eye-color, like a crooked finger. No street orphan picked it up. No one picked it up at all anymore, not since the burnings.

Unless they hadn't been born to it. Unless they'd been *made* of it.

A soul-fragment. A piece of a person, split off and sold, the way you might sell a jewel from a crown. He had seen it done exactly once, from the outside, in a tower room, in a language he'd pretended not to understand because understanding would have required him to act, and acting would have killed them both.

*The mountain remembers what the city burns.*

The Court Beneath the Mountain. Every child in Vellharrow grew up on the stories: the hollow folk, the keepers of debts, the ones who bought what grieving people were desperate enough to sell. Sereth had gone down the mountain road on the last night he saw her alive. He had assumed—had needed to assume—she'd gone to hide something. To bury the last of her work somewhere the Ashwarden's torches couldn't follow.

He had never asked what she'd paid. He had never asked, because some part of him had understood, even then, that the answer would have a shape he recognized. That it might be tall, and awkward, and twenty-two years old, and asleep in the next room while she walked into the dark to bargain for his life.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain, to the ghost of a woman with silver-threaded hair. "Whatever it was, whatever you did—it's done. It's finished. I'm nobody. I sell lockets."

From the daybed, the child stirred. Her lips moved. And though her voice was thin as paper and the fever had nearly drowned it, Dominic heard the words clearly, in plain Vellharrow speech, and felt the last of his twenty years fall away like ash from a burnt page:

"She sold me to save you. Now the mountain wants what it is owed."

* * *

Downstairs, the bell above the shop door rang a third time. Footsteps crossed the floorboards—heavy, unhurried, two sets—and stopped.

A voice called up the stairwell, pleasant and terrible, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it:

"Mister Sterling? Ashwarden's business. We'd like a word about your music box."

Dominic rose. The child slept on. And below, waiting patiently in his shop of small mercies, stood the law of Vellharrow, asking after the one thing in the world he could not afford to explain.
