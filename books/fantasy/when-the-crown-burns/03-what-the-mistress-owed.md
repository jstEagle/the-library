## 3. What the Mistress Owed

Dominic did not sleep. He sat in the back room of the Shop of Small Mercies with a single candle burning and the child asleep on the cot he'd made up from folded blankets, and he let twenty years of careful forgetting come apart thread by thread.

Her name had been Ysolde Vane. He had not spoken it aloud since the night the Ashwardens came to her tower on Corvess Hill, and even then he had only screamed it, which was not the same thing as saying it. Saying a name was an act of power in the Old Tongue; screaming one was merely an act of grief, and grief was legal everywhere.

The child slept with her small fists curled under her chin, and in sleep her face changed. The mute stillness fell away, and what remained was a face Dominic knew—knew from a hundred mornings of ink-stained fingers and laughter over failed transmutations, from the particular way Ysolde had of pressing her lips together when she was pleased with herself and pretending she wasn't. The child had that look now, unconscious and unguarded, like a portrait painted by someone who had loved the subject too much to be accurate about anything else.

A soul-fragment. He had known the words the moment they rose in him, unbidden, in the Old Tongue: *I am what was sold. I am what remains.* He had spent the hours since turning them over like coins he suspected were counterfeit, because the alternative was to accept them at their face value, and their face value was unbearable.

Ysolde had sold a piece of her soul. To the Court Beneath the Mountain. And the price of that sale—the consideration, the *quid* of the ancient bargain—had been his life.

He remembered the night of it now, though he had spent two decades not remembering it, which is a labor like holding a door shut against a flood. There had been blood. His own, a great deal of it, pooling on the tower floor while the Ashwardens' fire took the lower rooms. Ysolde had knelt in it with her hands pressed to the wound below his ribs, and her face had been terrible and calm, the calm of a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to.

"There is a debt I can call," she had said. "There is a court that owes me nothing but honors its ledgers absolutely. They will buy, Dominic. They always buy. But they will want something of equal weight."

"Then let me die," he had said—or tried to say. The words had come out as a wet whisper.

"You are not the one negotiating." She had smiled then, and it had been the worst smile he had ever seen, because it was her real one, the private one, worn at her own funeral. "You never were."

And then she had spoken words he had never been taught, in a register of the Old Tongue so deep it seemed to come up through the floor of the world rather than out of her mouth, and something beneath the mountain had answered.

He had woken three days later in a hayloft outside Vellharrow with a scar that ached before rain and no memory of how he'd gotten there. The memory of the bargain itself had been taken—he understood that now—as part of the price. The Court did not sell silence cheaply. His survival had cost Ysolde a fragment of her soul, and it had cost *him* the knowledge of what his survival weighed, so that he would live out his years in comfortable ignorance, selling lucky charms to farmers, believing himself merely a coward who had run from the fire while his mistress burned.

*She burned,* he thought, watching the candle. *They burned her, and I opened a shop.*

The child stirred. Her eyes opened—not all the way, just a sliver of dark iris catching the candlelight—and for a moment he had the vertiginous certainty that Ysolde was looking out of them, assessing him, finding him wanting.

Then she was only a child again, blinking, confused by the strangeness of a ceiling she didn't know. She sat up. She looked at him with an expression of grave patience, the way one looks at a slow student, and raised one hand. Her finger traced a shape in the air between them—a sigil, quick and precise, one he recognized with a jolt: the sign for *ledger*, or more properly for *account outstanding*, a character from the deep grammar of the Practice that Ysolde had used only twice in all the years he studied under her, both times when speaking of debts that could not be forgiven, only settled.

"You know why you're here," Dominic said quietly. "Don't you. You've always known."

The child nodded once. Then she pointed at him—at his chest, precisely, at the place below his ribs where the old wound lived—and drew the same sigil again, smaller, tighter, like a knot pulled closed.

*Your account,* the gesture said. *Outstanding.*

"I don't owe the Court anything," he said. "It's the other way around. She paid them. She paid them with—" His voice caught. "With herself."

The child shook her head slowly. And there was something almost sorrowful in it, the sorrow of an adult wearing a child's face, and she reached across the space between them and took his hand. Her fingers were cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Cold like stone in shade. Cold like the far side of a mountain.

She pressed his palm flat against her own chest, over her heart, and looked at him steadily.

And the knowledge came into him the way the memory of the bargain had come back—not gradually, but all at once, like a wall giving way.

The Court Beneath the Mountain did not deal in coin. Coin was a human conceit, a symbol standing in for value, and the Court despised symbols. It dealt in substance. Years. Breath. Names. Soul. When Ysolde had come to them, bleeding her apprentice's blood from her hands, asking them to restore what the fire would take, they had set their price, and she had haggled—and Ysolde Vane had *haggled*, because she would have haggled with death itself out of principle—and the final terms were these:

One fragment of her soul, held in escrow until such time as it could be made flesh and given independent life. That was the child sleeping in his shop, or rather the child was what the fragment had become after thirty years of gestation in the dark courts, growing, learning, waiting.

And one further condition, written in the ledger in letters that did not fade: *the debt shall be discharged by the beneficiary, or by his heirs, upon demand of the Court, in whatever form the Court requires.*

The beneficiary. Him. Dominic Sterling, who had walked out of the mountain's shadow with a healed body and a hollowed memory, had signed—had he signed? Yes. He could remember now, horribly, the feel of the quill, the way the ink had drunk into the page like water into drought-struck earth. He had signed in his own blood because ink was another symbol and the Court did not take symbols. He had signed while Ysolde watched, and she had wept while he signed, and he had thought at the time—I can remember thinking it—that her tears were grief for the soul she was selling.

They had not been grief. They had been guilt. Because the debt was not hers. It was *his*. His life was the loan; her soul-fragment was merely the collateral she had posted so that the Court would extend credit to a dying man. And the Court, patient as geology, had waited thirty years for the beneficiary to be called to account.

"They're going to collect," Dominic said aloud. His voice sounded very far away. "That's why you came. You're not just what she sold. You're the summons."

The child released his hand. She folded hers in her lap and regarded him with Ysolde's grave composure, and she nodded.

Outside, Vellharrow slept the sleep of the righteous—the smug, flammable sleep of a city that burned its witches and congratulated itself on the warmth. Somewhere a watchman's bell rang the third hour. The candle guttered, steadied, guttered again.

Dominic looked around the back room of the Shop of Small Mercies: the cot, the cold stove, the shelves of harmless trinkets he had spent twenty years building into a fortress of insignificance. Copper charms that did nothing. Knots tied for luck by a man who no longer believed in luck, only in the statistical mercy of being overlooked. He had built a life the way you build a dam against a river you know is coming—stone by stone, year by year, telling yourself each stone was for its own sake.

And the river had arrived anyway, in the shape of a mute child speaking the Old Tongue, carrying a bill of exchange drawn on his own existence.

"If I go," he said, and stopped. Started again. "If I answer the summons. What do they want? Do you know?"

The child was quiet for a long moment. Then she lifted her hand and began to trace signs in the air, slowly, carefully, the way one speaks to someone whose grasp of a language has gone soft with disuse. He followed her, translating gesture into meaning with the part of his mind that had never stopped being Ysolde's student, however hard the rest of him had tried to resign.

*They keep what is owed,* she signed. *But the ledger has grown. Interest. Thirty years of interest.*

"What kind of interest?"

Her finger hesitated in the air. For the first time since she had appeared at his door, the child looked away from him—looked down at her own hands, at the faint tracery of silver light still visible in the creases of her palms, and when she looked up again there was something in her face that was not Ysolde at all, something younger and more frightened, something that belonged to whatever age she truly was beneath the borrowed gravity.

*They don't only want the debt,* she signed. *They want the debtor. All of him. Every year he has lived on borrowed time. They want the twenty years back.*

Dominic sat very still. The candle flame stood upright and motionless, as if listening.

Twenty years. Twenty years of trinkets and small mercies, of morning bread and evening lamplight, of the slow accumulation of ordinary days—all of it purchased, all of it mortgaged, every breath he had drawn since the hayloft outside Vellharrow drawn on credit. If the Court collected in full—

The child watched him do the arithmetic. She did not soften it. Ysolde would not have softened it either; that had been the cruelty and the kindness of her, inseparable.

"And if I refuse?" he asked. "What happens to you?"

The child's hands were steady. *I return to the ledger,* she signed. *Unspent. Unfinished. A fragment forever.* And then, after a pause, her small cold fingers moving with terrible gentleness: *You know what that is. You have been one for twenty years.*

The bell outside rang the half hour. In the front room of the shop, on the shelf behind the counter, among the copper charms and knotted cords and other honest frauds, a single object that was not a fraud began—very softly—to glow.

Dominic had not seen it in twenty years. He had kept it because throwing it away had felt like a second murder, and keeping it had felt like penance: a plain iron key, unremarkable except that it had never once tarnished, and except that Ysolde had pressed it into his hand on the last night, saying, *When they call the debt—and they will call it, Dominic, they always call it—this opens the stair. The stair that isn't there.*

The glow brightened. And beneath the floorboards of the Shop of Small Mercies, faint but unmistakable, something knocked—three times, patiently, like a creditor at the door.

* * *

The child was on her feet before the third knock finished, standing between Dominic and the sound, arms slightly out, in a posture that was pure Ysolde Vane facing down an Ashwarden tribunal: small, immovable, and absolutely certain of her own authority.

"No," Dominic said hoarsely. "Whatever you're about to do—no. You don't protect me. That's the whole mistake everyone makes with me. That's the mistake *she* made."

The child turned her head and gave him a look of such frank, inherited disbelief that he almost laughed.

The knocking came again. Three times. And this time, beneath it, a voice—not heard through the floor so much as remembered through it, arriving whole inside his skull in a tone of vast, dusty courtesy:

*"Dominic Sterling. Beneficiary. The Court acknowledges your continued existence, and notes, with regret, that the term of grace has concluded. Present yourself at the stair within three days, bearing the key and the collateral, or the debt shall be collected in person, together with penalties."*

The voice withdrew. The glow of the iron key faded to a dull ember. The candle went out, though there was no draft.

In the darkness, the child found his hand in the dark without searching for it, the way you find a thing you have been looking for your whole life, and closed her cold fingers around his.

Three days. He had three days to decide whether to walk down a stair that wasn't there, into a court that fed on obligations, owing it everything he had.

Dominic Sterling, who had spent twenty years practicing the art of being no one, picked up the key, and found that his hands—his coward's, shopkeeper's, ordinary hands—were perfectly steady.
