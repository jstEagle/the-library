## 4. The Ashwarden's Question

The knock came at dawn, and it was not the knock of a customer.

Dominic Sterling knew the difference the way a sailor knows weather. Customers knocked twice, apologetic, their knuckles hesitant against the painted door of the Shop of Small Mercies as though the trinkets inside might bite. This knock fell three times, evenly spaced, with the patience of a man who had never once been made to wait and did not intend to begin. It was the knock of authority wearing its patience like a glove.

He was already awake. He had been awake all night, in fact, sitting in the chair beside the narrow cot where the child slept, watching the slow rise and fall of a chest too small for the weight it carried. The girl—she was a girl, he had decided somewhere in the small hours, though she might as easily have been a boy, might as easily have been nothing so simple as either—had not spoken again. She did not need to. The words she had spoken once, in the Old Tongue, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, were still hanging in the rafters of the shop. *You were bought with me.* He could hear them every time the wind shifted the shutters.

Now he rose, and he did what twenty years of careful living had taught him to do. He straightened his waistcoat. He arranged his face into the mild, faintly bored expression of a shopkeeper whose greatest concern was a shipment of brass hinges. He banked the fire in the back room until it was only embers, and he glanced once at the child, who had opened her eyes—grey eyes, old eyes, eyes he had seen close across a pillow and a lifetime ago—and who looked back at him with something that was not fear.

It was warning.

He crossed the shop floor, past the shelves of harmless things: candle-snuffers and compass needles that pointed nowhere in particular, love charms made of river glass and pigeon bone that worked only because people believed in them, luck-bringers, wart-cures, a jar of preserved moths he had never been able to sell. Twenty years of nothing. Twenty years of the smallest possible magics, the kind that were barely magic at all, the kind the Ashwardens called *craft* and tolerated the way one tolerates a limping dog.

The knock came again.

He opened the door.

The Ashwarden stood in the grey light of Vellharrow's morning like a post driven into the earth. He was tall in the way that made doorways seem an afterthought, and he wore the ash-grey coat of his office, its collar high, its buttons plain iron. His face was long and dry, a face like paper that had been left in the sun, and his eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of a winter sky over a frozen field. In his right hand, resting against his leg with the ease of long habit, was the ash-stave: black wood, banded in cold iron, its tip darkened by a hundred fires that had not been kindled for warmth.

"Ashwarden Corvane," Dominic said, and made his voice into the voice of Vellharrow's shopkeepers, round and unremarkable as a cobblestone. "An early call. Is it the census again? I've my papers—"

"It is not the census, Sterling." Corvane's voice was soft. That was the worst of it. The Ashwardens who shouted were the ones who had already decided; the soft ones were still reading you, the way a man reads a page before deciding where to burn it. "May I come in?"

Every instinct Dominic possessed said *no*, and every law of the city said that *no* was itself a kind of confession. He stepped aside. "Mind the threshold. The hinge sticks."

Corvane did not mind the threshold. He stepped over it with a small, precise motion, and Dominic felt the ash-stave pass through the doorway the way a fishhook passes through water—felt, in the old nerves he had spent two decades letting go quiet, the iron in it *wake*. Cold iron, quenched in the smoke of burned practitioners. It remembered them. It remembered him, or it remembered what he had been, and it hummed against its own silence like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Corvane paused in the middle of the shop floor and turned, slowly, taking in the shelves. His pale eyes moved over the trinkets with the unhurried attention of a man counting.

"You have a great many things," he said.

"I have a great many *customers*," Dominic corrected mildly. "The things are merely what stands between them and their money."

A pause. It might have been amusement. It might have been the moment before the fire. "A child was seen," Corvane said. "Two nights past. Entering this street. Entering, I am told, this shop." He turned. "A child of perhaps eight years. Alone. Barefoot. No name offered at any door along the way, and no door along the way opened for her but yours."

Dominic kept his face still. Behind him, at the top of the back stairs, he could feel the child listening—he could not have said how he knew, except that he had known her mistress the same way, had felt her attention across a room like sunlight through a window. "The city is full of lost children, Ashwarden."

"The city is full of many things." Corvane drifted toward the shelf of love charms and lifted the river-glass pendant, turning it in his fingers. The iron at his cuff clicked against the glass. "This one is dead. Glass and bone and string. It does nothing." He set it down with exaggerated care. "And yet you sell it, and they buy it, and no one burns, because it does *nothing*. That is the arrangement, Sterling. That is the mercy of the Crown. The Practice is death, but *nothing* is permitted. Do you understand the arrangement?"

"I have understood it for twenty years."

"Have you." Corvane's eyes came up. They were very pale and very still, and Dominic understood, with a lurch, that this man had been a boy in Vellharrow twenty years ago. Had perhaps stood in the crowd at the burning. Had perhaps watched a woman with grey eyes and a voice like a bell be bound to the stake, and had perhaps gone home that night and decided what he would become. "Then you will not mind a small question. It is the question we ask everyone, and it is the question that keeps this city from burning itself down." He folded his hands atop the ash-stave, both of them, like a man at prayer. "The child. When she came to your door. Did she *speak*?"

The silence stretched one heartbeat too long.

Dominic heard it stretch. He heard Corvane hear it too.

"She was mute," Dominic said. "Frightened. Cold. I gave her soup and a cot in the back. In the morning I'll take her to the foundling house on Chandler's Lane, as the law requires."

"Mute." Corvane said the word the way a man turns over a coin to check for clipping. "And yet." He began to walk, slowly, along the shelves, and his voice went soft again, softer, almost gentle. "There is a woman on Cordwainer Street who keeps birds. Finches. She swears to me that on the night in question, every bird in her house woke at the same hour and sang in a tongue she did not know. There is a lamplighter on the same street who swears the lamps burned blue. There is a widow who swears the frost on her window formed letters, though she could not read them and washed them away in terror." He stopped at the foot of the back stairs. "Old Tongue, Sterling. The birds sang it. The frost wrote it. The lamps burned it into the dark. And a child walked through it all to *your* door, of every door in Vellharrow, and you would have me believe she said nothing at all."

"The Old Tongue is a story mothers tell," Dominic said. His mouth was dry. "To frighten children from the marshes."

"Yes," Corvane agreed. "It is. It is also the language of the Practice, and the last woman known to speak it was burned in this square twenty years ago, and I was nine years old, and I stood in the crowd, and I have spent my whole life since learning to hear it in the dark." He turned, and for the first time his stillness cracked—not into anger, but into something worse, something like hunger. "So I will ask my small question once more, shopkeeper. The child. Did she speak?"

And Dominic, who had spent twenty years being no one, who had buried his name and his art and his dead beneath jars of preserved moths and pigeon-bone luck, looked at the Ashwarden's pale eyes and understood that lying now would not save the child. It would only save him, and only for a little while, and Corvane would come back with a warrant and a cart and men who did not ask soft questions.

"She spoke," he said quietly.

The word seemed to change the weight of the air in the shop. The trinkets on their shelves—the dead, harmless, *nothing* trinkets—seemed very slightly to lean in.

"She spoke the Old Tongue," Dominic went on, before Corvane could speak, before the ash-stave could rise. "And I understood her. And I will tell you what she said, Ashwarden, because you will not find it in any ledger of the Practice, and it will not comfort you." He drew a breath that hurt. "She said: *you were bought with me.*"

Corvane's hands tightened on the stave. "That is a confession."

"It is a *debt*," Dominic said. "And there is a difference, though your office has never learned it. Debts can be paid. Confessions can only be burned." He was walking as he spoke, past Corvane, toward the back of the shop, toward the old iron coal-hatch set into the floor behind the stove—the hatch he had sealed with lead and prayer and twenty years of deliberate forgetting. "You want the child. You will not get her. Not because I will fight you—I am an old man who sells nothing, Ashwarden, and we both know how that fight would end. But because she is not mine to give, and not yours to take. She belongs to a court that does not recognize your Crown, your ash, or your iron."

"The Court Beneath the Mountain is a fable."

"So was I," Dominic said, "for twenty years."

He knelt at the coal-hatch. His fingers found the lead seal, and the lead remembered him—it softened at his touch like wax, like grief, like everything he had ever tried to make hard—and beneath it the iron was cold, and beneath the iron was a dark that breathed.

Behind him, Corvane raised the ash-stave, and its tip began, very softly, to glow.

* * *

The stair went down farther than the shop had depth, farther than the street, farther than the city's old foundations, and Dominic descended it with the Ashwarden's light burning behind him like a hostile star, because Corvane had followed—of course he had followed—and because somewhere below, in a court that fed on inherited obligations, a ledger waited with his name in it, and a debt waited with his life as its currency, and a child who was not a child waited at the bottom of everything, holding the door.
