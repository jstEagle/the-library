## 5. Down the Stair That Wasn't There

The stair went down farther than the shop had depth, farther than the street, farther than the city's old foundations, and Dominic descended it with the Ashwarden's light burning behind him like a hostile star, because Corvane had followed—of course he had followed—and because somewhere below, in a court that fed on inherited obligations, a ledger waited with his name in it, and a debt waited with his life as its currency, and a child who was not a child waited at the bottom of everything, holding the door.

He counted steps because counting was a thing ordinary men did, and he needed, desperately, to be an ordinary man for as long as the descent allowed. Three hundred and eleven. Three hundred and twelve. The stone underfoot was older than Vellharrow's oldest wall, older than the Ashwardens' first pyre, worn smooth in the center of each tread by feet that had climbed down long before Dominic was born—or perhaps by no feet at all. He did not let himself think about that.

Behind him, Corvane's stave threw shadows up the walls in long, shivering spears. The Ashwarden had not spoken since the stair had opened beneath the shop's back room like a throat swallowing. He had simply raised the light, looked at Dominic with those grey eyes that had watched twenty years of trinkets pass through the Shop of Small Mercies without ever once being fooled by them, and said: *After you.*

* * *

At step four hundred, the air changed.

Dominic felt it before he understood it—a pressure against the skin, like wading into deep water, like the moment before lightning when the whole world holds its breath. The Old Tongue lived in this air. He could feel it pressing against the wards he'd stitched into his own flesh decades ago, fraying them thread by thread, patient as rot.

"You knew," Corvane said. His voice came flat and strange in the dark, stripped of the courtroom cadence he wore above ground. "You knew this was here. Twenty years you've sold candle-holders and luck-charms over the top of a door to the Court Beneath."

"I suspected." Dominic's voice came out rougher than he intended. "There's a difference."

"There is always a difference, with men like you. A difference between knowing and confessing. Between practicing and merely *keeping*. You've spent two decades hiding behind that distinction, Sterling. I've read your file. There are Ashwardens who've retired waiting for men like you to slip."

"And yet here you are, following me down instead of dragging me up." Dominic didn't turn around. "That's not procedure either, Corvane."

The silence behind him stretched long enough that Dominic heard his own heartbeat, heard the small wet sound of his breath in air that tasted of iron and cold honey. When Corvane finally spoke again, his voice had dropped to something almost private.

"The child spoke the Old Tongue. In my presence. That makes it my case, my jurisdiction, my *responsibility*—" a pause, and then, quieter, "—and I have burned forty-one people, Sterling. I remember every name. Do you understand what it does to a man, remembering every name?"

Dominic stopped on the stair.

He turned, slowly, and looked back up at the Ashwarden. Corvane stood three steps above him, tall and gaunt in his ash-grey coat, the stave's light carving hollows under his eyes. He looked, suddenly, less like a hunter than like a man carrying something heavy up a very long hill, year after year, with no summit in sight.

"No," Dominic said. "I don't suppose I do. But I know what it is to owe a life. And the child down there—" he gestured into the dark below, where the stair vanished into a darkness that wasn't merely absence of light but a kind of *presence*, a darkness with weight and appetite— "the child down there exists because of a debt I've been carrying since before you were sworn. So we're both walking toward something we can't put down. Maybe that makes us honest company, for once."

Corvane's jaw tightened. The light flickered. Then he stepped down past Dominic, taking the lead, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

At step six hundred and sixty-six—the number arrived unbidden, and Dominic hated it—the stair ended.

Not at a door. At a threshold with no door at all: an archway of black stone set into living rock, carved with script that moved when he wasn't looking at it directly. The Old Tongue. He could read fragments of it still, the way a man who'd once been fluent in a dead woman's voice could never wholly forget her grammar. *All debts descend. All names remain.* Above the arch, in letters older than the script itself, a single word in the language of the mountain's first masters:

**LEDGER.**

Beyond the arch lay a hall so vast that Corvane's stave-light simply gave up, its glow swallowed within ten paces, absorbed by a dark that drank rather than merely contained. And in that dark, sounds. The whisper of pages turning. The scratch of a pen that never lifted. And underneath it all, faint as a pulse through stone, a rhythm like breathing—not one breath but thousands, layered, the hall itself inhaling and exhaling with the patience of geology.

"Ledgers," Corvane murmured. "The old texts say the Court keeps accounts. Debts of blood, of years, of names. They say nothing in the mountain is given—only lent."

"Then someone lent me my life," Dominic said quietly, "and the interest has come due."

He stepped through the arch.

The dark accepted him the way water accepts a stone, and the hall opened around him—not gradually, but all at once, the way a dream reveals its true size. Shelves rose into blackness beyond sight, miles of them perhaps, or inches; distance meant nothing here. Each shelf held books. Each book breathed. And between the shelves moved figures—tall, narrow, grey as ash and smooth as river stones, faceless except where a face should be, and where a face should be there was instead a *name*, written in faint silver script that drifted across the blankness like fish under ice.

None of them looked at him. All of them knew exactly where he was.

One of them detached from the shelves and glided toward them, and as it came close, the silver script on its face resolved into letters Dominic could read, and he went cold from the scalp down, because the name swimming there was his own.

*Dominic Sterling,* the face said, in a voice like a page turning. *Entered the ledger forty-one years ago. Debt incurred: one life, returned from burning. Creditor: the Lady Vessaline Coldharbour. Collateral: twenty years, held in trust. Status:* the silver letters rippled, rearranged, and Dominic felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped into a well, *—called.*

"Called," he repeated. His mouth was dry. "What does called mean?"

The figure tilted its blank head, and somewhere in the vast dark, thousands of pages turned at once.

"It means," said a new voice, young and old at once, a voice like a bell rung underwater, "that the account has been opened, little borrower. It means she has sent you her heir, and the mountain has counted the child, and found the arithmetic wanting."

From between the shelves, holding a candle that gave no light, the mute child stepped into view—and opened its mouth, and spoke with a voice that was not its own.

"Hello, Dominic," said the dead woman's voice. "You're late."
