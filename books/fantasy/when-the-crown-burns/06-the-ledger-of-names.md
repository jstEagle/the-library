## 6. The Ledger of Names

The Hall of Accounts had no ceiling that Dominic could find, only a darkness that went up and up until it stopped pretending to be sky. The light came from the ledger itself—or rather, from the thousands of ledgers, for they stood in ranks upon shelves carved directly from the mountain's living bone, each spine glowing faintly, the way coals glow long after the fire has been declared dead.

His guide was a creature called Wren, though Dominic suspected the name was a courtesy rather than an identity. It had the shape of a tall, stooped clerk, grey as unpolished pewter, with fingers too numerous by half. It had not spoken since the stair. It simply walked, and he followed, because in the Court Beneath the Mountain, following was the only direction anyone ever gave you.

"You'll want to understand the rules before we open anything," Wren said at last, its voice like paper being folded. "Debts here are not numbers. They are *names*. A name given is a name owed. The Ledger does not record what happened between two parties. It records what each party *became* to the other."

Dominic's mouth was dry. Twenty years of trinkets and small mercies, twenty years of keeping his hands still, and now his hands would not stop trembling. "My mistress's name is in this hall."

"Everyone's name is in this hall." Wren paused before a shelf so high Dominic had to tilt his head back to see where it ended. "The question is in what hand it was written, and in what ink. Come. She will have kept her accounts beautifully. They always do—the ones who bargain out of love. It's the ones who bargain out of greed whose pages smear."

* * *

The ledger they brought down was bound in something soft and dark that Dominic refused, firmly, to think about. Its cover bore no title, but when his fingertips touched the leather, warmth bloomed there—familiar, specific, *hers*—and his chest seized as if a hand had reached through twenty years and closed around his heart.

Seraphine Coldharbour's hand covered the first page.

He had forgotten—no, he had *buried*—the particular grace of her script, the way she crossed her letters with small flourishes like birds taking flight. She had written spells the way other women wrote love letters. She had written love letters the way other women wrote scripture.

*In the year the ash fell twice,* the entry began, *I, Seraphine, called Coldharbour by those who fear me and nothing by those who loved me, set my mark against the Hollow King's.*

Below it, in a second hand—a hand like frost forming on glass, all sharp angles and cold patience—was the countermark. The Hollow King's signature looked less like writing than like a wound.

"Read it aloud," Wren said quietly. "The Ledger requires it. Debts acknowledged aloud are debts half-dissolved."

Dominic read.

*"Let it be entered," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, "that I, Dominic Sterling, called Ash-Child by the woman who saved me, owe to the Court Beneath the Mountain one life entire. Let the life be held in trust. Let the years of it accrue interest. Let the debt pass to my heirs should I fail to render it, and to their heirs, world without end."*

He stopped. The words were hers—he recognized them now, the phrasing, the rhythm—but the signature beneath them was his own. Younger. Shaking. He had signed it feverish and bleeding, nineteen years old, while Seraphine's blood dried on the stones beside him and the Hollow King's emissary waited with the patience of geology.

"I never understood what I signed," he whispered.

"No," Wren agreed. "You understood that you lived. That is generally sufficient, at the time."

* * *

They turned pages together, and the ledger told the story he had spent two decades refusing to tell himself.

There was the night the Ashwarden's riders came to Seraphine's tower, torches high, and she had bought his escape not with a spell but with a bargain—because spells could be traced, and bargains left no smoke. There was the price: not gold, not service, but *him*. His life, rendered to the Court as surety against a larger debt of hers, one she had incurred long before she ever took a ragged apprentice off the ash-streets of Vellharrow.

And there, three entries deeper, was the thing that made him sit down hard on the cold floor.

*Amendment,* Seraphine had written. *The Court demands flesh as well as years. I refuse. Instead let them take from me a fragment of my soul, unshaped, unnamed, to be held until the boy's debt comes due—and let the fragment be the payment, that no child of mine shall burn for what I lit.*

Beneath it, the frost-hand: *Entered. The fragment shall be shaped by the Court as it sees fit. It shall remember nothing. It shall be sold, traded, or spent according to the Court's pleasure. The mistress waives all claim.*

"She made herself into a child," Dominic said. The words came out flat and terrible. "She cut a piece of herself loose and let them—"

"Shaped it," Wren corrected gently. "Named it mute, so it could not bargain. Sold it upward through four hands over thirty years, which is why the price kept rising and the fragment kept forgetting. What arrived at your door last week was a soul worn down to its smallest honest size." The clerk's many fingers folded together. "But fragments are stubborn things, Dominic Sterling. Yours especially. It walked out of the Court's deepest vault, across forty miles of mountain, and found the one shop in Vellharrow where someone would recognize the Old Tongue when it finally broke its silence. Souls know their debts. Even small ones. Even mute ones."

Dominic thought of the child's eyes—grey, watchful, old in a way no child's eyes should be—and felt the floor tilt beneath everything he had believed about his own survival. He had thought his life was stolen. It had been *spent*, deliberately, by a woman who loved him, and the change had come home looking for him.

"What does the debt stand at now?" he asked. "After thirty years of interest."

Wren drew out a slimmer volume, ran a finger down a column of figures written in silver. "Your life, whole. Plus the fragment's freedom, which cannot be priced but must be paid. Plus passage, protection rendered, and one breach of vault." The finger stopped. "Or, alternatively—one soul of equivalent weight, rendered willingly. The Court prefers the latter. Whole souls spend better."

"And if I pay neither?"

"Then the child returns to the vault," Wren said, "and your debt continues accruing until your death, at which point it passes to—" the clerk consulted the ledger, and something almost like pity moved across its pewter face— "ah. It already has heirs named. The child itself. Clever, the old drafting. If you default, the fragment pays your debt with itself. The Court gets the whole of her either way."

* * *

Dominic sat a long while among the glowing shelves, listening to the sound the mountain made when it thought no one was listening—a low, slow breathing, like something asleep and digesting centuries.

Seraphine had built her trap with a jeweler's patience. Every exit from the debt led through him. If he fled, the child burned slowly in a vault. If he paid in coin or service, the Court would hold the fragment forever as leverage, because leverage was the only crop the Court Beneath the Mountain grew. And if he surrendered his soul—

No. Not his soul. He read the clause again, the third time, the fourth. *One soul of equivalent weight.* Not his whole self. His *weight* in soul. And what weighed most in Dominic Sterling? Not the trinkets. Not the shop.

The years. Thirty-one years of borrowed time, every day since the riders came, all of it interest on a principal he'd never known he owed. The Court had been holding his years like coins in a jar, letting them ripen.

"They don't want my soul," he said slowly. "They want the *life*. All of it, retroactive. Rendered back."

Wren's folded hands went very still. "Few see it that clearly. Fewer say it aloud."

"Because saying it aloud makes it real."

"Everything in this hall is real once spoken. That is rather the point of the hall."

Dominic got to his feet. His knees protested; he was fifty-one years old, and for the first time he counted those years not as a history but as a balance outstanding.

"Then enter it," he said. "Enter that I, Dominic Sterling, intend to discharge the debt of Seraphine Coldharbour in full. Enter that I claim audience with the Hollow King to tender payment. And enter—" his voice steadied, finding the old cadence, the Old Tongue rising in him like water finding an old channel— "*let the Ledger show that the debtor comes willingly, and that no power under the mountain compelled him.*"

The nearest ledger flared white-hot along its spine, and somewhere far above, in the dark that pretended to be sky, something vast turned its attention toward the Hall of Accounts.

Wren bowed, many-fingered and grave. "Audience granted," it said. "The King will see you at the turning of the hour. And Master Sterling—you should know that the last debtor to stand before the Hollow King and offer his years was Seraphine Coldharbour herself."

The clerk's voice dropped to a fold of paper.

"She offered them, and he refused her, and that refusal is the only reason any of this can still end differently. He has regretted it for thirty years. Do not give him time to regret it again."

Far off, through corridors of bone and burning names, the great doors of the throne hall began to open—slowly, with a sound like winter deciding—and a voice like frost forming on glass called down the dark:

"*Bring me the man who signs his debts in another man's blood.*"
