## 7. An Audience with the Hollow King

The throne room of the Court Beneath the Mountain was not a room at all. It was a throat.

Dominic understood this as he walked it — the way one understands things in dreams, without surprise. The corridor had been narrowing for an hour, its walls of black glass sweating a faint warmth, and now it opened into a vast chamber ribbed like the inside of some enormous gullet, lit from below by veins of ember-orange that pulsed in slow rhythm. A heartbeat. The mountain's, or something older wearing the mountain like a coat.

The courtiers lined the walls on both sides, dozens of them, perhaps hundreds — it was impossible to count them because they would not hold still in his eye. They were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful: intricate, cold, and made of nothing that could survive being touched. Some wore faces he almost recognized. One had his mother's jaw. Another wore the smile of a girl he'd loved at nineteen, before Vellharrow, before the trinkets, before the long burial of himself. They were not imitating these faces out of cruelty, he realized. They simply had none of their own, and so they collected.

*The Hollow Court*, the Ashwarden had called them, back at the threshold, before the stair swallowed him. *They feed on what is owed. Mind your debts down there, Sterling. Every word you speak is either payment or borrowing.*

Dominic kept his hands empty and his mouth shut, and walked.

At the far end of the throat, raised on a dais of fused bone-glass, sat the Hollow King.

He was not large. That was the first wrongness. Dominic had expected enormity — the stories all agreed on enormity, a giant crowned in iron, a mountain given manners. But the thing on the throne was slight, almost delicate, draped in robes the color of deep water, and where its face should have been there was only a smooth expanse of pale skin, featureless as an egg, with a single crown of black iron floating an inch above its brow, turning slowly, never touching.

It did not need eyes. Dominic felt its attention settle over him like wet wool.

"Dom-in-ic Ster-ling."

The voice came from everywhere — from the walls, from the ember-veins, from inside his own skull, spoken in his own voice but slowed, stretched, each syllable handled like something fragile and valuable. The courtiers shivered in unison, a ripple running down both walls like wind through wheat.

"You have walked far," the King said. "You have read the Ledger. You have spoken your name to the door, and the door has remembered you. Sit."

There was no chair. But the floor beneath him softened, obligingly, into something like a seat, and Dominic — who knew better than to refuse hospitality in a place like this — sat.

"I've come about a debt," he said. His voice sounded very small in the great throat of the hall. "Not mine. My mistress's. Seraphine Vael."

The name landed in the chamber like a stone dropped into still water. The courtiers went rigid. The ember-veins beneath the floor stuttered, missed a beat, resumed.

The Hollow King tilted its blank head.

"Ser-a-phine," it said, tasting the syllables. "Dead two decades. And yet her name opens doors here still. Debts do not die with their makers, Sterling. They wait. They ripen. They find new throats to climb out of." The crown turned above its brow, slow as a millstone. "You carry her voice in your mouth. I can hear it. You learned your Old Tongue from her lips, did you not? Every word you speak is a little resurrection."

"She sold something to this court," Dominic said. "Thirty years ago. A piece of a soul. I've come to buy it back."

"Buy." The King's voice curled around the word, amused. "With what coin? What does a maker of harmless trinkets possess that the Court Beneath the Mountain wants?"

And there it was — the question Dominic had been circling for the entire descent, through the ledger halls where every name of every debtor glowed in its niche like a votive lamp, past the alcove where SERAPHINE VAEL burned in letters of cold gold with a single line beneath it: *PAID IN FULL — ONE LIFE, HELD IN TRUST.* He had stood before that entry for a long time. He had told himself it meant she had paid everything, settled everything, that the child was merely held until some clerk below remembered to release it.

But the Ashwarden's parting look had said otherwise. And the ledger-keeper, a bent grey creature with fingers like candlewicks, had traced the entry with one wick-finger and whispered: *Held in trust. Held for whom, do you think? Read the surety line, man. Read who co-signed.*

He hadn't needed to read it. He had felt his own name before he saw it.

"I know what I have," Dominic said quietly. "I've known since the ledger hall. You didn't take a soul-fragment from Seraphine thirty years ago. You took it from *her bargain* — and the bargain was me. My life. She bought my survival from something worse than you, and she paid you in advance for the keeping of it. The child downstairs isn't just a fragment of her soul. It's the receipt. It's the proof that I'm alive on borrowed time." His hands were steady. Twenty years of steadiness, practiced daily behind a counter full of harmless things. "My years are yours. They always have been. I've come to surrender them — all of them — and take the child home."

Silence. The true kind, the kind that has weight and temperature. The courtiers did not move. Even the ember-veins seemed to dim, holding their breath.

Then the Hollow King laughed.

It was a terrible sound, not because it was cruel but because it was warm — genuinely, horribly warm, the laugh of something that had waited a long time for a guest to say the right thing. The walls drank it in. The courtiers' borrowed faces flickered, and for half a heartbeat Dominic saw what was underneath them: nothing. Smooth pale shapes, patient as eggs, waiting to be filled.

"Ohh," the King breathed. "Oh, she chose well. She chose *so* well. Do you know how many come down here to haggle, Sterling? To plead? To offer me trifles — memories, first loves, the names of enemies? They come to *bargain*, as if the Court were a market stall." The crown stopped turning. "But you. You walk in and hand me the account already balanced. You read the surety line yourself. No one reads the surety line."

"Seraphine taught me to read contracts," Dominic said. "She said the Practice was mostly paperwork with better lighting."

Another laugh, shorter this time, almost fond. The King rose from the throne — and up close it was taller than it had seemed, or perhaps height meant nothing here — and drifted down the dais steps toward him, robes trailing like ink in water. The courtiers bowed as it passed, a wave of bowing following it down the hall.

"Then let us be precise, since precision is your inheritance." It stopped before him. This close, Dominic could see that the blank face was not entirely blank — there were features there, faintly, like footprints in snow that had almost melted. A suggestion of eyes. A ghost of a mouth. As if the King had once had a face and had spent it, long ago, on something important. "Your years are mine, held in trust, thirty years accruing. If you surrender them freely — and they must be *freely* given; compulsion spoils them, turns them bitter on the tongue — then the fragment goes free. The child walks out of my mountain whole. And you—" the ghost of a mouth curved, "—you become what you have pretended to be for twenty years. Ordinary. Utterly, permanently ordinary. No Practice will answer you again. Not a spark. Not a whisper. You will die as men die, when men die, and no door below will remember your name."

Dominic looked at the creature that owned the rest of his life.

He thought of the shop. Of the smell of beeswax and cedar, of the little brass birds and the luck-knots and the love-charms that worked only because people believed they did. He thought of twenty years of deliberately broken spells, of power folded away like winter clothes in summer, of the small gray grief he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his personality.

He thought of the child's grey eyes, and the word she had signed in the dust of his floor: *home.*

"Yes," he said. "That's the trade."

"Stand, then."

He stood. The floor hardened back to glass beneath his feet. The Hollow King raised one pale hand, and from the dark above the throne something began to descend — a shape at first formless, then resolving: a crown. Not the iron circlet above the King's brow, but another, larger, wrought of black metal threaded with veins of dull red light, turning slowly as it fell, trailing embers like blood in water.

"The Crown of Years," the King said softly. "Every surrendered life passes through it. It burns what is given and pours what remains into the debtor's vessel. Yours will be... a large burning, Sterling. Thirty years is a fortune. Most souls who stand where you stand offer a decade. A childhood. A single beloved memory of summer." The crown descended lower. The heat of it reached Dominic's face, and within the heat he could hear them — voices, thousands of them, faint, all the lives ever poured through the metal, singing or screaming or both. "Kneel, and it begins. Once begun, it cannot be undone. The Court does not give refunds."

Dominic knelt.

The glass was warm under his knees. Above him the crown turned, and turned, and the red veins brightened, and the voices in the metal swelled — and one voice among them, one thread in the vast chorus, cut through all the others like a needle through cloth.

A woman's voice. Dry, amused, unmistakable even after twenty years.

*"Dominic. Look at the crown before you kneel any further, you sentimental fool. Count the points."*

His breath stopped. He knew that voice. He had buried that voice with her body, or what they'd buried of her body, and it had no business being here, in the metal, unless—

Unless she had never fully been paid out. Unless a piece of her had gone into the Crown of Years itself, thirty years ago, the way a careful merchant hides a coin in the lining of a coat.

He looked up. Seven points on the crown. Six blazing red.

One dark. Cold. Empty. Waiting.

The Hollow King's blank face hovered close, and though it had no eyes, Dominic felt it watching him watch the crown — and felt, for the first time since he'd entered the mountain, something that might have been uncertainty in the vast slow pulse of the place.

"Do you consent?" the King asked.

Dominic smiled, and it was Seraphine's smile, and he gave it to the Court Beneath the Mountain like a blade laid flat across his palms.

"Yes," he said. "But I'll choose which point burns me."
