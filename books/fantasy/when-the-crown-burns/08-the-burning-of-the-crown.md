## 8. The Burning of the Crown

The Hollow King did not rise when the child entered his hall. That was the first thing Dominic understood about him—that he could not rise, that the throne and the man had long ago become a single thing, root and stone, so that what sat before them was less a king than a mountain wearing a memory of one.

The hall itself was vast in the way of things grown rather than built. Pillars of black basalt rose from floors veined with silver, and along every vein ran light, slow as blood through a sleeping body. The ceiling was lost in dark, but from it hung chains—hundreds of them, thousands—each ending not in a hook or a cage but in a small glass bell, and inside each bell floated a name, written in light, turning slowly like a moth in amber.

Dominic counted eleven bells before he made himself stop. He knew what they were. He had known since the Ledger Room, since the archivist's dry voice had read out *Sterling, Dominic — obligation active — collateral: forty-one years* and something in his chest had gone cold and quiet.

The child's hand was in his. Small fingers, cold as river stones. The child had not spoken since the hall doors opened—not in the Old Tongue, not at all—but walked forward with the unhurried certainty of someone returning to a house they had been told was theirs.

"Kneel," said the Hollow King, "or don't. It changes nothing."

His voice was the strangest thing yet. It came from everywhere—the pillars, the floor, the silver veins—as though the whole court were a throat and he merely the thought passing through it. His face, where it emerged from the shadow of the crown, was worn smooth as a riverbed pebble. No eyes that Dominic could find. Only a suggestion of features, the way a face appears in smoke if you want badly enough to see one.

"We've come to end it," Dominic said. His voice sounded thin in all that stone. "The bargain. The debt. All of it."

"End it." The king turned the words over like coins of doubtful minting. "Twenty-three years you have lived on borrowed time, Dominic Sterling. Do you know what happens to a debt when the debtor dies? It does not die with him. It passes down. To children. To children's children. Your mistress understood this. It is why she paid what she paid." A pause, and the light in the veins dimmed slightly, as if the mountain itself had drawn breath. "It is why she paid *you*."

"I know what she paid."

"Do you?" Something moved across the smooth face—amusement, perhaps, or its fossil. "Then say it. Say it aloud, here, in the hall of obligations, where lies curdle and truths ring. What did Merris Vane pay for your life?"

The name struck him like a hand across the face. He had not spoken it in twenty years. He had barely let himself think it. *Merris.* Her hands ink-stained to the wrist. Her voice saying the Old Tongue the way other people said prayers—casually, intimately, as though the words belonged to her mouth and no other.

"She paid her heir," Dominic said. "She sold a fragment of her soul to the Court Beneath the Mountain. And she gave—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "She gave me the years that were meant for that child. Forty-one years. I have been living them. Wearing them. They never fit."

"They fit beautifully," the king said. "That is the obscenity of stolen time. It fits better than your own. You have loved on it. Grown old gracefully on it. Made trinkets and called it penance on it." The smooth face inclined toward the child. "And now you come to give it back. Why?"

"Because it was never mine."

"That is not a reason. That is an observation." The king's voice softened into something almost gentle, which was worse. "Men do not surrender their lives because of observations. They surrender them for love, or guilt, or terror. Which are you, Dominic Sterling? Love? Guilt? Terror?"

Dominic looked down at the child. The child looked back, and in that grey, grave face he saw Merris so plainly that his knees nearly went. Not resemblance—something deeper. The way she had listened. The way she had waited for people to finish lying before she answered them.

"All three," he said. "But mostly the first one. She loved her. Before she ever sold her. And I think—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think the fragment remembers being loved. I think that's why she found me. Out of everyone in Vellharrow, out of every shop of harmless trinkets, she walked to my door."

The hall was silent. Even the bells seemed to stop their slow turning.

"Yes," the Hollow King said at last. "Yes. That is the truth of it, rung clean." He raised one hand—or the suggestion of one—and the nearest bell began to swing, though no wind moved in the hall. Its chime was not a sound so much as a recognition. "Then we proceed. But understand the terms, mortal, for the Court Beneath the Mountain does not forgive misunderstanding, only record it."

* * *

They stood in the center of the hall, on a disc of silver that the veins fed into like tributaries. The archivist had appeared from nowhere—a tall, grey figure with the Ledger floating before it—and now opened the great book to a page near its beginning, where the ink had faded to the color of old bruises.

*"Vane, Merris,"* the archivist intoned. *"One soul, divided. One fragment, held in trust. One debt of years, transferred to Sterling, Dominic, against the day of settlement. Terms of release: the collateral must be returned willingly, wholly, and by the hand that holds it."*

"Willingly," Dominic repeated. "Wholly. By my own hand."

"You will kneel," the king said, "and place your years upon the scale. All of them. Not the ones you wish to keep. There is no keeping. You will feel them leave—you will feel everything they carried leave with them—and when they are gone, you will be what you would have been without her sacrifice. A man of fifty-two who should be thirty-one. Ordinary. Unmarked. Alive, if the transfer takes cleanly. Dead, if you flinch."

"And if I flinch?"

"Then the fragment returns to the Court, and the child remains what she has been for twenty-three years: a held thing. A bell." The smooth face turned toward the ceiling, toward the thousands of hanging names. "We do not waste collateral, Sterling. We hang it where we can hear it."

Dominic followed the king's gaze upward and saw, really saw, the bells for the first time. Names. Thousands of names. People who had bargained and balked. People who had knelt at this silver disc and felt their years begin to drain and had cried out *stop, stop, I take it back*—and the Court had taken them at their word and kept the difference forever.

He thought of his shop. Of the little brass birds and the mirrors that showed nothing and the music box that played a tune no one had written. Twenty years of harmless trinkets. Twenty years of hiding.

*Merris,* he thought. *You absolute, magnificent fool. You should have let me burn.*

He knelt.

* * *

The transfer did not hurt the way he expected. It hurt the way grief hurts—which is to say, it hurt everywhere at once, and then in one specific place, and then everywhere again.

The years left him like water leaving a broken vessel, and each year took its cargo with it. He felt the winter he was thirty-four leave, and with it the memory of snow on the shop roof, the particular silence of Vellharrow at dawn. He felt thirty-seven go, and the taste of the tea he drank every morning of that year. Forty. Forty-one. Faces blurred. Names slipped loose. He gripped the child's hand—when had she taken it?—and held on to the present moment as though it were a rope over a drop, because the past was pouring out of him and taking the man who remembered it along.

He did not flinch. He wanted to—he flinched inwardly, screamed inwardly, as forty years of loving and losing and making small brass birds poured away—but his body stayed kneeling, stayed still, because some part of him older than the bargain knew that stillness was the price.

And then, beneath the draining, he felt something else. Something flowing *in.*

Not years. Not time. Something thinner and brighter. A thread of gold running backward up the channel the years had carved, filling the empty places not with memory but with *recognition*—the way a tuning fork hums when its twin is struck. The fragment. The piece of Merris that had been held in trust for twenty-three years, passing through him on its way home, touching every hollowed-out place inside him and knowing it, *knowing* him, the way she had always known him—

*Oh,* said a voice that was not a voice, in the Old Tongue, in the language of dead mistresses and mute children. *Oh, Dominic. You kept the shop.*

And then it was gone, home, and the silver disc went dark, and the Ledger closed with a sound like a door in a very large house.

* * *

The child stood alone in the center of the hall, and she was changing.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible, beautiful thing about it—it was quiet. Color came into her face like dawn coming into a room. Her spine straightened. Her eyes, grey and grave, deepened to the green-black of deep water, of Merris's eyes, and when she breathed it was the breath of someone who had been holding it for twenty-three years.

The Hollow King stirred on his throne, and for the first time in centuries, perhaps, the mountain seemed uncertain.

"The fragment is released," the archivist said, in the tone of one reading a weather report. "The debt of Vane is settled. The account of Sterling is—" A pause. The grey head tilted. "Closed."

"Closed," the king echoed. And then, softly, in a voice that was almost human: "No one has ever paid in full."

The child—no, not the child; the girl; the heir—turned to look at him. And when she spoke, her voice was rusty from disuse but unmistakably, irrevocably her own:

"You're crying," she said. "Why are you crying? You won."

"I don't remember winning," Dominic said. It was true. He was kneeling on cold silver in a hall of hanging names, and the last four decades were a fog with a few shapes in it, and one of those shapes was a woman with ink-stained hands whose name he could no longer quite reach. "I don't remember much of anything. Tell me—tell me who she was. Please. You'd know better than anyone. Tell me who I'm mourning."

The girl crossed the hall and knelt beside him, and took his face in her two small hands, and looked at him with Merris's eyes.

"I'll tell you everything," she said. "But not here. Because there's something you need to see first—something the King doesn't know I saw, in the Ledger Room, on the page after yours." Her voice dropped, and the silver veins in the floor flickered, as if the mountain were listening. "There's another name in the book, Dominic. Another debt. And it isn't settled. It's just begun."

Above them, in the dark, one bell—one single bell among the thousands—began, very softly, to ring.
