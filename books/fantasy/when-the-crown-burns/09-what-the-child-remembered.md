## 9. What the Child Remembered

The crown had burned to nothing, and still the light of it hung in the Hollow King's hall like a held breath.

Dominic knelt on the black glass floor of the Court Beneath the Mountain, and he was empty. Not hollow—the way a cup is not hollow when it waits to be filled—but emptied, deliberately, the way a man empties his pockets at a gate. Twenty stolen years had passed out of him in the burning of that crown, and what remained was a body that ached in every joint, hands spotted with age he had never earned honestly, and a silence inside where the Practice had lived.

He could not feel the Old Tongue anymore. He could not feel anything of it. The language that had once hummed beneath his skin like a second pulse was gone, and in its place was only Vellharrow: rain on slate, tallow smoke, the small honest weight of tin.

The child stood where the throne had been.

She was taller than she had been an hour ago—or she seemed so—and her eyes were no longer the flat grey of a thing unfinished. They were green. A particular green. The green of moss on the north side of standing stones, of bottle glass held up to candlelight, of eyes Dominic had last seen closing in a room that smelled of fever and burnt rosemary.

"Say something," he whispered. His voice cracked on it. "Please. Anything."

The child looked at him for a long moment. Then, in a voice that was small and clear and utterly unchildlike in its cadence:

"You always did kneel too quickly, Dominic. It made negotiating with you very dull."

* * *

Her name—her true name, the one that had been carved out of her and sold with the rest—came back to her in pieces, the way a shattered mirror returns a face.

They sat in a side gallery of the court while the mountain digested what had happened. Servants of smoke and bone drifted past the archways, pretending not to look. The Ledger of Names lay open on a plinth between them, its pages fluttering though there was no wind, and across the page where her fragment had been recorded for forty years, a new line was writing itself in ink the color of dawn:

*Vessarine Coldharbour. Heir entire. Debt discharged.*

"I remember being sold," she said. She sat with her hands folded in her lap like an old woman, though her face was perhaps ten years old. "That's the cruelest part of it. Everyone assumes a soul-fragment doesn't know. It knows. It lies in whatever vessel they keep it in—in my case, a girl-child bought from a river village—and it remembers everything the whole remembers, and it cannot say any of it." She touched her throat. "You wondered why I was mute. I wasn't mute, Dominic. I was *full*. There was no room left in me for anything of my own."

"The Old Tongue," he said. "When you came to the shop. You spoke it."

"That was the first thing to leak through. The oldest thing. Language outlasts everything—it was hers before it was mine, before it was anyone's." She smiled, and it was her mistress's smile, wry at one corner, and it hurt him worse than the burning had. "You taught me the word for *door*, do you know that? In the shop. You thought you were teaching me. You'd say it every morning when you unlocked the shutters—*door*, in the Old Tongue, under your breath, because you couldn't help yourself. Twenty years of hiding and you still said grace over your own prison."

Dominic looked down at his hands. They were an old man's hands now. He was fifty-three years old and felt seventy, and somewhere in the arithmetic of that was the truth he had not yet let himself say aloud.

"How much did she give them," he asked quietly. "For me."

Vessarine was silent a moment. The Ledger turned a page by itself.

"You've guessed."

"I've guessed."

"Then say it, and I'll tell you if you're right. That's how she would have done it. She never gave answers to questions a person hadn't earned."

He closed his eyes. The hall was warm from the burned crown; he could still smell it, ozone and iron and something sweet underneath, like honey left too long near a flame.

"She was dying," he said. "The fever, the winter you—" He stopped. Started again. "I was twenty-two. I'd been her apprentice four years. And the Warden's men came to Vellharrow, or they were going to come, someone informed on the house—I never learned who. And she went down here, to this court, and she made a bargain. Her life was already forfeit; she knew that. So she didn't bargain with her life." He opened his eyes. "She bargained with mine. With the years of it. She sold them the best part of me—twenty years, taken off the top, the years I'd have been strongest, most alive—and in exchange they hid me. Made me ordinary in their records. Unfindable. The Ashwarden's fire passed over the shop like I wasn't there, because as far as any ledger in this mountain was concerned, Dominic Sterling had already died at twenty-two."

"And the fragment of herself that she carried," Vessarine said softly, "she pledged alongside it. As surety. If the debt were ever called, both would come due together—her piece of soul and your stolen years, bound in one instrument." She laid her hand flat on the Ledger. "You were never collateral, Dominic. You were the principal. Your survival *was* the currency. Every year you lived safely in that shop, ordinary and hidden, the debt grew richer. This court fed on it for forty years. The Hollow King wore your life like a ring."

* * *

Later—hours later, or what passed for hours in a place without sky—she took him down to see the vaults.

It was not a request he could have refused, and she did not ask. She simply walked, and the court parted for her now, the smoke-servants bowing, the bone-wardens turning their featureless faces away. Heir entire. Whatever the Hollow King had been before the crown consumed him, the mountain recognized succession the way a lock recognizes a key.

The vaults were shelves. That was all. Miles of them, cut into living rock, and on each shelf a vessel: jars of amber, boxes of iron, birds' eggs, loaves of bread gone hard as stone, a single glass slipper filled with what looked like seawater. Soul-fragments. Debts. Pledges. Forty centuries of bargains struck by desperate people against desperate people, all of it warehoused here, feeding the court slowly, endlessly, the way a tap feeds a cistern.

"Most of these will empty now," Vessarine said. "A crown is a promise made to everyone at once. When it burns, the promises burn with it." She ran her fingers along a shelf of amber jars, and where she touched them, the things inside stirred like fish rising to feed. "But some are private instruments. Those survive. Those I will have to honor, or renegotiate, or break—and breaking has costs I'm only beginning to understand." She glanced back at him. "You see why I needed to wake first. Why the fragment had to be returned to me whole before anything else happened. Someone has to read the terms."

"You're going to stay," Dominic said. It was not a question. "Down here. Ruling this."

"For a time. Long enough to unwind what can be unwound." She stopped before one shelf, one jar—a small thing, clay, unremarkable—and did not touch it. "And then? I don't know. I have forty years of someone else's memories and none of my own. I may be my mother's heir, but I am also a girl of ten who has never seen the sun except through a shop window." She turned to face him fully, and the green eyes were wet. "Which brings me to the last thing I remembered. The thing I came up the mountain road to tell you, before I even knew why."

* * *

They stood at the mouth of the stair that wasn't there—the hidden door in the mountainside that had swallowed him three days and one lifetime ago. Beyond it, impossibly, evening light. Real light. The grey-gold of a Vellharrow dusk, and the smell of rain coming.

"She left you a message," Vessarine said. "Not in a letter. Letters burn. She left it in the fragment itself, buried deeper than the name, deeper than the memory of the sale. I only found it after the crown burned—when everything else in me came home, this came home too, and it's addressed to you, and it's the reason I walked to your door instead of a hundred others' doors in that city."

Dominic's throat worked. "Tell me."

"She said: *Tell him the years weren't stolen. Tell him I chose which ones.*"

The words landed in him like stones dropped down a well, and he waited for them to hit bottom, and they kept falling.

"She could have given them the feeble years," Vessarine went on, gently. "The decades past sixty. The court would have accepted them—they feed on quantity, not quality; they barely taste the difference. But she gave them your prime instead. Do you understand what that means? She didn't sell your best years to save your life, Dominic. She spent them to buy you the *rest* of it. Every year past fifty-three that you live—that you're about to live—is the part she kept for you. Guarded. Hidden in plain sight, in a shop full of harmless trinkets, behind a sign that says Small Mercies." She smiled again, and this time it was entirely her own, young and trembling. "You thought you'd been robbed. You were the inheritance."

Dominic put his hand against the cold rock of the doorway. Beyond it the rain had begun, soft on stone, and somewhere below—far below, at the foot of the mountain road—the lamps of Vellharrow were coming alight one by one, ordinary lamps for ordinary people, and one of them would be outside his shop, and no one would be waiting there, and for the first time in twenty years that thought felt like freedom instead of sentence.

"There's one more thing," Vessarine said.

He turned. She had gone very still, and the light from the Ledger, which she had brought with her rolled under her arm, was leaking out between the pages like heat from a forge.

"The private instruments. Most are small—debts of gratitude, vows of silence, a farmer's firstborn promised against a blight two hundred years dead. But one was sealed the same night as yours. Same hand. Same wax." She held the Ledger out to him, open to a page near the end, and her voice dropped. "It's dated forty years ago, Dominic. It's in her handwriting. And it names a beneficiary who is still alive."

The rain fell. The lamps burned. Dominic Sterling, an ordinary man with an old man's hands and a free man's heart, reached out and read the name.
