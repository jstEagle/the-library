## 10. The Ordinary Man

The morning after he came down from the mountain, Dominic Sterling woke in his own bed above the Shop of Small Mercies and did not know, for three full minutes, who he was.

It was not the forgetting of a blow to the head. It was a silence where a hum had always been. All his life there had been a second pulse beneath his own, a low warm thrum of borrowed years ticking somewhere behind his sternum — forty-one years that were never his, wound into him by a woman with ash-grey eyes on a night he had spent two decades pretending not to remember. He had grown so used to it that its absence felt like missing weather, like waking in a house where a clock that had ticked since childhood had finally stopped.

He lay still beneath the blanket and listened to Vellharrow wake around him: the carters shouting in Ropewalk Lane, the gulls quarrelling over the fish market, the bell of Saint Ferren's tolling the hour with its usual cracked note. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that would burn him alive if it ever learned what he had been.

*What I was,* he corrected himself, staring at the water-stained ceiling. *Not what I am.*

He sat up slowly, the way an old man sits up, and discovered with a strange, floating surprise that he was not an old man at all. His knees did not ache. His hands, when he held them up to the grey light coming through the shutters, were steady — steadier than they had been in years, actually, because whatever else had gone out of him in the dark beneath the mountain, the tremor of a man living on stolen time had gone too. The years had been returned. Given away, rather. Every one of them poured out of him into a small mute child who now walked the halls of the Court Beneath the Mountain wearing the face and name and inheritance of Ilsevane Coldharbour's true heir.

Dominic said her name aloud, once, to the empty room. "Ilsevane."

The word fell flat. No resonance answered it. For twenty years, saying certain words in this room had made the trinkets on the shelves below stir in their sleep — the brass compasses trembling in their cases, the glass beads whispering against one another like teeth. Now nothing stirred. Now it was only a word, and a room, and a man sitting alone in it.

He got dressed. That was the whole of the ceremony. Shirt, waistcoat, the good coat with the frayed cuffs because the other coat was for funerals and he could not have said why he thought of funerals at all.

* * *

The shop looked wrong to him, and it took him the length of the stairs to understand why.

The Shop of Small Mercies had always been, in truth, a shop of small concealments. Every harmless trinket on those shelves — the luck-bringers and love-charms and bottled blessings that Vellharrow's superstitious poor bought from him at a copper apiece — had been a little lie with a little magic folded inside it, enough to keep his hands practiced, his mind limber, the old language warm in some sealed cellar of himself. He had told himself for twenty years that it was prudence. A muscle unused atrophies; a mage who never works grows dangerous or mad. So he had worked, small and sly, in the cracks of a city that fed practitioners to the Ashwardens' pyres.

Now he stood in the doorway and felt the shelves the way a deaf man feels a bell.

Nothing. Not one spark. The compasses were only compasses. The beads were only glass. The little stoppered bottles of "blessed rain" were rain off the roof, and the luck-knots were cord, and the whole long inventory of his life's quiet deception had been reduced, in a single night beneath the mountain, to merchandise.

He walked the aisles slowly, touching things. A silver thimble engraved with a wren. A mirror that had shown customers their faces as their mothers loved them — now it showed only a tired man in a good coat, forty-odd, going grey at the temples, with eyes that looked newly washed. He turned from it quickly.

On the counter lay the ledger, open to yesterday's page. Sales: three luck-knots, a bottle of blessed rain, a tin of headache salve. In the margin, in his own cramped hand, a note he'd written before descending the stair that wasn't there: *If I don't come back, give the shop to Marta Tull and tell her the rent is paid through midwinter.*

Marta Tull was the washerwoman two doors down who bought a love-charm every month for a husband who had been dead six years. She was not going to inherit a shop. Dominic picked up the pen, crossed out the line, and wrote instead: *Sold all stock at half price. Closing.*

His hand shook only a little, and even that, he suspected, was ordinary human shaking, the kind any man's hand does when he writes the end of something.

* * *

By noon he had carried the sign down. THE SHOP OF SMALL MERCIES, painted twenty years ago by a sign-writer who had charged him double and died believing he'd painted a charm-shop's respectable cousin. Dominic leaned it against the wall inside the door and stood looking out at Ropewalk Lane through the glass, at the ordinary traffic of an ordinary street, and tried to feel what he had imagined he would feel: free.

He felt scraped. He felt the way a house feels after the furniture is gone.

Because here was the thing no one had warned him about, not the Hollow King with his ledger of names, not the child with Ilsevane's eyes opening like dawn in the dark of the court: a man can carry a secret so long it becomes the beam his roof rests on. Pull it out, and you must learn what holds the walls up instead.

For twenty years, everything had been organized around the hiding. The shop was a hiding. The trinkets were a hiding. His careful, unremarkable, punctual life — supper at seven, shuttered windows, no friends closer than arm's length — had been a fortress built around the one true thing about him, and now the one true thing was gone, given away gladly, given away without regret even now, and the fortress stood empty with its gates open and no enemy left to keep out.

A knock came at the door. He flinched — an old flinch, the flinch of twenty years, boots on stairs meaning Ashwardens — and then laughed at himself, quietly, and opened it.

It was a boy of perhaps ten, holding a chipped bowl. "Ma says, do you have anything for burns, mister? Da caught his arm on the kettle."

Burns. Dominic heard the word land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Once — yesterday, last week, all the years before — he would have sold her a salve with a thread of working in it, a whisper of the Old Tongue knotted into the grease, and the burn would have healed overnight without a scar, and no one the wiser, and the Practice would have gone unpunished and unpracticed-in-vain. Once, the small mercies had been real mercies, and he had told himself that made them worth the risk.

"I've a salve," he said. "Lavender and tallow. It'll ease the pain and keep it clean. The healing will take its own time — a week, maybe more. There may be a scar."

The boy's face fell the way children's faces do when magic turns out to be absent from the world.

"That's what healers say," he muttered.

"That's what the world says," Dominic answered, and gave him the tin, and took the copper, and closed the door, and stood with his back against it for a long moment, breathing.

There might be a scar. There would be scars everywhere now, he supposed, in a life without shortcuts. Scars on the boy's father's arm, scars on the city, scars under his own ribs where forty-one years had been poured out like water from a jug. And yet — he turned the copper over in his palm, an honest coin for an honest tin — there was something in the transaction that felt, for the first time, clean. Nothing hidden in it. Nothing owed.

* * *

In the afternoon he did what he had been avoiding since dawn. He climbed to the attic.

Ilsevane's things were up there. Had been up there for twenty years, wrapped in oilcloth, because he had never been able to bury them and never been able to look at them. Her grimoire, its cover scarred by fire that had never quite consumed it. Her ring of keys to a house in another country that no longer existed. A single pressed flower, white as bone, that she had worn in her hair the night she made the bargain — the night she sold a fragment of her soul to the Court Beneath the Mountain to buy the years that saved his life, and let him believe, all this time, that the debt was hers alone.

*Your survival was the currency,* the Hollow King had said, turning the great pages of the ledger with fingers like candlewax. *She did not pay with her soul, Dominic Sterling. She paid with your future. Forty-one years, signed over in your name while you slept. You have been spending her money all your life.*

And the child — the fragment, the remainder, the piece of Ilsevane that had been mute and waiting in the dark courts for two decades — had taken the years back at last, and woken whole, and looked at him with ash-grey eyes and spoken, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, the first words of her new life:

*Thank you for carrying them. They were heavy, weren't they?*

They had been heavy. God, they had been heavy.

Dominic knelt in the attic dust and unwrapped the oilcloth and took up the grimoire. His hands knew its weight the way his hands had once known the weight of the Old Tongue, and now they knew it only as weight, paper and leather and ash. He could not read it anymore. He understood that with a calm that surprised him: the language had gone out of him with the years, or the years had been the language, or it hardly mattered which. What Ilsevane had written there was locked now, forever, in a tongue only one living person could speak.

One living person. Down the mountain. Wearing her mother's inheritance like a crown that had already burned once and learned better.

He wrapped the book again, carefully, and did not put it back among the shadows. Instead he carried it downstairs, and found a stout wooden box, and packed it with the ring of keys and the pressed flower and the grimoire, and addressed it in plain block letters to a place he had only heard described in fragments across a dying woman's fire, decades ago: *The Court Beneath the Mountain. For the heir, Ilsevane Coldharbour.*

There was no courier he trusted with such a thing. But there was, he remembered, an old road east out of Vellharrow, past the Ashwardens' tower and the burning-fields, a road that went down eventually, if you walked it far enough, the way all roads in that country went down eventually. He had seen it from the mountain's mouth two nights ago, lit by the first honest sunrise of his life.

He set the box by the door. Then he took the crossed-out ledger page, tore it out, and wrote a new notice for the window:

CLOSED. THANK YOU FOR TWENTY YEARS OF SMALL MERCIES. THE PROPRIETOR HAS GONE TO SETTLE AN OLD ACCOUNT.

It was even true. That was the luxury of being ordinary: you could tell the truth, and no one believed you anyway.

* * *

He left at dusk, when the lamps were being lit along Ropewalk Lane and the gulls were settling on the fish market roofs. He locked the shop door and hung the key on a nail beside it, in plain sight, because there was nothing inside worth guarding anymore except glass and cord and rainwater, and because an unlocked door was the most ordinary thing in the world.

At the corner he stopped and looked back once. The Shop of Small Mercies stood dark and honest in the lamplight, its windows full of nothing but itself.

"Goodbye," Dominic Sterling said aloud, and found that he meant it, and found also — walking east into the blue evening, an ordinary man with an extraordinary box under his arm, his footsteps making the plain flat sound of anyone's footsteps on anyone's street — that grief and freedom could sit in the same chest without crowding each other at all.

Behind him, Vellharrow's bells rang the hour. Ahead, the road went down.

And far beneath the mountain, in a hall where a crown had burned and a ledger lay closed at last, a child with ash-grey eyes lifted her head as if she had heard her name spoken very far away — and smiled, and began, in the Old Tongue, to write a letter of her own.
