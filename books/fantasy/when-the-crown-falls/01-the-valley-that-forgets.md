## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The frost had come early that year, and it came the way most things came to the Hollow Valley — quietly, without asking anyone's opinion.

Ines Vane walked the border at first light, when the mist still hung in the folds of the hills like laundry no one had bothered to take in. The boundary stones ran from the northern crag down to the river shallows, twenty-one of them, each taller than a man and older than any record the town clerks kept. They were grey as old teeth, and they hummed. Not a sound you heard so much as felt, a pressure behind the eyes, like standing too close to a struck bell that never finished ringing. The valley folk said you got used to it. Ines had lived here twenty-six years and he had never gotten used to it. He had only learned to carry it the way a man carries a limp — folded into his stride until it became part of the walk.

His breath came out in white ribbons. His boots crunched on ground that had not yet decided whether it was autumn or winter. He carried a spear he'd owned for eleven years — ash haft, iron head, a notch near the socket from the one time a wolf had tested him and lost — and a short sword he almost never drew, because drawing it meant something had already gone wrong, and in the Hollow Valley things almost never went wrong. That was the point of the valley. That was the whole arrangement.

The town of Marrow's Rest paid him four silver a month to walk this line and report anything unusual. Unusual meant: fire where no fire had been, strangers who would not give their names, birds flying the wrong direction, weather that behaved like weather somewhere else. Ines had made this walk every morning for eleven years, and in eleven years he had filed exactly four reports. Three of them had been about weather. The fourth had been about a dead deer with no wound on it, which the priest, Maren, had examined and declared "a natural death, merely an unusual one," which Ines had always felt was the kind of sentence that meant the priest didn't know either.

He passed the seventh stone and nodded to it. He did this every morning, though he would have denied it under torture. The seventh stone had a crack running through it like a river seen from very high up, and it was the closest thing the valley had to a shrine, because the god the valley had once worshipped — the god whose name had been spoken in a tongue that predated the kingdom, the god who had gone silent three hundred years ago and never spoken again — had supposedly touched that stone last, before the silence. The crack was said to be a fingerprint.

Ines did not pray to the god the way the town prayed, which was not at all, or the way the pilgrims used to pray, which was loudly and in crowds. He prayed the way a man talks to a father who has gone deaf: without expectation, without audience, in a low voice, on a schedule, because the habit was the only part of the relationship still alive.

"Morning," he said to the stone, quietly, in the old tongue's one word he knew. *Vael.* It meant *here*, or *present*, or *I am* — Maren had told him that once, twenty years ago, when he was a boy asking questions she no longer answered. He said it every morning. He had no idea whether it meant anything to anyone. That had never seemed to him a reason to stop.

* * *

The town of Marrow's Rest sat in the valley's belly like a stone in a fruit: necessary, unremarkable, and mostly ignored. It had a mill, a smithy, two taverns, a chapel that had been built for a god who no longer attended, and a population that had learned, over generations, the particular skill of forgetting. They forgot the war to the east. They forgot that the kingdom's banners had once flown from their chapel roof. They forgot that magic had ever existed anywhere, because magic could not cross the boundary stones, and what cannot enter a place eventually becomes a rumor, and what becomes a rumor eventually becomes a story told to children, and children grow up and stop believing it, and then the story dies, and then the forgetting is complete.

Ines was not from the valley. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows which house on a street is older than the rest — not discussed, simply understood. He had arrived at the age of nine, carried through the northern pass by a trader who claimed to have found him wandering, and the town had done the decent thing: fed him, housed him, taught him a trade, and never once, in twenty-six years, made him feel that any of it was more than a transaction. He had a room above the farrier's. He had a standing order at the baker's. He had a chair at the Salt Cellar tavern that no one else sat in, partly from courtesy and partly because the chair was near the door and the door let in a draft and no one wanted it.

He was tolerated. He knew the word for it, even if the town didn't use it. Tolerated the way a scar is tolerated: part of the body now, acknowledged only when it aches.

He went to the chapel on the last day of every month. He was the only one who did. Old Maren still kept the candles lit — two of them, on the altar where the god's statue had stood before the statue was removed and sold, or buried, or melted down; no one remembered which, and the ones who might have remembered had decided it was kinder to forget. Ines would sit in the empty chapel for an hour, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say to a god who could not answer, and because the silence itself had become, over the years, a kind of conversation. You speak. I am here. You cannot speak. I am still here.

He had sworn an oath to that silence when he was seventeen. He'd said the words in the old tongue, the only prayer Maren had ever taught him whole, and she had gone white as chalk when he finished and told him never to say it again, and he never had. But the words lived in him like a stone in a shoe. *Vael en torath. Vael en mira.* Here and present. Here and listening. He didn't know what the rest of it meant. He had said it anyway, and something in the room — not the god, the god was silent, the god had been silent for three hundred years — something had *listened*, and he had felt it the way you feel a door open in a far room: a change in the air, nothing more.

He had told no one. There was no one to tell.

* * *

The eleventh stone stood where the valley narrowed toward the river, and it was there, in the grey half-light with the mist burning off the water, that Ines stopped walking.

At first he thought it was a trick of the frost — the way light caught ice on stone could make patterns, could make letters, could make you see things. He had walked past this stone four thousand mornings. He knew every lichen on it, every chip, the way its shadow fell at each hour. And this morning the shadow was wrong, because the stone itself was wrong, because across its face, at the height of a man's chest, was a mark like a handprint made of char.

He touched it before he thought better of it. The stone was cold — the stones were always cold, colder than the air, cold as if they remembered a winter the valley had never had — but the mark was *warm*. Warm as skin. Warm as something that had stood there recently, pressing itself against the boundary, and had not been stopped.

Ines took his hand back and stood very still and made himself look properly, the way he'd been trained to look, the way eleven years of patrols had taught him: not at the thing but around it. The frost on the ground in front of the stone had melted in a rough semicircle, a body's width, maybe two. The grass beneath was scorched in a pattern like a bird's footprint — three toes forward, one back. And there, caught on a splinter of the stone's cracked edge, was a thread. Fine. Grey. Not wool, not linen, not silk. It caught the light strangely, the way spider silk does when it doesn't want to be seen.

Something had come *to* the stone. Something had pressed against it, the way he pressed against the seventh stone every morning, and the boundary had not stopped it, because the boundary stopped magic, and whatever this was had not been stopped, and Ines's skin crawled with the specific horror of a man who has guarded a wall his whole life and has just discovered the wall has a door in it.

He crouched. He did not touch the thread. He looked at the scorch mark again and felt, beneath the fear, something he did not have a name for — a recognition, faint and awful, like hearing a voice in another room say a word you know from a dream. The mark was not random. It had a shape. He had seen the shape before, or something like it, in the crack of the seventh stone, in the pattern frost made on the chapel window in deep winter, in the grain of the wood of the pew where he sat every month.

*Vael en torath,* said something in the back of his skull, in a voice that was not a voice. *Here and present.*

"Report it," he said aloud, to himself, because the valley was empty and the god was silent and there was no one else. "Report it to the clerk. Weather, unusual. Or: mark on stone, origin unknown." Four silver a month. Four silver, and a room, and a chair, and a town that tolerated him, and a god that didn't answer. That was the life. That was the whole arrangement, and the arrangement had worked for eleven years because he had never once found a door in the wall.

He stood there until the sun cleared the crag and the mist burned away and the mark on the stone stopped glowing warm and became, simply, a mark. Then he walked on, because the patrol had to be finished, and finished it — stones twelve through twenty-one, nothing, nothing, nothing, the river, the turn, the long walk home.

* * *

The Salt Cellar at midday was warm and loud in the way of places that are warm and loud because everyone in them has known each other since birth. Ines took his chair by the door. The draft found his neck. He ate the stew, which was mutton and turnip and the same as every day, and he listened to the talk, which was about the miller's roof and the price of wool and whether the winter would be hard, and he said nothing, because he had nothing to add that anyone wanted, and because the mark on the eleventh stone sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

Tam Weller, the smith's boy, caught him on the way out. Tam was nineteen, broad, cheerful, one of the few who spoke to Ines without the particular flatness the town reserved for him.

"Walk with me a bit?" Tam said. "Da wants to know if you'll take a commission. Gate hinge, and the post it's set in — he says the wood's gone soft and he wants a soldier's eye on where to set the new one."

"I'm not a soldier."

"You're the closest thing we've got." Tam grinned. "Come on, then. You can tell me if the winter'll be hard. You're always first up the crag; you see the frost first."

Ines walked with him. It was, he supposed, the closest thing to friendship the valley had ever offered him: being useful, being asked, walking beside someone for a quarter mile in the cold. He found, to his own surprise, that he was glad of it, and that the gladness frightened him more than the scorch mark had, because things you're glad of can be taken, and he had learned early — nine years old, carried through a pass by a stranger — that everything you have can be taken, and the only defense is to need nothing.

"The winter'll be hard," he said. "Frost came early and deep."

"Da says the same. Says the stones are colder than he's ever felt them." Tam said it lightly, but Ines felt the words land like a dropped hammer. *The stones are colder.* He thought of the warm handprint. He thought of the thread that didn't want to be seen.

"Tam." He stopped at the smithy gate. "If you ever saw something on the stones — something that shouldn't be there — who would you tell?"

Tam laughed. "The clerk, same as you. Or Maren, if it was strange-strange. Why? Did you see something?"

"No," Ines said. It wasn't even a lie, not exactly. He had seen something, but he hadn't decided yet what it was. "Hypothetically."

"Then I'd tell Maren. She's the only one who remembers there's anything to forget." Tam clapped him on the shoulder — a real touch, warm through the coat, and Ines stood a moment after the boy had gone inside, feeling the place on his shoulder like a brand, like evidence that he had been touched by someone who meant it.

He went home to his room above the farrier's. It was a small room, clean, cold, containing a bed, a table, a chair, a spear rack, and a window that looked north toward the pass through which he had arrived twenty-six years ago and through which, in twenty-six years, no one had ever come looking for him. He had checked, in the early years. He had watched that pass the way a stray dog watches a road. No one had come. Eventually he had stopped watching, and that had been the loneliest day of his life, though he hadn't understood it at the time. He had thought it was relief.

He sat at the table. He took out the report paper — the clerk's good paper, rationed, meant for weather — and he wrote: *Eleventh stone. Scorch mark, hand-shaped, warm. Thread, unknown fiber. Ground disturbed in semicircle. Recommend examination.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then he thought of the clerk's face, the flat polite face, the face that would say *Vane, you've been out in the cold too long*, and the report would go in a drawer, and the drawer would be forgotten, because the valley forgets, that is its whole nature, that is its gift and its arrangement — and whatever had pressed its hand against the eleventh stone would press again, somewhere else, somewhere no one was watching, and the door in the wall would open wider.

He burned the report in the candle. He watched it curl. Then he took his cloak down from its peg, because Maren's chapel was on the way to nowhere, and it was not the last day of the month, and he was going anyway.

* * *

The chapel was cold and smelled of tallow and dust and, beneath that, something older — stone that had been prayed in for centuries, stone that remembered. Maren was seventy, maybe older; her age had stopped being a number and become a quality, like the stones. She was polishing the altar rail with a rag, slowly, the way she did everything, and she looked up when he came in and did not seem surprised.

"You're early," she said. "It's not the last day."

"I know."

She set down the rag. She looked at him the way she had looked at him when he was nine and she was the one who'd insisted the town take him in — the look of a woman who had made a bargain once, long ago, and had been watching the interest accrue ever since.

"Something happened on the line," she said. Not a question.

Ines took the scrap of grey thread from his pocket — he had gone back for it, after the smithy, before the tavern, telling himself it was evidence, telling himself it was nothing — and laid it on the altar rail between them. It caught the candlelight and held it wrong, the way it had on the stone.

Maren did not touch it. She leaned close, and her breath stopped, and Ines watched the color leave her face in the space of three heartbeats, watched seventy years of composure crack like ice on a spring river.

"Where," she said. Her voice had gone thin.

"Eleventh stone. By the river narrows."

"When."

"This morning. The mark was still warm."

Maren closed her eyes. Her lips moved, and Ines realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the chapel, that she was praying — actually praying, the first time he had ever seen her do it, in a language that was not the valley's, not the kingdom's, a language that sounded like water over stones, like wind through a crack in a door.

When she opened her eyes, she looked at him differently. Not the way she looked at the foundling. Not the way she looked at the mercenary. The way you look at a door you thought was painted shut and have just seen move in its frame.

"Ines," she said, and her voice was steady again, which frightened him more than the trembling had, because steadiness like that is built on top of something. "How long has it been since you said your oath?"

"Twenty years. I've never said it again. You told me not to."

"Have you dreamed it? The words. Have you dreamed them?"

He had. Every night for twenty years, the same dream, the same two phrases turning over in the dark like stones in a riverbed. He had never told anyone. He told her now, and watched her go white again, and watched her grip the altar rail with both hands as if the chapel were moving under her.

"The mark was warm," she said, half to herself. "The boundary let it press. Three hundred years the stones have held, and the boundary let it *press*—" She stopped. She straightened. She became, with visible effort, the priest again, the keeper, the woman who had decided decades ago what she was willing to know.

"Go home," she said. "Bolt your door. Come back at dusk and I will tell you what I should have told you twenty years ago, when you first said those words in my chapel and something answered."

"Something answered?" Ines's voice came out strange. "You said the god was silent. You told me the god went silent three hundred years ago."

"The god is silent," Maren said. She picked up her rag. Her hands were not quite steady. "I never said nothing else was listening."

Ines walked home through streets going blue with dusk, past windows lighting up one by one, past doors closing against the cold, past the whole warm, forgetting, tolerated shape of the life he had built out of needing nothing. He climbed the stairs to his small clean room. He bolted the door, as she'd said. He sat on the bed with his spear across his knees, and he did not light the candle, and outside, far off toward the river narrows, toward the eleventh stone, something in the dark gave a single low sound — not a wolf, not wind, not anything the valley had a name for — and Ines, who had spent his whole life guarding a border between the world and the world, understood with perfect clarity that whatever it was had already crossed.

The knock, when it came, was small. A child's knock. Three soft taps, like a heartbeat.

He sat very still, spear in his hands, and the taps came again — patient, weak, unmistakable — and Ines Vane, who had spent eleven years filing reports about weather, rose and unbolted the door.
