## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The knocking began at the edge of hearing, the way important things always do.

Ines Vane was at his table with a whetstone and a knife that had seen better decades, working the edge in long, patient strokes, when the sound reached him — not a knock yet, more a suggestion of one, a soft arrhythmic tapping that might have been a branch, might have been wind-driven grit against the shutter. The Hollow Valley taught a man to dismiss such sounds. The valley was full of them: wind in the pines, the groan of the mill, old Tamsen's dog barking at nothing by the well. A place that forgot things as easily as the Hollow forgot them did not reward a man for listening too closely.

But Ines listened anyway. It was the one habit from his oath he had never managed to break.

The whetstone stopped. The knife lay quiet under his palm. Outside, the light was failing in that particular way it did in late autumn — not fading so much as draining, the color pulled down out of the sky like water through sand, until the mountains on either side of the valley were nothing but jagged silhouettes and the sky above them the color of an old bruise going yellow at the seam. He had lit no lamp. He rarely did until full dark. Lamplight invited people to knock.

The knock came again. Three times. Weak. A fist too small to make a proper fist.

He sat still for a long moment, letting the silence of the house settle back over him like a blanket he knew every fold of. The house was small and clean and almost empty: a table, two chairs, a bed, a hearth, a shelf of things that mattered to no one but him — his father's compass with its dead needle, a clay figure of the silent god with its face worn smooth, the folded gray sash of his order, kept in a chest he opened perhaps twice a year. He had built the house himself, or mostly himself, with help he had paid for and thanked and never asked for again. It stood at the end of the last lane in Hollowbridge, past the tannery, where the smell of the vats kept casual callers away and the only neighbors were the crows and, beyond the fence, the darkening fields that ran up toward the boundary stones.

People in town said Ines Vane lived like a man waiting for something. He had heard them say it, in the tavern, in the low careful voices people used when they spoke about him, as if his hearing were not famously good. He had never corrected them, because they were wrong. He wasn't waiting for anything. Waiting implied expectation. He had simply arranged his life the way a man arranges a room — everything where it could be found in the dark, nothing that could be knocked over, no sharp edges left out for anyone to fall against. Himself included.

The knock came a third time, and then a sound that was not a knock at all: a small body sliding down wood, a soft collapse, the scrape of something — someone — settling against his door like a sack of grain whose rope had finally given out.

Ines swore, quietly, in the way of a man who has had years of practice at keeping his swearing quiet, and went to the door.

* * *

The child was maybe eight, maybe ten — it was hard to tell, because the boy was so thin that age had nothing to hold on to. He wore no coat, though the evening had teeth in it. His clothes had been good once, a fine weave gone gray with road dust, and his shoes had been ruined by the road itself: the left one split along the sole, the right one missing entirely, the foot beneath it wrapped in strips of what had been, Ines realized with a small cold turn in his stomach, a sleeve cut from the boy's own shirt. His hair was dark and matted. His lips were cracked and gray. And he was burning — Ines felt the heat of him before he even touched him, radiating off the small body like a stone pulled from a fire, a fever so far past the point where fevers usually killed that the boy's survival in the open air of the lane seemed less like luck than like stubbornness of an almost offensive quality.

"Pell." The boy said it as Ines crouched, or Ines thought he said it — the word came out cracked and slurred, and it might have been his name, or might have been something else entirely. "Pell. Pell."

"That's not a word," Ines said, and then felt like a fool for arguing with a dying child. He got an arm under the boy's shoulders. The child weighed almost nothing. Lifting him was like lifting a bundle of kindling, if kindling trembled and muttered and clutched at your collar with fingers that had no strength in them but would not, could not, let go.

The boy was speaking. Not the cracked repetition of his name — other words now, a stream of them, low and fast and falling over each other, the way a man talks in a dream he cannot wake from. And the words were wrong. Not slurred, not fever-mangled — *wrong*, shaped in a mouth that had never been taught how to make them, vowels that bent in places the valley's speech had no bends, consonants that clicked and hissed in patterns that made the hairs on Ines's arms stand up under his sleeves. It was a language. It was unmistakably a language, with its own grammar and its own weight and its own terrible music, and Ines Vane, who had served on the borders of three provinces and heard every trader's tongue that came through the pass towns, had never heard anything like it in his life.

No one in the Hollow Valley spoke like this. No one in the Hollow Valley *could*. The valley did not keep out travelers — travelers came, bought flour, sold knives, moved on. But this was not a traveler's tongue. This was something older, something that did not belong to any road Ines had ever walked.

He carried the boy inside and shut the door behind him with his heel, and the click of the latch sounded, in the quiet that followed, like a decision being made.

* * *

The fever burned all night.

Ines did what his mother's memory told him to do, though his mother had been dead twenty years and had never taught him much beyond bread and stubbornness. He boiled water. He stripped the ruined clothes off the boy and put him in the bed — *his* bed, which felt strangely like handing someone a knife — and bathed the small burning body with cool cloth, over and over, wringing the cloth out and starting again, the rhythm of it almost like prayer, which was probably why it steadied him. He got a little broth into the boy's mouth, most of which came back out. He built the fire up and let it fall and built it up again.

And all night, the boy talked.

He talked in that wrong, old language, hour after hour, his voice wearing itself down to a thread and then, impossibly, finding more thread. Sometimes he wept while he talked. Sometimes he laughed, a small terrible laugh that was worse than the weeping. Sometimes he went silent for so long that Ines put down the cloth and bent close to check his breathing, and in those silences the boy's face changed — went still and listening, eyes moving under the lids as though he watched something happen somewhere Ines could not follow.

Once, near the black middle of the night, the boy's eyes opened. They were dark and enormous in the firelight, and they were not, Ines thought, entirely *present* — the look in them was the look of someone standing in a doorway, half in one room and half in another. The boy looked at Ines. Ines looked back. Neither of them moved.

Then the boy spoke again, and this time one of the words was different.

It came out of the stream of that unknown tongue like a stone surfacing in a flood — a single word in a language Ines knew, except that it was not quite the valley's word either. It was older. It had more weight. And Ines knew it, knew it in the place a man keeps the things he has sworn by, because he had said it himself, on his knees, at seventeen, with an old woman's hand on his head and the gray sash of the order being tied around his waist in a chapel so small it had barely deserved the name.

*Vessin.*

*I am the vessel. The god is silent, and I am the vessel of that silence. What the god cannot carry, I will carry. What the god cannot speak, I will hold unspoken.*

He had not said the oath aloud in fifteen years, but it lived in him the way a splinter lives in a palm — too deep to draw, too much a part of him to cut out. And the word *vessin* was the root of it, the old formal word the liturgy used, the word the priest had translated for him with a dry smile: *the thing that holds. The cup. The vessel.*

The boy said it again, looking straight at him, and this time there was nothing feverish or wandering in his eyes at all. He said it the way you say a name you have been searching a crowd for.

Then his eyes closed, and the fever took him back down, and Ines sat on the floor beside the bed with his back against the wall and did not sleep for the rest of the night.

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, the way it did when the valley was in one of its moods — the light arriving not from the east but from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless, the whole world lit like the inside of a pearl. Ines stood at his window with a cup of tea he had forgotten to drink and watched the town wake below him: smoke from the baker's, the tannery's first stink of the day, old Tamsen leading his mule out to the far field. Ordinary. Deliberately, stubbornly ordinary. The Hollow Valley was good at ordinary. It was the one thing the valley grew better than barley.

Behind him, in his bed, the child breathed. Slow, but even. The worst of the fever had broken somewhere in the small hours, the heat banking down from furnace to ember, and now the boy slept the flat, graceless sleep of the truly exhausted, one arm flung out, his ruined feet poking from under the blanket. Ines had wrapped the bare foot in clean linen during the night. He could not quite account for when he had decided to do that.

He should wake the priest. That was the sensible move. Take the boy to Maren at the chapel — she was old, she was sharp, and she was the only person in the valley who had ever shown any interest in the things Ines kept quiet. She would know what to do with a child who spoke a language that did not exist. She would know what to do with the word *vessin*, if Ines could bring himself to say it aloud, which he was not at all sure he could.

He should also, said a colder and more practical voice in the back of his head, ask how the boy had come to be at his door at all. Hollowbridge was a half-day's walk from the boundary stones. The boy had no coat, no right shoe, no provisions a child could have carried. Which meant someone had carried him, or driven him, or he had walked farther than any fevered child should be able to walk, in the dark, in the cold, on one shoe. And that meant — Ines's jaw tightened — that someone had wanted him here. Or had wanted him gone from wherever he had come from, badly enough that here, at the edge of everything, at the door of the town's least-loved mercenary, was better than wherever he had been.

He looked at the boy again. In sleep, the terror had gone out of his face, and what was left underneath was so ordinary it hurt — chapped lips, a smudge of dirt on one cheek, the small unconscious slackness of a child who trusted the room he was in. Trusted it. Trusted *him*, apparently, enough to sleep like the dead in a stranger's bed.

Ines set down the untouched tea. He got the good blanket from the chest — the one he never used — and laid it over the boy, tucking it with a care that felt, from the inside, like watching a stranger's hands move.

"You picked the wrong door," he told the sleeping child, quietly, in the valley's plain speech. "There's nothing here. There never has been."

The boy did not answer, of course. But as Ines turned away, the child murmured something in that old, wrong, beautiful language — a single phrase, soft as an exhale — and one of the words in it was *vessin* again, and Ines stood with his back to the bed for a long time, not moving, while outside the pearl-gray light of the Hollow Valley crept a little further across the floor.

He would go to Maren. He would go this morning, as soon as the boy could be moved or as soon as Maren could be fetched. He told himself that firmly, the way you tell a horse to stand, and began to believe it.

It was almost a relief when, an hour later, he looked out the window and saw old Maren herself already toiling up his lane, leaning on her stick, her face set like a woman walking toward a fire — as if, Ines thought with a cold settling in his gut, she had known before he did that she was needed.
