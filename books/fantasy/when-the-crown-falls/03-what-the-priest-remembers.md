## 3. What the Priest Remembers

Maren had not climbed his lane in nine years.

Ines knew this the way he knew most things in the Hollow Valley — not because anyone told him, but because he watched. Watching was most of what a mercenary was, when you boiled him down: a man paid to notice. He noticed that the priest's stick bit into the frozen mud at an angle that said her hip was worse than she admitted. He noticed that she had dressed for a journey longer than the walk from the shrine — a heavy traveling cloak, a satchel that clinked faintly with glass and iron, wool wraps over her hands though the morning was merely cold, not cruel. And he noticed that she did not once look back toward the town behind her, as if whatever she carried in that satchel mattered more than anything she was leaving.

He opened the door before she could knock. It seemed kinder than making her stand.

"You sent no word," he said.

"I sent my knees," Maren said. "They arrived yesterday evening, screaming." She looked up at him from beneath the hood, and her eyes were the same pale gray as the winter river — water that moved but never warmed. "And then a child came to me in a dream, speaking backward. So I thought I'd better come while my legs still answered to me."

Ines stepped aside to let her in. The warmth of the hearth rolled out to meet them both, and Pell, who had been sitting where Ines had left him — on the bench by the fire, wrapped in the oldest blanket Ines owned, a bowl of barley broth untouched at his feet — lifted his head.

The boy's fever had broken sometime in the small hours. That should have been a mercy. Instead it had left him awake and clear-eyed and silent in a way that was worse than the delirium, watching everything with the patient attention of something wild deciding whether a clearing was safe. Now those eyes fixed on Maren, and Ines saw the boy's whole body go taut as a bowstring.

"Easy," Ines said quietly, and was surprised to find he meant it.

Maren stopped three paces inside the door. She stood very still, the way you stand when you've come upon a bird on a nest, and after a long moment Pell's shoulders came down half an inch. Not trust. Something less than trust. But the bowstring slackened.

"He knows me," Maren murmured.

"He doesn't know anyone."

"Child," Maren said, ignoring Ines entirely, "I'm going to sit down now, and it's going to take a while, and you're going to watch me do it like it's a play at the harvest fair. Don't feel obliged to applaud."

She lowered herself onto the chair Ines dragged over, arranged her satchel in her lap, and only then looked up at him. The humor drained out of her face like water out of a cracked cup.

"Ines Vane. How long has he been here?"

"Since dusk yesterday."

"And before that?"

"Before that he wasn't mine to know about."

"Don't fence with me. I'm old and I haven't the patience and you'll only win, which will annoy us both." Her voice dropped. "How long have people been looking for him?"

The question went through Ines like a splinter finding its depth. He thought of the scorch marks on the boundary stones. He thought of the way the boy had come out of the dark already running, as if the dark itself were chasing.

"I don't know," he said. "But something crossed the border recently. Burned stone. No tracks I could read."

Maren closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she began unbuckling the satchel with hands that were steady in the way of things that have decided to be steady rather than things that are.

"Then we'd best hurry," she said. "Bring him to the table. Bring the light close. And Ines—" she caught his sleeve as he turned, her grip stronger than her frame suggested, "—whatever happens in this room this morning, you do not speak of it to anyone in the town. Not to the miller who buys your loyalty with bread. Not to the widow whose roof you mended. Not even to yourself, out loud, alone. Do you understand me?"

"No," Ines said honestly.

"I know," she said. "Sit down anyway."

* * *

Pell came to the table when Ines held out his hand, which was itself a small astonishment — the boy had flinched from every offered touch since arriving, taking food only when Ines set it down and stepped away. But now he slid off the bench and crossed the floor and put his small, fever-warmed fingers into Ines's scarred palm as if the arrangement had been agreed upon somewhere Ines hadn't been present for.

Up close, in good light, the boy was stranger than fever explained. His hair was white-blond at the temples, though he could not have seen more than eight or nine winters, and the color ran back into ordinary brown like frost retreating across a field. There was a mark at the base of his throat, below the collarbone — a small crescent of skin darker than the rest, smooth as a scar but shaped too deliberately, too cleanly, to be any wound Ines knew. And his eyes, in the full morning light from the window, were not quite brown. They were brown with something moving underneath, the way a riverbed is brown until the sun hits it right and you see the current.

Maren saw all of it. Ines watched her see all of it. He watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Say something to him," she said at last. Her voice had gone thin.

"He doesn't answer—"

"I know he doesn't answer *you*. Say something anyway. Anything. Ask him his name."

"Pell," Ines said. "That's what he calls himself. Or what they called him. I don't know which."

"Not that name." Maren's hand had gone to the crescent pendant at her own throat — the sign of the Silent God, worn smooth by sixty years of thumbing. "Ask him in the old form. The way the litany does it. *Who walks.*"

Ines stared at her. "You retired the old forms before I ever swore my oath. Nobody speaks them."

"Somebody does," Maren said. "*Who walks*, Ines. Please."

It felt absurd, kneeling slightly at the table's edge to bring himself level with the boy, asking a question in a dead cadence of a dead tongue to a fever-child who had appeared out of the night like something blown through a torn screen. But Ines had spent his life doing precisely such absurd things — kneeling, reciting, keeping faith with a god who had never once answered — and so the words came easier than he expected, rising out of some memorized stratum of his boyhood:

"*Sha veth kelan?*" Who walks?

Pell went utterly still.

Then the boy spoke — and it was not the broken, wandering syllables of the night before, the fever-language that had sounded like water over stones. This was deliberate. This was a sentence, laid out carefully, the way a child lays out treasures on a floor to show a guest. His voice was hoarse from illness but steady, and the sounds were round and deep and old in a way that made the hair rise along Ines's arms, because he had heard these shapes before. Once. Years ago. Kneeling in the shrine at fourteen, swearing his oath, the priest of that day whispering the archaic invocation under her breath — the words the god had spoken *before*, the words kept like a pressed flower in a book nobody opened.

"*Kelan sha vireth. Kelan sha domm. Kelan enna sol.*"

Maren made a sound Ines had never heard from her — a small, involuntary noise, high and wounded, like a hinge on a door that should have stayed shut.

"What is he saying?" Ines asked. "What did I ask him?"

"You asked who walks." Maren's knuckles were white around her pendant. "And he answered: *A walker comes. A walker comes home. The walker is the door.*" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and frightened and furious all at once, which was a remarkable thing to see in a woman who had buried half the town's dead and presided over the other half's weddings without visible disturbance. "That is the opening line of the First Litany, Ines. The litany of the *speaking years*. The words the god used when the god still used words."

The fire popped. Outside, a crow called once and thought better of it.

"That's not possible," Ines said. He heard how flat his own voice came out, the mercenary's trick, sand over quicksand. "The god went silent three hundred years ago. Before the valley. Before the crown fell the first time. Whatever tongue that is, it's just — old. Old speech. Scholars' speech. Maybe the boy's from some far province where they kept the—"

"Ines." Maren said his name the way you say *stop* to a horse stepping into ice. "I spent forty years copying the shrine archives. I have copied every fragment of pre-silence text that survives in this valley, and there are eleven of them, and I can recite all eleven in my sleep, and I have dreamed in that tongue more nights than I care to admit because the mind worries dead languages like a tooth." She leaned forward. "That is not scholars' speech. Scholars' speech is a corpse we prop up. What that child is speaking is *alive*. The vowels move. The grammar breathes. It has changed, the way living things change — grown dialects, grown softnesses, grown new roots on old stems." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Languages don't stay alive for three hundred years unless someone is still speaking them. And nothing has been speaking them. Nothing *could* be."

"Then what am I hearing?"

"I don't know." She said it plainly, and it was the plainness that terrified him. "But I know what the archives call the vessel of the voice. The one who carries what the god could not finish saying." Her eyes went to the crescent mark at the boy's throat, and Pell, following her gaze with his hand, touched the mark and then pointed — not at Maren's pendant, but past it, at Ines's chest, at the oath-scar over his heart where the shrine-ink had gone in when he was fourteen and never faded quite right.

"*Kelan enna sol*," Pell repeated softly, pointing. "Kelan." And he tapped Ines's breastbone, twice, gentle as rain.

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted.

"He's calling me a walker," Ines said. His mouth was dry. "I'm not anything. I'm a hired sword in a valley where nothing happens. I kneel to a god who's never said a word to me or anyone else in three centuries. If I'm a door, it opens onto a turnip field."

Maren didn't smile. That was the worst part. Maren always smiled at his self-deprecation; it was their old dance, priest and soldier, faith and doubt, thirty years of it. Now she only looked at him with an expression he'd last seen on the face of a woman standing at her son's graveside — grief arriving ahead of schedule, mourning something that hadn't finished happening yet.

"Tell me about the boundary stones," she said.

So he told her. The scorch marks, fanned outward like wings. The absence of tracks. The way the frost had refused to settle on the burned patches even in the coldest hour before dawn. And as he talked, Maren rose, slowly, painfully, and went to the window, and stood looking east toward the border ridge with her back straight as a lance.

"When I was young," she said, "there was a story the old priests told and the new ones stopped telling. That the valley wasn't founded. It was *poured* — like wax around something being sealed. That the borders aren't geography. They're a bandage." She turned. "We told ourselves the god went silent everywhere, and the valley was simply the place where people came to accept it quietly. But there's another reading, Ines. That the silence isn't everywhere. That the silence is *here* — that this valley is where the last of the voice was shut away, and the borders exist to keep it from leaking out." She looked at Pell. "Or to keep something from coming in after it."

"The scorch marks," Ines said.

"Something crossed," Maren agreed. "Something that burns what it passes. And now a child arrives wearing the voice of a dead god in his mouth, and within a day of his arrival, I dream of him speaking backward, which in the old texts means one thing only — that the voice is reaching for listeners, and the listeners are answering whether they mean to or not." She came back to the table and sat, heavily, and took both of Pell's hands in hers. The boy let her. "Child. *Vetha.* Can you tell me where you walked from?"

Pell's face did something complicated — fear and memory grinding against each other. Then he spoke, six or seven sentences, low and flowing, and twice he used a word that made Maren flinch as if struck.

At the end she was silent for a long time.

"Maren," Ines said.

"Burning," she said finally. "He says *burning*, and he says *the tall ones in gray*, and he says — he says the woman with the lamp told him to run east until the ground forgot him. East. Into the valley. Into the one place in the world where whatever he carries goes quiet enough to hide." She released the boy's hands and pressed her own flat against the table, steadying herself. "Ines, whoever the tall ones in gray are, they didn't follow him here by accident, and they won't stop at the border because the border stops other things. They made the border. Or they know how it was made. And when they come — and they will come, soon, because a thing like that boy cannot stay hidden in a place this small — they will not ask politely. They will burn their way in, exactly as they burned their way wherever he came from, and they will take him, and they will do with the voice what crowns have always done with voices."

"Which is?"

"Put it in a box," Maren said, "and charge admission."

* * *

Afterward — after Maren had drunk the tea Ines made without tasting it, after she had taught him four phrases of the living tongue so he could call for help, for food, for *run* — she stood at the door pulling on her wraps, and paused.

"There's a page missing from the archive," she said, not turning around. "Has been missing since before my time. The eleventh fragment ends mid-sentence, and the sentence is the one that matters: *what the walker must choose when the voice wakes.* Every copyist for two hundred years has noted the gap. Every copyist assumed the page was lost." She looked back at him over her shoulder, and the light from the doorway made her look ancient and carved. "Yesterday I dreamed the page. I dreamed it whole, Ines, and I dreamed it burning, and I woke with ash on my fingers."

"That's a dream," Ines said. "You're the one who taught me dreams are just the mind chewing."

"Yes," Maren said. "And I taught you that the god is silent. And this morning a child recited the First Litany in a tongue that has been dead for three hundred years and is somehow still breathing." She stepped out into the cold. "One of us is going to have to update the lessons."

She went down the lane leaning hard on her stick, and Ines watched her until the mist took her, and the watching felt like the last ordinary act of his life.

Behind him, softly, in a language older than any kingdom, Pell said a single word.

Ines turned. The boy was standing by the hearth, wrapped in the old blanket, looking at him with those riverbed eyes — and he pointed east, toward the ridge, toward the burned stones, toward whatever was coming.

Then he said it again, and this time Ines caught the shape of it, one of Maren's four phrases, the one she'd saved for last and made him repeat five times:

*Run.*

Outside, far off, on the eastern ridge, a crow went up all at once — a whole flock, hundreds, rising off the pines like smoke given sudden wings.

And beneath the flock, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of many horses moving at a trot.
