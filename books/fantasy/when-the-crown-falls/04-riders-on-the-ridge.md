## 4. Riders on the Ridge

The knock came at midmorning, which was wrong. Everything about it was wrong.

Ines knew the sound of a neighbor's fist on wood — the impatient double-rap of Tam Wicker wanting his fence mended, the shy scratch of old Sela come to trade salt for lamp oil. He knew the sound of Pell, too, though the boy had been with him only three days: a small, hesitant tapping, as if he feared the door might bite. But this was iron on oak, measured and heavy, the knock of a man who expected doors to open because he had never met one that refused.

Ines set down the knife he'd been using to scrape fat from a hide and stood very still in the middle of his one room.

Pell was asleep in the loft above, curled in Ines's spare blanket like a comma of bone and fever-sweat. The boy's fever had broken twice and returned twice, and each time it climbed, so did the words — that old language spilling out of him in his sleep, syllables that made the air feel thinner, made the candle flame lean toward him as if listening. Maren had died two nights past, her last breath spent on a warning: *Others will come for him, Ines. The crown does not forget what it buried.*

He had told himself she was an old woman frightened by an old story.

The knock came again.

* * *

There were six of them, and they wore the crown's colors the way a blade wears oil — smoothly, without warmth. Their horses were lathered despite the cold morning, and their breath hung in the air like small ghosts. The leader sat his grey mare with the easy slouch of a man who had ridden hard for days and resented every mile: a long face, a short beard going to iron-grey at the jaw, eyes the color of pond ice. On his breastplate, polished brighter than the rest of his harness, gleamed the sigil of the Regent's own household — a bell inside a crown.

"Morning," the man said, and did not make it a greeting.

Ines leaned against his doorframe and let his body do what twenty years of mercenary work had taught it: nothing. Loose shoulders. Idle hands. A man with no opinions worth killing for.

"Morning," he said.

"You'd be Vane. The sellsword." The officer consulted a leather folio without much interest. "Ines Vane. Sworn to the Hollow garrison patrol, licensed under warrant of the magistrate at Dunmarrow." He looked up, and the pond-ice eyes did their work slowly, taking in the house, the woodpile, the smoke, the silence. "You keep to yourself, they say."

"That's the general complaint."

A smile touched the officer's mouth without reaching anywhere near his eyes. "Captain Rell," he said. "Crown service, third bell-company. We're looking for something that was taken from a temple east of here. Burned, I'm afraid. Terrible business." He paused, letting the words sit. "A relic. Small. Portable. Sacred to the old faith, if you follow such things."

"I don't follow anything."

"No," Rell agreed pleasantly. "I'd heard that too."

Behind the captain, the five soldiers sat their horses in a loose crescent, and Ines read them the way he read all men: the young one on the left whose hands kept moving on the reins, nervous or eager; the big one who hadn't blinked enough since they arrived; the two who kept their eyes moving along the tree line out of habit rather than threat; and the last, oldest, who watched Ines himself with the flat professional attention of a man counting exits. That one worried him most. Nervous soldiers were loud. Careful ones were dangerous.

"A relic," Ines said. "And you think it came through the valley?"

"We think it came *somewhere*, and the valley is somewhere." Rell swung down from the mare, unhurried, and Ines felt the movement in his spine before his mind caught up — the way a man dismounts when he intends to stay a while. "Strange country, this. Magic doesn't cross your stones, I'm told. Nothing crosses them, really, unless it's meant to." He walked toward the boundary marker visible down the slope, its granite face furred with moss, and laid one gloved hand against it almost tenderly. "Scorch marks here. Fresh ones. Something burned coming over, or something burned trying to get back. Either way, it left a mark, and marks are how we find things."

"The lightning took some trees up-ridge last month," Ines said. "Stone catches it same as wood."

"In a valley where magic can't live?" Rell turned, and now the smile was gone entirely, and what remained underneath was worse — patient, thorough, the face of a man doing arithmetic. "Captain Vane. May I call you Captain? Your license says you held a commission once, before you came here. Harrowmark company, yes? You fought at Cael Ford. You have a reputation for never lying to an employer. They say it's the strangest thing about you — a sellsword with a priest's tongue. Is that true?"

"It's true I don't lie."

"Mm." Rell drew off one glove, finger by finger, and flexed his bare hand as though testing it against the cold. "Then this will be simple. I'm going to ask you four questions, and you're going to answer them the way you've answered everything else in your careful little life, and then my men and I are going to ride on and trouble you no further. First question." He held up one finger. "Have you seen a child pass through this valley in the last fortnight? Boy, perhaps eight years old, dark-haired, feverish?"

The world went very quiet.

It was not the silence of the god — Ines had lived inside that silence so long he barely heard it anymore, the way a man stops hearing a river outside his window. This was a different silence, smaller and sharper: the silence of a single moment stretched thin across a blade's edge. Somewhere behind him, inside the house, a floorboard settled with a soft creak, and Ines felt the sound travel up through his boots and into his teeth.

*Say no,* said the part of him that had survived Cael Ford, that had crawled out of that mud with eleven arrows' worth of other men's blood on him, that had built a life out of small controllable loyalties and clean transactions. *Say no, take their coin if they offer it, watch them ride out, and go back to scraping fat off hides until you die dry and warm and alone, which is what you chose, which is what you wanted.*

Pell had slept with his fists closed around Ines's finger the first night, when the fever peaked and the boy thought he was dying. He hadn't known he was doing it. That was the thing about children — they grabbed hold of whatever was nearest and strongest, and they didn't let go until they were ready.

"No," Ines said.

The word came out level. It came out easily. And that was the terrifying part — not that he lied, but that the lie cost him nothing, that it slid out smooth as water, that twenty years of a priest's tongue ended in a single syllable and he felt *relief*, bright and shameful, flooding through him like the first drink after a long fast.

Rell studied him. "Second question." Another finger. "Do you shelter anyone in that house? Servants, kin, strays?"

"I live alone."

"Third question. Have you seen anyone cross the boundary stones recently? Travelers, pilgrims, anything with a burn on it?"

"The valley sees few travelers. Those who come don't leave marks."

"Fourth question." Rell put his bare hand flat against the doorframe, close enough that Ines could smell the leather oil and horse sweat on him, and the pond-ice eyes came up level with his own. "If you *had* seen such a child — a stolen relic wearing skin, carrying the voice of a dead god in its mouth — would you give it up? To the crown. As your oath requires."

And there it was. Not a question at all. A hook dressed as a question, baited with everything Ines had ever claimed to be.

"My oath is to the valley's peace," Ines said. "Not to the crown's convenience. If a child passed through here fleeing a fire, I'd feed him and point him east and ask no questions, because that's what the valley does. It forgets. That's the whole point of it." He let a beat pass, let his voice roughen just slightly, the grain of truth inside the lie. "But no child has passed through here. So your fourth question is wasted on me, Captain."

For a long moment Rell said nothing. The wind moved through the pines along the ridge, and somewhere a crow complained, and the soldier on the far left shifted in his saddle with a creak of leather that sounded enormous in the quiet.

Then Rell smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was worst of all.

"Well answered," he said softly. "Truly. You know, I believe you're exactly the man they said you were. Which is why—" he stepped back, drawing the glove back on, unhurried, "—we'll be staying the night at Dunmarrow. Nice town. Good ale, I'm told. Chatty people." He mounted the grey mare in one fluid motion. "Chatty people are useful, Captain Vane. Someone always saw something. Someone always talks eventually. It's rarely the person you expect." He gathered the reins. "Enjoy your solitude."

They rode out along the ridge road at a walk, unhurried, deliberate, five riders and their captain silhouetted against the pale sky, and Ines stood in his doorway and watched them go and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down his spine, that he had not won anything. He had bought time. Hours, perhaps a day. Rell had not believed him — Rell had simply decided that fear worked more slowly than force, and that a town full of chatty neighbors would do the crown's work for free.

* * *

He barred the door and stood with his forehead against the wood and listened to his own heart, which was behaving disgracefully.

Not hammering. That he could have understood. No — it was *quick*, light, almost glad, the heart of a man who has just leapt a gap he'd spent his whole life walking around, and found the far side solid beneath his feet. Terror and exhilaration braided together so tightly he couldn't tell which was which. His hands were steady. His mouth still tasted of the lie, and the taste was not ash, as he'd always imagined a lie would taste. It tasted like bread. Like the first meal after fasting.

"You can come down," he said quietly. "They're gone. For now."

The loft ladder creaked. Pell descended one rung at a time, wrapped in the blanket, dark hair wild, eyes huge and fever-bright. He looked at Ines the way he always did — reading him, translating him, the boy's strange old soul working behind a child's face. Then he spoke, and it was half valley-tongue and half that older language, the words tumbling out mixed:

"They asked about me."

"Yes."

"And you said—" Pell frowned, searching. "You said the valley forgot."

"I said the valley forgets. It's what it does." Ines crouched to the boy's height, and the words came harder now, the way true things always did. "Listen to me, Pell. Those men will be back. Maybe tomorrow, maybe the day after, and next time they won't knock. So tonight we pack, and before dawn we go east, over the stones, out of the valley. There's a place beyond it. People who help people like you."

"People like me," Pell repeated, and touched his own chest, and for a moment his eyes flickered with something that wasn't entirely a child's — something old looking out through them, tired and vast and briefly present, like a face at a lit window glimpsed from a dark road. Then it was gone, and he was only a boy again, shivering. "I don't want to go. This is the only place I haven't been afraid."

Ines's throat tightened. He thought of the valley — the gray mornings, the tolerant nods, the town that had never once asked him to supper but had also never once turned him away. Twenty years of a home that was never really his, and he was about to burn it down for a feverish child he'd known three days, and the terrible, glorious truth was that he didn't hesitate. Hadn't hesitated since the moment Rell asked his first question and Ines heard himself answer *no*.

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry. Being safe and being free aren't always the same place."

Pell considered this with the gravity of the very young and the very old. Then he crossed the room and pressed his small hot forehead against Ines's chest, right over the disgracefully quick heart, and said nothing at all, which was somehow the loudest thing anyone had said to Ines in twenty years.

Outside, the light was already leaning toward afternoon. Down in Dunmarrow, bells would be ringing vespers soon, and chatty people would be buying ale for strangers with pond-ice eyes, and someone — Sela, or Tam Wicker, or the miller's boy who'd seen smoke from Ines's chimney at odd hours — someone would talk.

Ines began pulling his old campaign gear from under the bed, and his hands did not shake, and that frightened him more than anything.

By full dark, two figures slipped east through the pines toward the boundary stones, and behind them, far down the valley, Dunmarrow's evening bells began to ring — and rang, Ines thought grimly, like a town counting out the hours until morning.
