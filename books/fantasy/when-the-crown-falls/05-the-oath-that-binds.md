## 5. The Oath That Binds

The man was too big for his house.

Pell noticed that first, before the fever let go of him enough to notice anything else. The ceilings were low and the doorframe was narrow and the man had to duck when he came through it, like a tree bending in wind, and even then he seemed to fill the room from corner to corner. He smelled of iron. Not blood-iron, not exactly — though there was some of that too, old and washed-many-times — but iron like a well bucket, like a hinge, like the cold part of a coin held too long in the hand.

He did not talk. That was the second thing Pell learned about him.

Pell had been in the big man's house for three days before he understood that the silence wasn't anger. He knew angry silences. The temple had been full of them — the brothers going quiet at supper when the Mother Superior's spoon stopped tapping her bowl, the whole refectory holding its breath like a drowned thing. This was not that. This man's silence was more like the silence of stones, or of very old trees. It didn't want anything from you. It just *was*, the way weather is.

His name, Pell gathered from the one time another person came to the door — a woman with flour on her arms who left bread on the step without coming inside — was Ines. She said it twice, pointing at him with her chin, and Ines nodded once, the way a mountain might nod if mountains could be bothered.

*Ines.* Pell turned the name over in his mouth where no one could hear it. It tasted like a word from the other language, the deep language, the one that lived under his tongue now whether he wanted it or not.

Because that was the third thing: the words would not stop.

They came up out of him at night mostly, when the fever burned lowest and his mind floated free of his body like smoke off a doused fire. Words in the old tongue. *Vessha. Kellum-drai. Orenneth, orenneth, oren-sai.* He didn't know what they meant. He hadn't known them a month ago. They had arrived the way rain arrives, all at once, everywhere, soaking through everything he was, and now he could not wring them out.

Sometimes, saying them made the room go strange. The candle flame would lean toward him. The water in the basin would shiver into rings though nothing had touched it. And the big man — Ines — would watch from across the room with those sad grey eyes and not move, not flinch, not cross himself or spit or run, which is what the temple brothers would have done, what anyone sensible would have done.

He just watched. Like he was listening to something even in the silence. Like maybe the silence was talking to him too.

* * *

Pell remembered the fire.

He tried not to, because remembering it brought the smell back — hot pitch and burning feathers and something sweeter underneath, something that had made his stomach turn over even as he ran — but sleep dragged him there every night whether he willed it or not.

The temple of Saint Vessa-on-the-Marches had stood for four hundred years. Brother Callow said so every morning, ringing the dawn bell, his voice cracked as an old plate. Four hundred years of bells, four hundred years of candles, four hundred years of the same grey stone steps worn into soft curves by the feet of pilgrims. Pell had lived there since he was small enough to be carried in a basket. He had never known any other world than bell-calls and candle-smoke and the long cold corridor to the washroom, and the garden where Sister Amethe grew beans in rows straight enough to draw a line by.

Then the riders came at dusk, and the world ended in one night.

He remembered pieces. That was the worst of it — memory came to him in shards, like a dropped plate, and he could never hold the whole shape of it. A horn sounding wrong, too low, a sound that made his teeth ache. The Mother Superior's face, white as the flour sack, shouting words he couldn't make sense of. Fire climbing the bell tower like it had always wanted to, like it had only been waiting politely for permission. Brothers running with their robes hitched up like boys. Brother Callow standing in the doorway of the scriptorium, not running at all, just standing there with a book held against his chest while the fire came down the hall toward him, and Pell had screamed at him to come, screamed until his throat tore, and Brother Callow had only smiled — smiled! — and said, in a voice almost swallowed by the roaring:

*Run east, little sparrow.*

No. Not him. It had been the woman.

Pell frowned at the ceiling beam, chasing the memory through the smoke of it. There had been a woman. He was nearly sure. Tall, in travelling clothes the color of wet bark, with a sword she wore like other people wore walking sticks — carelessly, like it was part of her arm. She had come out of nowhere in the middle of the burning, out of the dark beyond the gate, and she had caught him up under one arm like a loaf of bread and run with him into the fields while the temple came apart behind them.

She had set him down past the barley, past the stream, in the black shadow of a hedgerow, and crouched so their faces were level. Her eyes were the color of river ice. There was ash in her hair.

"Listen to me." Her voice was low and fast and did not shake at all, which frightened him more than the fire had. "There are men behind me and more ahead. You're going to run east. Do you hear me? East, along the water, until the hills close in and the road gives out. There's a valley there. The stones will feel wrong. Cross them anyway."

"I don't—" His voice was a child's voice, thin and stupid. "I don't understand—"

"You will." She gripped his shoulders hard enough to bruise. "You'll understand everything, soon, and I'm sorry for it, I am sorry, we never wanted—" She stopped herself. Breathed once, in through her nose, the way soldiers breathe. "When you get there, find the man who doesn't speak. There's one in every village, if you look. Find the silent one and say—" and here she leaned close and put her mouth against his ear and spoke a word, one single word, in the old tongue, the deep language, the one that hadn't been inside him yet —

—and the word went into him like a hook into a fish's mouth, and he felt it settle somewhere behind his heart, and he began, right then, in the hedgerow in the dark, to burn with fever.

"Go," the woman said, and pushed him.

So he ran east. Along the water, as she'd said, until the hills closed in and the road gave out and the night got so thick he had to feel his way with his hands. He ran until his legs gave out, and crawled, and ran again, and the fever climbed in him like the fire had climbed the tower, and the last thing he truly remembered was stones — a line of tall grey stones standing in the bracken, humming, *humming*, a sound he felt in his teeth and his bones and the soft wet meat of his heart — and a voice in the old tongue rising up out of him unbidden, loud, louder than he had ever spoken, saying *oren-sai, oren-sai, open, open,* and the stones leaning apart like doors—

And then nothing. Then fever. Then the too-big house, and the too-big man who smelled of iron and never spoke.

*Find the silent one,* the woman had said.

Well. He had found him.

* * *

On the fourth day, Pell woke from a dream of the fire and could not tell for a long moment whether he was still dreaming. The room was dim. Rain ticked against the shutters. And the big man sat in the chair beside the bed — not looming, not watching this time, but bent forward over his knees with his head bowed and his hands loose between them, and he was praying.

Pell knew praying when he saw it. He had done it ten thousand times in the temple, kneeling on stone until his knees printed themselves red. But this was different. This man prayed with no words. His lips moved sometimes, shaping nothing, and his fingers curled and uncurled slowly, one at a time, like a man counting something invisible. And there was such loneliness coming off him — Pell could feel it the way you feel heat off a stove — such a great grey ocean of it that Pell forgot to be afraid.

"You pray to nobody," Pell said. His voice came out rusty. It was the first thing he'd said in days that wasn't the old tongue, and it startled them both.

Ines looked up. For a moment his face did something complicated — surprise folding into something softer, something almost like relief — and then it settled back into its usual stillness, like water after a stone.

"The god's gone quiet," Pell went on, because now that his own voice worked he couldn't seem to stop it. "That's what they taught us. The god spoke to the first kings and then it went silent, three hundred years ago, and that's why we have bells instead. Bells are how we talk to it now. Brother Callow said the bells carry our voices up." He picked at the blanket. "But I don't think bells can carry anything anywhere. I think they're just noise. I think—" He stopped. Swallowed. The next words came out smaller. "I think the god isn't gone. I think it's *hiding*. And I think it's hiding in me, and I don't know why, and I want it to stop."

The silence after that was very long. Rain ticked. The candle leaned, very slightly, toward the bed.

Then Ines rose from the chair, crossed the little room in two strides, and knelt down beside the bed so that his face was level with Pell's — a huge, scarred, sorrowful face, close enough that Pell could see the grey in his stubble and the old white line of a scar through one eyebrow. And slowly, deliberately, he raised one hand and touched two fingers to his own chest, over his heart.

Then he pointed at Pell.

Then — and this was the thing Pell would remember for the rest of his life, the thing he would turn over and over in the years after like a coin worn smooth — Ines took Pell's small hand in his large rough one, and guided those same two fingers to rest against Pell's own chest, over the place where the word the woman had whispered still sat like a swallowed coal.

And he nodded. Once. Slowly.

*I know,* the gesture said. *I know what's in you. You are not alone in this.*

Pell's eyes filled before he could stop them. He was eight years old and his whole world was ash and everyone who had ever tucked him in was dead or fled and a stranger-woman had planted a burning coal behind his ribs and told him to run — and here was this enormous silent man kneeling in the rain-dark, telling him with two fingers that he already knew, that he had known since the moment Pell collapsed across his threshold speaking a dead language, and he had stayed anyway. Had fed him. Had watched over his fevers. Had prayed his wordless prayers at his bedside.

"Why?" Pell whispered. It came out as a sob. "Why do you know? How do you—"

But Ines only shook his head, gently, and pressed Pell's hand flatter against his chest — and beneath the palm, faint but unmistakable, steady as a second heartbeat, Pell felt something answer. Something old. Something that hummed the way the boundary stones had hummed.

Something that recognized him.

Outside, far off across the rain-grey fields, a sound rolled up the valley road that was not thunder. Hooves. Many of them, moving fast, and beneath the drumming of them a thinner sound that Pell's new ears caught and understood even though he had never heard it before:

a hunting horn, blown low, the kind that makes your teeth ache.

Ines went still. Then he rose without a sound, crossed to the window, and eased the shutter apart one finger's width.

Whatever he saw, it made his jaw tighten. He turned back to Pell, and his grey eyes were no longer sad. They were awake now, sharp, decided — the eyes of a man stepping off a cliff and finding, to his own astonishment, that he means to fly.

He put one finger to his lips. *Quiet.*

Then he began, swiftly and silently, to pack.
