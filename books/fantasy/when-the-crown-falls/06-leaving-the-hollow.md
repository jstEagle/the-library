## 6. Leaving the Hollow

They left before the third bell of morning, when the fog still owned the valley floor and even the crows had not yet found their voices.

Ines carried Pell on his back, the boy's arms looped loose around his neck, his breath hot and shallow against Ines's collar. The fever had not broken in three days. It had only learned new shapes — sometimes a dry heat like a kiln, sometimes a damp burn that soaked the boy's shirt and left him shivering in his sleep, murmuring that old language through cracked lips. *Vesh-ka-lumen. Vesh-ka.* Ines did not know what it meant. He only knew that hearing it made the scar over his heart ache in a way he had never told any healer, because there was no honest way to describe an ache that felt like remembering.

The town was still asleep behind them. He had left coin on Maren's altar — more than the priest would have asked, less than she deserved — and a note in his blunt hand: *Gone east. Don't look for us. Burn this.* He wondered if she would. Priests were supposed to be good at burning things.

The streets were empty except for the baker's boy stacking wood outside the ovens, who watched Ines pass with the flat, careful eyes the valley gave its strangers. Ines had lived among these people for twenty-two years. He had pulled three of their children out of the flooded millrace, had stood the winter watch four seasons running without complaint, had buried their dead with the proper words to a god who never answered. And still they looked at him like that — like a tool left out in the rain, useful, rusting, not quite belonging to anyone.

He had told himself for two decades that this was enough. That contentment was a small fire you fed with small sticks, and if you never asked it to warm a whole house, it would never disappoint you.

Pell's fever-heat pressed against his spine like a second heartbeat, and Ines understood, with the quiet finality of a door closing, that the fire had grown whether he willed it or not.

* * *

The boundary stones began a mile past the last farm, where the road narrowed to a track and the track narrowed to a suggestion. There were eleven of them, though the oldest maps said nine, as if the valley had grown two more in its sleep. Each was taller than a man, granite gone black at the base where centuries of moss had been burned away by whatever current ran through them. They stood in no line a surveyor could draw — here one alone in a field of gorse, there three clustered like conspirators — but every child in the Hollow learned early where they stood, and every child learned earlier not to touch.

Ines had spent half his life patrolling them. It was the work the town paid him for, the only work they trusted to a man with no family to corrupt and no god to distract him: walk the line, mark the stones, report anything that crossed.

Nothing ever crossed. That was the point of the valley. Magic could not live here. Whatever force the founders had raised — stone and oath and something older than either — pressed down on the Hollow like a lid on a pot, and inside the pot, the world was ordinary. Wheat grew. Rain fell. Wounds festered and fevers killed and men died of plain, honest causes. Ines had always assumed he loved the valley for its peace.

Walking toward the stones now with a dying child on his back, he admitted what he had never let himself say aloud: he did not know if he loved the valley at all. He knew only that the valley had taken him in when nothing else would, and that gratitude and love are different animals wearing similar coats.

He set Pell down against the largest stone, in the shadow of it, and unwrapped the bundle of supplies he'd carried from the house: water, bread, the fever-tincture Odile's letter had described — no. Not Odile. Not yet. The tincture the herb-wife sold, bitter as regret. He coaxed a measure of it between the boy's lips. Pell swallowed reflexively, eyes rolling beneath lids thin as parchment, and kept murmuring.

Up close, Ines could almost catch the shape of the words now. Three days of listening had worn grooves in his attention, and some syllables landed in him like stones dropped down a well — he heard them fall, and fall, and never heard them land.

"Who taught you that tongue?" he asked quietly. "Who sent you east?"

The boy's answer was a whisper in a language older than kingdoms, and then, surfacing briefly through the fever like a swimmer breaking water: *"She said... run."*

"I know," Ines said. "I'm going to run for both of us."

He checked the sky. Grey, low, the sun a pale coin behind wool. Good. What he meant to do was best done in light that couldn't testify.

Because here was the thing Ines had discovered on the second night, sitting awake beside Pell's pallet while the boy burned: the boundary stones did not keep magic out of the valley. Maren had said as much before Ines left her — her voice thin, her hands shaking around her cup. *It isn't a wall, Ines. Walls keep things out. This is a lock. And locks are built for specific doors.* She had looked at him then with an expression he had spent his whole life learning to dread — the look of someone seeing a thing about him that he himself could not see.

He had not asked her what she meant. Some questions, once asked, cannot be unasked, and he had built his life on the discipline of leaving certain doors shut.

But the fever had changed the arithmetic. If the crown's riders came asking after a stolen relic — and they would come again; the sergeant's smile had been the patient smile of a man who could wait out a season — then Pell would die in the Hollow, or worse, be taken. The valley's silence would not protect him. The valley's silence might be the very thing killing him, smothering whatever he carried the way a pillow smothers a flame.

So: the stones. So: the crossing.

Ines lifted the boy again and walked toward the gap between the fourth and fifth stones, the narrowest passage, the one the patrol records called the Needle. His boots crunched on frost-brittle grass. His breath hung in the air. Everything was ordinary. Everything was exactly as it had been for twenty-two years, and he hated how much he wanted it to stay that way, and he hated more that wanting it was a betrayal of the warm weight on his back.

At the edge of the Needle he stopped.

There was a sensation he had felt his entire life and never named — a faint pressure behind the sternum, like standing too close to a waterfall, like a held note just below hearing. He had assumed it was the valley itself, the ambient hum of the wards. Every native felt it or claimed to. But Maren's face rose in his memory, pale and certain, and he thought: *they feel weather. I feel the lock.*

He stepped forward.

The pressure became a wall.

Not physical — his boot met no resistance, his body passed through air into air — but something in him slammed up against the boundary like a dog at the end of a chain. The ache over his heart flared white-hot. His vision sparked at the edges. And beneath his skin, in the marrow-deep place where a man keeps the things he refuses to know about himself, Ines felt a vast and patient weight take notice of him — the way a jailer notices the rattle of keys.

"Oh," he said aloud, to no one, to the frost, to the god who had never answered him in twenty years of prayer. "Oh, you bastard."

The valley wasn't keeping magic out.

It had never been keeping magic out.

* * *

He did not remember deciding to cross. He remembered the decision already made, the way you remember the middle of a fall rather than the top of it. Later he would tell himself it was for Pell, and it was — but there was a smaller, uglier truth nested inside the noble one, and he carried it across the line along with everything else: he needed to know what he was, and the only way to know was to leave the only home he'd ever had.

The Needle was six paces wide.

On the first pace, the hum behind his sternum rose to a whine, like a struck glass.

On the second, Pell convulsed against his back, and the boy's murmuring stopped — cut off mid-syllable, as if the valley had finally, mercifully, let him go.

On the third, Ines's scar split open with pain so clean and total that his knees buckled, and he went down on one hand in the frozen grass, Pell sliding from his back into the crook of his arm, and crawled.

On the fourth, the world ended.

Or began. He would spend the rest of his life unsure which.

Light came first — not from the sky, which stayed grey and indifferent, but from the boy. From under Pell's skin, from behind his closed eyes, from the spaces between his fingers, a radiance the color of dawn seen through honey, gold shot through with white, and it poured out of the child like water from a broken dam, like song from a throat long silenced. The frost on the grass flashed to steam. The gorse burst into impossible bloom, yellow flowers opening in seconds, decades of stored spring released at once. Somewhere behind them, back inside the valley, Ines heard — actually heard, across the line, through the wards — a sound like a great bell cracking, a single deep note of fracture that rolled over the hills and died.

And Pell breathed in.

It was not a gasp. It was a *drawing*, the way a bellows draws, the way the sea draws back before it rises. The boy's spine arched in Ines's arms. The fever-light flared so bright that Ines threw his free arm across his eyes, and through the cage of his fingers he saw the light gather — condense — pour inward instead of outward, sinking into the small body like rain into drought-cracked earth, and Pell exhaled, one long shuddering sigh, and opened his eyes.

They were brown. Ordinary brown. A child's eyes, clear for the first time in days, blinking against the ordinary grey morning.

"Ines?" the boy said. His voice was hoarse but level. Human. "Why is everything singing?"

Ines looked. The grass was green — greener than anything grew in the Hollow, green with a depth that seemed lit from within. The gorse flowers turned, very slowly, to follow the hidden sun. The air tasted of iron and honey and rain on hot stone, a taste he had no name for and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a dream. Magic. Not the stories' magic, not fireballs and flying castles. Just the world, unclenched. The world with its fist open.

And himself?

Ines reached inward, carefully, the way a man probes a wound.

Nothing.

No hum. No pressure. No vast patient weight. The ache over his heart was gone — gone entirely, a pain he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his own pulse, and its absence was worse than its presence, a missing tooth the tongue keeps finding. He felt the magic all around him the way a deaf man feels music through a floor: vibration without sound. Present. Real. Someone else's.

He sat down heavily in the wet grass, Pell warm and alive and blinking in his lap, and laughed — a short, ugly sound with nothing in it.

Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years of prayers into silence, of patrols along the line, of being the valley's useful stranger, and the whole time the walls weren't for the world. They were for him. He was the relic. He was the thing locked in the pot, and he had guarded his own cage with such devotion that they'd never even needed to post a guard.

"You're crying," Pell observed, with the terrible directness of children.

"No," Ines said, and wiped his face. "Frost."

"It's not cold." The boy considered him with grave, ancient patience, and then, softer: "You can stop holding your breath now. I think that's what you've been doing. All this time."

Ines had no answer. He gathered the boy up, shouldered the pack, and turned to look back — one last look, because grief deserves that much ceremony.

The Hollow lay below in the fog, grey and quiet and safe. Smoke rose from the baker's chimney. The eleventh stone stood black against the pale grass, and as he watched, the scorch marks at its base — the marks that had started everything, three weeks and one lifetime ago — faded, gently, like a bruise healing, until the granite showed clean and innocent, as if nothing had ever crossed, as if nothing had ever been kept.

A home that was never really his. He had known it, maybe, all along, the way you know a debt you refuse to count. But knowing a thing and walking away from it are separated by a distance no map measures, and Ines stood on the far side of that distance now, and it hurt exactly as much as he had always feared, and not one ounce more.

"Come on," he said to Pell. His voice came out rough but steady. "East. Before they find the bell."

"What bell?"

"The one that just broke."

* * *

They walked east through a world remade.

By noon the fog had burned off and the land had begun to show them things. A stream that ran uphill for twenty paces before thinking better of it. A hollow oak full of light with no source, warm as a sleeping animal. Once, a fox crossed the track ahead of them carrying what was unmistakably a flame in its mouth, delicately, like a mother cat with a kit, and vanished into the bracken without looking back.

Pell saw everything and asked questions about none of it, which frightened Ines more than fever had. The boy simply nodded at each wonder like a man greeting old acquaintances, and once, passing the oak, he laid his palm against the bark and said something soft in the old tongue, and the light inside brightened briefly, the way a fire brightens when breathed on.

"What did you say to it?"

"Hello," Pell said. "I don't know all the words yet. I know them, but I don't know what they mean. Like a song stuck in your head." He frowned, working at something. "It says the loud ones are coming. The ones who ring bells to make it quiet."

Ines stopped walking.

"When?"

"Soon." Pell shrugged, a child's shrug, helpless and total. "It doesn't do *when*. It does soon and long-ago and not-yet."

Behind them, far to the west, back along the way they had come, a sound reached them across the miles — thin at first, then layered, braided, deliberate. Bells. Not the valley's cracked note; these rang true and hard and in unison, tower answering tower, a chord of bronze rolling over the hills like a declaration.

Pell clapped his hands over his ears and stumbled, and Ines caught him, and the boy looked up with tears standing in his eyes and said, in the old language, one word that Ines somehow understood without knowing how:

*Hunters.*

Ines shifted the boy higher on his hip, turned east, and lengthened his stride.

The bells rang on behind them, counting down.
