## 7. The Road of Broken Kings

The pilgrim road east of the Hollow Valley was called the Road of Broken Kings, though no one Ines asked could say why. Some claimed a line of monarchs had once been buried along its length, their graves marked with shattered crowns instead of headstones. Others said it was where defeated kings had walked in exile, barefoot and crownless, watching the road unspool behind them toward everything they'd lost. Pell, when he was well enough to talk — which was more often now, his fever gone but the strange old tongue still rising out of him like water finding its level — pointed at the leaning milestone at the road's first bend and said a word that made the air taste like copper and rain.

Ines didn't ask what it meant. He wasn't sure he wanted to know.

They had been walking for four days. The valley's borders were two days behind them now, and Ines caught himself looking back at every rise in the land, half expecting to see the boundary stones, half expecting not to. That was the strange grief of it: the valley had never loved him, and he had never quite belonged to it, and yet leaving it felt like tearing away a bandage that had grown into the skin. The world beyond was louder. Grass smelled sharper. Wind had opinions. And magic — he could feel it the way a man without sight feels sunlight on his face, present everywhere, warming everything but him.

Pell walked beside him with his hand occasionally resting on Ines's sleeve, the way a child steadies himself against something large and slow-moving. The boy had stopped flinching at him somewhere on the second day. Ines had noticed the exact moment it happened — a rabbit bursting from the brush, Pell grabbing his arm instead of running — and had spent an unreasonable amount of time afterward thinking about it, turning it over like a coin too worn to read.

*Don't get used to it,* he told himself. *You're delivering him somewhere safe. That's all this is.*

The road delivered them to the caravan on the evening of the fourth day.

* * *

They heard it before they saw it: bells. Small ones, tin ones, the kind peddlers sew onto awnings to keep spirits honest. Then around the long curve of the road came a procession that looked less like commerce and more like a parade that had gotten lost twenty years ago and simply kept going.

A wagon painted the blue of deep water led the way, drawn by two mules who wore feathered hats against all decency. Behind it trundled a cart piled with cages — some holding chickens, one holding a goat with the imperious expression of a deposed duke — and behind that walked perhaps a dozen people of every age and shape: a broad-shouldered woman carrying an axe she clearly used for wood; a thin man juggling three stones without looking at them; two children chasing a dog; an old fellow asleep sitting down on a moving cart, which Ines would later learn was Tam's single greatest talent.

At the head of it all rode a woman on a piebald mare, and Ines knew her for what she was before she said a word. Hedge-witches had a look. It wasn't the herbs hanging from her saddle or the crow feather in her hatband or even the way her eyes — gray as river fog — went straight past him and fixed on Pell with an expression Ines could only call recognition. It was something underneath all that. A smell of struck matches and old rain. Magic knew its own, even here on the road where magic was supposed to be thin.

"Well," the woman said, reining in. Her voice was smoke and honey, roughened by years of talking over market crowds. "And what have we here? A soldier and a—"

She stopped. Ines watched her stop, watched the word die in her mouth, watched her gaze slide from Pell to Ines and back again with new care.

"A boy," she finished smoothly. "A boy and his... uncle?"

"Guardian," Ines said.

"Mm." She smiled, and it did not reach her eyes, which were still busy doing arithmetic. "Odile Vasseur. Witch, hedge variety, purveyor of charms, cures, love potions of dubious ethics, and the finest plum brandy between here and the coast. This is my family."

"Your family," Ines repeated.

"They wandered in. Most of them." She gestured at the strung-out crew with the fond weariness of a woman describing weather. "Jules there can juggle anything including knives, which is why we don't let him near knives. Brigit chops wood and heads. Marta and Sefa are learning letters and pickpocketing, in that order, and if you repeat that I'll deny it. Tam is asleep. He does that. And the goat is Duchess, and she outranks everyone."

Duchess the goat bleated, as if confirming the hierarchy.

"Ines," he said, because it seemed required. "This is Pell."

"Pell." Odile said the name slowly, tasting it, and something flickered across her face — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Well, Ines and Pell. You're walking east with no wagon, no tent, and enough food for one more day, unless I miss my guess about those packs. Where are you bound?"

Ines hadn't decided. That was the truth of it. He had a direction and not a destination, a boy and not a plan, and every mile east the road grew wider and more crowded with pilgrims heading toward the festival at Saint Aurel's shrine, and more dangerous with every set of eyes that might be crown eyes.

"We're bound," he said carefully, "east."

Odile laughed — a real laugh this time, sudden and warm, startling a bird out of the hedgerow. "Bound east. Saints preserve me. Listen, soldier. There's a camp up the road at the old tollhouse, forty wagons and a fire big enough to see from the moon. You'll eat better tonight than you have in a week, and the boy—" her eyes touched Pell again, gently now, "—the boy looks like he could use a night among people. No charge. Witches pay their debts, and I've a feeling I owe someone something where you two are concerned. Don't ask me who or what. I'll know when I know."

Every instinct Ines owned stood up and said *no*. Large groups were exposure. Kindness was a hook. Strangers were just dangers that hadn't introduced themselves yet.

But Pell tugged his sleeve and looked up at him, and the boy's eyes — fever-bright still, but steady now — held something Ines recognized despite himself. Hunger. Not for food. For the simple animal warmth of other humans, the thing Ines had spent thirty years convincing himself he didn't need.

"All right," Ines heard himself say. "One night."

"One night," Odile agreed solemnly, and then, wheeling her mare, added over her shoulder, "We'll see."

* * *

The camp at the old tollhouse was everything Odile had promised and several things she hadn't. Forty-odd wagons ringed a fire pit wide as a threshing floor, and the smell of stew and woodsmoke and roasting apples hung over it all like a benediction. Pilgrims, traders, a troupe of mummers arguing about masks, a funeral party escorting an urn to the sea — the road had gathered them all, and the fire made them briefly into one thing.

Ines sat at the edge of the light with his back to a wagon wheel and his sword across his knees, and watched Pell disappear into the middle of it.

It happened fast. One moment the boy was at his side, clutching a bowl of stew with both hands as if it might be revoked; the next, Marta and Sefa had discovered him, and Jules was teaching him to juggle with crumpled napkins, and Brigit — enormous, gentle Brigit — had lifted him onto her shoulders so he could see the mummers' rehearsal, and Pell was laughing. Actually laughing, high and startled, the sound of someone trying out a muscle they'd forgotten they had.

Ines's chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

He looked away. He ate his stew. He catalogued the camp the way he always did — exits, weapons, faces that watched too closely — and found, to his irritation, that the habit kept sliding. His attention kept going back to the fire, back to the boy, back to the small domestic miracle of children teaching Pell a clapping game whose rules seemed to involve hitting each other and shrieking.

"You're doing it wrong," Odile said, settling onto the ground beside him with a bottle and two tin cups.

"I'm eating."

"You're guarding. It's different." She poured brandy into both cups and pushed one at him. "You sit like the whole world owes you money. Relax your shoulders, soldier. Nobody's coming tonight."

"You don't know that."

"No," she agreed, and drank. "I don't. But I know this much: whatever's hunting that boy isn't in this camp, and if it comes down the road tomorrow, you'll want to have slept. Fear keeps fine company with exhaustion, and they make stupid children together." She studied him sideways. "How long since you slept properly?"

"I sleep."

"Liar," she said comfortably. "I can tell. You've got the look of a man who sleeps like a cat — one ear open, ready to bolt. Who taught you that? The army? Or something worse?"

Ines didn't answer. The brandy was very good, smoky and dark, and he resented how good it was.

They sat in silence for a while, watching the fire. Somewhere in the crowd, Pell shrieked with laughter, and Ines's traitorous chest did the complicated thing again.

"The boy speaks the old tongue," Odile said at last, quietly, without any of her market-day music. It wasn't a question.

Ines went very still. "You know it?"

"I know *of* it." She turned the cup in her hands. "My grandmother sang lullabies in it. Said it was the language the god used before the kings took its voice away and locked it in a crown. Said everyone could hear it once — not understand, hear, the way you hear rain on a roof — and that when the silencing came, half the world went deaf and didn't even know what it had lost." She glanced at him. "Where did he learn it?"

"He didn't learn it. It learned him." Ines heard the words leave his mouth and almost reached after them. But Odile only nodded, slow and unsurprised, and that frightened him more than any question would have.

"And you?" she asked. "What are you to him?"

"His guardian."

"That's twice you've lied to me tonight, soldier. I'm keeping count." But she said it lightly, and let it go, and refilled his cup, and somehow that — the letting go, the cup, the firelight — was harder to bear than interrogation would have been.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the camp had gone soft with snoring and the mutter of dreams, Ines lay awake under the wagon and listened to the night.

Pell was curled against Brigit's side in the witch-wagon, having fallen asleep mid-sentence during a story about a fox and a moon, and Odile had looked at Ines over the boy's sleeping body and said nothing at all, which was somehow an entire conversation. Ines had carried Pell to the wagon himself. The boy weighed almost nothing. He had gripped Ines's collar in his sleep, fingers hooked in the cloth, and had not let go until Brigit pried them loose with great tenderness, and Ines had stood outside the wagon for a full minute afterward feeling like a man who had set down something precious and couldn't remember how his hands worked.

Now, in the dark, he heard footsteps and tensed — then relaxed by degrees as Odile's familiar uneven gait approached and she lowered herself onto the wagon step above his head.

"Can't sleep either?" he said to the underside of the wagon.

"Witches sleep when the work's done. Work's never done." A pause. The creak of her settling. "I'm going to tell you a story, soldier, and you're going to pretend it's just a story, and we're both going to know better."

Ines said nothing.

"When I was young," Odile said, "I had children. Two. A girl who could make flowers open out of season, and a boy who talked to crows, and the crows answered. Little magics. The kind that used to be common as freckles, before the crown started collecting people like them." Her voice stayed level, but the levelness had a cost; he could hear it being paid. "Bell-priests came through our village. They had instruments for measuring — listening, they called it. My daughter lit a lamp without flint in front of the wrong person. That's all it was. A lamp."

Ines closed his eyes.

"They took them east," Odile said. "To the capital. To the bells. I followed as far as anyone could follow, and then I stopped following and started gathering. Every stray, every oddling, every child the road gave me — I gathered them like a woman picking up coins after a robbery. Not because it fixes anything. Because I couldn't stand the empty house." She laughed softly, without humor. "So when I say I know what it is you're carrying, soldier — that boy, that voice, that road you're walking — believe me. I've paid the toll on this bridge before."

The fire ticked. Somewhere a mule stamped.

"Why tell me this?" Ines asked finally.

"Because you lie badly and trust worse, and you're going to need someone watching your blind side before this road ends. And because—" she paused, and when she resumed, the smoke-and-honey voice had gone quiet and precise, "—because when I saw that boy today, I heard something I haven't heard in fifty years. Underneath his words. Like a bell rung so far away you feel it in your teeth instead of your ears." A breath. "And I heard it echo, Ines. Twice. Once in him."

She let the sentence hang there, unfinished, generous and cruel in equal measure.

"And once?" Ines asked, though he already knew. He had known since the boundary stones, since the fever-light, since the wound of the world that was himself.

"Once in you," Odile said. "Sleep, soldier. Tomorrow we ride together — you'll not talk me out of it, so save your breath. And somewhere east of here, I suspect, we're all going to find out exactly what it is you two are."

Her footsteps receded. Ines lay in the dark under the wagon with the brandy warmth fading and the cold coming up through the ground, and thought about the valley that had kept him, the god he'd prayed to in silence for thirty years, and the terrible, fragile, unbearable possibility that the silence had never been one-sided.

Near dawn, he finally slept — deeply, both ears closed, for the first time in longer than he could count — and dreamed of a door standing open, and voices on the far side of it, calling him home.

When he woke, the caravan was breaking camp around him with cheerful efficiency, Odile was pressing a second cup of brandy into his hand and refusing all argument, and Pell was riding on the mule-cart between Marta and Sefa, wearing a feathered hat, waving at Ines like he'd known him all his life.

The road bent east. Somewhere beyond the hills, Ines could not shake the feeling, riders were already moving — and one of them, at the crest of the far ridge, sat motionless a moment too long before turning away.
