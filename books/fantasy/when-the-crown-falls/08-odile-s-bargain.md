## 8. Odile's Bargain

The boy slept in the wagon like something the sea had given back.

Odile watched him from the driver's bench while the caravan wound down through the pine dark, and she did not like what she saw. Not the fever — fevers she knew, fevers were honest things that broke or killed and told you which they meant. It was the way the fever moved in him. It came in tides. It rose when the moon rose behind the clouds, though the moon was nowhere to be seen, and it fell at the turn of the hour as if some bell only he could hear had finished ringing.

She had seen that once before. Once was enough for any life, and more than enough for hers.

"Your witch is staring," said Tam, walking beside the wheels with his thumbs hooked in his belt. He was her oldest stray, gray now at the temples, still carrying himself like a man who expected to be useful. "You've been staring since the ford."

"I'm counting his breaths."

"And?"

"Too slow." She flicked the reins out of habit; the mules needed no telling on this road. "A child his size should breathe like a bird. He breathes like a well. Like something very deep deciding whether to come up."

Tam was quiet a moment. "Ines Vane says he talks in his sleep. Words nobody knows."

"Ines Vane says a great many things," Odile said, "with the face of a man apologizing for saying them." She softened it with a smile, because Tam worried when she went sharp, and because the smile cost nothing. But inside, the old cold place opened its door a crack, and the smell of a burning summer came out of it, and she shut the door again the way she had learned to do, quickly, before anything escaped either direction.

Below the road, the river ran silver where the trees let it show. Somewhere ahead lay the shrine at Marrow Ford, abandoned these thirty years, where they would make camp. She had chosen the route herself. She always chose the routes past dead holy places. Tam thought it was thrift — no tolls, no garrisons, no questions. Tam was a kind man and let himself believe convenient explanations.

It was not thrift.

* * *

She waited until the camp was made and the boy was fed — he ate like a wolf cub, all elbows and gratitude, and Ines Vane sat across from him not eating, just watching, the way a man watches a fire he has decided to sit beside forever without ever admitting it — before she took out her tools.

Not the healing tools. The other ones.

She brought them to the fire: a bowl of black clay glazed with ash, a vial of spring water she'd drawn at dawn from the coldest seam of the ford, three iron nails worn smooth as river stones, and a sprig of rowan gone brown in her keeping for longer than most of her strays had been alive. Ines watched her lay them out with the particular stillness of a man who has been searched by professionals.

"What is that?" Pell asked. His voice was better tonight. Steadier. As if whatever burned in him had banked itself down to coals.

"A question," Odile said. "I'm going to ask your blood a question, little one, and it's going to answer me honestly, because blood is a terrible liar. Better than you'd be, and better than me." She held out her hand. "May I?"

He looked at Ines. That was the thing about children, she thought, watching the look pass between them — they always looked at the large dangerous one first, as if danger were the same as permission. And Ines Vane, mercenary of the Hollow Valley, man of iron silences, gave a single nod so small you'd miss it if you weren't watching for it. Which she was. She had been watching him since the ford, too, and what she'd seen had frightened her nearly as much as the boy.

Because she had cut herself at the ford — a small thing, a slip of the knife gutting fish — and the blood had come slow. Too slow. Old blood. Blood that moved the way honey moves in winter, the way sap moves under bark in the last week of February. She had told herself it was the cold. She was a good liar, but she had been lying to herself for forty years and the two of them had long ago stopped believing each other.

"Will it hurt?" Pell asked.

"Not even a pinch." This was true. What might hurt was what came after, and she would carry that alone, as she carried everything.

She pricked his finger with a needle she'd boiled in wine, let three drops fall into the water, and set the bowl between them. Then she did the old work — the work that had gotten her branded, exiled, and finally employed, in that order. She hummed low, a note below hearing, and dropped in the rowan, and the surface of the water went still in a way water does not go still, flat as a coin, and then it began to shine.

Not reflect. Shine. From underneath.

"Oh," said Pell softly, and leaned toward the light the way children lean toward windows.

And the light leaned back.

Odile felt it happen — felt the boy's blood reach up through the water and take hold of her working and turn it around, gently, the way you'd turn a child's face toward something wonderful. Her question had been simple: *what sleeps in this child?* The answer rose through the bowl like a bubble through dark ale, and it was not an answer at all but a voice, thin and cracked and terribly tired, speaking a language older than any kingdom, older than the bells that had drowned it, and Odile understood every word.

That was the worst part. She understood every word.

*"—still here. Still here. Is anyone still listening—"*

Her hands shook. The bowl rang like struck glass. Across the fire, Ines Vane had gone white to the lips, and his hand had closed around the hilt of a sword he had no enemy to draw it against, and Pell was crying without knowing he was crying, tears sliding down into the light, and the voice said — softer, wondering, breaking apart like bread —

*"Brother-fragment. Little brother-fragment. You came back."*

Odile snatched the rowan out and clapped her palm over the bowl, and the light died, and the night came back in ordinary pieces: fire crackle, mule breathing, Tam's snores from the far wagon, the river going about its business. For a long moment nobody spoke.

Then Pell wiped his face and said, in the old tongue, one word. And then, in their tongue, translating himself with the frown of a boy doing sums: "Listening. It asked if anyone was listening."

"Yes," Odile said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Forty years of practice will do that. "Yes, it did."

Ines was looking at her. Not at the boy. At her. With those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and she knew — she knew with the certainty of a woman who has read omens in entrails and tea leaves and the flight of crows — that he had heard it too. Understood it too. Whatever he was, whatever the valley had made him or kept him from being, the language had gone into him the way water goes into dry ground.

"You knew what he was," Ines said quietly. "Before the bowl. Before the ford. You've known since you saw him."

The fire popped. A log settled. Odile considered, briefly, the lie — she was good at lies, had been paid handsomely for them in a former life, had helped build one so large it had silenced a god — and found she was too tired for it, and too old, and the boy was looking at her with those fever-bright eyes full of trust she had done nothing yet to earn and everything already to betray.

"Come walk with me, mercenary," she said. "Bring the boy. Leave the sword."

* * *

They walked down to the river, the three of them, past the leaning boundary post of the dead shrine, and Odile stood where the water slid over the stones and told them the truth, or as much of it as could be spoken aloud without the sky taking notice.

"There was a war," she said. "Not the war your histories sing about, with its battles and its heroes. The other part. The part that happened after, in the temples, in the bell towers. I was there for that part."

Pell hugged his arms around himself. "You were a soldier?"

"Worse. A priestess." The word tasted like ash and candle grease. "Of the Bell Order. We didn't fight the god, child. We fought its *voice*. Do you know why kings fear a god that speaks? Not because it commands armies. Because it answers questions. Any question. From anyone. A king with a silent god can say the god wants whatever the king wants. A god that speaks to farmers and fishwives and little boys in burning temples—" she spread her hands, "—well. Crowns are heavy things, and they float poorly on truth."

"The bells," Ines said. His voice was strange, roughened, as if the words were being dragged up from somewhere below his chest. "In the capital. They ring day and night."

"To drown the old tongue. Yes." She looked at him. "You feel it, don't you? The bells. Even here, miles away, you flinch when a church bell rings. I've watched you do it three times and you think nobody noticed."

His jaw tightened. He said nothing, which was its own confession, and she filed it away with the slow blood and the way the old language had lit him up like a lamp behind a shuttered window.
