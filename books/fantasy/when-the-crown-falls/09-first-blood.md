## 9. First Blood

The river came out of the hills the way all rivers do in the low country — without ceremony, sliding wide and brown-green through a valley of alders, so calm at first glance that you could mistake it for a road. The ford was a road, or had been once: flagstones laid by some forgotten king's engineers, worn smooth by centuries of cart wheels and pilgrim feet, now furred with weed and half-sunk in silt. Odile called it the Weeping Ford, because the stones wept water even in high summer, and because, she said, everything on this stretch of the old pilgrim road had a name that was really a warning.

Ines had learned to read her warnings the way he'd once read weather. Three days with the caravan had taught him its rhythms: the groan of the lead wagon's axle, which meant rain; the way Tam whistled off-key when he was frightened and on-key when he was lying; the particular silence Pell went into when the voice inside him stirred, his small hands going still around whatever he held, his eyes fixing on some middle distance where no middle distance was.

It was that silence Ines noticed first, that last morning before the ford.

They were breaking camp in the grey light, breath steaming, the horses stamping steam from their nostrils into frost-stiffened grass. Pell sat on the wagon step with a heel of bread forgotten in his hands, staring east — always east now, toward the capital they were trying to reach and the battlefield beyond it, though no one but Ines knew that part of the plan yet. His lips moved. No sound came out. Or rather, sound came out that wasn't sound — a pressure behind the ears, like the moment before thunder.

"Pell." Ines crouched in front of him. "What do you hear?"

The boy blinked, and the distance fell away from his eyes. "Water," he said. "A lot of it, talking over itself. And under the water—" He frowned, searching for the word in the valley tongue he'd been picking up with frightening speed. "Under it there are boots."

"Boots."

"Many boots. Walking in the river."

Ines stood slowly. He looked east along the road, where the land dipped through alder shadow toward the glitter of the ford, maybe two miles off. He looked at Odile, who was cinching a harness knot with more force than the knot required. He looked at the sky, pale and featureless as unglazed pottery, offering nothing.

He had spent twenty years reading border country for a living. Every instinct he owned was telling him the same thing, and every instinct had been trained in a valley where nothing ever happened, and so he did what he had done his whole life: he weighed the warning against the ordinary morning, and the ordinary morning looked heavier.

But only slightly.

"We cross fast," he said to Odile. "No stopping mid-river. Wagons single file, everyone armed who can hold a blade."

Odile's hands paused on the harness. She didn't ask why. That was the thing about her he still hadn't decided whether to trust: she never asked why. She simply recalibrated, the way water recalibrates around a new stone. "Tam rides point," she said. "Brannick walks the left flank. You take the boy."

"I always take the boy."

"Yes," she said, meeting his eyes, "and today I want to know exactly where you both are."

* * *

The alders closed over them as they descended, and the world became green shade and the smell of wet rot and the constant mutter of the river growing louder, until it swallowed the creak of the wagons entirely. Ines rode with Pell up on his saddlebow, the boy's slight weight against his chest, one arm looped around him. Pell smelled of woodsmoke and the rosemary oil Odile boiled into everything, and beneath that something else, faint and strange, that Ines had no name for — a scent like the air after lightning, like struck flint, like the moment a prayer leaves the mouth.

He had stopped praying somewhere on the second day of the road. Not deliberately. It was simply that the words felt different outside the Hollow Valley, spoken under an open sky where magic ran loose in the grass and the wind. Inside the valley, prayer had been talking to silence. Out here it felt like talking *past* something — something vast and turned away, or perhaps turned toward, listening. He hadn't decided which was worse.

"The water's saying names," Pell murmured. "Old ones. It keeps saying one twice."

"Which one?"

Pell tilted his head back to look up at him, and for a moment his eyes were not a child's eyes at all. "Yours," he said. "It says yours."

Then the trees ended, and the ford lay open before them, and there was no more time for riddles.

The river ran broad and slow here, maybe forty paces bank to bank, the flagstones showing as dark seams beneath the surface. On the far side the road climbed away through open meadow — good ground, clear sightlines, the kind of ground Ines would have chosen for an ambush if he'd been the one laying it. Which was, he understood a heartbeat too late, exactly the problem. He was looking at the far bank because that was where an enemy would come from.

They came from behind instead.

The first bolt took the lead wagon's mule in the neck. The animal screamed — a sound Ines would hear again in dreams for years — and plunged, dragging the wagon slewing sideways, Tam hauling the reins and shouting words that dissolved into the mule's screaming. Then the alders behind them shook, and men came out of the green shade the way spiders come out of a web's edge: unhurried, already positioned, six of them on the near bank and more moving along the treeline, crossbows leveled, gray cloaks gone green-dark with dye to match the leaves. Crown colors hidden, but the cut of the gear said crown anyway. Short swords. Military quarrels. A sergeant's hand-signs flicking down the line.

"Ambush," Brannick said unnecessarily, drawing steel.

"Down!" Ines hauled Pell off the saddle and rolled with him into the fern-brake as a second volley hissed past. A quarrel buried itself in the turf where his horse had stood; the horse itself shrieked and bolted, riderless, straight down the bank and into the river, and Ines watched three weeks of travel and coin go churning away downstream without feeling anything about it at all, because his whole body had narrowed to a single point of purpose: the boy under him, breathing hard, eyes enormous, alive.

"Ines—" Pell started.

"Don't talk. Crawl. Behind the wagon wheel, stay low, stay *small*." He pushed the boy toward the overturned second wagon, where Odile was already crouched with Brannick, her skirts dark with mud, her face set in an expression Ines had not seen on her before: not fear. Arithmetic. She was counting enemies and counting seconds and finding both sums disagreeable.

"Crown?" she asked him across the chaos.

"Hunters. Look at the quarrels — military shafts, but no livery. They don't want to be identified taking us." He drew his sword. The old weight of it settled into his palm like a hand clasping a hand. Twenty years of patrols, of standing watch over a valley where nothing happened, and here at last was the work he'd actually been trained for, arriving all at once, in the wrong place, for the right reason.

"They'll want the boy alive," Odile said. "That's our lever. They won't loose wild into the brake while he's in it."

"They'll loose at me fine."

"Yes," Odile said. "I know. I'm sorry." And there was something in the way she said it — not sorry enough, sorry the way you're sorry for a tool that will be used up — that Ines filed away without understanding, and would not understand until later, much later, when she knelt over him with his blood on her hands and told him what moved wrong in it.

No time. The hunters were advancing in a spread line through the alders, patient as tide. The sergeant — a lean man with a scarred jaw and the flat eyes of someone doing a job — raised two fingers, and the line began to close.

Ines did the arithmetic himself then, fast and cold. Six visible, likely four more in the trees. Crossbows need ten seconds to reload if they're good, fifteen if they're tired. The river at their backs, waist-deep at the center channel, slow enough to fight in but ugly. Odile and Brannick could hold the wagon line for a minute, maybe two. Tam was already cutting the dead mule free, faces white, hands shaking but working. Pell —

Pell was standing up.

"No—" Ines lunged, caught the boy's wrist, but Pell wasn't running, wasn't panicking. He was facing the advancing line with his chin lifted and his fists clenched at his sides, and the pressure Ines had felt at camp — the pre-thunder pressure — was rising off him like heat off a stone. The nearest hunter hesitated. Ines saw it plainly: a professional, steady man, faltering mid-step as if the ground had whispered something to him.

"*Kneel,*" Pell said, in the old tongue, and the word went through the morning like a crack through ice.

Two of the hunters dropped. Actually dropped — knees hitting mud, crossbows falling from slack hands, their faces emptied of everything except a terrible listening. The sergeant snarled something — "*Take him, he's a child, he's nothing*" — and the spell of it broke, and the line surged forward, and then there was no more room in the world for anything but iron.

Ines met them at the wagon line.

Later, he would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail, the way you fail to reconstruct any true violence — it comes back in shards, sensory and meaningless: the grunt of a man taking his point through the thigh; the specific rotten-egg stink of a misfired crossbow mechanism; Brannick roaring beside him, an ox of a man fighting with a wagon-brake lever because his sword had broken on the first parry; Odile's voice cutting through everything, high and commanding, shouting words that weren't words, that made the air thicken and the attackers' feet stick as if the mud loved them; Tam screaming; the mule screaming still, impossibly, somewhere downstream.

He fought the way he'd been taught in the valley, which was to say economically, without flourish, each motion costing exactly what it needed to cost and no more. Two hunters down. Three. The sergeant was good — better than good, crown-trained, and he fought like a man who had studied Ines specifically, angling to push him away from the wagon, away from Pell, herding him step by step toward the open stones of the ford.

It worked, because Ines let it work, because the only thing worse than being drawn away from the boy was the boy being drawn toward *him*, into the blade-work. He gave ground. The flagstones slicked under his boots. The river talked over itself around his calves, cold as a confession.

"You're the escort," the sergeant said. Conversational, almost. Blades circling. "Vane. Ex-border company. They told us you'd be trouble."

"They told you true."

"They also told us what you're carrying is crown property. Stolen relic. There's a pardon in it for you, Vane. Land, even. A name." The flat eyes flicked — just once — toward the shore, toward the small figure struggling in Odile's grip. "You're a foundling, aren't you? No house, no banner. This could be the first thing that's ever been offered to you."

And that was the moment, absurdly, in the middle of a fight, knee-deep in a river, that Ines understood he had already chosen — had chosen days ago, at a door at dusk, when he'd opened it — and that the choice was not for sale at any price, and that this knowledge felt less like nobility than like falling.

"No," he said, and attacked.

The sergeant was ready. Of course he was ready; the offer had been a feint, buying the half-second his partner needed, the second hunter rising out of the water behind Ines where he'd waded wide and silent along the stones. Ines heard him at the last instant — heard, twisted, took the sword-thrust through the meat of his shoulder instead of through his spine, and drove his own blade up under the man's jaw even as the pain arrived, white and total, a sun going off inside his chest.

The hunter fell backward into the river and did not rise.

The sergeant came on. Ines raised his sword arm and found it wouldn't answer — the shoulder torn through, the arm hanging by fury alone — and the sergeant's blade came down, and Ines caught it on the crossguard with his failing strength, and the two of them strained there, face to face, breath to breath, the river pushing cold against their legs.

"Nothing personal," the sergeant said.

"Everything," Ines said, "is personal," and brought his forehead down onto the bridge of the man's nose with everything he had left, which was not much, and then Odile was there — when had she crossed the water? — and the air *bent*, and the sergeant flew backward as if the river itself had spat him, crashing into the shallows thirty feet downstream and lying still.

Silence, then. The ringing, underwater kind.

On the bank, the remaining hunters were retreating into the alders, dragging their kneeling comrades, moving with the sudden unhurried urgency of men whose contract had changed price. One of them paused at the treeline and looked back — not at Odile, not at the wagons. At Ines. At the blood sheeting down his arm into the water, spreading pink, spreading wide.

The hunter made a sign Ines didn't know, a closed fist pressed to the sternum, and was gone.

* * *

They got him to the bank. He remembered fragments: Brannick's arms under his, enormous and gentle; the sky turning above him, pale pottery-pale; Odile's face appearing upside down in his narrowing field of vision, saying his name in a voice stripped of all its usual commerce, all its bargains, down to something raw underneath.

"This should have killed you," she was saying. "Do you understand me? This should have killed you. I've seen this wound. I've *made* this wound. Men die of it in four minutes."

"Then I'm quick," Ines managed.

"Stop. Stop talking." Her hands were pressing the wound, and her hands were shaking, and that frightened him more than the hunters had, because Odile did not shake, Odile calculated. "Your blood," she said, half to herself, half to whatever gods she kept, "your blood is *wrong*, Ines, look at it—"

He looked. He wished he hadn't. The blood welling between her fingers was dark — too dark, wine-dark, almost black at the center — and it moved strangely, sluggishly, thick as something old, and where it touched the river water it did not thin and scatter the way blood does. It beaded. It held its shape, like oil, like mercury, like something that remembered being more than blood.

Pell knelt at his other side. The boy's face was grey with shock, but his hands — his small hands hovered over the wound without touching, and where they hovered, the pain changed. Didn't lessen, exactly. Became *interested in something else*. Ines felt the torn edges of himself grow quiet, the way a forest goes quiet, the way a held breath—

"Pell," Odile said sharply. "Not yet. Not until I understand it. You could burn him out."

"He's leaking," Pell whispered. His eyes were full. "I can hear him leaking. It sounds like bells underwater. It sounds like *him*."

Ines wanted to say something reassuring and found his mouth full of the taste of coins, and the sky wheeled, and the last thing he saw before the dark took him was Odile leaning close, her lips at his ear, her voice low and fierce and afraid:

"Whatever you are," she said, "you'd better wake up and tell me. Because the valley didn't keep magic out, Ines. I've known that since the night you crossed the stones and felt *nothing*. And I think — God help us both, I think it was built to keep *you* in."

The river talked over itself. The bells rang underwater. He went down.
