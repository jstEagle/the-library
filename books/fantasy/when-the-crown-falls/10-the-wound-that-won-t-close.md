## 10. The Wound That Won't Close

Odile had stitched enough men back together to know when a body was lying to her.

She had learned her needlework in the worst possible school — the bell-priests' infirmary at Vael Corun, where the wounded came in cartloads during the last years of the border skirmishes and the priests taught her that flesh was just another text to be read. Flesh lied, though. Flesh lied constantly. It swelled around wounds it could not heal and closed over rot and called itself mended. A good healer learned to distrust it.

So when she peeled back the blood-soaked bandage on Ines Vane's side and saw the gash beneath — a spear-point's work, deep, ugly, the kind of wound that had killed three of her strays over the years — she did not gasp or cluck or any of the things a frightened old woman was expected to do. She looked at it for a long moment, and then she looked at his face.

He was watching her with those flat grey eyes, waiting to be told how bad it was.

"That should have emptied you," she said.

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it's true." She pressed two fingers along the wound's edge, gently, feeling for heat, for the softness of beginning corruption. The flesh was cool. Not cold — alive, warm as any man's — but there was a slowness under it, like a river running deeper than its surface suggested. "You took this wound yesterday at midday. It's dawn now. Any man I've ever sewn would be fevered by now, or dead. You're neither."

"I heal slowly," Ines said.

"No," Odile said. "That's the thing. You don't." She reached for her basket, drew out clean linen and her curved needle, and threaded it by feel while she studied him. "Slow healing looks like this: angry edges, weeping, fever climbing day by day until either the body wins or it doesn't. What you have is—" she searched for the word and didn't like any of them "—*patient*. Your blood isn't rushing to fix you. It doesn't need to rush. It's working the way stone works, the way old things work. Nothing about you is in a hurry, mercenary. Including your dying."

He said nothing. He had said very little since they'd dragged him from the ford, which she'd taken for shock at first, then for his usual silence, and now understood to be something else entirely. A man deciding what to admit.

"Let me sew you up," she said. "And then you and I are going to have a conversation that the boy won't hear."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear exactly. Odile had seen fear often enough to know its shape. This was closer to the look of a man standing at a door he had always believed was painted onto the wall, finding the handle turning in his hand.

* * *

The caravan camped that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border forts from the wars, its stones furred with moss and its broken arch framing a sky full of hard white stars. Odile set Pell to helping Tam wash the pots — the boy needed ordinary work after the horror of the ford, needed to feel his hands doing small useful things while his mind caught up to the sight of Ines bleeding into the river — and then she carried her basket and a lantern to the far side of the tower, where Ines sat propped against the wall with his shirt off and the firelight moving over him.

Up close, without the blood, his body told its own story. Scars everywhere, yes — a mercenary's map of old bargains with death — but beneath the scars something else. His skin had a stillness to it. She had noticed it before and misread it as discipline, the stillness of a man who had trained himself out of fidgeting. Now, with her healer's eye truly open, she saw it was not discipline at all. It was density. He sat like something carved, like a boundary stone given breath.

"This will hurt," she said, and began to sew.

He didn't flinch. Of course he didn't. She worked the needle through the wound's lips, drawing the flesh together stitch by stitch, and watched the tissue around each puncture with the attention of a woman reading scripture she had once recited and long since stopped believing.

The blood was wrong.

Not wrong the way a wound is wrong. Wrong the way an ancient thing is wrong. She let a drop fall onto her thumb and held it near the lantern. In firelight, blood should gleam red-brown, quick, wet-looking even when it dried. His dried slow. It darkened toward something almost violet, and it did not crust the way blood crusted; it settled, the way sediment settles in water left undisturbed for years.

"You're going to tell me a story now," she said, sewing as she spoke, keeping her hands busy so her voice could stay level. "And I'm going to warn you before you start: don't insult me with the small version. I served the bell-priests of Vael Corun for eleven years. I helped write the liturgies they used to bind the god's voice into the crown. There is very little about the divine blood that can surprise me, and nothing about it that frightens me more than what the crown does with people who carry it."

The needle paused in her hand. Ines had gone very still — that carved stillness again.

"The bell-priests," he said.

"A lifetime ago. Before I stole their books and ran." She tied off a stitch and cut the thread with her thumbnail. "I was young and I believed the silencing was mercy. The god's voice chose vessels, you see — children, mostly. And every vessel burned out. Every one. So the crown said: better a silent god than a god that eats its prophets. And I believed them, because believing them was easier than looking at what we were actually doing, which was building a cage." She looked up at him. "A cage with walls made of forgetting. Ringed bells in every city, drowning the old tongue. And in one valley in the middle of nowhere—"

"In the Hollow Valley," Ines said quietly, "magic cannot cross the borders."

"So everyone believes. Everyone in your town believes it. The soldiers believe it. I believed it, gods help me, and I helped build the doctrine." She set down the needle and wiped her hands, though there was hardly anything on them. "But I've been sitting with you for two days now, mercenary, and I've been thinking about that valley. Thinking hard. Because here is what I know about wards and borders and the old workings: they are built to keep things *out*. That's what a wall is. But the Hollow Valley isn't shaped like a wall. It's shaped like a jar."

The fire cracked. Somewhere beyond the tower, Tam laughed at something Pell had said, a bright ordinary sound, and both of them listened to it for a moment without meaning to.

"A jar," Ines repeated.

"A sealed vessel. A container." Odile leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "I went through everything I remember of the binding liturgies on the road today, while you slept. There's a verse — the ninth sealing, the one they call the Quiet Earth — that was never used on the god. It was designed for something else. For fragments. For pieces of the divine blood that got loose in the world after the war, pieces too small to make vessels of but too dangerous to leave walking free. The verse describes a working that doesn't repel magic." Her voice dropped. "It *anchors* it. Pins it to a place. Draws everything uncanny inward and holds it there, the way a lodestone holds iron filings. They were meant to bury fragments inside such a working and forget them."

Ines's breathing had not changed. That was the most frightening thing about him, Odile thought — a man hearing the floor dissolve under his life, and his breathing did not change.

"They buried me," he said.

"They planted you," she corrected softly. "Or they grew you, or they left you — I don't know the mechanics, and I doubt anyone living does. But think about what you told me on the road. A foundling. Left at the valley's edge as an infant, no record, no kin, appearing from nowhere the same decade the crown's surveyors re-dedicated the boundary stones. You didn't find the valley, Ines. The valley was built around you. Every prayer you ever sent to your silent god stayed inside those borders because *you* were inside them. The ward wasn't keeping magic out of the Hollow Valley." She held his gaze. "It was keeping you in. Keeping whatever you are folded up small and quiet, in a place where it couldn't wake."

For a long time he said nothing. The lantern hissed. Out past the fire, the night lay against the hills like a sleeping animal, and Odile became aware of a faint pressure in the air, the way it sometimes pressed before storms — except there was no storm coming. The pressure was coming off the man in front of her, subtle as heat off a stone at dusk.

"When I crossed the line," Ines said at last, "with Pell. I felt nothing."

"I know."

"Pell lit up like a struck bell. Magic came back into him all at once, and I stood there and felt *nothing*, and I thought — good. Good, that confirms it. Whatever the boy carries, I don't. I'm just a man. Just a hired sword who got sentimental." His jaw tightened. "You're telling me that's wrong."

"I'm telling you it's worse than wrong." Odile sat back. "It's exactly what the working was designed to do. Think, mercenary. If the Quiet Earth pins magic in place, then crossing the border wouldn't release anything in you — it would leave you exactly as sealed as before. The ward travels with the fragment. That's the whole genius of it. You could walk to the sea and still be a jar." She let that sit between them. "Pell woke because he was never warded. He was hidden, not bound — the woman who sent him east must have shielded him some other way. But you? You've spent your whole life inside a working whose only purpose was to keep you asleep. And now you're outside the valley, and the working is still on you, and somewhere in your blood there is a piece of the same voice that speaks through that child — and it has never once, in thirty-odd years, been allowed to say a single word."

The pressure in the air thickened. The lantern flame leaned, though there was no wind. Odile kept her hands loose and open on her knees and did not look away from him, because she had seen what happened to frightened powerful things when people looked away.

"How do you unseal a jar?" Ines asked. His voice was very quiet.

"I don't know." She said it plainly, because he deserved plainness. "Breaking a working that size would take the kind of power nobody's seen in three hundred years. Or—" and here she hesitated, because the next part was the part that mattered, the part she had turned over all afternoon like a stone with something underneath it, "—or it takes resonance. The liturgies describe it. A fragment near its source begins to answer it. Like glass singing when the right note is played nearby. The god's voice woke in Pell at the border because Pell is a vessel, tuned and ready. If a fragment came close enough to the source — close enough, say, to the place where the voice is kept—"

"The battlefield," Ines said. "Where we're going."

"Where you're going. Where you were always going, maybe. Maybe that's why the foundling basket was left at the valley's edge facing east, mercenary. Maybe whoever put you there wanted you kept safe until the world needed you. Or maybe—" she gentled her voice, because the next words were cruel however she dressed them, "—maybe the crown wanted you stored. A spare piece of the divine blood, corked in a valley, in case they ever needed to build a new crown."

His head came up sharply. "Cassian offered to bind the voice into a new crown. He said—"

"He said crowns cannot rule a people who hear their god directly. Yes. And a regent who needs a crown with a god's voice in it would need material to make it." Odile nodded slowly. "You're not just a man escorting a child, Ines. As far as Cassian knows, you might be the raw stuff of his new throne. Which means every step you take toward that battlefield is a step he wants you to take."

The fire had burned low. Beyond it, the camp sounds were settling — Tam banking the coals, Pell's small voice asking a question and getting a laugh for an answer. Ordinary sounds. Sounds worth protecting.

Ines looked down at his own hands, spread open on his knees, the hands of a man who had spent a lifetime believing he was small and controllable and safely human. Odile watched him look at them as though they belonged to a stranger — watched the identity she had shattered sit in ruins around him, and waited, because she knew this ruin. She had stood in it herself, twenty years ago, the night she finally admitted what the bell-priests' mercy really was. Ruin was survivable. Ruin was even honest. It was the rebuilt thing afterward that mattered.

"My whole life," he said finally, "I prayed to a silent god. Every morning. Thirty years of prayers that never went anywhere." A sound escaped him that was almost a laugh and wasn't. "They weren't going anywhere. They were going *inward*. I've been talking to myself in a sealed room my entire life."

"Or," Odile said, "you've been talking to the piece of it inside you. And it's been listening the whole time, and it has never once been able to answer." She rose, joints cracking, and gathered her basket. At the edge of the lantern light she paused. "Get some sleep, mercenary. Tomorrow we reach the crossroads at Marrow Ford, and after that the road bends toward the old battlefield. Whatever you decide to be when we get there — decide it knowing what you are. Not a man who failed to feel the magic come back. A man the magic has been waiting thirty years to meet."

She had taken perhaps ten steps toward the fire when the air behind her changed.

It was not a sound. It was a pressure, a single vast slow pulse, like the beat of a heart buried deep under the earth — and every hair on Odile's arms rose, and the lantern flame stood perfectly straight and burned a color it should not burn, a pale gold edged in violet, the color of the old liturgies, the color she had seen only once before, in the crown-chamber at Vael Corun, the day they silenced the god.

She turned.

Ines was sitting exactly where she had left him, hands flat on his knees, staring at them. And across the backs of both his hands, rising through the skin like veins of ore surfacing through quarry rock, thin lines of light had opened — faint, trembling, patient as everything else about him.

They pulsed once more, in time with that buried heartbeat.

Then they faded, and the night was only night again, and from across the camp Pell's voice rang out, clear and startled, calling into the dark:

"He's awake. Odile — *the voice says he's awake.*"
