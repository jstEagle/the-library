## 12. The City of Bells

They heard the city before they saw it.

It began as a tremor in the ground, a faint shiver Ines felt through his boot soles as the pilgrim road crested a long hill of yellow grass. He stopped, one hand going to the axe at his hip out of habit, and Odile laughed at him from the driver's bench of her wagon.

"Relax, mercenary. That's not an army. That's the city saying hello."

The sound arrived a moment later — a rolling wash of bronze and iron, thousands of bells ringing at once, layered over each other like waves breaking on a stony shore. It was not music. It had no melody he could find, no rhythm that resolved into anything a drummer could keep. It was simply noise, vast and ceaseless, pouring out of the haze ahead of them where the towers of the capital rose against the morning sky like the masts of a drowned fleet.

"It never stops," said Sefa, the caravan's tinker, riding up beside Ines on a mule too small for him. "Day or night. Rain or snow. They say the bell-priests ring them in shifts, and when the shifts change there's a moment — just a moment — when the sound almost fails. People hate that moment. They cross themselves and hold their breath."

"Why?" Ines asked, though he suspected he already knew.

Sefa shrugged, the way people in the lowlands shrugged at things everyone was supposed to know. "Because of what the bells are for."

Pell was sitting beside Odile on the bench, wrapped in a blanket against the autumn wind, and Ines watched the boy's face as the sound rolled over them. Pell had been quiet for three days — quieter than usual — ever since the night by the fire when he'd woken screaming words none of them understood, words that had made even Odile go still and pale. Now his eyes were fixed on the distant towers, and his lips were moving slightly, as if he were tasting something on the air.

"Pell," Ines said.

The boy didn't answer. His small hands had closed around the blanket's edge until his knuckles went white.

Odile saw it too. Her weathered face tightened, and she flicked the reins, urging the horses faster. "Keep close," she said, without looking back at Ines. "And keep your hand away from that axe unless I tell you otherwise. A city is not a battlefield. A city is a hundred thousand witnesses, and half of them are informants."

* * *

The capital announced itself in stages: first the smell — woodsmoke and river mud and horse dung and, beneath it all, something sweeter, incense burning from a hundred shrine-niches set into the city walls; then the walls themselves, grey stone stained black with centuries of soot from the bell-towers; then the gates, tall enough to admit a column of cavalry four abreast, choked with carts and pilgrims and beggars and merchants shouting prices over the endless bronze roar overhead.

Ines had seen cities before. He had worked contracts in two of them, brief ugly jobs he did not let himself remember often. But he had never heard anything like this. The bells were everywhere — in the towers, yes, but also in doorways, hung above shop signs, braided into the manes of parade horses, sewn in tiny brass clusters onto the hems of priests' robes so that every step they took was a small tin chime. The whole city rang. The whole city was a machine built for one purpose: to make noise.

To drown something out.

He felt it most clearly at the gate, where a bell-priest in grey-and-gold robes pressed a small silver bell into each traveler's palm as they passed. "For your doorway," the priest murmured, smiling with professional warmth. "So your home is always speaking, friend. So nothing else gets a word in."

Ines took the bell because refusing would draw attention. Its metal was cold and it seemed to hum faintly against his skin, and he had to fight the urge to drop it in the mud.

Beside him, Pell flinched as though struck.

Ines caught the boy's arm before he could stumble. Up close he could see sweat beading on Pell's forehead despite the cold, see the way the boy's breath came shallow and fast. "What is it?"

"They're lying," Pell whispered. His voice was hoarse, and beneath the hoarseness Ines heard something new — a second voice underneath, faint as a reflection in dark water. "All of them. All the little bells. They're saying the same thing over and over, and it isn't even words, it's just — noise shaped like words. And underneath—"

He stopped. His jaw clenched.

"Underneath what?" Ines kept his voice low, bent close as if comforting a tired child. Around them, travelers streamed through the gate, and the bell-priests watched the crowd with eyes that were more alert than their smiles suggested.

"Underneath," Pell said, "it's very quiet. And the quiet is trying to talk to me."

* * *

Odile found them lodging in a caravanserai in the river district, a sprawling timber building around a muddy courtyard, run by a one-eyed woman who asked no questions and charged extra for the asking. Their rooms were on the top floor under the eaves, and from the narrow window Ines could see six bell-towers rising out of the smoke, their bronze mouths swinging and booming without pause.

The plan, such as it was, was simple. Rest. Resupply. Pass through the capital quietly, like any other caravan, and take the north road toward the old battlefield while Cassian's hunters searched the wrong provinces. Odile laid it out over bread and hard cheese while Sefa counted their remaining coin and the twins — Ferro and Lark, the caravan's teamsters — argued cheerfully about whose turn it was to muck the wagon bed.

Ines barely listened. He was watching Pell.

The boy sat by the window with his knees drawn up, staring at the towers. Every few minutes his whole body would stiffen, as if a cold wind had passed through him, and his lips would move. Once, Ines caught a fragment of it aloud — a soft syllable, round and ancient, utterly unlike the valley tongue or the lowland speech or anything else Ines knew. Except he did know it. One word of it. The word from his oath, spoken over a silent altar twenty years ago, the word that meant, roughly, *I am listening*.

"You should sleep," Ines said.

"I can't." Pell didn't turn from the window. "It's louder here. Not the bells. Under the bells. It's like —" He struggled, hands pressing against his ears uselessly. "You know when you're underwater and someone shouts your name? Like that. It's been calling my name since we crossed the border stones. But here there are so many bells between us, and the bells are screaming, and it has to shout to get through, and when it shouts—" His breath hitched. "When it shouts, everything shakes."

Ines crossed the room and crouched beside him. He was not good at this. Twenty years of mercenary work had left him fluent in wounds and wages and silence, and illiterate in comfort. But he put his hand on the boy's shoulder, heavy and careful, the way you'd steady a bird.

"In the valley," Ines said slowly, "when the storms came down off the ridge, we'd bank the fire and sit close and wait for morning. There's no sense fighting a storm. You wait it out. You stay low. You stay together."

Pell finally looked at him. His eyes were fever-bright again, and in them Ines saw something that frightened him more than any crown soldier: recognition. The boy looked at him the way you look at a familiar thing in an unfamiliar place.

"The voice says you're loud too," Pell whispered. "Underneath. Like a big bell that nobody rings."

Ines had no answer for that. He stood, told Odile he'd take first watch, and went out into the courtyard where the noise of the city could fill his skull instead of thoughts.

* * *

It happened on their third night in the city.

Later, Ines would try to reconstruct the sequence of it — what woke first, what answered, which bell broke the pattern — and he would fail, because the truth was that everything happened at once, and it began in silence.

The silence came at midnight. Sefa had been right about the shift-change; there was a moment, each midnight, when the bell-priests' rotations overlapped imperfectly, a held breath in the city's roaring throat. Most nights it lasted a heartbeat. That night, Ines — standing watch at the window — felt it stretch. One heartbeat. Two. Five. Ten.

The city held its breath.

And into that gap, clear as a blade, a child's voice spoke a single word in a language older than any kingdom.

Ines spun. Pell was sitting upright in his blankets, eyes open and glowing faintly — actually glowing, a pale gold light like lamplight behind frosted glass — and his mouth was still moving around the shape of the word. *I am listening.* The word from the oath. The word Ines had sworn to a silent god in a valley where magic could not follow.

"No," Ines breathed. "Pell, no—"

But the boy wasn't done. He drew a breath that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his lungs, deeper than his body, and he *answered.*

Every bell in the city tolled backward.

There was no other way to describe it. The sound ran in reverse — a great inhaling roar, ten thousand bronze throats swallowing their own voices at once, the peal collapsing inward instead of spreading out. Windows rattled inward. Dust sifted from the rafters. In the courtyard below, horses screamed and the one-eyed innkeeper stumbled out of her rooms with a lamp, her face white. Somewhere across the city, a tower bell cracked — Ines heard it, a sharp report like ice giving way — and then another, and another, bells splitting along their seams as if the sound itself had turned against the metal that carried it.

And through the backward tolling, through the dying roar, Pell's voice rang out — except it was not entirely Pell's voice anymore. It was layered, doubled, a child's treble wrapped around something vast and exhausted and impossibly old, and it spoke not in the old tongue now but in words every soul in the city could understand:

**"I HEAR YOU. I HAVE ALWAYS HEARD YOU. WHY DO YOU BUILD WALLS OF NOISE TO KEEP ME OUT?"**

Then it ended. The light in Pell's eyes guttered out. The boy slumped sideways, and Ines caught him before he hit the floorboards, and outside the window the city of bells began — tentatively, fearfully, tower by tower — to ring again. Forward this time. Louder than ever. A wounded animal snarling at the dark.

* * *

They were moving within the hour.

Odile didn't waste time on questions. She threw coin at the innkeeper, woke the twins, and had the wagon loaded by lamplight while Sefa scouted the courtyard and Ines carried Pell, still unconscious, wrapped in blankets that hid the faint residual glow of his skin. The plan of passing quietly through the capital lay dead in the mud of the courtyard, and everyone knew it.

"There'll be soldiers at the gates by dawn," Odile said grimly, cinching a strap. "Bell-priests will have sent runners to the citadel the moment it happened. Half the city will swear they saw the boy. We go north gate, tonight, before they seal it."

"And if it's sealed anyway?" Ferro asked.

"Then we bribe, lie, or bleed," Odile said. "In that order."

Ines settled Pell in the wagon bed, among the straw and the spare harness, and the boy stirred and opened his eyes. They were ordinary again — brown, frightened, eight years old. "Did I do that?" Pell whispered.

"Yes."

"Was anyone hurt?"

Ines considered lying. He had lied exactly once in his career before this month, and lately it seemed to be all he did. "Some bells cracked. Maybe some people got cut by falling glass. I don't know."

Pell's face crumpled, and Ines found himself kneeling by the wagon bed, gripping the boy's shoulder. "Listen to me. What spoke through you — it told the truth. It said it hears them. That's not a crime in a just world. But this world has kings, and kings are afraid of being heard over. So we move, and we keep you safe, and we finish what we started. Can you walk if you have to?"

Pell nodded. Then, smaller: "It's still talking. Quieter now. It says... it says the crown knows its own sound. It says the crown has been listening *for* something, all these years. Not just drowning it out." The boy's brow furrowed, translating. "It says — 'the regent waits.'"

A cold finger traced Ines's spine. He thought of the riders on the ridge, weeks ago, asking after a stolen relic. He thought of the wound that wouldn't close, the blood that moved too slow and too old, the cage the valley had really been. He thought of Regent Cassian, whom he had never seen, who had let them escape a cell beneath the city — no. They hadn't reached the cell yet. That road might still be ahead of them. But the feeling was the same either way: the sensation of walking down a path someone else had cleared.

"Up," Ines said. "Everyone up. We ride now."

As the wagon rolled out of the courtyard into streets still echoing with frantic, over-loud peals, Ines looked back once at the bell-towers looming black against the stars, and understood that the city's constant ringing had never been a wall to keep the god's voice out.

It was a net. And tonight, they had shown the net exactly where they were.

At the north gate, torchlight was already gathering — too many torches, arranged too neatly, and among the grey-and-gold robes of the bell-priests moved figures in armor bearing a crest Ines knew from wanted notices nailed to every post from the border to the coast: the crowned fist of Regent Cassian.

A captain stepped into the road, raised a lantern, and smiled.

"Travelers," the captain called pleasantly. "The regent would like a word."
