## 13. Audience with the Regent

They took his boots first.

Ines had expected chains, and they gave him those too — iron cuffs that bit cold at his wrists, a collar of blackened steel that sat against his throat like a second jaw. But it was the boots that told him what kind of prison this would be. A guard knelt at his feet in the wagon's shadow, unbuckled each strap with careful, almost reverent hands, and carried them away. Barefoot prisoners could not run far. Barefoot prisoners were already broken.

The city had swallowed them somewhere between the last bridge and the first gate. Ines remembered fragments: bells ringing from every tower, so many and so loud that the sound stopped being sound and became weather; streets washed pale with morning light; faces turning toward their wagon with expressions he couldn't read — fear, hunger, something like longing. Pell had been taken separately. That was the thought that kept him sane through the hours of corridors and locked doors. *Taken separately* meant alive. Meant valued. Meant there was still time.

Now he stood in a hall so tall its ceiling vanished into gloom, lit by a hundred candles that burned without flickering, as if even fire here had been taught discipline.

"Audience" was the word the guards used, and they said it the way priests said *communion*. Ines had killed men who spoke of audiences. He had been summoned to them once or twice, by petty lords who wanted a blade deniable enough to hold without touching. He knew the choreography: the long walk, the measured silence, the throne set just high enough to make the neck ache.

There was no throne.

At the far end of the hall stood a plain table, oak, scarred by use, strewn with maps and papers weighted down with river stones. Behind it stood a man in grey — not the grey of poverty but the grey of a blade before it is honed, the grey of things kept sharp and simple. He was reading. He did not look up when the doors closed behind Ines. He did not look up while the guards withdrew. He read for the length of forty heartbeats, and Ines counted every one of them, because counting was what he did instead of praying, and he had not yet decided whether this room deserved prayer.

Then Cassian, Regent of the Three Crowns, set down his papers and looked at him, and Ines understood why there was no throne.

A throne would have made him smaller. The man needed nothing elevated. His presence arrived before his voice did, filling the space the way water fills a hull — quietly, inevitably, from below.

"Ines Vane," Cassian said. "Of the Hollow Valley. Sworn to the Silent God. Oath-bound, oath-kept, fourteen years in service to a deity that has never once answered you." He folded his hands on the table. "Do you know how rare that is? Not the silence. The faithfulness. Most men abandon a god after the first unanswered winter. You've given him fourteen years."

"You've read my file," Ines said. His voice sounded strange in the vastness, stripped of echo, as if the hall drank sound.

"I wrote your file." Cassian gestured to a chair across the table. It was a real chair, ordinary, worn at the arms. "Sit. Your legs will be more useful to both of us unshackled, but we'll come to that."

Ines did not sit. "Where is the boy?"

"Pell." The name came off Cassian's tongue gently, the way one handles an egg already cracked. "He's fed. He's warm. He's being spoken to kindly by people whose entire profession is kindness. Whether he remains so treated depends very little on me." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat. "It depends on you."

Ines crossed the floor and sat, because standing felt like a performance and he refused to perform for this man. The chair creaked under him. Up close, Cassian was younger than his voice — forty perhaps, with a face built for forgetting, the kind of face that appeared in no portraits because it resembled everyone's uncle, everyone's neighbor, everyone's trusted friend. Only the eyes refused anonymity. They were the color of iron left out in rain, and they looked at Ines the way a jeweler looks at ore.

"Do you know," Cassian began, "what the bells are for?"

"Bells ring the hours."

"They do. They also ring out the old tongue." Cassian turned one of the river stones idly in his fingers. "Three hundred years ago, this city had no bells. Then the last vessel of the god's voice died — badly, publicly, screaming words that unmade three city blocks and every person in them — and my predecessors made a decision. They would build a cage of sound around the language itself. Ring the bells always, everywhere, so that no child ever hears the old words clearly enough to learn them. So that the tongue dies not by decree but by deafness." He smiled faintly. "It has worked beautifully. Ask any citizen what the god sounds like and they'll tell you: bronze, bronze, bronze."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because you've spent your life inside a different kind of bell." Cassian set the stone down. "The Hollow Valley. Did you ever wonder why magic cannot cross its borders? Who built those boundary stones, and when, and why they placed a foundling boy precisely at their center?"

The question went through Ines like cold water through cloth. He kept his face still. Fourteen years of mercenary discipline held. "I wondered. I stopped wondering. Wondering doesn't pay."

"No," Cassian agreed. "It doesn't. It costs. Which is why most people let institutions do their wondering for them." He rose, walked to the nearest map, and tapped a region Ines knew better than his own heartbeat. "Your valley sits over the largest remaining vein of the god's buried speech. The stones don't keep magic out, Vane. They keep the resonance in — contained, muffled, harmless. And you—" he turned, and the candlelight caught his eyes and made them briefly terrible, "—you were placed there as a stopper in a bottle. A fragment of divine blood, sworn silent, planted in the quietest place in the world. My predecessors called you the Anchor. There are documents older than my office that refer to you by number."

The hall was very warm and very far away. Ines heard his own breathing, slow and deliberate, the breathing of a man keeping himself in one piece by force.

"You're lying," he said. "Or you believe your own lie, which is worse."

"I'm neither." Cassian came back to the table but did not sit. He leaned on it, closing the distance, lowering his voice as though they were two men sharing a secret over drinks rather than captor and captive in a hall full of disciplined fire. "I'll show you proof if you want it. I'll show you the ledger of your arrival — date, weight, the name of the priest who carried you east. I'll show you the orders for the boundary stones, cut three hundred years ago, amended twice: once when you were placed, once when the boy was born. They knew about Pell before Pell could walk, Vane. The crown watches every vessel the way a farmer watches a field."

"Why?" The word came out rougher than Ines intended. "Why does a crown care what a dead god whispers?"

And here Cassian smiled — truly smiled, with something almost like relief, as if Ines had finally asked the only question worth answering.

"Because the god is not dead. Because the god's voice chooses vessels. Do you understand what that means? It means divinity in this world is not inherited. Not bought. Not granted by coronation. It *chooses* — a farmer's daughter, a beggar's son, whoever it pleases, whenever it pleases — and it speaks through them, and everything it says is true, and everyone can hear it." He straightened. "Now ask yourself: what happens to a kingdom when truth arrives unbidden? What happens to law, to succession, to tax and tithe and treaty, when any child in any village might wake up tomorrow as the mouth of God?"

Ines said nothing. The silence stretched, and Cassian filled it, softly, reasonably, the way reasonable men always fill silences.

"The last war killed two million people. Not because the god was evil — the god is not evil, the god is not anything, it simply *is*, the way a flood is, the way fire is. It killed two million people because kings went mad trying to own it, and prophets went mad refusing to be owned, and ordinary people were crushed between them like grain between stones. So the crown made a choice. We bound the voice. We silenced it. We built bells and valleys and oaths and cages, and for three centuries no one has screamed prophecies in the market square, no army has marched under a living oracle's banner, no village has torn itself apart deciding whether its chosen vessel speaks for heaven or for hell." He spread his hands. "Three hundred years of peace, Vane. Purchased with one silence. Would you undo that trade?"

"Yes," Ines said.

Cassian blinked. For the first time, the regent looked genuinely surprised — a crack in the masonry, quickly mortared over. "You haven't even considered it."

"I've lived in the trade," Ines said. "Fourteen years in a valley that forgets. A god that doesn't answer. A town that tolerates me the way you tolerate weather. I know exactly what your silence buys, Regent, because I'm one of the things it bought. You didn't purchase peace. You purchased *quiet*. They're not the same thing, and the difference has a name, and right now that name is a nine-year-old boy in one of your cells."

Something moved behind Cassian's eyes — respect, perhaps, or the calculation of a man revising an estimate upward. He sat down at last, across the table, and for a moment the regency fell away from him and he was only a tired administrator at a scarred desk.

"You think I'm a tyrant," he said.

"I think tyrants always explain themselves beautifully."

"We do." Cassian laughed, short and genuine. "We must. Tyranny that admits it's tyranny lasts a season; tyranny that believes itself mercy lasts centuries. Yes, Vane. I am a tyrant. I have read every argument against myself and found them all true and proceeded anyway, because the alternative is the war again, and I have seen the ledgers of the last one. I have read letters written by mothers in cities that no longer exist." He leaned forward. "But here is what I offer you, and I offer it honestly, because you're the first man in twenty years to argue back instead of kneeling."

He slid a paper across the table. Ines looked down at it. It bore a seal he recognized from a dozen contracts he'd never signed — the crown's mark, and beneath it, lines of script.

"Legitimacy," Cassian said. "A name. Lands, or a commission, or both. Legal existence — you understand that you currently have none? The crown created you and the crown can acknowledge you, which means the crown can make you real. No more tolerated stranger. No more valley that forgets you. A family, if you want one — and I mean that in the bureaucratic sense, which is the only sense that lasts. Papers. Standing. A place at a table where decisions are made rather than endured." He tapped the paper once. "All of it, in exchange for one thing: help me bind the voice into a new vessel under crown authority. Controlled. Accountable. Answerable to law instead of whim. The boy needn't suffer — I give you my word, and my word is the only currency I've never debased. He'd live in comfort, honored, protected, consulted. He'd simply never be free, and neither would the thing inside him. As neither are you. As none of us are, Vane. Freedom is a story we tell children. Governance is what adults do instead."

Ines stared at the paper. The candlelight made the ink gleam like wet roads at night.

And the terrible thing — the thing he would carry afterward like a splinter under the skin — was that he understood. Sitting in that warm hall, barefoot, chained, hearing the logic unfold with all its terrible arithmetic, some starved part of him *leaned toward it*. A name. A door that opened onto rooms where he belonged. Fourteen years of tolerated weather, and here was a man offering to make him *real*, and offering it with proofs and ledgers and the honest confession of his own tyranny, and the offer fit the wound so precisely that Ines suspected — knew — that Cassian had studied the wound for years.

That was the seduction of it. Not the gold, not the lands. The *reasonableness*. Tyranny that admitted its price and paid it in stability. Silence that called itself peace. A cage that called itself a family.

He thought of Pell's fevered whisper in the dark of his house. The word from the old oath — the one word he'd recognized, the one that meant *brother*. He thought of Odile's hands steady on a needle. Of the caravan strays laughing around a cookfire. Of a door left open.

Ines pushed the paper back across the table with one finger.

"No."

Cassian exhaled slowly. "May I ask why? Genuinely. I want to know where my reasoning fails."

"It fails at the end," Ines said. "You said the voice chooses vessels. Farmers' daughters. Beggars' sons. Anyone." He met the iron-colored eyes. "You're afraid of exactly the right thing, Regent. A god that picks its own messengers can't be ruled. But you've spent three centuries solving that problem by cutting out the ears of the whole world. And the world is starting to hear again. Through bells. Through boys. Through—" he lifted his manacled wrists slightly, "—whatever I am. You can't chain a choosing. You can only delay it. And every year you delay it, the interest comes due."

For a long moment Cassian said nothing. Then he gathered the paper back, squared its edges against the table, and looked at Ines with something that was almost warmth and was entirely more frightening than anger.

"I hoped you'd refuse," he said quietly. "Do you know why? Because a man who refuses an offer like that is either a fool or a believer, and I've had no believers to study in a very long time." He stood. "You'll be kept comfortably. The boy likewise — for now. And in a day or two, Vane, I'm going to move you both, because there is a place I need you to see, and you'll see it whether you walk there or are carried."

"What place?"

Cassian paused at the edge of the candlelight, half his face already gone to shadow.

"The battlefield," he said. "Where the god's body-speech lies buried under a crown of stone. Where every vessel before you died. Where, I suspect, whatever you truly are has been waiting to wake up." The smile returned, thin as a blade's edge. "Rest well, Anchor. You'll want your strength. We ride at dawn — and you should know that I've already sent riders ahead. If your witch and her strays follow us, they follow into the one place in the world where I hold every advantage."

The doors opened. The guards came. And Ines was led back down the long corridor, barefoot on cold stone, counting heartbeats instead of praying — and for the first time in fourteen years, praying anyway.

* * *

The cell was clean, warm, and windowless, which told Ines everything about how valuable he was. They brought him food he had no appetite for and left him alone with a single candle and the sound of distant bells, ringing and ringing, drowning the old tongue out of the air.

He sat against the wall and pressed his palms flat against the floor, the way he had prayed as a child, and waited for the silence of his god.

It didn't come.

Instead — faint, fractured, arriving through the stone like water through a cracked wall — he felt something turn over in the deep places beneath the city. Something old. Something tired. Something that knew his name, and had known it longer than he had.

And beneath the bells, so soft he might have imagined it, Ines heard the beginning of a word.

*Brother.*
