## 14. The Cell Below

The dark in the cell below was not like other darks.

Pell had known the dark of the burning temple, which moved and breathed and ate the light the way a fire eats wood — hungry, alive, chasing him down corridors while the woman with ash on her hands pushed him through a door and said *run east* and shut him out of everything he had ever loved. He had known the dark of the Hollow Valley nights, which was gentle and thick as wool, full of cricket-sound and Ines's slow breathing in the next room. He had even known the dark behind his own eyelids during the fever, when the language poured out of him like water from a cracked jar and he could not stop it and did not want to.

But this dark was still. This dark had been waiting for him.

They had brought him down a stair that wound like the inside of an ear, past doors with iron bells bolted above their lintels — little bells, dull-mouthed, sewn shut with wire so they could never ring. His escort had been a soldier with kind eyes who would not look at him, and a priest with unkind ones who looked too much. The priest had carried a lantern of smoked glass, so that the light came out bruised and yellow, and at the bottom of the stairs he had said, in the tone men use for valuable animals: "He'll be comfortable enough."

Comfortable was a straw pallet. Comfortable was a bucket and a water jug and a candle stub that guttered without wind. Comfortable was walls of old stone sweating cold, carved — Pell ran his fingers over them in the first hour and found them covered — covered with writing.

Not the crown's letters. Not the letters of any kingdom.

His letters.

The old tongue crawled across every stone, floor to ceiling, worn shallow by centuries of touching air, and Pell stood in the middle of his cell with the candle shaking in his hand and read them the way you read your own name written somewhere impossible — on the moon, on the inside of a bird's egg. *Who wrote this?* he thought. *Who has been down here, saying these words, all this time?*

And something answered.

It did not come as sound. That was the first thing he understood, and understanding it made him sit down hard on the pallet, because he had spent his whole life being told what gods were supposed to be — the valley folk spoke of their silent god the way you speak of a rich uncle who never visits, with respect and a little resentment; the crown's bell-priests spoke of nothing at all, only rang and rang to fill the space where speaking should have been — and no one had ever told him that a god's voice might feel like remembering.

It came as remembering. A warmth behind his sternum. A pressure, soft and enormous, like snow settling on a roof. And then words, arriving already translated, arriving as if they had always been his own thoughts and were only now being claimed:

*Small one. At last. At last.*

Pell's mouth went dry. "Hello?" he whispered, and felt foolish, because the voice was not in the room. The voice was in the room the way water is in a well.

*You hear me,* the voice said, and there was such naked astonishment in it, such battered hope, that Pell's eyes filled before he knew why. *Three hundred years I have spoken into stone. Three hundred years I have spoken into bells that cannot carry, into crowns that will not listen, into the sleep of my own broken body. And now a child sits in my grave and says hello.*

"Is this—" Pell swallowed. "Are you the god? The one the stories—"

A pause. When the voice came again it was slower, and it hurt to listen to, the way a limp hurts to watch.

*I was,* it said. *I am what is left. Do not be afraid of me, small one. There is very little left to be afraid of.*

* * *

The candle burned down and Pell lit another from its stub, because he could not bear for the voice to speak in darkness, though the voice assured him it did not see with eyes anymore. He sat cross-legged on the pallet with his knees drawn up, eight years old and the most important person in a kingdom that wanted him in a cage, and he talked with a god.

He had expected thunder. He had expected the great rolling voice out of the temple murals, the one that had split mountains and crowned kings, the voice the war had been fought over. What he got was a whisper with holes in it.

The voice spoke the way an old man speaks after a stroke — sometimes whole rivers of words, clear and strange and beautiful, and sometimes just fragments, single syllables adrift, reaching for a name and finding only the shape where a name used to be.

*Once,* it said, *I said the word for river and the rivers remembered themselves. I said the word for wheat and the wheat stood up in the fields and bowed. Now when I try to say river I find — a wetness, a going, a — the shape of it without the heart of it. You must understand. I am not hiding. I am not sulking in silence, whatever the crowned men believe. I am shattered. I have been shattered since they broke me on this field and buried my speech under a crown of iron to keep the pieces from finding each other.*

"Where are the pieces?" Pell asked. It seemed important to know. It seemed like the kind of question the woman with ash on her hands would ask.

*Some in stones. Some in wells. Some in the bloodlines of families who never knew why their daughters dreamed true or their sons could calm a frightened horse with a touch.* A long pause, and when the voice resumed it was quieter, almost shy. *And some, small one, some walked out of my ruin wearing bodies. Fragments that took flesh and forgot. They live short lives and love small things and die believing they are only human. Most of them never wake.*

Pell thought of Ines then. He thought of the scar at Ines's collarbone that Odile had gone quiet over. He thought of the wound at the river ford that should have killed a man and had closed by morning, slow and strange, like something healing from the inside of time rather than the outside of it. He thought of how the valley had felt around Ines — how the man had stood at the boundary stones like a nail holding down a lid.

"The mercenary," Pell said. "Ines. Is he—"

The warmth behind his sternum flared, sudden and bright, and for one moment the whisper was not a whisper. For one moment the cell filled edge to edge with a presence so vast and so glad that Pell gasped aloud and grabbed the straw beneath him as if the floor might tip.

*BROTHER-FRAGMENT,* the voice said, and the word rang through the stone, through the old tongue written on the walls, through Pell's own bones. *My brother-fragment walks. My brother-fragment breathes and grieves and keeps his oaths to a god he thinks cannot hear him.*

"He can't," Pell said, dazed. "He prays and nothing answers. He told me. He prayed his whole life and nothing ever—"

*Nothing answered him aloud.* The vastness drew back in, folding itself away like a giant lying down in a small bed, until it was a whisper again, tired and tender. *But I heard. Every word, small one. Every night of his life I heard him kneel down in that valley and speak to me as if I were worth speaking to. Do you know what that is like? To be a broken thing in the dark, and to have one fragment of yourself, walking around in a body, kneel down and say* here I am, I don't know if you're listening, but here I am?

Pell did not answer. His throat was too tight.

*He kept me alive,* the voice said simply. *Not the priests. Not the kings with their bells. Him. One man kneeling in a valley where magic cannot cross the border, praying into the silence, year after year. That was a thread of light I followed through three hundred years of dark. That is why I called you east. That is why the woman sent you to his door. I did not send you to a stranger, small one. I sent you home.*

* * *

Time moved strangely below the earth. The guards changed at intervals Pell learned to count by the scrape of their boots, and once a day a slot in the door opened and bread and water came through, and once — only once — the priest with the unkind eyes came down with his smoked-glass lantern and stood in the doorway watching Pell talk to the walls.

"What does it say to you?" the priest demanded. His voice bounced off the written stone and came back smaller.

Pell considered him. He was learning things about himself in this cell — that he could be afraid and steady at the same time, the way a reed bends in wind without breaking. "It says you're standing on someone," he said.

The priest's face went grey. He left and did not come back.

After that, the voice laughed sometimes — a thin, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. *Good,* it murmured. *Let them be uncomfortable on their own graves. Let them lie awake wondering what sleeps under the foundations of their pretty city. Fear is a kind of honesty, small one. It is the only prayer the crowned men have said truly in three hundred years.*

But mostly the voice was not fierce. Mostly the voice was lonely, and Pell began to understand that loneliness was the truest thing about it — truer than its power, truer than its age. It told him about the war in fragments, images arriving behind his eyes like weather: banners burning on the field above; the crown of iron coming down; the terrible moment of being gathered up, gathered apart, spoken into pieces and scattered so that no king could ever again hold a god's tongue against a people's throat.

*They thought they were caging me,* the voice said. *Perhaps they were. But cages work both ways. They locked themselves out of heaven and called the lock peace.*

"Do you hate them?" Pell asked. "The ones who broke you?"

Another long silence. Down the corridor, boots scraped. The candle leaned.

*Hate requires a wholeness I no longer have,* the voice said at last. *I feel the way a mosaic feels, I think — each piece remembering it was once part of a face. No, small one. I do not hate. I miss. I miss hearing myself. I miss the sound of ten thousand voices answering me at dawn, not with worship — worship was always a small thing — but with ordinary speech. Farmers telling me about rain. Mothers complaining about their children. People treating me like a neighbor. That was my kingdom. Not crowns. Kitchens.*

Pell lay back on the straw, tears sliding sideways into his ears, and understood that he was crying for the same reason he had cried when the temple burned: because something irreplaceable was hurting, and he was small, and smallness in the face of great grief feels like guilt.

"I'll help you," he whispered. "I don't know how yet. But I'll help you."

*Oh, small one.* The warmth wrapped around him like a blanket pulled up by unseen hands. *You already have. You crossed the line and let me sing through you and every bell in that city rang backward — did you feel what happened then? Somewhere in that crowd, a girl heard her dead grandmother's voice in the bell-noise. Somewhere a soldier wept without knowing why. The pieces stir when I stir. You are not my vessel. Vessels are empty. You are my echo, and echoes are free.*

"And Ines?" Pell asked, because he needed to say the name aloud, needed it to exist in the air between them. "What happens to him?"

The voice was quiet a long moment. When it spoke, it sounded older than the world.

*That,* it said, *is the question that breaks everything open. Ask it again soon, small one, and loudly. Because the crowned man is coming to this field with his army and his offer, and your brother-fragment is about to be asked to choose between becoming a king's instrument and becoming nothing at all — and he does not yet know that neither of those is the only door.*

"What do you mean? What other door?"

The warmth pulsed, once, like a heartbeat. Like a knock.

*There is a third thing,* the voice said. *There is always a third thing. Tell him: the crown is not the only vessel. Tell him when the moment comes — and it is coming faster than any of you dream — tell him the pieces may choose their own shape.*

Then it faded, sinking back down into the stone like a swimmer resting between strokes, leaving Pell alone in the candlelight with the old words crawling up the walls and the taste of enormous news in his mouth.

Above him, faint through fathoms of earth, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then all of them, the whole city at once, ringing not backward this time but wild, urgent, wrong — and under the ringing, boots on stairs, many boots, coming down fast toward his cell.

Pell sat up. His heart hammered, but the warmth behind his sternum did not flicker, and the last thing the voice gave him, rising through the stone like water through roots, was calm:

*They're coming, small one. Be brave. And remember — tell the brother-fragment. Tell him about the third thing.*
