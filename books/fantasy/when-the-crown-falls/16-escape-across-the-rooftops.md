## 16. Escape Across the Rooftops

The message came folded inside a loaf of bread, which Ines thought was either very clever or very stupid, depending on how hungry the jailer was.

*Third bell. The baker's boy forgets his keys. The roof of the bell-tower is loose. Trust the dark.*

He read it twice by the light of a stub of tallow candle in his rented room above a dyer's shop, where everything — walls, floorboards, the inside of his own nose — smelled faintly of mordant and wet wool. Then he burned it. He had learned that much from Odile: paper could be tortured out of a man's pockets; ash could not.

The city had been ringing for six days now. Not the ordinary bells — those he had grown half-deaf to within two days of arrival — but something beneath them, a wrongness in the intervals, as though the ringers themselves were afraid and trying to drown it out with bronze. Pell was somewhere under all that noise. Ines tried not to imagine what the noise was for. He failed most hours of every day.

Odile arrived at dusk, hood up, moving through the dyer's yard like a woman who had memorized its shadows years ago and found them unchanged. She carried a basket of dyestuffs she did not need and set it down with the care of someone laying down a weapon.

"Third bell," she said. "You'll have perhaps forty breaths between the watch changing on the prison stair and the patrol rounding the granary. Forty breaths is nothing. Forty breaths is everything. It depends entirely on whether you've decided, beforehand, exactly who you are going to be."

"And who is that?"

"Someone who doesn't hesitate." She looked at him then, really looked, and her eyes were old and tired and kind in a way that frightened him more than any threat. "I knew men like you before the war, Ines Vane. Men who were good at waiting. The crown taught them to wait so long they forgot they'd ever been given anything worth taking. Don't be one of them."

"I'm not waiting anymore."

"No," she said softly. "You're not. That's why I'm here."

She unrolled a bundle across the floor: soft-soled boots, a rope wound around an iron hook, a vial of something that smelled like crushed green things and would, she promised, keep a man awake and steady for six hours and cost him two days of shaking afterward. And a key. An actual iron key, worn smooth, stamped with a sigil Ines didn't recognize — a bell crossed with a broken sword.

"Whose?"

"People who remember what the bells used to mean." She closed his fingers over it. "They don't want thanks. They don't want names. They want what you want — the boy out, the truth out, and the regent's pretty machine of silence cracked down the middle. That's enough common ground for one night."

* * *

The prison was built into the base of the great bell-tower itself, because Cassian understood architecture the way spiders understand webs: the thing that held you captive should also be the thing everyone looked up to. Prisoners entered through the tower's shadow and never came out again except in carts, and the city had learned not to look too long at carts.

Ines went in through the baker's alley, where a boy of perhaps ten sat on an overturned crate, whittling a stick into nothing, keys hanging from his belt like he'd forgotten them. He hadn't forgotten them. His eyes flicked to Ines once — quick, assessing, older than ten — and then back to his knife.

"The roof," the boy said, without looking up. "Third tile from the north edge. It lifts. Mind the drop on the other side; there's a walkway, but it's narrow, and if you fall you'll land on the watch-house steps, and they'll be *so* embarrassed, and then you'll be dead."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the bread." He went back to whittling. "And mister? The little lord — the boy — he talks in his sleep. In the old tongue. The whole block hears it. Some of the guards have started dreaming about it. Dreams about standing in a field, and something enormous breathing. They don't report it. They're scared of what happens to people who report things." He paused. "I think your god is already getting out. You're just carrying the rest of him."

Ines climbed.

The wall took his fingers gladly; years of valley stone had taught him hands that trusted cracks. The third tile lifted as promised, and he dropped onto a walkway no wider than a coffin laid sideways, three stories above a courtyard slick with evening rain. Below him, torchlight shivered on wet cobbles. Above him, the bells hung in their open arches like enormous sleeping throats.

Forty breaths.

He counted them. On the twelfth, the watch changed on the stair. On the nineteenth, the patrol rounded the granary and stopped — stopped — to argue about dice. On the twenty-sixth, he reached the barred window of the cell they'd told him about, and hooked the rope, and lowered himself past iron bars into a corridor that smelled of damp straw and old fear, and on the thirty-first breath he stood outside Pell's door with the smooth iron key in his hand.

On the thirty-second, the key turned, and the lock made a sound like a small, apologetic cough.

Pell was sitting upright in the corner, knees drawn to his chest, and when the door opened he did not cry out or scramble away. He simply looked up, and his eyes caught the torchlight from the corridor and held it, and Ines saw that the fever-light that had lived in the boy since the crossing was brighter now, banked but burning, like coals under ash.

"You came," Pell said, in the valley tongue, and then, immediately after, in the old tongue, the words overlapping like ripples from two stones thrown in the same water. "*You came.*"

"I said I would."

"Grown-ups say things." But he was already on his feet, already reaching, and his hand closed around two of Ines's fingers the way he'd done on the first night, and Ines felt something in his chest turn over and settle, the way a house settles, the way a decision settles when it stops being a question.

"Can you climb?" Ines asked.

"I can fly a little," Pell said seriously. "When nobody's watching. Not high. Just a little off the ground, like a step that isn't there. Is anybody watching?"

"Not yet."

"Then I can climb."

They went up the way water goes up — impossibly, quickly, when it has decided to. Out the window, along the coffin-width walkway, the boy ahead of him with his arms out for balance and his bare feet sure as a cat's, and once, only once, Pell stepped onto air where there was no ledge, and hung there for half a heartbeat, weightless, laughing silently, before his foot found stone again.

Below them, a guard looked up at exactly the wrong moment and saw exactly nothing, because the bells chose that instant to begin the night-watch peal, and every face in the courtyard tilted toward the sound like flowers toward a sun that lied.

* * *

The rooftops of the capital at night were a country unto themselves — slate hills and clay valleys, chimneys rising like standing stones, the spaces between buildings bridged by planks left by generations of children and cats and, apparently, rebels. Odile's people had marked the safe route in chalk symbols no larger than a thumbnail: a bell, a bird, a broken line where a jump was longer than honesty.

They moved through that country hand in hand, and Ines learned that Pell counted their jumps aloud in the old tongue, softly, like a prayer, and that the numbers were not numbers. Later, exhausted, hiding in the hollow behind a chimney while a patrol passed below, Pell translated for him: *"One-step. Two-step. Brother-step. Far-step. Home-step."*

"Home-step," Ines repeated.

"That's the last one. Before we stop running." Pell leaned against his side. "The voice says you're brother-fragment. It said it in the cell, when it got quiet. It says the fragments are supposed to find each other. That's why I wasn't scared in the prison. Well. I was scared. But not of being alone."

Ines said nothing. There was a pressure behind his sternum that had been growing for days, since the river ford, since Odile's words by the fire — *your blood moves wrong, too slow, too old* — and it pressed harder every time the boy called him *brother*, and he did not yet know if the pressure was grief or recognition or both wearing each other's clothes.

They crossed the temple district last, because it was the widest gap and the most watched. From the ridge of the old archive, Ines could see the whole sweep of the city below: the palace on its hill with lights burning in every window, the harbor black and glittering, and the streets between them filled — not with patrols, but with people. Crowds, loose and murmuring, moving nowhere in particular. Listening.

"They're listening," Pell whispered. "Everyone's listening. The bells are loud but underneath—" He pressed his palm flat against the slate roof tiles. "Underneath, it's like a heartbeat that isn't theirs. They can feel it. They just don't know what it is yet."

Down in the square below the archive, a woman stopped walking. Stood still. Tilted her head. Then another person, and another, until a dozen strangers stood scattered across the cobbles like trees in a field, all facing slightly different directions, all listening to something Ines could not hear.

His skin prickled. Something was coming. Something was *waking*, and the crown's great machine of bronze was failing to smother it, and every hour the failure grew louder.

"We move now," he said, "and we don't stop until the gate."

* * *

They did not make the gate.

They made the aqueduct instead — the old one, arched high over the river district, its channel dry for a century — because the east gate was already sealed, and Odile's chalk marks ended at the aqueduct's foot with a final symbol Ines hadn't been taught: a bell, crossed out.

Which meant: *no further. This is where we leave you.*

He understood why when he reached the top of the arch and looked back.

The city was waking behind them. Not in panic — in attention. Lamps kindling street by street. Doors opening. People stepping out into the night in their nightclothes, faces turned upward, toward the tower, toward the sky, toward whatever was seeping up through the bronze and the stone. Somewhere a watchman was shouting orders that dissolved into confusion. Somewhere else, a child was singing — high, clear, unmistakably in the old tongue, words the child could not possibly know — and the crowd near the child was not stopping her. The crowd was *repeating* her.

"They'll blame us," Ines said. "Whatever this is. They'll say the stolen relic did it."

"Yes," said a voice from the shadow of the aqueduct's piers, and Odile stepped out of it, breathless, her gray braid half-undone, a cut on her cheekbone she clearly hadn't bothered to mention. Behind her, three figures Ines took for her strays — the ferryman's daughter, the one-eyed ex-soldier, the quiet boy with the scarred hands. "That's rather the point. While the city looks at itself, no one looks at the east road. We bought you the night, Ines. Spend it well."

"You're not coming?"

Her smile was thin as paper. "Someone has to stay and be blamed convincingly. And someone has to make sure the blame lands on me and not on every hedge-witch and stray between here and the mountains." She pressed something into his hand — a folded map, oilcloth-wrapped, and a small tin of salve that smelled of comfrey and smoke. "The pilgrim road, then the shrine road, then the field. You know the field. Your blood will know it before your head does. When you get there—" Her voice caught, steadied. "When you get there, remember that I helped put the silence on it. Whatever you decide to do with what's buried there, decide it knowing that. Don't let me be the only one carrying it."

"Odile—"

"Go." She shoved him, gently, toward the east. "And boy—" She crouched to Pell's height, and her weathered face did something complicated and tender. "When the voice wakes fully, and it will, tell it Odile kept her word. Eventually. Late counts, with gods. Tell it late counts."

Pell kissed her forehead, solemn as a small priest, and said something in the old tongue that made her close her eyes as though struck.

"What did he say?" Ines asked.

"He said—" She laughed, wet-eyed. "He said the voice remembers everyone who listened. Even the ones who helped tie its mouth. *Especially* those."

Then they ran, east along the dry aqueduct, the city glowing behind them like a hearth seen from a winter road, and Ines did not look back again because looking back was a luxury the night could not afford.

* * *

Dawn found them in the hills, in a shepherd's hut with a collapsed wall and a view of the whole valley road below. Ines built a fire small enough to hide and woke Pell gently, and the boy surfaced from sleep already talking, in the old tongue, mid-sentence, as though he'd been carrying on a conversation all night with someone Ines couldn't see.

"It says hurry," Pell reported, blinking. "It says the man with the nice voice knows where we're going. It says—" The boy frowned, listening to something. "It says he's *letting* us."

Ines went very still.

"Say that again."

"Cassian. The nice-voice man. The voice says the gates weren't guarded because he wanted us through. It says the escape was—" Pell's small brow furrowed, working at the shape of it. "—a gift. Gifts are hooks. He wants us to run somewhere specific. He wants us to lead him to—" The boy's eyes came up, wide and suddenly frightened. "To the field. To the grave. He wants *us* to find it for him, because he can't. Because the grave only shows itself to—" He touched his own chest. "—to fragments."

The fire crackled. Somewhere downslope, a shepherd's dog barked twice and went silent.

Every muscle in Ines's body wanted to deny it, to find the flaw in it, to believe that for one night something had gone *right*. But he thought of the sealed gate that hadn't been sealed. Of the patrol that had argued about dice at the exact right moment. Of Cassian's calm, patient face in the throne room, the way a spider is patient. Of the regent's parting words: *Run, then. Run home to it.*

"He let us go," Ines said quietly. "From the beginning. Every door we thought we broke, he opened."

Pell nodded slowly. Then, with the terrible practicality of children, he asked the only question that mattered:

"So do we run somewhere else?"

Ines looked east, where the hills rolled down toward the pilgrim road, toward the shrine road, toward the old battlefield where a crown-shaped monument stood over the body-speech of a dead-or-dying god. He looked at the boy — his fever-bright eyes, his bare feet, his hand trustingly wrapped around two of Ines's fingers.

If they fled, Cassian would hunt them across every mile of the world, and the voice would wake anyway, ungoverned, and the regent would tear the kingdom apart searching. If they went on — they walked into the trap, but they walked into it *choosing* it, and traps could be turned. Odile's map. Odile's guilt. Odile's rebels, scattered like seed behind them. And a grave that showed itself only to fragments — which meant that whatever waited there answered to Pell, and to him, and to no crown.

"No," Ines said. "We run somewhere *first*. We just run faster than he expects, and we arrive before he does, and when he comes to collect his gift—" He fed a stick to the fire, and the flame leapt, and for a moment his shadow on the hut wall looked like something with wings. "—we'll have already opened it. Let him chase a grave that's already empty."

Pell considered this with the gravity of a general. "The voice says that's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's usually both."

"Okay." The boy smiled then, fever-bright and fearless, and lay back against Ines's arm. "Brother-step," he murmured, already sliding toward sleep. "Far-step. Home-step."

Ines sat watch as the sun rose over the hills, and far behind them, faint on the wind, the bells of the capital began to ring — and this time, he could almost swear, some of them rang backward.
