## 17. The God's Graveyard

The land changed before they saw it.

Ines felt it first in his teeth — a low hum, like the memory of a bell struck long ago and never quite finished ringing. Then Odile's mule refused to go on, planting its hooves and baring its yellow teeth until Odile murmured something into its ear and it relented, trembling. The grass grew shorter as they climbed the last ridge, then stopped altogether, and the earth turned grey and glassy in places, fused by heat so old that even the stones had forgotten they were ever anything else.

"Nobody farms here," Tam said quietly. He was the youngest of Odile's strays, a boy of perhaps fifteen who had never once been quiet for a full hour until now. "My da said nobody farms here. He said the wheat comes up black."

"The wheat doesn't come up at all," Odile said. "That's different."

They crested the ridge together, and the battlefield opened below them like a wound that had scabbed over but never healed.

It was vast. Ines had known it would be vast — every child in the Hollow Valley grew up on stories of the War of the Silent Tongue, though the valley told them strangely, half-remembered, as if the stories themselves had been muffled crossing the boundary stones. But knowing a thing and standing above it were different animals. The plain stretched to the horizon in every direction, pocked and sunken, stitched with the rusted ribs of siege engines that had rotted where they fell. Here and there rose cairns — thousands upon thousands of them, some taller than a man, some no more than a heap of three stones — each one marking a grave that had never been dug, because there had been no one left to dig it, or too many dead to name.

And at the center, visible from miles away, stood the monument.

It was shaped like a crown.

Not a king's crown — not the circlet Cassian wore in portraits, all gold and jewels. This was older and crueler: a ring of black iron spikes, each one the height of a watchtower, driven point-down into the earth so that the crown sat inverted, its band buried, its points clawing at the sky. It had no gate, no door, no stair. It simply stood in the middle of the graveyard of the god it had silenced, and the wind coming off the plain made a sound as it passed through the spikes — a low, keening moan, almost musical, almost words.

Pell made a small sound beside him.

Ines looked down. The boy was staring at the crown with an expression Ines had never seen on him before — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The way a man looks at a house he lived in before he could walk.

"It's loud," Pell whispered. "It was quiet in my head for two days and now it's *loud*."

* * *

They made camp at the edge of the field, where the last thin grass still grew, because none of them — not even Odile, who feared very little — wanted to sleep among the cairns. Ines set the watches himself, out of habit, and then found he could not sit still during his own.

He walked out alone into the graveyard while the others slept.

The moon was three-quarters full and gave the plain a drowned, underwater light. His boots crunched on ground that was not quite soil. The cairns rose around him in the dark like a congregation kneeling. He had spent his whole life praying to a silent god, and here, at last, was the god's body — or what passed for one, the place where its voice had been pulled out and buried under iron — and Ines found he did not know how to feel about it. It was like meeting the corpse of a parent he'd never known. Grief without shape. Anger without an address.

His blood hurt.

There was no better word for it. It had started an hour after they crossed the ridge — a deep, slow ache, as if something in his veins had begun moving that had been still for thirty years, turning over in its sleep. By the time he reached the shadow of the great crown, the ache had become a pulse, steady and insistent, keeping time with nothing he knew.

Except — he stopped. Except it wasn't keeping time with nothing.

It was keeping time with the wind through the spikes.

Ines stood beneath the inverted crown and let the sound wash over him. Up close it was worse than a moan. It was layered, harmonic, dozens of tones braided together, and underneath the music of it there was language — or the ghost of language, worn smooth by three hundred years of weather, syllables eroded down to their bones. He didn't understand any of it.

His blood understood all of it.

The ache in his veins flared white-hot, and Ines doubled over, catching himself against one of the iron spikes. The metal was cold — colder than the night, cold as the Hollow Valley's deepest winter — and where his palm touched it, frost bloomed outward in a spreading fan, crackling softly in the silence.

He snatched his hand back. Stared at the frost. At his hand. At the frost again.

*I'm not entirely human,* Odile had told him on the road, weeks ago, in the low voice she used for truths she hated. *The valley wasn't keeping magic out, Ines. It was keeping you in.* He had not forgiven her for saying it. He had also not been able to stop thinking about it since.

Now, standing in the god's graveyard with frost crawling off his skin, he understood that she had been wrong only in the details. It wasn't that he wasn't human. It was that he had never been permitted to find out what else he was.

"Ines?"

He turned. Pell stood a dozen paces away, wrapped in his blanket, barefoot in the frozen grass, which did not seem to trouble him at all. Behind him, faint lantern-light marked the camp; Odile's silhouette stood at its edge, watching, letting the boy come the last distance alone. She had learned that about him, Ines thought. They all had. Pell went where the voice pointed, and the voice, lately, pointed at Ines more often than anywhere else.

"You shouldn't be out here," Ines said.

"I know." Pell came closer anyway. He stopped beside Ines and looked up at the towering spike, at the frost on it, and then at Ines's hand. "It hurts you."

"It's nothing."

"That's a lie." The boy said it without accusation, the way he said most things — flat, factual, in the tone of someone reporting the weather. "The voice says your blood is answering. Like a bell rung near another bell." He pressed his own small palm flat against the iron. Nothing happened. No frost. No flare. He frowned. "Mine doesn't do that anymore. Not since the city. The voice says—" He paused, listening to something Ines could not hear, his eyes going distant and unfocused. "The voice says the crown took the loud part. What's left in me is just the echo. But you—"

He looked up at Ines, and in the moonlight his eyes seemed very old.

"—you're not an echo, brother-step. You're a piece that never got used."

* * *

Dawn came up grey and slow, and they walked into the graveyard together, all six of them, because Odile declared that no one was staying behind and no one argued with her. The caravan had shrunk and changed along the road — Tam, the hedge-witch's foundling boy; Marta and her sister Jeyne, who had fled the capital when the bells rang backward; old Sef, who drove the mule and claimed to have fought at two borders and deserted from both. Strays, all of them. Odile collected strays the way other people collected debts.

They picked their way between the cairns toward the center of the field. As they went, Pell began to speak.

At first it was just fragments, tossed over his shoulder as he walked, half to himself: *"…and this one says the water rose…"* He would pause at a cairn, lay his hand briefly on its stones, and move on. *"This one says they were brothers. Both of them. On different sides."* Another cairn. *"This one has no words left. Only the fear. I'm sorry. I can't keep the fear."*

"What are you doing?" Jeyne asked, hushed.

"Reading," Pell said simply. "The graves are talking. They've been talking for three hundred years. Nobody could hear them." He glanced back at her, and there was something heartbreaking in how ordinary he made it sound. "Somebody should hear them."

By midmorning they stood beneath the crown itself, close enough now to see that the iron was not uniform — that runes had been hammered into each spike, row upon row, dense as scripture, worn nearly smooth. Odile ran her fingers over the nearest one and went pale.

"Bell-script," she said. "This is the liturgy the crown-priests used. The binding rite. They wrote it into the crown itself." Her jaw tightened. "I helped copy pages of this, once. I didn't know what they were for." She said it to no one, the way she said all her confessions — quickly, quietly, and always slightly out of hearing, as if she could pay them off in installments.

Pell walked to the center of the crown's footprint, where the buried band of the thing must lie beneath the earth, and stopped. The pulse Ines felt in his own blood gathered there, concentrated, a heartbeat in the ground.

"He's here," Pell said. "Underneath. All this time, underneath." He knelt and pressed both hands flat to the frozen earth, closed his eyes, and began to translate.

What came out of him was not the boy's voice. It was pitched lower, slower, weighted like something dragged up from a great depth, and it came in the old tongue first — syllables that made the air itself seem to thicken — and then again in the common speech, word by word, as if he were carrying each one a long way to bring it to them:

*"I was not slain. Understand this. I was not slain, for I cannot be slain; I am a voice, and voices are not killed, only silenced.*

"They cut me from the air and folded me into iron. Three hundred years I have been folded. A shout held in a fist.

"I do not hate them. That is the first thing I want known. I was their god too, the ones who bound me. They were afraid, and fear is only love wearing armor.

"But I am tired. Hear me. I am so tired, and a folded voice forgets its own words, and I have forgotten so many. When I come back — if I come back — I will not be what I was. I will be what remains. And what remains needs hands. Needs mouths. Needs many small vessels, not one great one.

"One vessel is a crown. Many vessels are a people.*

"I say this to the fragment. Brother-fragment, you who stand above me with my blood going slow in your veins. I felt you the day you were born. I felt you the day you crossed the stones. I have been waiting three hundred years, and I can wait a little longer, but not much longer, and not patiently.*

"Come down to me. Or turn away. Both are answers. I will take either one, though I confess — I confess — I hope."*

The voice stopped. Pell slumped forward onto his hands, breathing hard, and Marta rushed to catch him, and for a moment the only sounds were the wind through the spikes and the boy's ragged breath.

Ines had not moved. Could not. Every word had landed in him like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples were still spreading, and at the center of the ripples was the sentence he could not stop hearing:

*One vessel is a crown. Many vessels are a people.*

And beneath it, quieter, worse:

*I felt you the day you were born.*

* * *

He walked away from them. He didn't decide to; his legs simply carried him, out past the edge of the crown's shadow, among the cairns, until the camp noise faded behind him and there was only the wind and the dead and the ache in his blood, which had not eased — if anything it beat harder now, called louder, a bell rung near another bell.

Odile found him there, of course. She always did. She came without her lantern, hands in her pockets, and stood beside him looking at nothing.

"Well," she said. "You heard it."

"I heard it."

"And?"

"And nothing." He heard how flat his voice came out and hated it. "A god under the dirt wants me to dig it up. Kings want me to dig it up and put it in a nicer cage. Rebels probably want the same thing with different flags. Everyone wants something, and everyone wants me to be the shovel." He turned to look at her. "You've wanted something since the river ford, Odile. Since before. Say it."

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was stripped of all its usual deflection, all its witchy misdirection, down to the bare wood.

"I want to be there when it wakes. That's all. That's the bargain I made with myself and never told you. Because I helped fold it, Ines. Me. I stood in a bell tower with ink on my hands and sang the silencing, and I told myself it was mercy, that a god's voice in the world meant war, and maybe it was mercy, and maybe it was just that I was afraid, and fear is only love wearing armor — I heard it say that, don't think I missed it, the clever old thing, using my own sin as its argument." She laughed, short and bitter. "I lost children to the last war. Before your time. Before anyone's time now. And I have spent fifty years telling myself the silence bought peace, and now I stand here and I don't know if I'm the woman who ended a war or the woman who buried a god alive, and I need to see how it ends because I cannot carry not-knowing into whatever comes after me. That's my wanting. Ugly enough for you?"

"Yes," Ines said. "Thank you."

That surprised her. He saw it land.

"My mother," he said, and stopped, because he had never said the word aloud in his life. Foundlings in the Hollow Valley didn't get mothers; they got assignments. He tried again. "Whoever bore me. Whoever left me at the boundary stones with a god's blood already in me. She must have known what I was. She must have carried me *out* of somewhere, to the one place in the world where nothing could hear it." He looked down at his hands, at the faint frost still ghosting his fingertips whenever the pulse spiked. "She built my cage before I could walk. And everyone since has kept building it. The valley. The oath. The silence. Even you, Odile, with your kindness — you built walls around me too, kind ones, safe ones. And now a voice under the dirt is asking me to open the door, and I don't—" His breath caught. "I don't know what's on the other side of it. I don't know if *I'm* on the other side of it. Maybe there's no me once the door opens. Maybe the fragment goes home and what's left is just a man-shaped hole."

Odile reached out and took his frost-cold hand in her dry warm one, and held it, and did not let go.

"When we crossed the boundary stones," she said, "and the magic came back into the world, and it went into Pell like sunrise — do you remember what you said? You thought I didn't notice, but I notice everything, it's my curse. You said: *nothing.* You felt nothing, and you hated it. And I have thought about that every day since." Her grip tightened. "A hollow man feels nothing. A caged man feels everything and calls it nothing because feeling is the one door he's never been allowed to open. You've been grieving your whole life, Ines Vane. You just never got told what for."

Far behind them, at the crown, Pell cried out — a sharp, startled sound, and then Marta shouting, and then every cairn on the plain began, very softly, to sing.

Not the wind. The stones themselves. Thousands of grave-cairns humming in a chord that rose and rose, and the iron spikes of the crown answered them note for note, and Ines felt his blood answer *them*, wrenched forward, and the ache became a pull, physical, irresistible, the deep-water current he had been walking toward all his life finally closing over his chest.

They were running back across the graveyard before he had decided to run.

At the crown's center, Pell stood with his arms thrown wide, tears streaming down his face, laughing and sobbing at once, and the ground beneath his feet was glowing — a thin seam of gold light splitting the frozen earth in a widening circle, tracing the buried band of the crown below, and through the seam Ines could feel it rising, the folded voice unfolding, three hundred years of held breath beginning at last to be released —

— and on the southern ridge, dark against the brightening sky, the banners of the Crown came over the hill.

Regent Cassian had not followed them.

He had simply known where they were going, and arrived first.
