## 19. The Choice of Fragments

The battlefield did not look like a graveyard. That was the first thing Ines understood, standing at the edge of it in the grey hour before dawn, when the mist lay over the plain like a sheet drawn up to the chin of a corpse. It looked like a meadow. Long grass bent and whispered under a wind that came from nowhere, wildflowers he had no names for nodding in the dark — white ones, pale as bone, growing only here, feeding on something beneath the soil that was not quite soil.

Beneath the flowers: three hundred thousand dead. Beneath the dead: the god.

And above them all, at the center of the plain where no grass grew, the monument rose against the lightening sky — a crown the size of a chapel, carved from black stone shot through with veins of something that caught the starlight and held it too long, the way a dying man holds a hand. It had no doors. It had no windows. It had only that shape, that terrible familiar shape, repeated in every kingdom's regalia for three centuries: the crown that had once been worn by a god, or had worn a god, depending on which story you believed.

Pell stood beside him, small hands clenched in the fabric of Ines's coat, and the boy was trembling so hard Ines could feel it through his own bones.

"It's singing," Pell whispered. "It never stopped."

Ines heard nothing. He had spent forty years hearing nothing — a silent god, an empty valley, a life lived in the quiet between one heartbeat and the next. But now, as they walked out onto the plain, something began to answer in him anyway. Not sound. Pressure. A deep ache behind his sternum, spreading outward along his arms like cold water rising in a well, and with each step toward the crown-stone the ache sharpened into something that felt almost like recognition.

*Brother-fragment,* the voice had called him, through Pell's mouth, in the cell below the city. He had hated the words then. He understood them now. Every step was a bell tolling inside his own chest.

* * *

They had come ahead of the army. Odile had insisted on it — *if we wait for Cassian, we've already lost; he moves armies the way other men move chess pieces* — and so the caravan had traveled through the last night in the dark, torches hooded, wheels wrapped in wool, following Pell's unerring sense of the thing beneath the earth. Now the wagons sat at the field's edge among the standing stones, and behind them, far to the south, the horizon glimmered with the torch-line of the regent's host. Ten thousand men. They would arrive by midmorning. Cassian would want to watch.

Odile came across the grass to meet them, her stick sinking into the soft ground, her face in the pre-dawn light looking older than Ines had ever seen it — older than the war she would not speak of, older than the graves she had helped dig with bell-priest hands.

"He'll offer you terms," she said. No preamble. She had stopped wasting words on him somewhere outside the City of Bells. "He already sent a herald ahead, did you see? White banner. He wants to negotiate because he thinks negotiation is how you win. He'll offer you a name. Land. Legitimacy. A place at court. Everything the valley never gave you." Her eyes were steady and pitiless and kind all at once, which was a trick Ines had never learned. "He knows what you are now, Ines. He's known since the ford. He's been building this offer for you the way a carpenter builds a coffin — to your exact measure."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked past him, at Pell, at the boy's white face turned up toward the black crown. "Because I've watched men stand in front of exactly what they've wanted their whole lives, and I have never once seen one of them walk away from it clean."

Ines said nothing. The ache in his chest had become a chorus.

* * *

Dawn came slowly, the way it does over old battlefields, reluctant, as if the sun itself remembered what happened here. And with the light came the herald — alone, unarmed, walking the long mile from the army's edge with the white banner furled under his arm like a sleeping snake. He stopped ten paces away and bowed, precisely, the bow of a man who has rehearsed this moment.

"Ines Vane," the herald said. "The Regent Cassian, Protector of the Realm, Voice of the Crown, bids you hear his terms."

"Voice of the Crown," Odile repeated softly, behind him. "Listen to what they call themselves. They've always known."

"The Regent offers—" the herald began.

"I know what he offers." Ines surprised himself with the steadiness of his own voice. "A name. A house. A place. A family." He paused. "Tell me the rest. Tell me what he wants me to do."

The herald's composure flickered — just slightly, just at the edges, the way ice flickers before it goes. "The voice must be bound," he said. "It cannot be permitted to speak freely. Three hundred years of peace—"

"Three hundred years of silence," Odile corrected, "are not the same thing."

"—three hundred years of peace," the herald continued, with the practiced patience of a man reciting scripture, "have been purchased at the cost of one imprisonment. One vessel at a time. One child at a time. The Regent proposes to renew the binding — a new crown, forged from the old stone, with the voice sealed within and guarded by an order sworn to the throne rather than to any god. He asks only this: that you, who carry the divine blood, lend your fragment to the working. Your blood will complete what the bell-priests began. And in exchange, you will be given everything you have never had."

Everything he had never had.

Ines stood very still. The wind moved through the white flowers. Somewhere beneath his feet, three hundred thousand soldiers slept on, and beneath them, deeper than graves go, something vast and tired and broken shifted in its long sleep, and the shifting passed up through the soil and the roots and the bones of his feet and became, in him, a longing so acute it was indistinguishable from grief.

*A name.* He thought of the foundling ledger in the valley temple, the blank line where a family should have been written. *A house.* He thought of the small stone rooms he had rented all his life, never nailed anything to the walls. *A family.* He thought of —

He thought of the caravan. Of Odile's terrible crooked kindness. Of Tam and the twins arguing about whose turn it was to feed the mules. Of Sefa teaching Pell card games the boy cheated at, badly, joyfully. Of the fire at night, and the way Pell had begun, somewhere on the road, to fall asleep leaning against Ines's shoulder without asking permission, the way children do when they have decided someone is safe.

That was the trap. That was the exact measure of the coffin. Cassian had not offered him gold or power, because Cassian had read him correctly — had read the fragment-blood in him, had understood what a man becomes when he is raised without a name. He had offered him belonging. And he had offered it at the price of the boy.

Because Ines understood that too, now, standing on the god's grave with the terms ringing in his ears. The binding required a vessel. It had always required a vessel. The old crown had not held the voice the way a bottle holds water; it had held it the way a fist holds a bird. And the fist needed something living inside it to close around. Three hundred years, one child at a time. Pell, caged in black stone until the stone wore through him and they went hunting for another vessel, another feverish child speaking a language older than kingdoms, another mother burning in a temple doorway saying *run east.*

"They'll dress it gently," Odile said quietly, when the herald had gone. "They always do. They'll say the boy will be honored. Revered. They'll build him a shrine and call his cage a sanctum and put flowers in front of it every year, and in fifty years no one will remember there was ever a child inside it at all. That's how it was done before. I remember the ceremonies." Her jaw tightened. "I sang in them."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then he takes the boy, and does it anyway, and kills everyone who helped him escape. He has ten thousand men, Ines. We have seven wagons and a witch with bad knees."

Ines looked down at Pell. The boy was staring at the crown-stone, and tears were running silently down his face, and his lips were moving — translating, always translating, the fragments the voice gave him in its ruined, patient way.

"What's it saying?" Ines asked.

Pell wiped his face with the heel of his hand. When he spoke, his voice was very small. "It says it's sorry. It says it's been sorry for three hundred years. It says—" the boy faltered, listening to something Ines could not hear, would never hear, "—it says it doesn't want to be saved if saving means another cage. It says it would rather end. It says—" and here Pell's voice cracked, and he pressed both hands flat against the ground, against the soil, as though feeling for a pulse, "—it says *let me be weather.*"

Let me be weather.

Not a king's oracle. Not a crown's instrument. Not a treasure locked in a sanctum, tended by priests, milked for prophecy. Rain. Wind. The shared murmur of a thing dispersed beyond anyone's reach — falling on kings and farmers alike, answering no one, owned by no one, feared by no one because nothing free can be commanded.

To give it that, Ines would have to shatter the crown. And the crown was not merely stone. He knew that now; he had known it since the ford, since the wound that would not close, since Odile had knelt over him in the wagon and told him the truth his whole life had been built to hide. His blood was in it. The fragment-blood, the divine remainder, the piece of the god that had been cut away and hidden in a valley and raised as a mercenary and taught to pray to its own severed self without ever knowing it. To break the crown, he would have to pour that blood back. All of it. Whatever he was — the strange slow blood, the strength that should have died at the river, the forty years of hollow quiet — it would go into the breaking, and he did not know what would be left after.

Maybe nothing. Maybe a man. Maybe a stain on the grass among the white flowers.

Cassian had offered him everything he'd never had. The god offered him nothing at all — only weather, only freedom, only an ending that might also be a beginning, with no guarantee attached.

* * *

Midmorning. The army crested the southern ridge like a tide of iron, banners snapping, and the sound of ten thousand boots reached the plain as a low rolling thunder that made the white flowers tremble. Cassian rode at the head of them on a grey horse, and even at this distance Ines could see the new crown glinting on his brow — not the black stone, not yet, but the lesser crown of office, the promise of the greater one.

The regent halted his host at the field's edge. He did not advance. He didn't need to. He dismounted, and walked forward alone, the way a man walks into a church, and stopped where the herald had stopped, and looked at Ines with an expression of genuine, unhurried warmth.

"You haven't touched it," Cassian said. "Good. Wise. It can't be handled carelessly." He gestured at the monument with something like reverence. "Do you know what I felt, the first time I stood here? I was nineteen. My father brought me. Everyone feels it — the weight. The presence. Even people with no gift at all feel it. Three centuries of silence, and still the ground hums. That is power, Ines. Real power. Not the theater of bells and thrones. And it is sitting under a rock in a field, doing nothing, while the realm frays apart." He spread his hands. "I'm not your enemy. I want to make you a prince of the blood. Legitimate. Named. Recorded in the rolls beside your ancestors — yes, we know who they were; the valley records were ours before they were yours. You'd be the first of your line to walk in daylight. And the boy—" his gaze slid to Pell, gentle, dreadful, "—the boy would be the most honored child in the history of the world. Every priest in the realm would tend him. Every king to come would kneel at his shrine. He would never want for anything."

"Except the door," Ines said.

Cassian's smile did not waver. "There is no door. There never was. That is the nature of sacred things. Ask your witch — she sang at the last binding. Ask her whether the vessel suffered."

"He means me," Pell said suddenly, stepping forward, and his voice carried across the grass with a resonance that made the nearest soldiers shift and mutter. "Ask me. I'm the vessel. I'm right here." The boy's chin lifted. "You're asking a dead man's opinion about being buried."

For one moment — Ines saw it, would remember it all his life — Cassian's certainty flickered. The regent recovered quickly; he had been managing men for thirty years. But the boy had landed a blow no sword in the host behind him could have landed, and everyone on that field knew it.

"You have until dusk," Cassian said at last, coolly. "Then my engineers begin, with you or without you. Bind it willingly, Vane, and be a prince. Refuse, and be a footnote." He turned, and walked back toward his army, and the tide of iron parted to receive him.

* * *

Dusk came the way it comes on battlefields: red, and slow, and full of birds coming home to roost in the standing stones.

The caravan stood with them at the foot of the monument. Ines had told them to go. He had said it plainly, at the last, kneeling in the grass beside the wagons: *this is not your fight, take Pell's trail south, cross the border mountains, disappear.* And Odile had looked at him with those pitiless kind eyes and said, "I helped bury this god once. You don't get to do the second funeral without me." And Tam had started hitching the mules — facing the wrong direction. And Sefa had said, "We're not going anywhere, you great stupid wall of a man," and the twins had nodded, and Pell had simply taken his hand and not let go.

So they stood together in the failing light, seven wagons' worth of strays against ten thousand spears, and Ines felt something in his chest that was not the god's ache at all. It was smaller and hotter and entirely human. It had no name in any language, old or new. It was the thing the valley had never given him and Cassian could never sell him, because it could not be given or sold — only chosen, and choosing was the whole of it.

"Odile," he said. "How did the bell-priests bind it last time?"

"Blood," she said. "It's always blood. They took a vessel's blood and wrote the binding into the crown with it — wrote the cage in the language of the prisoner. That's why it needs your fragment. Your blood is the god's own. A cage written in the god's blood cannot be broken from outside." She paused. "Or from inside. Which is why no one has ever tried."

"And if I use the blood the other way? Not to write the cage. To break it?"

"Then you're writing your own death-warrant in the same ink," she said flatly. "I won't lie to you. I've read the old rites. Dispersion requires the whole fragment returned. What's left afterward—" she shook her head, "—no record. Because no one has ever done it and lived to keep records."

"But it might work."

"Anything might work. That's not a reason." She gripped his sleeve, hard, her knuckles white. "Ines. Listen to me. I have buried children. I have sung at bindings. I have spent sixty years paying for one bad choice made by a frightened girl who believed the priests when they said it was necessary. If you do this and die, I will carry two funerals instead of one, and I am too old, do you hear me, I am too old to carry it." Her voice broke, finally, on the last word, and Ines saw beneath the witch the girl who had once stood in a choir loft and helped a crown cage a god.

He covered her hand with his own.

"Then help me make it quick," he said.

* * *

What followed, he did partly by rite and mostly by instinct, because the rites had been written by jailers and instinct was the only inheritance the valley had left him.

He climbed the monument. There were no stairs; the black stone accepted his hands and feet the way it accepted nothing else, the veins of trapped starlight warming under his palms, and he understood that the crown recognized him — had been waiting for him, perhaps, for forty years, for three hundred years, for however long a fragment can be kept from its whole. At the summit, the stone opened. Not dramatically. Quietly, the way a hand opens. Inside was a hollow shaped like nothing so much as a cradle, and in the cradle lay the old crown itself — not the monument, but the true crown, black metal grown dense as a collapsed star, humming with a frequency he felt in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the slow old blood that was about to stop being his.

Below, the army stirred. Horns sounded. Cassian's voice rang out, commanding, threatening, promising. Torches bloomed across the ridge like a rash of orange stars. None of it mattered. None of it could reach the top of this stone in time, and none of it, Ines understood with perfect clarity, had ever really been able to reach him at all.

He drew his knife — the plain mercenary's knife, valley-forged, the only honest thing he had ever owned — and opened his palm along the old scar from the river ford, and held his hand over the crown.

"Bird," he said aloud, to the thing in the cage, in the tongue of the oath he had sworn at nine years old to a god he hadn't known was himself. "I'm opening the fist."

And he poured his blood onto the crown, and spoke the binding backward.

It is difficult to describe what happened next, because description belongs to the world where things have edges, and the edges went first. The blood did not run off the black metal; it sank in, and where it sank the metal remembered that it had once been sky. Light came out of the crown the way sound comes out of a struck bell — all at once, total, in every direction — and Ines felt himself pouring out with it, forty years of held breath leaving his body, the fragment unraveling from him thread by thread like a banner coming loose in a gale. He heard, for the first and last time, the god's voice whole: not mighty, not terrible, but relieved — a sound like a great animal lying down at the end of a long day — and it said, in the old tongue, in Pell's translation echoing up from below, *thank you, brother-fragment, thank you, thank you—*

He seized the crown in both bleeding hands.

And he shattered it.

Not with strength. With permission. That was the secret the jailers had never understood, the secret written in the god's own blood: a cage written in the prisoner's language can be unlocked by the prisoner's consent. The god consented. Pell, below, arms flung wide, screaming the words of release into the darkening air, consented. And the crown — the true crown, the fist that had held the bird for three hundred years — came apart like frost in sunlight, like a lie told so long it finally forgets itself, and the voice went up.

Weather. Rain and wind and murmur, dispersing across the plain, across the ridge, into ten thousand soldiers who staggered in their ranks as something ancient and gentle brushed the inside of their skulls and asked them, each one, *what does your heart say?* — and the horns faltered, and the lines wavered, and men sat down in the grass among the white flowers and wept for reasons they would spend the rest of their lives explaining badly.

Ines fell.

He fell the long way down the side of the monument, and the last thing he saw before the grass came up to meet him was the caravan running toward him through the dispersion-light — Odile hobbling and swearing, Pell flying ahead of all of them, arms outstretched, the boy's face lit from within by the freed voice streaming past him like a river in flood — and the last thing he thought was not a prayer, because prayers were for gods, and the god was gone now, everywhere, free.

The last thing he thought was: *so this is what my name weighs. Nothing. Nothing at all. How light. How light—*

The grass received him. The white flowers closed over him like a book.

And somewhere above, in the first rain the battlefield had felt in three hundred years, the voice — dispersed, diminished, everywhere and nowhere, belonging to everyone — began, very softly, to sing.
