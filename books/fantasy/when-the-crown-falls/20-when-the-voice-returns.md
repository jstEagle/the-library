## 20. When the Voice Returns

**Ines**

He was falling through white.

Not darkness — he had expected darkness, had braced for it the way a man braces for cold water — but light, soft and total, like being held inside the petals of some enormous flower. The pain in his chest was gone. The pain in his *name* was gone. He floated in a place that smelled of rain on old stone and something sweeter beneath it, honey and myrrh and woodsmoke, the smell of temples he had never entered.

*Brother-fragment,* said a voice.

It did not come from outside him. It came from everywhere, from the grass and the rain and the marrow of his own bones, and it was not mighty. That was the strangest thing. He had spent his whole life imagining what a god's voice would sound like if it ever spoke again — thunder, surely, or the grinding of continents. But this voice was tired. It was warm. It sounded like his mother might have sounded, if he had ever had one, calling him home across a field at dusk.

*I am so small now,* the voice said, wondering at itself. *I am so many.*

"Where am I?" Ines asked, and heard his own voice crack apart in the whiteness. "Am I dying?"

*A little,* the voice said gently. *You broke yourself to break me free. That was brave and foolish and I have loved you for three hundred years without once being able to say so.*

The words went through him like sunlight through water. He had not known he could be loved by anything larger than a boy and a witch and a caravan of strays. He had not known he needed it until it arrived.

"What happens now?" he whispered. "To me. To Pell. To all of it."

*Now I learn to be weather instead of a king's instrument,* the voice said. *Now everyone who has an ear learns they have always had one. It will frighten them. Frightened people do terrible things, brother-fragment. You know this better than most.* A pause, and in the pause he felt the voice turn its attention outward, toward the battlefield, toward the army still standing in the rain with their banners drooping and their faces turned upward like men listening to a distant bell. *But first — you must come back. They will need you. Not as a weapon. As a witness.*

"I don't want to be anyone's witness," Ines said. "I want to sleep."

*Yes,* the voice agreed, and there was laughter in it now, thin and cracked but real. *That too. Sleep, then. But lightly. Lightly, Ines Vane. The world is about to get very loud.*

The whiteness folded around him like a blanket drawn up by careful hands.

* * *

**Pell**

Pell stood in the rain with his arms out and his mouth open and let the voice pour through him.

It did not hurt anymore. That was the miracle of it. For his whole remembered life the voice had been a stone in his chest, a fever, a word stuck sideways in his throat that no tongue in the Hollow Valley could pronounce. Now the stone had shattered into ten thousand grains of sand and every grain was singing, and the singing passed through him the way wind passes through an open window — touching everything, snagging on nothing.

Around him, people were falling to their knees.

Not just their ragged band — though Odile had both hands pressed over her mouth and Tam was weeping openly into the wet grass and the hedge-witch's mule stood trembling as though it understood every word. Not just the rebels who had crept onto the field behind Cassian's army. The *soldiers*. Rank upon rank of them in crown grey, spears slack in their hands, helmets tipped back, faces streaming with rain and something else, and Pell could hear what they were hearing because the voice told him, generously, sharing itself like a child sharing sweets:

A spearman from the coast provinces was hearing his drowned father's voice saying *the nets are mended, son, you can rest now.* A woman with a crossbow was hearing her own name spoken the way her grandmother used to say it, before the crown took the grandmother's village for taxes. A boy no older than Pell himself was hearing nothing at all except the sudden, unbearable knowledge that he was loved by something that had never once asked him for obedience.

"They can hear it," Pell said aloud, and his voice came out in two languages at once, the valley tongue and the old tongue braided together like rope. "Everyone can hear it."

*Everyone who listens,* the voice agreed. *Which is everyone, eventually. Even the ones who cover their ears. Especially those.*

Down the slope, Regent Cassian sat his white horse with his face like carved stone, and Pell felt the voice notice him the way you notice a splinter — small, sharp, not worth much pain but impossible to ignore.

*He cannot hear me,* the voice said, and there was pity in it, deep as a well. *Three hundred years of bells, brother-child. Three hundred years of drowning me out. He rang the bell so long inside his own skull that he has gone deaf to everything else. Poor man. Poor frightened man in a big chair.*

"He tried to cage you," Pell said. Anger rose in him, hot and clean. "He tried to put you back in the crown. He tried to put *me* back in the crown."

*And I forgive him,* the voice said simply, *because fear is only love pointed the wrong way, and someday — not today, but someday — someone will say something to him in the old tongue and he will weep like a child and not know why.* The voice rippled, and Pell felt it turning its attention outward, outward, past the battlefield, past the hills, out along the roads and rivers into the world where it was already arriving — unevenly, imperfectly, like rain falling on one field and not the next. *Go to your mercenary now, little vessel. You are not my vessel anymore. You are my friend. And your friend is breathing very shallowly, and the woman with the iron smell is shouting your name.*

Pell turned. Through the rain he saw Odile kneeling over the white flowers where Ines had fallen, her hands glowing faintly green, her voice raw from screaming.

He ran.

* * *

**Odile**

She had buried children in the last war. She had helped bury a god. She had thought herself past the age when the world could surprise her, and then the sky above the battlefield had split open like a struck bell and every hair on her body had stood up and sung, and she had learned, at sixty-one years old, that she had been wrong about everything.

She knelt in the ruined flowers with Ines's head cradled against her knee and pressed her palms flat against his chest, and she poured everything she had left into him — the green thread-magic of hedgerows and healing, the small stubborn craft of women who keep goats and grudges in equal measure. His blood moved under her hands. She felt it. Too slow. Too old. The same wrongness she had found in the wound at the ford, the current running backward through a riverbed carved for something else.

But it was moving.

"Breathe," she ordered him, because ordering was what she knew how to do when terror took her tongue. "Breathe, you great silent ox, breathe or I will follow you wherever you've gone and drag you back by the ear—"

His eyes opened.

They were ordinary brown eyes, and they had never looked so beautiful to her. He blinked up at the rain, at her, at the grey sky, and his brow furrowed slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water trying to remember which way the shore lay.

"Odile." His voice was gravel and rust. "Did we—"

"We won," she said, and laughed, and the laugh came out half sob. "Or something did. I don't know what we won. I don't know what *won*, if truth be told. But the crown's broken and the god's loose and Cassian's sitting his horse down there looking like a man whose house has just grown legs and walked away, so yes. We won."

"Ines!" Pell arrived like a thrown stone, skidding through the mud, and flung himself down against Ines's side with such force that the mercenary grunted. The boy was soaked through, shaking, radiant. "You fell. You fell and the flowers closed and I thought—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again in the old tongue, then caught himself, then said it in the valley tongue, carefully, like setting down something fragile: "I thought you were gone."

"I was somewhere," Ines said. His hand came up — slowly, wincingly — and settled on the boy's wet hair. "There was a voice."

"There's a voice everywhere now," Odile said grimly, glancing down the slope. "Listen."

They listened.

The rain was easing, and beneath it, threading through it, there was a murmur. Not words, exactly. Not yet. More like the sound a crowd makes before it becomes a crowd — a thousand separate breaths finding a shared rhythm. It came from the soldiers on the ridge. From the rebels in the treeline. From Odile's own strays huddled behind the overturned wagon, and from the mule, and from the grass, and from somewhere far to the east where, she would later learn, a fishing village woke to find their well water glowing faintly gold, and far to the south where a bell-tower in a market town tolled once, unstruck, and then went quiet forever.

"It's in everyone," Pell whispered. "It's not choosing kings anymore. It's just... going home. To everybody."

Odile looked down at Ines — at the man the valley had caged without knowing it, the fragment, the brother-fragment, the foundling who had sworn silence to a god that had been unable to answer — and saw tears cutting clean lines through the mud on his face.

She pretended not to see. Some mercies were quieter than others.

"Up," she said briskly, hauling at his arm. "All of you. We are lying in a battlefield in the rain with a defeated army two hundred paces away, and I am too old to celebrate victories in the mud. Help me get him up. Tam! Tam, get over here and take his other side, and if you cry on him I'll box your ears."

* * *

**Cassian**

Regent Cassian Vael did not dismount.

This was not pride, whatever his captains later claimed. It was that he did not trust his legs. Somewhere in the last hour the ground had stopped being solid in any way that mattered, and the only thing holding him upright was the saddle and thirty years of practice at appearing unmoved while the world ended.

He had planned for everything. He had planned for the boy's death, for the boy's capture, for the mercenary's defiance, for the witch's treachery — oh, especially for the witch's treachery; he had read her file twice. He had planned for the god's voice bound into a new crown, tempered and channeled, flowing through proper channels to proper authorities, and history would have recorded him as the regent who ended the wildness of magic and gave the kingdom three more centuries of peace.

He had not planned for the voice to belong to *everyone*.

Because that was the flaw, wasn't it? That had always been the flaw, three hundred years ago and now. A god who spoke to everyone could not be governed. A god who spoke to everyone made every plowboy a prophet and every scullery maid a vessel, and crowns meant nothing to people who could hear eternity murmuring in their own hearts. His ancestors had understood this. The bells had been built for this. And he had sat in the capital his whole life beneath the constant tolling, safe in the great deaf silence his forefathers had purchased with fire, and he had believed — he had genuinely believed — that he was keeping the peace.

Now a spearman three ranks back was on his knees in the mud, weeping, saying *father? Father, is that you?* and Cassian could hear nothing. Nothing at all. Just rain, and the murmur passing over him like wind over stone, and the terrible ringing quiet where everyone else's miracle should be.

"My lord." Captain Derev reined in beside him, pale under her helm. "The men are... the men aren't answering the call. Half of them have grounded their spears. Ossler's company is singing, my lord. I don't — I don't know what they're singing. I don't recognize the language."

*The old tongue,* Cassian thought. *They're singing in the god's own tongue, and I am the only man on this field who cannot understand it.*

"The rebels?" he asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, in a distant, hollow way.

"Gone to ground, mostly. Their leader's standing with the witch's people around the fallen man. No one's fighting, my lord. No one's fighting *anyone*." She hesitated, and then, because she was Derev, and Derev had always been honest to the point of insubordination: "What do we do?"

Cassian looked down the slope at the small cluster of figures — the witch with her green-stained hands, the boy with his soaked hair, the mercenaries, the strays, the whole improbable family of them helping a half-dead man to his feet. The boy looked up, met his eyes across two hundred paces of rain-soaked grass, and did not look away. There was no hatred in the child's face. That was somehow worse than hatred. There was *pity*.

And beneath the pity, faint as a bell heard through a wall, Cassian almost — *almost* — heard something say his name. Not *Regent*. Not *my lord*. His name. The one his mother had used, before the capital, before the bells, before the silence.

The moment passed. The wind shifted. He heard nothing.

"Sound the withdrawal," he said. "Orderly columns. No one touches them. No one follows them." He gathered the reins, and his hands, he noticed distantly, were shaking. "We are going home."

"And then, my lord?"

Cassian looked north, toward the capital, toward the ten thousand bells that had suddenly become the loneliest sound in the world.

"And then," he said, "I think we are going to have to learn to listen."

* * *

**Pell**

They made camp that night in the lee of the broken monument, because none of them had the strength to walk further and because, as Odile put it, the dead of three centuries deserved better than to have their graveyard abandoned the moment the shouting stopped.

Pell sat between Ines and the fire. Ines was propped against a wagon wheel, bandaged, gray-faced, alive — *alive*, and every time Pell looked at him and confirmed it again, something in his chest unclenched another degree. Tam was asleep against Odile's shoulder. The mule dozed standing up. Down the slope, the crown soldiers' campfires burned in neat rows, and from them, faintly, came singing — that same strange song, passed camp to camp, nobody teaching it and everybody knowing it.

"You hear it too," Pell said quietly. "Don't you. The voice."

Ines was quiet for a long time. The firelight moved on his face.

"Not the way you do," he said at last. "It's not words for me. It's more like..." He searched. "Like standing in a doorway with the door open. Cold air on my face. Knowing what's outside without seeing it." He looked at his own hands, flexed them, as if checking they were still his. "I'm smaller than I was, Pell. Whatever I was — the fragment, the blood, all of it — I gave most of it to the breaking. What's left is just..." He shrugged, winced. "Me."

"That's not smaller," Pell said.

Ines looked at him.

"That's not smaller," Pell said again, with the fierce certainty of a nine-year-old who has carried a god and knows exactly how heavy gods are. "That's just *you*. Finally just you."

Something moved through Ines's face then that Pell had no word for in either language — grief and relief tangled so tightly together they had become indistinguishable, the way a knot becomes stronger the harder you pull either end. He reached out and put his hand on Pell's head, heavy and warm, and left it there.

Across the fire, Odile stirred. "Boy," she said. "Come here a moment. Tell me what it's saying now. It's been muttering at me since sundown and I'll not have a god gossiping about me behind my back."

Pell grinned and started to rise —

—and stopped.

Because the murmur had changed. All across the darkened battlefield, in the soldiers' camp and the rebels' treeline and the wind moving through the white flowers, the voice's endless soft dispersal had paused, gathered, and turned — all of it, every scattered grain of it, from every direction at once — toward the east.

Toward the road. Toward the capital. Toward the city of ten thousand bells, which even now, fifty miles away, had begun to ring — not backward this time, not in panic, but in a slow, deliberate, questioning peal, as if the towers themselves had lifted their heads.

*Oh,* said the voice, in every ear on the field at once, gentle and startled and very slightly afraid. *Oh, little ones. Someone is ringing for me. Someone in the city of bells has finally stopped covering their ears — and they are asking, in the old tongue, in perfect grammar, in a voice I have not heard in three hundred years—*

Pell translated, his own whisper shaking:

*"Who has freed my sister?"*

The fire cracked. Ines went very still. And far to the east, the bells rang on, patient as gravestones, waiting for an answer.
