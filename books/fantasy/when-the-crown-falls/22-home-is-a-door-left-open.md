## 22. Home Is a Door Left Open

The knock came at dusk, the way it always did.

Pell heard it through the floorboards, through the smell of bread and woodsmoke, through the low song of hammers that had been Ines's music all summer. Three knocks, uneven, the last one weaker than the first — the knock of someone who had walked a long way and was not sure they would be welcome.

He was eleven now, or nearly. He had stopped counting the seasons by fevers and started counting them by what Ines built: the fence in autumn, the roof beams in winter, this house in spring, rising board by board at the valley's edge where the boundary stones used to stand. The stones were gone. Ines and six farmers from Hollow's End had pulled them down in the first week of thaw, and Pell had watched from the porch as each one toppled, feeling something in his chest loosen that he hadn't known was tight. The ground where they'd stood was just ground now. Grass already grew over the deepest socket, soft and green, as if the earth had been waiting years for permission.

"Door's yours," Ines said, without looking up from the beam he was planing. Shavings curled at his feet like pale leaves. "You answer it better than I do."

That was true, though Ines would never say why. People who came to the door these days came speaking the old tongue half the time — words that rose out of them like water finding a crack — and when Pell answered in kind, their faces did something wonderful. They unclenched. They looked at him the way he imagined you'd look at a well in a dry country.

He wiped his hands on his trousers and opened the door.

A woman stood on the step, travel-stained to the knees, with a bundle wrapped against her chest and frost still melting in her hair even though it was nearly summer. Behind her the road ran east into the long gold light, empty except for crows walking the ruts like small important officials.

"I heard—" she began, in the trade tongue, then stopped, and her mouth shaped itself around older sounds. *Vessel-of-the-quiet-house. Door-that-answers.* Her eyes went wide, as if her own voice had surprised her. "I didn't know I knew those words until just now."

"Most people don't," Pell said, also in the old tongue, because it seemed kinder. "Come in. There's bread."

* * *

They ate at the long table Ines had built too big on purpose, though he'd never admitted it was on purpose. Odile sat at its head, ostensibly mending a shirt but actually supervising everyone, which was her way of resting. The woman with the bundle was called Seraf, and her bundle was a girl of about two who slept through supper and woke only when the fire popped, turning toward the sound with an expression of grave attention, as if listening for something underneath it.

"She does that," Seraf said, apologetic. "Since the winter. She hums in her sleep sometimes. Songs nobody taught her."

Odile's needle paused. "Let me see her hands."

Seraf hesitated — Pell saw the old fear move across her face, the fear every one of them carried off the road, the flinch of people who had learned that being noticed could be fatal — and then let Odile unfold the small fists. In each palm, faint as breath on glass, was a mark like a spiral. Not ink. Not birthmark. It moved, very slightly, if you watched long enough.

"Hm," said Odile, in the tone she used when she was frightened and refusing to be.

"The marks are spreading," Pell said quietly. "I've seen four children with them since midwinter."

"And adults?"

"Adults get the dreams instead." He glanced at Ines, who had stopped planing and was standing very still by the window, the way he did when the world said something he needed to think about alone. "Ines dreams too. He won't say what about."

"I dream about the valley before there was a valley," Ines said, to the window. "Before the stones. Before anyone decided silence was safer than speech." He picked his plane back up. "It isn't a prophecy. It's a memory. There's a difference, and I wish more people understood it."

But his hands, Pell noticed, were not quite steady.

* * *

Later, when Seraf and the child were asleep in the east room and Odile had gone out to check her wards — she still set wards, out of habit, though nothing hostile had come up the road in a year — Pell carried two cups of chicory out to the porch where Ines sat watching the dark come down over the Hollow.

The valley looked different without its border. That was the strange thing. For three hundred years the Hollow Valley had been a held breath, a place where magic pooled at the edges and couldn't cross in, and now the breath had gone out of it and the whole landscape seemed — larger. Looser. Fireflies drifted past the porch rail in patterns that were almost, but not quite, writing. Somewhere down the slope, the stream ran a little brighter than water should.

"Do you miss it?" Pell asked. "Being silent-bound. Having the oath."

Ines considered this the way he considered everything, thoroughly and at length, while the cup steamed under his chin.

"I miss knowing what I was," he said finally. "Even when what I was was wrong. There's a comfort in a cage if you've lived in it long enough. You know where the bars are." He turned the cup in his scarred hands. "Now the bars are gone and I keep reaching for them anyway. Waking up some mornings reaching."

"Do you reach for them because you want them back?"

"No." And this he said without hesitation, which was how Pell knew it was true. "I reach because my hands don't know what else to hold yet."

Pell nodded and drank his chicory, which was terrible, because Ines made it, because Ines had never once in two years accepted Odile's offer to make it instead, because some things a man insisted on doing badly himself.

Below them, in the dark, the old manse field shimmered faintly where the god's body-speech lay buried under turned earth and wildflowers. Pell could feel it the way you feel a sleeping animal in a room — a warmth, a slow breathing, a murmur at the edge of hearing that was not one voice anymore but thousands, dispersed, shared, belonging to no crown and no temple. Some nights it spoke to him in fragments. Some nights it only breathed. Tonight it murmured something that might have been *brother-fragment*, and might have been wind.

"Ines," Pell said. "The word Seraf used. *Door-that-answers.*"

"Mm."

"That's what the god calls this house. I heard it tonight, from the field. It doesn't call it yours or mine. It calls it the door that answers."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. A firefly wrote something brief and illegible against the porch post and drifted on.

"Well," he said at last, gruffly, in the voice he used when feeling had gotten ahead of language. "Then we'd best keep answering."

* * *

Summer deepened. The house filled and emptied and filled again, the way a harbor fills with boats.

They came singly and in twos and threes: a stonemason whose chisels began singing hymns in a dead language; twin brothers from the coast who woke one morning able to hear each other's thoughts and found it so unbearable they'd walked inland looking for someone who could teach them to close the door a little; an old soldier of Cassian's broken army who arrived weeping and asking forgiveness for things done at a ford, and whom Ines fed and forgave in the same motion, saying only, "You were carrying orders. Now carry something lighter."

Odile taught them all — the ones who stayed, and some who only passed through — how to listen without drowning, how to close the inner ear like a shutter, how to tell the god's murmur from your own wanting. She was harder on herself than any student. Pell once caught her practicing the listening alone at midnight, eyes shut, jaw tight, like a woman returning to a country that had exiled her.

"You could stop," he told her. "No one would blame you. After what you did—"

"After what I did," she said, opening her eyes, "I am the last person alive who knows exactly how the silencing was done. Which means I'm the only one who can teach a hundred scattered strangers how to survive having voices in their heads." She tapped his chest with one crooked finger. "Guilt is a poor reason to live, boy, but it's an honest reason to work. Don't take either away from me."

So she worked. And the house worked. And the road east wore itself smoother with feet.

* * *

On the evening of the autumn equinox — a year, almost to the day, since Pell had collapsed feverish on this same doorstep, speaking a language older than kingdoms — the knock came again.

Pell knew before he opened it. He had been feeling it all afternoon, a pressure in the air like weather coming, and the field below had been murmuring louder than usual, and the fireflies had given up pretending to be insects and were hanging in the yard in slow constellations, spelling and respelling a single word that Pell could not yet read.

On the step stood a girl of perhaps fourteen, alone, mud to her thighs, hair full of burrs. She was shaking. But she stood the way Pell remembered standing on that first night: rooted, terrified, and utterly unwilling to leave.

Behind her, far down the eastern road, torchlight flickered. Many torches. Organized. Moving at a walk, unhurried, the way soldiers move when they believe the night belongs to them.

"They told me in the city there was a door that answers," the girl said. Her old-tongue words came out rough, unpracticed, but true. "They said the war is starting again. They said the Regent's heir has been hearing the voice since spring, and the crown wants vessels again, and they're taking children." Her chin trembled and steadied. "I ran for nine days. Please. I don't have anywhere else."

Pell stepped back and opened the door wide.

"Ines!" he called into the warm lamplit house, and heard the planing stop. "Get Odile. And bring the map."

Because the god's murmur had finally resolved, all at once, into words he could read, and the word the fireflies spelled in the yard was not *welcome* and not *home*.

It was *soon*.
