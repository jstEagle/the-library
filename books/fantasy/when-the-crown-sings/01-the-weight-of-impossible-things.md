## 1. The Weight of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, my master always said, is that they weigh exactly as much as you believe they do. A bottled echo might tip the scales at nothing—or at a grief, depending on who was holding it.

I had been holding the echo for an hour, and it was starting to feel like a funeral.

"Entry four hundred eleven," I said aloud, because the Ledgerhouse liked to be spoken to, and because my own voice kept me from listening to the silence in the next room, where Master Aldery coughed the way old buildings settle—slowly, structurally, with an air of finality about it. "One echo, bottled, provenance: the widow Vess of Harrow Lane, wept into a jar by her daughter at the funeral of—" I checked the slip tied to the neck with red thread. "Of no one she could name. Note: the echo answers to humming."

The echo hummed, obligingly. It was a thin sound, silver-gray, like the memory of a memory. Somewhere inside the glass, a woman who no longer existed grieved for a man whose name had gone out of the world, and the jar held it all without complaint, which was more than I could say for myself.

I wrote the entry in the day-ledger with the good pen—the goose quill that wrote only truths—and then, because procedure is a kind of prayer, I copied it again in the iron-gall ink that made lies itch. The entry didn't itch. The widow's daughter had wept honestly, even if she couldn't remember what for.

That was the trade, you understand. That was the whole of it. The Ledgerkeepers did not buy gold or grain or secrets worth armies. We bought and sold *impossible* things—the last word of a dying language, the shadow a man cast before he was born, doors that opened onto hours instead of rooms—and our only currency was accuracy. An item poorly described lost its weight. An item truly described kept its shape against the dark, sometimes for centuries.

Which meant that on the last night of the world—which neither I nor anyone else yet suspected—I was doing inventory.

I want it noted that I did not do this out of obliviousness. I did it out of love. There is a difference, though I grant the difference is narrow and mostly visible from the inside.

* * *

The Ledgerhouse stood on Coffer Street like an argument everyone had stopped having. Three stories of black stone, windows leaded and dusty, gutters full of moss that grew, if you looked closely, in the shapes of words nobody used anymore. The city around us had grown tall and bright and impatient; it wanted banks now, and counting-houses with brass fittings and honest arithmetic. It had forgotten that arithmetic was only ever the shallow end of accounting.

Inside, the shelves went back further than the building did. This was the first thing they taught you, or tried to: the Ledgerhouse is bigger than its walls, and the walls are a courtesy. Aisle nine ran forty feet past where the exterior stone said the property line stood. Aisle twelve you did not walk alone, because items there remembered being handled and got lonely. And at the very back, behind a door of black oak banded in iron, sealed with wax that had been poured by seven dead masters, sat the final ledger.

Nobody spoke about the final ledger. That too was procedure.

I climbed the stairs to the sickroom at dusk with a tray: barley tea, bread gone stale in the honest way bread does when no one has lied about it, and the small blue bottle that dulled pain at the cost of dulling everything else, which Master Aldery refused on principle.

"You're cataloging," he said, before I'd cleared the door. His voice had gone thin as ledger-paper. "I can hear the pen through the floor. Four hundred eleven?"

"Four hundred thirteen now. The key came back."

"The key." He closed his eyes as if the key were a personal affront, which in a sense it was; he'd appraised it himself, forty years ago. "To nowhere."

"To nowhere," I agreed, setting down the tray. "It turned up in the pocket of a coat donated to the Foundling Home. The matron recognized the teeth."

"Keys don't come back, Dominic."

"No," I said. "They're returned. There's a difference, and the difference frightens me."

He laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and I did the thing I hated, which was to stand there counting the seconds between one breath and the next, measuring him the way I measured everything, because measurement was all the comfort I knew how to give.

When the coughing passed he caught my wrist. His hand had been a strong hand once—a hand that had signed off on wonders—and now it felt like paper that had been rained on.

"Sit," he said. "There's something I should have told you years ago, and I keep finding reasons to tell you tomorrow."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"That," he said, "is what worries me." He looked at me for a long moment with eyes that were still, underneath the illness, terribly precise. "You've never asked about the final ledger."

"It's sealed."

"Seals are for other people's curiosity. Yours you've folded up and put away like winter clothes, and I've watched you do it for fifteen years, and I've let you, because the alternative was worse." His grip tightened. "So hear me now, while I'm still the master and you're still the apprentice, whatever the registry says. You will not read it. Not when I die—not especially when I die, death makes people sentimental about unfinished business. Not when the guild sends its assessors, not when the crown comes asking, not if the sky itself opens up and asks politely. Do you hear me?"

"The sky asking politely," I said. "You've been drinking the tea before I brought it."

"Dominic."

And there it was—my name in his mouth with the weight of an appraisal behind it, the way he said it when he wanted a truth and not a joke. I had been taken in at six years old, a foundling with a head for lists and a habit of remembering things exactly, which is either a gift or a wound depending on the decade. He had taught me that every impossible thing wants two contradictory things: to be known, and to be free. And that the Ledgerkeeper's whole art was standing in the doorway between those wants, forever.

"I hear you," I said. "I won't read it."

"Not unless—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Whatever the exception was, he swallowed it, and I watched him swallow it, and I have thought about that swallow more times since than any other single motion in my life. "No. Not at all. Swear it properly."

I swore it properly. We keep oaths in the blood-ink, the kind that itches. Mine itched for a week.

He relaxed by degrees after that, as if a debt had been paid, or deferred. I helped him lie back. At the window, the first stars were coming out over the city—Vesper and the Sisters and slow-wandering Orrin, the ones the almanac promised, punctual as clerks. I remember thinking they looked slightly wrong that night, slightly *eager*, like entries written in advance of events. But I was tired, and grief makes patterns of everything, so I lit the lamp and told myself it was nothing.

* * *

Midnight in the Ledgerhouse has a texture. Most people think midnight is silence, but silence is just the absence of noise, and midnight was never absent anything—it hummed. Every ward-stone in the city, from the great foundation-stones under the Citadel to the chips of rune-quartz set above shop doors against fire and theft, answered the hour together. A chord, very deep, felt in the teeth rather than heard. The city's name being said to itself, quietly, once a night, the way you touch a sleeping child to confirm the breathing.

I was at the long table finishing the day's entries when it happened.

Four hundred fourteen was a jar of yesterday—one of the small ones, holding a Tuesday from sixty years ago, weather included. I had the pen lifted. The chord began.

And broke.

Not faded. Broke, mid-note, the way a string breaks—there was a wrongness in the air like the taste of a bitten coin. The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The bottled echo on the shelf beside me stopped humming, which echoes are not supposed to be able to do, and pressed itself against the glass toward the window like a child at a fence.

Then the stars went out.

Not covered—there were no clouds, I will swear to that on the blood-ink to my dying day. They went out the way lamps go out when someone stops paying for the oil: Vesper first, then the Sisters one after another, west to east, unhurried, *orderly*, like accounts being closed. Orrin lasted longest, hanging there stubborn and dim, and then it too was gone, and the sky over the city was black cloth, and the blackness behind it was not empty but *full*, the way a ledger page is full before anything is written on it.

I stood up. My chair fell backward. I barely heard it.

Below the window, the city lay in the ordinary dark of a city at night—and then the ward-stones began to die. I saw it happen up Coffer Street, door by door: the little blue-white glimmer above the chandler's, gone. Above the cobbler's, gone. The great pale glow that always rose from the Citadel foundations, steady as a heartbeat for three hundred years, flickered once, twice—

—and went out like a held breath finally released.

In the sudden total dark, the only light in the room was the day-ledger itself. The pages gave off a faint gray shine, the shine of vellum that knows something, and I looked down and saw that the ink of my last completed entry—four hundred thirteen, the key to nowhere—had changed. The words hadn't moved. The words were exactly as I'd written them. But beneath them, in the margin, in a hand that was mine and yet not mine, smaller, neater, patient:

*Returned to sender.*

From the sickroom upstairs there came a sound I will hear until I stop hearing anything at all: Master Aldery, who had not left his bed in a month, standing. Walking. Coming down the stairs with a speed that no dying man should own, saying my name over and over, and under his voice, faint and rising from everywhere and nowhere—from the empty shelves, from the sealed door at the back of the house, from the black sky itself—a sound like a choir tuning.

Like something beginning to sing.

Or beginning to count.
