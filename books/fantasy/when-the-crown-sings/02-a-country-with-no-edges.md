## 2. A Country With No Edges

The morning after the stars went out, I did what any sensible person does when the sky stops working: I made tea.

Not because the tea helped. Because the ritual helped. Boil the water, warm the pot, measure the leaves—three scoops, never four, Master Ellery had beaten that into me over twenty years of apprenticeship until it sat in my hands deeper than my own pulse. The world could end at its edges, but in the Ledgerhouse kitchen, three scoops meant three scoops, and for the length of one steeping, order still existed.

Then I carried the pot up to the counting room, where my master lay dying on a cot we'd dragged between the shelves, and where the fog had come in through windows that had been shut since before I was born.

"You're still cataloguing," he said. It wasn't a question. His voice had gone thin as vellum.

"Someone has to." I set the tea down and picked up my pen. "The bottled echo from shelf nine is fading. I can hear it trying to remember what it was an echo *of*. And the key to nowhere has started rattling."

"Keys rattle."

"This one rattles *toward* things now. It wants to go somewhere. That's new, and new is bad, and bad gets written down." I inked the entry in a hand steadier than I felt: *Item 4,112 — key to nowhere — exhibiting directional intent — recommend observation.* The pen scratched against the silence of a city without wards. Somewhere outside, a bell rang the hour, but the sound came wrong, flattened, like a coin rubbed smooth.

"Dominic." He'd used my name. He almost never used my name; I was "apprentice," or "girl," or, on his better days, "the last one worth the trouble." "Come here."

I went. His hand, when it found mine, weighed nothing at all, which frightened me more than the fog or the dark sky or the key with ambitions. Ledgerkeepers are not light people. We carry too much.

"The roads," he said. "Tell me about the roads."

I'd sent a boy out at first light—Tam, fourteen, quick, the only apprentice left after the others fled east two nights ago. East, which was funny, because as of this morning there may not have been an east. Tam had walked the Vellum Road toward the border town of Harrow's End, where the tollgate stood, where the ward-stone stood, where the whole stitched seam between our country and the next had held for three hundred years.

He'd come back at midmorning, gray-faced, and hadn't spoken since except to drink the soup I put in front of him.

"He says the road ends," I told Ellery. "Not blocked. Not washed out. *Ends.* You walk along the Vellum Road and the milestones count down—Harrow's End, four leagues, three, two—and then there's fog, and the fog isn't weather. Tam threw a stone into it. No sound. He waited an hour. The stone didn't come back and neither did the sound."

"And the other directions?"

"North road, same. South, same. West—" I stopped.

"West," he prompted.

"West is where the sea should be. There's no fog. There's just... more country. Hills that weren't there. A river running uphill. Tam said the hills looked *invented*." I laughed, and hated how it came out, high and cracked. "Which is absurd. Country doesn't get invented. Country is the thing everything else is measured against. That's the entire point of a ledger. You can falsify an entry, you can lose a page, but the—the substrate—"

"The substrate is exactly what's gone." Ellery closed his eyes. "A ledger needs something to be a ledger *about*, girl. Take away the thing itself, and all your careful entries become poetry."

I sat down hard on the stool beside his cot. Through the window, the fog stood at the end of the street like a patient customer, the kind who waits all day without browsing, and somehow that was worse than if it had been pressing at the glass.

"Refugees started arriving an hour ago," I said, because someone had to say it. "From the villages near the eastern marches. They say—" My throat tightened around it. "They say their villages don't remember them. Not the people. The *villages*. One woman swears she walked through her own front door and her house didn't know her. The door stuck the way doors stick for strangers. Her dog barked at her. Her mother set a place at table for a daughter who died young, and when the woman sat in the chair, the chair complained under weight it shouldn't have felt."

Ellery was quiet a long time. When he spoke again, each word cost him visibly, like coins counted out of a nearly empty purse.

"People forget. People have always forgotten. But places forgetting—that requires the place to have been *unmade* somewhere. Unmade properly. On paper." His eyes opened, and they were clear in a way they hadn't been in days, the terrible clarity of the dying getting their affairs in order. "The sealed ledger, Dominic. Bottom shelf. Iron clasp."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going. "You told me never—"

"I told you never while I lived. I am negotiating different terms now." A ghost of his old dryness. "Third shelf down. Bring it here, and bring the reading lamp. If I'm going to break thirty years of discipline, I'll do it with decent light."

The sealed ledger was heavier than any book had a right to be—not physically, though it was that too, but the way a debt is heavy, the way a secret sits on the chest at three in the morning. Its cover was black calfskin gone soft with age, unmarked by title or number, because a ledger that couldn't be found couldn't be read, and a ledger that couldn't be read couldn't be *used*. Every guild has rules. Ours had one rule above all others: *nothing enters the final ledger that we are not prepared to make true.*

I set it on the cot beside him. My hands were shaking. Three scoops, never four. Order exists. Order exists.

"Read the last entry," Ellery said. "Only the last. Promise me."

"I promise."

The iron clasp opened at his touch—or perhaps at mine; afterward I could never be sure whose intention had turned the key—and the pages fell open to the end as if the book had been waiting, as if it had been lying face-down on that shelf for years with one finger holding its place.

The entry was dated tomorrow.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the last thing I own. The date at the top of the page was tomorrow's date. Not yesterday's. Not some ancient record misfiled across centuries. Tomorrow. The ink was old. The ink was *dry*. The handwriting was in the old master-hand, the formal register we use for transactions of state, every letter perfect, every abbreviation correct—and it read:

*Sale of Name: one (1) true name, being the name of the realm entire, sold complete with borders, history, and claim upon memory. Buyer: unknown. Broker: R. Calloway. Consideration: rendered. Terms: called due at the extinguishing of the stars.*

Below it, in the space where the guild seal goes, was the impression of the crown-and-quill, pressed deep enough to emboss three pages beneath.

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change, which was somehow the cruelest part; words should change when you need them to, the way people do.

"Riven Calloway," I said aloud, because saying it might make it smaller. It didn't. The name filled the counting room like smoke fills a locked room—no escape, just slow occupation of every corner.

"You knew him," Ellery said. Not a question either. He'd always known everything; it was his most infuriating feature and, I suspected now, possibly his most relevant one.

"He was the royal cartographer." My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing very far away from me. "Before he was disgraced. Before—" Before the scandal I'd never gotten a straight account of, the fall from grace so total that even the court gossip had gone vague about it, details dissolving like sugar in rain. Before he taught me to draw coastlines in the dust of the Ledgerhouse floor and laughed at how seriously I took the tides. Before he kissed me under the eaves during the Flood Fair and then vanished from the city for six years, and I told myself I'd stopped waiting after the second year, and I lied.

"He brokered the sale of the country," I said. "Our country. Its *name*, Ellery. Everything. And the entry is dated *tomorrow*, which means it hasn't finished happening, which means—"

"Which means it can still be unwritten." He gripped my wrist with what strength remained to him. "Everything in a ledger can be unwritten. That is the one mercy and the one horror of our trade. Find Calloway. He sold the memory of the transaction as part of his payment—he'd have to have; consideration must be rendered in kind, and what does a man like that have that anyone would want? He has *certainty*. He has *knowing*. So they took his knowing, and he's been walking around for years with a hole in him shaped like treason, wondering why he can't sleep." Ellery coughed, wet and long. "Find him. Make him remember. What a man paid, a man can demand back."

"And if he won't? If he did it knowingly, if he *chose*—"

"Then you'll know that too." His eyes held mine. "That's the price of reading, girl. You knew that when you signed your indenture. The truth doesn't care whom it inconveniences. Least of all the people who love each other."

Outside, the fog at the end of the street had crept closer. Two houses' distance now. I could see it moving the way water moves—deliberately, without hurry, certain of the level it will find.

"Rest," I told him, and tucked the blanket up, and blew out the reading lamp, and stood in the dark of the counting room surrounded by forty centuries of impossible things, holding a book that had already recorded tomorrow.

I did not cry. Crying is for after, and whatever this was, it wasn't over yet. Tomorrow hadn't happened. The ink was dry, but the day wasn't.

In the morning—if the morning came, if mornings were still a thing our country was entitled to—I would go down into the map vaults under the old palace annex, where disgraced cartographers went to drink themselves into irrelevance among coastlines that no longer needed drawing. I would find Riven Calloway. I would put the open ledger in front of him and watch his face while he read his own signature.

And if the man I remembered—the man who'd laughed at my tides, who'd kissed me once and thoroughly and then broken my heart by leaving—was somewhere inside the traitor who'd sold us all, then maybe, just maybe, I could make him look at what he'd done.

The fog reached the doorstep sometime in the night. I heard it arrive the way you hear snow: by the absence of everything else.

It stopped at the threshold. It waited, patient as a customer, for the Ledgerhouse to decide what it was willing to sell.
