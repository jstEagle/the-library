## 3. The Cartographer's Debt

The map vaults beneath the old Survey Office smell of cedar oil and rot, and I have come to think of the rot as mine.

I know how that sounds. A cartographer who has lost his country ought to at least keep his dignity, but dignity requires edges, and edges are what I've been losing. First the coastlines. Then the rivers. Now, some mornings, I wake on the vault floor with charcoal ground into my knuckles and no memory of drawing, and the maps around me are covered in coastlines I don't recognize—except I do recognize them, that's the horror of it. They're *right*. They're the true shape of a shore that stopped existing three weeks ago, and my hands remember it even though the world doesn't.

My hands are more loyal than I am. That's the only explanation left.

Tonight I'm working by candlelight on the western fens, because the fens went first and someone ought to mourn them properly. You can't chart a place that isn't there, but you can chart where it *was*, the way you can trace a scar and guess at the wound. My pen moves and the ink takes and for one moment the fen exists again on paper—reeds, eel-water, the bell of a drowned church ringing under the mud—and then it doesn't. The ink fades to gray. The gray fades to nothing. The paper stays blank, politely, the way paper does when it's embarrassed for you.

"Third time tonight," I tell the blank page. "You could at least have the decency to stay wrong."

The page says nothing. Pages are the last honest things in this kingdom.

* * *

I should explain about the disgrace, since everyone in the capital will tell you their version and all of them will be wrong.

Two years ago I was Royal Cartographer. I had a tower room, a stipend, and a commission from the old king himself: a full survey of the border wards, every stone and sigil, so the Crown could prove to the empire that our edges were sound. It was the finest work of my life. I measured the wards down to the width of a hair and found them perfect, and I said so in my report, and the king believed me, and then the stars started going out anyway.

So either I was wrong, or I was lied to, or something happened to the wards after I measured them. The court preferred the first option. Disgrace is cheaper than investigation. They took my seal, my title, and my access to anything important, which is why I now live in a basement full of obsolete maps, drawing ghosts.

What the court never asked—the question nobody thought to ask, because courts don't—is why I didn't argue harder. Why I signed the confession they put in front of me without reading past the first line. Why, when they stripped my office, part of me felt not ruined but *relieved*, like a man taking off a coat that had gotten too heavy.

I've thought about that relief for two years. I've turned it over the way you turn a stone looking for what's underneath.

Last week I found out what's underneath. There's a hole in me. Not grief—grief has a shape, and this is the absence of one. There's a stretch of my past, roughly four years back, maybe five, that I cannot see. I know I lived it. I have receipts. I have a scar on my left forearm I don't remember earning. But if I try to look directly at those years, my mind slides off, the way a pen slides off wet vellum, and comes back with nothing.

A cartographer's whole trade is knowing where things are. Imagine discovering a blank space in your own interior and being unable to say whether it's ocean or land or simply *unsurveyed*.

That's the madness they talk about, I suppose. Half-mad Riven Calloway, muttering to his maps. Let them talk. At least my maps and I agree on one thing: something was taken from both of us, and neither of us remembers the thief's face.

* * *

She comes down the vault stairs on the night the last ward-stone dies—I'll learn that later; at the time I only know the candles guttered all at once, as if the dark had inhaled.

I hear her before I see her. Ledgerkeepers walk a particular way, quick and precise, like people who are always slightly late for an appointment with the truth. And there is exactly one person whose footsteps I would know anywhere, because for three years I mapped her routes across the city the way other men write poetry: Dominic Vane, coming down my stairs with a lantern held high and her jaw set in the way it sets when she's frightened and refusing to admit it.

"Dominic." Her name comes out of me before I can decide whether to say it. Two years since the disgrace, longer since she stopped speaking to me, and her name still fits my mouth like a key I never surrendered.

The lantern swings toward me. She looks older. Everyone looks older lately; the country is aging us like wine left open in a storm. But her eyes are the same—dark, exacting, the eyes of a woman who reads everything twice—and right now they're doing something worse than glaring at me.

They're *reading* me.

"You're alive," she says.

"Barely. It's a popular condition."

"The whole city thinks you're dead."

"The whole city," I say, "has recently demonstrated a shaky grasp on what exists. I wouldn't take its word on anything."

That gets half a breath of a laugh out of her, quickly strangled. She comes closer, and the lantern light finds the maps spread around me, the faded ink, the blank pages, and I watch her understand them in real time—watch the Ledgerkeeper in her do the arithmetic I've been avoiding for weeks.

"You're drawing places that don't exist anymore," she says.

"I'm drawing places that won't *stay* drawn. There's a difference. Everything here exists for about four seconds and then the world repossesses it." I hold up the fen chart. "Watch. This is the Sedgefen, west of Harrowmere. Bell under the church. Eels in the—" and even as I speak, the lines go gray, then gone. "There. You saw it?"

"I saw it." Her voice has gone very quiet. "Riven. How long have your hands remembered things the rest of the world forgot?"

And there it is. The question underneath the question. Because she hasn't come about the fens, and she hasn't come about the disgrace, and she is standing in my basement at midnight holding a lantern like a woman carrying evidence into court.

"Why are you here, Dominic?"

She hesitates. In all the years I've known her, she has never once hesitated over information. Information is her native tongue. So when she hesitates, I feel it in my chest like a dropped anchor.

Then she opens the satchel at her hip and takes out a ledger. Black binding, iron clasps, sealed with wax stamped by the Guild's master sigil—the kind of seal that means *do not read*, which in the Ledgerhouse has always meant *read carefully*.

"I need you to look at an entry," she says.

"If it's about my debts, I'm aware—"

"It's dated tomorrow."

* * *

Here is what I learned in the next hour, sitting across from her at my worktable while the candles burned like they were being paid by the hour:

Someone sold the country's name. Not the land—the *name*, the true one, the one the wards are woven from. The transaction is recorded in the Ledgerhouse's final book, entered in advance, because impossible things keep their own schedule. Buyer unknown. Broker identified by initials only.

*R.C.*

She didn't say it aloud. She turned the ledger toward me and let me read it myself, which was crueler and kinder than saying it, and I sat there looking at my own initials in a dead language of debits and felt the hole in my memory ache like a tooth.

"That's not possible," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It's also true. We specialize in that at the Ledgerhouse."

"I would remember brokering the sale of my own country."

"Would you?" She said it gently, which made it worse. "Riven. What do you remember of the year you turned twenty-six?"

And I reached for it, the way I'd reached a hundred times, and my mind slid off the wet vellum of it, and came back empty, and this time I watched her watch it happen. Watched her see the blankness land on my face.

"There's nothing," I said. "There's a gap. Four years back, give or take. I told myself it was drink, or grief, or just a bad stretch poorly documented. Cartographers are supposed to notice gaps. I drew maps of coastlines I couldn't account for and called it professional curiosity." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You want to hear the joke? I surveyed the border wards for the king. I stood inside the strongest magic in the kingdom and certified it sound. And apparently somewhere in the years I can't see, I helped unmake all of it."

"You don't know that."

"My initials are in the ledger, Dominic."

"Initials aren't proof. Half the guild shares initials. R.C., R.C.—there's a Rosamund Cade who appraises bottled echoes, for pity's sake." But her voice shook on the last words, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't defending me. She was defending herself. She wanted it not to be me so badly that she'd brought the evidence to the one man with a motive to disprove it.

Which meant she'd already checked everywhere else.

"The memory was part of the payment," she said, when neither of us spoke for a while. "That's how the high trades work. You don't sell a thing that big and get to walk around remembering it. The broker's fee—the broker's *price*—is the memory of the brokerage. Clean hands, clean mind. Whoever did this paid themselves in forgetting."

"So whoever brokered it chose to be hollowed out rather than live with it."

"Yes."

"And I'm walking around with a hole in me shaped exactly like treason."

She reached across the table then—Dominic Vane, who touches people the way ledgers do, rarely and precisely—and put her hand over mine, charcoal-stained and shaking.

"Shaped like *something*," she said. "We don't know what yet. That's why I'm here and not the queen's guard. If it was you, I need to know why. And if it wasn't you—" her grip tightened "—then someone used your name, and I need to know that too, because I am not letting this guild's books close on a lie."

It was the closest thing to absolution anyone had offered me in two years, and it came wrapped in an accusation, and I loved her for both halves equally. That's the trouble with loving a Ledgerkeeper. Even her mercy is itemized.

"There's one way to find out," I said. "The debt itself. Whatever was traded, whatever was paid—it'll be structured like any impossible transaction. Collateral, terms, conditions of default. If I brokered it, the structure will be mine. I sign my work. Always have. Ask any examiner."

"You want to audit your own erased crime."

"I want to *chart* it," I said. "It's the only skill I have left. Something took a coastline out of my life, Dominic. I'd like to know where it went."

She looked at me for a long moment, lantern light moving on her face, and I saw her decide—not to trust me, exactly. Trust is a balance carried forward, and ours has too many outstanding entries. She decided to *proceed*, which in her language is worth more.

"The trail starts with the debt," she said. "The old king borrowed against something he had no right to borrow against. The note's been called due. Every day more people forget, and the forgotten ones—" she stopped, and her composure cracked just enough to show the fear underneath, the girl I'd known before the guild polished it into poise. "Riven, I found my mother's memories in a ledger once. Perfectly catalogued. Like entries. Like someone *wrote* her."

"Then we start with the king's creditors," I said, already pulling charts, because work is the only prayer I trust. "And Dominic—if we follow this trail and it ends at my own hand, I need you to promise me something."

"What?"

"Promise you'll read the entry all the way through before you decide what I am. All of it. Terms, conditions, footnotes. Don't sentence me off the summary."

"I never sentence anyone off the summary," she said. "That's the guild's sin, not mine."

She was wrong about that, as it happens. We were both wrong about nearly everything. But that night, in a basement full of fading maps, with the stars going out overhead and my own initials waiting in a black book like a hook set in water, it was enough to begin.

Because here is what neither of us knew yet, what the ledgers knew and kept folded away like a letter marked *do not open until the world ends*:

The debt had a purpose attached. Debts that size always do. And when I finally traced it—through the king's creditors, through the guild's sealed annexes, through the trade-routes of memory themselves—I would find the price written out in full, in my own handwriting, in a year I cannot remember:

*One life, saved entire. Payment deferred.*

And I would finally understand what I had bought with my country, and why, given the choice again, in the dark, with the same two names in front of me—

I would pay it all over again.
