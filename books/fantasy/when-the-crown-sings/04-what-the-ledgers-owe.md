## 4. What the Ledgers Owe

*Dominic*

There is a rule in the Ledgerhouse older than the building, older than the guild, possibly older than the concept of rules: *a debt does not vanish because the debtor dies.* It waits. It accrues interest in whatever currency it was denominated in—coin, favor, blood, name—and one morning it knocks on your door wearing its reading glasses.

I had always found this rule comforting. Debts were honest things. They told you exactly what you owed and to whom.

I was beginning to revise that opinion.

Riven and I sat in the reading room of the ruined Ledgerhouse with the sealed ledger open between us, because apparently I was the kind of apprentice who read the ledger her dying master explicitly forbade her to read. In my defense, the stars had gone out. Desperate times.

"Read it again," Riven said. He had his elbows on the table and his fingers pressed to his temples like a man trying to hold his skull together by will alone. "Slowly."

"'Entry four thousand one hundred twelve,'" I recited. "'Collateral pledged: the True Name of the Realm of Vessany, held whole. Debt incurred: Crown of Vessany, reign of Aldous the Third. Terms: callable at any time, without notice, without appeal. Broker of record: R. Calloway.'" I looked up. "That's the third time. It doesn't change."

"It might," he said. "Things keep not existing. I thought we'd established that."

The entry was written in the old formal hand, the kind they taught first-years until their wrists screamed. Every clause precise. Every term airtight. Whoever had drafted it had been very good at their work, which meant either a senior Ledgerkeeper or someone who'd studied us closely enough to counterfeit the style.

And dated—the part that still made my stomach drop through the floor—tomorrow.

"Ledger-logic," I said, mostly to myself, and pulled a fresh page toward me. When you can't fight an entry, you audit it. "A debt has three parts. The principal, the collateral, and the terms." I wrote them down. "Principal: unknown amount, owed by King Aldous the Third. Collateral: our true name. The actual name. Not 'Vessany'—that's just what we call ourselves, like a pet name. The real one, the one that holds the borders up."

"The one the wards are keyed to," Riven said quietly.

"The one the wards are keyed to. And the terms say callable without notice. So somebody called it. The buyer—" I tapped the phrase *buyer unknown* — "collected. The name left the country. And everything the name was holding up started to come down from the edges inward, because a name is a border, if you think about it. It's the line between what is called and what isn't."

Riven stared at me. "You're enjoying this."

"I am *coping*," I said, "through structure. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm also terrified."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible, crooked thing, but it was there. Then it went away, and he said, "Dominic. If the name was collateral, then the king borrowed against it. What could a king need so badly he'd stake the existence of his own country?"

I didn't answer, because I'd already turned the page, and the next entry answered for me.

*Entry four thousand one hundred thirteen. Disbursement: one life, restored whole. Recipient: D. Thackeray, age six. Source of funds: [REDACTED]. Note: broker's fee waived.*

The room got very quiet. Somewhere above us, the roof settled with a groan, shedding another shingle into the dark.

"That's me," I said. My voice sounded far away. "That's—I died when I was six. Fever. My mother carried me to a Ledgerhouse apothecary in Marrowfield, and I woke up, and everyone said it was a miracle, and my mother never talked about it again." I looked at the entry. At *source of funds: redacted.* At *broker's fee waived.* "Someone paid for my life. And the currency on the other side of the transaction was—"

"Don't," Riven said.

"—our name. Our country's name. My life was bought with the kingdom." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "All those years I catalogued impossible things. Bottled echoes. Keys to nowhere. And the most impossible thing in the whole guild was sitting in a ledger under my own initials."

"Dominic—"

"How old is the entry, Riven?" I did the arithmetic before he could stop me. "Twenty years. You were a junior broker twenty years ago. You would have been—" I stopped. Looked at him. Watched him look at nothing. "You don't remember. Of course you don't remember. The memory was part of the payment. That's how brokers settle accounts they can't bear to carry."

"I told you," he said, hoarse. "I brokered something. I know I did. I've spent twenty years drawing maps of places that used to be real, trying to find the hole where it should be, and I never knew why." His hands were flat on the table now, trembling slightly. "If I did this—if I traded a country for a child's fever—"

"You didn't trade it for a child's fever," I said. "You traded it for *me*. Someone asked a price you couldn't pay, and then someone offered you a price you couldn't refuse, and the thing you loved more than your country was standing in a bed dying of scarlet fever." I closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "I'm not angry at you. I'm trying to be. It would be simpler. But all I can feel is that a stranger twenty years ago decided I was worth more than the stars, and then forgot he'd decided it, and I've been walking around ever since on credit I never knew I was spending."

* * *

*Riven*

She fell asleep at the table around the fourth hour, cheek on the closed ledger, and I sat across from her doing the thing I do best, which is looking at the shape of a country by looking at its absence.

Here is what a cartographer knows that other people don't: every map is a confession. You draw what you believe is there, and the gaps tell the truth. Vessany's maps had gaps now. Not blank spaces—worse. Spaces that insisted they were full. Roads that continued confidently into fog. Towns marked in my own hand that, when I tried to recall drawing them, gave me nothing. The ink remembered. I didn't.

So I did what I always did. I took out my instruments and I audited.

The debt had a shape. Debts always do. Principal: unknown. Collateral: the Name. Terms: callable. But there was a fourth element nobody talks about, the one that keeps the whole rotten system upright: *the counterparty.* Somebody held the paper. Somebody had walked into a Ledgerhouse—or sent someone—and signed.

And here was the thought that kept me company while Dominic slept, the thought I would have given anything not to have: if the buyer had simply wanted the Name as a weapon, the country would be gone already. Snuffed. Instead it was unraveling slowly, edge inward, over weeks. Stars going out one constellation at a time. Villages forgetting themselves one family at a time.

That wasn't collection. That was *consumption.* Someone was eating us at the pace they could digest.

I'd seen it once before, in the empire's trade ledgers, back when I still had clearance and a title. A province across the sea that had been annexed not by armies but by accounting. Its people hadn't been conquered. They'd been *amortized.*

Every person who forgets, I realized, watching a lamplighter outside walk past the window and turn back with a face like an unscribed page—that's not a symptom. That's a payment schedule. Each mind that loses the country is a little more of the Name transferred. We weren't dying. We were being *paid out*, installment by installment, into someone else's vault.

I put my head in my hands. Twenty years ago I had set that machine turning. I couldn't remember doing it, which somehow made it worse—it meant the worst thing I'd ever done wasn't even mine to grieve properly. It belonged to whoever I'd been before the fee was collected.

Dominic stirred. "You're muttering," she said without opening her eyes.

"I'm auditing."

"At this hour?"

"The debt doesn't keep hours." I slid my notes across the table. She sat up, hair flattened on one side, eyes going sharp the moment they touched the page. That was the thing about her—even exhausted, even gutted, she read like a person defending a city.

"Payment schedule," she murmured. "Installments. So the Unwritten aren't casualties. They're *receipts*." Her jaw tightened. "Then every person who forgets is proof the contract is being honored. Which means the contract is still active. Which means—"

"Which means it can be renegotiated," I said. "Contracts live until they're discharged. Nothing's final until both parties agree it is."

"And the counterparty is 'unknown.'" She tapped the phrase twice, thinking. Then she went still in a way I recognized from the Ledgerhouse floor, the way she went still when an item in the catalogue lied to her. "Riven. My mother's lullaby."

"What?"

"When I was small, my mother sang to me. Every night, same song. And I've been remembering it since the fog came, and I—" She stopped. Started again, slower, the way you approach a wound. "It's perfect. The memory. Every note, every word, the exact scratch in her voice on the high line. Twenty years old and it hasn't faded *at all*. Memories fade, Riven. Mine don't. None of my childhood does. It's all—" she searched for the word and hated it when she found it— "*neat*. Like entries. Like someone wrote it down carefully so it would keep."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

"If your memories are entries," I said carefully, "then someone entered them. And if someone entered them—"

"Then some of what I remember may not have happened." She said it steadily, but her hands had closed around the edge of the table hard enough to whiten. "Or happened differently. Or—" She broke off, because the next sentence was the one neither of us wanted: *or I'm not entirely real myself, a girl assembled from well-kept records.*

But she didn't go there. I watched her decide not to go there. I watched grief come up through her like floodwater and watched her do the thing she does, the thing I was starting to suspect she'd always done: she built it into a wall and stood on top of it.

"Marlowfield," she said. "My mother sang in Marlowfield. If the Unwritten are being paid out from the edges inward, and the fog reached the eastern provinces first—" She was already standing, already reaching for her coat. "The payment schedule runs east to west. My mother is either forgotten or she isn't, and either way, I want to see the ledger line where it happened."

"Dominic." I stood too. "The capital's east."

"I know." She pulled the door open onto a street lit by no stars at all, fog pressing at the corners of the world like tide against glass. Far off, faint as a thread, something was singing—the crown, they said, each dawn, holding the last of reality together by a note. "And if the queen's court is still standing, then so is the original copy of the contract. Debts have three parts, Riven. I intend to meet the counterparty."

She stepped into the dark. After a moment, I followed, because I had twenty years ago, too, and following her was apparently the one debt I'd never managed to discharge.
