## 5. The Court of Ash

The capital announced itself the way a dying man announces himself: by smell, then by sound, then by the terrible effort of appearing alive.

We smelled it first—woodsmoke and cold stone and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like fruit going over. Then we heard it. A thread of sound rising from the palace hill at dawn, thin as wire, wavering as if someone were singing through a wall of water.

Riven stopped on the road and put his hand to his chest like a man hearing his own heartbeat from outside.

"That's the crown," he said. "It sings at dawn. It always sang at dawn. I used to sleep through it." He said this with the particular shame of a man who has learned, too late, that he slept through miracles.

"Can you hear what it's singing?"

"No." He frowned. "Neither can you."

He was right, and that was the horror of it. The song was audible and meaningless at once—a melody without words, or rather, a melody whose words had been *sold*, and our ears, being honest instruments, refused to pretend otherwise. We could hear that there was language in it. We simply could not hold any of it. It slid off the mind like rain off oiled leather.

"It's holding the city together," I said. "Badly."

The capital was holding itself together badly too. Streets ran to their ends and then hesitated, cobbles giving way to a gray smoothness that wasn't fog exactly but wasn't stone either—the same unfinished quality I'd seen at the borders, reality waiting for instructions that weren't coming. Houses stood in rows where houses had always stood, but some of them had no doors anymore, just walls with the memory of doors painted on them. In one window, a candle burned behind glass that showed nothing on the other side. Not darkness. Nothing. My eyes kept trying to look at it and kept arriving nowhere, like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

The people were worse. Or rather, the people were better than the buildings and that was somehow worse still. They moved through the streets doing ordinary things—carrying bread, arguing about cart-wheels, sweeping steps that led to nothing—and every third face had gone smooth. Not featureless. Smooth the way an egg is smooth: complete, finished, with nothing left to say. The Unwritten walked among the Written, and nobody screamed, which told me how long this had been happening. You can get used to anything if it arrives slowly enough. That may be the most damning sentence ever written about my country.

A baker's boy handed us rolls at the city gate and took Riven's coin without counting it. His face was whole; his hands were not quite his own—they moved with a rehearsed quality, gestures remembered rather than felt. When I thanked him, he smiled and said, "Welcome to Vessany," and the name of my country came out of his mouth sounding like a word in a foreign tongue he'd memorized for trade.

*Vessany.* Even I flinched at it now. A placeholder. A label on a jar whose contents had been poured out.

"We should go up," Riven said quietly. "Before the song stops."

"The song stops?"

"Everything stops eventually." He looked at the palace hill, where the spires caught the early light and threw it back wrong, slightly delayed, like light remembering its errand late. "I drew this city forty times, Dominic. Every tower in the right place. Now when I try to draw it, the ink runs uphill."

---

The palace guards let us through without challenge, which frightened me more than a fight would have. One of them had a whole face; one did not; neither asked our business. They opened gates that groaned on hinges that hadn't been oiled since before the fall, and the sound they made was so ordinary I nearly wept. Ordinary things were becoming rare enough to be holy.

The throne room smelled of ash that had never been fire. Cold braziers. Tapestries on the walls depicting battles I knew from schoolbooks—except the banners in the woven scenes were blank, the colors unraveling out of them thread by thread, pooling on the floor beneath in little drifts of dyed wool. A chamberlain with half a face bowed to us with perfect courtesy and announced us to no one, because he could not remember titles, only the shape of announcing.

And on the throne sat a girl who could not have been more than nineteen, wearing a crown too heavy for her, her left hand resting on the armrest and her right hand—

Her right hand was not there. Not wounded, not bandaged. *Unwritten*. The sleeve hung empty to a cuff that ended in smooth skin, as though she'd been born that way, as though the world had agreed on that story and committed to it.

She watched us come down the long floor with eyes that were the sharpest things in the room.

"You're the Ledgerkeeper," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the last one," I said. "Dominic Vane."

"I know." She rose, and the court around her—maybe thirty nobles, maybe twenty, the number wouldn't settle—rose with her, a rustling of silk and confusion, several of them bowing toward walls instead of thrones. "I've been reading your guild's records. What's left of them. Half the pages are blank now. Did you know that? The ledgers are forgetting along with everything else."

"I know," I said. "Paper remembers only as long as someone does."

"Then you understand my situation better than anyone alive." She came down the steps herself, waving off the chamberlain, and stopped three feet from me. Up close I could see the Unwritten had taken more than her hand. There was a softness at her temple, a patch where hair grew in a color that couldn't decide what it was. And when she blinked, sometimes the blink lasted a moment too long, like a lamp guttering.

"Your Majesty," Riven began, and dropped to one knee, and she looked at him with an expression I couldn't read until I realized it was recognition without memory—the face you make at a stranger who feels like a room you used to live in.

"Cartographer Calloway," she said. "You drew my father's kingdom. He had your maps framed in his study. He said you were the only man in Vessany who loved the country honestly—everyone else loved what they wanted from it." Her voice cracked on the past tense. "Get up. Kneeling is for courts that are sure they exist."

He got up. She turned back to me.

"Come. Walk with me. I can't think in that room. Everyone in it keeps looking at me to be real, and I'm the least real person here."

---

She led us through corridors where the windows showed a garden that was no longer entirely there—flowerbeds fading into gray at their far edges, a fountain that produced water with no sound. Servants passed us carrying trays of food that steamed convincingly. I didn't ask where it came from. Some questions are mercy to leave unasked.

"My name is Isolde," she said abruptly. "Isolde the Third. Except—" she held up her remaining hand and flexed the fingers, "—I'm not sure that's true anymore. I found my own coronation portrait last week. The plaque says *Isolde II*. And the woman in the painting has both hands." She laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "So either the plaque is wrong, or the painter was wrong, or I am wrong, and I have no way left to check, because the archives are blanking and my Lord Recorder hanged himself in the spring and everyone thought it was grief."

"Wasn't it?"

"He left a note." She recited it flatly, the way you recite something you've read too many times. "*The accounts do not balance. Forgive me.*" She looked at me. "That's ledger-language, isn't it? That's how your people talk."

My mouth had gone dry. "Yes," I said. "That's how we talk."

They walked us into a small morning room with a map table at its center—Riven made a sound low in his throat when he saw it, one of his own charts pinned under glass, coastlines he'd drawn in another life—and the queen shut the door on her court and stood with her back against it, and for a moment she was just a tired girl in a room with two strangers.

"Fix it," she said.

"Your Majesty—"

"No. Listen to me first, because I've rehearsed this and if you interrupt I'll lose my place." She held up her four fingers. "I am the Queen of Vessany. I say that every morning when the crown sings, out loud, to remind myself. Some mornings I believe it. Some mornings I watch my own hand fade while I'm saying it and I believe nothing at all. My court is evaporating politely. My people at the gates bring me petitions from villages that no longer remember themselves, asking me to help them find homes that never existed. And every dawn that song gets thinner, and I know—I *know*, Ledgerkeeper—that when it finally fails, whatever is holding the middle of the country together goes with it, and everything between the fog-lines becomes edge." She lowered her hand. "So I am begging you. Fix the ledgers. Restore the name. Do whatever your guild does. Save us."

And there it was. The thing I had carried out of the Ledgerhouse like a stone in my chest, the thing I hadn't said aloud even to Riven, standing in a dying girl's morning room wearing a crown.

Because I had read the sealed ledger. I knew what restoration cost. To return the name—to make the transaction void, the debt repaid in full—every mind that held the old world would have to be wiped clean to make room for the new one. Every person who remembered Vessany-as-it-had-been would wake as a stranger in a mended country. The queen's own subjects. The Unwritten might knit back into flesh, but the Written would pay the toll. Millions of memories, burned as fuel, so that the country could stand again on foundations no one would recall laying.

"You're quiet," the queen said. "That's a ledger-quiet. I've read enough of your guild's margins to know that quiet."

"Restoring the name isn't mending a fence," I said carefully. "It's—there are terms. Costs. Costs that fall on—"

"On whom?" She stepped closer. Her eyes were wet and furious and young. "On whom, Dominic Vane? Because I'll tell you what falls on *me* if you do nothing. On my hand, which I can feel fading the way you feel a foot fall asleep. On my people, humming songs they don't know at a border that doesn't exist. I have watched a chamberlain forget his own daughter's name and keep serving tea with perfect manners. So whatever your cost is, weigh it honestly, and then tell me whether it weighs more than *this*."

The truth sat in my throat like a swallowed key.

Tell her. Tell her that saving the many means unmaking the remembering few. Tell her that her own salvation is priced in everyone else's past. Tell her the honest entry, the one my master died trying to keep sealed:

*Sale of Name. Buyer unknown. Broker: R. Calloway.*

But I looked at her empty sleeve, and at Riven beside me with his ruined maps and his hollowed-out guilt, and I heard myself say:

"There may be a third term. Something the contract allows that isn't in the standard clauses. I need time. I need the original instrument of sale. And I need to know who bought us."

Something moved behind the queen's eyes—not hope, hope is too clean a word. More like a person underwater deciding to kick once more.

"The buyer," she repeated. "Envoy Carrek arrived a month ago. From the Aurelian Empire. He sits in my east wing, eats my food, compliments my wine, and waits." Her voice dropped. "He told me, very courteously, that when the song fails, his emperor will extend his protection to what remains. He called it *annexation of the inevitable*. He smiled when he said it. He has all his memories, Ledgerkeeper. Did you notice? In a country full of blanks, the one man who remembers everything perfectly is the man waiting to inherit."

The morning light through the windows flickered—just once, just slightly, the sun stuttering like a candle in a draft—and somewhere above us, faint and fraying, the crown sang on.

The queen straightened. Put her shoulders back under the weight of a country. Became, visibly, a monarch again, the way an actor becomes a king when the audience enters.

"Go to him," she said. "Ask your questions. But Dominic Vane—come back and tell me the truth. All of it. Even if the truth is that I'm already sold." She held out her remaining hand, and I took it, and her grip was fierce and cold. "I would rather die knowing the price than live as a line item."

Outside the morning room, Riven touched my elbow. His face had gone the gray of unfinished streets.

"Dominic," he said. "Carrek. The envoy. I don't remember him—but my hands do. When she said his name, my fingers curled. Like they used to curl around a pen when I was signing something."

He stared down at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger who had done something unforgivable.

"I brokered the deal," he whispered. "Didn't I. Whatever it was. My hands already know him."

Above us, the crown sang its wordless song, and the sun steadied, and somewhere in the east wing of a palace that was forgetting itself, a man with a perfect memory waited to collect.
