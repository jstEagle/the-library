## 7. The Unwritten Choir

They came out of the fog the way notes come out of a held breath.

Not walking so much as arriving, one after another, as if the fog had been holding them and had finally decided to let go. Dozens of them. Then more than dozens. They moved without hurry, without direction, drifting down the old border road toward the stones of Vessia like leaves deciding to be water.

And they were humming.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the first thing the Ledgerkeepers ever taught me: *name a thing correctly and it cannot lie to you.* The Unwritten were not singing. Singing requires a singer—someone who chooses, who shapes, who means. This was older than meaning. It rose out of them the way heat rises off stone at dusk, involuntary, patient, the last warmth a body has to give.

The tune snagged in me like a fishhook behind the ribs.

I knew it. Of course I knew it. I had known it before I knew my own name, which is the order in which one knows lullabies. My mother used to hum it while she worked the ledger-copies by candlelight, her pen scratching along underneath the melody like a second voice. She said it had no words anymore—that the words had worn away generations ago, the way stair-steps wear into curves—and that this was proper, because a song with no words can't be wrong.

*Hush now, hush now, the crown will keep you.*

That's what I'd always thought the nonsense syllables meant. Child-logic. A girl raised in a guild-house full of impossible things, assigning her mother's humming to the only crown she knew.

Riven stood beside me on the road, and I felt him not-touching me—the careful space he kept, the width of a sin he couldn't remember committing. "Dominic," he said quietly. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't walk into that fog after them. People who go in don't come back out *whole*." He paused. "I think I know that. I think I knew someone who—" His hand flexed at his side, grasping at a memory that wasn't there, closing on smoke. "There's a hole where that knowledge should be. It has your shape."

"Then we'll match," I said, and walked forward anyway, because I am my master's apprentice to the last, and the last thing Master Aldery taught me was that dread is just curiosity wearing mourning clothes.

* * *

Up close, the Unwritten were worse and better than the reports claimed.

Worse, because their faces were not blank the way an unwritten page is blank. They were blank the way a page is blank *after* the ink has been scraped off—the ghost of letters still visible if the light came from the right angle, a brow that remembered frowning, a mouth that kept the faint crease of a smile it could no longer source. Each of them was a palimpsest. Someone had been written there once, thoroughly, lovingly even, and then someone else had needed the skin.

Better, because they were not frightening. That was the thing no report would say. They were gentle. They drifted past Riven and me without seeing us, and one of them—an old man with a soldier's ruined posture—paused, turned his scraped face toward me, and hummed a little louder for a moment, as though offering me the only thing he had left. As though I looked hungry.

Maybe I did.

"They're following the song," Riven murmured behind me. He had his map-case open, was sketching as he talked—I'd learned that about him; when the world frightened him, he drew it until it behaved. "The crown sings at dawn, yes, but it doesn't stop. It thins. Gets quieter through the day, like a bell fading, and then at midnight it's nearly nothing, and then dawn winds it again. They're moving toward the strongest note." He tapped the sketch. "Toward Vessia. Toward whatever's left of the singing."

"Why?" I asked, though I already felt the answer forming in my own chest, cold and enormous.

"Because it's the last thing in the country that still knows its name," he said. "And they've lost theirs. You don't stop walking toward the sound of your own name. Even if you can't remember it's yours." He looked up from the sketch, and his eyes were wet and furious with each other. "Even if someone sold it."

I didn't answer that. There would be time. There was going to be so much time, all of it owed.

* * *

I found her an hour past noon, near a fallen waystone, sitting with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a coach that stopped running years ago.

My mother.

The reports had said the forgotten became Unwritten. The ledgers had said it too, in that dry ledger-hand that makes horror look like bookkeeping. But nothing had prepared me for the arithmetic of it: that the woman who taught me my letters, who pressed a bottled echo into my hands on my twelfth birthday because she thought every child should own one impossible thing, who was dead—had been dead for nine years, I had copied the death-entry myself, I had *filed* it—

That she should be here. Alive. Blank. Humming.

Unless she had never died. Unless the entry I filed was one of the neat ones—one of the *too-perfect* ones, the entries I'd been turning over in my mind for days now, my childhood memories arranged like specimen drawers, everything labeled, everything in its place, nothing smudged, nothing torn. A childhood with no loose threads is not a childhood. It's a forgery. And I had been living inside the forgery, calling it grief.

"Dominic," Riven said softly, behind me. Warning or comfort. With him lately it was hard to tell the difference.

"It's her," I said. My voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside me. "It's my mother."

I knelt in the mud in front of her. She didn't see me. Her eyes went through me the way light goes through a window—present, passing, unaltered. Up close I could see the scraping more clearly: the shape of her still there under the erasure, the particular set of her jaw that I had inherited, the small scar on her chin from a kettle, from a story she used to tell, from a story that was gone now along with everything else.

But she was humming.

*Hush now, hush now, the crown will keep you.*

And this close, with the fog pressing in and the country thinning around us like breath on glass, I finally heard it. Not the child-version. Not the nonsense-syllable version. The *structure* of it—the intervals, the rise and fall, the way the third phrase climbed and hung suspended before resolving downward, unresolved, unfinished, waiting—

It was the crown's song.

Not similar. Not an echo. Not a family resemblance. The same melody, note for note, the song the enchanted crown sang over Vessia at every dawn to hold the last of reality together, and my mother had been humming it over my cradle, over my fevered childhood nights, over the top of my head while I copied ledgers, my whole life, and I had thought it was just a lullaby because I was a child and children believe their mothers' songs belong to them.

"It's the same," I whispered. "Riven. It's the same song."

He crouched beside me, and I watched him hear it too—watched the cartographer in him take the two melodies and lay them over each other the way he'd lay two maps of the same coast drawn a century apart, looking for the drift. There was no drift. Perfect overlay.

"That's not possible," he said. "The crown's enchantment is royal work. Court-made. Sealed vaults, king's own blood in the binding—"

"Then explain a washerwoman from the Ledgerhouse quarter humming it to her daughter thirty years before anyone alive heard the crown sing." I reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward a bird you're afraid to startle—and touched my mother's folded hands.

She stilled. For one moment the humming stopped, and the silence was worse than any scream, and then her head tilted, minutely, toward me. Not seeing. But *listening*. The way you listen when a sound touches something deeper than memory—deeper than the scraped page, down in the paper itself.

And she hummed the next phrase.

The phrase the crown never sings. The phrase that isn't in any court record, any vault, any binding—because I could feel it, the way you feel a missing stair in the dark, that the crown's version *stops short*. The royal song ends where the lullaby continues. My mother's version had a whole final movement the crown had never been taught—or had been made to forget.

The Unwritten around us had gone quiet. All of them. Dozens of scraped faces turned toward us—not toward *me*, I understood suddenly, but toward the melody, toward the completion of it, the way starved men turn toward the smell of bread. Their lost names weren't gone. They were *in the song*. The whole song. The part the crown didn't know. The part mothers had been keeping, generation after generation, in the only vault no broker could enter—in the wordless place where women sing to children.

Memory as inheritance. The name of the country hadn't been sold out of the ledgers alone. It had been *hidden*—distributed, scattered, braided into a lullaby and sung into the ears of children for three hundred years, so that no single sale, no single signature, no single betrayal could ever hold all of it.

Someone had planned for this. Someone had known the name would be sold, and had made sure that the buying of it would be incomplete.

My mother's scraped face turned fully toward me now. Her lips moved. No sound came—but I read the shape of it, the way I'd read her lips through sickroom doors as a child, and the shape was not a word.

It was a note. The next note. Held out to me like an apple from her hand.

"Dominic," Riven said, and his voice had gone strange. "Look at the waystone."

I looked. The fallen border-stone, dead and gray since the wards failed, had a thread of light in its carved grooves. Faint. Flickering. Keeping imperfect time.

With her humming.

The stone wasn't listening to the crown anymore. The crown's song was failing—it had been failing for days, thinner each dawn, the queen's court crumbling around its melody. But this was the rest of the song. The unsold part. The part carried in the throats of mothers and the scraped memories of everyone the empire forgot to buy completely, because you cannot purchase what was given away freely, and love gives freely, always, without ledger or contract—

"The choir," I breathed. "They're not drawn to the crown's song. They *are* the song. The rest of it."

My mother waited, lips shaped around the next note, patient as she had been patient over every fevered night of my childhood, certain that eventually I would learn the tune.

I opened my mouth to take it from her.
