## 8. Terms of Surrender

*Dominic*

The fog-line was exactly where the country used to end, which is to say it was nowhere at all. You walked the old north road until the milestones stopped agreeing with each other—here a stone that said ASHFORD, TWELVE MILES, there a stone that said nothing, its letters gone smooth as an egg—and then you walked a little further and the world simply gave up pretending.

I had expected something dramatic. A wall of grey, a curtain, a door. Instead the fog was polite. It stood about chest-high, like a hedge that had heard of boundaries but never met one, and beyond it I could see the empire's camp: neat rows of white tents, cookfires burning with a steadiness that felt obscene, soldiers drilling in perfect silence because sound, apparently, was one of the things they'd purchased along with everything else.

"They're waiting for us," Riven said beside me. He'd shaved, or tried to; there was still a stripe of stubble under his ear like a cartographer's error he couldn't bring himself to correct. "Envoys love waiting. It's the only part of diplomacy where they feel powerful."

"You've met this one before."

"I've met everyone before. That's what being royal cartographer meant—you memorized faces so you could draw their borders." He flexed his hands. They hadn't stopped shaking since the vaults. "Dominic. Before we go down there. If it comes to a price—"

"It won't come to a price."

"Everything comes to a price. You taught me that. You, specifically, with your ledgers and your little ink-stained sermons about equivalent exchange."

"I also taught you that some debts are fraudulent," I said. "This one was. The king never should have been allowed to collateralize a name. And you were a child when they used you as a pen."

"I was nineteen."

"A child," I repeated, in my master's voice, the one that ended arguments, and Riven had the grace—or the cowardice—to let it go.

We walked down into the fog together. It didn't part so much as tolerate us, beading on my sleeves like cold sweat. Behind us, faint and fraying, the crown's dawn-song carried from the capital, thin as thread through a needle's eye. Ahead, the Unwritten had begun to gather along the fog's edge, hundreds of them, blank-faced and humming. My mother among them. I did not look at her. If I looked at her now, I would not be able to negotiate, and negotiating was the one thing left I knew how to do.

The envoy received us in the largest tent, seated behind a table so bare it looked scrubbed of history. She was small, silver-haired, with the kind of face that had been beautiful in the way a ledger is beautiful—all its lines in service of function. When she saw Riven, she smiled with genuine warmth, and I hated her instantly and completely.

"Riven Calloway," she said. "You look terrible."

"I'm told memory loss does that."

"And you must be the apprentice." Her eyes moved to me, cataloguing. I knew that look. It was how merchants appraise goods, how Ledgerkeepers appraise entries. "Dominic Vane. Last of your guild. Do you know, we tried to buy the Ledgerhouse too? Your master refused. Stubborn man. He said some things shouldn't have owners." She spread her hands. "And yet here we are, and everything has an owner, including, at present, your entire country."

"The contract," I said, laying the sealed ledger on the table. "Clause nine. I want to hear the terms of surrender read aloud."

"Surrender?" She raised an eyebrow. "There's been no war."

"There's been a theft."

"A *purchase*," she corrected gently, "which is not the same thing, whatever your guild's sentimental accounting might claim. But very well. Clause nine." She recited it from memory, which told me she'd read it many times, probably aloud, probably savoring it:

"*Should the debtor fail to render payment by the final dawn, the asset—the true name of the realm herein described—shall remain the property of the buyer in perpetuity. Said contract may be voided only upon two conditions: first, should the name be returned willingly by one holding authority over it; or second, should the name be sung whole by a living voice that holds it entire.*"

She closed her eyes briefly, enjoying herself. "*Note well:* the singing of the name consumes the singer's memories as fuel. All of them. Irrevocably. The voice may survive. The singer does not, in any sense the word implies."

The tent was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the Unwritten hummed their broken harmonies, and beneath them, miles away, the crown sang its automated fragment, and the two melodies almost touched.

"That's it, then," Riven said quietly. "That's the whole trap. Willing return requires someone with authority over the name—which means the crown, which means a queen who is half-Unwritten and couldn't hold a whole sentence, let alone a whole name. And singing it means dying."

"Not dying," the envoy said. "*Forgetting.* There's a difference. We're not monsters, Miss Vane. The singer would live. They'd simply wake up with nothing—no past, no loves, no griefs. A blank page." She smiled again. "Some people pay good money for that."

I thought of my mother's face, smooth and humming. I thought of how she'd smiled at me in the fog without knowing me, and how some animal part of me had wanted to shake her until the knowing came back, and how another part had wanted to leave her there, because a mother who doesn't know you is a wound that reopens every morning.

"I'll do it," Riven said.

He said it the way he drew coastlines—quickly, decisively, before the coastline could change. "I hold the name whole. Parts of it, anyway—I brokered the sale, I carried the documents, I remember more than anyone alive except you. And my memories are—" he laughed, a short ugly sound, "—Dominic, half of mine are already gone. I sold them years ago without knowing it. What's left isn't worth the fuel. Let me spend the worthless coin."

"No."

"Dominic—"

"No." I heard my own voice crack on it and hated that too. "You don't get to make this trade. Not this one. You already made one trade for me—do you understand what that did? Nineteen years, Riven. Nineteen years I've been walking around bought and paid for and not knowing it, and every kindness you ever showed me I have to re-examine now, because I can't tell anymore what was yours and what was the debt's. If you burn yourself out singing that song, I will spend the rest of my life wondering whether the woman who wakes up next to me is a person or a receipt."

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair. That's rather the point of contracts."

The envoy watched us with the patience of someone watching weather. "If I might note," she said, "the clause doesn't specify *whose* living voice. Only that it holds the name whole. And from our own assessments—" she tapped a document on the table, "—only two people in existence currently qualify."

"Then we have time," I said, "to make a third."

*Riven*

Here is what nobody tells you about loving a Ledgerkeeper: they will find the loophole. Always. You can offer them your heart on a plate and they will pick up the plate, examine the maker's mark, and ask who else has eaten from it.

After the tent, after Dominic's *no* that sounded like a door slamming, we walked the fog-line while she thought. I knew better than to interrupt her thinking. Twenty years ago I'd made that mistake exactly once and she'd catalogued my interruption in a little book she kept called *Objections, Spurious.*

"She's right that we need a third voice," she said finally. "But she's wrong about why. It's not mercy. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic."

"A song burns memories as fuel. Yes? So the size of the fire depends on the size of the fuel. One voice, one lifetime—that's barely enough to sing a whole country into being. It would gutter halfway through. The envoy knows that. It's why she offered the terms so readily. Either condition fails." She stopped walking and turned to face me, and the fog beaded in her hair like she was wearing a crown of cold pearls. "But what if the song had *harmony*?"

I felt it land in my chest before I understood it in my head. "The Unwritten."

"The Unwritten." Her voice shook, just slightly, the first crack in all that ledger-steel. "They don't remember their names. But they remember the *song*, Riven. My mother is out there humming a lullaby that matches the crown's melody note for note. They've lost everything except the music. And music is memory—it's just memory wearing a different coat. If the choir sings with the lead voice—if the whole country sings itself back—then no single memory has to burn alone. The cost gets shared. Distributed across every throat that joins in."

"And the lead voice? The one that holds the name whole? Someone still has to stand at the front and carry the melody. Someone still pays."

"Yes," she said. "Someone pays. But not everything. Not *nothing left*. Just—" she pressed her hand flat against her sternum, "—enough. Enough to matter. Enough to hurt."

I looked at her for a long moment. Fog between us. Humming behind us. The crown's far-off song fraying toward its final dawn.

"You're volunteering," I said.

"I'm the last Ledgerkeeper. Holding things so others don't have to is literally my job description." She tried to smile and failed. "Besides, I've been auditing my own memories, Riven. Did I ever tell you that? Chapter four. My childhood is too neat. Every entry perfect, every scene composed. My mother's lullaby, our kitchen, the day I found the Ledgerhouse—I don't *remember* those things, I *recorded* them. Someone wrote them into me. Which means most of what I'd be burning isn't even mine. It's inventory."

"That's the saddest thing you've ever said, and once you told me my maps were 'a cry for help with a legend and scale bar.'"

"I stand by both."

I reached out and took her hand. She let me. That was new, or newly possible, since the truth about the sale had come back to me—since I'd remembered standing in this same fog twenty years ago, nineteen years old, signing away a country because the alternative was a girl bleeding out in a ditch with empire soldiers closing in. I had chosen her. I had never once regretted it, which was its own kind of horror, and its own kind of grace.

"If you sing," I said, "you'll lose pieces. Real ones, mixed in with the written ones. Things that were actually ours. You know that."

"I know."

"And afterward you might not know me."

Her grip tightened. "Then I suppose," she said, "you'll have to introduce yourself. You're decent enough at drawing borders between things. Draw one around us and label it."

Above the fog, impossibly, one star flickered back—not returned, just flickering, like a candle remembering it had a wick. The crown's song swelled a fraction. Down the line, the Unwritten turned their blank faces toward it, and their humming shifted, found a key, began to climb.

The envoy emerged from her tent, watching, and for the first time her composure slipped. "What are you doing?"

"Renegotiating," Dominic called back, and her voice carried the way a ledger-line carries, straight and sure across the whole white page of the fog. "Tell your emperor to check clause nine again. It says a *living voice*. It says nothing about how many."

She turned to me in the greying dark, and dawn was coming—the last dawn, or the first one, depending entirely on what we did in the next hour.

"Wake the choir," she said. "Bring me my mother. And Riven—" her hand found mine in the fog, "—when I forget, remind me that I chose this. Freely. Write it down somewhere I'll find it."

"I will," I promised. "In the margin. Where all the honest notes go."

The crown began to sing.
