## 9. When the Crown Sings

I had always assumed that if I ever stood before a dying court and a choir of the faceless, about to sing a country back into existence, I would be more frightened than this.

I was not less frightened. I was simply past the part of fear that screams, into the part that takes inventory. Ledgerkeeper to the end. I counted what I had: one lullaby learned at my mother's knee—or rather, written there, in handwriting suspiciously like ledger-script. One crown, singing its automated fragment on the dais, thin as wire. One Riven Calloway, standing too close, the way he always did when he was terrified and pretending it was cartographic concern.

And one true name, folded inside me like a letter I'd been carrying my whole life without knowing the address.

The throne room had not been swept since reality began fraying. Ash from the court's burned documents drifted across the marble in grey drifts, and the young queen sat her throne with half her face still unfinished—a smudge where her left eye should have been, though she'd insisted we not mention it. The nobles who remained were worse: translucent at the edges, flickering when they forgot themselves mid-curtsey. One countess had been holding the same bow for three days because she could no longer remember how bows ended.

Above us, through the shattered skylight, the sky was wrong. Not dark—dark would have been honest. It was *unwritten*, a pale nothing where stars had been, and where the fog pressed against the edges of the broken glass like something reading over our shoulder.

"They're coming," Riven said quietly.

He didn't need to point. Through the great doors, down the ruined avenue, the Unwritten were gathering—the way fog gathers, the way grief gathers. Hundreds of them, blank-faced, drawn by the crown's failing song as moths are drawn by the last candle in a house. And among them, walking with the patient certainty of someone following a melody home, was my mother.

She wore the shawl I remembered. Of course she did. I had remembered it so many times, so perfectly, that it had probably outlasted the original.

"Dominic." The queen's voice came out cracked. Half a voice for half a woman. "If this fails—"

"It won't fail," I said.

"You don't know that."

"I'm a Ledgerkeeper. We never know anything. We just write it down anyway." I turned to face her, and I made myself look at the smudged eye, because she deserved to be seen whole even if she wasn't. "Your Majesty. When I sing, everything the name touches will come back. Borders. Stars. Faces. But the name has to be sung by someone who holds it *whole*—every street, every debt, every grave. And the song burns memory as fuel. All of mine."

The court went very quiet. Somewhere in the galleries, the countess finally finished her bow, which felt oddly like an omen.

"That is why I offered first," Riven said behind me.

"And that is why I refused," I said, without turning around. "You already paid once. You paid me, you absolute man. You traded your country's name for my life when I was nine years old, and you've spent twenty years not remembering it. Nobody gets to pay twice on my account. That's not sacrifice, that's bookkeeping fraud."

"Dominic—"

"Besides." Now I turned, and I let him see all of it, every terrible thing I felt, unledgered. "Someone has to remember me afterward. That's the whole design. The country exists as long as someone remembers it. Well—I exist as long as someone remembers *me*. And I trust exactly one disgraced cartographer to hold the map of who I am."

His jaw worked. Twenty years of erased guilt and three weeks of re-learned love moved across his face, and he nodded once, like a man signing something he knew would ruin him.

"Then hold on to me," he said. "Whatever unravels. Hold on."

* * *

We went down the steps together, past the dead court, out onto the ash-strewn plaza where the Unwritten waited. They parted for us—not in fear, but the way water parts for a keel. My mother stood at their center, and up close her blankness was worse than any horror: her face was smooth as an unwritten page, and yet her lips moved, humming.

The lullaby. The crown's song. The same seven notes, braided through both, waiting all these years for someone to notice the stitch.

"Mama," I said, uselessly, wonderfully. "I found your song."

Her humming did not change. But her head tilted, very slightly, toward my voice. A page turning toward a draft.

I took off my gloves. Ledgerkeepers work in gloves; ink stains, memories stain worse. Bare-handed, I reached for the crown's song where it hung in the dawn air, and I reached for the lullaby in my chest, and I pulled them together the way you braid rope—over, under, over—and then I opened the letter I'd been carrying all my life and began to sing the country's true name.

I will not write the name here. Some entries must remain sealed; ask my apprentices someday, if they're brave.

But I can tell you what it did.

The first note took my mother's kitchen—the smell of bread, the chipped blue bowl, the way she said my name with the *o* rounded soft. I fed them into the song and felt them go warm and then weightless and then gone, and the sky above the palace flushed pink where pink should be, one star lingering stubbornly at the horizon like a clerk refusing to close the books.

The second verse took my apprenticeship: Master Ellery's cough, the smell of sealing wax, the exact weight of the bottled echo in my palm. The wards flared back to life across the city, ward-stone by ward-stone, a constellation lighting in reverse, and somewhere out in the fog the borders began to knit—roads remembering where they went, hills rolling back into place like shoulders settling.

The third verse was harder. It wanted my mother entire—not the perfect ledger-copy, but the real her, the tired her, the her who sang off-key on purpose to make me laugh. I hesitated, one note hanging, and the song began to take things I hadn't offered. My name. My—

Hands closed around me from behind. Riven. His arms locked across my collarbone, his mouth at my ear, and he was *giving*, I felt it, a warmth pouring into me along his grip like coin counted into a beggar's hand.

Our first meeting—the archive dust, my elbow in his ribs reaching for a misfiled atlas. Our last night before the borders fell, bad wine on the observatory roof, him naming constellations wrongly on purpose. The way he said *Dominic* when he thought I was asleep. He poured them into me faster than the song could burn, a smuggler running goods across a border only he could see, and I understood suddenly what he'd done twenty years ago: this was the shape of his love. He paid. He never told anyone. He kept the receipt hidden even from himself.

"Don't you dare," I sang, because the song had taken my words for a moment and given them back tuned. "Don't you dare spend yourself dry, Riven Calloway, I forbid it, I'll audit you—"

"Ledgerkeepers," he murmured, "always threatening audits at the worst moments," and gave me another year.

The song crested. The name rang out whole over the plaza, over the fog, over the empire beyond it—which recoiled like a hand caught in a till, its claim dissolving, because a name freely sung by a living voice voids all liens upon it. I felt the contract tear. I felt the buyer's cold attention withdraw from our edges like a tide going out.

And the Unwritten began to come back.

It started with my mother. Her face returned the way dawn returns—not all at once, but feature by feature, the lines around her eyes arriving first, then the mouth that hummed, then the eyes themselves, finding me, widening, filling with twenty years of stolen time. Around her, hundreds of faces bloomed open like pages under a wet brush. The countess in the gallery gasped and sat down hard. The queen's left eye arrived with a blink and immediate, furious tears.

The stars came out. All of them, in daylight, briefly—reality showing its receipts—before fading politely into blue.

The song ended. Songs end; that is their nature.

I stood in the sudden ordinary morning, in Riven's arms, in a country that existed again, and I took inventory of myself.

Mother's kitchen: gone. Apprenticeship: gone. The observatory roof: gone—but no, wait, there it was, fainter, secondhand-warm, *his* version of it now living in my head because he'd poured it into me. I was a house most of whose rooms had been sold to pay the song, furnished now with borrowed pieces, and I stood very still among them, cataloguing.

"Dominic?" Riven's voice was careful. "What do you remember?"

I looked up at him—at this man I loved, whom I loved, present tense, I checked twice—and found the truth sitting in me like a new entry.

"I remember that you love me," I said slowly. "I remember that I love you. I remember the shape of everything, and almost none of the contents." My mother was walking toward me across the plaza, real, alive, calling my name in a voice I no longer recognized but somehow still answered to. "Riven. I can't remember her face. She's right there and I can't—"

"Then we start a new ledger," he said, and his voice broke on it, and he held on. "Page one. Right now. I'll dictate. You'll write. That's what you do."

My mother reached us. She took my face in her hands, and I saw her understand—saw her see the blankness where she should have been in me—and she did the only thing a mother could do.

She began to hum.

Seven notes. The crown's song. The lullaby. And this time, as she hummed, I felt something settle into the empty rooms of me—not the old memories, those were spent, burnt clean—but something new being written, in her voice, in real time.

Behind us, on the palace steps, the young queen cleared her throat with the delicacy of someone about to make a deeply inconvenient request.

"Keeper Vane," she said. "Now that the country exists again... there remains the small matter of what, precisely, it *is*. And who sold it. And who bought it back. And—" her unfinished eye had finished, and it fixed on me with terrifying royal attention, "—the ledgers will need to be reopened. Honestly, this time. I assume you're the one to do it?"

I looked at my mother humming. At Riven, who had loved me twice, once blind. At the country, breathing again around its fragile, remembered edges.

"Yes," I said. "But I'll need a new book. Page one starts today."

* * *

That evening, in the gutted Ledgerhouse, I set fresh paper on the master's desk and dipped my pen, and my hand shook only a little.

*Entry the first,* I wrote. *Of the new ledger.*

*The country's name was sold. It was bought back with a song. The singer remembers almost nothing, and is remembered by everyone—and that, I am told, is how a kingdom survives: not on stone, not on borders, but on being held in mind by someone who refuses to put the pen down.*

*I am that someone. Heaven help the kingdom.*

Below it, in a different hand—careful, cartographic, learning itself again—someone had already added a line:

*She forgot the observatory roof. I haven't. Ask me anytime.*
