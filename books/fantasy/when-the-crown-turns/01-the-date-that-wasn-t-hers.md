## 1. The Date That Wasn't Hers

The ferryman's name was Oskar Brenn, and he had drowned on a Tuesday, which Astrid Voss considered typical of him. He'd been the sort of man who did everything slightly wrong and somehow arrived anyway—ferried passengers across the Sill with one oar longer than the other, sang songs whose words he'd forgotten halfway through, and once, memorably, delivered a coffin to its own funeral because the family couldn't afford two trips.

Now he lay on her workbench in three pieces of river-washed bone she'd bought from his widow for four coppers and a jar of plum preserves.

"Steady now," Astrid murmured to him, though he was in no state to hear it yet. She turned the long bone of his forearm under the lamp, her carving knife moving in small, patient strokes. *Oskar Brenn. Ferryman. Forty-one years.* The letters came out clean and deep, the way names ought to be. A name carved shallow faded; a name carved deep lasted as long as the bone did. She had opinions about this. She had opinions about most things, kept carefully to herself, which was how she'd managed forty years in Thornfell without anyone remembering much about her at all beyond *the bone-carver on Tanner's Lane, good work, quiet sort.*

Invisible. That was the trick of it. Not hidden—hidden made people curious—but unremarkable. A woman who carved names for the dead and asked no questions and gave correct change.

She was finishing the last stroke of the *n* when the whisper started.

It always began like this: a sound like wind through a keyhole, or someone humming behind a wall. Then it gathered into words. Most of the dead said very little. *Cold,* they said, or *where are my boots,* or, heartbreakingly often, *tell Marta I didn't mean it about the goat.* The dead were not profound. They were mostly just interrupted.

Oskar Brenn whispered: *"Don't count them."*

Astrid's knife stopped. "Count what?" she asked, out loud, because there was no one else in the shop and she'd long since stopped caring how it looked.

*"The turns,"* he said. His voice had the wet echo of the river still in it. *"Don't count the turns. Some things count you back."*

"That," said Astrid, "is nonsense."

But her hands had gone cold around the knife, and she set it down with exaggerated care, the way you do when you don't trust yourself to be casual about something. The whisper faded into that particular silence the dead leave behind, like a room after a guest who took nothing but left everything rearranged.

She looked down at the finished bone. *Oskar Brenn. Ferryman. Forty-one years.* All correct. All ordinary.

Except that beneath her name-line, in letters she had absolutely not carved—in letters finer than her own hand could manage, thin as spider silk, as if the bone itself had grown them—the words read:

*Thirteen turns.*

* * *

"You're pale," said Maretta, sliding a cup of chicory across the counter of the Salt & Anchor as if medicine required no discussion. Maretta was seventy, built like a barrel of nails, and had decided sometime in the previous decade that Astrid was insufficiently fed and insufficiently socialized, and appointed herself the remedy for both without consulting either party.

"I'm always pale. I work indoors."

"Paler." Maretta squinted at her. "Someone die?"

"A ferryman."

"Well, that's honest work, dying. Sit. Eat." She pushed over a plate of bread and cheese that had clearly been intended for someone else and would be denied ever having existed if asked.

Astrid sat. She ate. And she tried, in the manner of a sensible woman, to reason her way out of the morning.

Bones held residue. Every carver knew this—it was why you worked with the window open, why old carvers went strange in their final years. A bone remembered being part of somebody, the way a bell remembers being rung. What she'd seen on Oskar's arm-bone might have been a flaw in the grain. Might have been her own tired eyes inventing letters out of striations, the way you saw faces in knots of wood.

Might have been.

"Don't count the turns," she muttered into her chicory.

"Eh?" said Maretta.

"Nothing. How's your hip?"

"Wretched. Your mother's hip was wretched too, and her mother before her. It's the damp. It's the—" Maretta stopped, because a stranger had come through the door, and strangers in Thornfell were events, like comets.

He was tall and grey-cloaked and moved like oil poured slowly, and when his eyes passed over the room Astrid felt them the way you feel a draft under a door. He ordered nothing. He asked Maretta one question—too low for Astrid to catch—and then left again, unhurried, and the room exhaled.

"Huh," said Maretta. "Customs, probably. We've got customs men crawling everywhere this week. Something about old books." She snorted. "As if anyone in Thornfell can read."

Astrid could read. Astrid could read three scripts and half a fourth, a fact known to exactly no one, because a bone-carver who read was charming and a bone-carver who read *old things* was a question, and questions were how people became memorable.

She finished her bread, thanked Maretta, gave correct change for nothing, and walked home the long way, past the river, where the water ran high and brown with snowmelt and did not, at any point, whisper anything at all.

* * *

The bookstall only appeared on Thursdays, in the alley behind the tannery, and only appeared to people who didn't go looking for it. This was not magic, whatever Yeva Kroll's runners claimed. It was commerce. Dov Hessel sold smuggled print from a handcart and had learned, through decades of fines and confiscations, that the safest customer was the one who stumbled on you by accident.

Astrid stumbled on him by accident every Thursday.

"Dov."

"Bone-carver." He never used her name. Neither of them wanted the transaction to exist on paper, spoken, or in the memory of God. "Got sermons. Got almanacs. Got a cookbook with a chapter missing that I'm told is the *interesting* chapter. Got poetry, if you're feeling unwell."

"History," said Astrid. "Old. Local."

Dov's eyebrows performed a small negotiation above his spectacles. "Local's expensive."

"So is the rope they'd use on both of us. Show me what you have."

What he had, under a false bottom beneath three bawdy pamphlets and a tax ledger, was a slim book bound in cracked green leather, no title on the spine, water-stained at the corners. The moment her fingers touched it, the hair on her arms stood up—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The same feeling she got from bones that had been loved.

"What is it?"

"Found it in an estate lot upriver. Old house, old family, everybody gone. The Regency men took the furniture and burned the papers, missed this because it was inside a chair cushion, which tells you all you need to know about Regency men." He shrugged. "Century old, near enough. Prophecy stuff. Moon-counting, end-of-days, the usual nonsense people wrote before they had real troubles."

"Prophecies are contraband twice over," Astrid said. "Once for the content, once for the age."

"Everything worth owning is illegal, bone-carver. That's how you know it's worth owning."

She haggled him down to an amount that hurt, paid in coins she'd carved her mark off of, and told herself she was buying it to destroy it safely, which was even true, mostly, in the way that many of her decisions were true mostly.

At home, with the shutters latched and the lamp trimmed low, she opened it.

The first pages were the usual moon-madness—charts of lunar turns, verses about crowns and doors, the fevered geometry of a mind that had stared too long at the sky. But the handwriting shifted partway through, grew smaller, more urgent, and the margins filled with annotations in a second hand, and then a third, generations of readers passing the book along like a bucket in a fire line.

And then she reached a page with a drawing.

Hands. Two hands, drawn from the wrists, palms up, fingers slightly spread—the posture of a carver presenting work, or of a beggar asking alms, or of a body laid out for burial. The drawing was fine, confident, a century old. And around the wrists, where a carver's calluses would sit, the artist had inked small marks. Seven on the left. Nine on the right.

Astrid set the book down. Walked to the window. Stood there breathing until the panic became merely unpleasant instead of catastrophic. Then she lit a second lamp, held her own hands up to the light, palms up, fingers spread, and counted.

Seven on the left. Nine on the right. Sixteen calluses, mapped like a constellation, in precisely those places, because sixteen years of knife-work puts calluses where it puts them and nowhere else, and no one in the history of the world had ever thought to draw hers before tonight.

Beside the drawing, in faded iron-gall ink, was a date.

She knew the date the way you know your own birthday, the way you know the number of stairs to your shop. It was a date she had never expected to see written anywhere, because it belonged to no anniversary, no feast day, nothing. It was simply—

Tonight.

The ink was a hundred years dry. The date was tonight.

Astrid Voss sat down on her stool very slowly, the way you sit when your legs have resigned without notice. Around her, in the walls, in the floorboards, in the old beams of a building older than the Regency, the resident dead—who had murmured their small cold nothings for as long as she'd lived here—went suddenly, completely silent.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like a hundred whispers braided into one, they said her name.

Not *Astrid.* Not *Voss.*

Her other name. The one she'd never been given, and had never once heard aloud—and understood, in her marrow, all the same.
