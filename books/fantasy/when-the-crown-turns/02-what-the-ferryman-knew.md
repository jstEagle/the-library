## 2. What the Ferryman Knew

The dead of Thornfell kept reasonable hours, mostly. They liked to murmur at dusk, gossip at midnight, and go quiet before dawn out of some old courtesy Astrid had never been able to explain. So when she set out along the river road an hour past sunrise with a lantern she didn't need and a bone-carving kit in her satchel, she felt faintly ridiculous—like a woman calling on a neighbor at an indecent hour to borrow sugar.

But the ferryman had drowned at noon, and noon was when he'd be strongest.

His name had been Tam Vetch. She knew that much because she'd carved it herself two nights ago, into a length of river birch, the letters deep and clean so his widow could hang it above the door. What she hadn't told anyone—not the widow, not the priest, not old Marta who ran the dye-house and noticed everything—was what Tam's ghost had whispered while her chisel was still moving.

*The thirteenth moon. Don't let them read it.*

She'd assumed he meant something ordinary. A debt ledger, maybe. Ferries attracted debts the way gutters attracted leaves. But then last night she'd found the book—the smuggler-bookstall book, the century-old prophecy with tonight's date inked beside a drawing of hands that were unmistakably hers—and *don't let them read it* had stopped sounding like a man worried about money.

So here she was, walking the river road in broad daylight like a lunatic, going to interview a corpse.

"You could have just haunted me," she muttered to the mist coming off the water. "That's the traditional arrangement."

The river gave no answer, which was typical. The dead of Thornfell were polite to the point of uselessness. They whispered warnings and half-names and never once, in thirty years of listening through her chisel, had any of them managed to say anything useful in fewer than three attempts.

Tam Vetch was waiting where she expected him: at the ferry landing, or rather the memory of one, since the actual ferry had gone down with him. He stood on the water the way the drowned always did, ankle-deep in nothing, looking mildly embarrassed about the whole situation. He'd been a big man in life, barrel-chested, with a beard you could lose a spoon in. In death he was grayer and slightly translucent, but the beard remained impressive.

"Morning, Tam," Astrid said.

He brightened. "You can see me proper today! Not just—" he wiggled his fingers vaguely, "—the mumbly way."

"The mumbly way is how I prefer it, honestly." She sat down on the landing stones and opened her satchel, taking out a blank bone tablet and her smallest knife. Old habit: when the dead talked, she took notes. It kept her hands steady. "I need to ask you some questions."

"About my toll box? Because Hesta knows where I hid it, under the third floorboard, and if she says otherwise she's spending my savings on ribbon again—"

"Not the toll box." She turned the tablet toward him. On it, copied from the prophecy book in careful strokes, was the drawing of the hands. "I need to know what you meant. *Don't let them read it.*"

Tam went still. The river mist seemed to gather around him, interested.

"That's not mine," he said slowly. "That drawing. That came through me, but it isn't mine. Things come down the river, carver-girl. Always have. Words, songs, names. The water remembers what the land wants to forget." He squinted at the drawing. "Where did you find its twin?"

"A book. A hundred years old."

"And dated for tonight?"

Astrid's mouth went dry. "How did you know there was a date?"

"Because everything's got a date," Tam said, with the patience of a man who had spent forty years explaining tides to farmers. "Births. Deaths. Ferries sinking. And doors opening." He looked at her then, really looked, and his ghostly face did something complicated. "You've got river-light on you this morning. You didn't used to."

"I don't know what that means."

"No," he agreed. "But you will. Ask me your questions, girl. I'll answer what I'm allowed."

"*Allowed?* By whom?"

"By the rules of dying," Tam said. "Which are stricter than the Regency's customs code, and I'll thank you not to tell either party I said so."

Astrid wrote that down, mostly to have something to do besides shiver. The morning was cold, and the mist had begun to move against the wind, drifting upstream toward town instead of out to the lowlands. Toward Thornfell. She decided not to think about that.

"The book," she said. "There's a prophecy in it. About a door."

"Out of Vessany," Tam said immediately.

Her knife slipped and gouged a line across the tablet. She stared at him. "Vessany doesn't exist."

"Course it does. It's just not *on* anything anymore. There's a difference between a country and a place on a map, same as there's a difference between a man and a name on a board." He tapped his own chest, where forty years ago a heart had beaten. "I ferried the border crossings back when Vessany was still allowed to be a word. Before they drowned it. And I'll tell you what every crossing-man knew and no map shows: countries don't die when the armies come. They die when nobody remembers their names. That's why they burned the records first. That's why they flooded the capital second. Memory sinks slower than stone, see, but it does sink."

"And the door?"

"The door's how it stops sinking." Tam's voice dropped, and though he was already barely more than breath and gray light, somehow he became less. "Every erased country gets one. A way out. Not for the living—for the *held*. All those souls standing in line for forty years because the gate they died at leads nowhere anymore." He shook his head. "It needs a key. The prophecy would have named it."

"It didn't. It drew hands." She held up the tablet again. "These hands."

Tam looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he looked at her hands, resting on her knees, chisel-callused, ink-stained, unremarkable. His expression did the complicated thing again, worse this time.

"I know those hands," he said quietly.

"They're mine. I checked. Believe me, I checked."

"I ferried people across that river for thirty years, girl. I remember hands. Hands are what you take the coin from." He drifted a little closer, and the cold came with him. "I carried a woman across once, heavy with child, in the last month before the drowning. She wasn't running from the Regency. She was running *toward* something. Going home to have her baby on Vessani ground, even if Vessany only had weeks left. Said a child born on home soil keeps the country alive in her bones whether the maps agree or not."

The river made a sound. Not a wave-sound. Something closer to a held breath.

"She never made the return trip," Tam said. "I heard later the child lived. Grew up somewhere north. Learned a trade." His eyes, pale as riverwater, settled on Astrid's hands. "A trade with her hands."

"Don't," Astrid said. Her voice came out strange. "Whatever name you're circling around, don't say it."

"I wasn't going to." For the first time, the ferryman smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it frightened her more than anything else that morning. "Not because I'm cruel. Because saying it out loud makes it *findable*. Names are handles, carver-girl. You of all people should know—you put them on bones so the dead can be gripped by memory. Well. Some names, once spoken in the open air, can be gripped by other things too."

"Like what?"

"Like men in gray coats who burn books." Tam turned his head, suddenly, toward the road behind her. Astrid turned too.

Down the river road, where the mist was flowing the wrong way, a rider was approaching Thornfell. Even at this distance she could see the coat: gray, regulation, cut in the style the Regency's customs office favored. But customs officers rode dray horses and complained about tariffs. This man rode like a blade rides a scabbard, straight and economical, and the morning light caught something at his collar that gleamed.

"Inquisitor," Tam said. Not a question.

"You can tell from here?"

"I can tell from anywhere. They carry a particular silence with them. Like a hole in the world's conversation." He began to fade, the mist taking him back. "Girl. Listen. The book—"

"Yes?"

"Hide it or burn it, and understand that both choices are traps. Hide it, and they'll find it eventually, and finding means reading, and reading means they learn what it points to. Burn it, and the words don't die—they just move. Into smoke, into ash, into whoever breathed near the fire." His voice was thinning to a thread. "Prophecies aren't paper. Paper's just where they wait."

"Then what am I supposed to do?" Astrid stood up, satchel clutched to her chest like a shield. "You're telling me I can't keep it and can't destroy it. Those are the only two things a person can do with a book!"

"There's a third thing," Tam said. He was nearly gone now, just a suggestion of beard and kindness in the fog. "The thing the book itself is asking. But you won't like it, and I'm not allowed to say it, and anyway—" the mist swallowed him mid-word, and the last thing she heard, faint as a coin dropped in deep water, was:

"—ask the blind woman. Kroll. She owes the river a favor."

Then he was gone, and Astrid stood alone on the landing stones with a gouged tablet, aching hands, and the certain knowledge that a man in a gray coat was about to arrive in her town asking questions.

* * *

Thornfell was waking when she walked back. Smoke rose from chimneys; Marta's dye-house stank gloriously of madder root; two boys were fighting over a stolen apple with the total commitment of professional soldiers. Normal life. Invisible life. Astrid moved through it the way she'd moved through it all her life—head down, steps unhurried, a woman so thoroughly unremarkable that neighbors greeted her with nods rather than names.

Except today the nodding felt like a countdown.

She reached her shop—a narrow building wedged between the chandler's and a wall, with a sign showing a carved hand holding a chisel—and stopped dead in the doorway.

Someone had been by already. Not inside; the wax seal on her door lintel, a superstition she maintained because it kept honest customers honest, was unbroken. But pinned neatly to the post was a printed notice, fresh ink, official crest:

*BY ORDER OF THE REGENT'S COMMISSION FOR RECORDS AND CONTINUITY. An item of historical significance has gone missing from a private collection. Any person possessing or offered materials relating to the pre-Settlement period should present them at the customs house within three days. Cooperation will be noted favorably. Non-cooperation will also be noted.*

"Continuity," Astrid read aloud, tasting the word. Forty years ago they'd called it what it was: erasure. Somewhere along the way the Regency had learned that people resisted erasure but welcomed continuity, the way sheep welcome a shepherd.

The notice was signed with a name: *Serel Dane, Inquisitor-Inspector.*

Underneath, smaller, almost an afterthought: *Inquiries conducted at the sign of the Drowned Bell, beginning this afternoon.*

Astrid took the notice down, folded it, and went inside. The shop smelled of birch shavings and linseed oil, the smell of her whole life. On the workbench lay the memorial tablets waiting for delivery, each with its name carved true. On the shelf above sat her tools, her father's tools, her grandmother's tools—

Her grandmother's.

She looked at the locked chest in the corner for a long moment. It had been in the family longer than she'd been alive. Her mother had told her, once, laughing: *Your grandmother said we keep it for the wool.* Nobody kept locks that good for wool.

And now a man whose job title contained the word *continuity* was coming to town, and a drowned ferryman had told her that hiding and burning were both traps, and a hundred-year-old book with her own hands drawn in its margins sat wrapped in oilcloth beneath her floorboards, humming to itself like something impatiently waiting to hatch.

Astrid Voss had survived thirty years by being no one worth noticing. Every instinct she owned said: stay small, stay dull, say nothing, give the gray coat nothing but a boring afternoon.

But the dead didn't whisper to boring women. The dead had never once, in all her years of carving, mistaken her for someone else. Whatever she was—and the thought made her palms sweat—it had started long before tonight's date in a dead man's book.

"All right," she said to the empty shop, and to the chest, and to the book under the floorboards, and possibly to herself. "If I can't hide it and I can't burn it, then I'd better understand it. Completely. Before he does."

She pulled up the loose plank, unwrapped the oilcloth, and set the prophecy book on her workbench beside the memorial tablets—names for the dead, and one book full of a date for the living. Outside, hoofbeats sounded on the cobbles, slowing.

They stopped directly in front of her shop.

Through the window, Astrid watched a gray-coated man dismount with unhurried precision, glance up at her carved-hand sign, and stand there for a moment, reading it the way other men read threatening letters.

He didn't knock. He simply waited, as if he already knew she was watching, and had all the time in the world.

Astrid closed the book, slid it back under the floorboard, and went to answer a knock that hadn't happened yet.
