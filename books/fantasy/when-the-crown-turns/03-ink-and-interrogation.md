## 3. Ink and Interrogation

The inquisitor arrived on a Tuesday, which Astrid considered a personal insult. Tuesdays were her market days, the one morning she allowed herself to be seen by more than three people at once, and now she would have to spend it being seen by exactly the wrong person.

She spotted him from her shop window before he ever reached her door: a tall man in the grey-and-silver of the Regency customs service, walking the length of Carver's Row with the unhurried gait of someone who had already decided how the day would end. He stopped at every shop. He spoke to the chandler. He spoke to the wool-merchant. He spoke to old Piet at the pie stand, who would tell him anything he wanted to know and several things he didn't, because Piet had never met a stranger or a secret he could keep.

Astrid had perhaps four minutes.

She used two of them badly—wiping flour off her hands that hadn't been there since breakfast, straightening bones that were already straight—and then made herself stop, because fussing was a tell. She had spent thirty-one years learning how not to be noticed, and the first rule was that guilty people tidy things.

The second rule was that honest-looking people opened their doors themselves.

So when his knock came—two measured raps, polite as a tax notice—she opened it with the expression of a woman mildly inconvenienced by bureaucracy, which required no acting at all.

"Good morning." He removed his hat. Grey eyes, weathered face, a voice like dry paper. "Serel Dane, Regency Customs and Excise. We're conducting routine inspections along the river trade routes."

"Routine," Astrid said. "Of course. Would you like to inspect my bone dust? I have three grades. The coarse one gets everywhere."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "Bone-carver."

"Astrid Voss. Fourth house from the bridge, if you're taking names for your ledger. Which you are, because everyone does."

He did take out a ledger. She watched him write her name and noted that he wrote it slowly, the way people write words they intend to look at again later.

* * *

The inspection itself was almost funny. Serel Dane examined her workshop with the thoroughness of a man looking for smuggled brandy in a place where there had never been so much as a cork. He lifted her carving knives and tested their edges against his thumb. He peered into the jar of linseed oil. He held up a half-finished memorial bone—a shepherd's name, a good long life, a lamb carved small at the foot of it—and asked what it was.

"Memorial work," Astrid said. "The family pays, I carve the name and dates, they bury it under the hearthstone. It's an old border custom. Keeps the dead close without keeping them *loud*."

"Loud," he repeated.

"You've never lived with an unquiet ghost, then. Consider yourself fortunate." She took the bone back from him and set it down with exaggerated care. "Mostly they just murmur. But some of them have opinions about furniture."

That earned her the smile's cousin again, briefer this time. He moved on to the back room, where the finished pieces waited in rows on linen shelves, each one tagged with wax paper: names, dates, prices. He read them. All of them. She could see his lips moving faintly, and she understood suddenly that this was not a man searching for contraband. This was a man reading a list.

"The ferryman," he said, stopping at the newest piece. "Corin Mace. Drowned the ninth of last month."

"That's right. His sister commissioned it."

"And you knew him?"

"I carve this town's dead, Master Dane. I've known most of them better after than before." She kept her voice light, kept her hands loose at her sides. "Why do you ask about Corin?"

He turned. For a moment the customs-officer blandness slipped, and underneath it was something sharper and wearier. "His body came up with something in his fist. A scrap of paper. Old paper. It went to the magistrate, and the magistrate sent word to my office, and my office sent me, because—" he paused, choosing, "—because we are very interested in old paper these days."

"Are you."

"There's a book missing," he said. "From a private collection. A century old, give or take. Prophecies, genealogies, that sort of thing. Nonsense, mostly, but expensive nonsense, and its owner wants it found." He watched her face as he said it, and she gave him the face he deserved: mild, slightly bored, the face of a woman thinking about lunch. "You wouldn't have seen it. It would look out of place here. Leather gone black with age, brass corners, a drawing on the third page of—"

"Hands?" Astrid said.

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, Piet was arguing with a goose, and the sound of it seemed absurdly far away.

Serel Dane's eyes narrowed by a degree. "You've seen it."

"I'm guessing," Astrid said, and let herself laugh—a real laugh, startled out of her, which was somehow worse than any lie. "Forgive me. You described a book with a drawing of hands on the third page, and I thought, well, of course it has hands. Every cursed book in every fireside story has hands on the third page. It's tradition. Like ravens. Is it ravens too? Please tell me there's a raven."

He stared at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he huffed—the ghost of a laugh himself, quickly strangled. "No raven."

"Pity. I'd have bought it off you on the spot."

"I don't think you would have." He closed his ledger. "Thank you for your time, Mistress Voss. Your shop is admirably free of contraband."

"High praise from the Excise."

At the door he paused, hat in hand, and looked back at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere. "One more question, purely for the ledger. Where was your family from? Before Thornfell."

The question landed wrong. Not dangerous-wrong—*strange*-wrong, like a key that fits a lock you didn't know you had. "Here," she said. "We've always been here."

"Mm," said Serel Dane. "That's what I'd heard." And he put on his hat and went to bother the cooper next door.

* * *

She should have felt relieved. Instead she stood in her doorway watching him work down the Row, and the unease sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because she had lied, and lying was nothing new—but she had also *noticed*, and noticing was the problem. Two things, specifically, and both of them small, and both of them refusing to stay small.

The first: when Serel Dane had removed his hat, the light through her window had caught his right hand, and on the smallest finger of that hand he wore a signet ring. Old silver, worn thin, with a crest pressed into it. She had seen that crest before. Not on men—on bones. On the memorial pieces her grandmother had carved, forty years ago, before her hands shook too badly to hold a graver: little flourishes worked into the borders of royal names, a crown over crossed keys, elegant and strange and utterly unlike anything else in Thornfell's grave-goods. Her grandmother had called the design *the seal of home*, and when child-Astrid had asked where home was, her grandmother had said *nowhere you'll ever need to spell*, and had changed the subject so completely and so fast that Astrid had learned, without knowing she was learning, never to ask again.

Vessany. The vanished country. The one that wasn't on maps anymore, that the schoolmaster skipped in his lessons with a cough and a shuffle of pages, that grown-ups mentioned only in the lowered voices reserved for drownings and disgrace. The Regency had erased it forty years ago—unmade its borders, scattered its people, struck its name from every record—and here was a Regency inquisitor wearing its crest on his finger like a wound he couldn't stop touching.

And the second thing: the chest.

Her grandmother's chest sat in the corner of Astrid's bedroom, where it had sat since the funeral six years ago. Black oak, iron-banded, locked, the key lost before Astrid was born—or so the family story went. Astrid had never once tried to open it. It was the one object in her life she treated the way other people treated shrines: with the particular reverence of someone who suspects the god inside might be embarrassed to be seen.

When Serel Dane had walked past the bedroom door on his way to inspect her storeroom—had walked within six feet of that chest, not glancing at it, not knowing it existed—Astrid had heard it hum.

Not creak. Not settle. *Hum.* A low, resonant note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, rising as he passed and fading as he moved away. She had told herself it was the floorboards. She had told herself it was the wind. She had stood in the storeroom doorway smiling her honest smile while every hair on her arms stood up and the chest sang softly to itself behind her, and she had thought: *it knows him. Or he knows it. Or both.*

Now the shop was empty, the afternoon light was going amber, and Astrid climbed the stairs to her bedroom and stood in front of the chest with her arms crossed, feeling profoundly foolish.

"Well?" she said.

The chest said nothing. Chests generally didn't. But the silence had a quality to it—the silence of something listening back.

"Don't do that," she told it. "Whatever you are, whatever Grandmother kept in you, don't start *now*. I have had a very strange month. There was a book with my hands in it. There was a drowned man whispering about doors. And today a man from the government walked through my shop wearing a dead country on his finger, and you *hummed at him*, and I am not a woman who frightens easily, but I am also not a woman who talks to furniture, so one of us is changing, and I'd rather it weren't me."

The chest hummed. Once. Softly. Almost—she would have sworn it, though she'd have denied it under torture—an affectionate sort of hum.

"Oh, wonderful," Astrid said, and went downstairs to make tea, because there are problems that cannot be faced sober and problems that cannot be faced without tea, and hers was somehow both.

* * *

She drank the tea slowly, watching the street through the curtain. Serel Dane finished with the cooper, finished with the tanner, and stood a while at the end of the Row looking at the river. Then he did something odd. He took off his signet ring, held it up to the last of the light, turned it twice—and put it back on the wrong hand. Left instead of right. As if he'd caught himself wearing it where someone might see, and corrected the error a moment too late.

As if he were hiding it. From whom?

Not from her; he'd shown it to her freely enough, whether he meant to or not. From his own people, then. From the Regency whose livery he wore. An inquisitor carrying a Vessani signet into towns along the old border, asking about old books, writing down names—

Astrid set down her cup. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Underneath the steadiness, something was rearranging itself, quietly and thoroughly, the way ice shifts on a river before anyone hears the crack.

For thirty-one years she had built her life on a simple architecture: be useful, be unremarkable, be nobody's business. Carve the dead their names, keep the living at a polite distance, want nothing loudly. It had worked. It had even, she'd believed, made her happy, in the way a well-made chair is happy: functional, sound, unexamined.

But the chair had started humming. And a man with a dead country on his finger had read every name on her shelves like a man memorizing a list. And somewhere out there was a book with tonight's date inked beside a drawing of her own two hands, and a drowned ferryman who had died holding a scrap of old paper in his fist, and a locked chest that sang when danger walked past its lid.

Her grandmother had known. That was the thought that finally broke through, cold and clear as river water. Her grandmother had known *something*—had carved the seal of a vanished kingdom into memorial bones and hidden it all in a chest she'd locked against her own granddaughter's curiosity. Had raised Astrid invisible on purpose. Had taught her, stitch by stitch, how to be no one.

Which meant invisibility wasn't Astrid's nature. It was her inheritance. A costume fitted to her before she could walk.

And costumes, Astrid thought, setting her cup in the basin with a small decisive click, could be taken *off*.

She went upstairs. She knelt in front of the chest. She ran her fingers along the iron banding, the black oak, the lock with its empty keyhole—and stopped.

There, cut into the underside of the lock plate, so small she'd never seen it in six years of not-looking: a mark. A crown over crossed keys.

The seal of home.

Astrid sat back on her heels and laughed once, short and disbelieving, at the sheer theatrical patience of her own family. Then she got up, went to her tool wall, and took down the finest saw she owned—because she was a bone-carver, and bone-carvers understood one great truth above all others:

Anything locked can be opened. It's only a question of how much you're willing to cut.

Outside, the sun went down over Thornfell, and somewhere across the river, in a room with a lamp and a ledger, Serel Dane wrote *Astrid Voss* one more time, slowly, and underlined it.
