## 5. Thirteen Turns

The almanac in Astrid's shop was her grandmother's, which meant it was older than the Regency, older than the bridge, and possibly older than the concept of punctuality. Its pages were soft as cloth, its margins crowded with a spidery hand that had recorded forty years of frosts, births, ferry crossings, and one memorable incident involving a goat and the customs house.

Astrid sat up past midnight with it open beside the prophecy book, counting moons.

She counted three times, because the first two counts had made her feel ill.

Twelve turns. The moon would fill and empty twelve more times before the date inked in the old book — the date that matched the ferryman's warning, the date that now lived behind her eyes like a splinter she couldn't dig out. Thirteen turns from the night she'd found it. She'd already spent one.

"Twelve," she said aloud to the empty shop, because saying things aloud made them smaller. It usually worked. It did not work tonight. "Twelve turns, and then I die of something unspecified, in a manner unspecified, for reasons involving a door and a crown I've never seen."

The fire ticked. The bones on their racks said nothing, which was unusual; they were generally terrible gossips after dark.

She turned back to the prophecy book and read the passage again, slowly, the way she'd learned to read difficult inscriptions — not hunting the meaning but letting it come to her like a shy animal.

*On the thirteenth turn the heir shall stand at the door out of Vessany, and the crown shall know its own.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought: *Before that day, small things shall go awry for her, as the world begins to lean.*

"The world begins to lean," Astrid muttered. "Wonderful. Very reassuring. Not ominous at all."

But it was the *small things* that snagged her, the way a loose thread snags. Prophecies about crowns and doors were grand and unfalsifiable — who could test them? But small things. Small things could be tested. Small things could be *avoided*, if you knew they were coming.

She read on, and there, three lines down, in the same cramped hand:

*First the milk shall sour, and the step shall fail, and what is dropped shall break.*

Astrid looked at the jug of goat's milk sitting on her cold hearthstone, bought fresh that morning from Widow Marun, who watered it shamelessly but not enough to matter.

She did not touch the milk. She moved it instead — carried it outside into the frost-cold shed where the temperature would hold it honest, and set it on the high shelf, well away from anything warm or souring. Then she came back inside, swept her doorstep clean of ice so no step could fail there, and — after some deliberation — took the one fragile thing she owned, her mother's blue glass ring, and put it in a padded box in a padded drawer in a locked chest.

"There," she told the prophecy book, with the satisfaction of a woman checkmating an opponent across the table. "Sour nothing. Slip nowhere. Break nothing. Let's see your lean now."

The book, being a book, declined to respond. But somewhere in the walls, faint as breath, the dead laughed.

* * *

The next morning, Astrid woke to a sound she hadn't heard since childhood: rain in Thornfell, in deep winter, falling soft and wrong against the shutters.

By noon the frost had turned to slick black glaze over every stone in town. By mid-afternoon, Widow Marun's goat — the famous goat, the customs-house goat — had slipped on the glazed cobbles outside Astrid's shop, careened into the water barrel, and knocked it over, and the barrel had rolled, and the barrel had been full, and the resulting flood had found the one low gap under Astrid's door and poured itself cheerfully across her floorboards, straight into the locked chest's corner where she kept —

Well. Where she had kept, until that moment, her mother's blue glass ring, which she had moved at the last minute to the padded drawer, except that in the chaos of mopping she had picked up the drawer to empty the water and the ring had been in her apron pocket, and her apron pocket had caught on the latch of the chest, and the ring had flown out, arced through the lamplight with a kind of deliberate grace, and shattered on the hearthstone into eleven pieces.

Astrid stood in the wreckage of her careful plans, soaked to the knees, holding a broom, and began — somewhat hysterically — to laugh.

"Sour nothing," she said. "Slip nowhere. Break nothing."

The milk, she discovered when she fetched it from the shed, had frozen solid and burst its jug. Of course it had. Cold soured it just as thoroughly as warmth; she simply hadn't thought of cold, because she'd been thinking like a woman defending a house instead of a woman arguing with fate.

That was the lesson, she realized, sitting on her wet floor among the blue glass shards, picking up the largest piece and turning it in her fingers. The prophecy didn't predict events. It predicted *outcomes*. Avoid the milk going sour and the milk froze. Sweep the step and the goat arrived from a direction no sweep could cover. Fate wasn't a road; it was water, finding the low place, always the low place, no matter how you built your walls.

And yet.

She held the biggest shard of blue glass up to the light. Her mother's ring. Her mother, who had died when Astrid was six, who existed now only in this glass and in the finger-bone key Astrid wore at her throat and in whatever else waited in the locked chest she still hadn't fully searched.

Fate could find the low places. Fine. Then Astrid Voss would spend twelve turns becoming very good at having no low places.

"You can lean all you like," she informed the world, and the world, dripping, declined to answer.

* * *

It took her two days to piece the ring back together, working by lamplight with tweezers and fish-glue, and even then it was a ghost of itself — a lattice of cracks, blue light showing through every seam. She wore it anyway, on her right hand, over the glove. A repaired thing. A thing that had broken and been deliberately, stubbornly reassembled by someone who refused to let it stay broken.

She found herself looking at it while she worked, and thinking: *that's what I'll do.* Whatever the thirteenth turn brought — a door, a crown, a death — she would go to pieces if she must, and then she would pick herself up and glue herself back together along new lines, and the light would come through differently, but it would come through.

It was, she admitted, a strange comfort to take from a shattered ring. But bone-carvers learn early that everything worth keeping has been broken at least once.

* * *

Serel Dane wrote his reports the way he did everything else: precisely, completely, and without a single word he wouldn't be able to defend under oath.

*To the Office of Inquiry, Third Bureau, from Inquisitor Serel Dane, resident, Thornfell district.*

*Subject: recovery of proscribed text, provisionally identified as the Vessani oracle-cycle known as the Lament of Moons.*

*Progress: minimal. I am aware this will not please you. I have chosen to report accurately rather than pleasingly.*

He paused over that last line, quill hovering. Three years in Thornfell had taught him that his masters in the capital preferred reports shaped like arrows — straight, pointed, aimed at a conclusion they'd already drawn. He had learned to write them that way. Tonight he crossed the sentence out and rewrote it:

*Progress: slow. The text has not surfaced in any of the usual channels. The bookstall keeper Harn remembers selling a volume matching the description but cannot say to whom — a failure of memory I believe is genuine, though whether it is his own or one he has been kindly permitted, I cannot determine.*

*Local sentiment bears noting. Thornfell forgets Vessany the way a riverbed forgets water — superficially, completely, and with visible reluctance whenever it rains. I have interviewed eleven residents under customs pretense. All were cooperative. None were truthful. This is not an accusation; it is a description of the town's collective genius.*

He dipped the pen again, hesitated, and then added the part he'd been circling for two days:

*A note on method. The Regency's position is that Vessany never existed, and that texts suggesting otherwise are forgeries or seditions. I have held this position myself and defended it in writing. However. I have now examined four documents in this district whose paper, ink, and binding predate the Annexation beyond reasonable forgery. One does not forge a century backward without leaving seams, and these documents have none. I do not yet know what this means. I report it because accuracy is the whole of my value to the Bureau, and because a man who reports only what pleases his masters is a weapon, and weapons are used by whoever holds them.*

*I request guidance on one point specifically: if the text is recovered intact, is it to be destroyed or archived? The standing instruction says destroyed. The standing instruction has said destroyed for forty years, regarding an entire country's paper trail. At some point, someone should ask what we are so afraid of reading.*

He signed it, sealed it, and set it aside for the courier, feeling the particular unease of a man who has just put his head slightly above a parapet to see whether anyone shoots.

Then, because he was Serel Dane and thoroughness was both his virtue and his prison, he opened his case notes and added a private annotation, in the smaller hand he reserved for things he didn't want to reread:

*The bone-carver, Voss. Interviewed twice. Lies beautifully — the best liar I've met in nine years of inquiry, and I include my superiors. But here is what troubles me: she lied about the bookstall before I mentioned the bookstall. She lied about the date before I gave the date. Which means either she has the text, or she knows something that functions identically.*

*Second point, which I record reluctantly: her hands. The oracle fragment describes "the maker of names." Voss carves names for a living. Coincidence is possible. I have built a career on coincidences being possible.*

*Third point, which I record with real reluctance: when I showed her the signet, she looked at it the way people look at a face they half-remember from a funeral. Recognition, or grief. I genuinely could not tell which, and I have rarely been unable to tell which.*

He closed the notebook. Outside his window, Thornfell glittered under its coat of black ice, beautiful and treacherous, and somewhere in it a woman was presumably lying to someone with great skill.

He found, to his considerable annoyance, that he hoped she was lying well enough to stay free.

* * *

The dead in Astrid's walls had opinions about the ring.

"It holds," whispered the drowned ferryman, who had taken up semi-permanent residence near the chimney since his burial, apparently finding her shop more interesting than the river. "Cracked things hold. Ask me how I know."

"I'd rather not," Astrid said, fitting a lid onto a finished memorial urn. "You're a bad example."

"I'm an excellent example. I held together right up until I didn't." A pause, watery and amused. "You counted the moons."

It wasn't a question. The dead always knew what she counted; counting was half her trade.

"Twelve left," she said. "And every time I dodge one little doom, it comes around sideways. Sour the milk — it freezes. Clear the step — a goat arrives. I'm starting to think the prophecy isn't a prediction at all. It's a bill. And it intends to collect."

"Mm." The ferryman's voice drifted closer, thoughtful. "When I ferried the living, there was a man who rowed upstream every day of his life. Strong fellow. Rowed against the current so long he believed the river ran backward. One day he stopped rowing, and the river took him home so fast he screamed the whole way." A ripple of something like laughter. "Don't be him, carver. Fighting the current and calling it swimming — that's how folk drown in calm water."

"And what would you suggest? Drifting?"

"I'd suggest learning what the current *is* before you fight it. You've been dodging pebbles. The prophecy hasn't even shown you the waterfall yet."

Astrid set down the urn. "There's a waterfall?"

"There's always a waterfall." The voice was fading now, thinning toward the chimney draft. "Door out of Vessany, carver. Crown that turns. You've read the words a hundred times. Have you asked yourself the only question that matters?"

"Which is?"

"What's on the *other side* of the door — and why does a country full of patient dead need a living heir to open it?" A last eddy of cold air stirred the shavings on her floor. "Ask the chest. Ask the letters. Ask the name the ferryman wouldn't say."

"I'm asking *you*, and you won't say it either!"

The silence that followed had a smile in it. Dead men were insufferable. They had all the time in the world and none of the answers, and they enjoyed both facts enormously.

* * *

That night Astrid climbed to her attic and unrolled her grandmother's map of Vessany for the hundredth time, and for the hundredth time failed to make sense of its borders, because the borders moved. Not magically — she'd checked, with candles and fixed reference points and the grim patience of a woman testing for hauntings — but cartographically. Rivers bent differently depending on which village the mapmaker had loved. Roads appeared where roads had been wished for. It was less a map than an argument, conducted lovingly, over forty years, about what a country ought to be.

In the margin, in her grandmother's hand, a single line she'd read a dozen times without hearing:

*The moon keeps thirteen. The maps keep forgetting. Only the names remember.*

Astrid sat back on her heels.

Only the names remember.

She was a maker of names. She had carved eleven thousand of them into bone and slate and wood — every name a small anchor thrown into time, a way of saying *you were here, you mattered, you are not erased*. It had never been a grand calling. It had been a trade, quiet and necessary, the work of a woman determined to remain invisible.

And here was a country that had been erased — borders dissolved, language outlawed, maps redrawn, even its name struck from the ledgers — surviving, if the whispers in her walls were true, entirely in names. Names carried by exiles. Names whispered by the dead. Names waiting, perhaps, in a locked chest, in a prophecy book, in the muscle-memory of her own two hands.

The moon would turn twelve more times. Fate would keep finding the low places, and she would keep mending what broke, gluing herself along new seams, letting the light come through differently.

But she was done playing defense.

Astrid rolled up the map, took the lamp downstairs, and stood before her grandmother's chest — the one that hummed when she passed, the one that had given up regalia and letters and a bloodline she'd never asked for. There were drawers in it she had not opened. Compartments she had not dared. A false bottom she had only suspected.

Whatever the prophecy wanted from her, it would have to catch her moving forward.

"Well," she said to the chest, and to the listening dead, and to the moon hanging white and patient over Thornfell like an eye that had already seen the ending. "Let's see what else you're hiding."

Her hand found the hidden seam.

The chest hummed — louder than it ever had, a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, like ten thousand voices inhaling at once — and somewhere far off in the mountains, in roads that no longer existed, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
