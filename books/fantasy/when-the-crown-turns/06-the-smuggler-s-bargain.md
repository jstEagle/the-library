## 6. The Smuggler's Bargain

Yeva Kroll smelled the girl before she heard her.

Not in any romantic way—smuggling had cured Yeva of romance around her fortieth year—but in the way you learn to smell everything when your eyes stop doing their share of the work. Tallow. Bone dust. River mud dried on boot leather. And underneath it all, threaded through it like wire through wax: fear. Fresh fear, about a week old, worn smooth from handling.

The girl had been standing outside the Salt Cellar for two nights now.

"Third time she's circled," muttered Doss, who worked Yeva's counter and possessed all of Yeva's caution and none of her patience. "You want I should scare her off?"

"No."

"You want I should let her in?"

"No."

"You want me to do anything?"

"I want you to stop asking questions while my soup is still hot." Yeva blew across the bowl and listened. The girl had stopped circling. She was standing at the mouth of the alley that led down to the cellar door, breathing like someone rehearsing a speech.

Yeva had been blind for thirty years, which meant she'd been blind longer than most people in Thornfell had been alive, which meant she'd earned the right to be smug about how much better she knew things than they did. She knew the girl was young. She knew the girl's hands were a carver's hands—the smell of bone dust got into the blood eventually, came out through the skin—and she knew the girl was carrying something wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, because nobody stood in an alley rehearsing a speech to their own boots over nothing.

She also knew, because Doss had told her with great relish, that an inquisitor had come to Thornfell ten days ago asking after old books.

Well, then.

* * *

The knock, when it finally came, was polite. That alone raised Yeva's opinion of her by a full measure. Smugglers got two kinds of knocks: the kind that broke the door and the kind that pretended to be friendly, and this was neither. This was a knock from someone who understood that the person on the other side of it might be more frightened than they were, and who had decided to account for that.

"Come in before the whole street hears your courage," Yeva called.

Footsteps down the stairs. Careful ones, counting them. Good. A person who counted stairs was a person who planned to leave the same way.

"You're Yeva Kroll," said the girl.

"And you're loud. Sit. There's tea if you pour it yourself; I've broken three cups this month and Doss has started keeping score." Yeva settled back in her chair, hands folded over her stick, and turned her face toward the sound of the girl—Astrid, she'd learn later, though at the time the girl gave no name, which was its own sort of courtesy. "You've been pacing outside my establishment for two nights like a cat deciding whether the dog is asleep. Say what you want."

A pause. Then, carefully: "I need passage. Into the mountains. The high passes, north of the reservoir."

"Nobody goes into those passes."

"That's rather the point."

"Mm." Yeva sipped her soup instead of smiling. "And what's up there that's worth dying for?"

Another pause, longer. Yeva heard the girl's fingers moving against something in her pocket—bone, small pieces, clicking softly. A nervous habit. Or a decision being made one click at a time.

"A country," Astrid said at last. "Or what's left of one."

The word landed in the room and sat there. Vessany. Nobody in Thornfell said it out loud. Forty years since the Regency had struck it from every map and record and gravestone, forty years since the reservoir waters had risen over the capital, and the name had become the kind of thing you only said the way you said curses—in private, quietly, looking over your shoulder.

Yeva set down her spoon.

"Three bones," she said. "That's my price. Three carved bones, your choosing, laid on my table before we go. Not money. Money can be traced, and I don't buy what can be traced."

"That's—that's it? You're not going to ask me why?"

"I just did. You answered." Yeva tilted her head. "Would it help if I asked again and looked skeptical?"

"I—no. Yes. I mean—" The girl made a sound halfway between a laugh and a hiccup, and Yeva revised her estimate upward. Fear like hers, that could still crack into laughter, was good material. It held weight. "Everyone keeps telling me not to trust you."

"Everyone is correct. But everyone also tells you not to swim in the river, and you'll notice the ferryman drowned anyway. The world is full of bad advice delivered confidently." Yeva reached out and tapped the table twice. "Three bones, girl. And one warning, free of charge, because I'm feeling generous and because you'll need it more than the bones."

"What warning?"

"The mountains remember." Yeva let the silence stretch, because a warning delivered quickly was a warning half-delivered. "Whatever they told you about the high roads—whatever maps, whatever stories—they're wrong in the way that matters. Vessany wasn't just a country with roads through those peaks. It was a country the mountains ate. Every pass, every bridge, every shrine—the stone took them when the Regency flooded the valleys, and stone keeps what it takes. You'll walk roads that aren't there anymore except to the dead, and the dead will walk them beside you, and some of them will be pleased to see you and some of them will ask you things you won't know how to answer." She leaned forward. "Can you carry that?"

The girl didn't answer right away. Yeva heard her turn something over—not the bones this time, but the question itself, testing it for cracks the way a carver tests stock.

"I've spent my whole life listening to the dead," Astrid said finally. "It's the living I find difficult."

Something in Yeva's chest, an old rusted hinge, creaked.

"Well," she said gruffly. "That's either very brave or very sad, and knowing my luck it's both. Pour yourself that tea. We leave in two days."

* * *

They left at moonrise on the second night, which was later than Yeva would have liked but earlier than she'd feared, because Astrid arrived early and then hovered awkwardly by the stable door while Yeva finished packing, making helpful comments about rope coils that were, in fact, correct.

"You've done this before," Yeva observed, cinching the last strap.

"Done what?"

"Packed like someone running. There's a technique to it. Amateurs bring too much of what they love and not enough of what they need."

"My grandmother taught me." A beat. "Most of what I'm carrying is hers."

Yeva filed that away with the other oddments the girl kept dropping—grandmother, bone-carving, a voice that went careful and flat whenever anyone mentioned the inquisitor. Thirty years of blindness taught you to keep a shelf for such things and wait until the shelf was full enough to read the label.

Doss saw them to the edge of town, complaining the entire way, which was his version of affection. "If she gets you killed," he told Astrid, "I get first pick of your boots. She'll tell you she doesn't care about anything, but she cried for a week when the mule died, so calibrate accordingly."

"Doss."

"Telling her. For calibration."

"He's lying," Yeva said. "It was four days."

The road out of Thornfell climbed along the river, and Yeva felt the town fall away behind them the way she always did—a thinning of smells, woodsmoke giving way to pine, the particular hush of open water replaced by the chattering arrogance of mountain streams. She'd walked this road perhaps two hundred times. She knew it the way you know a scar.

What she hadn't expected was how the girl walked.

Astrid moved quiet as spilled flour, which was smuggler training nobody had given her, and she kept pace without complaint, and when Yeva stumbled once on wet root—only once, and she'd deny it forever—the girl's hand was there under her elbow before the stick even slipped.

"You felt that coming," Yeva said.

"The root. It was sticking out funny."

"Hm." Yeva resumed walking. "You watch with your ears too."

"I watch with everything. It's exhausting."

"It is," Yeva agreed, with feeling. "Nobody tells you that. They make blindness sound like a tragedy and sightedness sound like a picnic, and the truth is everybody's straining at something all the time, and the wise ones admit it." She paused at the fork where the smuggler path split from the shepherd track and put her hand on the cold stone marker. "Left here. And girl—Astrid, isn't it? Don't bother denying it, Doss asked at the chandlery, he has no subtlety—when we cross into the high passes tomorrow, there's a thing I have to show you, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from the rocks."

"Hear it from the rocks?"

"You'll see." Yeva found the path with her stick. "It's why my price is bones and not coin. You'll understand that too, eventually, and you'll either forgive me or you won't, and I've learned to live with both."

"That's a deeply unsettling sentence."

"Thank you. I practice."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of an old toll-house, or what had been a toll-house when there had been a country to collect tolls for. Astrid built the fire small and shielded, another thing nobody had taught her, and Yeva ate her bread and listened to the girl unwrap the oilcloth package she'd carried against her spine the whole way.

"What are those?" Yeva asked, though she'd known since the alley.

"Memorial bones. Names of the dead." A soft clatter as Astrid laid them out, presumably between herself and the fire. "My trade. I carve names into bone so the dead have somewhere to rest their voices. It sounds mad when I say it out loud."

"It doesn't."

Astrid went quiet in a different way. "No?"

"I grew up in a country that erased its dead on purpose," Yeva said. "Forty years ago they took every name off every stone in Vessany and drowned the stones besides. You think carving names into bone is mad? Girl, it's the sanest thing anyone's doing in this whole gray corner of the world." She tore her bread and chewed. "Why these three?"

"They were the ferryman's crew. The ones who drowned with him last month." Another hesitation, the kind Yeva was learning preceded the girl's truest sentences. "One of them whispered something to me. About a door. About a thirteenth moon." The fire popped. "About a name he wouldn't say."

Yeva kept her face still, which was easy, since her face had retired from active duty decades ago.

"And you thought," she said slowly, "that the answer to a ghost's riddle was up a mountain that eats countries."

"When you say it like that—"

"Good. Keep saying it like that. Anyone who can't laugh at their own doom hasn't accepted it yet, and acceptance is the difference between arriving and merely traveling." Yeva lay back against her pack and folded her hands on her stick. "Sleep. Tomorrow the road gets strange."

"How strange?"

"Do you hear that?" Yeva lifted one finger.

Astrid listened. Wind in the pines. Stream-water below. Ordinary night sounds.

Then, underneath—faint, rhythmic, patient as dripping stone—the unmistakable cadence of marching feet. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Coming up the pass from the drowned valley, keeping perfect step, going nowhere.

Yeva smiled thinly at the girl's sharp intake of breath.

"The mountains remember," she said. "Told you. Now sleep. They won't trouble us tonight—they never do. They're just practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Coming home," said Yeva Kroll, and closed her eyes.
