## 7. Roads That No Longer Exist

The pass began where the map ended.

Not metaphorically—Yeva had actually stopped at a certain point on the trail, unfolded her oilcloth map of the Regency's borderlands, and run her thumb along the blank space above the last surveyed ridge line. "Here," she said, "the cartographers gave up. Which is cartographer for *here be the interesting part*."

"You're blind," Astrid said. "How do you know what's on your own map?"

"I drew it. Before." Yeva folded the map away with the brisk finality of a woman closing a door on a room she didn't wish to discuss. "I could draw it from memory if I were dead, too. Probably better. Dead men have excellent recall and no appointments."

Astrid had learned, over three days of walking, that Yeva Kroll's blindness was less an absence than a different kind of sight—one that worked sideways, through sound and stone and the particular way air moved when it had somewhere to be. She navigated the high passes better than any sighted guide Astrid had ever heard of, which was fortunate, because the passes themselves seemed determined to be unheard of.

The road they followed was not on any map because, officially, it did not exist. The Regency had spent forty years unmaking Vessany: burning records, renaming rivers, resettling villages, and—most thoroughly—erasing the roads. Roads, Yeva explained, were how countries remembered themselves. A road was a promise that two places belonged to each other. Destroy the roads and you destroyed the belonging.

"But stone is stubborn," she said, tapping her boot against the trail. "They pulled up the paving stones and sold them for walls and wells. They burned the waymarker posts. They renamed every bridge after some Regency minister with a face like a wet Tuesday. But the ground remembers the weight of feet. And the dead—" she smiled thinly, "—the dead remember everything. They have nothing else to do."

Astrid felt it before she understood it. She had spent her whole life attuned to the dead the way other people are attuned to weather—a pressure behind the ears, a taste like old coins. Since Thornfell, since the chest, since the whispers of her true name had begun following her through her sleep, that sense had sharpened until it was almost painful. And here, on this slope of scree and dwarf pine, the pressure was enormous. Not one ghost. Not dozens.

Thousands.

"They're here," she said quietly.

"Of course they're here." Yeva adjusted the strap of her pack. "This was the King's Road once. From the capital to the northern garrisons. Soldiers marched it for three hundred years. Couriers. Pilgrims. Brides going north to marry men they'd never met." She said the last part lightly, but something in her voice snagged on it. "You'll want to walk in the middle of the road, girl. Not the edges. The edges are where the ditches are, and the ditches are where the ones who died badly still are."

Astrid moved to the middle of the road.

* * *

By midday she could see them.

It began as a shimmer at the corner of her vision—the way heat shimmers above summer stones, except this was cold-shimmer, winter-light, the visual equivalent of a word on the tip of the tongue. Then, as they climbed into the cloud layer and the world contracted to twenty paces of gray, the shimmer resolved.

Soldiers.

They marched beside the party—not toward them, not around them, but *with* them, in loose formation, keeping pace with an ease that suggested they had been walking this road so long the walking had become their shape. Their uniforms were Vessani: the long coats with the collar-tabs shaped like mountain peaks, the colors faded to the gray-blue of deep ice. Their faces were the worst part. Not gaunt, not screaming, not any of the things faces are supposed to be in stories about ghosts. Just tired. Patiently, endlessly tired, the way a field looks after harvest.

One of them turned his head as Astrid passed.

He looked at her the way you look at a stranger who reminds you of someone—and then, without any decision she could see, he straightened, brought his heels together, and saluted.

Astrid stopped walking.

"What," she said, to no one, to everyone.

"Keep moving," Yeva said. "If you stop, they stop, and then we're all standing in a cloud getting rained on by our own history."

"He saluted me."

"Mm." Yeva's hand found Astrid's elbow and steered her forward. "They do that."

"Why? They don't know me. They can't know me. I'm just—" *a bone-carver from Thornfell, nobody, invisible, that was the whole point* — "I'm no one."

"You're a woman walking the King's Road wearing a chest-key carved from royal blood around your neck, in the company of the court cartographer, heading toward the drowned palace." Yeva said it dryly, but her grip on Astrid's arm was gentle. "To them, that adds up to something. They don't know what. They've forgotten most things—names first, usually, poor devils—but the body remembers its orders. Salute the standard. Follow the crown. And something about you smells like crown to them."

Astrid touched the key through her shirt. It was warm. It was always warm now, which she tried not to think about, mostly unsuccessfully.

More soldiers appeared as they climbed. Whole companies, materializing out of the cloud the way fish surface in a still pond. Some marched; some stood at rest along the verges, hands folded on rifle-stocks that had long ago rotted to nothing in their ghostly grips. One sat on a boulder mending a boot that did not need mending, his needle flashing in and out of leather that wasn't there, and he glanced up at Astrid with the mild incurious look of a man at his work, then went back to it.

"They don't speak?" she asked.

"Oh, they speak." Yeva's mouth twisted. "But not to the living, usually. Not words you'd want. Mostly they talk to each other. You'll hear it if the wind turns right. It sounds like—" she paused, hunting for it, "—like a market day heard through a wall. All that life, all that noise, and none of it for you."

The wind turned right, an hour later, and Astrid heard it.

Chatter. Laughter, even—thin and distant, laughter with forty years of dust on it. Someone complaining about the boots. Someone singing, four bars of a song over and over, never reaching the fifth. Someone saying a name, a woman's name, *Maretta, Maretta*, in the tone of a man rehearsing an apology he would never get to make.

Astrid walked faster.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation—a Vessani rest-house for couriers, its roof fallen in, its hearth still somehow drawing. Yeva built a fire with the efficiency of someone who had done it ten thousand times, and Astrid set out the food: hard bread, smoked fish, the bitter alpine tea Yeva carried in a tin that had once, judging by the crest scratched into its bottom, belonged to a colonel.

"I stole this from a colonel," Yeva said, catching Astrid looking. "He was dead at the time. I feel that makes it charitable rather than theft. He'd clearly finished with it."

"Do you ever miss it?" Astrid asked. "Before. The court."

Yeva stirred the fire. For a while Astrid thought she wouldn't answer.

"I miss the maps," she said finally. "Drawing a country is a kind of loving it. Every river bend, every mountain's shoulder—you learn a place so intimately that when they take it away, it's not grief exactly. It's phantom limb. I still reach for Vessany sometimes. Reach out my hand to trace a border that isn't there anymore." She tapped her closed eyes. "Fitting punishment, really. They took the country; heaven took the consolation prize."

"That's not funny."

"No," Yeva agreed. "But if I don't say it like a joke, I'll say it like a scream, and screams frighten the ghosts." She sipped her tea. "Speaking of which. We have company."

"I know. I've known all evening." Astrid had been aware of them since sundown: a ring of soldiers around the campsite, standing watch. Standing *guard*, her mind corrected, and she didn't know why the correction mattered, only that it did. "Why are they doing that?"

"Old habit. Waystations were guarded at night. Courier posts especially." Yeva tilted her head, listening to something Astrid couldn't hear—or could, faintly, a low murmur like wind through wheat. "Or perhaps they've decided you're worth guarding. Careful, girl. Being important to the dead is a heavy honor. They give it the way drowning men give you their trust. With both hands, and expecting you to hold them up forever."

Astrid looked out into the dark beyond the firelight. She couldn't see them now, but she could feel them, a constellation of pressures arranged in a careful circle. And at the edge of hearing, that murmuring again—and threaded through it, unmistakable, a word repeated softly, reverently, passed from mouth to mouth around the ring like a candle being handed along:

*Astrid. Astrid. Astrid.*

Her own name. Not her true name, not the buried royal one from the letters in her grandmother's chest—just *Astrid*, plain as bread. They knew it anyway. Or they knew enough of it.

She pulled her blanket to her chin and did not sleep so much as hover near it, and all night the sentries held their watch, and once, near the coldest hour, she heard one of them begin to hum. It was a lullaby. She recognized it from her grandmother, from a kitchen in Thornfell, from a childhood she had thought was ordinary—and she lay very still and let a dead man sing her to sleep.

* * *

Two days higher, the snow came, and with the snow, trouble.

Yeva heard it first: dogs. Far below and behind, on the lower trail, the specific music of Regency hounds—bred in kennels outside the capital, trained to track by scent and by something worse. "Ward-hounds," Yeva spat. "They smell magic the way other dogs smell fear. And you, my dear, have been leaking royalty into the landscape since Thornfell."

"How far?"

"A day. Maybe less, if their handler is competent. He is. I can tell by the spacing of the whistles." She was already breaking camp, her hands moving fast and sure. "Serel Dane doesn't whistle twice unless he's found sign. He whistles twice when he's pleased. I hate a man who's pleased."

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him. Everyone in the trade knows of Serel Dane. The inquisitor who actually reads the books he confiscates." She snorted. "Dangerous quality, in a Regency man. Curiosity. It's how they get you—they promote the curious ones precisely because curiosity is a leash."

They climbed. The trail narrowed to a ledge above a drop that the cloud politely hid, and the dead soldiers kept pace alongside, and Astrid noticed something strange: where the living path crumbled or vanished entirely—where forty years of erasure had chewed the road down to bare assertion—the ghosts walked on solid footing. They stepped across gaps in the ledge as though crossing bridges that weren't there. At one point the trail simply ended, a landslide having eaten thirty yards of mountainside, and Yeva stopped, faced the void, and said:

"The road goes on. Trust the road."

"There is no road."

"There is no *visible* road. There is a difference, and the difference is currently holding up several hundred tons of dead soldiers' conviction." She held out her hand. "Take my arm. Step where they step. The stones remember being a road even if the stonemasons are forgotten. Put your foot where a thousand years of feet have put theirs, and the mountain will make an exception for you."

It was the maddest thing Astrid had ever done, and she had once carved a memorial for a man who wasn't dead yet because he'd paid in advance. She stepped out over the void, onto nothing, onto the memory of paving stones—and her boot found purchase. Not stone, exactly. Something between stone and certainty. She crossed thirty yards of impossible ground with her heart in her ears, and on the far side the trail resumed, real and gritty underfoot, and she laughed—a short, shocked bark of laughter that echoed off the cloud.

"It held!"

"Of course it held." Yeva stepped across after her, serene as a woman crossing her own parlor. "Countries are stubborn things, Astrid Voss. The Regency has been trying to kill this one for forty years and it still holds up strangers in the mountains. That's not nothing. That's not nothing at all."

Behind them, faint through the snow, a whistle sounded. Twice.

And then—Astrid froze—a third time, and a fourth, a signal pattern she didn't know but Yeva clearly did, because the old smuggler's face went grim as slate.

"That's not Dane's pattern," she said. "That's the *regimental* pattern. He's called for support. Mounted troops out of Fort Marren." She stood listening, head cocked, snow gathering on her shoulders like a second, whiter cloak. "Girl, we have until tomorrow's dusk. Maybe. After that there will be too many of them for even these mountains to be rude to."

Astrid looked back down the trail, into the white murk where her hunters climbed. Then forward, up, where the pass crested somewhere unseen—and where, she realized suddenly, the ghosts had changed. The soldiers marching beside them had altered course, all of them, gently, imperceptibly, the way a stream bends around a stone. They were no longer following the King's Road.

They were converging. From every direction, down side-paths and goat-tracks and routes that existed only in memory, the dead of Vessany were funneling toward a single point ahead—a valley, Yeva said, where the smoke of woodsmoke rose thin and human through the snow.

Smoke meant fires. Fires meant people. Living people, in the high passes where no living people were supposed to be.

"Why are they all going there?" Astrid whispered.

Yeva smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"Because," she said, "there's a village ahead that keeps a sleeping queen. And I think, Astrid Voss, that the road has decided you've slept long enough."

* * *

They reached the valley mouth at dusk, snow thickening, the hounds' music lost now beneath the wind—or paused, which was worse. The ghosts had gathered ahead of them in their hundreds, rank upon rank of faded blue-gray, standing in the falling snow with their heads bowed, and Astrid walked through their midst feeling like a needle drawn through the eye of everything her country had ever lost.

At the valley floor, lights. Windows. A church bell that rang in a rhythm Astrid's blood recognized before her mind did—an old Vessani tolling, the one for *a homecoming*.

The elders were already coming out to meet her.
