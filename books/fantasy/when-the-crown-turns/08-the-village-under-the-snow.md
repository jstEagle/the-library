## 8. The Village Under the Snow

The snow began an hour before they saw the smoke.

It came down soft and sideways off the pass, the kind of snow that erases rather than covers, and Yeva stopped at the head of the trail with her face tilted into it like a woman listening to a letter being read aloud.

"Smoke," she said. "Two fires, maybe three. Bread, if my nose hasn't rotted along with everything else."

"You can smell bread through falling snow?" Astrid asked.

"I can smell bread through forty years of forgetting," Yeva said. "Snow is nothing." She turned her blind eyes toward Astrid, and there was something in them—something careful, almost apologetic—that Astrid had learned to distrust over the past week. "There's a village in this valley. You won't find it on any map."

"Nothing we're looking for is on any map," said Tam, one of Yeva's runners, a boy of perhaps seventeen who had not yet learned that smugglers were supposed to be taciturn. He was shivering so hard his teeth clicked between words. "Can we *please* go toward the bread?"

"We can," Yeva said slowly, "but I want it understood that once we do, some things stop being undoable." She said it to the snow as much as to them. "Villages like this one survive by never being found. We are about to be found by them instead. That's how it works. They'll have seen us since the pass."

Astrid looked back up the trail—the way they'd come, the way the hunters had been coming for three days now, always a half-day behind, patient as winter itself—and then down into the valley, where the smoke rose thin and gray against the white.

"Then let's not keep them waiting," she said.

* * *

The village did not look like much. That was, Astrid would later understand, entirely the point.

A dozen houses huddled along the valley floor under drifts of snow, their roofs steep and dark-timbered, their windows glowing amber. Smoke curled from stone chimneys. A goat regarded them from a pen with the flat disinterest of all goats everywhere. There was a well, a woodshed, a shrine of some kind—a small stone alcove beside the path where someone had left a bowl of milk and a sprig of pine—and nothing else. No banners. No markers. A village that could have belonged to anywhere, which was exactly what a village like this one needed to look like.

Except for the language.

They heard it before they reached the first house: two women talking in a doorway, voices carrying easily across the cold air, and the words were wrong. Not the Regency tongue Astrid had spoken all her life, not the clipped border dialect of Thornfell, but something older, rounder, with vowels that opened like doors. Astrid's grandmother had hummed in that language sometimes, when she thought no one was listening—little lullabies with no meaning Astrid had ever known, just sound and warmth.

She knew the sound of it the way you know your own heartbeat: without ever having learned it.

"Vessani," she whispered.

"Old Vessani," Yeva corrected quietly. "The court dialect died with the capital. This is what it sounded like before the palaces got hold of it." Her mouth did something complicated. "My mother spoke it. I was made to forget it. They took the maps first and the language second and the eyes last, and they called it mercy each time."

The women in the doorway had gone silent. One of them was staring—not at Yeva, not at Tam or the other runner, but at Astrid, with an expression Astrid could not read and did not like, because it was too much like recognition.

"Say nothing," Yeva murmured. "Let me do the talking. I'll buy us a barn and a night, and by morning—"

But the older of the two women had already stepped out into the snow. She was small and broad and wrapped in wool the color of undyed flax, and she walked straight up to Astrid as if Astrid were the only person standing in the valley, and she said something in that round old tongue, softly, like a question.

"I'm sorry," Astrid began, "we only need shelter until—"

The woman reached out and pushed back the hood of Astrid's cloak.

The cold hit her ears, and then the silence hit her harder.

The woman was looking at the left side of Astrid's neck, just below the jaw, where a birthmark sat like a thumbprint of wine-colored ink—a mark Astrid had spent her whole life hiding under scarves and collars and later, deliberately, under the habit of being unremarkable. Her grandmother used to touch it when Astrid was small, tracing its edge with one cracked fingernail, saying nothing, wearing an expression Astrid had never understood until very recently and wished she still didn't.

The woman turned. She said one word to the village—not loudly, not dramatically, just clearly, the way you'd say *fire* or *flood*—and the word went through the street like wind through wheat.

"*Sovna.*"

"What does that mean?" Astrid asked Yeva, though some traitorous part of her already knew, the way you know the shape of a fall before it happens.

"It means 'the sleeping one,'" Yeva said. "It's what they call the queen who was never crowned. The heir who vanished when Vessany fell. There were stories." Her voice was very even, the way it got when she was holding something down with both hands. "Stories that the bloodline didn't end on the execution lists. That a child was carried north over the mountains. That she'd come back when the moon—" She stopped herself.

"When the moon finished its thirteenth turn," Astrid said flatly.

"You've been reading ahead in the prophecy again."

"I've been living ahead in it. It's different."

By now people were coming out of the houses. Not running—walking, unhurried, wrapping coats around themselves as they came, as if summoned by something more reliable than alarm. Old men. Young mothers with children on their hips. A blacksmith still holding his tongs. Perhaps thirty people gathering in the falling snow, and every single one of them looking at Astrid.

She had spent thirty-one years perfecting invisibility. She had learned to stand in doorways, to speak at the volume of furniture, to carve names for the dead because the dead didn't stare. And now thirty strangers stood in a circle around her in a valley that did not exist, and every face was turned to her like a flower to a bad sun, and the feeling that rose in her chest was not the belonging she had dreamed of her whole life.

It was terror. Pure, clean, animal terror, with a terrible sweetness underneath it, like honey poured over a knife.

"They're going to kneel," Yeva said quietly. "When they're sure. They're going to kneel, and after that there is no barn and no quiet night and no morning where we slip away. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand," Astrid said. "I wish I didn't."

The old woman—the first one, the one who had pulled back her hood—was studying her face now, searching it. Then she reached into her coat and drew out something small and held it up between them: a carved bone pendant, worn smooth by generations of thumbs, worked in a pattern Astrid knew better than her own signature. It was the maker's mark of the Voss workshop in Thornfell. Her family's mark. Her own mark, cut into every memorial bone she'd ever sold.

The woman said something long and trembling in Vessani, and though Astrid understood almost none of the words, she understood all of it anyway: *We waited. We kept the songs. Tell us it wasn't for nothing.*

And Astrid—who had lied beautifully to an inquisitor, who had lied to customs men and tax collectors and neighbors for three decades—found that she could not produce a single falsehood in this valley, in this snow, in front of these faces.

"I don't know what I am," she said, in the Regency tongue, and Tam translated it haltingly into the old language, his voice cracking on the grammar. "I carve names for the dead. That's all I've ever been. I didn't ask for—"

The old woman touched the birthmark with two fingers, gentle as touching a flame, and then she knelt.

Right down into the snow, knees folding without ceremony, hands pressed together, forehead bowed. And the others—thirty of them, fifty, more coming out of the houses, a whole village folding down like grass—knelt with her, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing Astrid had ever heard.

Tam leaned toward Yeva and whispered, far too audibly, "What do we do? Do *we* kneel?"

"If you kneel I will never forgive you," Yeva said. "Help me look inconspicuous."

"You're a blind woman standing in a kneeling village. Inconspicuous has left the valley."

Astrid stood alone in the center of it, snow settling on her shoulders, her heart hammering somewhere up around her teeth, and thought: *This is what I wanted.* All those years in Thornfell, carving other people's names, watching families gather and grieve and belong to each other while she went home alone to a shop full of the dead—she had wanted exactly this. To be seen. To be known. To walk into a room and have it recognize her.

She had simply never imagined it would feel like drowning in slow motion, with everyone smiling.

*Get up,* she wanted to say. *Please get up. I burn porridge. I talk to ghosts because living people frighten me. Three days ago I hid in a culvert from a man with a warrant and cried about it afterward. Whatever you think knelt down in the snow just now, she doesn't exist.*

But the old woman lifted her face, and her eyes were wet, and behind her the whole village waited with the patience of people who had waited forty years already and knew how to do it, and Astrid understood that whatever she said next would be remembered longer than anything else she ever said, including, possibly, the things she hadn't said yet.

So she did the only honest thing available to her. She knelt too—down into the snow in front of the old woman, eye to eye, which made several villagers gasp and made Yeva mutter something unrepeatable—and she took the old woman's hands in hers.

"I don't know how to be what you waited for," she said, and let Tam translate, word by stumbling word. "But I know how to listen. So tell me everything. Start with the sleeping queen. Start at the beginning."

The old woman laughed then—a wet, broken, joyful sound—and said something that Tam translated with visible difficulty:

"She says: the beginning is a long story, and the bread is nearly done, and a queen should not hear it standing in the snow."

"I'm not a queen," Astrid said automatically.

"She says," Tam continued, "that's what makes it a long story."

* * *

The house they were brought to was the largest in the village, low-beamed and fire-warmed, and it smelled of bread and pine resin and, faintly, of tallow and old paper. The elders gathered inside—seven of them, six men and the old woman, whose name was Maret and who turned out to be the village's speaker, which seemed to mean equal parts mayor, priest, and grandmother to everyone.

They fed them first. Thick barley soup, dark bread, butter with salt crystals in it, and tea brewed from something piney that warmed Astrid from the sternum outward. Tam ate like a rescued animal. Even Yeva relaxed by degrees, her hands memorizing the bowl and spoon and cup, her shoulders descending from around her ears for the first time in days.

"The hunters," Maret said, through Tam's translation. "How close?"

"A half day," Astrid said. "Maybe less. They follow the prophecy book—they know where it points."

Maret exchanged a look with the eldest of the men, a stooped fellow with a beard like frost on wire. Something passed between them that Astrid couldn't parse, but it had the weight of a decision already half-made.

"And they will come here?" Maret asked.

"They'll come wherever I am."

"Then we have tonight," Maret said, "to tell you why that matters more than you know." She settled onto the bench across from Astrid, folded her hands, and became, visibly, a different creature—not a grandmother offering soup but a keeper of something, a woman who had carried a story for decades the way Astrid carried her carving knives: carefully, constantly, close to the body.

"There is a shrine above the village," Maret began. "You passed its foot on the road—the bowl of milk. Every dawn for forty years, someone has climbed to it and left an offering for the sleeping queen. My mother did it. Her mother started it, the winter the child was carried north. We do not know your name—we were never told it, only that a daughter of the line lived, and that the moon would finish its thirteenth turn, and that she would come home over the passes." Her voice caught, steadied. "Forty years, *sovna*. Forty years of milk and pine and songs in a dead tongue, sung to a mountain. And today you walked out of the snow with the mark on your throat, and my mother's prayers grew legs."

Astrid set down her spoon. Her hands were not quite steady.

"Maret. What if I told you I don't want to be prayed to? What if I told you I've spent my whole life making sure nobody looked at me twice, and today a village knelt in the snow, and I haven't stopped wanting to be sick since?"

The elders murmured. Maret only smiled, and it was a kind smile, but it had iron in it somewhere.

"I would say," Maret answered, "that you sound exactly like the stories say the old kings sounded, before the crown wore them smooth. And that is why the village prays to a *sleeping* queen, *sovna*. Not a waking one. Sleeping. Because a queen asleep cannot be turned." She leaned forward. "You have heard what the crown does?"

"I've seen it," Astrid said quietly. "In the prophecy. It turns whoever wears it into someone else."

"Then you understand our prayers better than we do. We never prayed for a ruler. We prayed for a remembrance." Maret's eyes glistened in the firelight. "The Regency erased our country from the maps. But they could not erase it from us. As long as one village sings the songs, Vessany breathes. As long as one child learns the words, it lives. You are not our throne, *sovna*. You are our proof."

Somewhere in Astrid's chest, something that had been clenched since Thornfell loosened by a single degree.

Proof. Not power. Not a throne. A memory with a pulse.

"That," she said, "is the first thing anyone has asked of me since the snow started that I might actually be able to give."

"Do not promise yet," Maret said, raising a hand. "Hear the rest first. Hear what the shrine holds. Hear what my grandmother buried beneath it the night the soldiers burned the capital, and why the village has guarded it for forty years without knowing what it was." She glanced at the window, where the snow fell thick and blue in the last light. "And hear it quickly. Because your hunters are a half day behind you, and there is a boy in this village who carries messages over the pass, and tomorrow he must run either to warn the other valleys—or to carry word of you to the lowlands, if you decide you are not what we waited for after all."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow kept falling, soft and endless, burying the paths they'd come by.

Yeva's hand found Astrid's wrist beneath the table and gripped it, once, hard.

"Well," Astrid said, to the room, to the elders, to the mountain, to the moon wheeling invisible somewhere above the clouds. "Tell me everything. And someone please pass the bread."
