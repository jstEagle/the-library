## 9. Serel's Doubt

The trouble with hunting a ghost, Serel Dane had decided somewhere around the fourth day in the mountains, was that the mountains were on its side.

He sat on a wet stone at the edge of the pass called the Shepherd's Tooth, eating a ration biscuit that tasted like a punishment for something he couldn't remember doing, and watched his three men fail to make camp. Corporal Vess was arguing with a tent peg. Private Osk was arguing with Corporal Vess about the tent peg. And young Merrit—Merrit who had joined the Inquisition because he liked the hats—was standing at the treeline with his head tilted like a dog hearing a whistle.

"Serel," Merrit said. "There's someone singing."

"There is not someone singing."

"There is," Merrit insisted. "Down in the gully. A woman. She's got a lovely voice, actually."

Serel put down the biscuit with the gravity of a man laying down a sword. "Merrit. We are pursuing a fugitive bone-carver through country that officially does not exist, guided by maps that officially do not exist, on the orders of a ministry that officially does not know we're here. If there is a woman singing in a gully in the middle of hostile wilderness, she is either a smuggler, a spy, or bait. Which of those three things would you like to go and meet?"

Merrit considered this with the seriousness it deserved, which was none. "Bait," he said finally, "because then there'd be something to catch."

It was, Serel reflected, almost impossible to argue with that kind of logic. It was also, unfortunately, correct. He stood, brushed the wet off his coat, and said, "Vess, Osk—with me. Merrit, you stay here and guard the biscuits."

"The important job," Merrit agreed solemnly.

* * *

The woman in the gully was not singing anymore by the time they reached her. She was sitting on a flat rock as though she'd been waiting for them, which, Serel would learn later, she had. She was perhaps nineteen, wiry, with wind-chapped cheeks and the unbothered expression of someone who had been caught before and found it overrated.

She had a satchel. The satchel was already open. Whatever had been inside it was already gone, buried somewhere in the scree by a girl who knew these hills better than the hills did.

"Good evening," Serel said.

"Is it?" she said. "I hadn't checked."

"Corporal, her papers."

"I don't have papers," the girl said helpfully. "Nobody up here has papers. Papers are a lowland thing. You'll notice the goats don't have any either."

Osk found her papers anyway—or rather, found the absence of them, and the absence of anything else, and reported this with the particular frustration of a man who had searched a great many pockets in his career and been defeated by all of them.

"What's your name?"

"Bryn."

"Your real name."

"That's the one I use most often."

Serel crouched down to her level. This was a technique he had learned early: never loom. Looming made people lie bigger. At eye level, people lied smaller, and small lies had edges you could grip.

"Bryn," he said. "Three nights ago, an old woman crossed the pass at Hollow Ford. Blind. Travelling with a party of five. One of them was a woman from Thornfell, a carver. You've carried messages for Yeva Kroll. Don't bother deciding whether to admit it—I can see you deciding, and I can also see that you're bad at it."

The girl—Bryn, if that was even a quarter of her name—looked at him for a long moment. Then she smiled, and it was not a friendly smile.

"You're the inquisitor," she said. "The one Yeva calls *the polite one*. She said if you caught me, I should tell you whatever I liked, because you'd write it all down anyway." She nodded at the notebook in his coat pocket. "You always carry it. You ask questions like you're going to fix something with the answers. It's very strange. Everyone else in your profession asks questions like they're going to break something."

Serel felt that land somewhere under his ribs, in the place where he kept the things he didn't examine. "What does Yeva Kroll say I'm going to break?"

"Nothing yet." Bryn shrugged. "But she says everyone breaks eventually. She says it's just a question of what you're holding when it happens."

* * *

They took her back to camp because they had nowhere else to take her, and because Serel had learned long ago that a prisoner was simply a source of information that required feeding. He gave her tea. He gave Merrit the job of watching her, which was a mistake, because within an hour Merrit was showing her card tricks and she was pretending not to be impressed, and by the second hour they were both laughing at something Serel decided he did not want to know about.

He interrogated her properly at dusk, by the fire, alone. Not because the techniques required privacy, but because he suspected the answers would.

"Where is Astrid Voss going?"

"I don't know."

"Bryn."

"I genuinely don't. That's the whole point of Yeva's roads—you don't get told where you're going until you've arrived, because people who don't know can't be made to talk. I carry sealed packets. I walk where I'm told. I sleep in places that smell like sheep." She wrapped her hands around the tin cup. "You want to know something funny? I've been running Yeva's lines since I was fourteen, and I have never once known what I was carrying. Tonight's the first time anyone's ever asked me a question I could actually answer."

"And what question is that?"

She looked at him over the rim of the cup. For a moment the flippancy went out of her face entirely, and what was underneath looked much older than nineteen.

"You asked where she's going," Bryn said. "But here's my question for you, inquisitor. When you find out—and you will, because you're thorough, Yeva says thoroughness is your one flaw besides the notebook—what are you going to do about it? Because I've heard things too. Down in the valley villages. And some of what I've heard isn't about her at all."

"Tell me."

"No," Bryn said pleasantly. "You haven't earned it. But I'll trade you. You answer one of mine first, honest, no notebook-face, and I'll give you something worth the walk."

Serel turned his cup slowly on his knee. Around them the fire cracked and the mountain breathed its enormous cold breath, and somewhere above, the moon was working through its turns toward a date written in a book he had been ordered to burn unread.

"Ask," he said.

"Why do you wear the signet?"

His hand went to his collar before he could stop it—to the ring on the chain beneath, the old silver signet stamped with the nine-pointed star of Vessany, the vanished country, the thing he had taken from his grandfather's effects and never shown anyone and never quite managed to throw away.

"It's evidence," he said. "Recovered material. I keep it catalogued."

"You wear it next to your skin."

"Catalogued close," Serel said, "in case of loss."

Bryn laughed—not cruelly, which was worse. Then she leaned forward, and her voice dropped, and the firelight made her look, briefly, like every messenger of bad news in every story he'd ever pretended not to believe.

"They're coming behind you," she said. "Not to help you. Behind you. Second Column, two hundred strong, out of Fort Marren, with writs signed by the Ministry of Interior Records. Do you know what their orders are?"

"Tell me."

"To raze the valley villages." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report weather. "All seven of them. Every house, every shrine, every grave-marker with a Vessani name on it. The people are to be relocated south and dispersed so they can't regroup. The records call it 'landscape normalization.'" She let that sit. "Two hundred soldiers, inquisitor, marching to erase the last living pieces of a country, and the only reason you weren't told is that you're the kind of man who writes everything down, and they didn't want it in your notebook."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He sat apart from the fire with his notebook on his knee and did not write in it, which for Serel Dane was a kind of screaming.

He thought about the word *normalization*. He turned it over the way he'd turn a suspect coin, looking for the seam, the false metal. There wasn't one. It was exactly what it sounded like: the language of clerks applied to the burning of homes. Somewhere in the Ministry of Interior Records there was a form for this. Someone had designed the form. Someone had approved the ink allowance.

He thought about his grandfather, who had died when Serel was nine, who had hummed songs in a language nobody translated, who had kept a signet ring in a drawer with his fishing line and never once explained it, and who had wept once—only once, silently, at a festival, when a band played something old and a stranger asked them to stop.

He thought about the villages. Seven of them. He had passed through two on the way up—the one with the terraced barley, the one where the innkeeper's wife had given his men extra bread and refused payment because, she said, "you'll be hungry again tomorrow, and so will I, and that's how it goes." He tried to picture that woman relocated south and dispersed. He tried to picture the bread. He found he could not picture the bread, and that failure frightened him more than anything else, because he had always been able to picture things. It was his gift. It was why he was good at his work—he could see the crime in the witness's words, see the body in the ledger's gaps.

He could see this. That was the problem. He could see it perfectly.

"Can't sleep either?"

Merrit dropped down beside him with the gracelessness of youth and offered him half a biscuit. Serel took it, mostly to have something to do with his hands.

"Merrit. Why did you join the Inquisition?"

Merrit chewed thoughtfully. "Honestly? The hat. And then—" he shrugged— "the lectures. First year, they teach you the Charter. 'The law exists so that no person may be erased.' That's the actual line. I wrote it on the inside of my hat so I'd remember." He tapped the brim. "Why'd you join?"

Serel looked at the dark where the valley villages were, invisible below the ridgeline, full of sleeping people who had given his men bread.

"I joined because someone erased my family's name," he said, "and I wanted to be the kind of man who stopped that from happening."

Merrit nodded slowly. "And how's that going?"

Serel laughed—a short, startled sound, surprised out of him. "Ask me again tomorrow."

* * *

In the morning he went to Bryn's rock, where she sat eating the breakfast his own quartermaster had provided her, looking entirely too comfortable for a prisoner.

"If I release you," Serel said, "you'll run straight to Yeva Kroll and tell her everything I know."

"Yes," Bryn agreed. "Immediately. Possibly before I finish leaving."

"And if I keep you prisoner, you'll escape within a day, because you've already picked my lock twice and only stayed because the tea is better than Yeva's."

"Also yes. Your tea is excellent. What is it?"

"Nettle and dried apple. My mother's recipe." He crouched to her level, eye to eye, no looming. "One more trade, Bryn. You gave me information. Here's mine, free, no notebook. The Second Column reaches Fort Marren in six days. After that, nothing I do matters—my reports, my objections, my little ring on its little chain. Six days. Tell Yeva that number. Tell her it's the only useful thing I've ever given anyone."

Bryn studied him for a long time. Then she did something unexpected: she reached out and touched the chain at his collar, gently, the way you'd touch a wound, and drew out the signet into the morning light. The nine-pointed star caught the sun.

"She knows," Bryn said quietly. "Yeva's known about you since Hollow Ford. Why do you think she sent me to sit in a gully and sing?" She tucked the ring back beneath his collar herself, careful as a nurse. "You weren't catching me, inquisitor. I was delivering you. To yourself." She stood, shouldered her empty satchel, and grinned suddenly, nineteen again. "The tea really is lovely. You should write that down."

Then she was gone up the scree like water running uphill, and Serel Dane sat alone on a wet stone with a notebook full of nothing, a warrant in his pack, and a crack running through him from crown to foundation.

Six days. He took out the notebook at last, and after a long moment, he did not write a report to the Ministry. He wrote a letter instead, addressed to no ministry at all, beginning: *To whoever will listen, and may there still be such a person—*

Far below, past the ridgelines and the terraced barley and the graves with their patient carved names, a river ran down out of the mountains toward a drowned city, carrying on its grey surface the reflection of a moon with one turn left to make.
