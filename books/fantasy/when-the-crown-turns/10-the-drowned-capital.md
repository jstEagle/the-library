## 10. The Drowned Capital

The reservoir announced itself long before they saw it.

Astrid felt it first as a change in the dead. For days the Vessani soldiers had marched beside them through the passes, patient as weather, saluting her at intervals with a solemnity that had stopped startling her and started, instead, to ache. But now, as the road bent down toward the valley's throat, the ghosts began to press closer—not crowding, exactly, but gathering, the way family gathers at a sickbed. Their whispers thickened until Yeva, walking ahead with her white eyes fixed on nothing, said quietly:

"They know where we are."

"Can you hear them?"

"I can hear *you* hearing them." Yeva's mouth twitched. "It's like traveling with a kettle that never quite boils. Come on. The last mile is the one the mountains argue about."

The trees thinned. The road widened into something older and grander than any smuggler track—a paved way lined with stones carved in spirals, each spiral worn smooth by centuries of feet. And then the forest simply ended, and there was the water.

It filled the valley like a held breath. Grey-green, still, enormous. Where mountains should have risen on the far side, only their shoulders showed above the surface, drowned hills wearing forests like drowned men wear coats.

"That's not a lake," Astrid said. Her voice came out strange, flattened.

"Lakes have names," Yeva said. "This has a number. Reservoir Four, the Regency calls it. Built thirty years ago to water their southern provinces." She turned her face toward the water with an expression Astrid couldn't read—grief worn so smooth it looked almost like calm. "They didn't just flood a valley, girl. They flooded a capital."

Astrid's hands went cold inside her gloves. "Old Vessany."

"What's left of it." Yeva dismounted and began unpacking the bundles from her mule with brisk, unsentimental movements. "The spires they left above waterline, out of laziness or spite, I've never decided which. The rest—" she nodded at the still water, "—is down there. Streets. Markets. The palace. Forty years of a country, pickled."

One of the soldier-ghosts stepped forward, right up to the waterline, and stood at attention. Then another. Then all of them, dozens now, ranks forming along the shore in perfect silence, facing the drowned city the way men face a grave.

"Oh," Astrid said softly. "Oh, they've been waiting here."

"They've been waiting everywhere," Yeva said. "But yes. Here most of all." She pressed three small objects into Astrid's palm: bone charms, each no longer than a finger, carved with spiraling channels and pierced through with holes. "Breathe through these when you're under. Don't ask me how they work; my grandmother made them and refused to explain, which I've always taken as proof that even she didn't fully know. One charm lasts about an hour. I have seven. After that, we're fish."

"And you're coming down with me?"

Yeva smiled, and for once it wasn't wry. "I drew these streets once, ink by ink, from memory, after they took my eyes. You think I'd miss seeing them again?" She tied a cord around her own neck with two charms strung on it. "Besides, someone has to hold your rope. Drowned cities eat people who wander off admiring things."

* * *

Going under was like walking into a memory that hadn't finished deciding what it remembered.

The first step swallowed Astrid to the knee, then the waist, and the cold was total, ancient, indifferent. She put the charm between her lips. It hummed against her teeth, and air came—not fresh, exactly, but *remembered*, tasting faintly of woodsmoke and bread and something floral she couldn't name. The water slid past her face without entering her mouth, parting around her breath like a curtain around a lamp.

Below the surface, the world changed its rules.

Light fell in long green columns from above, catching sediment that drifted like slow snow. And rising out of that snow: streets. Whole streets, cobbled and intact, running away into the green gloom. A fountain stood at the first crossing, its stone nymphs furred with silt, one arm raised forever in greeting. A bakery's sign hung crooked over a door, the painted pretzel still visible beneath forty years of soft grey film. In a window box, the skeleton of some stubborn flower had gone to lace.

And the dead were everywhere.

Not the soldiers alone—everyone. Market women drifting between stalls that no longer held anything. A man sitting on a bench outside a shuttered house, hands folded, waiting with the particular patience of someone who has waited so long that waiting has become his profession. Children chasing something Astrid couldn't see, their laughter arriving not as sound but as pressure against her ribs, bubbles of joy rising through water that had tried to drown their laughter and failed.

They all turned when she passed.

Not with hunger. Not with the grasping desperation of the restless dead she'd known in Thornfell, clawing at the edges of the living world. These dead turned the way a room turns toward a window opened in winter—toward light, toward warmth, toward something they'd stopped believing would come.

A woman detached herself from a doorway and drifted close. Up close, Astrid could see the fine grain of her, the way her edges shimmered like heat-haze. She reached out—and stopped, her hand hovering an inch from Astrid's sleeve, trembling.

"You're warm," the woman whispered. The words arrived inside Astrid's skull, in old Vessani dialect, and Astrid understood them the way she understood the ferryman's warnings—not learned, inherited. "You're *warm*, and you're walking our streets."

"I'm sorry," Astrid said, and the charm translated her breath into bubbles that somehow also became words. "I'm so sorry. We're trying to help you cross."

The woman's hand withdrew slowly. "Cross," she repeated, tasting the word. "Forty years we've been told to wait. Wait for the heir. Wait for the return. Wait, wait, wait." Her shimmering face tilted. "You have her hands."

"Whose?"

But the woman only pressed both her own ghost-hands over her heart and drifted backward, back into the doorway, back into the dark of her drowned house, whispering something Astrid couldn't catch—though the word *hands* recurred, and so did a name that made Astrid's chest tighten: *Vessa. Vessa, come home.*

"That's your name they're saying," Yeva murmured beside her. The blind smuggler moved through the water with uncanny ease, one hand trailing along walls she had never touched but clearly knew. "Or half of it. Vessany was named for Queen Vessa the First, who founded it. The dead get sentimental with their history."

"They keep saying I have her hands."

"You do." Yeva said it flatly, factually, the way she'd say *the pass is closed* or *the soldiers are behind us*. "I've been watching them since Thornfell. Same knuckles. Same long second finger. The royal line had hands like yours—it's why they were carvers, originally, before they were kings. The first Vessa carved the border-stones herself." She paused at a corner, head cocked, listening to some geography only she could hear. "Left here. The palace is four streets down. Try not to gawk at everything; we haven't got charms to waste."

Astrid gawked anyway. It seemed the least she could do.

She gawked at the amphitheater with its rows of seats still ranked and waiting, silt lying in them like snow that had fallen indoors. She gawked at the library, its doors burst outward, books floating in slow constellations through the flooded reading rooms, pages turning gently in currents no one could feel anymore. She gawked at a schoolhouse where small desks sat in rows and a slate still bore, ghost-faint beneath the grime, the beginning of an alphabet lesson: *A is for—*

*A is for Astrid,* whispered the dead children, delighted, circling her. *A is for us. A is for awake.*

"Don't encourage them," Yeva said, but her voice had gone rough.

It was on the third street that Astrid stopped being able to gawk and started being able to do nothing but mourn.

It was a residential lane, narrow, ordinary. Laundry lines still stretched between upper windows, rotted sheets hanging like flags of surrender. On a doorstep sat a pair of boots, side by side, placed neatly as if their owner had only stepped inside for a moment and meant to come right back out. There was a child's chalk drawing on the cobbles at the base of the steps—a sun, a stick-figure family, a lopsided house—preserved under the silt like a fossil. And on the door itself, scratched deep by some frantic hand in the final hours before the water came:

*WE ARE VESSANY. REMEMBER US.*

Astrid stood in front of that door for a long time. The green light shifted overhead. Somewhere far above, birds crossed the reservoir's surface, and their shadows flickered over the drowned street like the quick passage of years.

"I used to carve names," she said. Her voice sounded thin even to herself. "In Thornfell. Little bones, little letters. People paid me to remember their dead, and I thought that was a small trade. Honest work. Nothing more." She touched the scratched words on the door, and beneath her glove the wood was soft as skin. "But this—" Her throat closed. She breathed through the charm, steadied herself. "This is what happens when nobody remembers. This is a whole country that nobody was paid to carve."

"No," Yeva said quietly. "This is a whole country that somebody was *paid* to forget. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and it sits in a marble hall in Corvane signing orders." She put her hand on Astrid's shoulder—firm, real, alive. "Mourn it properly later. Right now, we walk. The palace is close, and the dead are getting louder, and loud dead draw living ears."

"The Regency patrols the water?"

"Reservoir Four has a garrison. Small, bored, badly paid—the Regency doesn't station its best near embarrassing places. But bored men with boats are still men with boats." Yeva's jaw tightened. "And lately they've been less bored. Someone in Corvane remembered this place exists. That's never good news for anyone."

* * *

They came to the palace by the processional way, because Yeva insisted that if you were going to arrive, you should arrive correctly, and because—as she admitted with something dangerously close to shyness—she wanted to see it done properly once, even blind, even drowned, even forty years too late.

The processional way was a bridge of white stone arcing over a canal, lined with statues of the old kings and queens. Silt had given them all the same grey dignity; their faces had softened into anonymity. Except the last statue, the newest, a woman with her hands extended palms-up in welcome, and though the features had eroded, the hands had not. Long fingers. Prominent knuckles. A second finger longer than the ring finger.

Astrid held her own hands up beside them, underwater, in the green light.

They matched.

The dead gathered along the bridge in silence, hundreds of them now, standing in ranks that had no order but had meaning. As Astrid walked, they bowed—not deeply, not theatrically, just the small inclination of the head that one gives to someone whose arrival matters. It went on and on, bow after bow, street after street, and Astrid found herself nodding back to each one because refusing felt worse than returning it, and by the hundredth bow her cheeks were wet inside her mask of water and she couldn't have said whether it was the reservoir leaking in or something else.

At the palace doors—tall as trees, bronze, crusted green—two sentinel-ghosts stood flanking the entrance. They had been on guard, Astrid realized, for forty years. No relief had ever come. No order had ever released them. They had stood at their post through three decades of dark water, and when Astrid approached, they came to attention with a crispness that broke her heart, and one of them spoke, his voice ringing through the flooded court like a bell through fog:

"She returns. Open the way."

The doors, which had been sealed since the drowning, groaned inward.

Beyond lay the great hall, and Astrid had expected ruin, and ruin was there—chandeliers collapsed into sculptures of rust, banners dissolved to threads, a throne on its dais furred grey with silt like a sleeping animal. But the dead had kept other things. Along the walls, in niches, they had arranged objects salvaged from the drowning: a teapot. A violin with no strings. A doll with one eye. A farmer's hat. Hundreds of small ordinary treasures, carried down through the water by hands that had nothing else left to carry, arranged with the care of people setting a table for guests who might someday come.

"This is what they did," Yeva whispered. She had stopped at the threshold, her blind face lifted, tears cutting clean lines through the silt on her cheeks. "All these years. While they waited. They curated. They remembered. Every object in this hall belonged to someone, and they know every name." She laughed, wet and broken. "I mapped this country twice, girl. Once with my eyes and once without. But they—they mapped it in *grief*. Object by object. Name by name."

Astrid walked the length of the hall slowly, and the dead made way for her, and at the foot of the dais she stopped, because there, on the bottom step, laid out with terrible neatness, was a single object apart from all the others.

A crown.

Even crusted with silt, even drowned, it seemed to drink the green light rather than reflect it. It was beautiful the way certain wounds are beautiful—too perfectly made, too deliberate, its spiraling points curling inward like the country's own carved border-motifs turned against themselves. The dead nearest it stood at a careful distance. None of them touched it. None of them looked directly at it, the way people avoid looking at a thing that has already taken something from them.

"That's it," Astrid said. Not a question.

"That's it," Yeva confirmed. "The Turning Crown. Last worn by King Aldric the Third, your great-great-grandfather, may he rest easier than he lived." Her voice dropped. "Don't touch it. Not yet. Not tonight. Whatever you decide to do with it, decide it in daylight, with sleep behind you and food in you. Crowns are like bargains—worst considered at midnight."

Astrid didn't touch it. But she stood before it a long moment, and the crown, or whatever slept inside the crown, seemed to stand before her too, patient as the dead, patient as the water, waiting to be chosen.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of reservoir, came a sound that did not belong to the drowned world: the rhythmic knock of oars against a hull. A patrol boat, crossing the surface, slow and methodical, dragging something behind it on a line.

Yeva went rigid. "That's not fishing gear," she breathed. "That's a sounding chain. They're measuring the depth over the palace." Her blind eyes, for once, seemed aimed at something very precisely. "Girl—we need to move. Tonight. Because if the Regency is taking soundings over the throne room—"

The oar-knocks stopped. Directly overhead. In the silence that followed, through the water, clear as a bell, came a voice from the boat above, distorted but unmistakable, giving an order:

"—flood it again. All of it. Nobody leaves the ruins alive."
