## 11. The Turning Crown

The dead did not rush her.

That was the thing Astrid had learned about them in the drowned streets, wading chest-deep through water the color of old pewter, breathing through the bone charm pressed between her teeth. The living rushed you. The living had errands, debts, opinions. The dead had nothing but time, and they spent it the way she spent it at her workbench: patiently, one small careful motion at a time.

They lined the flooded avenues of Old Vessany like lamplighters who had forgotten their lamps. A woman in a baker's apron stood at a doorway that was now mostly doorway-shaped memory, her hands still dusted with flour that forty years of water had never quite washed away. Two boys leaned over a canal railing that existed only as a suggestion, watching fish that were no longer there. An old man sat on a bench, and when Astrid passed, he lifted two fingers off his knee in the lazy salute one gives a neighbor.

None of them spoke. Not yet. But the water carried their waiting the way it carried light, in long wavering ribbons, and Astrid felt it press against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"They're not leading us to safety," she said, when she surfaced in the great flooded square where the palace rose out of the reservoir like a whale's skull. "They're leading us somewhere. I can feel it. Like being herded by very polite sheepdogs."

Yeva stood beside her on the palace steps, water streaming off her elbows, her blind eyes aimed at nothing and somehow at everything. "Palaces," she said, "are where countries keep their hearts. Even drowned ones. Even especially drowned ones." She wrung out her sleeve with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had been wet before and expected to be wet again. "The dead want you to see something. I'd wager my boat on it, if I still had a boat."

"You traded your boat."

"I traded *a* boat. There's a difference, and it's called optimism."

Astrid almost smiled. It was getting harder to smile, the deeper they went. The water here was colder, and the whispers in the walls had changed key. Back in Thornfell, the dead whispered names — her trade, her whole life, the last words of the drowned ferryman and the fevered child and the grandmother who wanted to be remembered as *stubborn but kind*. Here, the dead whispered only one word, over and over, in a dialect Astrid was only now beginning to understand because her blood apparently understood it for her:

*Vessa. Vessa. Vessa.*

The name of the country. Or the name of the woman the country had been named after. Or possibly both. Astrid was learning that Vessany had never been good at separating its queens from its geography.

The palace doors were bronze, taller than three men, and open. Of course they were open. The Regency had drowned this place forty years ago; it had never needed to lock it. You do not lock a grave.

* * *

Inside, the water was shallower — knee-deep in the great hall, waist-deep in the side corridors where the ceilings had sagged like tired shoulders. Astrid's charm-bone clicked against her teeth with each breath. Yeva moved through the flooded halls with her hand trailing the walls, reading the stone the way Astrid read bone, and once she stopped and said, "Here. This was the map room," with such quiet grief that Astrid did not ask her to elaborate. Not yet. There was a conversation coming, Astrid could feel it, the way you feel a storm two valleys over. Yeva had been carrying it since the mountain passes, folded up small inside her like a letter she kept deciding not to send.

But that was later. The dead were tugging now, gently, insistently, and the tug had a direction: up.

Stairs. A grand staircase that broke the water's surface halfway up and climbed into air — real air, dry and stale and tasting of dust and old cedar. Astrid hauled herself out of the reservoir and lay gasping on the landing, and Yeva pulled herself up beside her, and for a while they simply breathed, two drowned rats in a dead queen's house.

"Cozy," Yeva said at last.

"You're blind. How do you know it's cozy?"

"I can smell the mold. Mold means shelter. Shelter means we're not walking. By my standards, that's practically a feather bed."

The dead had stopped at the waterline. Astrid realized it slowly, the way you realize music has ended. They stood in the flooded hall below, hundreds of them, faces upturned, and they did not follow her up the stairs. Whatever was up here, they would not — or could not — come to it. They could only send her.

"That's encouraging," she muttered.

"Isn't it," said Yeva, who had heard the silence too, in her own way.

The corridor at the top of the stairs was lined with portraits. Astrid walked past them slowly, holding her little lamp — a stub of tallow she'd kept dry inside a hollowed knucklebone, because a bone-carver trusts nothing that isn't bone. The paintings were dark with forty years of damp, but faces still surfaced out of the gloom like swimmers.

Kings. Queens. A whole gallery of them, and Astrid stopped, because she knew these faces.

She knew them the way you know a face in a crowd — not from memory, but from the mirror. The strong jaw of King Aldric the Third was her jaw. The deep-set gray eyes of Queen Maren were the eyes that had stared back at Astrid every morning of her life while she complained about them. A girl of perhaps sixteen, in the frame at the corridor's end, had Astrid's exact crooked left eyebrow, the one that made her look perpetually skeptical, which to be fair she usually was.

"Oh," Astrid said quietly. "Oh, that's not fair."

"What?" said Yeva.

"Nothing. It's just—" She touched the frame of the sixteen-year-old. "I've spent my whole life being nobody. Being so nobody that customs officers forget my name before I've finished saying it. And it turns out I have forty relatives up here with my exact face, and I never got to meet a single one of them."

Yeva was silent a moment. Then: "That's the cruelest thing drowning does. It doesn't just take people. It takes the *meeting*."

The corridor ended at double doors of black oak, carved with the Vessani sigil — a crown set inside a door. Astrid had seen the sigil on the map in her grandmother's chest, on the signet Serel Dane had worn, on the regalia fragments wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping child. Crown in a door. Door in a crown. She had assumed it was decorative.

She was beginning to suspect nothing in Vessany was merely decorative.

The doors were not locked. They were, however, heavy, and Astrid had to put her shoulder into them, and when they swung inward, the air that came out was different — colder, older, the air of a place that had been holding its breath.

The throne room was dry.

That should have been impossible. The reservoir pressed against these walls; the halls below sloshed; the ceilings wept. But the throne room sat in its own pocket of stillness, high on the palace's spine, and the air in it was thin and silver and utterly without water, as if the flood had come all this way and then, at the last door, had bowed and declined to enter.

Astrid understood why the dead had not followed. This room did not belong to them.

At the far end, on a dais of white stone, under a dome pierced with a single round window that let down one column of moonlight, stood a throne. And on the throne's armrest, placed there as casually as if a servant had just set it down and stepped away, was the crown of Vessany.

* * *

It was smaller than she expected.

She had imagined something monstrous — a wheel of gold and iron, spikes and jewels, the kind of thing that requires neck muscles and a lack of self-preservation. But the crown of Vessany was slender, a circlet really, worked silver with a pattern of interlocking doors, and it was beautiful the way her own carvings were beautiful, which is to say it looked like it had been made by someone who loved the person it was for.

That was the trap, of course. Astrid knew traps. She'd spent forty years learning how a thing can be lovely and lethal at once. A river is lovely. A promise is lovely.

"Yeva," she said. "What do you know about this crown?"

"Less than I should," Yeva said from the doorway, where she had stopped as if the threshold were a rope she would not cross. "More than most. The court cartographers weren't allowed in the throne room, but the palace is small and gossips travel through walls faster than water." Her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was rationing truth. "They said the crown was made before the country was. That the country was made *for* the crown, not the other way around. That the first queen put it on and came out of the coronation knowing every road, every river, every name in Vessany — and forgetting the name of the village she was born in."

Astrid looked at the crown. The crown, in the moonlight, looked back. She was aware this was not a thing crowns could do, and she was aware it was doing it anyway.

"Every heir," Astrid said. "The prophecy said the crown *turns* whoever wears it. Turns them into someone else."

"That's what your book said."

"I've been thinking about that word. *Turns.* In Thornfell we say the river turns the millstone. We say grief turns a man. It doesn't mean he moves. It means he's *ground*." Her mouth had gone dry. "Yeva, I've carved memorial bones my whole life. Do you know how you tell when a person's soul has left a body? The hands go first. The hands stop being theirs. Everything a person makes with their hands, that's the last part of them to go."

She stepped forward. The dead below the waterline had gone utterly silent. Even Yeva had stopped breathing, which for Yeva was an act of tremendous discipline.

"I have to see," Astrid said. "I have to *know* what I'm deciding about."

"You could decide not to touch it," Yeva offered. "People do that. It's a whole category of decision."

"The door won't open for a woman who won't choose. I tried leaving, remember. The mountain pass sealed itself like a jar. There's no middle road. There's only—" She gestured at the crown. "Whatever this is."

And she reached out, and she touched it.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe the vision, and she would fail, and she would keep trying, because some things deserve to be failed at forever.

It began with hands.

Not her hands. A man's hands, broad and scarred, gripping a plough, and then the same hands gripping a scepter, and the plough was gone, and the man could not remember what his palms had been calloused *from*. She was inside him — no, she was *watching* him, the way the dead watch, and she heard his thoughts go quiet one room at a time, like a house being closed for winter. *I was someone,* he thought, on a throne, in a crown, beloved by ten thousand people, and he could not finish the sentence, because the someone had been turned, and the turning never gave back.

Then a queen who had loved music. Astrid stood in her skull and heard the last song she ever remembered, and then heard the crown whisper over it — *you are Vessany now, you are every road and every harvest and every grave, you have no need of songs* — and the queen nodded, because it seemed reasonable, and the music left her the way water leaves a tipped cup, and she reigned forty years and never once hummed.

Then a king who had been afraid of the dark as a boy, and the crown took the fear, and with it went the memory of why he'd been afraid — his brother, lost in the mines — and with the brother went the grief, and with the grief went the love, and the king signed orders with steady hands and felt nothing at all, and the nothing felt, to him, like peace, and that was the worst thing Astrid had ever witnessed, worse than any drowning.

They came faster then. Heir after heir. A poet. A farmer's daughter. A boy of nineteen who wept for three days before his coronation and came out serene, and Astrid screamed inside the vision *don't put it on, don't, run, the door is behind you, there was always a door behind you* — but the boy was forty years dead and the vision was not a warning, it was a *record*, and records do not take corrections.

Each of them had walked in whole. Each of them had walked out as a vessel shaped exactly like a person, filled with the country — every border, every law, every name in every village — and empty of everything else. The crown did not destroy them. That would have been a mercy with edges. The crown *used* them. It turned them the way a key turns in a lock, and when the lock was done with them, it left them in the ground, worn smooth, blank, their names remembered by everyone and their selves remembered by no one, not even themselves.

And the last vision was not a king or a queen at all.

It was a woman with gray eyes and a crooked left eyebrow, standing in this exact room, holding this exact crown, and she was not wearing it. She was looking at it the way Astrid was looking at it. And she said — in Astrid's own voice, in the Vessani dialect Astrid's blood knew and her ears were only now learning — *the crown turns, and it never gives back, and so I will be the one who does not turn, and they will hate me for it, and it will be the only honest thing any of us ever did.*

Then the vision let her go, and Astrid Voss was on her knees on the white stone dais, and her hands were shaking, and they were still hers. That was the only thing she could be sure of. Her hands were still hers.

* * *

"Astrid." Yeva's voice, from the doorway, stripped of all its dry humor. "You went somewhere. You went somewhere for a long time. The moon moved."

Astrid pushed herself up. Her legs held, which felt like an unearned gift. "Yeva," she said, and her voice came out scraped. "How many heirs wore that crown?"

"Eleven that the histories name."

"Twelve." Astrid looked at the crown, still sitting on the armrest, patient as a spider. "There were twelve. The last one never wore it. She picked it up, and she saw what I just saw, and she put it down, and she was the one who hid the regalia, who wrote the letters in my grandmother's chest, who made sure the bloodline got out of the country before the water came." Astrid laughed, and it was not a kind laugh, it was the laugh of a woman standing at the edge of something enormous. "It was my grandmother. Or my great-grandmother. The thirteenth heir. The one the prophecy is *about*."

"The prophecy says you die," Yeva said quietly.

"The prophecy says the thirteenth heir dies on the thirteenth turn." Astrid wiped her face with a hand that obeyed her, that was hers, that she would carve with until the day she actually died, thank you very much. "And I finally understand what it meant. It doesn't mean a knife. It doesn't mean drowning. It means that if I put that crown on, Astrid Voss dies. Not her body. *Her.* The bone-carver. The woman who complains about her eyes and over-salts her soup and talks to ghosts because they're better company than most of the living. The crown doesn't kill you, Yeva. It *replaces* you. And it never gives back."

Silence. The good kind — the kind two survivors can stand inside together.

Then Yeva said, in a voice Astrid had never heard her use, small and old: "I knew a cartographer who refused to give up her maps. They took her eyes for it. And some nights, in the dark, I have thought: at least the crown would have left me my eyes."

Astrid turned. Yeva stood in the doorway with her face tilted toward the throne, toward the crown she could not see, and her hands — her hands were shaking.

"Yeva," Astrid said slowly. "How did you know the road through the passes that isn't on any map? How did you know the palace doors would be open? How did you know to bring me here, to *this* room?" She took a breath, and the breath hurt. "Who were you, before you were Yeva Kroll?"

The blind smuggler stood very still in the doorway of the throne room, and the moonlight came down through the round window and touched her face, and for the first time since Thornfell, Yeva looked less like a woman deciding what to say, and more like a woman who had run out of roads that didn't lead here.

"I was," she said, "the Keeper of the King's Maps. And I have been guiding you toward this crown since the day you walked into my camp with three carved bones and a dead man's warning — because the last thing I ever promised the crown of Vessany, before they took my eyes, was that I would bring them an heir."

The crown, on its armrest, seemed to lean a little closer.

And somewhere below, beneath the waterline, the dead of Vessany began, very softly, to sing.
