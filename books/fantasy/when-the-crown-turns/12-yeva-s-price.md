## 12. Yeva's Price

The singing under the water did not stop.

Yeva had heard it before — once, forty years ago, on the night they took her eyes. She had been standing in the map room of the palace with a torch in one hand and the master atlas of Vessany in the other, and the courtiers in the corridor below had begun to sing, low and wordless, the way people sing when they already know how a thing ends. She had thought then that it was the most beautiful sound she would ever hear.

She had been wrong. It was more beautiful now, because now it was not a farewell. Now it was a summons.

"You knew," Astrid said. Her voice came out flat, scraped clean of everything soft. "The whole time. From Thornfell. From the ferryman. You knew what I was, and you didn't tell me."

They stood in the high gallery above the flooded throne room, where the crown sat on its stone armrest like a cat pretending to sleep. The water below was black and patient. The dead beneath it were still singing, and Yeva could feel them the way an old dog feels weather — in the joints, in the scars, in the places that had never quite healed.

"I suspected," Yeva said. "There's a difference. Suspecting is a craft. Knowing is a luxury I stopped affording decades ago."

"Don't." Astrid's voice cracked down the middle. "Don't do that. Don't be clever at me. Not tonight."

Yeva turned her face toward the girl — toward the heir, toward the bone-carver, toward the last living thread of a country Yeva had spent her whole life drawing and her whole old age failing to save. She could not see Astrid. She had not seen anything since the executioner's man had held the hot iron to her eyes and asked, one final time, for the maps. But she could hear her breathing: quick, shallow, angry. And underneath the anger, worse — hurt. The particular hurt of someone who has just discovered that the first person to truly *see* her had been lying about it from the start.

"Sit down," Yeva said. "This is going to take longer than you have patience for, and my knees have less than either of us."

Astrid did not sit. Of course she didn't. Yeva sighed and lowered herself onto a fallen beam, arranging her stick across her lap like a scepter, because if you could not have dignity, you could at least have posture.

* * *

"I was born in Old Vessany," Yeva said, "in a house on Cartographers' Row, three doors down from the ink-seller who watered his indigo and charged full price for it. My father drew coastlines. His father had drawn coastlines. When I was six years old I could draw the Vessani shore from memory, all two hundred miles of it, including the little island off Cape Meren that only appears at low tide, which is why half the Regency's navy has run aground there over the years and why I consider those drownings the finest work of my career."

Astrid made a sound that was almost a laugh and absolutely refused to become one.

"I was good," Yeva went on. "Not talented — good. There's a difference. Talent is what you're given; goodness is what you do at four in the morning when your eyes are bleeding and the survey has to reach the capital by spring. By thirty I held the King's commission. Keeper of the Maps. I drew every road, every pass, every smuggler track through the mountains — yes, including yours, girl, the ones we walked here. I drew them because a country that cannot be drawn cannot be defended, and I loved Vessany the way you love a body: completely, specifically, and with an unsentimental knowledge of all its worst parts."

"And then?"

"And then the Regency came." Yeva's hands found each other in her lap, knuckle against knuckle, an old habit. "You know the history as they teach it. The annexation. The dissolution. A country erased from maps forty years ago — well. Someone had to erase it. They came to me with a list. Every map, every chart, every survey, every tracing. And when I refused—" she touched her own face, briefly, the way she sometimes did when she wanted to remember what seeing felt like, "—they decided that if I would not give up the maps, I should no longer be able to make use of them."

"But you kept them."

"I kept them." She said it simply, because there was no other way to say a thing like that. "Burned into memory. Every line. I can draw you the whole of Vessany in the dark, Astrid Voss, because I have been walking around inside the dark version of it for forty years."

Silence, except for the singing below, which had changed key without Yeva noticing when. It did that. The dead were never in any hurry.

"So when the Regency burned the archives and drowned the capital," Astrid said slowly, working it through, "you were the only map left."

"I was the only map left," Yeva agreed. "Which makes me either the last loyal servant of a dead kingdom or the world's most stubborn grave marker, depending on the day."

"And me? Where do I come into this grand cartographic tragedy?"

Yeva tilted her head. Below them, the water lapped at the throne room steps with the sound of someone knocking politely on a door that would never be answered.

"The prophecy book," she said. "The one you found at the stall in Thornfell. You've wondered, I imagine, why a century-old text about the royal line of Vessany happened to be sitting on a smuggler's table in a border town, priced to move, on the exact night you needed to find it."

"It crossed my mind once or twice."

"I put it there." Yeva let that land. "Forty years ago, when the palace fell, the royal archivist gave me the book and told me to keep it moving — never let it rest, never let it be found by the Regency, and above all, never let it find its heir too early. An heir who learns her fate with a lifetime left to dread it will break before the date arrives. So I carried it. I moved it. For forty years I passed it hand to hand through every smuggler network from here to the coast, always keeping it a few years' walk ahead of where the bloodline might plausibly be. And then, last autumn, I heard a rumor out of Thornfell. A bone-carver. A woman who hears the dead whisper their names. A woman nobody remembers, in a town nobody visits, whose grandmother came from nowhere at all."

"You could have asked me," Astrid said. "You could have *told* me."

"I could have," Yeva said. "And you'd have done exactly what you're doing now, except forty miles back down the mountain, with Serel Dane's hunters between you and anywhere safe. Some truths are cargo, girl. You don't unload them in rough water."

"That's not your choice to make!"

"No," Yeva said quietly. "It isn't. That's rather the point of the confession."

* * *

Astrid did sit down then. Yeva heard it — the scrape of stone, the exhale, the small defeated sound of someone whose legs have voted against their principles.

"Say the rest," Astrid said. "All of it. If we're doing confessions, we're doing them properly. What else haven't you told me?"

Yeva considered lying. It was, she reflected, a testament to how thoroughly the girl had gotten under her skin that she even considered it — Yeva Kroll had lied to customs officers, border lords, two husbands, and one archbishop, and had slept soundly after every occasion. But lying to Astrid Voss had begun to feel like smudging a map she'd spent her life keeping clean.

"The bargain," she said. "Three carved bones for passage. You thought you were paying me."

"I did pay you. I watched you take them."

"I did take them. I'm a smuggler; taking things is my whole personality." Yeva turned the stick in her hands. "But I'd have carried you for nothing, and you'd have known it, and known-it-for-nothing is a debt people choke on. So I invented a price so you could believe you'd bought your way free and clear. That was kindness, of a kind. The kind that keeps ledgers."

"What else?"

"The valley village. The elders who knelt to you." Yeva's jaw tightened. "I chose that route deliberately. I could have taken you through the northern passes — faster, drier, no kneeling peasants. I took you through the valley because I needed you recognized. I needed you to *believe*. An heir who doubts her own blood will hesitate at the crown, and hesitation, in that room downstairs, gets people killed."

"You used me."

"I guided you," Yeva said. "It's the same trade with better lighting. Yes. I used you. I have been using you since Thornfell, Astrid Voss, and I am not going to stand here — sit here — and pretend otherwise, because you deserve better than a comfortable lie, and also because you carve bones for a living and would see through me inside a week anyway."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why does it matter so much to you? Your kingdom's dead. Your eyes are gone. You got out. You survived. Why spend forty years dragging a prophecy book around the borderlands like some — some relic peddler?"

And there it was. The real price. Yeva had known this moment would come since the campfire outside Thornfell, and she had rehearsed a dozen versions of it, and every one of them had sounded braver in rehearsal.

"Because I didn't refuse them for Vessany," she said. "That's the story I tell in taverns, and it's a good story, and it's a lie. I refused them because I was proud. Because they sent a clerk — a *clerk*, a wet-eared boy with a Regency seal and ink-stained cuffs — to demand my life's work, and I looked at him and thought, *I will not be ordered about by a boy who has never surveyed anything higher than his own desk.* Pride, Astrid. Not love. Not duty. Pride. If they'd sent someone I respected, I might have handed over the maps and retired to the coast to drink myself blind the honest way."

Below them, the singing paused — the way a room pauses when someone says something true — and then resumed, softer.

"By the time I understood what I'd actually done," Yeva went on, "what my pride had bought — a country erased, a people scattered, villages burned to erase the *dialect*, Astrid, the *words children speak* — by then it was too late to be a hero. All that was left was the debt. And debts are the one thing I've never smuggled past. So yes. I found you. I guided you. I put the book in your path and the road under your feet and the village in your way, because I promised the crown of Vessany an heir, and because I needed — " her voice caught, an old hinge refusing to turn, " — I needed it to mean something. The eyes. The forty years. All of it. I needed someone to walk the roads I bled for and make them *lead somewhere*."

The silence stretched. Yeva counted her heartbeats the way she counted paces on an unfamiliar road, waiting for the verdict.

"You should have told me," Astrid said at last. "In the valley. When they knelt. You should have told me then."

"Yes."

"You let me think I stumbled into it."

"You did stumble into it. I just arranged the staircase." Yeva spread her hands. "Girl, I have deceived kings, priests, and the entire Regency Survey. You are the first person in forty years I've wanted to tell the truth to, and look how badly I managed even that. Belonging is wasted on the honest; we lie longest to the people we most want to keep."

"That's not wisdom, that's just sad."

"It's both. Most wisdom is."

* * *

He heard Astrid stand. Cross the gallery. Heels on stone, unhurried — and Yeva realized, with a lurch she hadn't felt since her sight went, that she had no idea what the girl intended. To walk away. To curse her. To go down alone to the crown and the water and the singing dead, and leave Yeva Kroll sitting on a beam in the dark with her stick and her confession and nothing else.

Footsteps stopped in front of her. A pause. Then something pressed into her hands: small, smooth, familiar.

One of the three carved bones. The ferryman's.

"You took payment for passage," Astrid said. "Fine. Then I'm buying something else. The truth, from now on. All of it, even the ugly parts, even the parts about you. Especially those." A breath. "Because I spent my whole life being invisible, Yeva. Nobody saw me, and I told myself I liked it that way. And then you — a blind woman who's never seen my face — you're the person who's seen me most clearly of anyone alive. I'm not giving that up because you're a manipulative old smuggler with a martyr complex."

"Manipulative old smuggler with a martyr complex," Yeva repeated, turning the bone over in her fingers. Its edges fit her palm like it had been measured there. Perhaps they had been. "That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me since the annexation."

"It wasn't kind. It was accurate."

"Even better." Yeva closed her hand around the bone. "Then we're mended, you and I. Badly, with visible stitching, the way everything worth keeping gets mended."

"One condition."

"Name it."

"When this is over — whatever happens with the crown, whatever the dead decide — you teach me the maps. All of them. The real ones, the ones behind your eyes. Vessany shouldn't die twice."

Yeva smiled, and was glad, for once, that no one could see her doing it. "Girl," she said, "that was the plan from the beginning. I told you. I'm a map. Maps exist to be copied."

Astrid laughed — short, wet, real — and sat down beside her on the beam, shoulder to shoulder, the way travelers sit when the road has finally stopped arguing with them.

For a while neither spoke. Below, the dead sang. Above, through the broken roof of the gallery, Yeva could feel the moon coming up — the twelfth turn nearly spent, one left, the air itself gone taut as a drawn bowstring.

Then Astrid stiffened.

"Yeva." Her voice dropped. "Lights. On the reservoir rim. A lot of them."

Yeva turned her face toward the waterline, listening past the singing, past the lapping, and caught it: boots. Many boots. The particular disciplined rhythm of Regency infantry, and beneath it the thinner, colder note of lantern glass and orders being given quietly.

"They've sealed the rim," Astrid whispered. "Torches all along the edge. Yeva — they're not searching. They're *positioning*."

Yeva's stomach went cold and old. She knew that formation. She had mapped it often enough, in another life, for another army. Troops spaced evenly along a height above a basin, facing inward, holding position.

That was not a search pattern. That was a containment line.

Or, if the orders were what Yeva feared they were — a firing line waiting for the word to open the sluices and drown the ruins a second time, deeper, permanent, with everyone inside.

"Get away from the edge," she said, rising, stick finding stone. "Get everyone back from the waterline. Now."

Far off along the rim, a single lantern began to move against the tide of torches — fast, erratic, pursued.

Someone was running.
