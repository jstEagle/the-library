## 13. Hunters at the Waterline

The orders came at dawn, and they came in Serel Dane's own hand.

That was the trick of it, he understood now—the Regency never sent a man an order he could refuse. They sent him the ink and the seal and the blank space where his signature went, and they let him do the rest himself. *By authority vested in the Office of Inquiry, the reservoir at Old Vessany is to be raised two fathoms by nightfall of the twelfth turn.* Two fathoms. Enough to drown the sunken streets a second time, deeper this time, permanent this time. Enough to close over anyone still walking them.

He read it four times in his tent while his tea went cold, and on the fourth reading he noticed what he should have noticed first: there was no clause for prisoners taken. No clause for survivors. No line that said *except*.

The Regency had not written an order. It had written an ending, and left his name at the bottom of it.

"Captain?" Lieutenant Brask stood at the tent flap, young and pink-cheeked and terribly eager, the way men are when they have not yet been asked to do something unforgivable. "The engineers are asking about the sluice gates. The old mechanisms are Vessani work—pre-annexation—and half the manuals were burned with everything else, so."

"So," Serel said.

"They're saying it might take until midday. Maybe later. The iron's rusted through in places." Brask hesitated, then added, with the small cruelty of innocence, "Might give us time to flush out whatever's down there first. Sweep the ruins properly before we raise the water. If there are smugglers in the streets, we could take them alive. That'd be a feather in—"

"Yes," Serel said. "That would be a feather."

Brask beamed and went to tell the engineers to work slowly, which was the last honest labor anyone would perform at the waterline that day.

* * *

Serel walked the shore alone.

The reservoir had been built eleven years after the annexation, a triumph of the Regency's engineering and its arithmetic both: if you cannot govern a country, drown it. Old Vessany lay beneath forty feet of gray-green water, its spires visible on calm days like fingers reaching up through a sheet, and for forty years the maps had said RESERVOIR NO. 4 in tidy block letters, as though a nation could be renamed into nothing.

But nations, he was learning, did not drown so easily as that.

He had felt it first in Thornfell, interviewing a bone-carver with steady hands and a liar's excellent posture. He had felt it in the passes, where his hunters reported ghosts marching in formation and he had written *mass hysteria, altitude* in his report and not believed one word of it. And he felt it now, standing at the waterline while behind him three hundred soldiers unspooled chain and stakes and rope, sealing the shore the way you'd seal a wound you intended to make worse.

The water was watching him.

Not metaphorically—or not only metaphorically. Faces moved beneath the surface, pale and patient, hundreds of them, thousands, drifting through the drowned streets like fish through kelp. They did not menace. That was the terrible thing. They simply waited, the way the dead wait everywhere, except these dead were waiting for something specific, and their patience had a direction to it, and the direction was toward a woman with carved bone charms strung around her neck who was, at this moment, somewhere under all that water, breathing through magic older than the Regency and walking streets the Regency had spent four decades insisting did not exist.

"Sir." Sergeant Ilma fell into step beside him, her boots crunching on the shingle. She was fifty, gray at the temples, and had served in three border wars without once being promoted past sergeant, which she claimed was because she kept telling colonels the truth. "You've read the order."

"I've read the order."

"And?"

"And it says raise the water by nightfall of the twelfth turn."

"I can read, Captain. I'm asking what *you* say."

Serel watched a heron stalk the shallows, absurdly graceful, hunting fish above the roofs of a dead city. "I say," he said carefully, "that the order contains no provision for prisoners."

Ilma was quiet for a moment. "No," she agreed. "It doesn't."

"The woman down there is a civilian. The smugglers are civilians. There may be valley people—there may be children. The order treats them all as sediment."

"It does."

"And I am required to sign it."

"You are," Ilma said, "required to sign it. Yes." She looked out at the water with him, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "You know what I did in the third border war? Before you were shaving?"

"Tell me."

"There was a village. Kellin Hollow. Orders were to clear it—burn it, move the people east, standard erasure work, we called it *resettlement* in the reports." She turned a stone over with her boot. "I burned it. I was twenty-six and I signed my name and I told myself the law was the law. Forty years I've been telling myself that." She looked at him then, and her eyes were very level. "I'm not telling you what to do, Captain. I'm telling you what the telling-yourself costs, and that the price goes up every year, and it's collected all at once at the end, in a lump sum, usually at three in the morning."

"That's not regulation encouragement, Sergeant."

"No, sir. It's worse. It's experience."

* * *

He stalled the way a good officer stalls: procedurally, tediously, with paperwork.

The sluice mechanisms were Vessani-made and required careful handling; he ordered the engineers to document every stage. The divers' survey was incomplete; he extended it by a day. A runner arrived from the valley villages with news that the local population was restless, and he dispatched two companies to "monitor the situation," which everyone understood to mean *stand between the villages and the guns*, and which he wrote down as *containment*. Each delay bought hours. Each hour, he told himself, was a small country saved.

By the second evening, the Regency noticed.

The message came by fast rider, sealed twice, and Serel broke it open standing in the wind at the waterline with the drowned city glittering faintly below him in the last light. It was short. The Regency's messages were always short; brevity was how power kept its hands clean.

*CAPTAIN DANE. THE TWELFTH TURN IS TOMORROW. YOU WILL EXECUTE ORDER 9-VES BY NIGHTFALL OR BE RELIEVED AND EXECUTED WITH IT. THERE ARE NO EXCEPTIONS. THIS IS YOUR LAST INSTRUCTION.*

Below it, in a different hand—a smaller, colder hand he recognized as Undersecretary Vane's—someone had added a single line:

*We know about the bone-carver. We know what she is. Do not imagine your hesitation is private.*

Serel stood very still.

So they knew. Of course they knew. They had probably known since Thornfell, since the customs inspection where he'd worn the old Vessani signet on his finger like a fool and watched a bone-carver lie to him beautifully and thought, *she's hiding something*, when all along the thing she was hiding was the same thing he was: a country, folded up small, carried against the ribs.

He thought about Astrid Voss's hands. Carver's hands, nicked and capable, the kind of hands that made names for the dead because someone had to. He had interrogated her across a customs table and she had lied to him with such warmth, such perfect reasonable warmth, that he'd walked out half in love with the lie itself. And now she was down there, under the water, among the dead of a nation he'd been raised to believe was a clerical error, and the Regency wanted the water raised over her head, and the order was in his hand, and his signature was the only thing standing between the pen and the flood.

"Captain?" Brask again, appearing at his elbow with the boundless timing of the young. "The engineers say the sluices could be ready by noon tomorrow. Early, even, if we push. Shall I tell them to push?"

And there it was. The moment, delivered by a pink-cheeked boy who would carry whatever happened next for the rest of his life, the way Ilma carried Kellin Hollow, the way every soldier carries the lump sum eventually.

Serel looked at the order in his hand. Then at the water. Then at the boy.

"No," he said.

Brask blinked. "Sir?"

"Tell them to stop work entirely. Tell them the mechanisms are unsafe. Tell them—" and here Serel heard his own voice go strange and calm, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge and discovering, mid-fall, that he had always meant to jump— "tell them I said the Regency does not drown prisoners. Tell them I said it loudly."

"Captain Dane." Brask's face had gone carefully blank, the blankness of a man recalculating everything. "With respect. That's—"

"Treason. Yes. I'm given to understand the word has a definition, and I appear to be memorizing it."

* * *

They arrested him at dusk.

It was done politely, as such things are. Colonel Ashe arrived with two officers and a file of soldiers, and read the warrant aloud at the edge of the reservoir while the water below went glass-still, every drowned face turned upward, listening. Serel stood at attention through all of it. It seemed important to stand at attention—to show that a man could break faith with an order and keep faith with something else, some older thing, the thing the order had been wearing as a disguise.

"Surrender your signet," Ashe said, when the reading was done.

Serel looked down at the old Vessani ring on his finger—the one he'd worn in Thornfell, the one Astrid had noticed, the one that had started all of this or finished it, depending on how you counted. He had taken it from evidence years ago, a confiscated curiosity, and had told himself he wore it to remember what he was investigating. He had known, even then, that he was lying.

He worked it off his finger. Held it a moment. Then, instead of handing it to Ashe, he crouched and set it gently on the shingle at the waterline, where the tide could reach it.

"For the record," he said, "it belongs to them."

Ashe's jaw tightened. "Captain Dane, you are relieved of command and charged with treason, obstruction, and conspiracy against the Regency. You will be held for transport to the capital."

"I'll cooperate fully," Serel said. "In fact, I look forward to the trial. I intend to testify. At length. With documents." He smiled, and found to his surprise that it was a real smile, the first in weeks. "There's rather a lot of documentation. The resettlement orders alone run to nine volumes. I've been keeping copies. One develops habits."

"You're digging your own grave."

"I'm digging everyone's," Serel agreed pleasantly. "Yours included, if you sign the next order. Ask Sergeant Ilma what the telling-yourself costs. She gives excellent rates."

They took his sword and his papers and marched him up the shingle road toward the stockade, and Serel Dane went willingly, hands bound, back straight, past the ranks of soldiers who would not meet his eye—except Ilma, who did, and gave him the smallest nod in the history of the army, which from her was a parade-ground salute.

Behind him, as the light failed, the reservoir began to glow.

Faintly at first—a green-gold shimmer rising from the drowned streets like heat-haze inverted, like lanterns being lit window by window in a city that had waited forty years for someone to come home. The soldiers on the shore stopped working. Some knelt. One began, helplessly, to hum an old song no one had taught him, a song in a language the Regency had struck from the registers, and the hum spread along the waterline like fire along a fuse.

Serel turned his head to watch, and what he saw made his breath catch: figures in the water now, not drifting but *gathered*, moving with purpose through the flooded avenues toward the great dark shape of the palace, thousands upon thousands of the patient dead converging like iron filings toward a magnet, toward a hall, toward a decision.

They were going to vote. He understood that suddenly, completely, the way you understand thunder after lightning. An entire nation, silent for forty years, was about to hold its first assembly—and the agenda was a crown, and a woman, and whether a country gets to live again or finally, mercifully, die.

"Move along," said the soldier prodding his shoulder, though the soldier's voice shook, and his eyes never left the glowing water.

"As you wish," Serel said. "Though I'd hurry, if I were you. I don't think history plans to wait for either of us."

They led him away into the dark, and behind them the drowned city lit itself, street by street, like a country remembering its own name.
