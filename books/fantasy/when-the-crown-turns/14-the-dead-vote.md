## 14. The Dead Vote

The dead of Vessany did not arrive all at once. They came the way snow comes—first a few flakes drifting through the flooded colonnade, then a flurry, then a weather.

Astrid stood in the great hall of the sunken palace with water to her knees and her bone charm pressed between her teeth, breathing through it in slow pulls. The hall had once held court. She could see its bones beneath the ruin: the dais at the far end, half-collapsed; the ranks of pillars like drowned trees; the gallery where musicians had played, now home to a school of pale fish that scattered whenever a ghost passed through them, which was often, because ghosts were passing everywhere now, arriving by the hundred, by the thousand, rising out of stairwells and doorways and the green murk of windows, gathering the way people gather when word goes round that something is about to be decided.

"They heard," Yeva said beside her. The old smuggler's blind eyes were turned toward the dais, though Astrid knew she saw nothing of it—or perhaps everything, in whatever way cartographers see maps after the maps are gone. "Forty years I told myself they were sleeping down here. They weren't sleeping. They were waiting for someone to ask them a question."

"Nobody asked?"

"You don't ask the dead to vote on whether they should keep dying." Yeva's mouth twisted. "You just hope somebody eventually has the nerve."

Astrid's hands were shaking. She noticed this with the detached professionalism of a woman who had spent thirty years keeping her hands steady over carving knives, and found it didn't help. "I haven't asked anything."

"You walked into their palace wearing your own face." Yeva shrugged. "In Vessany, that was always question enough."

* * *

They came in trades and professions first, which was how Astrid began to understand what she was looking at. A cluster of women in aprons who could only have been bakers, still dusted with flour that had been dust forty years ago. Soldiers in the grey-green of the old Vessani guard, their uniforms rotted to lace but their posture intact. Children—there were children, and Astrid's chest clenched so hard she had to grip Yeva's arm—a dozen of them, chasing each other through a pillar as if the pillar were a game of tag, and maybe it had been, once, before the water came.

An old man near the front wore the chain of a magistrate. A young woman carried a ledger that existed only as an idea now, her fingers curled around its absence. A priest in vestments gone to shadow stood apart from everyone, hands folded, watching Astrid with an expression she recognized from her shop: the look of someone deciding how much truth a stranger can carry.

And above them all, on the broken dais, on a cushion of rotted velvet, sat the crown.

It was smaller than she remembered from her vision of it. Smaller than it had any right to be, for the weight it carried. Iron worked into a circlet of thorned vines, each thorn tipped with a bead of some dark glass that caught the filtered light and gave nothing back. It did not gleam. Crowns that gleam want to be seen. This one wanted to be worn, which was worse.

The hall filled until Astrid stopped being able to count. Three thousand? Five? The dead of a whole country, or as many of them as had stayed—and most of them had stayed, she realized. That was the thing nobody had told her about erasure. You can burn the maps and flood the cities and strike the name from every ledger, but you cannot make the dead leave. The Regency had drowned Vessany and discovered that drowning is not the same as dismissal.

Then a hush went through the assembly—not the hush of a crowd settling, but the hush of a crowd that has done this before, in another life, and remembers the shape of it.

A woman stepped forward from the ranks of the soldiers. Tall, grey-haired even in death, with a scar across her throat that predated her death by decades. Astrid knew her without knowing how: General Maren Ostrev, last defender of the eastern passes, whose name appeared in none of the Regency's histories because the Regency's histories pretended the passes had never needed defending.

"Astrid Voss," the general said, and her voice carried through water and stone alike, the way voices do in dreams. "Daughter of Lise Voss, daughter of Ilse, daughter of Queen Sabine the Third, whom the histories also pretend never lived. We have watched you walk our roads. We have saluted you, and you were too polite to ask why." A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but its ghost—moved through the assembly. "We are going to argue now. In front of you. Because for forty years we have argued without you, and it turns out that was the whole problem."

"I didn't come here to be argued over," Astrid said, and was surprised by how steady her voice came out. "I came to bury a country properly."

"Yes," said General Ostrev. "That's rather the dispute."

* * *

It began, as such things often do, with the reasonable people.

"We are five thousand souls," said the magistrate with the chain, rising, "and we have been patient. Patient through the flooding, patient through the renaming, patient while our language was declared a dialect and our history a rumor. Patience was a virtue when there was something left to wait for. There is not. Our houses are silt. Our fields are reservoir bottom. But she—" he pointed at Astrid, and his finger trembled, and Astrid understood suddenly that this was not anger, it was the effort of pointing at hope without breaking it, "—she is alive. She is blood. Put the crown on her head and Vessany lives again. Not as territory. As *people*. Us, gathered to her. A nation needs no map. It needs a hearth, and she is the hearth."

Murmurs of assent, warm and low, rolling through the hall like the water itself.

"And where," said a new voice, dry as old paper, "would this living nation live?"

The priest had stepped forward. Up close, Astrid saw that his vestments were choir robes, a boy's size grown into a man's ghost. He looked sixty and fourteen at once, which is a particular horror of the drowned.

"In her," the magistrate said. "Through her. A queen carries her people."

"A queen carries her people *to their graves*," the priest said. "I buried this country, Magistrate. I was fourteen when the water came and I have spent forty years conducting funerals for a nation that refuses to admit it's over. Do you know what the crown does? Have any of you actually asked what it does?" He turned, slowly, addressing the whole hall now. "Every heir who wore it is still in there. Did you know that? Not gone. *In there.* Wound around the iron like wire. Sabine the Third is in there. Her father is in there. Nine generations of Vessani monarchs, folded into a circlet of thorns, each one overwritten until there was nothing left of themselves to overwrite with." His voice cracked, and for a moment he sounded exactly his true age. "The crown doesn't make queens. It eats them. And it has been hungry for forty years, and you are proposing to feed it a living girl because we are lonely."

"She would not be eaten," the magistrate said. "She is strong. She carved her way here through mountains the Regency erased—"

"So did the last one," the priest said. "So did all of them. Every heir was strong. That's how you can tell the crown isn't picky."

The argument broke open then, and Astrid stood in the middle of it like a stone in a river.

They argued the way families argue—with old grievances surfacing mid-sentence, with names invoked as weapons, with forty years of compressed grief detonating in every direction. A baker shouted that her son had died holding the eastern wall and she would not see his sacrifice traded away for a quiet grave. A soldier shouted back that her son had died at fifteen and deserved rest more than resurrection. An old woman sang three lines of a lullaby in dead Vessani and half the hall wept and the argument faltered and then resumed, angrier, because grief interrupted is worse than grief expressed.

Astrid looked for Yeva and found her standing very still, face tilted up toward the vaulted ceiling, listening to the argument the way other people listen to music.

"You knew this would happen," Astrid murmured.

"I hoped." Yeva's voice was barely audible under the din. "Hoping is my vice. I've kept it hidden for fifty years, same as everything else worth having."

* * *

Hours passed, or what Astrid's body insisted were hours; underwater light made its own clockless afternoon. The arguments cycled. The same positions, worn smooth by repetition, like pebbles in a streambed:

*Crown her. A nation is its people, and we are still here.*

*Break it. A nation is also its ending, and we have earned ours.*

*Crown her, and let her choose later—*

*There is no later. The crown doesn't give back. Ask anyone in it.*

*Ask anyone in it,* Astrid thought, and the thought landed in her stomach like a dropped hammer.

Because that was the thing. She had touched the crown once, in the sunken throne room, days ago—or a lifetime ago—and she had *seen* them. Nine generations of heirs, layered inside the iron like rings in a tree, each one dimmer than the last, each one having entered believing themselves strong enough to remain themselves. The last one, a young king with ink-stained fingers, had still been trying, at the very center, to remember his own middle name. He hadn't managed it in forty years.

They were in the hall now too, she realized. Some of them. Faint figures at the edges of the assembly, translucent even by ghost standards, drawn to the debate like moths who could no longer remember why they loved light. One of them hovered near the dais, close to the crown, and when the argument swelled loudest, Astrid saw its lips moving: *don't, don't, don't*, over and over, in a rhythm too worn to be words anymore.

"Crown her!" someone bellowed, and the hall roared.

"Let us *rest!*" someone else bellowed, and the hall roared back, and Astrid understood with terrible clarity that both sides were roaring for the same reason. Not policy. Not principle. Loneliness against weariness. Hope against mercy. And no one in this hall could lose gracefully, because losing meant either continuing forever or ceasing entirely, and those are not graceful options.

Yeva leaned close. "They're going to ask you soon."

"Ask me what?"

"What you want." The old woman smiled, thin and crooked. "It'll be the first time anyone's asked in forty years. Try to have an answer that isn't just fear wearing a coat."

* * *

It was the child who broke the deadlock, the way children do, by saying the unsayable.

He detached himself from the pack of ghost-children—he couldn't have been older than nine, and he had the specific gravity of a boy who had drowned doing something he'd been told twice not to do—and marched to the center of the hall, directly in front of Astrid, and looked up at her with the frank appraisal of the young.

"Are you going to be our queen?" he asked.

The hall went silent. Five thousand dead waited on a nine-year-old's question, because children cut through knots that adults spend lifetimes tying.

"No," Astrid said.

The silence curdled. The magistrate's face fell. Somewhere in the ranks of the soldiers, a woman made a small wounded sound.

"But," Astrid continued, and her heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her teeth, in her palms, in the scar tissue of her knuckles where thirty years of knife-work had written their own history, "I'm not going to leave you the way the Regency did, either. Nobody gets to erase you twice."

She waded forward—actually waded, water dragging at her skirts, until she stood in the open space between the two factions, the crown behind her on its rotten cushion and the whole drowned nation in front of her.

"I've spent my whole life making myself invisible," she said, and her voice shook, and she let it, because Yeva was right, fear was there, but fear wasn't all that was there. "Small town, quiet trade, head down. I thought that was survival. Then I learned I was born to a throne I never wanted, and I thought: fine, I'll refuse it quietly too, slip out the back of destiny the way I slipped out of everything else." She looked at the magistrate. "But there's no third path. I've looked. Believe me, if anyone could find a middle way out of this, it's a bone-carver—we're professionally familiar with the space between living and dead."

Someone laughed. It broke something open in the hall, a little seam of warmth.

"Here's what I know," Astrid said. "I know how to carve names. That's all I've ever known how to do, and I used to think it was a small thing. It isn't. A name is the difference between a body and a person. A name is the difference between a country and a place on a map that got painted over." She turned to face the whole assembly, all five thousand of them, the bakers and the soldiers and the children and the faint ring-worn kings at the edges. "I won't wear the crown. What's inside it deserves better than another tenant, and so do I. But I will not break it tonight, in the dark, in a hurry, just to end an argument. If it must be broken—if that's truly the mercy—I'll do it properly. At the forge. With my own hands. And before I do, every one of you gets to say your piece. Not vote. *Say.* All of you. The ones who wanted crowning and the ones who wanted rest and the ones who couldn't decide. Forty years you've been talked about. You're going to be talked *to*, at least once, before the end."

The silence that followed was different from the others. It had weight, but not pressure. It was the silence of a room full of people realizing they have been offered something they'd stopped believing was on offer.

The priest sat down on the flooded steps and put his face in his hands and cried, or did whatever ghosts do instead, and several of the soldiers pretended not to notice, badly.

General Ostrev studied Astrid for a long moment. Then she drew herself up and did the thing she had done on the roads, the salute Astrid had never asked about—but this time she held it, and spoke.

"The hall will speak, then. All night, if it takes. And you—" the general's scarred throat moved as she swallowed something old, "—you will listen to all of it. That's the job you've chosen instead of the crown. It's heavier. I want you to know that before you start. Queens get to delegate grief. Carvers have to hold every name themselves."

"I know," Astrid said. "I've been holding names my whole life."

* * *

They began at moonrise, which filtered down through forty feet of reservoir water in long silver ropes, and they did not stop.

The magistrate spoke first, an hour on the injustice of the treaties, and Astrid listened. A baker spoke of her son, and Astrid listened. The soldier whose boy had died at fifteen spoke for four minutes total and then stood weeping in the silver light, and Astrid listened. They came to her in ones and twos, and she received them the way she received mourners in her shop: with attention, which is the only currency the dead accept.

Near midnight—or the water's version of midnight—Yeva settled onto a dry-ish ledge beside her and pressed a flask into her hand. The tea inside was terrible. Astrid drank it anyway.

"You realize," Yeva said, "that tomorrow you have to go to the forge."

Astrid looked across the hall, to the dais, to the small iron circlet waiting on its cushion with the patience of a trap. Around it, faint as breath on glass, the ring of nine generations of the eaten stood watch, and the innermost one—the young king with ink-stained fingers—was looking at her, and his lips were moving, and this time, this once, the words resolved:

*Thank you.*

Astrid closed her eyes. "Tomorrow," she said. "Tonight, I'm listening."

And the dead of Vessany, five thousand strong, kept talking until morning.
