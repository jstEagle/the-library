## 15. Twelfth Turn

The moon came up over the reservoir like a coin held underwater—washed pale, slightly bent at the edges, as though the water itself were trying to spend it and couldn't quite manage the transaction.

Astrid counted it from the palace balcony, because counting was what she did. Twelve turns had passed since the night in Thornfell when she'd found tonight's date inked in a dead man's book. One turn remained. Thirteen. The number that made ferryman ghosts go quiet and inquisitors reach for their irons.

"You're doing the thing with your fingers again," Yeva said from behind her. The old smuggler sat wrapped in a soldier's cloak she'd absolutely stolen, her blind eyes aimed at the moon with the confidence of someone who could see it better than anyone. "Counting. You count when you're frightened. You counted the stairs down to the flooded quarter—forty-one—and you counted the drowned bells we passed—nine—and I'd wager my good leg you've been counting moons since we left the valley."

"Forty-two stairs," Astrid said. "And it's not fear. It's arithmetic."

"It's fear wearing arithmetic's coat." Yeva scratched under the cloak at some itch the dead couldn't reach. "There's no shame in it. Fear's just the body keeping books. The trouble is when you let it do your sums for you."

Astrid didn't answer. Below them, Old Vessany lay drowned and patient, its streets green with silt, its windows full of dark water and darker ghosts. Somewhere down there, thousands of the dead were waiting on her decision—the ones who wanted her crowned, the ones who begged her to break the crown, and the growing third party who mostly just wanted everyone to stop shouting in the throne room.

She had come up here to be alone with the problem. She should have known better. In forty years of smuggling, Yeva Kroll had apparently never once allowed anyone to be alone with anything.

"The door," Astrid said finally.

Yeva went still in the way that only truly dangerous people can go still. "What about it?"

"I want to try it."

* * *

The door was not, strictly speaking, a door.

That was the first thing Astrid had learned about it, back when she'd gone looking in the prophecy book's marginalia and found a century of frustrated annotations. *Where is the door? No map shows it. The cartographer's hand shakes here.* The shaking hand had belonged, she now knew, to a younger Yeva Kroll, court cartographer, before the Regency took her eyes for her loyalty.

It stood—or seemed to stand—in the lowest gallery of the sunken palace, past the throne room, past the hall where the dead had argued for three nights until their voices wore thin as river glass. A doorway of black stone set into a wall of ordinary gray stone, with no hinges, no handle, and nothing behind it but more wall. It was older than the palace around it. Older, the dead said, than Vessany itself. The founders had built their capital around it the way a snail builds around its shell: carefully, reverently, and without ever quite understanding what they were carrying.

Astrid stood before it now, holding a lamp that burned underwater-proof oil Yeva had procured through channels she declined to describe, and felt like a woman knocking on a house where nobody lived.

Or rather—she put her palm flat against the black stone—like a woman knocking on a house where everybody lived, and all of them were listening, and none of them would answer until she said exactly the right thing.

"Well?" Yeva stood back near the dry archway, arms folded. She refused to come closer. Astrid had noticed that. The blind cartographer who had mapped every road in Vessany would not walk the last ten feet to the one door her maps had never shown.

"It's cold," Astrid reported. "Colder than the water."

"That's not an answer."

"I'm getting to it." She pressed harder. The stone was smooth as polished bone, and something about it made her fingertips ache the way they ached after a long day of carving—a deep, working ache, as if the stone recognized her hands and was asking them what they intended to make of themselves. "Door," she said aloud, feeling immediately foolish. "I am Astrid Voss. Bone-carver, of Thornfell. I have brought—" What? What had she brought? Three carved bones traded to a smuggler. A grandmother's letters. A hammer she'd carried across two mountain ranges because it was the only tool she trusted. "I have brought myself, and I would very much like to leave."

Nothing. Not even an echo. The lamp flame leaned toward the black stone, then away, like a dog deciding whether to bark.

"Try the name," Yeva said quietly.

Astrid's jaw tightened. "That's not my name."

"It is, though. That's the whole rotten heart of it." Yeva's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You've spent your whole life being nobody, girl, and you were so good at it that even now, standing in front of a door that opens for queens, you're still trying the nobody first."

Astrid looked at the door. The door, insofar as doors can look back, looked at her.

"Astrid Vessany," she said.

The word *Vessany* left her mouth and the entire gallery changed. The water in the walls shivered. Far above, through thirty feet of reservoir, the moonlight bent. And the black stone warmed under her palm—not much, not enough—but it *warmed*, the way a sleeping animal warms when it hears a familiar footstep, and Astrid's heart slammed once against her ribs with something that was almost hope.

Then the warmth stopped. Held. Considered. And cooled again, deliberate as a shopkeeper closing his till.

"No," Astrid whispered.

"What happened?" Yeva demanded. "What did it do? Describe everything, and don't you dare leave out the parts that frighten you."

"It knew me." Astrid's voice came out flat and strange. "It warmed when I said the name. And then it—it tested me. Like weighing coin. And it found me short."

"Short of what?"

Astrid pulled her hand back and stared at it. Her carver's hand. Her grandmother's hand. The hand drawn in a century-old book beside tonight's date.

"Short of a decision," she said.

* * *

They climbed back up through the palace by lamplight, past corridors where the drowned dead drifted like kelp and watched her pass with expressions she was learning to read. Hope was the hardest one. Hope was everywhere, worn thin, patched, handed down through forty years of waiting like a coat too small for anyone.

In the throne room, a delegation was waiting. There was always a delegation. Tonight it was Marshal Ondrek—a ghost whose chest bore the spear-wound that had killed him in the last defense of the city, and who had spent the intervening decades standing at attention beside a throne no one sat on—and a cluster of elder ghosts who had once been tailors and schoolteachers and one extremely persistent baker.

"Lady Astrid." Ondrek bowed. He always bowed. It made her want to scream. "The assembly asks whether you have reached a decision regarding the crown."

"I've reached a decision that I haven't reached a decision."

The baker ghost sighed. Even dead, he managed to convey the specific disappointment of a man whose bread has come out wrong. "The thirteenth turn comes in nine days."

"I can count."

"Then count this," said a new voice, thin and urgent, from among the elders. A woman stepped forward—had been a midwife in life, Astrid had learned; she had delivered half the royal line into the world and mourned every one of them out of it. "Child, the door does not wait forever. When the moon completes its turn, whatever you have chosen—that is what will be true. Forever. Crowned or broken. There is no leaving it for later. Later is the one country the Regency never managed to erase, and even it has borders."

"I know."

"Do you?" The midwife's ghostly face was kind and pitiless. "Because we have watched you for twelve turns, little carver. You carve beautifully. You listen beautifully. And you decide nothing. You hold every grief in both hands and refuse to set a single one down. That is not mercy. That is only weight."

Astrid opened her mouth to argue and found, to her horror, that she couldn't.

She thought of Thornfell. Of the life she had built there, plank by careful plank: the shop with its shutters half-closed, the work done well but never so well as to draw notice, the neighbors who knew her name but not her face, the lovers who had drifted away murmuring that she was hard to know. She had called it survival. She had called it wisdom. She had built an entire philosophy out of standing in doorways—present, useful, uncommitted—and she had been proud of it.

And now she stood in front of the oldest doorway in the world, and it was telling her the philosophy was a lie.

* * *

"You knew," Astrid said that night, in the chamber they'd claimed above the old armory. "Before we ever came down here. You knew the door wouldn't open halfway."

Yeva sat sharpening a knife she had no practical use for anymore, since her runners did the fighting and her reputation did the rest. The whetstone whispered. "I suspected."

"You drew the maps. You wrote in the margins. *The cartographer's hand shakes here.* You shook because you already understood what it was."

The whetstone stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the reservoir, pressing patiently against the walls, the way it had pressed for forty years.

"When I was court cartographer," Yeva said, "I was sent to survey the door. Official duty. The old king wanted it recorded—measured, dated, catalogued. So I measured it. And while I was measuring it, I put my hand on it, the way you did tonight, and I asked it to let me see what was on the other side." She turned the knife over. "Do you know what it showed me?"

"No."

"Nothing. Because I hadn't decided anything. I was a woman who kept every road open—that was my gift, that was why I was the best mapper in Vessany, because I never committed to any single route, I held them all. And the door looked at me the way a toll-keeper looks at a traveler with no destination." Her mouth twisted. "It doesn't hate the undecided, Astrid. That's the cruel part. It simply doesn't see us. We're not worth the trouble of refusing."

Astrid hugged her knees. "So it's the same for everyone. Claim or renounce. Queen or executioner. Nothing else."

"Crown or hammer," Yeva agreed. "Wear it or break it. And before you ask—yes, I've wondered for forty years which I would choose. And no, I still don't know. That's why I'm blind and alive instead of sighted and certain. Certainty gets people hanged in my experience. But—" she pointed the knife at her, gently, "—you don't get my experience. You get yours. Nine days of it."

"Nine days," Astrid said, "and then either I become a stranger wearing my body, or I kill the last living piece of a country that's asking me to save it."

"Now you're doing sums again."

"Somebody has to. All the accountants are dead."

Yeva laughed despite herself—a short, barking sound—and threw her a strip of dried fish, which Astrid caught and immediately regretted catching, because it meant the conversation was continuing.

"Eat," Yeva said. "Then sleep. Then tomorrow, you go back down to that door and you knock again, and the day after, and the day after that, if that's what you need. Knocking isn't deciding. But it's honest work, and you're a craftsman. Start there."

* * *

She knocked every day.

On the second day, she tried bringing the crown itself, carried wrapped in cloth like something contagious, and held it up to the black stone while reciting her intention to *consider* it thoroughly. The stone stayed cold as a creditor's heart.

On the fourth day, she brought the hammer instead, and announced—in front of a gallery full of ghosts who had come to watch, because the dead of Vessany had developed, over twelve turns, an insatiable appetite for drama—that she was considering breaking it, possibly soon, pending further reflection. The stone warmed slightly, the way it might warm to a familiar lie, and cooled.

On the sixth day she shouted at it. On the seventh she wept at it, which she hated herself for, and which changed nothing. On the eighth she brought nothing at all and simply stood there until her lamp burned out, and in the dark she heard the door—not open, not speak, but *breathe*, one long slow exhalation of patience, like a grandmother waiting for a child to finish a tantrum and remember her manners.

On the ninth morning, Yeva found her on the balcony again, counting clouds.

"Last turn tonight," the old woman said. "Moon rises full at sundown. After that, the date in your book stops being a threat and becomes a fact." She lowered herself onto the bench beside Astrid, joints crackling like green wood. "So. Last chance to surprise me. What's the plan?"

"I don't have one."

"Mm."

"I keep trying to find the third path, Yeva. The one where nobody loses. Where I'm not a queen and not a destroyer, where the crown stays whole and the dead stay waiting and I stay—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at all of herself, the careful invisible architecture of Astrid Voss— "*me*. And there isn't one. I've walked every road in this drowned city and the door is right. There are two roads and they run opposite directions and I have to pick one before moonrise or the picking gets done *to* me."

Yeva nodded slowly. "Your grandmother wrote something in her letters about that. Did you ever finish reading them?"

"I read them all."

"All of them?"

Astrid frowned. "All of—there were eleven letters. I read eleven."

"There were twelve." Yeva smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman about to spend the last coin of a very long savings. "The twelfth wasn't in the chest. I've been carrying it since the valley. I was told to give it to you when you were ready." She patted her cloak. "I'd hoped you'd be ready sooner. But stubbornness runs in your family, apparently, on both sides."

Astrid's heart was suddenly very loud. "Give it to me."

"When the moon's up." Yeva stood, unhurried, maddening. "Not before. Some things need darkness to read properly, and some daughters need to hear their grandmothers' last words with no light left to hide behind." She paused at the doorway, blind eyes finding Astrid with unnerving accuracy. "Wash your face, girl. Eat something. Tonight you meet yourself, one way or the other—and I've known you since the mountain passes, and I'll tell you what I told the Regency when they took my eyes."

"What's that?"

"They were burning the wrong thing." And she was gone, down the stairs, counting them under her breath out of forty-year habit, while Astrid sat alone on the balcony with the drowned country below her and the thirteenth moon climbing toward evening, and the terrible, liberating understanding settling over her at last like snow:

Whatever she carved tonight, she would have to live inside it forever.
