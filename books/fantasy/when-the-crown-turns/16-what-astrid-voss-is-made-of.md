## 16. What Astrid Voss Is Made Of

The dead of Vessany did not sleep, exactly. They settled—like snow, like dust, like the fine grey silt that had come down over the drowned streets when the reservoir waters first swallowed them and had never quite been disturbed since.

Astrid sat in the flooded scriptorium of the old palace with her boots propped on a desk that had spent forty years underwater, and she tried to remember the last time she had been alone.

Not alone in the way of an empty room. Alone in the way of *nobody watching*. Because there were thousands of them here—the patient dead of a drowned country—and every single one of them was watching her with the particular tenderness of people who had decided, without consulting her, that she was theirs.

"You're staring," she told the room.

The room stared back. A woman in scholar's robes drifted near the window arch, translucent as breath on glass. A soldier stood at attention by the door he had presumably failed to defend. Somewhere above, water dripped through a crack in the vaulted ceiling with the patience of a metronome, marking time for a country that no longer kept any.

"I'm going to work," Astrid announced, to no one, to everyone. "You can watch if you must. But I'd thank you not to comment."

They commented anyway. Not in words—the dead of Vessany had mostly lost the habit of words—but in that pressure against the air, that feeling like standing too close to a warm oven. Approval. Anticipation. Hope, which was the worst one, because hope had weight, and hers was carrying several tons of it.

She took out her knife and her kit and laid everything on the desk in its proper order: blades by size, rasps, the little jar of beeswax and ash she mixed herself, the oilcloth. Forty years of habit—or rather, twenty-six years of it, since she'd been nine years old and her grandmother had first put a bone blank in her hands and said, *the dead don't need much from us, but they need this.*

Then she reached into her satchel and drew out the last thing, and set it apart from the others, because it was different.

A bone blank. Long, clean, taken from the femur of an ox Yeva had butchered two valleys back. Unmarked. Waiting.

Her own memorial bone.

* * *

In Thornfell, she had carved perhaps four thousand of these. She knew the number because she kept a ledger, because a bone-carver who didn't keep a ledger was a bone-carver who eventually carved the same name twice, and the dead noticed such things. Four thousand names. She could recite maybe six hundred of them still, the ones that had mattered or the ones that had been strange enough to stick—a ferryman named Oddmund Twice-Drowned, a miller's daughter called Little Wren who had died at ninety-one and been mourned by everyone in town under a name nobody could explain.

And in all those years, in all four thousand bones, she had never once carved her own name. Not because of superstition—well, not only because of superstition—but because memorial bones were for the finished. For people whose story had stopped and could therefore be written down honestly. Astrid Voss's story had never stopped. It had just gone on and on, quiet and gray, a life lived at the exact volume where nobody turns to look at you.

That had been the whole art of it. That had been the whole *point*.

And now here she sat in the corpse of a kingdom, holding a blank bone, and the truth of her situation was this: in eleven days, either she would be a queen, or she would be nothing at all, and both of those options required her to write something down.

"Right," she said. "Let's see what we're working with."

* * *

The first cut is always the name. That was doctrine. Her grandmother had taught it, her grandmother's teacher had taught it, and somewhere back along that line someone had decided it and it had held ever since, the way good rules do—you couldn't honor a person until you'd named them, and you couldn't name them until you'd decided what they were worth naming.

Astrid held the blade over the blank surface and did not cut.

*Voss*, she thought. *Astrid Voss.* But even thinking it felt like wearing someone else's coat. Voss was the name her grandmother had chosen off a customs ledger forty years ago, plucked from a column of dead men because it was common and forgettable. Before that—

Before that there was another name, the true one, the royal one, the one the walls of Thornfell had started whispering the night she opened the chest, the one the dead here breathed at her constantly, reverently, like a prayer they'd been saving up. She had not spoken it aloud once. She wasn't going to start now.

"That's the trouble," she told the scholar-woman by the window, who had drifted closer and was pretending very badly not to read over her shoulder. "Everyone keeps handing me names. The Regency calls me a seditionist. Serel Dane called me—" she paused, remembering his face across the customs table, the signet of a vanished country glinting on his finger, "—something complicated. Your lot call me by a name that belonged to eleven other people before me, and look how well it went for them." She turned the blank bone in the lamplight. "I'd like to know what *I* call me. Is that so unreasonable?"

The dead, collectively, had no opinion. Or rather, they had ten thousand opinions and no consensus, which amounted to the same thing.

So she did what she always did when a commission stumped her. She started with the facts.

* * *

She began at the top of the bone, away from where the name would go, and cut small, plain letters—the honest inventory, the parts that wouldn't change:

**ASTRID VOSS. BONE-CARVER.**

Simple. True. She sat back and looked at it and felt, absurdly, like crying.

Because here was the thing she had figured out somewhere between the flooded market square and the sunken palace hall, wading through streets where ghosts pressed close to touch the hem of her coat: she had spent her whole life believing that invisibility was a wound. That being unremarkable, forgettable, *unseen*—that it was damage done to her, a missing piece, and that someday belonging would arrive and fill the hole.

And then belonging had arrived. It had arrived in the form of elders kneeling in the snow, and soldiers saluting, and thousands of dead voices saying her blood-name like it was the answer to a question they'd been asking for forty years. And it had felt—she could admit this now, alone, honestly—wonderful for about half a day. Warm as a bath.

And then she had touched the crown.

She flexed her right hand, remembering. The vision came back unbidden, the way it had every night since: queen after queen after king after queen, each face blurring into the next, each one stepping up bright-eyed and certain and then slowly, gently, *lovingly* erased—opinions smoothed out, griefs filed away, the crooked tooth fixed, the limp corrected, the inconvenient love quietly dropped, until what remained on the throne was a perfect vessel called Vessany and nothing else inside it at all. Eleven heirs. Eleven people who had walked into that crown carrying themselves and walked out carrying only the country.

The crown turned whoever wore it into someone else entirely. That was the prophecy's plain meaning, and she had finally understood it not as riddle but as simple product description.

"So," she said to the scholar-woman, "you'll understand why I'm not eager to add *QUEEN* to the list."

The scholar-woman tilted her head. And then, in the smallest ghost-voice Astrid had heard from any of them—most of the dead communicated in pressure and presence, but this one had clearly been a talker in life—she said:

"What else are you?"

Astrid blinked. "What?"

"You've listed your trade," the dead woman said. Her voice sounded like pages turning. "Your trade isn't you. What else?"

"That's—" Astrid gestured at the bone. "That's the whole point of a memorial. Name and trade. Maybe a deed, if they did one. My grandmother said flourishes were vanity."

"Your grandmother," said the scholar-woman, with the mild precision of someone who had spent her life correcting dissertations, "was hiding from a government. Hiding people compress themselves. You're allowed more than two lines now."

Astrid opened her mouth to argue and found, to her considerable annoyance, that she didn't have an argument. Just a lifetime of compression.

"All right," she said. "All right. More than two lines."

* * *

It took her the better part of the day, and she loved every minute of it, and she hated several of them.

She carved **DAUGHTER OF MAREN**, and had to stop for a while after that, because her mother's finger-bone was the key around her neck and some cuts go deeper than the knife goes. She carved **GRANDDAUGHTER OF THE WOMAN WHO CHOSE THE NAME VOSS OFF A LEDGER AND MADE IT MEAN SOMETHING**, which was too long, and she shortened it, and then lengthened it again, because the dead could wait and the truth shouldn't be rushed.

She carved **SHE LIED WELL TO AN INQUISITOR AND FELT BAD ABOUT IT AFTERWARD**, which made the soldier by the door make a sound that might have been a laugh, if soldiers' laughs survived death in any recognizable form.

She carved **SHE WAS AFRAID OF THE DARK UNTIL SHE WAS TWELVE AND IS STILL NOT FOND OF IT**. She carved **SHE TALKED TO HER WORK ALL DAY AND CALLED IT PROFESSIONAL NECESSITY**. She carved **SHE ONCE SHORTCHANGED NO ONE IN TWENTY-SIX YEARS OF TRADE, WHICH THE PEOPLE OF THORNFELL FOUND SUSPICIOUS AND RATHER ANNOYING**.

That one was important. She wanted it on the record. In Thornfell, honesty of that consistency was considered slightly deranged, and she had earned a great deal of side-eye for it, and she regretted none of it.

But the middle of the bone was where the real work was. Because the scholar-woman kept coming back—not hovering, exactly, but present, in the way of a good editor—and every time Astrid wrote something flattering about herself, the woman would drift a little closer, and Astrid would sigh, and cross it out, and write the truer thing underneath.

*SHE WAS BRAVE.* Crossed out. *SHE WAS AFRAID OF ALMOST EVERYTHING AND DID THE THINGS ANYWAY, WHICH IS THE ONLY KIND OF BRAVE THERE IS.*

*SHE LOVED HER TOWN.* Crossed out. *SHE LOVED HER TOWN AND NEVER LET IT LOVE HER BACK, BECAUSE SHE THOUGHT THAT WAS SAFER. IT WASN'T.*

*SHE BELONGED TO VESSANY.* She held the knife over that one a long time. Then she crossed it out too, gently, and wrote: *VESANY DOES NOT BELONG TO HER. SHE IS WILLING TO CARRY IT FOR A WHILE. THESE ARE DIFFERENT THINGS AND SHE SPENT ELEVEN DAYS LEARNING THE DIFFERENCE.*

By the time the light through the high water-windows had gone from green-gold to blue-grey, the bone was full from end to end, and it said nothing about crowns or thrones or prophecy, and it was, she thought, the best piece of work she had ever done. Better than the ferryman's bone. Better than Little Wren's.

Because it was the first one she'd ever carved of a person who was still deciding who to be.

* * *

"Now the name," she said.

The dead gathered. She felt them come in from the flooded streets and the drowned gardens and the palace hall above, felt the air thicken with them, ten thousand presences leaning in, and she understood that whatever she cut next, they would take it as a verdict. Their verdict. The name of their queen, or the renunciation of her.

"No," Astrid said aloud, and her voice cracked a little, and she let it. "No, I'm sorry. This one isn't for you. This one's for me."

She set the blade at the head of the bone, beneath the inventory, in the place of honor, and she thought about her grandmother choosing *Voss* off a ledger—a stolen name, a practical name, a name worn like a coat against the cold. And she thought about the blood-name whispering in the walls, a beautiful name, a heavy name, a name that had eaten eleven people alive.

And she thought: *a name is just the first line you carve. And I am the one holding the knife.*

She cut three words. Small ones. Plain ones. The same hand that had carved four thousand honest bones in a border town that never once looked twice at her.

When she finished, she held the bone up to the last of the light so the dead could read it, and read it herself, aloud, in a clear voice that echoed strangely off the flooded stone:

"Astrid Voss. Maker of names."

Not queen. Not heir. Not the girl in the prophecy or the last leaf of a cut-down tree. A maker of names—the person who stood at the border between the living and the dead and wrote down what things were *called*, which was, when you got right down to it, the oldest job there was, older than kings, older than crowns. Kings got remembered by what they owned. Carvers got remembered by what they *made*. She knew which side of that line she stood on.

The silence that followed was enormous.

Then the scholar-woman smiled—the first fully-formed expression Astrid had seen on any of them—and said, "Well. That will do nicely."

And the pressure in the air changed. Not disappointment. Something else. Something like ten thousand people exhaling at once, except that exhale had a direction to it, a lean, and Astrid felt it all pointing at her like a compass needle and thought: *oh. Oh, they think I've decided. They think the carving means I've chosen peace, that I'll walk away, that the door stays shut.*

Maybe they were right. Maybe that was what it meant.

She wrapped the bone in oilcloth with hands that were almost steady, and put it away at the bottom of her satchel, beneath the map and the letters and the key of her mother's finger-bone. Tomorrow she would count the moon again. Ten days now. Nine, if you counted the way the prophecy counted, which seemed designed to frighten.

She climbed the drowned stair toward the dry halls, past the soldier, who saluted her—not as a queen, she noticed. As a fellow soldier, one craftswoman to another, someone who did hard precise work in bad conditions. She returned it.

At the top of the stairs, in the dark of the dry corridor, she stopped.

Because she had lied to the dead. Not in the carving—every word of the carving was true. But in what she'd left out, and she was suddenly, terribly aware of it, the way you become aware of a stone in your boot only when you stop walking.

*Astrid Voss. Maker of names.*

There was one name left to make. One blank still waiting, down in the treasury vault, on a circlet of old gold that had eaten eleven people and was hungry for a twelfth. And the door out of Vessany would open for no one who refused to choose—no third path, no middle way, no clever compromise. Claim it or break it. Wear it or shatter it. Those were the only lines the prophecy would carve.

She looked back down the dark stair, toward the flooded city full of patient dead, toward the forge in the palace's east wing that she had passed three times already and pretended not to see—an old royal forge, still sound, still stocked, with hammers hanging on the wall that had shaped crowns and could just as easily, she suspected, unshape one.

"Well," Astrid Voss said to the darkness, in the tone she used for difficult commissions. "Best pick out a good hammer, then."
