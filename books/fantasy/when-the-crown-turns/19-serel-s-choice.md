## 19. Serel's Choice

The cell they put him in smelled of wet rope and old fish, which Serel Dane considered a fitting commentary on his career.

He had been arrested at the waterline with his hands still raised in the gesture of a man giving orders—which, in fairness, he had been. The order he'd been refusing was *open the sluices*. The order he'd been following was nothing at all, just standing there, boots in the mud, watching the drowned city glimmer beneath three feet of green water like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Captain Voss—no relation to Astrid, though the universe clearly enjoyed its little jokes—had read the arrest warrant aloud with the enthusiasm of a man reading a laundry list. Then two soldiers had marched him to the pump-house, locked him in, and posted a guard whose main qualification appeared to be a magnificent ability to fall asleep sitting up.

Serel sat on an upturned crate and took inventory of himself, as he'd been trained to do. One inquisitor, formerly of the Third Circle. One signet ring of a country that officially did not exist, currently burning a hole in his pocket where he'd hidden it from the arresting officers. Zero faith left in the institution that had raised him from the age of nine, fed him, taught him letters and law and how to make a man confess to things he hadn't done.

*Well,* he thought. *Not zero. Perhaps one-tenth. The soup had been good.*

The lock rattled.

Serel stood, because a man about to be executed or interrogated ought at least to die standing, and the door swung open on the round, apologetic face of Corporal Brannic—the same Brannic who had once, in another life, carried Serel's luggage across three provinces and complained about it in four languages.

"Sir," Brannic whispered, "you'll want to come with me. Quickly, quietly, and sir—I want it noted I think this is a terrible idea."

"Noted," Serel said. "Whose idea is it?"

"Mine, mostly. And Hesk's. And Marta's. And, if we're being honest, half the garrison." Brannic shifted his weight, uncomfortable. "We heard what the orders were, sir. For the valley villages. For the people still down in the water when the sluices open again. We're border men. Half of us have grandmothers who speak the old tongue when they've been drinking. Nobody wants to—" He stopped, jaw working. "Nobody wants to be the hand that does it."

Serel looked at him for a long moment. "You understand that helping me is treason."

"Yes, sir."

"And that treason at the Regency's convenience is called loyalty, but treason against its convenience is called hanging."

"Yes, sir." Brannic almost smiled. "But you always said the law wasn't the same thing as the Regency. You said it in the training yard, first year. You said, 'The law is a map, lads, and the Regency is only one cartographer, and cartographers can lie.' We remembered."

Serel had no memory of saying this. It sounded exactly like something he would have said, back when he'd believed it, before forty years of erased maps had taught him that sometimes the cartographers were simply following orders too.

"Where are my guards?" he asked instead.

"Asleep. Marta drugged the tea. She says hello and also that you owe her for the tea."

"I owe everyone for everything," Serel said, and followed him out into the rain.

* * *

The reservoir edge at night was a black mirror with teeth. Rain needled the surface; somewhere below, streets and steeples and a palace full of patient dead lay drowned in their second erasure, waiting for the third.

The sluice house stood on the near bank—a squat stone building with iron wheels and chains thick as a man's wrist. Two engineers worked there under lamplight, and above them, on the ridge, the garrison's remaining officers kept a cold watch. The order had come down from the capital in the Regency's finest bureaucratic prose: *reopen the sluices at dawn, raise the water level six feet, hold until further notice.* Further notice meaning forever. Meaning drown it deeper. Meaning whatever slept in those ruins should never again have air enough to whisper.

And the valley villages downstream would lose their winter pastures. And anyone still in the ruins—and Serel knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent weeks chasing her, that *she* was still in the ruins—would be sealed under twelve feet of dark water with no charm strong enough to breathe through.

Brannic led him along the reed beds to a point where the bank dipped low, and there, crouched behind a fallen willow, were Hesk and Marta and a dozen soldiers Serel had trained, fed, yelled at, and occasionally defended before tribunals. They looked up at him with the particular expression of children showing a parent something broken and hoping it can be fixed.

"The sluice house," Serel said quietly. "That's the choke point. If the engineers don't turn the wheels, the order fails tonight. By dawn the whole chain of command will know someone refused, and the Regency will either send a bigger force or—"

"Or someone will have made it impossible," Marta said. She was a small woman with a scarred chin and the patience of a stone wall. "Chains jam, sir. Wheels rust. Paperwork goes missing. There are ways to slow a machine that don't require anyone to stand in front of it."

"There are," Serel agreed. "And then there's the faster way, which is a man standing in front of it and refusing to move, loudly, with witnesses, so that every soldier on that ridge has to decide, right now, whether he drowns a city or disobeys an order." He looked out at the black water. "I've spent my career making people confess. I know exactly how much a single stubborn voice is worth."

Hesk frowned. "You mean to get yourself arrested again, sir."

"I mean to get myself arrested *properly* this time. With testimony. On the record. In front of witnesses who'll carry the story whether the Regency likes it or not." He pulled the signet ring from his pocket and turned it over in his palm—the crowned gate of Vessany, worn nearly smooth. "They sent me here to destroy a book. I'm going to give them a library."

Marta snorted. Brannic crossed himself in the old way, thumb to brow, which no one had done openly in Thornfell for forty years.

"You could run, sir," Brannic said softly. "Yeva Kroll's people could have you over the mountains by Thursday. Nobody would blame—"

"Nobody would blame me," Serel said, "and that's precisely the problem. I've been unblamed my whole life. It turns out I can't stand it." He straightened his coat, checked that his seals and warrants were in order—he would need them, evidence of everything—and added, with the dry tone that had once made first-year trainees laugh nervously: "Besides, I'm told the company down there is excellent. Thousands of ghosts and a bone-carver. Far better conversationalists than the Third Circle ever was."

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, and with it came Serel Dane walking up the sluice-house steps with his hands empty and his voice carrying across the water like a bell.

He planted himself between the engineers and the great iron wheel, and he announced, in the formal register reserved for tribunal proceedings, that he was Serel Dane, Inquisitor of the Third Circle, and that he was refusing a lawful order on the grounds that the order was a crime.

The engineers stopped. The ridge went silent. Somewhere a crow complained about the interruption.

"The order is lawful," said Captain Voss, coming down the slope with his warrant case under his arm, moving with the weary patience of a man who had done this dance at three other postings. "Step aside, Dane. You're under arrest already. Don't compound it."

"I'll compound it thoroughly," Serel said. "Let the record show: on this date, the Regency ordered the raising of the Vessany reservoir by six feet, knowing—knowing, Captain, because I have read the survey reports, and so have you—that the raising floods the winter grazing of four villages, displaces some three hundred souls, and seals beneath the waterline any persons remaining in the ruins of Old Vessany. I have counted those persons. There are at least two living. And there are—" he paused, and decided that truth, having brought him this far, deserved the whole of itself— "there are thousands of others. Dead ones. Vessani dead. Who have waited forty years for their country to remember them, and whom this Regency has decided to drown a second time rather than let them rest."

Silence. Rain on stone. The crow again, more insistent.

"Ghosts," Voss said flatly.

"I had the same reaction," Serel said. "I invite you to go down and check. Take a lamp. Knock politely." A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but its cousin—moved along the ridge, and Serel seized it the way he'd been trained to seize hesitation in an interrogation room, gently, and turn it. "Every man up there has a grandmother. Every grandmother has stories. Ask them tonight what language she curses in when she burns the bread. Ask them why the old songs count to thirteen. You already know the answer. You've always known. The Regency didn't erase Vessany from your hearts—it only asked you to stop writing it down. And today it asks you to write it down in water, on top of the drowned, and call that justice."

Voss stepped closer, lowering his voice. "Dane. I liked you. You were decent, for an inquisitor. Go inside, withdraw the statement, and I'll report you as confused. Ill. It happens in the marsh country. You'll do six months' leave and come back with your pension intact."

"And the wheel turns anyway."

"And the wheel turns anyway."

"Then I'll stand here," Serel said, "and be confused in front of witnesses."

What happened next, he would spend many long nights reconstructing, and never quite get the order right. But roughly: Marta emerged from the reed bed with a wrench the size of a forearm and declared the sluice mechanism unserviceable. Roughly: Hesk and six others walked down from the ridge and stood beside him without a word, in a line, facing the water. Roughly: the engineers looked at each other, looked at the growing line of soldiers, and quietly sat down on the step to smoke, which in the grammar of border garrisons meant *we are not turning that wheel.*

Roughly: Captain Voss looked at all of it, and at the black water, and at the line of men, and did the arithmetic that every officer eventually does between his career and his soul.

Then, from far out on the reservoir, came a sound.

It was faint. It was, by every natural law, impossible. It was singing—low, layered, in a language with too many soft vowels, rising off the water like mist off a spring field. The Vessani dead, thousands of them, in the flooded streets of their beautiful corpse of a country, singing.

No one moved for a long time. Even the crow shut up.

"That's not an echo," Marta said at last, very quietly.

"No," Serel said. His throat was tight. "That's a choir."

Captain Voss closed his warrant case. "Sergeant," he said to no one in particular, "note the time. Note that the sluice mechanism is unserviceable. Note that repairs will require... assessment." He looked at Serel, and something in his face had gone old and tired and free. "Assessment can take months. Get off my bank, Dane, before I remember I'm supposed to arrest you properly."

"I'm not getting off your bank," Serel said. "I'm surrendering. Formally. To the civil authority at Thornfell, for trial, with full testimony. That's the only way this survives the week—if I stand in a courtroom and say all of it, under seal, where they can't simply shoot me and burn the notes." He held out his wrists, almost cheerfully. "Arrest me properly, Captain. Do it by the book. The book is the only weapon I've got left, and I intend to use every page."

Voss stared at him. Then, slowly, he took out his irons.

"For what it's worth," he said, snapping the first cuff closed, "my grandmother cursed in the old tongue. Thirteen counts. Every time."

"I know," Serel said. "I interviewed her in 'forty-one. Lovely woman. Terrible liar. She told me everything and I wrote it down and buried the report." He winced. "I intend to dig it up."

* * *

The wagon to Thornfell took two days, and Serel spent both of them writing.

They gave him paper because he asked for it with such polite, relentless formality that denying him seemed more trouble than granting it—a trick he had taught a hundred young inquisitors and now, at last, used on himself. He wrote the order for the flooding, verbatim, from memory. He wrote the survey reports. He wrote the names of the valley villages, and the number of souls in each, and the dates the Regency's own clerks had recorded their grandparents' births in the old tongue before being ordered to stop. He wrote about the book of prophecy, and the girl with ink-stained fingers who had lied to him beautifully, and the moment in her shop when he'd realized she was protecting something worth more than the Regency's maps.

He wrote about the singing.

By the second evening his escort—Brannic and one other, the rest having developed sudden, convenient duties elsewhere—sat across from him in the wagon bed while the mountains went purple behind them.

"They'll hang you," Brannic said. Not cruelly. Just checking the arithmetic.

"Probably," Serel agreed. "Or they'll try, and discover that a public trial of a Third Circle inquisitor means public testimony, and public testimony means the whole country hears the words *Vessany* and *drowned* in the same sentence for the first time in forty years." He tapped his stack of pages. "They can hang the witness. They can't un-hear the hearing. That's the whole trick of law, Brannic—it cuts both ways, and the Regency has spent four decades pretending it doesn't. I'm going to remind them."

"And if they just... lose the paperwork? Disappear you quietly?"

"Then Marta has copies. And Hesk. And Yeva Kroll, who I suspect has copies of things the Regency hasn't even done yet." He smiled, thin and real. "I spent my life teaching men that a confession extracted in fear is worthless. Time I gave the Regency a taste of the opposite: a confession offered freely, in daylight, that they cannot make worthless no matter what they do to me."

Brannic was quiet a while. Then: "The bone-carver. Voss. You chased her halfway across the country."

"I did."

"You going to testify for her too?"

Serel looked out at the darkening road, at the distant lights of Thornfell beginning to prickle through the dusk—her town, her little shop with its shavings and its lamps, the place where a woman had carved names for the dead because no one else would.

"I'm going to testify *about* her," he said. "There's a difference. She doesn't need my protection anymore—whatever she's found down in that water, it's bigger than anything I could shield her from. What she needs is the world outside the water to change shape around her while she works. Trials change shapes." He gathered his pages, squared their edges with the fussiness of a lifelong clerk. "Besides, I owe her. She saw what I was before I did."

The wagon rolled on toward the lights. Behind them, far off, the mountains held their drowned country, and Serel Dane—arrested, ruined, certain for the first time in thirty years—began drafting his opening statement.

In Thornfell, unknown to him, a bone-carver was lighting her lamp and reaching for her knife, and the moon overhead was one night short of full.
