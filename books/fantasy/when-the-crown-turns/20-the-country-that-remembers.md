## 20. The Country That Remembers

The road down from the high passes was kinder than the road up had been, which Astrid suspected was the mountains' way of apologizing.

Yeva walked ahead of her with the particular confidence of a woman who had been walking these roads since before they were erased—staff tapping stone, chin lifted as though she could see the whole valley spread out below them, though her eyes had gone dark and milk-pale forty years ago in a Regency cell. She navigated by memory and by the smell of pine resin and the particular way the wind came off the reservoir, and she navigated better than most sighted people Astrid had known.

"You're limping," Yeva said, without turning around.

"I'm favoring my left ankle. There's a difference."

"There is not."

"There is. A limp is involuntary. This is a negotiation."

Yeva snorted, but she slowed anyway, and Astrid loved her a little for pretending not to have done it.

They had left Old Vessany three days ago, after the last of the dead had crossed through the door in the reservoir wall. Three days of quiet that should have felt like grief and instead felt like something Astrid didn't yet have a word for—the silence after a held breath, the silence of a room where someone has finally stopped suffering. The drowned city was only water now. The dead were only names again, which was all they had ever asked to be.

And Astrid Voss, who had spent thirty years making herself small enough to slip through the world unnoticed, was walking toward a valley full of people who knew her face, knew her name, and intended to keep knowing both for the rest of her natural life.

She found, to her considerable surprise, that she wasn't dreading it.

* * *

The village of Haldenmark announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and boiling cabbage and the particular yeasty sweetness of bread left to rise too near a fire—and then by sound: children shouting somewhere above them, a dog with strong opinions about strangers, the rhythmic knock of a loom. The houses were built into the hillside the way old Vessani houses always were, half-buried, stubborn, their turf roofs already going green with the spring.

The elders were waiting at the village gate. They had been waiting, Astrid realized, since the runners came through with the news—since Serel's soldiers turned back, since the flooding began and stopped and began and stopped, since word arrived that the queen had taken a hammer to the crown of Vessany on the eve of the thirteenth turn and broken it across the anvil like a bad hinge.

Old Maret stood at the front of them, ninety if she was a day, leaning on a stick carved with the seven-pointed star of the old royal house. When she saw Astrid coming up the road, she did not kneel.

Astrid had braced herself for kneeling. She had braced herself for titles, for prostration, for the terrible weight of being looked at by a hundred people at once. What she had not braced for was Maret reaching out and taking her by both wrists, turning her hands over, and examining them with the critical eye of a woman who had judged a great many things in her long life.

"Carver's hands," Maret said. "Calluses here, here. Ink under the nails." She looked up. "Good. We've had queens. What we need is someone who can work."

"I broke your crown," Astrid said. It came out more defensive than she'd meant it to.

"We heard." Maret released her wrists. Behind her, the other elders watched—not with anger, Astrid saw, but with the careful attention of people deciding whether to be relieved or devastated, waiting to find out which one they were allowed. "Tell me why."

Astrid told her. She told it plainly, the way she'd learned to tell things to the dead: the vision in the sunken palace, every heir who had worn the crown and been worn away by it in return, until nothing remained of any of them but the shape of a throne. She told them about the assembly of ghosts, the ones who had begged her to take it up and the ones who had begged her to break it, and how in the end she had understood that the crown had never belonged to Vessany at all—it had belonged to whoever wore it, and it ate them, and it would have eaten her.

"And then I broke it," she finished, "and the dead went through the door, and they rested. All of them. Forty years of waiting, ended in an afternoon."

The silence afterward was very long. Somewhere behind the elders, the opinionated dog had given up barking and was chewing on something it probably shouldn't have been.

Maret leaned on her stick. "My grandmother waited in this valley her whole life for a queen to come back," she said. "She died waiting. My mother waited too. I confess I expected to wait as well." Her voice wavered, and steadied. "But my grandmother also used to say that Vessany was never the crown. Vessany was the names. Every name spoken, every name remembered. The Regency took our maps and our borders and our king, but they never once managed to take a single name out of a living mouth."

She turned to the other elders. "Well?" she said. "Are we relieved or are we devastated?"

An old man named Torvald, who had one clouded eye and the air of a man who had been disagreeing with Maret for sixty years, raised his hand. "Relieved," he said firmly. "I never wanted to kneel to anyone younger than me. Terrible for the knees. Terrible for the character."

The laughter that went through the little crowd was ragged and wet-eyed and real, and Astrid stood in the middle of it and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched so long she'd forgotten it was there.

Belonging, it turned out, did not feel like being seen. It felt like being *usable*. Like having hands that someone needed.

* * *

They gave her the loft above the weaving house, which had the advantage of being warm and the disadvantage of containing the loom, which Torvald's daughter operated from before dawn with the steady enthusiasm of a woman possessed. Astrid slept through it eventually. You could sleep through anything if you'd spent a week breathing through a bone charm at the bottom of a drowned city.

Yeva took the room next to hers and immediately began reorganizing it.

"You don't live here," Astrid pointed out, watching the blind cartographer feel along the walls with the proprietary confidence of a returning exile. "You have a life in Thornfell. A route. Runners. A whole smuggling operation."

"I had a life in Thornfell," Yeva said. "I had a duty in Vessany. I finished the duty. Now I intend to have a life in both places, because I am old enough to do what I want and too old to care what anyone thinks about it." She set her staff in the corner by touch, precisely, the way she did everything. "Besides. Someone has to draw the new map."

"What new map?"

"The one you're going to make necessary." Yeva tapped the wall, once, meaningfully. "You're going to carve every name we can recover. Every Vessani name. From the registers, from the grave-markers, from the mouths of the old ones before the old ones finish dying. And when you have them—" she shrugged, invisible shrug, audible somehow anyway, "—someone will want to know where they all lived. Where they married. Where they're buried. Names without places are just a list. Names with places are a country."

Astrid sat down on the edge of the bed and thought about that for longer than she meant to.

"A country made of memory instead of territory," she said finally. "That was what I told the dead. In the hall. Before the vote."

"I know what you told the dead. One of them told me. They tell me everything; it's very annoying." Yeva smiled, faintly, in the direction of Astrid's voice. "I'm holding you to it. I spent forty years memorizing roads for a country that stopped existing, and I'll be damned—I'll be *damned*, Astrid—if the only thing those roads were ever good for was one walk and a funeral."

* * *

The work began the next morning, and it began, appropriately, with bones.

Not human ones—at least, not mostly. The valley villages had kept, through four decades of erasure, a strange and scattered archive: cattle bones scored with names, door lintels carved with family lines, a church beam in Haldenmark's chapel covered edge to edge in baptismal records in the old Vessani script, which half the village could still read aloud and none of them could write anymore. The Regency had burned the paper archives. Paper burns. Bone, it turned out, was harder to burn than anyone in the capital had anticipated—a fact Astrid found deeply satisfying, professionally speaking.

She set up in the chapel, because it had good light and a table, and because the dead of the valley seemed to appreciate having somewhere official to loiter. They didn't whisper to her much anymore. Since the crossing, the whispers had gone quiet everywhere, like birdsong after a storm—but she still felt them sometimes, a pressure at the edge of her awareness, the way you feel someone standing behind you in an empty room. Patient. Interested. Waiting to see what she'd make of them.

The system, once she worked it out, was simple. Each recovered name got its own bone—a sheep scapula for the farmers, a deer antler tine for the hunters, river stones drilled and threaded on cord for the ones whose families had no animals to spare. On each piece she carved the name in the old script, and beneath it, small, the place: Haldenmark, or Sorel's Reach, or the drowned streets of Old Vessany itself, for the ones whose bodies had never come up from the reservoir.

It was slow work. It was going to be years of work. There were, by Yeva's best estimate and the chapel beam's corroborating testimony, upward of eleven thousand names.

Astrid carved the first one on the second morning, and her hands shook, and she let them.

*Vessa Ilmari Kollen.* Born in the spring of the year the maps burned. Drowned at nineteen in the reservoir's rising, along with her street, her school, and everyone she'd ever practiced dances with. Astrid had met her ghost in the flooded capital—had walked beside her for three streets, the girl humming a song about plum blossoms, patient as stone, waiting forty years for someone to come and say her name properly.

Astrid said it properly now. Out loud, in the empty chapel, in the old pronunciation that made the middle syllable fall soft instead of sharp.

Somewhere in the walls, something exhaled.

* * *

The children discovered her by the end of the first week, as children discover everything: completely, immediately, and without respecting any of the boundaries adults construct against exactly this outcome.

It started with one—Torvald's granddaughter, a girl of perhaps eight named Liss, who had the round serious face of someone taking detailed mental notes for future use. Liss appeared in the chapel doorway on a Tuesday, watched Astrid carve for the length of an entire name, asked four questions (Why bones? Why not wood? Do the dead mind? Can I watch?), received four answers, and returned the next day with two friends. By Friday there were nine of them. By the following week, Astrid had instituted a formal apprenticeship structure mostly as a means of getting them to stop touching the knife drawer unsupervised.

"You understand," she told them solemnly, on the first proper lesson day, "that this is sacred work, and also that if any of you cut yourselves I will be explaining it to your grandmothers, and I would rather face the entire Regency army."

"We won't cut ourselves," said Liss, who cut herself within the hour, and was bandaged, and lectured, and returned the next day anyway, bandaged finger held up like a medal.

But they learned. Goddess, they learned—faster than any apprentice Astrid had trained in Thornfell, hungry for it in a way that made her throat tight. These were children who had grown up speaking their grandparents' forbidden dialect in whispers, who had been taught to flatten their vowels in town, who knew the word *Vessany* the way you know a swear word: powerful, dangerous, not for daylight. And now here was a woman with a famous face carving their ancestors' names into bone in broad view of the road, and no soldiers came, and nobody vanished in the night, and the sky stayed up.

The world had changed. Not all the way—not yet, not everywhere—but here, in this valley, on this ground, the old names could be spoken at full volume, and the children spoke them like they'd been waiting their whole short lives for permission.

Which, Astrid realized, they had.

* * *

Serel Dane arrived in the third week.

He came alone, on foot, in plain clothes, carrying nothing but a satchel and the particular hunched posture of a man who has recently lost his career, his freedom, and most of his certainties, and is trying to figure out what a person is supposed to be afterward. The villagers spotted him a mile off—the Regency walk gave him away, that measured heel-strike of a man trained to enter rooms like a verdict—and by the time he reached the gate, half of Haldenmark was watching from doorways with folded arms.

Astrid met him at the chapel steps.

"I'm not here on business," he said, before she could speak. His voice was rougher than she remembered. "There's no business. I resigned my commission. Formally. In writing. It's being treated as desertion, which—" a thin, bitter almost-smile, "—is accurate enough. I came to testify. To your people, to anyone who'll listen, to whatever tribunal eventually forms when the Regency finishes wobbling. I have names. Dates. Orders signed. What was planned for these valleys." He set the satchel down on the chapel step, carefully, like it might go off. "And I came to ask if there's work here. For a man with useful hands and no country."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. The last time she'd seen him, he'd been holding a Regency line at gunpoint while the water rose, buying her minutes with his arrest.

"You know," she said, "the dead talk about you."

He went pale. "They—what?"

"Not badly. Mostly they argue about you. Whether you're brave or just late." She picked up the satchel and handed it back to him. "Bring this inside. If you're going to testify, you'll do it properly, with tea, and you'll start with the part where you stalled the flooding order, because Maret has questions and Maret does not forgive evasions."

Inside, the apprentices stared at the former inquisitor with the frank, unblinking hostility of children who had heard what his kind did. Serel bore it admirably. Liss, after ten minutes of intense observation, decided he seemed sad rather than evil, and offered him a whittling scrap as a gesture of provisional goodwill—an offer he accepted with the gravity of a man receiving a knighthood.

Yeva, arriving later, took one look at him and delivered her verdict with characteristic bluntness: "Good. We needed someone who knows how the Regency thinks. He can carry the map cases. He has a trustworthy face, which is wasted on honesty, but we'll manage."

* * *

By midsummer, the monument had a shape.

It didn't look like monuments were supposed to look. There was no statue, no plinth, no bronze general pointing at a horizon. Instead, there was the long wall of the weaving house, newly faced with pale timber, and on that timber, row upon row upon row, the bones themselves—mounted on pegs, each one angled to catch the morning light, each one bearing a name in the old script and a place beneath it. Eleven thousand pegs. The villagers had started making them in the evenings, the whole valley at it, grandmothers and children and the formerly-inquisitorial alike, until the pegs piled up faster than Astrid could carve and she had to institute a waiting list, which she found both absurd and moving.

People came from the other villages to see it. They came quietly, in twos and threes, and stood before the wall running their fingers along the rows, finding their own names, their mothers' names, the names of streets that were underwater now. An old woman from Sorel's Reach found her brother, drowned at fourteen, and stood before his bone for an hour, and when she finally turned around she said, "He hated cold water. He complained about it constantly. Nobody's written that down anywhere. Everyone remembers him as brave." And Astrid got out a fresh bone and carved, beneath the name, in small letters: *Hated cold water. Complained constantly.*

That, she decided, was the whole point. History kept the brave parts. Memory kept the complaining. A country made of memory had room for both.

The dead, for their part, had mostly stopped haunting. But on summer evenings, when the light came low and gold across the wall of bones, Astrid would sometimes feel them at the edge of things—not trapped, not waiting, just *near*, the way family lingers at the edge of a wedding. Watching the children learn the script. Listening to the old dialect spoken at full volume in the market. Checking, gently, idly, that the names were being kept.

They were. That was the answer she'd have given, if any of them ever asked directly. They were being kept.

* * *

One evening in late summer, Yeva found her at the wall after the apprentices had gone home, running her thumb along a finished row, checking her own work the way she checked everything twice.

"Do you regret it?" Yeva asked suddenly. "The crown. Any of it."

"No." Astrid said it without hesitation, which surprised her a little; six months ago the question would have hollowed her out. "Do you?"

"I regret forty years of thinking the maps mattered more than the people reading them." She tilted her head back toward the wall, listening to it the way she listened to everything—wind through bone, faint and dry, like pages turning. "This is better than any map I ever drew. And I drew some very good maps."

They stood together in the gold light for a while, the blind woman and the bone-carver, neither saying anything, both of them home.

Then, from the direction of the village gate, hoofbeats—fast ones, urgent ones, the kind of riding that means news rather than travel. A runner from the south road, lathered horse, dust-caked coat, pounding up the track toward the chapel square with a leather dispatch case strapped across his chest and an expression Astrid recognized from the worst weeks of the journey: the expression of a man carrying something that couldn't wait for morning.

Yeva heard him first, the way she always did. "That's one of mine," she said. "Thornfell colors. He wouldn't ride like that unless—"

The runner pulled up hard at the foot of the chapel steps, dismounted badly, and handed Astrid a sealed packet with shaking hands. The wax bore the seal of the Regency chancery—which meant nothing good—and beneath it, in a hurried hand she recognized as Serel's old correspondent, a single line visible through the broken seal:

*The Council has fallen. They are asking for the woman who broke the crown.*

Astrid stood very still, the packet open in her hands, the summer light going amber on the wall of eleven thousand names behind her.

"They're asking," Yeva repeated softly, beside her. "Or they're summoning."

Astrid looked at the words again. *Asking.* After everything—the erasure, the drowning, the flooding orders, the tribunals that hadn't happened yet—they were *asking*.

"Well," she said, to Yeva, to the wall, to the patient dead lingering at the edge of the light, "I suppose I'd better decide what I'm willing to be asked for."
