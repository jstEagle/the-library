## 21. The Thirteenth Moon

The night before the thirteenth turn, Astrid carved a bone for herself.

Not a memorial—she'd done that already, months ago in the drowned dark of Old Vessany, when she still believed the prophecy might keep its appointment. This was different. This was a *thank-you* bone, which was not a thing bone-carvers traditionally made, because the dead were rarely in a position to receive them. But the dead of Vessany had never been traditionalists.

She sat at her workbench in Thornfell with the window open to the summer dark, and she carved into a piece of river-ox rib the words that had been sitting in her chest since the crossing:

*For the eleven thousand and more, who waited forty years and forgave me for taking so long.*

The shop smelled of shavings and lamp oil and, faintly, of the mountain herbs Yeva chewed like an old woman half her age, which she was not, being older than that by a comfortable margin. Yeva sat in the corner chair she had quietly annexed upon arrival and never surrendered, her blind eyes turned toward the window as if the moonrise were something you could hear coming.

"You're grinding your teeth," Yeva said.

"I'm concentrating."

"You're grinding your teeth *while* concentrating. It's very efficient."

Astrid set down her knife. Outside, Thornfell was doing what Thornfell always did on a summer night: dogs arguing over garbage, the ferry bell clanking in the wind off the water, someone's grandmother singing badly two doors down. An ordinary town on an ordinary night. Except that tomorrow, if a century-old book inked by a dead woman's trembling hand was to be believed, the moon would complete its thirteenth turn, and Astrid Voss was supposed to die.

She found, examining the feeling honestly, that she was mostly annoyed about it.

Not afraid—that had burned off somewhere between the flooded palace and the door in the reservoir wall, spent like a candle down to the stub. What remained was the irritation of a craftsman handed a bad commission. She had broken the crown. She had opened the door. She had stood for six hours while eleven thousand ghosts filed past her, each one pausing to touch her hands—the hands from the drawing, the carver's hands—and say nothing, because there was nothing left to say, only warmth passing through like light through glass. She had done everything the prophecy asked except die, and now it wanted her to die anyway? Sloppy work. She'd have refused the commission.

"You're smiling," Yeva said. "That's worse than the teeth."

"I was thinking that if I do die tomorrow, I'm going to haunt whoever wrote that book."

"Vessa the Seeress." Yeva pronounced the name with the careful reverence of a woman who had once drawn maps for a living and therefore held strong opinions about people who wrote things down carelessly. "Dead two hundred years. Very thorough haunting. You'll be busy."

"I'll manage. I have references."

Yeva laughed—a dry rustle, like wind through map-paper—and then went quiet in the way she did when she was deciding whether to say a thing. Astrid had learned to wait out these silences. They always ended in either wisdom or insult, and both were useful.

"When I was court cartographer," Yeva said finally, "there was a saying in the palace. *The crown turns, but the country remembers.* We thought it meant the crown made kings forget themselves and the land kept the true shape of things underneath. Rivers remember their courses. Mountains remember being mountains." She tilted her head toward Astrid. "I think we had it backwards. I think it meant the crown turns whoever wears it—but the country remembers *them*. Whoever they were before. Whatever they chose. That's the part that lasts."

"That's very poetic for a smuggler."

"I was paid by the word in my previous career. Old habits."

Outside, the first edge of the moon cleared the rooftops—not yet full, not yet turned, just a pale coin beginning its last ascent. Somewhere in the walls of the shop, faint as breath, the dead who had chosen to linger stirred. Not all of them had crossed. A few hundred had stayed behind, the way some guests linger after a funeral because leaving feels too much like the thing being mourned. They didn't haunt Astrid. They simply kept her company, the way they had kept the whole drowned country company for forty years, patient as sediment.

One of them whispered against the window glass, so softly that even Astrid barely caught it:

*Moon's rising, little carver.*

"I know," she said aloud.

Yeva raised an eyebrow. "Talking to the dead again. People will talk."

"People have been talking since chapter three," Astrid said, and then frowned, because she had no idea why she'd said that, and decided to blame the nerves.

* * *

The moon took its time.

It always does, on the nights that matter. Astrid tried to work and couldn't; tried to sleep and wouldn't; finally gave up around midnight and sat in the doorway of her shop with a blanket around her shoulders and a cup of Yeva's terrible mountain tea going cold in her hands, watching the thirteenth moon climb over Thornfell like a slow white judgment.

Yeva sat beside her. Neither spoke much. There was a particular quality to silence shared with someone who has walked roads that don't exist with you, and they had earned theirs.

Around two in the morning, hoofbeats.

Astrid felt them before she heard them—a tremor through the doorstep, the old road-bone of Thornfell carrying weight up from the valley road. Then the sound itself: a single rider, fast, no attempt at stealth, which in recent months meant either a messenger or a fool, and the Regency had run short of fools willing to ride alone into a town full of Vessani sympathizers and one extremely territorial blind ex-cartographer.

The rider reined up in the street. Lamplight caught gray livery—not inquisitor black, not soldier red, but the plain dove-gray of the Chancery, the civil service that had survived four regencies by the simple method of being too boring to execute.

"Astrid Voss?" the rider called. Young voice. Nervous. "Chancery courier. I've a letter for—" He squinted at the paper in his hand. "For 'the Bone-Carver of Thornfell, who broke the Crown.' Is—is that you?"

"It's me," Astrid said, without getting up. The moon was nearly overhead now. If she was going to die, she intended to do it comfortably seated, reading her mail.

He dismounted stiffly and held out the letter with both hands, the way you offer something to a queen or a bear. Up close he looked about nineteen and deeply regretted his career choices. His eyes flicked past her into the dark doorway, where Yeva sat motionless, and he flinched.

"She doesn't bite," Astrid said.

"I heard stories—"

"The stories undersell her. Take the compliment and go home, courier. Your horse wants its stable."

He went. Astrid broke the seal—plain wax, no crest, another Chancery habit—and read by moonlight.

Yeva listened to the paper crackle. "Well?"

"It's Serel." Her throat tightened on the name. Months now since the waterline, since the soldiers who owed him their consciences had cut his bonds and let him walk back into the flooding ruins to hold the line, since his surrender and his testimony and the long tribunal winter they'd all followed from afar like weather reports. "He's been released. Cleared. The tribunal found in his favor—found in *everyone's* favor, actually. The flood orders are public. The razing orders for the valley villages are public. Three councilors have resigned, two have fled, and one has—" she paused, savoring it—"been arrested by his own guards, who apparently read the testimony and developed opinions."

"And Serel?"

"He writes that he's been offered a position. Rebuilding the archives. Deciding what gets remembered." She looked up at the moon, almost full now, a coin balanced on its edge. "He says he intends to start with Vessany. All of it. The maps, the names, the language. He says—" her voice caught, and she let it, because there was no one here to perform composure for—"he says the country deserves a better cartographer than the ones who erased it, and he knows where to find one."

Yeva snorted. "Flatterer."

"He also says," Astrid continued, "that the Chancery has extended a formal invitation. To me. To attend the restoration hearings. As—" she read the phrase twice, because the moonlight might be playing tricks—"as 'the recognized living memory of the Vessani nation.' They want me to advise on the record of the dead."

"They want to put you in a room," Yeva said flatly, "and see what you are."

"Yes."

"And you'll go."

"Of course I'll go." Astrid folded the letter carefully along its creases. "But on my terms. No throne, no title, no crown-shaped anything. If they want the woman who broke the crown, they get exactly that. Nothing turned."

Above them, unhurried, indifferent, the moon completed its turn.

Full. Whole. Thirteen of thirteen. The date from the book, arrived at last, riding the sky over a border town that had never appeared in any prophecy worth the ink.

Astrid sat very still and waited to be annihilated.

The dead in the walls held their breath—or performed holding it, since breathing was largely decorative for them now. Yeva's hand found hers and gripped it, hard, map-calloused fingers over carver's calluses.

The moon shone.

The dogs argued. The ferry bell clanked. Someone's grandmother, two doors down, had moved on to a second song, worse than the first.

Nothing killed her.

Nothing even tried. The great appointed hour came and went the way most appointed hours do—quietly, slightly behind schedule, smelling faintly of other people's cooking. The prophecy, Astrid realized, staring up at that fat white face, had been fulfilled months ago in a flooded forge, by a hammer blow and a choice. The date hadn't been her death. It had been the deadline. And she had met it the way she met every deadline: by finishing the work early and spending the day of delivery feeling smug about it.

She started laughing. She couldn't help it. It came up out of her like water through a cracked dam, helpless and undignified, and Yeva joined in after a moment, and somewhere in the walls the lingering dead began to laugh too, a sound like wind through eleven thousand small bones, and the grandmother two doors down paused her singing to bang on the wall and tell them all to shut up, because even miracles must coexist with neighbors.

* * *

Dawn found them still in the doorway, tea long cold, the letter open between them, the moon gone pale and embarrassed at having overslept its own apocalypse.

"So," Yeva said, as the sky went the color of a fresh-shaved bone. "The capital. The hearings. The Chancery wanting to know what you are."

"I know what I am." Astrid turned her hands over in the gray light—the hands from the drawing, scarred and steady, still smelling faintly of river-ox rib. "I'm a maker of names. Everything else is decoration."

"Mm." Yeva stretched, joints popping like green wood. "And when they ask you to be more?"

"Then I'll carve them a refusal so beautiful they'll frame it."

There was a knock at the door.

Both women went still. At this hour, in this town, a knock meant one of three things: trouble, news, or Mrs. Halloway from the bakery, whose sense of boundaries had died decades before her husband did. Astrid opened the door prepared for any of the three.

It was none of them.

It was a girl—ten, maybe eleven, thin as a winter branch, standing in the street with dust on her boots that Astrid recognized at once, because she'd walked through it herself: the pale gold dust of the high passes, the roads that no longer existed. The girl was Vessani. Astrid knew it the way she now knew many things—not by learning but by remembering, the blood in her keeping accounts the crown never got to touch. And the girl was looking up at her with an expression Astrid knew from mirrors, from the night her grandmother's chest had first hummed under her hands: terror and hope fighting for the same face.

"You're her," the girl said. "The one the dead thank. My mother sent me—from the valley. From the sleeping queen's village." She swallowed. "They've come down from the mountains, the others like us. There are hundreds camped outside Thornfell. And they won't come closer until—" her voice wavered, steadied— "until you tell them whether they're allowed to exist again."

Behind Astrid, Yeva murmured, "Well then. Decide what you're willing to be asked for."

Astrid looked at the girl—at the dust of roads that shouldn't exist, at the fear and the hope, at the future standing on her doorstep in boots too big for it—and reached for her carving knife.

"First," she said, "tell me your name. We'll start there."
