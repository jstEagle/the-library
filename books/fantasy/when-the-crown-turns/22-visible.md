## 22. Visible

The bell above the door of Voss & Daughters Bone-Carving had a crack in it, so it rang a half-tone flat, and Astrid had decided she liked it that way. A perfect bell would have felt like someone else's shop.

She was working the small wheel when the children arrived—four of them on Thirdday afternoons, boots stamping snow off in the doorway, smelling of woodsmoke and the honey cakes they were supposed to have eaten before coming and had instead smuggled in their pockets. Maren, who was nine and left-handed and furious about every right-handed tool in the world. The twins, Oskar and Liss, who could not be trusted near the polishing cloth because they polished everything, including each other's elbows. And little Tam, who was six, and who had not yet spoken a word in her shop but had carved, in his third week, a bone bead with a spiral so fine it had made her eyes sting.

"Chairs," Astrid said, without looking up. "Aprons. If I find shavings in your tea again, Maren, your mother will hear about it, and then we will all suffer."

"My mother *likes* you now," Maren said, dragging her stool to the bench. "She says you're the reason the ferry runs again."

"The ferry runs again because Yeva bullied the harbormaster." Astrid set down the rib she'd been shaping—a memorial for old Anders Holt, who had died in his sleep the week before, gentle as a lamp going out. "I only did the name."

"You did the *name*," Maren repeated, in the reverent tone children used when they had decided something was magic and refused all correction. "You heard him say it."

Astrid let it go. There was no explaining to a nine-year-old that hearing the dead was less like magic and more like listening at a door you couldn't close—that most of what came through was ordinary. *Cold,* said the drowned ferryman, still, some nights, from the river. *The water is cold, tell my wife the coat.* She had told his wife about the coat. She had also told her about the door, the thirteenth moon, the crown, and the forty years of held breath, because a woman married to a ferryman deserved the whole of it, and because Astrid Voss had spent too long being careful with the truth to start rationing it now.

"Today," she said, tying her own apron, "we do letters. Curved ones. Anyone can carve a straight line; even a mule with a chisel can manage a straight line. Curves are where you find out whether you respect the bone."

Tam looked up at her. He didn't speak, but he tilted his head, and she had learned to read him the way she read whispers—the question was in the angle.

"Why curves?" she said aloud, for him, because the other three needed it too. "Because a name isn't a fence post. It's a riverbank. It has to bend around the person it belongs to."

Oskar said, "That doesn't make any sense," which was fair, and Liss said, "It makes *perfect* sense, you just aren't listening," which was also fair, and Astrid handed out the practice bones and let them argue.

* * *

The shop had changed in ways that took her months to stop noticing.

For eleven years she had kept it dim. Low lamps, shutters half-drawn, her own face angled away from the window whenever a customer lingered. Invisible—not hidden, nothing so dramatic as hidden, just *unremarked*, the way a stone in a wall is unremarked. It had been her grandmother's trick first, then her mother's, then hers: be useful, be quiet, be nobody's business. In Thornfell, where a third of the town had Vessani blood and everyone knew better than to say so, it had been survival dressed up as temperament.

Now the shutters stood open to the afternoon light. Her sign—ASTRID VOSS, CARVER OF NAMES—had been repainted by Maren's mother, who had added, without asking, a small blue flower under the text, the kind that grew wild in the valley villages and nowhere else in the Regency. Astrid had stared at it for an hour the day it went up, waiting to feel afraid.

She hadn't. That was the strange part. Serel's testimony had reached the capital in the spring, and the Regency had wobbled—publicly, messily, the way great clumsy things wobble—and the inquisitors had been recalled from the border towns to sit in offices and write reports nobody read. Some nights she still woke at the sound of boots in the street. But the boots belonged to the night watch, and the watch captain bought his mother's memorial bone from her shop, and paid full price without haggling, which in Thornfell was practically an oath of fealty.

The dead had gone quiet, too. Not silent—she didn't think she'd ever be fully rid of them, and had stopped wanting to be—but quiet, the way a house is quiet when everyone in it is finally asleep. The thousands who had crossed through the door out of Old Vessany were gone where the gone go. What remained were the local dead, the ordinary dead, the drowned ferryman and Anders Holt and a chorus of grandmothers with opinions, and those she could live among. Those, it turned out, she had always lived among. They were the only company she'd ever had that never asked her to be less than she was.

"Mine keeps curving the wrong way," Maren announced, holding up a practice bone with a B that drooped like a tired man against a wall.

"Then ask it why," Astrid said. "Bones are honest. If your letter's sagging, either your hand is tired or your heart isn't in the name yet. Which is it?"

Maren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "I'm carving my grandfather," she said. "But I only met him twice."

"Then carve the two times," Astrid said. "The smell of his pipe. The way he called you *little magpie*. Put that in the curve. Nobody reading it needs to know he was tall. They need to know he loved you."

Maren bent back over the bone. Tam slid off his stool, came around the bench, and stood at Astrid's elbow, watching her hands on Anders Holt's rib. After a while he held up his own practice piece: a spiral, and beneath it, in wobbly new letters he must have practiced a hundred times on scrap, four characters of the old Vessani script.

Astrid went very still.

"That's *rest well*," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Where did you learn that?"

He shrugged, the universal answer of the six-year-old, and pointed—at her, at the open shutters, at the blue flower on the sign, at the whole visible world of the shop. As if it were obvious. As if of course a child in Thornfell would learn the dead language of a drowned country, now that the adults had stopped pretending it was shameful to speak it.

Yeva had started teaching it, Thursdays, in the meeting hall. Blind as she was, she still knew every road—including, she claimed, the roads inside words. *"Vessany isn't a place anymore,"* she'd told the first class, *"so we'll carry it in our mouths until it learns to walk again."* Forty people had come. Then sixty. The elders from the valley sent song cycles written on birch bark. Someone's uncle remembered a lullaby. The country that had been erased from maps was reassembling itself, letter by letter, in the mouths of children who had never seen it and would not let it die anyway.

Astrid touched Tam's shoulder. He leaned into it, briefly, the way cats do, and went back to his stool.

* * *

The traveler came at the gray end of the afternoon, when the light went silver and the children had been collected by their various mothers, and the shop smelled of bone dust and cooling tea.

She was perhaps fifty, dressed for the road in good gray wool, mud to the knee, with the flat patient accent of the capital. She stood a long time in front of the display wall before she spoke, and Astrid let her stand. People needed different amounts of silence before a thing like this, and after everything, Astrid had learned to give it.

The wall held bones. Not grim ones—she had worked hard on that, over the years, on teaching Thornfell that a memorial bone was not a skull on a shelf but a sentence in stone, a hand held out across the dark. Anders Holt's rib, finished that morning, curled with pipe smoke and *little magpie* in another family's grief. A wrist-bone for the drowned ferryman, worn smooth where his widow touched it every morning on her way to market. And along the top shelf, behind glass, the long work-in-progress: slats of antler and whalebone, hundreds of them, each bearing a single recovered name of Vessany. The monument she was building instead of a kingdom. It filled two shelves now. Yeva estimated there were eleven thousand names. Astrid had stopped estimating. She just carved, three or four a week, and let the shelf grow the way a country ought to grow—slowly, by hand, one person at a time.

"You're the one," the traveler said at last. "The one who wouldn't take the crown."

"I'm the one who broke it," Astrid said. "Which sounds similar and isn't."

The traveler smiled. "There are songs already. In the capital, if you can believe it. Bad ones. The sort where you can tell nobody involved has ever held a hammer."

"There usually are." Astrid poured a second cup of tea and pushed it across the counter, because the woman had the look of someone who had walked farther today than her pride would admit. "Songs are how the Regency digests things it can't swallow. It'll sing me into a harmless little martyr and go on exactly as before. Or it won't. Serel's testimony is still working its way through the courts. Either way, the names get carved."

The traveler wrapped both hands around the cup. Steam rose between them.

"Can I ask you something impertinent?"

"People generally do."

"Do you regret it? Refusing it. You were offered a kingdom. An actual country, brought back from the dead, with you at the head of it. Most people would have—" She stopped herself, gracefully. "Well. Most people would have."

Astrid looked at the wall of names. At the blue flower on the sign outside, catching the last of the light. At the practice bones stacked by the door, waiting for tomorrow's children, including a small spiral-and-script piece she was going to mount on cord for Tam, whether he asked or not.

"For a while," she said, "I thought the choice was between a crown and nothing. That's what I'd been raised to believe without anyone saying it out loud—that you could be invisible, or you could be *someone*, and invisibility at least kept you alive. My grandmother hid us. My mother hid us harder. I hid us best of all, so well that I hid myself from myself. I was very good at it. I recommend it to no one."

"And the crown was the opposite of hiding."

"The crown was the opposite of *me*. That's the trap nobody warns you about. It wasn't offering to make me visible. It was offering to replace me with a queen—to turn me, the way it turned every heir before me, until there was nothing left in the body but the office. I saw them, in the vision. Eleven of them, standing in that flooded hall, wearing themselves like borrowed coats. The last one had been on the throne nine years and I could not have told you one true thing about her. She was magnificent. She was nothing." Astrid turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "So no. I don't regret refusing. But it's the wrong question, if you don't mind my saying."

The traveler raised an eyebrow, capital-style.

"The wrong question?"

"Aye. Because refusing the crown wasn't the gift. Any coward can refuse a crown; I've known cowards, I've *been* one. The gift was what I found out I was allowed to want instead." She nodded at the shelves of names. "I wanted to belong somewhere. All my life, that was the hunger underneath everything—I thought it meant blood, throne, a country with my name on it. Turns out belonging is a blind woman bullying a harbormaster. It's a left-handed girl arguing with a letter B. It's a boy who doesn't talk, learning a dead script off my shutters. It's a widow touching a wrist-bone every morning and nodding at me through the window like we share a secret, because we do. I didn't get a kingdom." She smiled, and found, not for the first time, that it came easily now, unguarded, teeth and all. "I got something rarer. I got a self. Carved it myself, same as the rest. Took longer. Holds better."

The traveler was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its interview-shape and gone simply human.

"They'll want to put that in the songs."

"Tell them to leave out the part where I cry at the ferryman's coat. A legend needs her mysteries."

The traveler laughed, finished her tea, bought a small carved moon for her sister—"for no one dead, just for love, if you take such commissions"—and went out into the dusk, the cracked bell ringing its flat half-note behind her.

* * *

Yeva arrived after closing, as she did most evenings, letting herself in with the confidence of the blind and the entitled. She carried bread and a bottle of plum brandy and the particular smugness of a woman whose Thursday class had, that week, produced its first student fluent enough to argue with her.

"The Kroll girl—no relation—corrected my pronunciation," she said, dropping onto the stool by the stove. "*My* pronunciation. I was court cartographer, and a child of eight corrected my vowels."

"She's right, though. You say *vessa* like a northerner."

"I say it like a woman who learned it from books, because the men who could have taught me proper were busy being executed." Yeva poured the brandy with deadly accuracy for a blind woman, which was one of her smaller daily miracles. "Drink. You've got your thinking face on."

Astrid drank. Outside, the first real snow of the season was starting, fat and unhurried, settling on the sign, the shutters, the blue flower.

"Eleven thousand names," Astrid said. "Say we finish. Say the shelf holds all of them, and the villages teach the songs, and the courts do the right thing for once in their miserable lives. What then? What does Vessany *become*, with no territory and no crown and no queen?"

Yeva snorted. "What it always should have been. A memory with a pulse. Countries made of borders get eaten, Astrid. We watched it happen. Countries made of names—" she tapped her own breastbone, where, Astrid knew, a scarred brand sat under the wool, the mark they'd given her for refusing to surrender the maps, "—countries made of names have to be killed one person at a time, and we are *terrible* to kill."

Astrid laughed, and the dead in the walls laughed with her, faintly, the way old friends chuckle at a joke they've heard before but still like.

Later, when Yeva had gone home through the snow, Astrid banked the stove and stood a while at the window with the lamp low—not hidden now, just resting, the way you dim a room to watch weather. Down the street, the ferryman's widow passed with her lantern, lifted it toward the shop window in her nightly salute, and walked on toward the river.

On the counter lay tomorrow's work: blank bones, pale and patient, waiting for names. Beside them, a slip of paper Yeva had left, birch-bark, covered in the old script in a child's careful hand—Tam's, she realized, done in Thursday class, addressed to her in letters she could read now because Yeva had made her learn along with everyone else.

She translated it slowly, sounding out the curves, the riverbanks of it.

*Rest well, carver. Come spring, the valleys send more names. Bring your hammer.*

Astrid set the note up on the shelf beside the first hundred names, where the lamplight would find it every morning, and blew out the flame, and the shop settled into the dark around her—visible, at last, even with its eyes closed.
