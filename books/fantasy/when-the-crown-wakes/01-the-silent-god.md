## 1. The Silent God

There are three rules to guiding in the Thornfells, and I wrote all three of them myself, which makes them the only scripture I've ever trusted.

One: never ask a client's name. Names are debts waiting to be called in.

Two: never take sides. The day you pick a side is the day you become worth killing.

Three: pray if you must, but don't expect an answer.

I was breaking rule three at dawn, the way I broke it every dawn, kneeling in the snow behind the last wagon with my bare hands pressed flat against the frozen ground like I could feel a pulse in it. *Vael.* Just the name. I didn't have anything else to say anymore. Twenty years of prayers had worn themselves down from psalms to pleas to this — a single word, dropped into the silence like a coin into a well that stopped echoing years ago.

Nothing answered. Nothing ever did. The mountain just sat there under its snow, vast and indifferent, wearing its crown of peaks the way it always had, as if no god had ever lived inside it at all.

"Joubert." Hesk's voice, from the lead wagon. "You done talking to yourself? We move."

I stood, brushed the snow off my knees, and put the grief away where I kept it — somewhere behind the ribs, in a pocket I didn't check often. "We move," I agreed.

* * *

Hesk Brann ran salt and iron over the pass in winter, when the king's tollmen were too cold and too stupid to watch the high trails. He was a broad man with a laugh like a rockslide and the eyes of someone who counted everything twice. I'd guided for him four seasons now, which made him the closest thing I had to an employer, and the fact that I still didn't know his real name meant he understood the rules too.

He had six wagons, eleven men, and a mule named Duchess who hated me on principle. The cargo was salt in the front wagons and something heavier in the back two that nobody asked about, because asking about cargo violates a corollary of rule one that every smart smuggler learns before their first winter: never ask what you're carrying, because then you're responsible for it.

My job was simple. Get them over Sorrow's Notch before the next storm, down the far side to the Cinderhollow road, and gone. For that they paid me in silver, a dry corner by the fire, and the particular courtesy people show a man who knows where all the bodies are buried because he's the one who avoided burying them there.

"You've got a look," Hesk said, falling into step beside me as we climbed out of the treeline. "That look you get."

"I have one look. It's cold."

"It's the look of a man listening for something." He spat, expertly, into the wind so it blew back on him, which told you everything about Hesk's relationship with cause and effect. "Waiting for your god to whisper in your ear?"

"My god's dead," I said. It wasn't true, exactly. Dead gods stay dead. Vael was something worse — silent. A god who'd gone quiet mid-prayer, twenty years back, like a man walking out of a conversation without excusing himself. The priests said it was punishment. The priests said a lot of things, right up until the temples emptied and the priests got honest work or dishonest ones.

"Shame," Hesk said. "A man with a god's got an edge. A man without one's just got his wits."

"Wits don't freeze," I said. "Gods do."

He laughed his rockslide laugh and went back to his wagon, and I went back to the thing I was actually good at, which was reading a mountainside the way other men read a face.

* * *

Sorrow's Notch earned its name honestly. It's a throat of stone between two shoulders of the Thornfells, narrow enough that the wagons had to go through single file with inches to spare on either side, and the snow lies in it deep enough to swallow a horse to the chest. There are places along it where the wind funnels through and screams, and places where the wind doesn't blow at all, and the second kind are worse, because silence up here has always felt less like absence and more like holding its breath.

I walked the notch ahead of the wagons, testing the drifts with a ten-foot pole, calling the safe line back over my shoulder. Left of the cairn. Right of the split boulder. Never trust snow that looks smooth, because smooth means it's lying about what's underneath.

Halfway through, I stopped.

Not because of the snow. Because of the birds.

Crows — actual crows, not the metaphorical kind — had been following us since the treeline. That was normal. Crows follow caravans the way beggars follow funerals, hoping something dies. But these had gone quiet. All of them, at once, perched along the notch's southern rim in a ragged black row, watching us pass with their heads cocked, and not one of them making a sound.

Birds go quiet for two reasons. Something's hunting them, or something's scaring them.

"Hesk," I called back. "Hold the line. Hold it *still*."

"What is it?"

"Company."

Because there — far back along our trail, at the mouth of the notch, small with distance but unmistakable — moved a line of figures. Six of them. No wagons. Skis. They moved the way trained men move over snow, economical, spaced wide, and they'd been following our tracks long enough that they weren't even trying to hide the following anymore.

Rival crew. Which meant Hesk's cargo in the back two wagons was worth stealing, which meant I'd been paid to guide a target, which meant rule two — never take sides — was about to get expensive.

* * *

We came out of the notch into the high bowl beyond, a white amphitheater ringed by cliffs, and I called the halt there because ahead the trail forked — the low road, longer, exposed along the ridgeline, and the high cut, shorter, steeper, threading beneath a hanging cornice of snow the size of a cathedral.

Every instinct I had said take the low road. Every instinct also said the low road was exactly where six skiers would expect us to go, because the low road was where the ambush ground was good.

So I stood in the snow with the wind pulling at my hood and did the arithmetic I'm paid for. Six men on skis, moving fast, would reach the ridge junction in maybe two hours. They'd assume we'd take the low road, because everyone takes the low road. If we took the high cut instead, we'd be under the cornice — dangerous, but the cornice had held for thirty years, and I'd crossed beneath it eleven times and was still alive to count them.

Or.

Or I could give them what they expected, and make them regret expecting it.

"Hesk. How attached are you to your cargo?"

"Deeply."

"How attached are you to a fight?"

His eyes did the counting-twice thing. "Depends who's paying for the funeral."

"The men following us want what's in your back wagons. They'll take the low road to get ahead of us. So we'll let them think we're taking it." I pointed at the high cut. "Then we won't."

"That cornice kills people."

"People die everywhere. Up there, though, they only die if the mountain's in a mood, and I know how to read its moods." I was already turning. "Get your teams moving. Slow until the cut, fast after. And tell your boys if anyone prays, keep it quiet. Noise carries."

"And you?" Hesk said. "What'll you be doing?"

"Being worth my pay."

* * *

The high cut was everything the low road wasn't — steep, blind, and singing. That's what I call it when wind moves through a narrow passage just right, a thin high note like a wet finger on glass. Most men hate the sound. I love it, because it means the air is moving, and moving air means the cornice above is shedding weight in whispers instead of avalanches.

We climbed. The wagons groaned. Duchess expressed her opinion of me at length. Behind us, the notch stayed empty, and I let myself believe — briefly, foolishly — that the skiers had taken the bait clean.

Then the crow landed on my shoulder.

I want to be clear about this: crows land near me sometimes. They're carrion birds and I'm a man who spends his life around things that might become carrion. But they don't land *on* me. Birds know something about people, some animal ledger of who's safe, and every bird I'd ever met had filed me under *not*.

This one weighed almost nothing. Its feathers were blacker than the shadow under a rock, black enough to look wet in full daylight, and when it turned its head and looked at me with one eye, I felt that eye like a fingertip pressed against something behind my sternum.

"Bad idea, this road," it said.

I did not scream. I want that noted for posterity. What I actually did was walk three more steps, very calmly, while my entire understanding of the world quietly rearranged itself, and then I said, "Talking crow."

"Talking crow," it agreed. Its voice was rough and dry, like wind through a dead tree, and underneath the roughness there was something else, something familiar in a way that made my skin crawl. A cadence. A rhythm I knew. I'd heard that rhythm in liturgy once, in another life, in a temple full of smoke and singing, before the singing stopped.

"Vael's voice," I said, before I could stop myself. The old prayer, the first words every child in the mountain villages learned. *Vael's voice in the storm, Vael's hand on the stone.*

The crow was quiet a moment. When it spoke again, the roughness had dropped away, and what was left underneath was older and sadder and made my chest hurt. "Something like it. A piece. An echo. Don't make it strange, Joubert. You've been talking to me for twenty years. I just haven't been talking back."

Twenty years. The exact number. The exact number of years since the god went silent, and here was a bird on my shoulder claiming to be the silence itself, and I found that I couldn't look at it, so I looked at the trail instead, and kept walking, because the wagons were still climbing and the cornice was still overhead and a man can have a theological crisis on his own time.

"Why now?" I said finally. "Why talk now?"

"Because you're going to need—" It stopped. Its talons tightened on my shoulder, tiny points of pressure. "Because something's coming. And because you prayed this morning."

"You heard that?"

"I hear everything you say to me. I always have. That's not the same as being able to answer." A pause. "Most days it isn't even close."

Ahead, the lead wagon crested the lip of the cut, and the trail opened onto the long descent toward Cinderhollow, white and empty and safe-looking, and I was so busy staring at the crow that I almost missed what the crow had clearly already seen.

Downslope, half a mile, tucked into the rocks off the low road where it merged with ours: six men. On foot now, skis abandoned, spread out in a skirmish line. Waiting for us. Except we hadn't come the way they'd planned, and now they were scrambling, cutting upslope through knee-deep snow to intercept, and one of them had raised something to his shoulder that caught the light.

Not a crossbow. A horn. A signal horn, braying three flat notes across the bowl.

And from the ridge behind us — the ridge we'd just left, the ridge I'd sworn was empty — three notes answered.

"They split," the crow said. "Two ahead, four behind. They wanted you in the middle."

I closed my eyes for one breath. One breath is all I ever allow myself, because panic is a luxury billed at interest.

Behind us, four skiers cresting the notch. Ahead, six men climbing. Between them, seven wagons, eleven men who fought for coin rather than conviction, one mule with a grudge, and me.

Rule two said never take sides. But rules are for keeping clients alive, and right now the only side available was theirs.

"HESK!" My voice cracked across the bowl. "Back wagons, axes out! We're boxed!"

The crow's talons bit my shoulder. "Oscar," it said — my name, my actual name, which no client knew, which I hadn't spoken aloud in years, in that voice that was almost a dead god's voice — "there's a ravine, quarter mile east. I can buy you the time to reach it. But you're not going to like the price."

"There's always a price," I said, and drew my axe. "What is it?"

"You'll have to stop being invisible," the crow said. "They know your name now. And so does anyone they've told."

Below us, the six men began to climb, and above us the four came down, and somewhere in the middle of it all, the snow started again — soft, thick, endless, falling on the just and the unjust and the man with the axe who'd broken all three of his own rules before noon.
