## 2. Blood on the Map

There are three rules to surviving a winter in the Thornfells, and I'd learned all of them the expensive way. Keep your wood dry. Keep your knife closer than your friends. And never, under any circumstances, let anyone learn your name.

I'd kept that last one for eleven years. Eleven years of being "the guide," "the mountain man," "hey, you," and once, memorably, from a smuggler with frostbite and no manners, "the ugly bastard who got us down alive." I answered to all of them. I answered to nothing at all, really, which was the point. A man without a name is a man without a ledger entry, and a man without a ledger entry is a man nobody comes looking for when debts come due.

The waystation at Greyhollow Pass had been my winter home for six of those years. It wasn't much — a stone squat built into the mountainside by miners who'd struck nothing and moved on, leaving behind a hearth that smoked if the wind came wrong and a cellar that smelled like the inside of a dead thing. But it had walls, and walls were wealth in Thornfell. I shared it with a store of salted goat, a barrel of turnips going soft, and the particular silence of a god who'd stopped listening.

"Evening, Vael," I said to the empty room, because habit is a harder master than any crime lord. "Quiet day. Nobody died. You're welcome."

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered in years. Somewhere back there — I couldn't have told you when, exactly; gods don't leave notes — Vael had gone quiet, the way a hearth goes cold: gradually, then completely, then it's just a black square in the wall you keep almost forgetting not to touch.

I still prayed the way some men still talk to dead wives. Not because it works. Because stopping would mean admitting what it meant.

* * *

The storm came in two days before midwinter, which the old-timers called the Long Dark's doorstep, and it came in angry, the kind of white roar that erases the difference between sky and ground and makes even the crows sit down and think about their choices. I'd banked the fire, checked my stores twice, and settled in to lose a week to sleep and whittling.

Which is why the knocking annoyed me on a professional level.

Nobody knocks in a storm like that. Nobody *travels* in a storm like that. Things that move in weather like this are either desperate, stupid, or sent, and all three categories had historically cost me money or blood.

I took up my axe — not for company, just for conversation — and opened the door onto a wall of screaming white.

He fell through it. Literally fell, into my doorway, into my floor, a snow-crusted shape in a courier's oilskin that had given up being waterproof somewhere around the second ridge. He was maybe forty, grey-stubbled, and his hands were pressed flat against his belly like he was holding himself shut.

"Door," he said. "Said there'd be a door."

"There's a door," I said. "Congratulations. Now get off my threshold before you freeze to it."

I half-dragged him to the fire, and that's when I saw the blood. Not the dramatic kind, spread around for effect. The bad kind — dark, soaked through his coat, welling slow between his fingers every time he shifted. Gut wound. Deep. The kind where the question isn't whether but how long, and how much of that time he wanted to spend talking.

"I don't take patients," I told him, already pulling my needle case down anyway, because apparently I'm a liar even to myself. "And I especially don't take patients who bleed on my only rug."

"No rug," he managed. His eyes were doing something worrying — sliding around the room like the walls might open doors of their own. "You're the one. The one they said. Greyhollow. Said there'd be—"

"Said who? Who said?"

He laughed, and it turned into a wet cough that spattered the flagstones red. "Careful," he wheezed. "Questions. That's how they find you. Asking questions."

"Then we'll both die ignorant." I cut the coat away from the wound and did my best not to make a face. I've seen a lot of wounds. This one had ambition. Something had gone in below his ribs and twisted on the way out, and whatever he'd done to keep moving afterward should have killed him twice over. "Whoever did this used a hook or a barbed blade. Tallyman's tool, if you know the trade."

His eyes snapped into focus on me, sudden and sharp, and for a moment the pain went out of them entirely, replaced by something worse.

"You *do* know the trade."

"I know knives. Sit still."

I stitched him while the storm screamed against the shutters. He held better than most, biting down on a strap of leather, sweat freezing in his beard. When I tied off the last knot he caught my wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong in a man leaking his life onto my floor.

"They followed me," he said. "Three days. Maybe four. They don't tire. They don't eat. You understand me? They *don't eat.*"

"People always eat eventually."

"Not these." He swallowed. "Tallymen. Court men. I stole from the Court beneath, friend, and now the ledger wants its balance, and the balance is—" another cough, longer this time, "—the balance is everything."

"The Court beneath," I repeated. "That's a story. A fireside tale brokers tell to scare couriers into honesty. Souls sold under the mountain, lanterns and ledgers, the antlered throne. Children's stories."

"Children's stories are the ones that are true," he whispered. "Everything else is the lie adults tell so they can sleep."

I gave him watered wine and made him drink it. Outside, the wind found a new note, lower, almost like a voice trying out words. Inside, the fire cracked and spat, and the dying man watched me with the patience of someone who has already seen the end of his own story and is just waiting for the page to turn.

"What's your name?" I asked, mostly to fill the silence.

Something flickered across his face — fear, real fear, the kind I recognized because I wore it myself every day like an old coat. "Don't do that," he said. "Don't ask. Names are currency here. You know that. Why do you think you live up here alone with your turnips and your—" his gaze drifted to the little carved figure of Vael I kept on the mantel, a crude antlered shape I'd whittled during my first lonely winter, "—your silent god."

"I didn't say anything about a god."

"You didn't have to. You look at it the way debtors look at the door." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "I had a god too, once. We all did, before the Court started buying them out from under us."

* * *

He lasted until well past midnight. Long enough to drink most of my wine, long enough to tell me fragments — a vault, a reckoning coming, a hundred years of bargains about to be sealed forever, words like *lantern* and *key* and *she knows your line* — none of it connected, all of it delivered in the tone of a man trying to hand off a burning brand before his arm gives out.

And then, near the end, he asked for my hand.

"Don't," I said. "Whatever this is, I don't want it. I'm a guide. I walk people over mountains and I ask nothing and I'm owed nothing, and that arrangement has kept me breathing for eleven years."

"It kept you *invisible,*" he said. "Different thing. Invisibility ends, friend. It always ends. The only choice you get is what you're holding when it does."

"My name," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and hard, "is not for sale."

"No," he agreed. "It's for giving." And he pressed something into my palm and folded my fingers over it, and leaned close, and with the last breath in a body that had carried it three days through a killing storm, he whispered:

"*Joubert.*"

The world went very small and very loud.

Not my name — I hadn't heard it spoken aloud since I was nineteen years old and running from a village full of graves. Not the name I'd buried along with everything else. But close enough. Close enough to be a key turning in a lock I'd bricked over decades ago.

"How," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "How do you know that name."

But he was already gone. Just like that — the light behind his eyes guttering out the way a lamp does when the oil runs true to the bottom, no drama, no last words, just a man-shaped absence where a man had been. His hand slid off mine. The parcel in my fist stayed.

I sat there a long time. The storm beat on the roof like it wanted in. On the mantel, the little wooden Vael stared down at me with its blank carved face, and for the first time in years I found myself wishing — actually wishing, with heat behind it — that the god would wake up and tell me what to do.

Gods never do. That's the first thing you learn about them, and the last.

I opened my hand.

A map, folded small, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. The seal bore a mark I didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye that had been closed by force. I turned it over twice. I held it near the fire. I thought about throwing it in.

Here's the truth about me, the thing I'd built my whole life on top of and pretended wasn't load-bearing: I am a coward. A competent coward, a well-paid coward, a coward with excellent boots and a flawless memory for mountain passes, but a coward. Cowards survive by keeping their hands empty. And every instinct I owned was screaming at me to feed this thing to the fire, bury the courier in the snowdrift, and wake up tomorrow as nobody again.

Instead I broke the seal.

Inside was a map of the Thornfells — not the surface, though the surface was there, faint, like a skin stretched over something else. Beneath it, inked in a careful, cramped hand, were tunnels. Galleries. Stairways going down farther than any mine I'd ever guided. And at the heart of it, drawn with a precision that looked less like cartography and more like confession, a vast chamber marked with a single word:

*COURT.*

And beside it, smaller, in different ink, added later by a shakier hand: *they keep what they buy. Ask about the famine sales. Ask about the children.*

Ask about the children.

My mouth had gone dry as old bone. I knew about the famine. Everyone in the Thornfells knew about the famine — the winter twenty-some years ago when the crops rotted in the ground and the granaries stood empty and whole villages went quiet between one spring and the next. My village had known it intimately. My village had been one of the ones that went quiet.

I had been fed that winter. I remembered that much, and I had spent twenty years not asking why, the way you don't press on a bruise, the way you don't look too long at the one empty bed in a house where everyone else got thinner and thinner until—

Stop. That door stays bricked.

I was still staring at the map when I heard the crunch of boots on snow outside, and realized, with the special clarity that arrives right alongside disaster, that the storm had stopped.

* * *

They came in through the door without knocking, which told me everything before I saw their faces. People who knock want something from you. People who don't knock have already decided what you owe.

Four of them, filling my doorway with snowmelt and menace. I knew two by sight — Brannic Hale's crew, out of Cinderhollow, the kind of smugglers who give smuggling a bad name. Brannic himself stood front and center, a slab of a man with a beard full of ice and a smile he used like a weapon.

"Well now," Brannic said. "The hermit has visitors."

"He had one," I said. "He died. Mind the blood, it stains."

"That so?" He stepped inside uninvited, because of course he did, and his eyes went to the body by the fire, then to the red-spattered flagstones, then to me, sitting far too casually with my hands — I hoped — looking empty. "Courier fella, was it? Came through Cinderhollow maybe a week back. Asking questions about the deep roads. Asking about *Greyhollow.*" He let that hang. "Man pays for information, he expects exclusive rights to it."

"He didn't pay me anything."

"No. But he *gave* you something. Saw him watching this place on the maps. Saw the way he asked about who winters here." Brannic crouched, picked up the dead man's abandoned wine cup, sniffed it, set it down with delicate disgust. "So here's the arrangement, hermit. Whatever he left with you, it comes with me. And in exchange, I don't burn this lovely hovel down with you in it, which, between us, is a bargain. That hovel's worth more than you are."

I will say this for Brannic Hale: he was honest about what he was. There's a comfort in that. Most dangerous men dress it up.

"Let me ask you something," I said, standing slowly, letting him see how tall I was, letting him remember the stories about the guide who'd brought caravans through passes that ate other guides whole. "When the courier came through Cinderhollow asking questions — did you sell him out? Is that why there are Tallymen three days behind him instead of seven?"

Something moved behind Brannic's eyes. Satisfaction, maybe. Or its nervous cousin.

"Maybe I mentioned him to the right people. Maybe those people pay better than he did."

"And maybe," I said, "those people don't stop at couriers. Maybe they follow the trail backward. Courier to waystation. Waystation to the men who talked. And then those men find out what happens to people who sell names to the Court." I shook my head, slow and sorrowful. "Brannic. You beautiful idiot. You didn't make a sale. You made a *ledger entry.*"

For a moment, doubt crossed his face like a cloud crossing a field — quick, and gone, but enough. Enough to make his crew shift. Enough to make the biggest of them glance back at the door, at the clean, silent, terrible night beyond it.

"Give me the package," Brannic said, but the smile had lost its edge.

"There is no package."

"Then you won't mind if we look."

What happened next happened fast, the way these things do. Brannic grabbed for me; I gave him the shelf of turnips instead, and the soft ones exploded across his chest with a smell that could strip paint. His big friend swung a hatchet at my head and got the mantel instead, and the little wooden figure of Vael spun off and hit the floor, and I swear — I *swear* — the temperature in the room dropped like something had exhaled.

Then the window burst inward.

Not the shutters — the shutter-boards behind them, torn loose by a black shape that came through in a whirl of feathers and fury, shrieking in a voice that was not a crow's voice, that was not any bird's voice, a voice like a bell rung underwater, like stone learning to speak. The big man with the hatchet took a wing-beat across the face and went down howling. Another caught claws across the eyes. The third simply turned and ran, out into the dark, and I couldn't blame him. Some sounds reach past courage into older wiring.

Brannic stood in the wreckage of my turnip shelf, bleeding from the cheek, staring at the crow perched on my table with its wings half-spread and its head cocked, and I watched a lifetime of hard-earned cynicism fail him completely.

"What in the deep roads *is* that," he breathed.

"Household pest," I said. "Get out, Brannic."

He got. They all got, the wounded dragging the blinded, stumbling into the night, and I stood alone in my ruined waystation with a dead man, a shattered mantel, a bloodstained map hidden in my shirt, and a crow.

The crow looked at me. Its eyes were wrong for a crow — too dark, too deep, like looking down a well and seeing stars at the bottom.

"You," it said, in that bell-underwater voice, softer now, almost tired. "You prayed tonight. First time in years you *meant* it."

I didn't answer. My heart was doing something complicated.

"They'll come back," the crow said. "Him, or worse. The Tallymen follow paper trails, and you, Oscar Joubert, are covered in footprints now."

"Never take sides," I said numbly. "Never ask names. That's how you stay alive."

"Yes," the crow agreed, hopping once, closer. "And how is that working out for you?"

Outside, far down the pass, something howled — not wolf, not wind. And the map lay against my chest like a coal that wouldn't cool, and somewhere beneath my feet, beneath the stone, beneath the silence of twenty years, I felt — or imagined, God help me, I *prayed* I imagined — something vast and patient begin, very slowly, to wake.
