## 3. What the Crow Knew

I left the waystation with a dead man's map against my ribs and three armed men at my back, which by any honest accounting meant I had traded one problem for three worse ones.

The snow had turned to that wet, clinging sort that announces every footstep like a town crier. I went out the back, through the woodshed where old Marta kept her split logs and her cheaper liquor, and took the goat track north. It was not a good route. It was the only route that didn't pass a window.

Behind me, someone kicked in the waystation's kitchen door. I heard Marta swear at them with the fluency of a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived a tax collector, and then I stopped listening, because listening to other people's trouble is how you end up carrying it.

My rules were simple. Never ask names. Never take sides. Never be the most interesting thing in any room, ravine, or mountain pass. For eleven years those rules had kept me alive through passes that killed better men, and now a stranger with a hole in his gut had broken all three of them in under a minute by dying on me and saying my name.

*Joubert.*

Nobody said Joubert. Joubert was a name you gave to debt collectors when they came asking, a name that belonged to nobody worth finding. And yet this man — this bleeding, raving man who'd crawled half a mountain to reach a waystation he couldn't have known held anyone at all — had spent his last breath on it.

Which meant somebody had told him my name. Which meant somebody wanted him to find me. Which meant, if I was very lucky, they only wanted the map.

I did not feel very lucky.

* * *

The ravine was called Hollowman's Cut, though I never learned by whom or why, because asking questions about names is against my rules even when the names belong to geography. It ran down from the high shoulder of Thornfell like a scar, steep walls of grey stone furred with pine, a frozen streambed along the bottom that made for fast walking and loud walking, depending on how much you cared about the difference.

I cared about the difference. But the walls narrowed, and the snow deepened, and by the time I realized the cut was a funnel I was already halfway down its throat.

They came over the rim on both sides. Three of them, as advertised, plus a fourth I hadn't counted — a big quiet one who'd been behind me since the waystation and was better at snow than I'd given him credit for. Four men, crossbows, and me with a short sword, a knife, and a map I was starting to think seriously about eating.

"Joubert." The leader was a lean man with a frostbitten ear and the patient voice of someone paid by the day rather than the result. "You've got something that isn't yours."

"That's funny," I said. "I was about to say the same to you."

He smiled the way winter smiles. "Hand it over and walk away. Nobody pays us enough to chase you into spring."

"You'd be surprised what people pay me," I said, and drew my sword, because talking was done and we both knew it.

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting four men in a narrow place: it is the only time four men are worse than one. They got in each other's way. I got the first one across the forearm, dropped his crossbow into the streambed, and put my shoulder into the big quiet one hard enough to buy myself three steps of running room.

Three steps was not enough. A bolt took me through the meat of the left arm — above the elbow, ugly but honest, the kind of wound that bleeds a lot and kills you slowly if you're stupid about it. I went down on the ice, and the leader stood over me with his sword level, breathing steam, looking almost apologetic.

"Told you," he said. "Nobody pays us enough."

And that was when the crow spoke.

* * *

It came off the rim of the ravine like a thrown knife, black against the grey sky, and it did not caw. Crows caw. This thing *spoke*, and what it spoke was one word in a voice that went through me like a hook through the gills:

***"DOWN."***

Every man in that ravine went down. I went down too, face-first in the snow, because the word wasn't a suggestion. It was the kind of word that lives in your spine below the part where decisions get made. I have knelt to officers. I have knelt to priests, back when priests still had gods worth kneeling to. I had never been *put down* by a word before.

Then the light came.

I want to describe it properly, because I've had twenty years of practice describing things badly and this deserves better. It wasn't fire. Fire eats. This *gave*. It poured off the crow like heat-shimmer made visible, gold-white and utterly silent, and where it touched the snow the snow didn't melt so much as remember being water and step aside politely. The leader of the hired men screamed — not in pain, I don't think, though maybe pain too, but in the particular horror of a man whose worldview has just been repossessed. He scrambled up the ravine wall on hands and knees. The others followed. The big quiet one dropped his crossbow and left it there, which told me everything I needed to know about how much he was being paid.

In the space of ten heartbeats I was alone in Hollowman's Cut with a bleeding arm, a dead man's map, and a bird.

The crow landed on a stone in front of me and folded its wings with the smug economy of a creature that has never once doubted itself.

"Well," I said, because my mouth has always run ahead of my sense. "That's new."

"You prayed last night," the crow said. Its voice was wrong for a bird and right for something else — low, dry, like wind moving through a stone corridor, like an old man reading aloud to himself. "At the waystation. You do it every night. Out of habit, you tell yourself."

"I don't talk to birds."

"No. You talk to Vael. Vael just hasn't answered in eleven years." The crow tilted its head, one eye catching the light — and the eye was wrong too, amber where it should have been black, with a depth to it no eye that size should hold. "I'm answering now. Try to keep up."

I sat back in the snow and pressed my hand harder against my arm and considered, briefly and seriously, whether I had frozen to death in the ravine and this was the part where the brain got sentimental. It would explain a lot. It would explain the voice, which I knew — gods help me, I *knew* that cadence, the flat dry patience of it, the way it landed on the second syllable of every sentence like a man setting down a heavy load.

Eleven years ago I had prayed with that voice answering. Not in words, mostly. In weather. In luck. In the specific feeling of a safe path under your feet when every sign said otherwise. Mountain folk called it Vael's whisper. Guides like me called it pay dirt. Then one winter it simply stopped, the way a hearth stops, and the whole range went cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

"You're dead," I said. "Or I am. One of us is definitely dead."

"I'm a fragment," the crow said. "A piece. A splinter of something that used to be much larger and is currently very tired. And you, Oscar Joubert, are bleeding on my mountain, holding a map that will get you killed unless you let me help you, which — based on your expression — you are going to resist for another few minutes out of principle. Shall I wait?"

I looked at the wound. It was bleeding a lot. I'm told that's the blood doing its job, but knowing that has never once slowed it down.

"How do you know my name?"

"The same way the dying man knew it. The same way *they* knew it." The crow hopped closer, unhurried. "Names travel, Joubert. Yours has been traveling toward Thornfell for a while now. You can outrun men. You cannot outrun a name once it starts moving."

"And what do you want?"

"What I've always wanted." Something moved behind the amber eye, some vast slow thing turning over in shallow water. "To finish what was started. To open what was closed. To bring home what was sold." The crow paused, and for the first time its voice lost a little of its iron. "But mostly, right now? I want you to stop bleeding, read the map, and stop pretending you haven't already decided to follow it. You read it at the waystation. I watched you read it. Your hands shook."

"My hands were cold."

"Your hands were shaking."

* * *

I bound the arm with a strip of shirt and one of Marta's dish towels, which she would have charged me for, and built a small fire in the lee of the ravine wall because shock is real even when talking crows aren't. The crow sat on a stone across from me and watched me take out the map with the expression — if a bird can be said to have expressions, and this one could — of a tutor watching a student finally open the book.

The map was good work. Wax-sealed, oilskin-backed, drawn on vellum by someone who understood that mountains are arguments between stone and water and drew both sides fairly. It showed the Thornfell massif in detail I'd never seen anywhere, including passes I'd found myself and sworn never to reveal. And threaded through it, in faded red ink, were lines that weren't roads at all. They converged, all of them, every line on the map, on a single point beneath the central peaks.

Beneath. The cartographer had drawn the inside of the mountain as carefully as the outside, and at the heart of it, marked with a seal I didn't recognize and a symbol like an antler crossed with a key, was a chamber labeled in a spidery antique hand:

*THE HOLLOW COURT.*

"Soul-traders," I said. "The dying man kept saying it. *The Court beneath.* I thought he was raving."

"He was raving," the crow agreed. "Raving accurately. There is a market under Thornfell, Joubert. It has operated for a hundred years this coming season. They deal in lanterns. Each lantern holds a soul, bought from the desperate, the indebted, and the grieving, and each bargain is sealed until the centennial reckoning — after which they are sealed forever." The crow's feathers lifted slightly, the way a man's hackles rise. "Forever is a long word. They use it lightly."

I stared at the map. And that's when I saw it — small, tucked into the upper corner where the foothills began, a mark I knew better than my own handwriting. A crooked little triangle for a roof, a stroke for a chimney, and beside it, in the same spidery hand:

*Emberlin.*

My village. The village I'd walked out of at fourteen with frostbite on two toes and nothing else, the village that starved the winter the grain failed, the village I had not gone back to in twenty years because there was nothing there to go back to except graves and guilt.

Except the map said otherwise. Because under Emberlin, faint as breath on glass, ran one of the red lines. Down. Into the dark. Toward the Court.

"They were there," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like sound through a closed door. "During the famine. They came to *my village.*"

"Yes."

"People sold—" I stopped. Started again. "There was a family up the lane. The Aldouses. They had grain that winter when nobody had grain. Everyone said they'd been blessed. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said what they needed to say to sleep at night," the crow said gently, which is a strange thing for a god's splinter to manage, and it managed it anyway. "Keep reading."

I kept reading. My eyes went where they went, past the Court, past the trade-roads of red ink, to a marginal note in the corner, tiny, cramped, written by a different hand than the cartographer's — hurried, desperate, the handwriting of a man writing while dying:

*Ledgers list sales by family name. J — see vol. 3.*

*J.*

Just a letter. One letter, and a volume number, and twenty years fell on me like a rockslide.

Because there was another thing about the famine winter that I didn't say to anyone, ever, not in drink, not in delirium, not in the prayers I still sent up out of habit to a god who never answered. There was a girl in my house that winter who ate less than I did. Who gave me her share and said she wasn't hungry, the way children lie, badly, with their eyes on the bread. Lise. Eight years old. Sharp-eyed, quick-fingered, laughed like water over stones. And in the spring, when the relief carts finally came up the valley, my mother told me Lise had gone to live with cousins in the lowlands, and I was fourteen and starving and I *believed her*, because believing her meant I didn't have to think about why Lise hadn't said goodbye.

She hadn't said goodbye because she'd been sold. Her soul, or her body, or both — and the map in my shaking hands suggested which ledger her name sat in, and suggested that whatever was left of her was sitting in a lantern under a hundred years of stone, waiting for a reckoning that would lock the door forever.

The fire cracked. Somewhere above the ravine, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant voices.

"Burn it," I said.

The crow said nothing.

"Burn it. That's the smart move. That's always been the smart move. I stay small, I stay unnamed, I guide smugglers and ask nothing, and I die warm in a bed someday instead of in a hole under a mountain. Burn the map, burn the waystation behind us, and go back to being nobody." I was gripping the vellum hard enough to crease it. "Tell me I'm wrong."

"You're wrong," the crow said.

"Why? Give me one reason. One good reason, and I'll listen."

The crow hopped down off its stone and walked — actually walked, with the deliberate dignity of a very old man crossing a room — until it stood close enough that I could see my own reflection doubled in its amber eyes.

"Because you've spent eleven years praying to a silent god," it said, "and calling it habit. Because a dying man bled out on a floor to put this map in your hands specifically. Because somewhere under this mountain there is a lantern with your family name on it, and it has been waiting twenty years for exactly one person foolish enough and stubborn enough to come for it." It paused. "And because, Joubert — you asked me for one reason, so I'll give you the truest one — I have been silent for eleven years too. And I am so *tired* of silence. Come with me. Let's both stop being quiet."

I looked at the map. At the little crooked triangle that had been home. At the red thread running down into the dark.

Reluctant hope is a stupid feeling. It's hope with its coat already on, standing by the door, pretending it can still leave. I felt it climb into my chest anyway and start unpacking.

"If we die under there," I said, "I'm haunting you."

"Vael doesn't haunt," the crow said, lifting off my knee in a whir of black feathers. "Vael *guides*. Now bind that arm properly and move — the men who ran will find braver friends by morning, and the road to Cinderhollow is three days' walk."

"Cinderhollow," I repeated. "Why Cinderhollow?"

"Because," said the crow, settling on my shoulder as naturally as if it had always lived there, "there is a priestess in Cinderhollow who knows what the Court's seal means. And you, Joubert, are going to need every friend a fool can gather."

I shouldered my pack, took one last look at the waystation smoke rising far behind me, and started north.

For the first time in eleven years, when I prayed that night — out of habit, I told myself — I had the distinct impression someone was listening.

It didn't make the ache go away.

It made the ache *point somewhere*.
