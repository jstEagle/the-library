## 4. The Priestess of Ashes

I had been defrocked for three years, four months, and eleven days when the man with the dead god's eyes walked into my chapel of ash and broken pews, and I want you to understand that I was counting. Not out of self-pity — well, not *only* out of self-pity — but because a woman who has given her whole life to an institution and been spat out by it needs to keep some kind of ledger, even if the only entries are grievances.

The Church of the Quiet Flame had stripped me of my vestments, my name in their rolls, and my right to administer any rite more sacred than blessing a fisherman's nets. They had left me, however, my habit, which I refused to surrender on principle, and my voice, which they had tried very hard to take and failed, because you cannot excommunicate a throat.

So I preached anyway. In Cinderhollow, on the border where the Thornfell mountains rise up like a wall between the decent world and whatever lives behind it, I preached in a roofless chapel with soot for incense, and I told anyone who would listen — which on most days was a goat, two drunks, and a boy who came for the warmth of my brazier rather than the fire of my words — that the Hollow Court's bargains were theft. That no soul should be collateral. That the church's long silence about what happened beneath the mountain was not discretion but complicity.

The goat found this compelling. The drunks found it an excuse to argue. The boy grew up and moved away, which is the fate of most congregations.

And then, on a gray morning with snow coming down like the sky had finally lost patience with us all, Oscar Joubert walked in out of the weather, and everything I had been saying into the void for three years, four months, and eleven days suddenly had ears.

* * *

I knew him before he spoke. Not by name — names came later, names always come later in my experience, usually attached to trouble — but by the quality of his silence. You spend enough years listening for gods and you develop an ear for the places they've stopped answering. This man carried that silence the way other men carry scars: carefully, constantly, in a way that meant it still hurt when the weather changed.

He stood in the doorway of my ruined chapel with snow melting off his shoulders, and he looked at the space where an altar used to be — I'd burned the old one myself, in defiance or grief, I've never fully sorted out which — and then at me, standing there in a habit gone gray at the hem, holding a poker over a brazier like a shepherd's crook.

"You're the priestess," he said. "The one they threw out."

"They didn't throw me out," I said. "They performed a solemn rite of removal, with hymns. It took four hours. There was a reading of my errors. It ran to eleven pages." I set down the poker. "You'll want to know if I'm bitter. I am. Enormously. It's the last honest thing the church left me."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, maybe, kept in a drawer somewhere.

"I'm looking for records," he said. "Soul-sales. Out of Thornfell. Twenty years back, give or take."

And there it is. Twenty years is how long I have been asking that question in one form or another, from pulpits and street corners and once, memorably, through the keyhole of a bishop's privy, which cost me a fortnight's penance and gained me nothing but the sound of a fat man's indigestion.

"Sit down," I said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Everyone would rather stand. Sit down anyway. What I'm going to tell you goes better sitting." I dragged a bench clear of ash with my foot. "Records of soul-sales don't exist. That's the first thing you need to understand. The Court keeps its ledgers beneath the mountain, and the church keeps its copy of the ledgers in a vault under the Basilica of the Ninefold Flame, behind a door blessed so many times it would take a saint to open it. I know because I asked to see them once. Formally. In writing. With citations."

"What happened?"

"I got the eleven pages." I sat across from him, close enough to the brazier that its heat bit at my knuckles. "But absence of records isn't absence of sales. The Court doesn't advertise. It doesn't need to. Famine does the advertising. War does the advertising. A mother with three children and no grain does the advertising better than any broker ever could. The Court just has to be *there*, patient as stone, when someone gets desperate enough to ask the wrong question in the right place."

His face did something complicated. I've spent a lifetime reading faces — it's half of preaching, the other half being stubbornness — and what crossed his was grief wearing the mask of a man doing arithmetic.

"You know someone who sold," I said.

"I'm asking the questions," he said.

"Of course you are." I let it go. Men like this one — and I have known a hundred of them, pilgrims and penitents and men carrying things too heavy for their spines — will tell you everything eventually, but only after they've decided you're safe, and safety is a currency they hoard. "Then let me tell you what I know, and you can decide afterward whether I'm worth the questions."

* * *

Here is what I told him, and I will tell it now the way I told it then, because some sermons bear repeating:

Before the Quiet Flame, before the Ninefold hierarchy and the bishops and the eleven pages of my errors, the people of the Thornfell valleys worshipped a mountain god. Vael, they called him. The Shepherd of the High Places. He wasn't a god of thunder or conquest. He was a god of paths — of keeping travelers alive, of showing the way through when the way seemed impossible. Every village in those valleys had a cairn to him. Every smuggler who ever crossed the passes whispered his name, whether they believed or not, because belief is optional but courtesy to the thing keeping you from falling off a cliff is not.

And beneath the mountain — this is the part the church buried, and I mean *buried*, I have seen the orders with my own eyes, sealed and signed and dated — beneath the mountain there was always a court. Older than the church. Older than Vael's cairns, maybe. A place where souls could be weighed and traded, where the desperate could buy miracles with the only coin they truly owned.

The early church fought it. That's in the oldest texts, the ones nobody reads because they're written in a dialect that died with the people who spoke it. Saints went down into the mountain with lanterns and came back with nothing, or didn't come back. And slowly, century by century, the fighting stopped. Not won — *stopped*. Doctrines were adjusted. The Hollow Court became a story mothers told children, then a story nobody told at all. And the church's silence was purchased, though I never proved with what, and the day I stood in the synod hall and said the word *complicity* out loud was the day the eleven pages began their journey toward my life.

"The bargains are theft," I said to him now, and my voice cracked on it the way it always cracks, three years later, because some sentences are load-bearing. "That was my heresy. Not that the Court exists — the archives prove the church has always known. My crime was saying that a bargain made by a starving woman for her child's grain is not a bargain at all. That consent extracted by desperation is rape of the soul. That every contract in that mountain is a crime wearing legal dress, and that the church's silence makes us accomplices." I looked at my hands, chapped and smoke-stained, hands that had once held a crozier and now held a poker. "They took my orders for telling the truth. So you can imagine how inclined I am to believe the truth has stopped being true just because you've walked in out of the snow."

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the chapel's broken rafters, and somewhere down the street a door banged, and the ordinary sounds of Cinderhollow went on around our ruin like water around a stone.

"There's a map," he said finally.

I confess my heart did something undignified. Three years of preaching into the cold, and here at last was a man with a *map*.

"Show me."

He hesitated. Of course he hesitated. But then he drew it from inside his coat — wax-sealed, bloodstained at one corner, handled by someone whose fingers had shaken — and he broke the seal right there on my brazier's edge, and unrolled it across both our knees.

I am not proud of what happened next. A defrocked priestess ought to have composure. But when I saw the seal's impression — the antlered crown pressed into green wax, the nine-pointed sigil beneath it — I made a sound. I have been told since that it was halfway between a laugh and a sob, and I believe it, because that is exactly what it was.

"That seal," I said, when I could speak. "Do you know how many times I've drawn that seal? In chalk, in charcoal, in the margins of stolen documents, trying to make bishops look at it. Do you know how many of them looked?" I touched the wax with one finger, gently, the way you touch a wound. "That is the mark of the Hollow Court's high brokers. The antlered crown. Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me. He said the Court beneath. He said—" and here the man paused, and something in his jaw tightened, "—he said my name. Knew it without being told."

I studied him then, really studied him, past the mercenary's weathered exterior and the careful blankness, down to the thing underneath, and I saw what I had seen in the doorway and now understood: a man praying to a god who wasn't answering. I'd seen that look in mirrors.

"This map leads to the Court," I said. "This route — these are the old pilgrim ways, the approaches the saints used. Nobody living knows these passages. Nobody living except," and I heard my own voice change, and I hated how eager it sounded, and I said it anyway, "except me. I spent six years in the archives before they burned me out. I read every account, every fragment, every saint's martyrology. I know the tunnel entrances. I know the tollgates. I know the rites that might — *might* — get a soul-lantern open without a broker's key."

He looked at me the way men look at a card they suspect is too good to be true.

"What do you want for it?"

Ah. There it was. The question every man asks a woman eventually, though usually with less honesty. I stood, and I brushed the ash from my habit, and I gave him the answer I had been rehearsing for three years, four months, and eleven days, to an audience of goats.

"Take me with you."

* * *

He didn't say yes immediately. I respect that. Immediate yeses are worth nothing; they're given by people who haven't counted the cost.

"You're a priestess," he said. "Defrocked or not. The mountain under Thornfell isn't a place for—"

"For what? For women? For clergy? For old women?" I held up fingers. "I'm fifty-one, I can walk thirty miles a day, I can read four dead languages, I can perform last rites, blessing of travelers, consecration of ground, and the Rite of Unbinding, which is the only sacrament the church ever wrote specifically for use against the Hollow Court, and which has been performed exactly twice in recorded history, both times badly. What precisely do you imagine I'm unfit for?"

"That's not what I—"

"It is what you meant. It's fine. Everyone means it." I picked up the poker and stabbed the brazier's coals harder than they needed stabbing. "Let me tell you what I actually am, Joubert — yes, I know your name, the dying man shouted it and you flinched, I notice things, it's the whole basis of my ministry. I am a woman whose entire life was built on the promise that the flame she served was honest. And then I found out it wasn't, and they took everything, and I stayed here, in this border town, at the foot of the mountain where the lies are buried, and I preached theft every single day to a goat." I looked up at him. "I am not asking you to rescue me. I am asking you to aim me. I have six years of knowledge and three years of rage and nowhere to put either. You have a map with a broker's seal on it and a face like a man walking toward something he shouldn't. Put us together and see what burns."

Silence. Snow ticked against the broken window above the empty altar niche. Somewhere the goat complained about something, because goats always find a grievance.

"The road's dangerous," he said at last.

"Yes."

"People following me. Bad people."

"Good. I've been wanting an audience of bad people for years. They listen better than goats."

"The crow—" he started, and stopped, and I watched him decide not to explain the crow, and filed that away with great interest, because a man who travels with an unexplained crow is a man with an unexplained everything.

"Joubert." I stepped close, close enough that he had to look down at me, which tall men hate. "Twenty years ago, someone in those valleys sold a soul. Maybe several. Maybe a whole famine's worth. And every one of those souls is sitting in a lantern under a mountain while brokers trade them like spice, and the church that should have burned that place to bedrock a thousand years ago instead signed a treaty and locked the evidence in a vault." My voice was shaking now. Let it shake. Three years of controlled delivery and here, at last, was somewhere to put the tremor. "I don't care what you're after. Revenge, rescue, redemption, plain greed — I've seen all four walk into chapels and they all kneel the same. Take me with you, or I will follow you anyway, and I promise you I am far more useful in front of you than behind you."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he did the thing that decided me, though he never knew it: he laughed. Short, rusty, surprised out of him like a bird flushed from cover — the laugh of a man who hadn't expected to find anything funny again, and resented it.

"All right," he said. "One condition. When I say run, you run."

"Priestesses don't run."

"Ex-priestesses run beautifully. I've seen it."

"We'll see," I said, and went to pack.

* * *

I packed quickly, because I owned almost nothing: three books the church hadn't thought worth confiscating, a knife, a spare habit, the poker (a woman must keep her crozier), and a jar of ash I had sworn an oath over, alone, on the night of my defrocking, that I would carry until the day the Court answered for itself. The oath had felt theatrical at the time. Most oaths do, in the moment. It's only afterward, when you're rubbing the ash into your palms on a frozen road beside a stranger with a dead god's silence, that you learn what you actually swore.

As we left the chapel, I paused at the doorway and looked back once. Eleven pages of errors. Four hours of hymns. A goat, two drunks, and a boy long grown and gone. I had hated that chapel and loved it in the same breath for three years, and leaving it felt less like escape than like a door finally opening in a wall I'd stopped believing had a door.

"Well?" said Joubert, already out in the snow.

"Coming," I said.

And I want to record, for whoever finds this account, the exact state of my soul in that moment: terrified. Utterly terrified. I had spent three years safe in my bitterness, and bitterness is a warm coat, and I was walking out of it into the cold with a man I didn't trust yet toward a mountain I had only ever known in documents, chasing a seal I had drawn a thousand times in chalk.

But underneath the terror — and I checked, the way you check a wound to see if it's still bleeding — underneath it, something had caught light. Something small and furious and familiar. Faith, maybe. Or just the shape faith leaves behind, the way a fire leaves heat in the stones long after the flames die.

Either way, it was burning. And I followed it up the road, into the snow, toward the crown beneath the mountain.

It was only later — much later, on the road, when we stopped to eat and I finally asked him about the crow — that I learned the next piece of the puzzle. He told me the crow spoke. Told me the cadence it used. And when he repeated the word it favored, the old valley-word, the shepherd's greeting that hasn't been prayed aloud in twenty years, I stopped chewing.

"*Vael*," I said.

He watched me carefully. "You know it."

"Joubert," I said, and my heart, that tired old bell, began to ring, "there is something you need to hear before we go another mile. And I warn you — it changes everything."

He set down his bread. The crow tilted its head. And I began, at last, to tell him what the archives said about where a god goes when it falls silent.
