## 5. A Thief's Debt

There are three rules to surviving in Cinderhollow, and I learned all of them the hard way by age eleven. One: never owe Master Corvane anything you can't pay with your hands. Two: never sit at a card table where the house smiles. Three: never, ever trust a man who loses well.

I broke rule two on a Tuesday. Rule three came for me the same night.

* * *

The Gilded Rat wasn't gilded and had never once, as far as anyone could prove, contained a rat — the cats saw to that. It was a low-ceilinged den off Tanner's Row where the ale was watered, the candles were tallow, and the games were honest in the way that a knife fight is honest: somebody was going to bleed, and it was rarely the house.

I wasn't there to gamble. I was there because Master Corvane's bookkeeper, a damp little man named Fessick with ink permanently under his nails, had decided my debt needed "reviewing," which in Corvane's language meant growing. Two hundred crowns, he'd said, tapping my ledger page like a doctor delivering bad news. Up from one-fifty since midwinter. Interest, he'd called it. I'd called it theft, but only inside my head, where it was safe.

Stealing to feed your siblings gets you indentured. Indenture gets you debt. Debt gets you owned. It's a machine, and every gear is shaped exactly like a person who once had somewhere else to be.

So I was working the Rat that night — not the tables, the pockets. A girl of sixteen with quick fingers and a face people forget the moment they look away from it; that's worth more than beauty in my trade. Beauty gets remembered. Forgettable pays rent.

That's when the tall man came in.

He didn't belong, and everyone knew it the way sheep know a wolf even when the wolf is wearing a very good coat. Grey-eyed, grey-stubbled, with the kind of stillness that mountain men have — the kind that doesn't fidget because fidgeting wastes warmth. He scanned the room once, slow, like a man counting exits, then sat down at the back table where Tam Vessler ran cards.

Tam Vessler ran crooked cards. Everyone knew that too. Tam had a partner in the kitchen named Doss who could deal seconds so smoothly you'd swear on your mother's grave the deck was clean, and Tam collected debts for Corvane on the side, which meant losing to Tam was less like gambling and more like donating.

The tall man ordered nothing. He put a silver crown on the table and asked to play.

I should have gone back to work. Instead I drifted closer, because I have this flaw — Anwen would later call it curiosity, Oscar would call it stupidity, and I call it being right most of the time — where if something smells like a disaster, I want front-row seats.

"Name's Tam," Tam said, shuffling. "You passing through?"

"Guiding a caravan north when the snow breaks." The man's voice was gravel over ice. "Waiting out the weather."

"Then let's make the weather profitable." Tam smiled his table smile. Doss, behind him, began dealing.

I watched the tall man lose. That was the first strange thing. Not lose badly — lose *well*. He bet just enough to look hopeful, folded at moments that looked cautious rather than informed, and groaned at the right times. He was playing bad cards beautifully, like an actor who'd studied the part. And underneath the performance, his eyes were doing something else entirely. They weren't on the deck. They were on Tam's hands. On Doss. On me, twice, quick as a pickpocket's touch.

Third hand, he lost four crowns. Fifth, six more. By the hour mark he was down twenty and the crowd around the table had thickened with that particular Cinderhollow hunger, the smell of blood in the water.

"Bad luck follows some men," Tam said pleasantly, raking the pot.

"It's been following me a long time," the man agreed. "It'll get bored eventually."

Another hand. Another loss. And then Tam leaned back, fat and satisfied, and said the words I'd been waiting for without knowing I was waiting:

"You're light, friend. Twenty short. We don't extend credit here — except." He tapped the table. "We do take other collateral."

Here it comes, I thought. The hook. This whole evening had been baited line and sinker, and now Tam would suggest the stranger work it off, or sign something, or —

"I have one thing worth more than twenty crowns," the stranger said. "A contract."

Tam's eyebrows went up. So did mine, mentally.

"Debt paper," the stranger said, and pulled a folded sheet from inside his coat. "Signed and sealed. Owed to me by a party in Thornfell. Worth two hundred crowns if you can collect. I'm tired of carrying it."

Two hundred crowns. My number. My exact, freshly-reviewed number. The candle flame seemed to lean toward the paper.

Tam took it, read it, and laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him. Because the paper wasn't a promissory note from Thornfell. It was an indenture contract. *Pip Marrow,* it read, in Corvane's clerk-hand, *indebted to the estate of M. Corvane, term indefinite, transferable at holder's discretion.*

Except it couldn't be. That contract lived in Corvane's strongroom. I'd seen the strongroom. I'd cased the strongroom twice, back when I still had dreams.

"This is stolen," Tam said, but he said it the way a man says *this is interesting.*

"It's transferred," the stranger said. "Find a magistrate who cares."

Now, here's the thing about Tam Vessler: he hated Corvane. Quietly, the way a dog hates its chain, but hated him. Corvane skimmed Tam's collections. Everyone skims everyone in Cinderhollow; it's practically our export industry. But owning a piece of paper that said *Pip Marrow* on it — that was power Tam hadn't had at breakfast, and Tam liked power the way drunkards like gin.

"The girl herself," Tam said slowly. "She's the collateral?"

"She's the debt. She's also the only forger in three towns who can do what Corvane used her for. Ask him why he never sold her south. Men have offered."

Every head at the table turned to look at me. I gave them my best unimpressed stare, which was difficult, because internally I was doing arithmetic at panic speed. If that contract was real, I belonged to a stranger. If it was fake, Tam would hand me to Corvane by morning and Corvane would add the insult to my ledger. Either way, Tuesday had gone to hell.

"Twenty crowns against the paper," Tam said. "And the girl covers the difference in work."

"One condition." The stranger laid his last coins down. "She walks out of here tonight. With me. Free of Corvane, free of you, free of this table."

"And if she runs?" Tam grinned. "You're buying a cat in a sack, friend."

The stranger looked at me then, properly, for the first time. Grey eyes, tired, with something buried deep in them that I'd spend weeks trying to name and finally would: grief, old and settled, like a stone at the bottom of a well.

"If she runs," he said, "she was never mine to hold."

* * *

They played it out. Of course they did; men always play it out, even after the ending's obvious, because the game stopped being about money pages ago and started being about who blinks.

The stranger lost.

Not quickly. He made Tam sweat for eleven hands, clawing back to even once — I clocked that hand, the only one where Doss's dealing went straight, and I understood: the stranger had been watching for it, betting big only on honest deals, bleeding slowly on crooked ones. He'd mapped the cheat within ten minutes of sitting down and then chosen to lose anyway, carefully, expensively, so that the loss would look like bad luck instead of intent.

Why? Because a man who wins against Tam Vessler leaves with a target on his back. A man who loses everything leaves with pity. Pity opens doors. Targets nail them shut.

He pushed his last coin across the felt and Tam swept up the pot, the coins, and the contract, and slid the paper across to — no. Wait. Let me get this right, because I've turned it over enough times to wear grooves in it.

Tam won the coins. The contract went to the stranger, because that had been the stake: coins against paper, loser takes nothing, winner takes all. Tam thought he'd taken twenty crowns off a sad mountain man and gained a favor to dangle over Corvane later. The stranger walked out with me.

Rigged. All of it rigged, both directions at once, a card trick performed on a card sharp. I figured that out around dawn. What I couldn't figure out, then or since, is what it cost him — because I asked Fessick's cousin later, the one who counts Corvane's liquid assets, and twenty crowns was real money to a caravan guide. Real money, spent deliberately, to buy a stranger's debt paper he could have gotten no other way.

Nobody does that. Nobody sane.

* * *

He was waiting outside the Rat's back door when my shift ended, breath steaming, a black crow hunched on his shoulder like a furry judgment.

"You're coming with me," he said. Not a question. Also, notably, not grabbing my arm, which put him ahead of every man who'd ever claimed to own me.

"I'm going home to sleep," I said, "and in the morning I'm going to find out what you paid Tam, and then I'm going to decide whether to rob you or thank you. Possibly both."

"Fair." He handed me the contract. My own name, my own signature — coerced at fourteen, but mine — and beneath it, fresh ink: a transfer notation, witnessed, sealed with plain gray wax. No crest. No claim. "Read it before you decide anything."

"I can read," I snapped, and then read it, because I'm a hypocrite with standards.

It said what he'd said. Ownership: Oscar Joubert. Terms: none listed. Duration: none listed. In the space where Corvane's contracts listed punishments in loving detail, this one had a single line, added in different ink, different hand: *Release available on request.*

"On request," I read aloud.

"You want release, say the word. You're free. You were free the moment Tam signed; the rest is paperwork."

"Nobody holds paper for fun, Joubert. What do you want?"

He was quiet long enough that the crow shifted on his shoulder and muttered something in a language that made my teeth itch. When he answered, he looked past me, down the street, toward the slums where my sisters were sleeping three to a bed.

"I need a forger. Court credentials — the Hollow Court, if you know the seal. Papers good enough to fool brokers who've had a century to learn their own marks. I'm told you're the best."

"I'm told that too," I said. "By me. Frequently."

"Will you do it?"

Ah, there it was. The trap I kept walking around the outside of, looking for the door. There's always a door. Corvane's contracts had doors — tiny ones, locked ones, doors painted on walls. So where was his?

"What happens," I asked slowly, "when we're done? Whatever 'done' is."

"Then you're done. Paper burns."

"Just like that."

"Just like that."

"Liar," I said, with feeling. "Nobody — " and here my voice did something embarrassing, cracked slightly, so I pushed through it — "nobody spends twenty crowns and risks Tam Vessler's knife for *just like that.*"

"No," he agreed. "They don't."

He didn't explain. That's the thing I want on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them: he didn't explain, didn't charm, didn't promise. He stood in the cold with a god-shaped bird on his shoulder and let the silence do his arguing, and the silence was infuriatingly persuasive.

"Why her?" I demanded. "You need a forger, fine. Why buy the debt instead of hiring me? Day rates exist. I'd have taken the job for coin."

"Would you have left your sisters with Corvane holding your paper while you rode into the mountains with strangers?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it.

"He'd have collected," I admitted. "Hostage-adjacent. That's his favorite genre."

"So I bought the paper instead." He shrugged, one shoulder, disturbing the crow. "Seemed simpler than a war."

* * *

I went home first. He waited in the alley — didn't follow, didn't watch the door, just said *dawn, the north road, come or don't* — and I climbed the stairs to our room and sat on the edge of the bed where Bette and small Sef were tangled together under one blanket, breathing in the syncopated rhythm of children who've learned sleep is food.

Corvane couldn't touch them now. That was the arithmetic. Whatever else this mad mountain man wanted, whatever trap I hadn't found yet, the ledger between us already showed a profit no one had ever shown me: two children safer tonight than last night, bought with a stranger's money and a stranger's patience.

I packed a bag. I took the silver candlestick — mine, fair stolen, sentiment attached — and I wrote a note for Bette that said *gone to work, back when I'm back,* which is the Marrow family motto and has served three generations without ever once being true.

At dawn, the north road. He was there, and so were two others: a woman in travel-worn priest's grays with ash-colored eyes and the posture of someone who'd been struck by her church and gotten up anyway, and she looked at me the way you look at weather — assessing, unhorrified, prepared.

"Sister Anwen," Oscar said.

"Pip," I said.

"Mm," said Sister Anwen. "A thief, a defrocked priestess, and a man who talks to birds. The Hollow Court will laugh itself into fits."

"That's the plan," Oscar said, deadpan, and the crow on his shoulder made a sound I swear on the silver candlestick was laughter.

We walked north, into the white shoulders of the world. I told myself I was along for the wages, for the adventure's end, for the moment I found the catch and pried it loose. I rehearsed my exit the way other girls rehearse weddings.

But somewhere past the first mile marker, Oscar fell into step beside me and asked, quietly, "Your sisters. Will they eat, while you're gone?"

"There's a neighbor. Paid in advance." Paid with his coin, actually, though I hadn't asked permission to spend it and didn't intend to mention it.

He nodded, and said nothing else, and somehow that nothing was louder than Corvane's entire library of threats. Somewhere behind us, Cinderhollow smoked in its valley like a banked fire. Ahead, Thornfell stood up against the sky with its secrets shut.

I caught myself walking faster to keep up. Not because I was owned.

Because, for the first time in four years, I was curious where I was going — and nobody, as far as the paper said, could stop me from finding out.
