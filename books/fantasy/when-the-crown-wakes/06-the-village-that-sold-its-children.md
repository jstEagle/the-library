## 6. The Village That Sold Its Children

The map wanted us to go home.

I didn't tell the others that. I told them it was a supply stop, a ruin with a well that might still work, two days off the pilgrim road. Anwen looked at me the way priests look at liars — which is to say, professionally, without judgment, and with a small notebook already open in her head. Pip just said, "Ruins have good pickings," and started planning what she'd steal from people who couldn't complain.

The crow said nothing, which from the crow was practically a sermon.

We came down out of the high pines on the third morning, and there it was: Ashenmere, my village, sitting in its bowl of dead fields like something the world had set down and forgotten to pick back up again.

Twenty years of weather had done its work. The roofs were mostly gone, collapsed into the houses like tired spines. The chapel still stood — they always do, built by men who thought God cared about load-bearing walls. Snow filled the lanes ankle-deep and unbroken. No tracks. No smoke. No crows on the gallows-tree at the crossroads, which even as a boy I'd thought was an odd thing for a village to own.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something. "Charming."

"You've been here before," Anwen said. Not a question.

"I grew up here."

Pip's head came around fast. "You *grew* up? I thought you were assembled out of spare parts and bad decisions."

"Both things can be true."

*You did not tell us,* the crow said, hopping along a fence rail beside me. Its voice had Vael's cadence still, that low rolling warmth, though the god behind it was mostly gone. *Why?*

Because saying it out loud would make it real. Because I'd spent twenty years walking past this valley on the far side of the ridge, taking the long way around like a man avoiding a debt collector. Because some doors you don't knock on unless you're ready for whatever answers.

I said none of that. "It wasn't relevant."

"Everything about you that isn't smuggling is relevant," Anwen said mildly, and walked her mule down into the village.

* * *

Ashenmere in winter had been loud once. I remembered that much. Sled runners, dogs, my mother's voice carrying across the yard, Lise laughing at something — always laughing, she laughed so easily, the way children do before they learn what things cost. The famine winter took the noise first. People stopped talking when there's nothing to say except the same sentence over and over: *it will thaw soon.* Then it took other things.

I walked the main lane with my boots crunching and let the memories come up slow, the way bodies come up out of deep water — bloated, wrong, recognizable.

There was the Harrow house, where old Tam Harrow had lived. Tam had been the first to deal with whoever dealt, back when the grain carts started arriving in the second month of winter. Nobody asked where the grain came from. That's the thing about hunger — it doesn't make you stupid, it makes you *polite*. You stop asking questions the way you stop complaining about cold feet. It's not worth the energy.

There was the well. Still there. I dropped a stone down it and never heard it land.

There was our house, or what was left of it, leaning against the hillside like a drunk against a wall.

And there was the field behind it, where I'd last seen my sister standing.

I didn't go in the house. Not yet. I stood in the yard and looked at the door hanging open on one hinge and thought, with the flat clarity that sometimes comes instead of feeling: *I am thirty-four years old and I have never once come back here, and now I know why.*

*Oscar,* the crow said quietly, from the fence post. Just my name. It had learned, somewhere in its long dim centuries, when a name is a question and when it's a hand on the shoulder.

"Not yet," I said.

* * *

Anwen found the ledger in the chapel.

Of course she did. Anwen could find scripture in a rockslide. She came out an hour later holding a book wrapped in oilcloth, her face doing something complicated, and said only, "Inside. All of you. There's a firepit in the vestry and I need light."

The vestry smelled of mouse droppings and old incense, the ghost of a thousand services soaked into the walls. Pip got a fire going with the efficiency of someone who'd burned a lot of furniture she hadn't paid for. Anwen unwrapped the book on the altar — because of course she used the altar, she had no superstitions left but all the instincts — and opened it flat.

It was a parish register. Births, deaths, marriages. And then, in the back pages, in a different hand, cramped and hurried:

Grain received. Names listed.

"Do you know what this is?" Anwen asked.

"A shopping list," Pip said.

"A *ledger*." Anwen turned the pages toward the firelight. "Look at the columns. Name, date, consideration rendered. Consideration *rendered*, Pip. This isn't a priest's hand. This is a broker's clerk. Someone from the Court came here, in the famine winter, and wrote down every bargain in a church book because—" she stopped, and her jaw tightened, "—because it was funny, I think. Or because they wanted it witnessed. Contracts of that kind need witnesses."

"Contracts for what?" Pip asked, and I watched her read the columns, watch her lips move over the names, and I knew the exact moment she understood, because she went very still, the way she does when she's casing a room and spots the guard.

"Bargains," Anwen said. "Souls sold for grain. Look at the amounts. A bushel of barley per soul. Two bushels if the soul was young — young souls keep better, apparently. There are forty-one names here." She looked up at me across the altar, and her voice gentled into something I hated, because it was pity. "Oscar. Is your sister's name here?"

I didn't want to look. Looking would make it a fact, and facts don't die of neglect the way hopes do.

I looked.

Third column from the top. *Lise Joubert. Midwinter. Two bushels barley, one of salt pork.*

Two bushels. My sister, who used to braid crow feathers into her hair because she liked how the birds followed her around, who once gave her whole supper to a beggar woman and lied about not being hungry — my sister had gone into a broker's book for less grain than I'd seen smugglers spill on the ground and laugh about.

Something surfaced in me then. Slowly. Like a drowned body coming up after years, swollen past recognition, and I knew it for what it was even as I pretended otherwise.

Guilt. Wearing my own face.

* * *

Here is what I remembered while the fire cracked and Anwen read names aloud in a low voice, a requiem for people who weren't dead, exactly, which is somehow worse:

I remembered being fed.

That was the shape of it. That was the whole ugly architecture of it. In the worst weeks of the famine, when the soup was mostly water and memory, there was always bread for me. Always. My mother would cut it thin and give me the bigger piece and say, *you're growing.* And Lise would sit across the table with her empty bowl and her careful smile and say she'd eaten earlier, she wasn't hungry, she'd had a big lunch, and I believed her, because I was nine and believing her was easier than counting the pieces of bread.

She starved politely. That's what I can't get past, twenty years on. She starved *politely*, so I wouldn't notice, and I noticed anyway, and I ate the bread.

And then one morning she was gone, and my mother wouldn't say where, and my father sat looking at his hands like they belonged to a stranger, and there was a sack of barley by the door that hadn't been there the night before. Two bushels' worth, maybe. I remember thinking the sack looked small. I remember thinking, *that's not enough to trade a person for,* and then I remember thinking nothing at all for a very long time.

They sent me away that spring. To an uncle in Thornfell who drank; then to a caravan master who worked me; then to the roads, where I learned the two rules that kept me alive. Never ask names. Never take sides. Stay small. Stay invisible. A man nobody remembers can't be blamed for anything, least of all by himself.

I'd thought I was being clever all these years. Turns out I was just hiding in the one place guilt couldn't follow.

Except it had followed me. It had been following me the whole time, wearing my boots, eating my rations, sleeping next to my fire. Every unanswered prayer to Vael had a little voice under it I'd refused to hear: *you ate the bread, Oscar. You ate the bread.*

"Oscar." Anwen's voice, close. "You've gone somewhere."

"Yeah," I said. "Sorry. Familiar territory."

Pip was fidgeting with her knife, flipping it, catching it. She does that when feelings get too close and she needs something mechanical between her and them. "So the parents sold their kids," she said. "For *barley*." Her voice had an edge on it. Pip was indentured at eleven for stealing bread. She has opinions about the price of food.

"For survival," Anwen said carefully. "The Court doesn't take souls from the desperate, Pip. It *offers*. That's what makes it obscene. It waits until a person has nothing left to lose but what they love, and then it offers them a way to keep breathing. And the terrible truth—the truth I preached until they stripped my office—is that most people say yes. Not because they're evil. Because they're *hungry*."

"My mother said yes," I said.

The fire popped. Everyone found somewhere else to look except the crow, who looked at me the way gods look at people, which is to say patiently, which is to say unbearably.

"She signed," I went on, because now the body was fully surfaced and thrashing and there was no pushing it back under. "Or pressed her thumb, or whatever you do. And my father carried the grain inside. And I ate the bread that came after, and I lived, and she—" I gestured at the ledger, at the neat clerk's hand, at *two bushels barley, one of salt pork*. "She's been in a lantern under a mountain for twenty years because I was hungry and she wasn't."

"That's not—"

"Don't." I held up a hand at Anwen. "Don't absolve me. I've had twenty years of practice absolving myself and it doesn't hold weight. I know what I did. I ate the bread."

"You were *nine*," Pip said.

"I was nine," I agreed. "And she was seven, and she gave me her supper twice a week for a month, and neither of us got a choice about any of it, and it still doesn't change what happened." I closed the ledger. My hands were steady, which felt like a betrayal too. "So we're going to the Hollow Court. Not for adventure. Not for the reckoning or the map or any of it. I'm going to get my sister back, and then I'm going to find out if there's a version of me that can live with having been fed."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire settled. Somewhere out in the ruined village, wind moved through a broken roof, a sound like a long exhale.

Then the crow spoke, and its voice was different — lower, older, with something underneath it that made the hairs on my arms stand up.

*You were not the only one who was fed, Oscar Joubert.*

I turned. "What's that supposed to mean?"

*This village kept a god,* it said. *A small one. A shepherd-god of the high fells. Grain and safe passes and lambs that lived through late snows — small miracles, small tithes. Your chapel's altar stone is older than your chapel. Do you know why the famine came at all?*

"The winter was hard," Anwen said slowly. "The histories say—"

*The histories say what the Court paid them to say.* The crow hopped down from the rafters, onto the altar, beside the ledger, and its black eyes caught the firelight like coals. *The Court does not only buy souls, priestess. It buys silence. It bought this valley's god's silence first — a starving god makes a cheap bargain — and with the god silent, the fields failed, the herds sickened, and the people grew desperate enough to sell their children for barley. The famine was not weather. It was marketing.*

The word landed in the vestry like a dropped anvil.

"They *caused* it," Pip whispered. "They starved a whole village to stock their shelves."

*I told you,* the crow said. *Bargains need witnesses. Desperation needs cultivating. The Court has never once taken anything. It has only ever arranged for things to be given.* It looked at me then, and I swear on every rule I ever kept, the bird looked sorry. *Your mother did not sell your sister, Oscar. She traded her for the right to feed you. Those are not the same transaction, though the ledger lists them together. The Court writes everything in one column. It is the great talent of brokers: making love look like commerce.*

I sat down on a vestry bench that groaned under me. Twenty years of a story I'd told myself — *they sold her, they chose the grain, I was the price paid and the profit both* — and the story had a crack straight through it now, and through the crack I could see the other shape of it: my mother pressing her thumbprint to a page in this chapel, weeping, asking not *what will you give me* but *will it be enough to keep him alive through thaw.*

It didn't make the guilt smaller. Nothing was going to make the guilt smaller; I suspected I'd be carrying it to my grave like a smuggler's pack. But it changed the weight's *shape*, and a man can carry a strange weight longer than a familiar one.

"So the Court starved Ashenmere," Anwen said, and her voice had changed too — the bitter faithlessness burned off it, and underneath was the thing they'd defrocked her for keeping: faith, banked coals, ready. "Forty-one souls. How many villages? How many famines that were actually harvests?"

*More than the ledgers hold,* the crow said. *But the ledgers are about to be open for audit.*

"What's that mean?"

*The centennial reckoning,* it said. *When every contract comes due and every seal is set forever. And when a certain fragment of a certain god finishes gathering what it has spent twenty years gathering.* It fluffed its feathers, and for half a heartbeat the shadow it cast on the wall was not a bird's shadow at all. *Patience, priestess. We have a lantern to steal first.*

* * *

We camped that night in the chapel, because none of us wanted to sleep in a house, and because Anwen said churches were as safe as anywhere and safer than most. Pip took first watch. I lay on a pew staring at the rafters and listening to the wind walk the ruined lanes outside, and I thought about a girl with crow feathers in her hair, and whether she'd grown, in there, in the dark, in the lantern. Whether souls grow. Whether she was still seven, waiting, or whether she was twenty-seven now and knew everything and was waiting anyway.

Around midnight I heard small footsteps on the flagstones, and then Pip's voice, low, from the end of my pew.

"Hey. Smuggler."

"Pip."

"When we get her out." She was picking at the wood grain, not looking at me. "Your sister. When we get her out. What happens to a person who's been a light in a jar for twenty years?"

"I don't know."

"Right." A pause. "Well. When we find out — I'll be there. For the finding out part." She scowled at the floor as if it had accused her of sentiment. "Somebody's got to teach her to pick locks. Can't have her growing up useless."

"Thank you, Pip."

"Don't thank me, it's creepy." She stalked back to her post by the door, shoulders up around her ears.

I slept, eventually. Better than I had in years, which felt like another betrayal, and I let myself have it anyway.

We broke camp at grey dawn, and it was Anwen who found it — pinned inside the ledger's back cover, a slip of paper in that same cramped clerk's hand, missed by everyone including, I think, the clerk who wrote it. A note. A schedule.

*Delivery of forty-one units to the Hollow Court, deferred at buyer's request. Deferred annually thereafter. Next scheduled transport: upon the centennial reckoning, when all accounts settle.*

Anwen read it twice. Then she handed it to me, and her hand was shaking.

"Oscar. They never moved her. None of them. Forty-one souls from this village, and the Court *kept them here*—"

The crow lifted off the altar in a rush of wings, out through the broken window, up into the pale morning sky, and its cry came back down to us thin and sharp as a blade being drawn.

Not a crow's cry. A warning.

Down in the valley, on the road we'd come by, riders. Six of them. Grey cloaks. Faces like blank coins.

Tallymen.

And they were riding straight for the village that had sold its children — for the ledger, for the slip of paper in my hand, and for the four travelers who'd been fool enough to dig it up.
