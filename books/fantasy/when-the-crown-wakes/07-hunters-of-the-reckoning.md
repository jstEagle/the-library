## 7. Hunters of the Reckoning

I want it on the record that I never wanted to be a hero.

Heroes get songs. Heroes get statues. What heroes mostly get, if you look at the statistics, is dead in a ravine with their boots stolen before the body's cold. I grew up in Sable Row, where we have a saying: *the tall grass gets cut first.* I had spent nineteen years being very short grass indeed, and it was working fine for me right up until a mercenary with a face like weathered rope lost a rigged card game to me on purpose and ruined everything.

"Left," said the crow.

"I see it," said Oscar Joubert, who did not see it.

"You don't," said the crow, whose name — and I want to be precise here, because precision matters when you're describing the insane — was either *Vael's Fragment* or *Soot* depending on which of them you asked, and both of them would lie about it. "The trail bends left past the split stone. The map says right."

"The map is twenty years old," Oscar said.

"The map was drawn by someone who wanted pilgrims dead," the crow said. "Trust me. I was there."

Anwen, riding behind me with her defrocked priestess's hands folded like she was about to say grace or break someone's fingers, leaned forward. "You were there for the drawing of a map?"

"I was there for most things," said the crow, smugly, "until recently."

This was the whole journey so far, if you're wondering. Four days out of Cinderhollow, up into the high shoulders of Thornfell where the pines grew crooked and the air tasted like iron filings, and every hour of it this: a god-fragment giving directions, an ex-priestess asking questions nobody wanted answered, and me, Pip Marrow, debt-broken thief, professional liar, current horse-owner by accident, trying to work out how my life had become a story with a crow in it.

The answer, as always, was siblings. You steal bread for three younger mouths long enough and eventually you steal from the wrong lockbox, and then you're indentured to Vasko Marrow-no-relation, and then a scarred mercenary sits down across from you at a card table, loses four hundred marks he clearly can't afford, and says "you're free, now earn it" like that's a normal thing to say. My contract papers were folded in his breast pocket. He'd won them off Vasko's man fair and square and then handed them to me like they were nothing, which meant they weren't nothing, which meant I owed him, and owing people is how short grass ends up cut.

So: heroes. Dead in ravines. Keep up.

* * *

The ambush came at dusk on the fourth day, at a place called the Shepherd's Stair, where the trail narrowed between two walls of granite and the horses had to go single file.

I knew something was wrong before anyone else did, and I'll tell you why: because I'd spent six years learning to read rooms, and a room can be a mountain pass too. There were no birds. There had been birds all day, cheeky little alpine things that screamed at us for crumbs, and now there were none, and the snow on the ledges above us was unbroken except —

Except not. Except there, and there, and there, three patches of white that didn't shimmer quite right, the way snow does when it's actually snow instead of somebody's idea of snow.

"Soot," I said quietly.

The crow's head swiveled. It had been dozing against Oscar's neck, and I watched it wake all the way in a single heartbeat, feathers going from rumpled to razor.

"Yes," it said. "Pip. Get down."

I got down. I'm a thief; getting down is half the profession. I hit the snow behind my mare just as the world tore open.

They came off the ledges like avalanches that had learned to hate. That's the only way I can describe Tallymen, having since seen more of them than any living person should: big, wrong-jointed figures in grey-white leathers, faces hidden behind smooth brass masks stamped with a single vertical line — a tally mark, Anwen told me later, one mark per soul collected, and some of those masks were *covered* in them, hundreds of thin lines scratched over each other until the brass went black. They carried hooked poles and lengths of chain, and they moved without a single wasted motion, and they made no sound at all. No shouting. No war cries. Just silence and arithmetic coming down the rocks at us.

There were six. I counted because counting calms me, and because I figured we'd want an accurate number for whatever passed for a funeral.

"Horses!" Oscar roared, and shoved his gelding's flank toward the open end of the pass even as he drew steel. "Run them through, don't stop, don't—"

A hook took the gelding's tack and ripped. The horse screamed — I will hear that sound for the rest of my life, horses shouldn't scream, everyone knows horses scream but you don't *know* it until — and went down sideways into the snow, and Oscar rolled clear with the kind of ugly efficiency that told you he'd done this exact roll too many times to count.

My mare bolted the other way, thank every saint I don't believe in, straight back up the pass — and a Tallyman stepped out from behind the split stone and put its hook across her chest, gentle as a shepherd, and pulled her down.

They didn't want us. I understood that about ten seconds in, huddled behind a boulder while brass masks turned slowly, methodically, scanning. They wanted the *map*. The one sewn into Oscar's coat lining, the bloodstained wax-sealed thing that had gotten a courier gutted and gotten us hunted and gotten me, personally, dragged up a frozen mountain in winter by a man who couldn't lose a card game properly.

"Pip." Anwen dropped down beside me. She had a knife out, which surprised me; she had the air of someone who fought with hymns. "How many?"

"Six. Two on the horses, four closing."

"Can you pick a lock?"

"Under these conditions? I can pick anything. Why?"

She pointed. Fifty feet up the wall of the pass, half-hidden behind an icicle curtain, was a rusted iron door set into the rock — some old shrine or hermit's cell, long abandoned, hanging open on a padlock that had been rained on for a century.

"That," she said.

"That's fifty feet of open ground with four tallymen in it."

"Yes," said Anwen. "So run well."

I want to be clear about what happened next, because later versions of this story — yes, there are later versions, Pip Marrow gets *songs*, apparently, and every single one of them is wrong — make it sound brave. It was not brave. It was the fastest I have ever moved in my life, skidding through snow with my heart trying to exit through my ears, while somewhere behind me steel rang on brass and the crow's voice cracked the air like a whip:

"*Thief! Down!*"

I dove. A hook whispered through the space where my spine had been. I came up under the Tallyman's guard — it hadn't expected that, none of them expected anything from me, I was the small one, the afterthought, and I've built my entire existence on being the afterthought — and drove my knife up under the brass mask's edge, into whatever was under there.

It made a sound like a ledger page tearing. It fell. I didn't look at what was under the mask. I still haven't. Some doors you leave shut.

Then I was at the door, and the padlock was frozen solid, and my hands were shaking so hard the picks chattered like teeth.

"Steady," I told myself. "Steady. It's just a lock. It's just a lock with death behind you instead of a dog."

Behind me, Oscar was losing.

I saw it in glances, because I couldn't not watch. He fought like water going downhill, all economy, no flourish, and he was still losing, because there were five of them now and they didn't tire and they didn't bleed where he cut them — or they bled something dark and slow, like ink. One hook caught his coat and tore it open and I saw him twist away from the second hook with inches to spare, and I saw the third Tallyman come around behind him, pole raised, patient as winter.

And the crow burned.

I don't have better words. Later, much later, Anwen would try to give me theology for it — *a fragment of the shepherd-god spending itself, grace made visible* — but what I saw was simpler and worse. The crow went up off Oscar's shoulder like a thrown brand, and it was *on fire*, black flames with a white core, and it hit the third Tallyman's mask face-first and the mask ran like candlewax. The thing beneath dropped its pole and staggered, and where the flame touched it, it came apart, not burning but *unwriting*, edges going soft and grey and gone.

The crow wheeled overhead, brighter than it had ever been on the road, brighter than any bird has a right to be, and its voice came out doubled now, its own dry rasp underneath and something vast and old speaking through it:

"*These are not yours to tally.*"

The remaining four stopped. All four, mid-motion, like clockwork figures with a hand clapped over their gears. Their brass heads tilted upward in unison, and for one long moment the Shepherd's Stair held nothing but wind and firelight and the screaming of my mare, still tangled and thrashing below.

Then the nearest Tallyman spoke. Its voice was flat as a ruled line.

"The reckoning comes. All accounts settle. Return what is borrowed, fragment, or be entered among the debts."

"We'll keep the account open," the crow said, in its own voice alone now, and it sounded tired. God, it sounded tired. "Run along."

And they went. Backwards, unhurried, melting into the snowfield shadows until the walls of the pass stood empty, and the only proof any of it had happened was a torn-open mask cooling in the drifts and two horses down and Oscar Joubert on one knee in the snow, breathing like a bellows, blood sheeting down his forearm from a hook-gash.

* * *

We lost the horses. Both of them. My mare had broken a leg in the tangle and Oscar did what had to be done while I stood with my back turned and my eyes stinging, telling myself it was the wind. His gelding had run off into the dark with its tack shredded and wouldn't come back for whistling or praying. By full dark we were afoot, four of us and a scorched, diminished crow, hauling our packs up toward the rusted iron door Anwen had spotted, because sleeping in the open after a Tallyman ambush seemed like the sort of decision you don't get to regret twice.

The hermit's cell was one room, stone-floored, with a dead hearth and a shelf of psalters gone soft with damp. Anwen got a fire going. Oscar sat against the wall letting her stitch his arm, jaw tight, making none of the noises a normal man would make.

I sat by the window with my knife across my knees and did inventory. One knife, dulled. Two lockpick sets, one snapped. Zero horses. One contract, technically mine now, binding me to a man I'd known nine days. One map leading to an underground court where souls got sold, which I was apparently committed to breaking into.

"So," I said, because the silence was worse than talking. "Those things. They knew the crow. Called it *fragment*. Called what it did *borrowed*." I looked at the bird, huddled on the shelf amid the ruined psalters, its feathers singed and dull. "What exactly did you borrow, and from whom, and what happens when they collect?"

The crow said nothing.

"They said the reckoning settles all accounts," Anwen said quietly, snipping thread. "That's centennial language. Court law. Every bargain sealed forever when the hundred years turn." She tied off the stitch and looked at the crow with an expression I couldn't read — pity and hunger mixed together, a scholar looking at a text that might burn her. "You're not hiding from the Court, are you. You're *running out of time* alongside it."

"Crow," Oscar said. Not a question. A warning.

"There are things I haven't told you," the crow said. Its voice was smaller now, sanded down. "Yes. And I'll tell them. Before the reckoning, I swear it on — " it paused, and something flickered behind its eyes like a lamp guttering, "— on what's left of me. But not tonight. Tonight you should sleep."

"Oh, wonderful," I said. "Mysteries. Love a mystery. Mysteries have never once gotten anyone killed."

"Pip." Oscar's voice, low. I turned and found him watching me with those grey eyes that missed nothing, the ones that had read my tells across a card table and folded four hundred marks into my hands anyway. "You counted six. You got the door open under pressure. You put a knife where it needed to go." He nodded once, the smallest nod, the mercenary's nod, worth more than applause. "Good work."

And here is the humiliating part, the part I'll deny under oath: something in my chest went warm and stupid and glad. Nineteen years of being the smallest thing in every room, the expendable one, the kid they sent through the window because windows eat kids, and one gruff word from a man who owned my contract and I glowed like the blasted crow.

"I didn't do it for you," I muttered. "I did it because dying unpaid is the one sin Vasko's people never forgive."

"Naturally," said Oscar.

"Obviously," said Anwen, smiling.

"Obviously," echoed the crow, and for once there was no smugness in it. It hopped down onto the sill beside me, close enough that I could feel faint warmth coming off its scorched feathers, like a stone that had been near a fire. "But for what it's worth, small thief: you ran well."

Outside, far down the valley, something answered. A single horn note, long and flat and toneless, rolling up from the darkness like a tally mark being drawn across the sky. Then another. Then another.

Three notes. Three travelers accounted for.

Counting us.

Oscar was on his feet with his hand on his knife before the echo died. Anwen snuffed the candle. And the crow looked out at the darkness with its bright, borrowed eyes and said, very softly:

"We need to reach the tunnel before they finish their arithmetic. Walk fast, children. The reckoning isn't waiting, and neither am I."
