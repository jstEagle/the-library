## 8. Road Beneath the World

The map had a third layer.

I want to be clear about how I felt about this. The map had already cost me a waystation, a night's sleep, and most of my faith in the fundamental decency of strangers. It had blood on it — not mine, which I counted as a personal victory — and it had led us to a village where every house was a mouth full of dark and every field grew nothing but stones. And now Anwen, kneeling in the ruins of a shrine with her sleeves pushed up like a woman about to scrub a floor, informed me that we'd been reading it wrong.

"Wrong," I said. "We've been walking for nine days on your interpretation."

"And now I'm correcting it." She traced a line of faded ink beneath the wax seal, something hidden under the old light and visible only when she tilted the parchment toward the fire. "There's a watermark. Church work. See the pilgrim's staff? This isn't a treasure map, Joubert. It's a route."

"A route to what?"

"To under." She looked up at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the bitter defrocked-priestess scowl she wore like armor, but an older expression, one that belonged to a woman who had once believed in something without flinching. "Under Thornfell. There were pilgrim tunnels, before the church sealed them. Before the church decided it was more profitable to forget what it had built. This is one of them."

Pip, who had been picking through the shrine's collapsed altar with the enthusiasm of a magpie at a funeral, popped her head up. "Sealed how? Locked doors I can do. Sealed could mean anything. Sealed could mean walled up. Sealed could mean *warded*."

"Stone," Anwen said. "And prayer. The stone will yield to anyone who knows the rite. The prayer—" She hesitated, and I watched her decide how much truth to spend. "The prayer will only open for someone who still carries the faith. Even if they've lost everything else."

"You're defrocked," Pip said helpfully.

"I'm aware."

"They took your—"

"*I'm aware.*"

The crow sat on my shoulder through all of this, silent as it had been since the Tollgate, since it had paid my debt of names and gone dim as a guttering candle. It weighed almost nothing now. That frightened me more than Tallymen or sealed doors. A god should have some heft to it, even a broken one. Mine had become the weight of a thought you're trying not to think.

"Show me the route," I said.

* * *

Anwen talked while we walked, because talking kept her from praying, and I think praying scared her worse than anything under the mountain could.

"The tunnels were cut in the first centuries, when the mountain god was still... attended." She chose the word carefully, the way you'd step around a sleeping dog. "Pilgrims came from all over the lowlands to walk into Thornfell and speak their griefs into the dark. Vael heard them. That was the whole religion, really. No sacrifices, no tithes. You walked down, you said what you'd lost, and you walked back up lighter. Then the church hierarchy decided that a god who charged nothing was a god who couldn't be administered, and the Court—" she spat the word like a pit — "the Court was happy to help them forget. Pilgrims stopped coming. The entrances were sealed. And the Court moved into the space the faithful left empty, the way rot moves into a fallen tree."

"And nobody noticed a cathedral growing under their holy mountain?"

"People notice what they're taught to notice." Her voice went flat. "I preached against the Court for eleven years, Joubert. Do you know how many of my own congregation reported me? Fourteen. I counted. They weren't evil. They just couldn't afford to hear it. Their mortgages were held by Court brokers. Their sons' debts. Their daughters' dowries. When your whole life is collateral, you learn not to look at the ledger."

Pip walked ahead of us, pretending not to listen, listening to everything. She'd been quiet since the Tallyman fight — quieter than her particular brand of quiet, which usually involved commentary. Now she just watched the treeline thin into scree, and scree into bare rock, and rock into the first black shoulder of the mountain proper, and her hand kept drifting to the scar on her cheek where the Tollgate's lie had marked her. She touched it the way other people touch lucky charms. In reverse.

We found the entrance at dusk, if dusk means anything on a mountainside where the sun gives up early. A shelf of granite, a fall of scree, and behind it — when Anwen knelt and pressed her palm flat and spoke words in a language that made the crow lift its head — a seam in the world.

The stone didn't open so much as *agree* to disagree with itself. A crack widened into a gap, the gap into a doorway, and cold air breathed out at us, air that smelled of deep places and old incense and something underneath both, something mineral and alive, like the inside of a bell.

"Rite holds," Anwen said, and her voice shook only a little. "Faith holds. Apparently they missed a spot when they defrocked me."

"What did you say to it?" Pip asked.

"'I am small, and the mountain is large, and I come anyway.' It's the opening line of the pilgrim's litany." Anwen smiled without any humor in it. "It's also, as it happens, true."

I lit the lantern. We went in.

* * *

I've guided smugglers through passes that would make a goat write home to its mother. I've crossed icefields where the wind files your bones down to essentials. I thought I knew what dark was.

I didn't know what dark was until I stood in a tunnel built by believers, where the dark wasn't absence but presence — a dark with intention, a dark that had been waiting a long time for footsteps.

The tunnel descended in long switchbacks, walls dressed with carvings worn soft by centuries of pilgrim hands. I ran my fingers along them as we walked: figures climbing, figures kneeling, figures with antlers standing watch. Every few hundred paces there'd been a niche for a votive lamp, long empty, and Anwen lit each one as we passed with a word and a touch, so that our path unspooled behind us like a rosary being told.

"It shouldn't still work," she murmured, half to herself. "The lamps. The rites are dead, the order is scattered, I'm a heretic with a criminal record. And yet." She cupped the flame in her palm like a bird she was afraid would fly off. "Maybe faith isn't something the church owns after all. Maybe it's just something the church *rents*."

"Careful," I said. "That's the kind of talk that got you defrocked."

"That's the kind of talk that got me *defrocked*, yes. It's also the kind of talk that's going to get us to the bottom of this hole alive."

Pip brought up the rear, and I caught her looking back at the lit lamps more than once, counting them, because Pip counts everything — exits, coins, lies, kindnesses. Kindnesses last, in her ledger. She keeps them in a separate column and doesn't trust any entry in it.

We'd been descending maybe three hours when the wall changed.

It started as a flicker at the edge of the lamplight, the kind of shimmer you blame on tired eyes. Then another. Then dozens. The stone around us had begun to glow — faintly, fitfully, the way stars do through moving cloud — and when I pressed my hand against the rock, the glow gathered toward my palm like minnows toward bread.

"Oh," Anwen said softly. "Oh, they're still here."

"Who's here?" Pip asked, though her voice said she already knew, the way voices do when the answer is too big for daylight.

"The sold ones." Anwen's hand hovered over the stone, not quite touching. "When the Court takes a soul, it doesn't destroy the body's light. It banks it. Stores it. The overflow seeps into the mountain itself. These are—" She swallowed. "These are souls in the walls, Joubert. Leaking. Waiting."

The cavern opened around us then, wide as a drowned cathedral, and I understood that what we'd been walking through was only the porch.

They hung in the rock everywhere I looked. Pale lights, hundreds of them, thousands, drifting slow as snowfall in reverse, rising through the stone in currents I could almost read — migration patterns of the imprisoned. Some burned steady. Some pulsed, quick and arrhythmic, like hearts trying to remember their tempo. And some flickered in patterns that were almost, almost like speech.

Pip had gone very still. "Is that what happens to you?" she asked, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she didn't bother fixing it. "If they take your soul. Is that where you end up? In a *wall*?"

"No," Anwen said gently. "This is the spillage. What leaks out. The rest goes into the lanterns, into the vaults, into the ledgers." She turned to me. "But somewhere below, there are lights that aren't leaking. Lights that are *kept*."

I wasn't listening anymore.

Because one of the lights had stopped.

In a cavern full of drifting souls, one small pale flame near the far wall had gone motionless, and it was pointed — I know how that sounds, lights don't point, but it *pointed* — at me. At me specifically, the way a dog points, the way a child points across a market square at the one face it's been watching for.

My mouth was dry. My heart was doing something embarrassing in my chest.

"Lise," I said.

Just her name. Just two syllables into a cavern full of strangers' suffering, and I felt like a fool the moment they left my mouth, because twenty years is twenty years and a name is not a key—

The light flared.

Not much. A candle catching its breath. But it flared, and it leaned toward me through the stone, straining against whatever held it, and the lights nearest it dimmed slightly as if making room, the way a crowd parts.

"Joubert." Anwen's hand was on my arm. "Joubert, what—"

"She heard me." My voice came out wrong, scraped, like I'd been the one buried in stone for twenty years. "Anwen. *She heard me.*"

"That's not possible. Souls in the stone don't— they're residue, they're—"

The light pulsed again. Twice. Quick. Quick. Like someone tapping a rhythm on a window. Like a signal we'd invented as children, tapping on the wall between our rooms when we were supposed to be asleep: *still here. still here. still here.*

I was moving before I decided to move. Across the cavern floor, over fallen pillars furred with mineral bloom, until I stood at the wall with my palm flat against the stone and the light pressing up against my hand from the other side, warm, impossibly warm, warmer than anything in this frozen country had a right to be.

"I'm here," I said. "Lise. I'm here. I found the map, I found the road, I'm coming. Do you understand me? I'm coming."

The light flared so bright it threw my shadow up the wall, and then it did something that broke me in a place I hadn't known was still intact.

It settled. It went calm. Steady as a hearth-fire. Not hope, exactly. Something better than hope. The light of someone who has stopped calling for help because they've finally heard footsteps.

Behind me, Pip made a small sound. Anwen was saying something about the reckoning, about days remaining, about how the centennial seals everything permanently, how after that night no bargain can ever be broken, no lantern ever opened, no soul ever freed — and I heard her, I heard every word, and every word landed like a stone dropped down a well.

Twenty years I'd thought my sister was dead. Dead was a thing I knew how to carry. Dead has rituals. Dead has graves.

She wasn't dead. She was *down*. Twenty feet of rock and a hundred years of theft away, tapping on the wall, waiting for me the way she used to wait at the village gate when I came back late from the hills — patient, certain, keeping my supper warm.

And in a handful of nights, a woman wearing silk gloves and a broker's smile intended to lock that door forever.

The crow stirred on my shoulder. It had gone bright again — not much, a coal rather than a candle, but brighter than it had been since the Tollgate — and when it spoke, its voice was Vael's cadence, the cadence I'd prayed into for thirty years and gotten silence back.

*"Now you understand why I spent everything,"* it said quietly. *"Come. The way down is short, and the night is shorter."*

I took my hand off the stone. The light stayed steady. Watching me go. Trusting me to come back.

I have broken contracts, abandoned employers, and once left a baron's son hanging by his bootlaces from a bridge, and I have never once lost sleep over any of it. But walking away from that wall, knowing what waited behind it, knowing what was coming to seal it shut —

That, I felt.

"Anwen," I said, turning back toward the descending dark, and my voice didn't shake, which surprised us both. "How fast can you pray?"
