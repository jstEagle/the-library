## 9. The Tollgate of Names

I have prayed in a great many places. In a cathedral of white marble where the echo made even a whisper feel like doctrine. In a ditch, once, with an arrow through my shoulder and my order's name already struck from the rolls. In a brothel in Cinderhollow, because the woman who ran it paid for blessings and I had stopped being particular about whose roof I blessed from.

But I had never prayed *downward* before.

The pilgrim tunnel went into the mountain at an angle that felt less like walking and more like being swallowed politely. The old church had built it — my old church, though the church and I had parted ways with considerably more heat than a simple defrocking usually requires — and you could see it in the stonework. Every fifty paces a niche carved into the wall, meant to hold a votive lamp. Every hundred, a relief of Vael's antlered crown worn smooth by centuries of pilgrim fingers. The church had known this road existed. The church had sealed it anyway, and called the sealing piety.

That was the thing about my former brothers and sisters that no heresy trial ever managed to wring out of me: they were not liars. They believed every word they said. That was what made them dangerous.

"Anwen." Pip's voice floated up from behind me, thin in the dark. "Question. When you said the tunnel was 'perfectly safe,' was that priestess-safe or merchant-safe?"

"There's a difference?"

"Merchant-safe means nothing's going to eat us. Priestess-safe means nothing's going to eat our *souls*, which is somehow worse."

"She has you there," Oscar said. He was ahead of me, as he always was, moving through the dark like a man who'd been born owed to it. The crow rode his shoulder, and its eyes caught the light of my little lamp and threw it back wrong — too gold, too deep, like looking down a well that looked back.

"The tunnel is safe," I said, with the mild patience I had once used on novices and now used on thieves. "The destination is another matter entirely."

"And you're only mentioning this *now*," Pip said, "because?"

"Because if I'd mentioned it on the surface, you'd have argued on the surface, and I wanted at least one day of travel where nobody argued with me."

Oscar made a sound that might have been a laugh in a man who could still afford one. The crow croaked something that was definitely a laugh. Even in the dark, even here, that bird laughed like it remembered being divine, and every time it did, something in my chest turned over and knocked.

Because I knew what it was. I had known since Cinderhollow, since the moment it spoke and the cadence of it landed on me like a hand on the back of the neck. I had spent eleven years telling anyone who would listen — and afterward, mostly, no one listened — that the Hollow Court's bargains were theft. That souls could not be sold, only stolen, and that any god who permitted it was either complicit or conquered. They stripped my vestments for it. Called it pride. Called it bitterness.

It was neither. It was arithmetic. And somewhere beneath Thornfell, in a cathedral-cavern full of lanterns, the sum of all that theft was waiting for us, and the last coin of the god who'd tried to stop it was riding on a mercenary's shoulder, getting brighter every time it spent itself to save us, and dimmer every time after.

I had been a priestess long enough to know what spending yourself looks like. I had done it in smaller denominations for years.

* * *

We camped in one of the wide rest-chambers the old builders had cut, where the ceiling rose high enough that my lamp couldn't find it, and the walls were covered in graffiti of a very specific kind. Not names. Prayers. Thousands of them, scratched and painted and pressed into the stone by hands that had come this way hoping to buy back someone they loved.

*I gave a winter for my mother's breath. Let it be enough.*

*Take my singing. Take my years. Give him back his eyes.*

*Vael who walks the mountain, look down. Look down. Look down.*

I read them by lamplight while the others slept, and I did not pray, because I had found over the years that prayer without certainty is just talking to yourself with better posture, and I was not yet ready to admit that was all I'd ever been doing.

"Which one are you?" Oscar's voice, low, from the dark near the tunnel mouth. He didn't sleep much. I'd noticed. A man who guides smugglers learns to sleep like a cat, in installments, with one ear open.

"I'm not asking anything of the stone, if that's what you mean."

"That's not what I asked."

I turned my lamp toward him. He sat with his back against the wall, arms loose over his knees, watching me with those grey eyes that took everything in and gave back almost nothing. The crow slept against his neck, feathers dull, breathing in a rhythm that was almost — but not quite — a heartbeat.

"No," I admitted. "None of these are mine."

"But you know the road."

"I helped seal it."

He didn't move. Didn't judge, either, which was his particular cruelty and his particular kindness both. He just waited, the way stone waits, until the words came up out of me whether I wanted them to or not.

"I was twenty-six," I said. "Junior to the Archlector of Cinderhollow, which sounds grander than it was; mostly I catalogued relics and corrected the spelling in novice sermons. A delegation came from the mountain diocese. Sealed documents. They told us the pilgrim tunnels into Thornfell had become... compromised. That things below had learned to imitate holy voices. That the faithful who descended were lost, and the merciful thing — the *orthodox* thing — was to close every road and forbid every descent, so that no more souls wandered into the dark."

"And you believed them."

"I *wanted* to believe them." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Do you know how heavy it is, Oscar Joubert, to be the person in the room who says *wait, that doesn't add up*? Everyone looks at you like you've brought a smell in with you. Eleven years I've carried that weight, and I have gone over that council session ten thousand times, and I have never once found the moment where I could have said the right thing loudly enough. So yes. I believed them. Or I let myself be believed *with* them, which amounts to the same sin with better paperwork."

"What changed your mind?"

"A woman came to the cathedral door three winters later. Her son had gone under the mountain to buy back his daughter's soul — famine debt, the whole ugly story — and she'd had a letter from him. One letter, written before he descended, saying *if I don't come back, Mother, it's because I bought something worth more than the price.* And the church had told her he was lost. Condemned. Beyond rites." I looked at my hands. "And I stood there in my vestments, in a building full of gold, and I lied to her face. I gave her the words we give. *He is with the god now.* And she thanked me. She *thanked* me, and she walked out into the snow, and I sat down in the vestry and understood that I had finally, completely, run out of ways to call theft by another name."

"So you preached against the Court."

"So I preached against the Court. Loudly. In the nave, during the Feast of the Sealing, which was tactically stupid and spiritually necessary. They took my vestments, my vows, and my name from the rolls." I smiled, and it felt like an old scar flexing. "They left me the certainty, though. That's the one thing they can't confiscate. I know what's under this mountain. I've always known. I just needed someone mad enough to walk me down to prove it."

Oscar was quiet a long moment. Then: "The tollgate. You knew about it."

Of course he'd gotten there. He got everywhere first; it was why he was still alive.

"Yes."

"And you didn't mention it."

"It doesn't matter what I mention," I said. "You'd walk into it regardless. Your sister is behind it. The only question the gate will ask is what each of us is willing to lose to pass, and I have been answering that question for eleven years without being asked. At least this way it's official."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he rarely did, like I was terrain worth mapping — and said nothing, which from Oscar Joubert was practically a blessing.

* * *

The gate announced itself by silence.

For two days the tunnel had murmured around us — water somewhere in the rock, the tick and settle of the mountain, the small noises of four travelers and one bird. Then, all at once, the murmur stopped, the way conversation stops when a stranger enters a room, and we rounded the last bend and there it was.

A wall of black stone spanning the tunnel floor to ceiling, seamless except for a single archway at its center. No doors. No bars. Just an opening, and standing in it —

I want to say statue, because statue is the comfortable word. But statues do not breathe, and this breathed. Slowly. Tectonically. A figure of grey granite some twelve feet tall, robed in its own stone, faceless except for a shallow depression where a mouth should be. Its head was bowed, as if in eternal courtesy, and set into its chest, where a heart would go, burned a single pale flame that cast no shadows.

Behind it, through the arch, the tunnel continued downward into a darkness that felt *occupied*.

"The Tollgate of Names," I said. My voice sounded very small. "Every account agrees on the essentials. It stands between the pilgrims' road and the Court's domain. It asks each traveler for their true name. Not the name they're called. The name they *are*. And it takes payment accordingly."

"Payment how?" Pip asked. She'd gone very still, which for Pip was the loudest alarm she owned.

"The accounts differ." I kept my eyes on the warden. "Some say it takes the name itself — you forget who you are. Some say it weighs the name and takes what it finds truest. The church records call it *the honest mirror*. Which tells you the church feared it more than any demon, because the church never names anything honestly unless it has to."

"Then we go around," Pip said.

"There is no around. This wall runs into the living rock for a mile in either direction. Believe me — I read the surveys. Twice. Bored out of my mind in a relic archive, reading surveys of a tunnel I was never supposed to think about." I stepped forward. "There's a protocol. There's always a protocol. You approach alone. You state your business. It asks. You answer, or you refuse, and refusal has its own price — the accounts say the gate simply does not open, and the traveler must turn back. But the accounts also say no one has ever refused."

"Why not?" Oscar asked quietly.

"Because everyone who comes this far," I said, "has already decided what they'll pay."

I felt him absorb that. Felt the whole group absorb it — Pip hugging her elbows, the crow's feathers lifting along its neck, the pale heart-flame of the warden pulsing like a slow tide.

Then Oscar moved forward, and I put out my arm.

"No."

He looked at my arm. Then at me.

"You first," I said. "Not because I'm brave. Because I'm expendable, and I know exactly what I'm offering, and none of you do yet. Watch what it takes from me. Learn the rules off my loss instead of yours. That's not sacrifice, Oscar, that's *tactics*, and I'll thank you not to make it noble."

"That's a lie," he said. "You're the worst liar in this company, and you travel with a professional thief."

"Then consider it penance," I said, "with tactical benefits," and I walked past him before my courage could file a formal complaint.

* * *

Up close, the warden was worse. The stone of it wasn't inert — it had grain, like wood, like it had grown rather than been carved, and the breath of it moved the air in slow tides that smelled of deep places and cold iron. The flame in its chest threw light onto my face and took none of it back.

I stopped three paces out, spread my empty hands, and did the thing I had rehearsed in my head for two days and my whole life besides.

"I am a traveler on the old road," I said. "My business lies beneath. I come to unmake a theft."

The depression where its mouth should be opened. What came out was not sound so much as *weight* — a voice like a millstone turning, felt in the teeth and the breastbone.

**ANWEN.**

Just the shape of it, really. A question built from half my name, offered like a keyhole waiting for a key.

My heart was going like a drummer boy's. I made myself breathe. Made myself remember the woman in the snow, and the letter, and the eleven years of talking to myself with better posture.

Here was the truth I had arrived at, somewhere in the dark between the rest-chamber and this arch: I did not know what waited below. I did not know if the crow was truly Vael, or a fragment, or a fraud, or a grief-dream the mountain had learned to wear. I did not know if the Court could be broken, or if Lise Joubert's soul could be freed, or if anything I had faith in amounted to more than wishful thinking wearing vestments.

Sacrifice without certainty. That was the whole of my theology now. Everything else had been confiscated.

But I knew this: if I refused, I turned back, and I had nowhere to turn back *to*. And if I lied to a thing called the honest mirror, I suspected I would learn precisely what my name weighed, and I did not think I could survive knowing.

So I gave it the only currency I had left.

"My name is Anwen of the Order of the Veiled Lamp," I said clearly, "sworn at fifteen, defrocked at thirty-seven, and I am — I *was* — the last priestess my order trained who could read the old rites. Take what you must. Only let me pass."

**ANWEN OF THE VEILED LAMP,** the millstone voice said, almost gently, **THE NAME IS WEIGHED. THE TRUTH IS FOUND. THE PRICE IS TAKEN.**

The flame in its chest flared white.

And my life left me.

Not all of it — I understood that even as it happened, understood it the way you understand a tooth coming out, the absence arriving whole and shaped. It took the order. All of it. I felt the cloister go first, the bell schedule, the taste of the morning bread, the exact grey of the novices' eyes. Then the studies — the old script dissolving off the shelves of my mind like frost off a window, the rites I had promised to carry, *gone*, the hymns, the arguments, the Archlector's face, the council chamber where I had failed to speak loudly enough — gone, gone, gone, eleven years lifted out of me like a splinter drawn clean, and underneath it, astonishingly, *me*: a woman named Anwen who had a lamp, a road, a purpose, and no idea whatsoever where she'd learned either.

I was on my knees. I didn't remember kneeling.

I could still see the woman in the snow. That, they hadn't taken. Whatever else I'd lost, the reason for all of it remained, planted in me like a nail. I clung to it. It was, I realized dimly, the only thing I had ever actually needed to keep.

"Anwen!" Pip reached me first, of course — Pip, who pretended to care about nothing, who had already checked my pulse with fingers that shook slightly. "Hey. Hey. What did it take? What's missing? Count backward or something, what's—"

"I don't know," I said, and heard my own voice with a stranger's ears. "That's the answer, child. I don't know what I've lost. That's the price of it."

Oscar crouched in front of me. Grey eyes searching mine, looking for the woman he'd bargained with, and whatever he found seemed to be enough, or nearly.

"Do you know us?" he asked.

"I know you're Oscar Joubert. I know you're going after your sister. I know—" I stopped. Frowned. "I know the rites. Not *how* I know them. But I know them. Like a song stuck in the body when the mind forgets the words." I looked at my own hands, steady on the stone floor. "It seems it took the story and left the skill. I suppose even a tollgate can't take what was never mine to begin with."

The crow landed beside me. It looked at me with those gold-well eyes, and it made a small sound — not a word, not quite — that I felt in the place where my vows used to live, and I understood that it was sorry, and that sorry, from a fragment of a god, is a strange and terrible comfort.

"Your turn," I told Oscar, and got up off my knees. "Watch what it weighs. And whatever you do—"

"I know," he said. "Don't lie to it."

* * *

Oscar approached the way he approached everything: directly, unhurried, like a man walking into weather he'd already decided to ignore.

**OSCAR,** said the warden.

"My name's not for sale," Oscar said. "Neither is anything it's attached to. I refuse."

The silence that followed had texture. The heart-flame dimmed, brightened, dimmed again, like a thing considering. Then the millstone voice rolled out again, and this time there was something in it that had not been there for me — something almost like regret.

**REFUSAL IS ANSWERED. THE ROAD STAYS SHUT. TURN BACK, OSCAR JOUBERT, OR PAY IN ANOTHER COIN.**

"There's no other coin," Oscar said flatly. "You take names or you take nothing."

**THERE IS ALWAYS ANOTHER COIN.**

And the crow stepped off his shoulder.

"Wait—" Oscar's hand came up too late. The bird dropped to the ground between them, folded its wings, and looked up at the warden with its head cocked, and when it spoke, it spoke in that cadence that had stopped my heart in Cinderhollow, the cadence of a god remembering itself:

"He keeps his name. I'll stand surety. Take from *me*."

**YOU HAVE SO LITTLE LEFT TO TAKE, SHEPHERD.**

"I know," said the crow. "Take it anyway."

The flame flared. The crow cried out — a raw, ragged sound, nothing divine in it at all, just a bird in pain — and the light of it *drained*, there was no other word, gold pouring out of its feathers like dye out of cloth in a flood, until what stood shivering on the stones was small and black and ordinary and dim, dimmer than any crow has a right to be, its eyes gone from well-deep gold to a faint dying ember.

Then the archway stood open. Just an opening in a wall, breathing cold air from below.

Oscar picked the crow up in both hands like something rescued from a fire, and the bird let him, which frightened me more than the warden had. Gods do not let themselves be held. Fragments, maybe. Fragments that have given away nearly everything.

"It'll recover," I said, and hated how uncertain it sounded, because it was. "What Vael spends, Vael — what it *was* — it regathers. Slowly. Given time."

"We don't have slowly," Oscar said. His voice was level. His hands were not. "Anwen. How far to the Court?"

I consulted the knowledge that lived in my body now, orphaned from its story. "A night's walk. Perhaps less. The road bends down through the bone galleries and opens above the Court itself."

"Then we keep moving." He tucked the crow inside his coat, against his chest, the way you'd carry an ember you meant to keep alive till morning. "Pip. Anwen. Stay close, stay quiet, and if anything down there offers you a bargain—"

"We say no," Pip said. "We say no, and then we run, and then we improvise."

Despite everything — the ache in my head where my life used to be, the dim bird, the open dark — I almost smiled.

"Child," I said, "that's the finest piece of liturgy I've heard in eleven years."

And we went down together, four travelers and half a god, into the dark that had been waiting a hundred years to collect.
