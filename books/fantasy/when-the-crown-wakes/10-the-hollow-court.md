## 10. The Hollow Court

There are three things I know about rich people, and I learned all of them before I was twelve, mostly by robbing them.

One: they never count their own money. Two: they think being seen is the same as being safe. Three: they will believe any lie that flatters them, and the bigger the lie, the harder they'll fight to keep believing it.

I'm telling you this now because we were about to walk into a room full of the richest people in the world — rich in a currency you can't steal, only spend — and my whole job was to make sure Oscar Joubert and Sister Anwen looked like they belonged there. Which meant making them look rich. Which meant, gods help me, making them look *stupid*.

"Stop fidgeting with the seal," I hissed at Anwen for the fourth time. She kept touching the wax medallion on her chest like it was a wound she was checking for bleeding. "You're a buyer. Buyers don't check if their credentials are real. Buyers have never once wondered if anything about themselves is real."

"And what am I buying?" she asked, for the fourth time.

"Grief," I said. "Everyone down here is buying grief. Just look sad and expensive."

Oscar, ahead of us both, said nothing, which was his way of agreeing. He'd been quiet since the Tollgate, quieter than usual, and he kept glancing at the crow riding his shoulder like a man checking on a sick friend. The crow had paid his toll — given up its name, or part of whatever passes for a name inside a god — and it had come out of the exchange dimmed, feathers gone grey at the edges, voice down to a rasp. It hadn't spoken since. That scared me more than the warden had. A talking crow is a marvel. A silent one is a bad omen wearing feathers.

The tunnel opened without warning.

One moment we were walking through black stone that sweated cold, and the next the rock simply gave up, and there it was: the Hollow Court.

I've been in the Grand Cathedral in Vellmark once, on a job. Stole a bishop's ring off his finger during communion, which tells you everything about my feelings regarding cathedrals. The Grand Cathedral was built to make you feel small so you'd give away your coin. This place wasn't built at all. It had been *hollowed* — a cavern the size of a city's heart, carved out of the mountain's bones, and every surface of it had been made to make you feel small so you'd give away something worth more than coin.

Lanterns hung from chains that rose into darkness past seeing. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one held a light that wasn't fire — softer, slower, the way a heartbeat is slower than a drumroll — and each one pulsed at its own rhythm, so the whole ceiling of the cavern seemed to breathe. The light they cast was pale gold, the color of candlelight through skin, and it fell on marble floors that some long-dead mason had polished until they mirrored the lanterns back at themselves, so you couldn't tell where the lights ended and the floor began. Walking across it felt like walking on the sky of some dead world.

"Oh," said Anwen, very quietly. Then, with the particular bitterness of someone whose faith has just been spat on: "Oh, they made it *beautiful*."

"They always do," I said. "Ugly crimes need pretty rooms."

And then, because I'm Pip Marrow and I can't help myself even when I'm terrified: "Also, nobody's watching the doors. Note that. Rich people never watch their own doors. They pay other people to."

The other people, in this case, stood along the walls in ranks: tall figures in grey livery, faces hidden behind smooth porcelain masks with no features except two slits for eyes. They didn't move. They didn't shift their weight. They stood the way furniture stands, and I decided right then that I would rather fight six Tallymen than be touched by one of those.

"Ushers," Oscar murmured. "Don't run near them. Don't touch them. Don't let them touch you."

"You say that like you know."

"I say that like it's obvious."

It was, actually. Everything down here was obvious, once you looked at it right. That was the horrible trick of the Hollow Court: it ran on the same rules as every market I'd ever picked clean. There were goods. There were sellers. There were buyers. There was a price list, and the price list was written in flesh and memory instead of silver, but a ledger is a ledger. I understood this place instantly, the way you understand a lock by the shape of your pick, and understanding it made me want to be sick.

Because the goods were *people*. Were breathing on that ceiling, right now, in their little glass prisons, pulsing away at the rhythm of whoever they used to be.

*Keep your face bored,* I told myself. *Bored and expensive.*

* * *

The trading floor was a circle of white stone at the center of the cavern, and around it rose tiers of seats like an amphitheater, and in the seats sat the buyers.

That was where I finally understood what kind of wealth we were pretending to have. These weren't merchants. Merchants dress to impress each other. These people dressed to *end conversations*. Silks that drank the lantern-light and gave nothing back. Jewelry that moved like it was alive, and maybe was. And masks — everyone wore masks, porcelain and silver and one horrifying one that seemed to be made of folded paper and yet never creased — because down here, apparently, even the buyers didn't want to be known. Everyone selling souls anonymously. Everyone buying them anonymously. A whole economy built on not having to look anyone in the face.

Anwen leaned close. "How do we know who to talk to?"

"We don't talk to anyone," I said. "We stand in the good seats, we look disappointed by everything, and we wait. Watching is free. Bidding costs. We are here to watch."

"What are we watching for?"

"Lise," Oscar said, from behind us, and his voice had a crack in it that he papered over fast. "A lantern with her name. Or — our name. Joubert."

Right. The family name. He'd spotted it once already, days ago, on a manifest fragment we'd lifted off a dead courier's effects — *Joubert, L., provenance: Thornfell villages, vintage: famine winter* — vintage, like she was wine. But manifests tell you a soul is in stock. They don't tell you where in this cathedral-cavern of ten thousand lights your sister is hanging.

So we watched. And I'll tell you what watching the Hollow Court does to you: it makes the horror ordinary. That's the silk glove of the thing. In the first hour, every time a broker lifted a lantern and announced its contents — *one soul, adult male, farmer's stock, lightly worn* — I flinched. By the third hour I was cataloguing security arrangements. By the fifth I caught myself thinking *that one would fetch well* about a light that had been a grandmother somewhere, and I had to go stand in a corner and breathe until the thought died.

That's how they get you. Not with chains. With routine. You spend enough time in the room where people are merchandise, and your heart learns the local grammar.

The brokers worked the floor in half-masks of beaten copper, each with a different animal hammered into it — fox, moth, heron, hound. They moved between the buyer tiers carrying lanterns on long poles, tilting them toward the light so the buyers could inspect the glow inside, the way a horse-trader pulls back a lip to check the teeth. One broker, a fox-masked woman with a voice like poured honey, was working our tier, and I heard her describe a lantern's contents as *"a gentle soul, very compliant, ideal for hearth-work"* and the buyer — a fat man in a mask shaped like a smiling moon — nodded along like she was describing a milking cow.

Compliant. Ideal for hearth-work. Somewhere above us, in a little glass belly, a person who had once had a favorite song was glowing on command.

"Steady," Oscar murmured. He'd put a hand on my shoulder without looking at me. Maybe I'd gone stiff. Maybe he could hear my teeth. "We're tourists. Tourists don't start fires."

"Not yet," I said.

"That's the spirit."

* * *

It was the fox-broker who showed us what this place really was.

She came to the center of the trading floor and struck a small bell, and the murmur of the tiers died the way water dies when it freezes. "Honored collectors," she said, honey-voiced, "a rare offering tonight. A debt-collection."

Two ushers brought a lantern to the floor — a big one, iron-framed, its light guttering and frantic compared to the calm pulses overhead. And behind the ushers, walking free, came a man. Old, well-dressed, unmasked, which marked him as either very powerful or already dead and too polite to mention it.

"This gentleman," the fox-broker purred, "purchased his father's soul forty years ago. A fine vintage. He has kept it burning in his hall ever since, as is his right." She tilted her head at the old man. "Tell the Court why you've brought him back to us."

The old man's voice was steady. Practiced. "My son has disgraced the family. My father — this soul — swore an oath before witnesses that no descendant of his would ever inherit while unworthy. The bargain holds me to it." He spread his hands. "I wish to amend the terms. Sell me the right to release the oath."

Murmurs through the tiers. Anwen's hand found my wrist and gripped it hard enough to bruise.

"And the amendment price?" the fox-broker asked sweetly.

The old man named a number. It was obscene. It was a fortune that could have bought a town, fed a province, and the whole room barely shifted in its seats.

The fox-broker lifted the iron lantern and turned it so all could see the frantic light within. "The seller consents," she announced, "as he must. Souls sold to the Court may be resold, amended, collateralized — but never freed by their owners. Only the Court unmakes a bargain, and the Court's prices are final." Her mask caught the light. "Your father's oath will bind your bloodline for another century, honored collector. Unless."

"Unless," the old man said, and here his voice finally shook, just slightly, just enough, "you accept the alternative lot."

The ushers brought forward a second lantern. Small. New. Its light was bright and quick and *young*, pulsing fast, the way a child's heart goes.

"My grandson," the old man said. "Age nine. Unbargained. Clean title."

The room hummed with appreciation, the way rooms hum over a good vintage. Somewhere in the tiers a woman said, "Clean stock is so rare these days," in the exact tone I'd use about uncut silk.

They took the boy's light. They amended the dead man's oath with it. The grandfather bowed to applause, collected his papers, and walked out of the Hollow Court lighter by one grandson and heavier by nothing at all, because the bargain had bound him first and would bind his blood after him, and everyone in that room understood this perfectly and found it reasonable.

Bargains bind past death. Debts inherit. Children pay for oaths their grandfathers swore. I knew that. I'd known it since I was eleven years old and signed my name to a contract to keep my sisters fed, a contract whose ink I could still feel sometimes, wrapped around my ribs like wire.

But knowing a thing and watching it performed in a marble amphitheater with applause are different animals entirely.

I didn't cry. I want that on the record. Pip Marrow does not cry in front of porcelain men. But my hands were shaking, and Anwen took one of them and held it, and her palm was shaking too, and neither of us said anything, because what was there to say? *This is normal here. This is Tuesday.*

Oscar wasn't watching the old man.

He was standing very still, staring up at the tiers of hanging lanterns, and his lips were moving. Reading names. Row by row, chain by chain, reading the little brass plaques riveted beneath each lantern — because of course there were plaques, of course the merchandise was labeled, inventory is inventory — and his eyes were going back and forth, back and forth, faster and faster, a man searching a crowd for one face.

"Pip," he said. His voice came out wrong. Flat. Careful. The voice of a man holding something heavy at arm's length so it doesn't drop on his foot. "The east chains. Third tier. Tell me what that plaque says."

I found the lantern he meant. Mid-sized, older glass, its light burning low and slow and stubborn, the way a candle burns that refuses to go out. The plaque beneath it was green with age.

I read it. I read it twice, because the first time my eyes slid off it like water off oil.

"*Joubert*," I said. "*Joubert, Lise. Provenance: Thornfell villages. Vintage: famine winter.*"

Nobody spoke. Above us, ten thousand lights breathed. Below us, the fox-broker rang her bell for the next lot, and the Court went on buying and selling the insides of people, and the whole vast beautiful machine of it turned and turned, indifferent as weather.

Oscar reached up, slowly, and touched the crow on his shoulder. The crow was dim as ash, but under his fingers I saw — or thought I saw — one feather flicker, faint as a dying coal remembering fire.

"There you are," Oscar whispered. To the lantern. To the light. To his sister, twenty years sold and twenty years waiting, hanging in a glass belly three hundred feet above a throne crowned with antlers.

And that's when I felt it — that prickle on the neck, the one every thief knows, the feeling of being *counted*. I turned, casual as anything, bored and expensive, and scanned the ushers along the wall.

One of them had turned its blank porcelain face toward us. Just one. Just ours.

Its head tilted, very slightly, like a lock considering a key.

"Oscar," I said softly. "Don't look up again. And don't look at the door. But I think somebody just read *our* label."
