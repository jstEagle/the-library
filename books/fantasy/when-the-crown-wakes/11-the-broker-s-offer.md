## 11. The Broker's Offer

I had been in the Hollow Court for less than an hour when I learned its first rule: everything here is polite until it isn't.

The cathedral-cavern swallowed sound the way a rich man swallows debts. Our footsteps — mine, Pip's, Anwen's — vanished into the vaulted dark above us, where the ceiling rose so high that the lantern-light of the brokers below didn't reach it. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some drowned leviathan, and strung between them, on wires thin as spider silk, hung hundreds of soul-lanterns. Pale lights in glass and iron, each one tagged with a lead seal, each one swinging almost imperceptibly in a draft that came from nowhere and went nowhere.

Below them, masked brokers moved through the aisles like clerks in a church that had forgotten what it used to worship. Their masks were porcelain, smooth-faced, identical except for the color of the ribbon at the throat — white for junior clerks, silver for factors, and gold for the one woman at the far end of the hall who did not move among the lanterns because the lanterns, in a sense, moved among her.

Anwen had coached us on the walk down from the pilgrim tunnel. *You are buyers. You are bored. You are mildly offended by the prices.* She'd lost her memories to the warden at the Tollgate of Names, but her body remembered how to lie in a sanctuary, which told me something about her order I probably didn't want to know.

So we walked like bored buyers. Pip had forged our credentials on the road with a stolen stylus and a talent for other people's signatures that would have made her old master weep with pride or fury, possibly both. My papers said I was a factor out of Cinderhollow named Aldous Vey, come to appraise stock on behalf of clients who preferred not to be named. Which was true, in a way. My client preferred not to be named because my client was a dead man's map and a talking crow.

The crow rode under my coat, against my chest, in a sling Anwen had sewn from an old altar cloth. It weighed nothing and it hummed faintly, like a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out. Every few minutes it shifted, and I felt the shift all the way through my ribs, and every time I felt it I thought about how Lady Sereth's people had been circling us since the Tollgate, patient as interest accruing.

We made it forty minutes. I counted. Forty minutes of watching a broker in a white mask sell a grandmother's soul to a merchant whose hands shook too much for a man buying someone else's afterlife. Forty minutes of Pip memorizing guard rotations with her eyes while her face performed boredom well enough to win a festival prize. Forty minutes of Anwen standing very still near a shelf of lanterns marked with parish names, her lips moving around prayers she could no longer remember learning.

Then a bell rang once, softly, somewhere above, and every broker in the hall stopped moving at the same time, the way birds stop when a hawk crosses the sun.

"Factor Vey," said a voice behind me, "you're a long way from your ledger."

I turned around slowly, the way you turn toward a snake you've already stepped over once and are hoping was asleep.

She was smaller than I expected. That was the first obscenity of Lady Sereth: that the woman who ran the market where my sister's childhood had been sold for grain stood perhaps five feet tall in soft grey robes, with her mask pushed up onto her crown of braided silver hair, her face bare. Everyone else hid. She didn't need to. Her face was pleasant in the way of portraits commissioned by the subject — fine bones, mild eyes, a mouth built for smiling at funerals. The only wrong thing about her was that her shadow fell the wrong way. The lanterns lit her from three sides, and still her shadow leaned toward me, across the floor, like it wanted to shake my hand.

"You have the advantage of me," I said.

"I have more than that." Her smile deepened by a degree, calibrated, I'd have sworn, to the millimeter. "Oscar Joubert. Of Thornfell. Son of Marta the weaver and a father you don't remember clearly enough to grieve properly. Brother to Lise, who liked to catch frogs by the mill pond and let them go again, because she only ever wanted to hold things, not keep them."

The cavern got very quiet around me, or I did. Hard to say which.

"That name is worth money here," I said. "You should charge yourself for using it."

"Names are the only currency I respect." She folded her hands. Her rings were antler-bone, pale as teeth. "Come walk with me, Factor Vey. Or Joubert. Whichever you'd rather be, for the length of our conversation."

Everything in me said run. But running in the Hollow Court is just dying with better posture, and besides, Pip and Anwen were ten feet away pretending to examine a lantern labeled *Ferryman's Rest Parish, Batch 12*, and if Sereth knew my name then she knew theirs, and walking away wouldn't un-know it. So I walked. And to my lasting irritation, she offered me her arm, and I took it, because refusing would have been a statement and statements are expensive here.

We strolled the aisles of the dead like a couple at a fair.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Oscar?" she said. "You survived. Twenty years guiding smugglers through passes that eat guides, staying small, staying unnamed, asking nothing of anyone — including your god. Do you know how rare that is? Most men who lose their god either die of it or spend their lives shouting into the silence trying to get a refund. You just... got quieter. It's practically a discipline."

"You've had me watched."

"I've had everyone watched. It's not flattery; it's bookkeeping." She nodded at a lantern passing overhead on its wire, a light the color of weak tea. "That one belonged to a Tallyman who skimmed. He thought I wouldn't notice. Now he notices everything, forever, from inside a jar. The point is, I know about the courier. I know about the map. I know you've been carrying a crow under your coat that hums like a struck bell, and I know exactly what it is."

"It's a crow," I said. "It eats my rations and insults my planning."

"It is a fragment of Vael, shepherd of Thornfell, last of the mountain gods, and it is the single most valuable item of stolen property to enter my Court in a hundred years." Her voice stayed pleasant, but underneath it I heard something vast adjust its weight, the way ice shifts on a lake before it decides. "Your god did not go silent, Oscar. Your god went *shopping*. Do you understand the difference? Silence is absence. Shopping leaves receipts."

I thought of the jailer-ghost's story, still days away from being told to me, and understood nothing yet, and understood everything anyway, the way you understand a wound before anyone explains anatomy to you.

"And here is my offer," she continued, "because I am generous, and generosity is simply cruelty with better timing. The lantern with your family's mark on it — the girl who caught frogs. I will give it to you. Free. No bargain, no seal, no reckoning clause. You walk out of my Court with your sister's soul under your arm, and you go home, and you live whatever small visible life you like. In exchange, you give me the map, and you give me the bird."

"The bird," I repeated.

"The bird," she agreed warmly. "Which is not yours. Which never was yours. Which has been spending divine property that belongs, by right of salvage, to this Court. A hundred years ago the ledgers of this place were written in a god's voice, Oscar, and that voice walked out mid-contract and left us holding paper. I want my collateral back. Give it to me, take your sister, and be a man again instead of a errand for a dead religion."

I looked at her shadow leaning toward me and I thought: *she rehearsed this.* Not the words — the shape of them. The mercy first, so the price feels like gratitude. The insult to the crow dressed up as legalism. The picture of home painted in frog-pond colors so I'd stop thinking and start aching.

And God help me — Vael help me, whoever that was now — it nearly worked. For three full seconds I stood in a cathedral of the sold and saw only one lantern, and my hand was already rising toward it, and every rule I'd lived twenty years by — never ask names, never take sides, stay small — lay down and offered its throat.

Then I felt the crow shift against my chest. Once. Like a sleeper turning over.

"No deal," I said.

Sereth's smile didn't falter, which was somehow worse than anger. "May I ask why? Sincerely. I find refusal instructive."

"Because free things in this place cost the most," I said. "And because you called it salvage. Gods aren't salvage, lady. Whatever else it is, it's *mine* — it chose me, it bled for me twice already, and I don't sell what chooses me."

"How refreshingly medieval." She withdrew her arm from mine with the grace of a queen declining a dance. "Then keep stalling, Joubert. Stall beautifully. But understand: the reckoning comes in six nights, and when it comes, every bargain in this Court seals permanently. Including the one written in your family name. After that night, no power under heaven or inside this mountain can open that lantern — not the crown, not a god, not grief itself. So take whatever time you need to arrive at the only answer there is." She turned, then paused, and glanced back with something that was almost kindness, which is the most frightening thing a monster can do. "Do visit the vault, by the way. Buyers may request viewings. I think you'll find her very well preserved. Children keep, you know. Like jam."

She walked back into the lantern-light and her shadow finally stopped reaching for me, and I discovered I'd been holding my breath long enough to see spots.

Pip materialized at my elbow wearing the expression of someone who'd heard every word and filed half of it. "Well," she said. "That went great."

* * *

They gave us guest quarters, because of course they did. The Hollow Court doesn't throw you out; it tucks you in. A cell carved from the living rock, furnished better than any room I'd ever slept in — a real bed, a basin, a lamp that burned without oil — with a door that locked from the outside and a lock that Pip examined for exactly four seconds before announcing, cheerfully, "I could pick this, but I'd rather not find out what it does when I do."

So we sat. Anwen meditated, or slept sitting up, or did whatever memoryless ex-priestesses do at night in the belly of a stolen god's prison. Pip re-sewed false seams into her sleeves where she kept her picks, muttering inventory. And I sat on the bed with my back to the wall and opened the sling, and the crow climbed out onto my knee.

Up close, in lamplight, it looked worse than it had at the Tollgate. Its feathers had gone grey at the edges, like frost claiming a hedge. When it moved, the hum under my ribs stuttered.

"You heard all that," I said.

It bobbed its head. *Heard,* it said, in that voice like wind through a specific valley I'd spent my whole life trying to stop missing. *Offer stands open. Six nights.*

"You're not going to tell me to take it."

*No.*

"Why not? It's your neck she wants. One bird for one sister. Any honest arithmetic says—"

*Any honest arithmetic,* the crow said, *sold Lise Joubert for eleven sacks of barley.*

I shut up. It hopped closer, and its eye caught the lamp — black, but with a depth to it now, a shine like looking down a well and seeing a star at the bottom.

*I will tell you a thing,* it said. *Since she offered truth for truth, roughly speaking. You think I came to you at the ravine by chance. You think Vael scattered, and a piece drifted, and it landed on the one man in Thornfell with a reason to walk into the Hollow Court. Coincidence wears that coat sometimes. This time it didn't.*

"Meaning what?"

*Meaning I made a bargain too.* The word *bargain* came out of its beak like a bone it had swallowed sideways. *Long ago. Before your famine. Before your village. I was younger then. Whole, mostly. There was a debt owed to this place — not by me. By the church above. Anwen's church, though she doesn't remember it, and would hate it, and is right to. The Court held a lien on the mountain itself, Oscar. On the springs, the passes, the births. Every child born under Thornfell was born encumbered. And I — Vael — went down here to renegotiate.*

The lamp flickered. Neither of us looked at it.

*What did you pay?*

*Instalments,* said the crow. *A god's voice, to start. Then the sight out of one eye — you'll notice crows see poorly straight ahead; that's me. Then years. Decades. Memory by memory, feather by feather, coin by coin, buying back children one lantern at a time and hiding them in myself, because a soul kept inside a god can't be entered into a ledger. I have been walking out of this mountain lighter every century, and calling it shepherding.* It paused, and its head drooped, and for a moment it was just a tired bird on a tired man's knee. *Lise was the last coin. I bought her with what remained of my voice. That is why your prayers stopped being answered, Oscar. Not because I abandoned you. Because I had nothing left to answer with. I spent your god on your sister, and I have been too ashamed to say so since the ravine.*

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere far off, water dripped in the dark with the patience of stone.

I should have been angry. Part of me was — a hot, small, childish part that had spent twenty years praying into a blank sky and getting told tonight that the sky had been *shopping*. But mostly what I felt was something I didn't have a clean name for. It was the feeling of learning that the empty chair at the table wasn't abandonment. It was the chair being carried, all those years, to somewhere cold, to sit beside someone who needed it more.

"You let me believe you'd gone," I said finally.

*Believing I'd gone kept you small,* the crow said. *Small kept you alive. If you'd known a fragment of Vael roosted on your roof, you'd have done something brave and stupid by the second winter.*

"That's... accurate," I admitted. "I hate that it's accurate."

*Yes. It's why I liked you.*

I reached out and touched its head with one finger, the way you'd touch anything holy and breakable. The feathers were warm. Warmer than a bird should be. Warm like a banked fire, like a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out — except now I understood it wasn't deciding. It was waiting. Saving.

"Six nights," I said. "She seals the bargains. She takes you. Unless."

*Unless,* the crow agreed, and something in the hum beneath its feathers changed pitch — deeper, older, like a bell heard through a mountain. *Unless the reckoning becomes something else entirely. I did not spend a hundred years eating instalments of myself just to be collateral, Oscar. I have been gathering. Every fight. Every prayer you wasted on me out of habit. Habit is a kind of offering, did you know that? You fed me for twenty years without knowing it.*

"So what's the plan?" I asked. "Because I'll warn you, my plans tend to involve walking away quietly, and I'm starting to suspect that's off the table."

The crow looked up at me, and in its eye the star at the bottom of the well brightened.

*The plan,* it said, *is that in six nights, Lady Sereth is going to try to devour me in front of her entire Court. And I am going to let her get her mouth very close indeed.*

Outside the cell door, boots passed in the corridor — unhurried, numerous, counting us the way she counted everything. Pip looked up from her sleeves. Anwen opened her eyes without seeming to wake.

And under my hand, the last coin of a silent god began, very quietly, to warm.
