## 12. Lantern Light

The Hollow Court kept time in lanterns.

I don't mean candles. I mean that everywhere you looked, light came from glass and brass and the pale things inside them, hanging from iron hooks along the cavern walls like fruit nobody had gotten around to harvesting. Some burned steady. Some guttered. A few, here and there, flickered in a rhythm I didn't want to think too hard about, because it was almost — *almost* — the rhythm of breathing.

Three days of posing as buyers had taught me three things about the Court. First: never look directly at a lantern for longer than courtesy allows, because brokers notice longing the way sharks notice blood. Second: the masked acolytes who tended the racks moved on fixed rounds, and Pip had counted their footsteps until she could recite them like scripture. Third: Lady Sereth's attention was a physical weight, and it had been resting on me since the night she smiled and said she'd known my father's face.

So when Pip spread her stolen floor-plan across our rented cell's cot and tapped one small inked square deep beneath the throne dais, I felt something I hadn't felt in twenty years of staying small.

Purpose. Sharp as a fishhook.

"Vault's under the antlered throne," she said. "Of course it is. Everything down here flows toward that chair like it's the bottom of a drain. Two ways in." She held up fingers. "The processional stair, which is guarded by six acolytes and a warden who weighs your soul before letting you pass — no thank you — or the ash-chute."

"The what?"

"Where they burn the contracts that get defaulted. It's a shaft, comes down past the vault's ventilation gallery. Old construction. Whoever built this place figured nobody would ever climb *up* a chimney full of dead men's paperwork." She grinned, and it wasn't entirely a nice grin. "Nobody ever figures on me."

"And if the chute's watched?"

"Then we die in a chimney," she said cheerfully, "and honestly, after the last week, that's not the worst offer I've had."

Anwen sat by the cell's one lamp, hands folded in her lap, watching us plan heresy with the serene expression of a woman reading a psalm only she could hear. They'd taken her memories at the Tollgate of Names — her order, her years, her own face's history — but they hadn't taken whatever lived deeper than memory. She still prayed. She prayed to nothing now, or to everything, kneeling each dawn with her palms open like a woman catching rain. Watching her, I'd started to wonder whether faith was a thing you believed or a thing you *were*, and whether the Court understood that difference, and whether that misunderstanding might be worth something to us.

"Sister," I said. "You don't have to be part of this part."

"I know." She looked up. Her eyes were still strange without their history behind them — wide, unhurried, ancient as a well. "But the acolytes keep vigils in the Nave of Small Mercies, and I have been told — " she paused, choosing words carefully around the holes in herself — "I have been *told*, by whoever tells me things now, that a vigil interrupted by a penitent is a vigil the keeper cannot leave. Not without breaking their own vows. Their vows are the bars they live inside, Oscar. I remember enough of vows to know how to knock on them."

"You don't remember your own name half the mornings."

"I remember how to be sorry beautifully," said Anwen. "It appears to be the one thing they couldn't take. Let me spend it."

* *

We went down on the third night, during the Change of Ledgers, when every broker in the Court retired behind silk curtains to count what they owned — counting souls the way misers count coins, alone, in the dark, because even among themselves they trusted no one.

Pip went first up the ash-chute, and I want it recorded that she climbed forty feet of vertical soot-slick stone with knife-holds she cut herself, humming. The crow rode my shoulder, dim as a coal banked under ash. We'd agreed it would stay quiet and stay small; Sereth wanted it badly enough that its brightness was a beacon, and every hour it spent dull was an hour of her hunters looking elsewhere. But even banked, I could feel it against my neck. Warmth without heat. Like carrying a hand that hadn't decided yet whether to hold yours or let go.

The ventilation gallery opened off the chute through a grate Pip had loosened on a previous reconnaissance disguised as admiring the architecture. Below us, the vault.

I had imagined a dungeon. What I saw was a granary.

Racks upon racks upon racks, descending into gloom in long terraced rows, and every rack hung with lanterns — hundreds, thousands, more than I could count before my eyes gave up trying. Pale lights behind glass, some bright, some barely embers. The silence down there was total except for one sound I'll hear until I die: a faint, continuous, layered shimmer, like wind through wheat, like a crowd whispering just below the threshold of words. Ten thousand souls, all murmuring at once. All waiting.

"That's not treasure," I heard myself say. "That's a *harvest*."

"They file them by the year of purchase," Pip whispered, unrolling a scrap of map against the gallery rail. "Sereth showed you the ledgers. Ledgers reference shelf-codes. Lise Joubert, sold famine-winter, Thornfell valley — that's the oldest tier, way back, where the light's worst." She glanced at me, and for once the thief's grin was gone, and what was underneath it was young and kind. "Oscar. Whatever's in that lantern, it's been in there twenty years. Be ready for it to not be — "

"I know what's in the lantern."

"Yeah," she said quietly. "Okay. Go. I've got the lockwork on the archive cage — Sereth's private codes are in there, and if tonight goes wrong, we'll need proof of *something*. Twelve minutes. Scream if you're dying, I hate guessing."

She dropped over the rail like spilled water and was gone between the racks.

* *

I found the row by the shelf-code scratched into the iron: a famine winter, a valley name, a number that had once been a girl.

Her lantern hung at the end of the rack, apart from the others, smaller than the rest — child-sized, the glass fogged on the inside, the brass fittings green with age. And it was dim. Gods, it was dim. Other lights on that rack burned with at least the stubbornness of coals; hers was a thread, a single gray filament of glow, the last ember in a fire everyone else had stopped feeding.

I took it down with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and the moment it touched my palms the whispering around me seemed to fall away, and out of twenty years of silence a voice said:

"*Oscar?*"

I have taken blades. I have taken a broken arm in the Thornfell passes and set it myself with my teeth gritted and my god saying nothing, nothing, nothing. None of it was this. My knees went, and I ended up sitting right there on the vault floor between the racks, holding a lantern the size of a loaf of bread, while my sister's voice — eight years old, unchanged, *preserved* — said it again.

"*Oscar, is that you? It's dark in here. It's been dark a long time.*"

"Lise." My voice came out ruined. "Lise, it's me. I'm here."

"*You got big,*" she said wonderingly, and then, with the terrible practicality of children: "*Did you bring food? I'm so hungry, Oscar. Mama said someone would come. She said it would be soon.*"

Mama had died believing that, I thought. Or maybe believing nothing at all; I'd never been brave enough to ask her, in the years before the fever took her, what she'd traded her daughter's soul for. Grain. One sack of barley, probably. Enough to feed the son and let the daughter slip.

"I brought something better than food," I said. "I brought a way out. I just need a key."

"*Oh.*" A pause. Inside the glass, the gray filament pulsed, quickened, like a heartbeat remembering hope. "*The lady with the antlers has all the keys. She came and talked to me sometimes. She asked me questions about you. She wrote down my answers.*"

Ice walked up my spine. "What kind of questions?"

"*Whether you'd come. Whether you loved me. She said love makes a soul heavy, and heavy souls hold their shape longer.*" The little voice faltered. "*Oscar, why does she want to know if you love me?*"

Because a soul that holds its shape sells at a premium, I thought. Because twenty years ago Sereth priced a child's despair, and somewhere in those ledgers my sister's suffering was an asset appreciating on a shelf. Because everything in this place wore silk gloves, even the horror. Especially the horror.

"I don't know yet," I told her, and made myself stop shaking, and made my voice steady, because she had been waiting twenty years and she deserved ten minutes of steadiness. "Listen to me, Lise. In six nights there's going to be a reckoning. Everything in this place changes that night. When it does, I'm coming back, and I'm opening this glass, and you're walking out of here. Can you wait six more nights?"

Silence. Long enough that I pressed the lantern to my chest like I could warm it by force of will.

"*Six nights isn't so long,*" she said finally. "*I waited twenty years. I can do six. But Oscar — *" the filament flared, urgent — "*don't trust the pretty lady. She lies with her mouth and her eyes tell the truth, and her eyes are hungry. Everyone down here is hungry. That's what this place is. A hunger wearing a crown.*"

"I know," I said. "I've met the crown."

And then I turned the lantern slightly and saw, stamped into the brass base, the thing that turned heartbreak into something harder: a seal. Antlered. And beneath the seal, a keyhole — fine, intricate, shaped like nothing any locksmith on the surface would recognize. Shaped, when I looked closer, exactly like the tines of the crown that sat on the throne two hundred feet above my head.

The lanterns didn't open with keys. There was one key, and it was furniture.

Every soul in this room, locked behind a single door, and the door was the seat of the thing that owned them.

I sat there a moment longer than I could afford, forehead nearly touching the warm glass, listening to my sister hum a lullaby she remembered and I had forgotten, and felt something in me finish hardening. For twenty years I'd stayed small because staying small meant staying safe. Somewhere in the last weeks the arithmetic had inverted. Small was just another word for gone.

Six nights. Then the throne, and the crown, and whatever price came with touching either.

* *

Twelve minutes became fifteen, and the whispering of the racks began to change pitch, and I was rising with the lantern tucked against my ribs when the vault's far doors opened with a sound like a held breath released.

Light poured in. Torchlight, and behind it voices — acolytes, four of them, masks gleaming bone-white, moving along the racks with censers swinging. Not the normal round. This was a search pattern, methodical as a comb drawn through hair.

They weren't searching the vault.

They were being *drawn away* from something. From the Nave above — and I thought of Anwen, kneeling in the aisle of Small Mercies, being beautifully sorry at men whose vows were their cages, buying me minutes with the one coin she had left.

"*Oscar,*" whispered the lantern against my ribs. "*Someone's coming. Hide me.*"

"Not you," I breathed. "Never again."

I pressed back into the shadow between racks as the torchlight swept nearer, and that was when I heard it — from up in the gallery, thin and clear through the ventilation grate, Pip's voice, pitched low and easy in the accent of a Court functionary:

"— honestly, Master Warden, if the Lady wants the ash-archives audited before the reckoning, that's her business, but *nobody* told *me* — "

Buying time too. Both of them spending themselves like coins so I could stand in the dark holding the whole reason we'd come.

And then the torchlight swung across my rack, and the nearest acolyte stopped.

Stopped. Tilted his bone mask. And in the black sockets of the mask, two points of light kindled — not torchlight. Something colder. Something that knew.

"There you are," said the mask, in a voice like Sereth's smile given lungs. "Lady Joubert's brother. She did tell us to expect you. She said — " the acolyte's head cocked, courteous, terrible — "*let him hold it first. Grief makes men slow.*"

Behind me, up through the grate, Pip's chatter stopped mid-word.

And against my chest, my sister's lantern flickered wildly, like a candle in a closing door.
