## 13. Betrayal's Price

I've been robbed before. Professionally, I mean — it's a hazard of the trade, like splinters or suspicious dogs. I've had my take lifted by cutpurses, my fence turn crown's witness, my own brother sell me out for a loaf of bread and half a sausage, which, in fairness, was a very good sausage.

But I have never once been robbed by my own cleverness. Until now.

Here's the thing about the vault job: it was beautiful. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the world ends. Pip Marrow, indentured thief, debt-broken, scarred by a talking gate, picked the lantern vault of the Hollow Court the way you'd pick a plum — clean, quick, no mess. Anwen's diversion was singing in the pilgrim gallery, a hymn she didn't remember learning, and the acolytes drifted toward it like moths with excellent pensions. Oscar stood at the vault door wearing his merchant face, the one that says *I am boring and rich and would like to remain both*. And I went in.

The vault was a cathedral's cold cousin. Lanterns in their thousands, hung in ranks that vanished upward into dark, each one burning with a small pale flame that wasn't quite fire. Soul-light. I'd seen one lantern before, back in Cinderhollow, in a broker's window, and I'd told myself it was just a fancy candle.

It is not a fancy candle.

Up close, every lantern has a weight to it. Not physical — I mean the way a room feels different when someone's crying behind a door. The whole vault was a thousand doors, and behind every one, somebody crying.

I found the Joubert lantern on the third rank, because of course I did. I'd memorized the ledger page Oscar had shown me, the family name in that horrible copperplate, and there it hung at eye height like it had been waiting for me. Small lantern. Steady flame. When I put my hand near the glass, the flame leaned toward my fingers the way a cat leans toward a warm lap.

"Hello, Lise," I whispered, because that's what you do. You talk to the merchandise. It keeps you from thinking about the fact that the merchandise is a person.

I had the lantern wrapped and slung before the trap closed, and here's the part that still makes me want to scream into a pillow: there was no trap. Not really. No snare, no alarm, no hidden Tallymen dropping from the ceiling. The trap was arithmetic. The vault had exactly one exit, and the exit had exactly one door, and the door had exactly one Lady Sereth standing in front of it, arms folded, antlered mask tilted at a courteous angle, as though she'd been waiting for a delivery.

"Ah," she said. "The Marrow girl."

I did what any professional does when caught red-handed with a soul. I smiled. "You've got the wrong vault. I'm just — dusting."

"Bring it here, child."

And here's the thing — my legs moved. Not because she compelled them. Because I looked at her and my body did the math I should have done an hour earlier: one of her, one of me, and her the kind of one that unmakes people. I walked the lantern over like an obedient little apprentice and hated every step.

She took it from my hands with the tenderness of a woman accepting flowers. "You know," she said, "I had wondered which of them would come to the vault. The priestess prays too loudly. The shepherd is too careful. But thieves always come to the vault. Thieves can't help themselves. It's why we stopped locking it, centuries ago."

"Stopped locking it," I repeated.

"Locks are for things you fear to lose." She turned the lantern in her hands, watching the flame lean. "I fear nothing in this mountain. I *own* this mountain."

I want to say I spat in her mask or told her my mother was a queen or did something with spine. What I actually did was say, "Oscar's not going to like this," in a voice about an octave higher than my own, and then two Tallymen came out of the dark like they'd been standing there since the mountain was young, and that was the end of my career as a master thief, all four days of it.

They didn't even hurt me. That was the worst part. They just walked me down through the Court while the masked brokers turned to watch, and the whisper went through the crowd like wind through wheat: *stock, stock, fresh stock.* And I understood, with the special clarity that only arrives when it's too late to be useful, that I had never been a guest in this place at all. From the moment we'd walked in wearing forged credentials that Pip Marrow herself had made — credentials she'd signed, because I'm thorough, because I'm *good* — I had been inventory with legs.

* * *

They held me in a room with no door.

I mean that literally. No door, no bars, no window. Just a round stone chamber with a bench, and when they put me inside, the wall sealed over the entrance like water closing over a stone. I hammered on it for a while. The stone had the courtesy not to laugh, which I appreciated.

So. Inventory. Let me tell you what inventory does while it waits to be shelved. Inventory talks to itself. Inventory makes jokes, because the alternative is screaming, and Pip Marrow has a rule about screaming: only do it where nobody can charge you for it.

"Right," I said to the room. "Assess. You got caught. Bad. But you got caught doing the job, which means the job's half done, which means somewhere upstairs a lantern's been moved, which means Oscar knows something's wrong, because if the lantern's gone and I'm gone, even a shepherd can do that sum." I paced. The chamber was eight steps across. I know because I paced it forty times. "And Anwen's still loose, and the crow — the crow's worth more than all of us, so they'll keep it whole, so there's a thread. Three threads. Pull one, the rest follow. That's how locks work. That's how *everything* works."

The wall rippled.

I backed up. The stone went translucent, then clear as ice, and beyond it I could see a corridor of the Court — and standing in the corridor, hands folded, was Lady Sereth.

"You count steps when you're afraid," she said. Her voice came through the stone as if the stone were only politely pretending to be solid. "Your mother did the same. In the holding cells under Cinderhollow, the winter they took her. Eight steps. She wore a groove in the floor."

My mouth went dry. "You knew my mother."

"I knew everyone's mother, child. I have receipts." She let that sit. "I'm not here to gloat. Gloating is for brokers with small ledgers. I'm here to make you an offer, because unlike your shepherd friend, you understand value."

"I don't make deals with people who keep my mum's paperwork."

"You'd rather I keep yours?" She gestured, and the wall showed me something else: a ledger page, copperplate, my name on it. *Marrow, P. Indentured. Balance outstanding.* And below it, in fresher ink: *Reclaimed. Stock.* "Your soul has been appraised. It's a good soul. Clever, loyal, stubborn past the point of sense. It will fetch a fine price at the reckoning. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless you tell me where the crow goes when it sleeps."

I laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. "That's it? That's the big offer? You've got me in a rock box and your question is *where does the birdie nap?*"

"The crow is the last unsecured asset of a dead god. It has been hiding in this world for twenty years, and in three nights it will be sealed beyond even my reach. Where it sleeps, it dreams. Where it dreams, it can be taken." She leaned closer, and behind the antlered mask I caught a glimpse of something old and tired and hungry. "You are going to be sold either way, Marrow. The only question is whether you're sold *whole*."

And that's the trap in it, see. That's the craftsmanship. She wasn't lying — I'd have smelled it, lying's my trade too. She was telling the truth in a shape designed to fit my hand like a knife. Because I did want to live. I wanted it so badly my teeth ached. And all I had to do was sell the one creature that had burned itself down to a cinder twice to save people who weren't even its people.

"Here's my counter-offer," I said. "You can take your ledger and — " and then I said some things about Lady Sereth that my mother would have washed my mouth out for, and a few things she'd have applauded.

Sereth didn't get angry. She got *polite*, which is so much worse. "Three nights," she said. "Think about the eight steps." And the wall went solid, and I was alone with my arithmetic again.

I paced. Eight steps, forty times, four hundred times. And somewhere in there, between step and step, the despair finally caught up with me — not the dramatic kind, the quiet kind, the kind that sits down next to you on the bench and puts its weight against your shoulder like a friend. Because I'd done the sums in Cinderhollow, hadn't I? I'd told myself: *these three are different, these three came back for me.* Oscar, who'd lost a card game so badly it could only have been on purpose, buying a stranger's debt with his last coin. Anwen, who'd held my hand when the gate took its price from my face and said the scar was a signature, not a stain.

And I'd believed it. That was the stupid part. I'd walked into a vault with a lantern in my teeth because I'd believed, for the first time in my rotten lucky life, that somebody would come back for me.

They would. That's the thing that made me cry, finally, in the dark, where nobody could charge me for it. They *would* come. Oscar was already coming, I'd have bet my hands on it. And it wouldn't matter, because Sereth didn't want the lantern or the map or any of the things we'd spent the whole road north chasing. She wanted the crow. We hadn't been thieves breaking into the Court.

We'd been the delivery service.

* * *

The wall opened on the third day, or what I decided was the third day, because a girl has to keep a calendar somehow and hunger is as good as any bell.

Two Tallymen walked me through corridors I tried to memorize out of habit — left, down, the gallery with the singing stones — though I knew it was useless. Nobody escapes the Court by knowing the way out. The way out isn't a door. It's a bargain, and bargains are the one lock I can't pick.

They brought me into the reckoning hall early, before the crowds, and that's where I saw the crow.

It hung in a cage of white iron at the foot of the antlered throne, and gods, it looked *small*. I'd seen it burn like a falling star over the ravine. I'd seen it go eye to eye with a stone warden and win. Now it sat on a perch with its feathers dull and its head tucked, and the cage had a light inside it that pulsed slow, like something being drained by the drop.

"Look at me," I said to it, as the Tallymen marched me past. "Hey. Featherbrain. Look at me."

The head came up. One eye, black and bright as a wet stone, found mine.

"I didn't tell her anything," I said. My voice cracked in the middle and I let it. "Whatever she says, whatever she offers — I didn't — "

"*Hush,*" said Sereth, from the throne steps, "or I'll take your voice now and appraise it separately."

The crow made a sound. Not a caw. Something lower, rougher, a sound like a word worn down to its grain. And I swear on my mother's sausage — the good one, the one she sold me for — I understood it. Not the meaning. The *cadence*. It was the way Oscar talks when he's out of prayers and keeps praying anyway.

They sat me on a bench at the hall's edge and left me there, and the brokers began to arrive for the reckoning, mask by mask, silk over silk, until the cathedral-cave was full of that soft rustling horror-sound of a crowd that has no faces. And I sat with my hands cuffed in iron that drank the warmth out of my skin, and I watched the cage, and I did the only sums left to me.

Oscar was coming. Anwen was coming. Two threads against a mountain.

Then the doors at the far end boomed, and the crowd went quiet in a way that had teeth in it, and Sereth rose from her steps, and I heard her say the words I'd been dreading since the wall closed over my head:

"Bring the shepherd. It's time he learned what his god cost."

And the Tallymen dragged Oscar in — bruised, bound, one eye swelling shut, still wearing that stubborn merchant face like armor — and they threw him down at the foot of the throne not ten feet from me, and the last thing I saw before the reckoning began was the crow pressing itself against the white bars, and the light in the cage pulse once, hard, like a heartbeat that had just remembered what it was for.
