## 14. The Bargain Beneath

I had preached for thirty years that faith was a lamp you carried into dark places, and I had believed it right up until the moment they took my memories at the Tollgate of Names and left me standing in a cell with nothing but the shape of my own conviction and no idea what had filled it.

That is not entirely true. They did not take everything. They took the order — the sisters' names, the seminary at Aldermoor, the twenty years of rites and rubrics, the face of the woman who taught me to read scripture by candlelight. What remained was stranger and, I suspect, more honest: I still knew how to pray. My hands still folded themselves without instruction. When the jailer-ghost came down the corridor at what I assumed was nightfall, dragging its chains of ledger-iron, my knees wanted to bend before my mind understood why.

"You're the one who used to be somebody," it said.

"I'm the one who's going to be somebody again," I said, "once I remember who."

It made a sound like wet gravel approving of a joke. Ghosts in the Hollow Court are not the souls in the lanterns — those are stock, inventory, light in glass. The ghosts are something else: people who died inside their bargains, whose debts were so entangled with the Court's ledgers that death couldn't untangle them. They serve out their obligations as wardens, clerks, sweepers of ash. This one had been a jailer for longer than it could remember, which for a ghost is a very long time indeed, since remembering is the first thing the Court confiscates.

It liked me. I never learned why. Perhaps because I was the only prisoner who spoke to it as though it were a person rather than a fixture. Perhaps because when it passed my cell I said the evening office from muscle memory, half the words wrong, and it stopped to listen with an expression I can only describe as homesick.

* * *

Oscar Joubert does not do well in cages. I learned this on our third day in the dark, listening to him pace the cell next to mine — four steps, turn, four steps, turn — with the metronomic patience of a man who has decided that if he cannot escape he will at least wear a groove in the floor deep enough to hide in. Pip, two cells down, had gone quiet in the way that frightened me more than pacing; silence in a thief is either scheming or despair, and I could no longer remember which signs distinguished them. The crow was caged apart from all of us, in a lantern-cage of black iron that drank the light around it, and every hour it seemed dimmer, its voice thinner, until I began to wonder whether we had walked into this mountain carrying a god only to watch it gutter out like a tallow end.

"Anwen," Oscar said through the bars, on the third night. "You're the one with the theology. Talk to the ghost."

"Theology," I said, "requires a subject."

"Then talk to it anyway. You're good at talking. It's the one thing they didn't take."

He was right, which annoyed me, which is the natural condition of Oscar being right. So when the jailer-ghost made its rounds I did not pray this time. I asked it a question instead, the way you'd ask a tired traveler about the road.

"How long have you been here?"

"Long," it said. Then, after a pause in which I could hear it deciding to be honest: "I don't know. Time doesn't move down here the way it does up there. The Court bought my death along with the rest of me. A man who sold his death can't finish dying. So I walk the corridors and count the cells and try not to think about the fields above Thornfell where I was born."

"What was your name?"

"Gone. First thing they take." It rattled its chains, almost fondly. "They let me keep the chains, though. Sentimental, I suppose. Or cruel. With brokers it's always both."

"Who were the brokers?" I asked. "Before. Before the Court. Where did it come from?"

And the ghost went still — truly still, stiller than the dead, stiller than stone — because I had asked the question that nobody down here asks, the question the walls themselves seemed to lean in to hear.

"You want the history," it said. "The real one. Not the liturgy the acolytes chant."

"I want the truth. It's the only currency I have left that they haven't taxed."

Another gravel-laugh. Then it looked up and down the corridor, the way living smugglers do, and I understood that even here, beneath the world, caution was a habit older than memory.

"Come to the grate at the far wall," it said. "Where the old foundation shows through. There's a crack there where the mountain still listens. If I'm going to commit heresy against my employers, I'd rather the mountain heard it too."

* * *

The crack in the far wall was narrow as a knife-cut, and cold air moved through it, smelling of pine and snowmelt and distance. Real air. Air that had touched the sky. I pressed my forehead to the stone beside it and felt, faintly, a pulse — slow, vast, patient — and some fragment of me that had survived the Tollgate intact recognized it for what it was before my mind supplied the word.

A heartbeat. The mountain was alive. Or it had been. Or it was trying to be again.

The ghost settled across the corridor from me, chains pooling like a shed skin, and began to speak in the tone of a man unburdening himself at last — not confessional, exactly, but close. Confession requires shame, and shame requires selfhood, and it had been stripped of both. What it had instead was grievance, and grievance keeps better than memory.

"Before the Court," it said, "there was a shepherd."

"A man?"

"A god. Vael." The name landed in the cell like a dropped coin, and I felt Oscar go rigid in his cage three paces away — I could hear it, the sudden absence of his pacing, the held breath. "Vael of the antlers, Vael-of-the-slopes, the god who walked the high passes in the shape of a great horned beast and kept the flocks of the living the way any shepherd keeps sheep: counted them, guarded them, grieved for the ones the winter took. The mountain was its body. Every spring melt was the mountain turning over in its sleep. The old villages — yours, priestess, and yours, smuggler — they were built on the god's shoulders because nowhere under Vael's gaze was ever unsafe."

"I've heard that name," I said carefully. "In fragments. In the rites I can't remember learning."

"You would have. The church began as Vael's cult. Shepherds' prayers, harvest blessings, the lighting of lamps on the peaks so the god could see its flock in the dark." The ghost's voice roughened. "And then the merchants came. Not with swords. Swords the mountain could shrug off. They came with arithmetic."

"Bargains," I said.

"Soul-bargains. The oldest trade in the world, and the only one the gods never thought to forbid, because what sane creature imagines a market for the self? A farmer short on grain sells a season of his luck. A mother sells her grief. Small things, small prices, and the brokers kept the books and smiled. And then the famine came — the famine you know, Joubert, the one that emptied your village — and the bargains stopped being small. Whole villages sold whole children rather than watch them starve. And the Court grew fat, and the Court grew clever, and it learned that a soul in a lantern is worth more than a soul in a body, because a soul in a body might one day be free, and freedom is terrible for business."

"And Vael?" I asked. "What did the shepherd do while its flock was sold?"

For a long moment the ghost said nothing, and the cold air breathed through the crack, and somewhere far above us I swear I heard wind moving through stone like breath through teeth.

"At first it raged. That's the part the church burned out of the records — the early years, when Vael broke broker caravans in the passes and swallowed their strongholds into the rock. But you cannot rage your way through a ledger. Every caravan it destroyed, three more came over the southern passes. Every bargain it shattered, the debt simply transferred to the children of the debtor. The Court had found the one weapon a shepherd cannot fight: paper. Contracts signed in love. What was Vael to do — punish the starving for feeding their children?"

"So it stopped fighting the brokers," I said slowly, feeling the shape of it before the ghost said it, the way you feel stairs in the dark before your foot finds the edge.

"It started buying," the ghost said. "Quietly. One soul at a time. It couldn't buy them back openly — the Court would have raised the prices to infinity, priced a god out of its own flock. So Vael went to the brokers in disguise, in the shape of a plain gray crow, and it paid. And here is the part you must understand, priestess, the part that will reorganize every bone in your body: it paid with itself."

The pulse in the stone behind my forehead seemed to quicken.

"A god is not a purse," the ghost went on. "But a god is made of things — voice, dominion, name, divinity — and each of these can be spent, if the spender is willing to become less. Vael spent its voice first. That's why the prayers stopped being answered. Not abandonment. Purchase. Every whispered prayer for twenty years, every unanswered cry in every snowbound pass — the god wasn't silent because it had left. It was silent because its silence had already been spent, coin by coin, buying back the sold."

I put my hand flat against the stone. I am not ashamed to say I wept. Somewhere in the dark, Oscar Joubert sat down heavily, and the sound of a hard man folding was louder than any weeping.

"Its dominion next," the ghost said. "The storms it once called, the paths it once opened through the passes — spent. Its name, spent. And each purchase it made, each soul it bought back, it hid — because a recovered soul is just another asset the Court can repossess. It hid them in the only vault it trusted. Itself. It folded them into what remained of it, and what remained got smaller, and grayer, and more birdlike, until what was left of the great shepherd-god of Thornfell was a black crow with a god's memories and a beggar's purse."

"The crow," I said. "Our crow."

"Your crow. Which has been sitting in a lantern-cage forty feet from where Vael's last purchases sleep, growing dimmer by the hour, because the Court finally caught it. Do you understand now what Lady Sereth holds? Not a talking bird. A treasury. The last unspent coin of a god, plus every soul it ever ransomed, packed into feathers and stubbornness."

* * *

I sat with that for a long time. The ghost waited, patient as only the unfinished-dead can be, and eventually I asked the question I had been circling since the beginning, the question that had brought me defrocked and memoryless into a mountain's gut:

"Why did the church help them?"

The ghost's chains shifted. "Ah. Your order."

"My order. Whatever it was. Whoever I was. I know the rites, ghost. I know the litanies. I know that somewhere in the liturgy I memorized there are words that map exactly onto the Court's contracts — I felt it at the reckoning preparations, before they caught us. The seal on the courier's map. The tollgate's demands. Our liturgy and their ledger are the same document wearing different clothes. How?"

"Because the church was founded twice," the ghost said. "The first founding was Vael's — shepherds and lamplighters, keepers of the flock. The second founding came later, after the god went silent, when the Court understood that a silent god leaves a vacancy. Grief needs somewhere to go. The bereaved need someone to tell them their dead are at peace. And the Court —" its voice curled with contempt — "the Court is nothing if not accommodating. It founded a church. It staffed it with the sincere. It taught them rites that were, word for word, the language of contract, dressed in vestments. Every funeral prayer your order ever sang was a receipt. Every blessing was a lien. The faithful knelt and surrendered their dead into the lanterns believing they were sending them to heaven, and the brokers booked them as inventory."

"That's why I was defrocked," I said, and the memory surfaced then, not returned but surfacing, like a body rising in spring water — a synod chamber, my own voice shaking, telling men in fine robes that the bargains were theft, that the church had become the Court's storefront. "I saw it. I saw it and I screamed it and they took my collar and my name and I still didn't know how right I was."

"They defrocked you because you were dangerous," the ghost said, "and they locked you in a cell because Sereth is cautious, and they kept you alive because —" it paused, and I heard it choose honesty over kindness — "because the church's old rites still work down here. Recovered, remembered, spoken true, they can unbind what the Court binds. Sereth fears your memory coming back more than she fears the crow escaping. Memory is the one thing she cannot buy, because it was never ours to sell."

Behind me, in the dark, Oscar's voice came low and cracked: "Lise. Her soul. Whose coin bought it?"

And the ghost was quiet for so long that I thought it would refuse, and then it said, gently, the way you tell a man his house has burned:

"The last one. Vael's final purchase. It spent the last of its divinity — the last actual substance of godhood it possessed — to buy a starving girl from a village called Joubert's Ford. That was the price of your sister, smuggler. Not gold. Godhood. The Court took a god's divinity in payment and wrote it into the ledger as 'miscellaneous livestock, one head.' And when it was done, when nothing was left to spend, the crow flew away small and silent and has been trying to finish the work ever since, one feather's weight of hoarded strength at a time."

There was a sound from Oscar's cell that I will not describe except to say it was the sound a man makes when twenty years of unanswered prayers rearrange themselves into an answer he cannot bear.

* * *

The ghost rose to leave when it had finished — rounds to complete, cells to count, a debt to serve — but at the corridor's mouth it paused, and turned, and looked at me with eyes like banked coals.

"Priestess. One more thing. A gift, since you treated a chain-rattler like a person."

"Ghost, I've been given nothing but gifts lately and most of them were knives."

"This one isn't." It drifted closer to the crack in the wall, to the cold clean air, to the pulse. "The mountain listens. It has listened for twenty years to a god's silence, and it remembers what its god sounded like, and tonight it heard the history spoken true in its own halls. Things change when the mountain hears its own story told. Ask anyone who was here the last time." Its coal-eyes glinted. "And your friend the crow — check on it. Tonight. Whatever Sereth thinks she has caged, she has miscounted. Gods are bad inventory. They don't sit still on the shelf."

It rattled away down the corridor, chains whispering, and left me alone with the crack in the wall and the pulse beneath my palm — faster now, I was nearly sure, though I told myself I was imagining it, the way I had once told myself the bargains were theft and been right.

I went to the front of my cell and looked toward the distant iron cage where the crow hung, a smudge of darkness drinking light.

"Hey," I said softly, in the cadence of the old rites, the ones my hands remembered even if I didn't. "Shepherd. If you can hear me — we know now. All of it. What you spent. What you paid. What you carried."

The crow did not answer. But it lifted its head, and in the dark of its cage two points of light opened — not the dull gleam of an eye, but something older, banked, patient, like coals deciding whether to become a fire.

And beneath my knees, faint but unmistakable, the floor of the Hollow Court trembled — once, softly, like a sleeper turning over — and far above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, I heard the first thunder of a storm gathering over Thornfell, though the sky had been clear as glass when they brought us down.

Sereth had miscounted. The mountain had heard its own story.

And it was waking up.
