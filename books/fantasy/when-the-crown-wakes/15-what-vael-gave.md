## 15. What Vael Gave

The cell they gave us was better than I expected, which is the sort of sentence that should tell you everything about how low my expectations had fallen.

It was a real room, at least. Stone walls without the weeping. A bench wide enough to sleep on. A barred window that looked out onto nothing — not darkness exactly, but a kind of held breath, the space between lanterns where the Court kept its unlit hours. They'd put our cages side by side against the far wall, Pip's and mine and the crow's, close enough that if I stretched out my arm I could have touched the black iron of his prison.

I didn't stretch out my arm. Not yet. There are some conversations you have to walk toward slowly, like the edge of a cliff you already know you're going to jump off.

Pip had cried herself out an hour ago, curled on her bench with her back to us, her small shoulders doing that thing they do when she's determined to be asleep and furious about it. Anwen sat cross-legged in the middle of the floor with her eyes closed, murmuring fragments of rites she'd recovered from whatever broken place Sereth's trap had knocked them loose. Half of what she said was in Old Liturgical and half was just her own name, repeated like a woman testing whether it still fit.

And the crow watched me.

He'd been watching me since the jailer-ghost left. Since Anwen finished telling us what Vael had been — the shepherd-god of Thornfell, who hadn't abandoned us at all, who had spent twenty years buying back stolen souls one coin at a time until there was nothing left of him but feathers and a voice.

"Say it," I finally told him. "Whatever it is you've been not-saying since the ravine. Say it now."

The crow tilted his head. In the gloom of his cage, those banked coals of his eyes brightened by a degree. "You want the accounting, then."

"I always want the accounting. That's why I'm good at my job." I leaned my head back against the stone. "Every smuggler I ever guided, every toll I ever paid — I wanted to know where the money went. So. Where did it go? All these years. All this strength you keep burning through, saving us, spending yourself down like a man drinking his own blood. What were you *saving it for*?"

The silence went on long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Long enough that Pip's breathing changed on her bench — slower, deeper, the fake sleep of someone listening with everything she had.

"You remember the famine winter," the crow said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I remember being fed."

"You remember more than that. You remember Lise giving you her portion and telling your mother she wasn't hungry. You remember the grain cart that came in the third week, and the men in gray hoods who didn't take coin." His voice was gentle in the way that a surgeon's hands are gentle. "You were seven. You weren't supposed to understand. But you understood enough to hate yourself for eating, and you've been eating carefully ever since. Small portions. Small life. Never taking more than your share of anything, including happiness."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the ground the answer stands on." The coals flared briefly. "The Court took two hundred souls from that valley that winter. Your sister's among them. And when I came down out of the high passes to see what had happened — when I found my flock sold out from under my own mountain — I made a bargain of my own."

"With Sereth."

"With the Court itself. With the ledger beneath the throne." He shifted on his perch, and for a moment the cage seemed too small for him, the way a word can seem too small for a meaning. "I traded my voice. My divinity. Everything I was, paid out over time, in installments, to buy souls back. But the Court doesn't sell, Oscar. That's the first thing you have to understand. It only *leases*. Every soul I bought back, I could only buy into escrow. Held. Waiting. Not free."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the reckoning. For the hundredth year, when all contracts come due and get sealed forever — or when someone standing in the right place, holding the right key, unmakes them instead." The crow's feathers rustled, and I saw it now, what I should have seen in the ravine, on the road, in every firelit camp where he seemed a little brighter after each fight: he hadn't been recovering. He'd been *storing*. Twenty years of installments, hoarded feather by feather, hidden inside a body small enough that no broker would think to look twice. "Everything I am now is the last payment. The final coin. And there are only two things it can buy."

The room felt very quiet. Even Anwen had stopped murmuring.

"Tell me," I said.

"Option one." His voice dropped into something almost conversational, which from him meant deadly serious. "I pour everything into you. All of it. You become what I was — what I *am* — a god of Thornfell, whole and waking, strong enough to break into that vault tonight, crack every lantern, and burn the Court's ledgers before Sereth finishes her morning tea. No reckoning needed. No risk of their sealing ceremony going through. Just you, with power enough to move mountains, doing it alone."

"And option two?"

"I keep it. I spend it at the reckoning, in front of the whole Court, when every contract in existence is gathered together to be sealed. One act. Total. Every bargain unwritten at once — not just the ones I bought, not just Lise. *All* of them. Every soul in every lantern in that vault, freed in a single stroke, in front of the brokers, so they can never do it again." He paused. "But it has to happen at the reckoning. Which means surviving until the reckoning. Which means trusting that three prisoners and a caged bird can break out of the Hollow Court in time to reach the throne."

"And if we fail," I said, "everything seals forever. Including Lise."

"Yes."

I looked at him for a long moment. "There's no version where we split it. Half to me, half to the reckoning?"

"No. Power isn't grain, Oscar. It doesn't divide. It's a single coin, and coins spend whole or not at all." The coals dimmed slightly, and something in his tone shifted — became older, wearier, the voice of a god rather than a bird. "I know what you're thinking. You're thinking: take the sure thing. Save your sister. Damn the rest. Two hundred souls from one valley, and how many valleys? How many centuries? You could save *her*, tonight, with certainty, and let the world keep its rot."

"Is that wrong?"

"It's human," he said simply. "It's the most human thing there is. And I would not think less of you. I bought her soul with my last true coin knowing full well I was choosing one girl over thousands. Love is allowed to be small. That's what makes it love and not arithmetic."

Something cracked open in my chest at that. I thought about Lise's lantern in the vault, the small pale light that had answered when I called her name. I thought about carrying godhood in my chest like a second heart, walking out of this mountain remade, and what I would owe afterward — what a god owes, what a god *is*, whether there's any such thing as a god who gets to go home.

"And if I take it," I asked quietly, "what happens to you?"

"Nothing you need to weigh. I'm a fragment. Fragments don't get endings, they get *uses*." He ruffled his feathers, brisk again. "Pour me into you and there's nothing left over. No crow. No voice. You'd carry all of me, and I'd be — done. Finished. A story with its last page turned."

"So it's not just my choice. It's your death either way."

"My death was twenty years ago, in a ledger hall, when I signed away my name." The coals steadied. "What's left is only deciding what the corpse buys."

From her bench, without turning around, Pip spoke. Her voice was rough from crying but steady underneath, the way it always was once she got past the worst of it. "If you take it," she said, "you save Lise. If you don't, you might save everyone, including Lise, if we don't all die horribly first." She rolled over and fixed me with one red-rimmed eye. "No pressure, Joubert."

"Pip—"

"No, I mean it. Everyone keeps waiting for you to decide like it's a private thing, but it's not, is it?" She sat up, knees pulled to her chest, and pointed at the crow's cage. "That thing — *him* — he's been planning this since before any of us were born. He picked you up in a ravine. He's been burning himself out keeping us alive because *you're* the decision, Joubert. We're just the people who have to live in whatever you choose." She swallowed. "So choose like we're real. Because we are."

Anwen opened her eyes. When she spoke, her voice had the cadence of liturgy, though the words were plain. "The church taught that faith is certainty. That was the first lie. Faith is choosing without certainty and calling it devotion anyway." She looked at me across the dark cell. "I lost my memories to a stone warden and I still know that much. Whatever you choose, choose it *as* a choice. Not as fear wearing a sensible coat."

I laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprising us all. "You lot realize I'm a smuggler's guide. My entire skill set is knowing which passes aren't watched and keeping my mouth shut."

"Then keep your mouth shut," Pip said, "and open your ears. What's your gut say?"

My gut. Twenty years of unanswered prayer, and here was the answer at last, sitting in a cage asking me to spend him. My gut said: take the power. Save her. Walk out of here a god and damn the arithmetic, because I had spent my whole life being small and careful and it had saved nothing and no one.

But that was fear talking. Fear wearing my sister's face.

I thought about the jailer-ghost's story. Vael, the shepherd, counting his flock night after night, refusing to lose even the strays. A god whose whole nature was that no one got left behind — not the lame ewe, not the lost lamb, not the soul that some broker decided was cheap enough to sell. If I took his power and saved only Lise, I wouldn't be honoring him. I'd be unmaking him twice: once when I consumed him, and once when I betrayed everything he'd spent himself to stand for.

And Lise — grown now, somewhere under all those years of lantern-light, still a child inside the glass but *herself*, still waiting — Lise had given me her bread when we were children. She had starved so I could eat. If she knew that her freedom cost ten thousand others theirs, she would never forgive me. She'd walk out of that vault free and look at her brother the way you look at a stranger wearing a loved one's face.

"The reckoning," I said.

The coals blazed.

"You're certain?" the crow asked, and his voice trembled — actually trembled, the first time I'd ever heard it do that.

"No," I said. "Anwen says that's the point." I stood up and crossed to his cage, and this time I did stretch out my arm, pressing two fingers through the bars to the small black head. He leaned into them, warm as a banked fire, warmer than any bird had a right to be. "But I know which choice leaves me able to look at my sister. I know which one she'd make. She proved it with a heel of bread when we were seven." I swallowed hard. "Spend it all. Every bargain. All of them. At the reckoning, in front of everyone, so it can never be built again."

For a moment the crow pressed harder against my fingers, and the warmth coming off him was almost too much to bear, like holding your hand over a hearth you've missed for twenty years.

"Then listen carefully," he said, "because we have perhaps a day, and a great deal to do. The reckoning requires three things to function: the crown on the throne, the ledger beneath it, and the chief broker to speak the sealing words. Remove any one and the ceremony fails. Break the crown's authority and the lanterns can't be locked. Corrupt the ledger and the contracts can't be read. Silence the broker and no one can speak the seal." His eye gleamed up at me, coal-bright, fierce. "Sereth thinks she holds all three. She holds none. The crown answers to the mountain, and the mountain is *mine*. The ledger was written in stolen names, and I remember every one. And Sereth—" the coals flared white-hot, and for an instant I saw through the feathers to something vast and antlered and grieving, something that had walked these peaks when the world was younger, "—Sereth speaks with a voice that was borrowed from me. I intend to take it back."

Above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the thunder rolled again — closer now, angrier, the storm over Thornfell gathering itself like a fist.

Pip was already on her feet, digging in the lining of her jacket for lockpicks the Tallymen had somehow failed to find, her eyes bright with the particular madness that seized her whenever a lock was stupid enough to exist. Anwen had risen too, straightening her ruined vestments with the automatic dignity of a woman preparing to conduct a service.

"What do you need from us?" Anwen asked.

"Everything," said the crow. "Starting with getting out of this cage. Oscar — the bar on the left side, third weld from the top. Pip forged the iron and I've been working the seam since they brought me in. One good pull."

I gripped the bar. Looked at him one last time in the coal-dark.

"When this is done," I said, "when every lantern is empty and every ledger is ash — what happens to you? To the last coin, spent?"

The crow was quiet a moment. Then, softly, in a voice that was almost — almost — the voice from my childhood prayers:

"Then, little shepherd, you'll find out what a god sounds like when he comes home."

I pulled the bar. The weld parted like a promise being kept, and somewhere far above us, the storm broke over Thornfell all at once, and the first drop of rain hit the mountain like a starting bell.

* * *

They came for us at the changing of the watch, which was the mistake I'd expected Sereth's people to avoid making. Routine kills vigilance; even in a cathedral-cavern full of damned souls, guards get bored between bells. Two acolytes in gray, keys on a ring at the lead one's belt, moving down the row of cells with the shuffling gait of men who had done this a thousand times and would do it a thousand more.

We were ready for them. Or rather, Pip was ready for them, crouched behind the door with a length of torn blanket twisted between her fists, and I was ready for them, flat against the wall beside the frame, and Anwen was ready for them in the way that frightened me most — standing in plain view, hands folded, wearing the serene expression of a priestess about to deliver a blessing.

The door opened. The lead acolyte looked up into Anwen's face and hesitated, because serenity is more alarming than a weapon, and in that half-second of hesitation Pip's blanket-loop went around his throat and my elbow found the second one's temple, and it was over in the time it takes to regret a bad bet.

"Keys," Pip whispered, already lifting them off the unconscious man. Her hands were shaking. I noticed she didn't mention it, and neither did I. "Joubert. The crow's cage first."

"Not yet." I looked down the corridor, toward the distant glow of the Court proper, where the reckoning preparations were already underway — scaffolds of pale bone-lattice rising around the throne, acolytes carrying lanterns in procession, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a life in a glass skin. "First we need to know where they're keeping the schedule. When does Sereth start the sealing?"

"Midnight bell," said a voice from the floor.

The lead acolyte was awake. Pip's loop must have slipped. He was looking up at us with the flat calm of a man who had already accepted his death and found it boring. "The reckoning begins at the midnight bell. You have six hours. And it won't matter, guide, because the vault is sealed, the crown is warded, and the Lady Sereth has been preparing for a thief with a map since before you were born."

"Six hours," I repeated.

"Six hours," he agreed, almost kindly. "Spend them wisely."

Pip knocked him out properly this time. Then she looked at me, keys jingling in her fist, and grinned with all the bravado of a sixteen-year-old who had decided to be terrified later.

"Well," she said. "Six hours. Anyone got a plan that doesn't end with all of us in lanterns?"

The crow lifted his head from his cage, and his eyes were twin fires now, and when he spoke, his voice carried down the corridor like the first word of a storm.

"Yes," he said. "Bring me to the ledger hall. It's time someone audited the books."

Far above, the rain hammered the peak, and beneath our feet, patient as a debt, the mountain waited to be introduced to its own name.
