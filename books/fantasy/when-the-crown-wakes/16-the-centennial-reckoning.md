## 16. The Centennial Reckoning

Here's a thing nobody tells you about the end of the world: it has excellent seating.

I know because I had one of the best seats in the Hollow Court, and I'd have traded it for a ditch in Cinderhollow, no questions asked. The vault cellars beneath the lantern gallery opened onto the great cavern through iron grilles, and from my little barred window I could see everything — the tiers of masked brokers descending like frost down a windowpane, the soul-lanterns strung overhead in their thousands like a sky that had been picked clean of stars and hung back up as inventory.

And at the center of it all, on a dais of black stone beneath the antlered throne, a cage.

And in the cage, a crow.

They'd named tonight the Centennial Reckoning, which sounded grand until you understood what it meant: every bargain ever struck beneath Thornfell, every soul sold for grain or gold or a winter's mercy, was about to be sealed. Permanently. No renegotiation, no redemption, no appeal. The ledgers would close, and everyone inside them — including, if Sereth got her way, a certain fragment of a silent god — would belong to the Court forever.

The crow looked small in that cage. That was the thing that kept snagging in my chest like a fishhook. It had always been small. Small and rude and too clever by half, riding Oscar's shoulder and calling him *little shepherd* in that voice like a bell rung underwater. But small things can be enormous. I'd spent my whole life being small and knowing exactly how much weight a small thing can carry when it decides to.

"Stop looking at me like that," I muttered to the cage, though it couldn't hear me through three feet of stone and a grille. "You're going to be fine. We've got a plan."

We did not have a plan. We had a lockpick set, a priestess with holes where her memory used to be, and a mercenary who hadn't slept in two days because he kept hearing his sister's voice inside a lantern he couldn't open. As plans went, it had all the structural integrity of wet paper.

But then, I'd broken out of worse with less. Probably. I couldn't actually think of anything worse, but I was choosing not to examine that too closely, because despair is just fear that's done its bookkeeping.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last three days deserve documenting, if only so someone knows how stupid we were.

After Sereth's trap sprang — after she took the crow and took me red-handed in the lantern vault with Lise Joubert's lantern sitting in my satchel like the world's most damning souvenir — they'd split us up. Standard practice. Divide the merchandise. Oscar went into the deep cells with the jailer-ghosts. Anwen went somewhere I didn't know, and for two days I assumed the worst, because assuming the worst is a hobby of mine and I'm very good at it.

Me, they kept in the vault cellar. Which, I want to be clear, was an insult. I'm a professional. I have stolen things from under the noses of people who could smell lies. And they put me in a room full of locks.

Well. One lock, technically. But it was a Court lock, old work, older than any city I'd ever robbed, and it sang to me the way good locks do — a hum in the teeth, a riddle in the tumblers. They'd taken my picks, obviously. They'd left me my boots, my belt, my hairpins, and my dignity, in roughly that order of value to them.

Amateurs.

The first night I worked the hairpin against the door out of pure spite, not because I expected to escape but because I wanted the lock to know somebody was thinking about it. And that's when I found the second thing in the cellar besides me: a loose stone behind the water jug, and behind the loose stone, a girl.

Not a whole girl. A hand, reaching up through a gap in the flagstones, pale as candle wax, with a scrap of parchment tied to one finger.

I screamed. Quietly. I'm a professional screamer too.

The hand belonged to Anwen. Or most of her did — she'd wriggled up through some pilgrim crawl-space from the adjacent cell, gray-faced and shaking, and when she got her shoulders through the gap she sat on my floor breathing like a landed fish while I tried to remember how to form sentences.

"You're alive," I said, brilliantly.

"Barely." She looked around my cell with the flat calm of a woman taking inventory of a disaster. "They questioned you?"

"They asked me who taught me to pick locks. I said a badger." I shrugged. "It's as true as anything."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. The first time I'd seen her almost-smile since the Tollgate of Names took her memories, since she'd given up her name and her order and twenty years of certainty to get us through that gate. She still didn't remember who she'd been. But fragments were coming back — she'd told us that much before the trap — fragments surfacing like bodies in a thawing lake, and some of them were useful.

"The rites," I said. "Did you find them? The ones you half-remembered? The unbinding words?"

Her face did something complicated. "I remembered more than the words." She held up her hands, and I saw the burns on her fingertips, fresh and precise, in the shape of a sigil I didn't recognize. "I remembered why the church sealed the tunnels. And I remembered what the sealing rites are *for*." She met my eyes. "Pip. The church built the Court's locks. All of them. Every cage in this place answers to the same first-principle binding, and I know its counter-sign now. Not perfectly. My hands remember better than my head does. But—"

"But you can open things," I finished.

"I can open *one* thing. Maybe two, if the god is generous and my fingers hold." She flexed her burned hands, wincing. "So choose carefully."

Oh, I'd already chosen. I'd chosen the moment I saw the cage on the dais.

"The crow," I said.

She nodded slowly. "Oscar first. He'll never forgive us otherwise."

"Oscar will forgive us anything if the crow flies." I grinned at her, and it felt feral even to me. "Besides, we need him. Nobody fights like a man with nothing left to lose except one bird."

* * *

Getting to Oscar was the part I'm least proud of, mostly because it involved the worst crime in my personal catalog: I let myself get caught on purpose. Again. By the same people. There are thieves in Cinderhollow who'd disown me.

It went like this. The night before the Reckoning, the acolytes came to fetch me for questioning — Sereth liked her interrogations fresh, apparently, like bread — and instead of picking my newly-opened lock and running, I sat on my cot with my hands visible and my eyes down and let them take me. Because the questioning rooms connected to the deep cells. I'd heard the guards say so. And because Anwen couldn't fit through the crawl-space carrying anything, and someone had to bring the tools.

The tools were this: two hairpins, a sliver of bone I'd snapped off my soup bowl and honed against the flagstones for two days until it could shave the hair off a moth, and a folded scrap of parchment with a sigil drawn in Anwen's careful hand, copied from memory she didn't know she had. I carried all of it in the lining of my boot, which the acolytes searched with the thoroughness of men who had never met a girl who grew up hungry in Marrowgate.

The questioning room smelled of tallow and old iron. The Tallyman across the table had a ledger-face, all ruled lines and columns, and he asked his questions in a voice like a clerk reading aloud. Where did you learn the vault's layout. Who helped you. What did the crow say to you.

"It says a lot of things," I told him. "Mostly insults. You wouldn't like it."

He raised a quill. I watched his hands, not his eyes — eyes lie, hands tell the truth — and waited for the moment his attention dipped to the page.

That was when the lights went out.

Not all of them. Just ours. The tallow lamps guttered in the same instant, both of them, which is not a thing lamps do, and in the dark I heard a sound I will hear on my deathbed: a low, vast exhalation, like a mountain shifting in its sleep, moving through the stone of the questioning room. The Tallyman made a small noise. His quill clattered.

In the dark, something pressed a key into my hand. Cold iron, heavy, real.

Then the lamps relit, and the Tallyman was staring at the doorway, where nothing stood, and I was staring at an iron key I very much intended to keep, and neither of us said anything because some silences are load-bearing.

Later — much later, after — Oscar claimed he'd been praying. Not asking for anything, he said. Just talking, the way you talk to someone who never answers, because the habit outlives the hope. And maybe the mountain had finally decided to listen. Or maybe the crow, caged above the Court with divine fire banked in its hollow bones, had reached down through the stone toward the one person it loved and nudged the world half an inch.

Either way, I had a key, a distracted guard, and approximately nine seconds of darkness-shaped opportunity.

I used eight of them.

* * *

The deep cells were exactly as cheerful as advertised, which is to say they were carved from the mountain's living rock and lit by soul-lanterns, which means the light itself was made of people. I try not to think about that too hard. I fail regularly.

Oscar was in the third cell down, and when my borrowed key turned in his lock he came off the cot like a spring released, and for one horrible moment I thought he was going to hug me, which would have been unbearable for both of us. He settled for gripping my shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"Pip."

"Don't make it weird. We're escaping. Move."

"Anwen?"

"Crawled halfway here on her own burned hands. She's waiting at the junction under the gallery." I handed him the parchment scrap. "She says the counter-sign works on the cages. One use, maybe two. And Oscar—" I made him look at me. "Sereth's going to eat the crow tonight. On the dais. In front of everyone. That's the opening of the Reckoning ceremony. So whatever we do, we do it during the ritual, when every broker in the Court is watching the throne."

He read the sigil twice, folded it away, and there was a look on his face I'd only seen once before, in the ravine, right before the crow spoke and the world tilted. It wasn't hope exactly. Hope is soft. This was hope that had been through a forge.

"Good," he said. "Because I've been talking to the walls for three days, Pip, and last night—" He stopped. Shook his head. "Last night something answered. I don't know if it was Vael or the mountain or Lise. But the Court thinks it's won. The Court thinks everything is locked." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely terrifying. "Locks are your department. Let's go prove a point."

* * *

The Reckoning began at midnight, and the Court dressed for it.

From our hiding place — a maintenance gallery above the lantern vaults, wide enough for three thin, terrified people and thick with the dust of a century — we watched the brokers assemble in their masks of silver and horn. The tiers filled. The antlered throne caught the light of ten thousand soul-lanterns and threw it back in colors that don't have names. Somewhere below, a choir of acolytes began a drone that vibrated in my back teeth, and the great brass ledgers of the Court were carried in procession, each one chained shut, each one holding more lives than a city holds people.

And then Lady Sereth ascended the dais, and the crowd went quiet the way snow goes quiet, total and oppressive, and two Tallymen carried forward the cage.

The crow was standing. That's the detail that broke my heart and rebuilt it in the same breath. They'd starved it, dimmed it, drained it for three days — I could see the gray in its feathers, the way it swayed like a drunkard — and it stood anyway, at the front bar of its cage, looking directly at the throne. At the crown of antlers. Like it was checking the work.

"Oh, you magnificent idiot," I whispered. "Hold on."

Sereth's voice filled the cavern without effort, the way water fills a bowl. "One hundred years," she said, "since the first bargain. One hundred years of honest trade beneath honest stone. Tonight the ledgers close. Tonight every debt becomes permanent, every purchase eternal, and the Court's stock—" her masked face turned toward the cage "—is enriched by stolen divinity."

She lifted the cage one-handed. The crow didn't flinch.

"And what does the Court do," Sereth said softly, "with a god that will not stay bought?"

The answer, apparently, was: it eats it. She set the cage on a brazier-stand beside the throne, and the acolytes began lighting the coals beneath, and the crowd murmured its appetite, and I discovered that rage is a very clarifying emotion. All my fear just — filed itself neatly away and pointed at her.

"Now," I breathed.

Anwen was already moving. She'd spent the last hour inscribing the counter-sign in chalk-dust and ash along the gallery rail, her burned fingers steady, her lips moving through words that surfaced from wherever lost things go. When the choir hit its crescendo and the first coal flared beneath the cage, she pressed both palms flat against the stone and spoke the unbinding.

I felt it happen. I don't have the vocabulary for what it felt like — closest I can get is the moment a lock's last tumbler gives, that tiny surrender, magnified until it shook the mountain. Every cage in the Hollow Court sighed open at once.

Every cage.

The lantern-racks in the vaults below us rattled. Cell doors far underground boomed like distant thunder. And the cage on the dais — the cage over the coals — simply fell apart, its bars folding outward like a flower deciding to bloom, and the crow dropped through the collapsing smoke and did not fall.

It rose.

Gray feathers kindled. I watched the color come back into it like dawn coming into a valley, black burning through to ember, ember to gold, and the Court's perfect silence shattered into screaming. Brokers fled their tiers. Tallymen drew blades on shadows. And Sereth spun toward the throne, toward the crown of antlers, because she knew — anyone with sense knew — that the crow wasn't the prize anymore.

The crown was.

Which was fine. That was the plan. That was always the plan, the mad, paper-thin plan: let the Court spend its terror on the bird, and send Oscar up the pilgrim stair toward the throne while nobody sane was watching.

Nobody sane was watching. Unfortunately, Sereth had never once in a hundred years been accused of sanity.

She saw him. Of course she saw him. And she moved to intercept, robes streaming, and I did the single bravest and stupidest thing of my career:

I threw the lantern.

Lise Joubert's lantern — the one I'd been caught stealing, the one they'd returned to the racks, the one I'd re-stolen from the vaults on my way past because I am, above all else, consistent — arced through the smoky air of the Hollow Court, end over end, glowing like a falling star.

Everything stopped. Everything, everywhere, all at once. A thousand masked faces tracked that little glass light. Sereth froze mid-stride, because a broker's first law is that stock does not fall, and Oscar caught it out of the air four steps from the throne, cradling it against his chest like something newborn.

"Go!" I screamed at him, my voice cracking down the middle. "GO!"

He went.

And the crow — blazing now, a small sun with wings — wheeled once over the chaos and dove, not at Sereth, not at the fleeing brokers, but straight down between Oscar and the throne, planting itself on the antlered crown like it had been flying toward that perch for a hundred years.

The mountain woke up.

I felt it through the soles of my feet before I heard it: a groan in the deep stone, vast and slow, the sound of something enormous turning over in a sleep of centuries. Dust sifted from the ceiling. The soul-lanterns flickered — all of them, thousands, a whole sky of stolen people guttering at once — and in the flicker I swear I saw the lights lean, all together, toward the throne. Toward the crow. Toward home.

Sereth recovered faster than the world did. Her mask came off — I'll never forget that, the choice she made, shedding the last pretense of civilization — and underneath she was already something else, something the contracts had eaten down to the wire, and she lunged for the throne with a scream that had no human parts left in it.

Oscar turned to meet her with a lantern in one arm and nothing in his hands.

And the crow opened its beak, and spoke with a voice that was not a crow's voice, that was not any voice that had spoken in Thornfell for twenty years, and it said:

"*Enough.*"

The word hit the Court like the first day of spring hits a frozen river.

I grabbed Anwen's arm. She grabbed mine. Below us, the ledgers' chains began, link by link, to snap.

"Hold onto something," I told her. "I think the reckoning's about to go both ways."

Above the throne, the crown of antlers blazed, and the mountain opened its eyes.
