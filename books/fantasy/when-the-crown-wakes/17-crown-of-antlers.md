## 17. Crown of Antlers

The throne room of the Hollow Court was the kind of place that made you understand why people used to worship in caves. Not because caves are holy — because when the dark is that old and that patient, you start looking for something bigger than yourself to blame it on.

I came up out of the pilgrim tunnel with Pip's lockpicks still warm in my pocket and Anwen's rites still ringing in my ears, and I did what any sensible man does when he's about to commit the largest theft in recorded history: I checked my boots were tied.

The crow — caged now, somewhere behind me in Sereth's keeping, and every step away from it felt like walking with a broken rib — had told me one thing before they took it. *The crown is a key, not a chair. Remember which you're holding.*

Gods. Even in fragments, even in a cage, they can't resist a bit of advice.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last hour deserves some accounting, if only so someone writes down how stupid we all were.

The reckoning was tonight. The Court had assembled itself the way a wound assembles pus: brokers in their silk masks, acolytes in their bone-white robes, lanterns by the thousand strung along the cavern walls like a sky that had been harvested. Every soul sold in a hundred years, hanging there, waiting for the centennial seal to close over them like ice over a drowning man's face.

Sereth had paraded the crow on a silver perch at the foot of the antlered throne. She'd meant it as theater. It worked. Half the Court leaned forward to look at the little black bird that was, allegedly, stolen divine property, and while they looked, Pip went over the vault wall like she'd been born on a rope, and Anwen stood up in the middle of three hundred masked monsters and began to sing.

Not a hymn, exactly. Something older. The rites her order had buried, the ones her lost memories had come back carrying like contraband. Her voice wasn't beautiful. It was true, which is worse, and the acolytes nearest her started swaying like wheat, because whatever the church had been before it served the Court, it had been this first: people singing at the dark, refusing to flinch.

That was my window. I took it.

* * *

The dais steps were carved from a single piece of black stone, and each one had a name on it. Not decorative names. Real ones. I recognized the style of the lettering from the ledgers below — the same careful clerk's hand, the same little flourishes. A thousand names going up, and at the top, the throne, and above the throne—

The crown.

It hung in the air, actually hung, turning slowly on nothing, and it was exactly what the name promised: antlers. Vast ones, branching, pale as winter wood, and threaded through every tine were threads of light. Soul-light. Hundreds of filaments running down out of the crown and into the throne, into the floor, into the ledgers of the world beneath the world.

Every lantern in the room answered it. I could feel it in my teeth, a hum like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

*Take it,* said something in my head, and for one horrible moment I thought it was Vael, but it wasn't. It was the crown itself. Of course it was. A key that wants to be a chair. That's the oldest trick there is — power doesn't need to fight you, it just needs to fit your hand.

I climbed anyway.

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing from gods-shaped-holes: the closer you get, the more reasonable it sounds. By the fourth step I could hear the crown thinking at me, and its thoughts were generous. *You've carried enough. You've been small enough. Take me and never be invisible again. Take me and no Tallyman will ever touch what's yours. Take me and you could buy her back a hundred times over.*

"You're selling," I said through my teeth, "to a man whose whole family history is bad purchases."

The crown didn't answer. It didn't have to. It just kept shining, patient as debt.

At the top of the stairs I stopped, because Sereth had noticed.

I don't know how else to describe it except that the whole room tilted toward her attention, like iron filings finding a magnet. Below, Anwen's song faltered. Somewhere in the vault dark, I heard Pip swear — a good sign; dead people don't swear — and then the crowd parted, and Lady Sereth looked up at me across the length of her own cathedral-cavern, and smiled behind her porcelain mask.

"Joubert," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like a coin being bitten. "You're standing on my stairs."

"They're nice stairs." My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the mercenary's whole art, really. "Names on every one. Whose idea was that?"

"The Court remembers its customers." She drifted forward, silk whispering over stone, and the crowd closed behind her like water. "As it will remember you. Put the crown down, Oscar Joubert, and we'll discuss terms. You want the girl's lantern. Fine. You want the bird. Fine. You've earned—"

"No."

The word came out of me before I'd decided to say it, which is either faith or stupidity, and I've stopped pretending I know the difference.

Sereth paused. "No?"

"I've seen your terms." I kept climbing. Two steps left. One. "Twenty years ago my parents bought grain with my sister's soul, and your Court called that fair trade. Your enforcers put a knife in a child thief for picking a lock. Your church sang your praises until it forgot its own name. I'm done discussing terms with you. I'm here to break them."

"And you think *that*—" her gloved hand rose, indicating the crown, the throne, the humming web of light, "—will break anything? Child. That is the Court. That has always been the Court. Whoever holds it holds every bargain ever written. You would not be ending us. You would be *becoming* us, and you would do it gladly, because you have wanted to matter since the day your sister starved and you ate."

The worst part was that she wasn't wrong about the wanting. The worst part was that the crown pulsed warm when she said it, like a dog hearing its name.

I reached the top.

* * *

The antlers were cold. That surprised me. Everything else in this place ran hot — the lanterns, the ledgers, the greed — but the crown sat in my hands like river-ice, and the light threading through it went very, very still.

And the voice stopped.

All that generosity, all that whispered promise, gone silent the instant I actually held it. Because the crown had done its work already, hadn't it? It had shown me the shape of the man who would take it, and I had looked at that shape, and I had said no.

Power doesn't need to fight you. But sometimes it needs you to fight *it*, once, out loud, where the whole dark can hear.

"Oscar." Sereth had reached the foot of the dais. Her mask had tilted up, and behind the porcelain eyeholes something moved that I decided not to examine too closely. "Set it down. Last kindness I will offer."

"I'm not setting it down." I turned, crown in both hands, and looked out over the Court — the brokers frozen mid-bargain, the acolytes swaying to Anwen's half-finished song, the thousand lanterns burning their patient green-gold burn. Somewhere among them, one lantern bore my family name. One small light, twenty years dimmed, still waiting.

"I'm using it."

I found her the way you find a face in a crowd — not with eyes, exactly. The crown knew its own ledger. I thought *Lise Joubert* and the light in the antlers bent, flowed, pointed, and there: third tier, eastern wall, a lantern no bigger than a loaf of bread, flickering like a candle someone kept relighting out of stubbornness.

The crown's key-nature woke under my hands. I felt the mechanism of the whole Court turn toward my intent — locks I couldn't see, seals older than the mountain, the great centennial binding coiling tight around every soul in the room, ready to snap shut forever at midnight.

I fed Lise's name into it.

The antlers blazed. Light ran down the threads, out of the crown, across the ceiling of the world, and touched her lantern, and the seal on it — a wax mark I knew, the same seal that had been on the map that started all this, the Court's signature on my family's ruin — cracked like an egg.

The lantern opened.

What came out wasn't fire. It was smaller than that and infinitely larger: a thread of pale gold, drifting up, uncoiling after twenty years of being folded small, and for half a heartbeat the whole Court went quiet enough that I heard it.

I heard *her*. Not words. Just the sound a child makes when she finally stops waiting — that long, shaking exhale, the one that comes right before crying or laughing, whichever wins.

Then the light shot toward me like a swallow coming home, and Sereth moved.

* * *

I'd like to tell you I saw it coming. I'd like to tell you I'm quick enough to dodge a broker-god crossing forty feet of cathedral in the time it takes a soul to remember how to fly. What actually happened is that I threw myself sideways off the dais with the crown still in my hands, which everyone will tell you is the wrong way to fall, and Sereth's hand — no longer a hand, something with too many joints and a glove stretched over it like a lie over a ledger — raked the air where my head had been and took the top two steps of the throne instead.

Stone didn't shatter. Stone *unmade*. The steps simply stopped having ever existed, and the absence hissed.

"YOU WOULD SPEND IT ON ONE?" Her voice had come apart from her mouth entirely now, filling the cavern, cracking lantern-glass along the eastern wall. "A HUNDRED YEARS OF LEDGERS, AND YOU WOULD SPEND THE KEY ON ONE SOUL?"

"Yeah," I said, rolling, getting my feet under me, hugging the crown like a baby and hating how it looked and loving how it felt. "Turns out that's the whole point of keys. They're not for thrones. They're for doors."

She came again, faster than anything that size had a right to move, and I learned something important about the crown in that instant: it didn't make me strong. It made me *positioned*. When I raised it, the light in the antlers bent between us, and Sereth broke off her strike like a dog hitting an invisible fence — because the crown outranked her. Because I was, for as long as I held it, the thing she answered to.

Which meant I had maybe a minute before she remembered she didn't care about rank anymore. Things that are losing stop caring fast.

Below, everything caught fire at once — not literally, though with Anwen involved I wouldn't rule it out. The acolytes had stopped swaying and started singing *with* her, the buried rites surfacing in them like drowned things finally allowed up for air. Pip came over the vault wall with a rope, a grin, and what looked like Sereth's personal seal-ring on her thumb, shouting something about "the big cage, get the bird, GO." And in the silver perch at the foot of the throne, in a cage of bone-white bars, the crow pressed itself against the lattice and looked at me with one eye that was suddenly, terribly bright.

*Now,* it said, in Vael's cadence, in the voice I'd prayed into silence for fifteen years. *Oscar. Let go of the crown. Set it on the throne and step back, and whatever happens next — don't catch me.*

"Whatever happens next" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, I remember thinking. Then Sereth hit the dais again and the whole structure lurched, and Lise's freed soul-thread circled my head once — once, warm as a hand on my cheek, familiar as a voice through a bedroom wall — and arrowed down toward the cage, toward the crow, toward the fragment of god that had spent twenty years buying souls one coin at a time.

Toward the last coin it had ever spent.

Me.

I set the crown on the throne. I stepped back. And the mountain, deep under my boots, drew its first breath in a hundred years.

Sereth screamed. The cage burst open like a seedpod. And the crow rose into the air between us, unfolding, unfolding, *unfolding*, wings becoming weather, feathers becoming snowfall, a small black bird becoming the shape of everything the mountain had ever been before we taught ourselves to sell each other —

— and it looked at me, and it said, in a voice like the whole pass at dawn:

*"Hold on to her. This part is loud."*
