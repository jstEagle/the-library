## 18. The God Who Woke

The crow came out of its cage like a held breath finally let go.

I'd seen it do small miracles before — drive off Tallymen in a ravine, burn a hole through a warden's stone fist, gutter and dim and refuse to die. But this was different. This was the difference between a candle and the thing candles are named after.

It rose from Sereth's iron-bound prison on the reckoning dais, and the cage didn't open so much as *stop mattering*. The bars were there one moment and the next they were simply an idea someone had once had about metal, and the crow passed through them the way morning passes through a window.

Then it began to sing.

Not caw. Not the dry, sardonic croak I'd lived with for months, the voice that sounded like Vael's cadence poured through a funnel of feathers. This was singing the way avalanches are weather. The sound went through my chest, through my teeth, through the stone floor of the Hollow Court itself, and every soul-lantern in that cathedral-cavern flickered in answer — thousands of them, hanging from the vaulted dark like a harvest of pale fruit, each one a person, each one flickering like they remembered something.

"Oh," said Pip, beside me, her lockpicks still buried in the warden's knee-joint where she'd jammed them during our breakout. "Oh, that's *him* him."

"That's the mountain," Anwen said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard since before the Tollgate took her memories — certainty. Borrowed or recovered, it was certainty.

The crow unfolded.

I don't have better words than that, though I'm a novelist by trade now if not by training, and I've spent twenty years finding words for things. It *unfolded*. Wings that had been the size of my two hands became wings that were the size of the cavern's arches, then wings that were the arches, black feathers shading into granite at the tips, into snowline, into the grey light of a dawn happening somewhere far above us, because the crow was becoming the mountain and the mountain was becoming awake.

Vael stood in the Court.

Not stood — *was*, the way a mountain is, the way winter is. Antlers like the throne's crown but alive, branching, budding with green despite the deep dark. Eyes like thaw-water. And threaded all through it, faint as veins under skin, a voice — the voice it had sold, coin by coin, to buy back souls like Lise's, keeping only enough of itself to hide in a crow's body and wait.

Twenty years of unanswered prayers. Twenty years of me talking into silence and calling it faithlessness. And the whole time, the silence had been *spent*. On her.

I knelt. I want to say I chose to kneel, but honestly my knees made the decision without consulting me.

"Get up, Oscar Joubert," said the god, and its voice was everywhere and also, somehow, exactly the crow's voice, dry and fond and a little tired. "You'll want your feet. This next part is loud."

* * *

Sereth did not run. I'll give her that much.

The chief broker of the Hollow Court stood on her dais beneath the antlered throne — the throne I'd climbed minutes ago, the crown I'd used to crack Lise's lantern open and refused to keep — and she watched her god wake up with the expression of a woman reviewing a ledger and finding an entry she couldn't explain.

"This is theft," she said.

The word landed in the cavern and rolled around like a dropped coin. Around us, the Court was coming apart at the seams. Acolytes fled down side tunnels. Brokers tore off their masks and ran, which told you everything about what masks are for. The great ledgers — the ones chained to their lecterns, the ones recording a century of bargains — were beginning to smoke.

"You wrote theft into law," Vael said, "and called it commerce. You wrote my flock into columns and called it interest." The mountain-god's head turned, slow as a season. "I kept accounts too, Lady Sereth. Mine are older than yours."

"It was all lawful," she said, and her voice cracked on *lawful*, just slightly, the way ice cracks before it goes. "Every bargain witnessed. Every contract sealed. The centennial reckoning locks them forever — that is the covenant, that is the *order*—"

"The order ends tonight."

What happened next, I watched from the floor with Lise's lantern — empty now, her soul gone up into the light-streaming chaos above us — clutched against my chest like the world's most precious empty bottle.

Vael breathed in.

That's all. It breathed in, the way you or I might draw breath before speaking. But the Court's ledgers felt it. Every chained book snapped its chains. Pages lifted, thousands upon thousands of pages, a blizzard of contracts rising toward the antlered dark, and as they rose the ink left them — I saw it happen, saw the words peel off the vellum like smoke off a snuffed candle, curling upward, unraveling.

And with every word that unwrote itself, a lantern somewhere in that vast ceiling went out — not dark, but *open*, its pale light spilling free instead of trapped, streaming upward through stone that had stopped being an obstacle and started being a suggestion.

Souls going home. Hundreds of them. Then thousands.

A man near me — one of the masked brokers who hadn't run fast enough, frozen in place — suddenly clutched his chest and wept, because whatever he'd purchased with someone else's soul was leaving him, draining out through his hands, and what was left underneath looked small and old and terribly human.

"Wait," Sereth said. She had her own ledger now, pulled from the dais, hugging it like a child hugs a mother in a burning house. "Wait — the contracts bind both ways. If you unmake theirs, you unmake mine, and mine is—" she turned pages with shaking fingers, hunting, "*mine is the oldest one here—*"

"I know," said Vael. "I've read it."

She looked up. For the first time since I'd met her, silk gloves and all, Lady Sereth looked afraid.

"What did you sell?" she whispered.

"Nothing," said the god, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "You were never bought, Sereth. You *volunteered*. That's the tragedy of you. A hundred years ago you walked into this dark on your own feet and asked to be its keeper, and no one made you, and no one ever made you leave."

Her ledger caught fire from the inside. I watched her try to hold onto it anyway. I watched the fire take the pages, then the binding, then climb her sleeves like ivy, and she didn't scream — she argued, right up until the end, reciting clauses at a god while she came apart into ash and light, and the last thing she said, very quietly, was:

"The books have to balance."

"They do now," said Vael.

And the Hollow Court exhaled a hundred years of held breath, and the roof of the world opened.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next hour, and I confess my memory of it is mostly light.

The soul-lights streamed upward through the waking stone, and the mountain guided them. I could feel it doing the work — feel the whole mass of Thornfell shifting around us like a great animal turning over in sleep, old pilgrim tunnels reopening after centuries, forgotten roads remembering themselves, the god reading its own bones like a map and sending each light down the passage that led to its life.

Some of those lives were long over. Some lights hesitated at forks in the dark, and Vael would bend close — the whole mountain bending, snowfields groaning — and speak to them in that voice like thaw-water, and whatever it said, they'd go on. I choose to believe it was kind. Everything else about that night was kind, in the way that a flood can be kind if it carries you instead of drowning you.

Pip stood in the middle of the dais with her arms crossed, watching ten thousand years of underworld economy dissolve, and said, "So does this mean my indenture contract counts as unmade? Asking for me."

"Ask it yourself," Anwen said.

"I'm not shouting at a mountain."

"You shouted at a Tallyman twice your size last week."

"He wasn't a *god*, he was just large and religious about arithmetic—"

They were bickering. We were standing in the collapse of the cruelest institution on the continent, and they were bickering, and I loved them both so much it physically hurt, the way cold water hurts, clean and shocking.

Anwen caught me looking. Her memoryless eyes — the Tollgate had taken her order, her rites, her past — had been filling back up all night, fragments returning with every rite she performed, and now she looked at me with the expression of a woman remembering why she'd become a priestess in the first place.

"Go," she said softly. "She's waiting for you. Gods can mind the traffic."

* * *

Lise rematerialized forty feet above the dais, in the middle of the ascending river of lights, and fell.

I caught her. Of course I caught her; I'd been catching her for twenty years in every dream I'd had since the famine winter, and my arms knew the job even if the weight was wrong — wrong because she was heavy now, because she was grown, because the child I'd failed had become a woman I'd never met.

We went down together onto the stone, and I held her, and she was solid, she was warm, she was *breathing*, and for a long moment neither of us said anything because there are no words for that and we're both, apparently, people who know when to shut up.

Then she pulled back and looked at my face, and I braced myself, because I knew what she'd find there. Guilt has a look. I'd been wearing it so long it had probably grown into the bone.

"You fed me," she said.

"Lise—"

"The famine winter. You were nine. They gave you the bread because you were smaller, because Mother thought—" her voice caught, steadied. "You snuck half of it back to me every night. Under the blanket. You thought I didn't know."

I hadn't known she'd known. Twenty years I'd carried the memory of eating while she starved, and the truth was standing in front of me with dirt on her knees and her dead brother's bread crumbs in her history, and it had known the whole time.

"You were a child," she said. "You were a *child*, Oscar, and you gave away half your food, and when they came for souls it was *my* name I gave, not yours, because I'd already decided you were the one who got to live. So don't you dare kneel there wearing that face like it's penance. It's not penance. It's just pride wearing sackcloth."

"I prayed to Vael for you," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing. "For twenty years. Every night. And it never answered, and I thought—"

"I know what you thought." She touched my face, and her hand was real, calloused at the fingertips in a way no ghost's hand should be, in a way that meant she'd *lived* somewhere, done *something*, all these years inside the lantern. "It answered the first night. It just answered with everything it had left."

Above us, the mountain rumbled — and I swear on whatever's left to swear on, it sounded embarrassed.

"Yes, thank you," I called up, wet-eyed and wry, because some habits survive even catharsis. "Very moving. Everyone's very impressed."

"She's always been dramatic," Lise said.

"*Always,*" said the god, from everywhere.

* * *

By the time the last lights had gone home, the Hollow Court was just a cave again. A big one, beautiful in its way, vaulted and dark and smelling of stone and old incense, but a cave. The throne stood empty. The crown of antlers sat on the dais where I'd left it, and it looked smaller now, and colder, and I kept my distance from it the way you keep distance from a well you once nearly fell into.

Vael gathered itself down. The wings folded. The granite softened into feather. And then there was a crow again, sitting on the empty throne, preening, looking extremely pleased with itself.

"A word of thanks would not go amiss," it said.

"You had my prayers for twenty years."

"And dreary they were, too. 'Please watch over Lise. Please forgive me. Please, please, please.' Do you know how many gods get prayers like that? You could have asked for something interesting. Weather. A horse."

"You went *silent*."

"I went *broke*," the crow said, and for a moment the fondness in it was edged with something older and sadder. "There's a difference, Oscar. Silence is a choice. Poverty is a price. I paid mine in installments, one soul at a time, and yours was the last coin in the purse." It hopped down the throne's arm. "You'd have done the same."

"I'm not a god."

"No. You're something rarer. You're the sort of person a god spends itself on." It cocked its head at me, one bright eye. "Which brings us to the awkward part."

"There's an awkward part."

"There's always an awkward part. I'm restored. Mostly. The mountain is awake, the ledgers are ash, and I have power again — not all of it, the reckoning burned some of what I'd hidden in the crow, but enough. Enough to offer you a share. Your mercenary gifts back. Your magic. More than you ever had. A place at my side, shepherd of the passes. The faithful would come. The faithful are *already* coming — I can hear them on the roads, walking toward the mountain like it's spring."

I looked at Lise. She was watching me with an expression I was going to spend the rest of my life learning to read, and the first thing I read in it was: *this is your choice, and I will love you either way.*

Twenty years ago I'd have said yes before the offer finished. Twenty years ago I'd have crawled over broken glass for a scrap of Vael's attention, for the warmth of being chosen, for anything that would fill the hole where my sister used to be.

But the hole wasn't empty anymore. She was standing in it. She was *standing in it*, alive, arguing with a crow.

"No," I said.

The cavern went quiet. Even Pip and Anwen, pretending not to listen by the tunnel mouth, went quiet.

"I want to walk ordinary roads," I said. "With her. While she learns the world she missed — which is apparently most of it, because the last thing she remembers is a famine and the first thing she's going to learn is what an orange is. I want to be mortal. I want to be tired at the end of the day for ordinary reasons. I spent twenty years invisible, and I'm done with it — but I'm also done with being *more* than human. Both of those were ways of not being a person." I shrugged, and it came out steadier than I felt. "I'd like to try being a person."

Vael regarded me for a long moment. Crows are good at regarding.

"Very well," it said at last. "No power, then. No gifts. No place at my side." It hopped closer, and its voice gentled into something I recognized — the voice from my childhood prayers, the one I'd thought I'd imagined. "Only this: my silence, whenever you need it. Not absence. Company. The quiet that sits beside you on a cold road and doesn't ask anything of you. You've earned that much, Oscar Joubert. You earned it the night you were nine years old and split a piece of bread."

I didn't cry. Much.

* * *

We camped that night in the mouth of the pilgrim tunnel, because none of us could face climbing yet, and because the mountain had opened a ledge overlooking the deep valley and the view was the kind of view you don't walk away from quickly.

Below us, in the dark, lights were moving. Freed souls, walking home along roads that had remembered themselves. Some alone. Some in twos and threes, finding each other the way Lise and I had found each other. Far off toward Cinderhollow, a point of light that might have been a lantern or might have been a window glowed steady against the foothills.

"So," Pip said, around a mouthful of hardtack. "The Court's gone. The ledgers are ash. Which means every debt in the underworld is officially unenforceable." She grinned, and it was the sharpest, brightest thing on that mountainside. "Including the contracts on about forty indentured kids I can name. Somebody's going to have to go tell them."

"Somebody with excellent lockwork and a grudge," Anwen said.

"Somebody with a *church* to build," Pip shot back. "Don't think I didn't see you eyeing that empty cathedral-cave. You've got that look. The 'I'm going to found an order' look."

"I have recovered my memories," Anwen said with dignity, "and discovered that my order served the Court for a century, so yes. Someone owes the mountain an honest church. I intend to be the someone."

They went on planning — Pip listing names, Anwen listing reforms, the two of them building a future out loud the way other people build fires — and I leaned back against warm stone and listened, and Lise leaned against me, and the crow settled on my shoulder, small again, ordinary again, by choice.

Above us, dawn was coming. I could feel it in the rock, a slow gathering brightness, the mountain itself turning its face east.

"Oscar," Lise said quietly, when the others' voices had softened into planning-murmur. "The road out of Thornfell. The smuggler roads. Is it far?"

"Not far. Slow, maybe. There's a lot of world you haven't seen."

"Good," she said, and closed her eyes, and smiled. "I've waited twenty years in a bottle. I'd like to take the long way."

High above, the first light of morning struck the highest peak of Thornfell — struck something antlered and ancient and awake that crowned it — and the mountain glowed like a prayer finally, finally answered.
