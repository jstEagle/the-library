## 19. Ledgers Unwritten

I had spent thirty years believing the church was a house. Stone walls, a bell, a roof to keep the rain off the sacraments. It took the unmaking of an entire underground economy of souls for me to understand that a church is whatever room you stand in when you tell the truth about what things cost.

The Hollow Court, in its death throes, had been very honest indeed.

The ledgers burned for three days. Not paper — the Court's books were written in something older than paper, in contracts knotted into the stone itself, and Vael's waking pulled those knots loose one by one like a mother undoing a child's braids after a long winter. Each contract that came undone made a sound. I don't know how else to describe it. A sound like a held breath released after twenty years, fifty years, in Pip's case — no, not Pip, she was never sold, only indentured, which is what the respectable world calls selling when it wants to keep its hands clean — a sound like that, filling the caverns until I thought the mountain would crack from all the exhaling at once.

I stood in the great hall on the second day and watched souls go home.

They came out of their lanterns as light first, then as people, flickering into solidity the way frost forms on a window — slowly, then all at once, then slowly again. Most of them were confused. Some were frightened. A woman who had been sold in the reign of the last broker's grandmother looked down at her own hands and wept because they still had her wedding ring on them, and the ring was rusted through, and everyone she'd ever loved was dead, and the mountain had kept her ring safe for sixty years while it let everything else rot.

"Where do they go?" I asked the crow.

Oscar wasn't there. He was below, in the vault, sitting beside his sister the way you sit beside a fire you're afraid will go out. So it was the crow who answered, perched on the antlered throne that nobody sat on anymore because nobody dared, and it said, in Vael's cadence, in Vael's terrible gentle patience:

*Home. Whatever home remains.*

"And if home doesn't remain?"

*Then they walk until it does.* The crow turned its head, one black eye catching the light of ten thousand departing lantern-souls. *You of all people should know the road doesn't require a destination. Only walking.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and the crow had the grace to pretend it hadn't heard.

Here is what nobody tells you about winning: afterward, there is so much laundry.

* * *

Anwen of Cinderhollow — defrocked, disgraced, formerly Sister Anwen of the Order of the Quiet Flame, currently a woman with holes in her memory where her order used to be — stood in the ruins of the Hollow Court's counting floor and did the one thing she knew how to do. She organized.

It was Pip who named the problem. Pip Marrow, seventeen years old, newly owner of a criminal empire she hadn't asked for, came up from the lower vaults on the third morning with ink on her fingers and an expression I was beginning to recognize as her thinking face.

"So here's the thing," she said, dropping a stack of recovered contract-fragments on a broken altar we'd repurposed as a table. "The Court kept records. Obviously. Brokers love records more than they love money, and they loved money like drowning men love air. And every record has a name, a village, a date of sale, and usually a buyer."

"Usually?"

"Some sales were to other brokers. Some were to rich bastards who wanted a dead grandmother's luck bound into a ring. But most—" she tapped the stack "—most are just names. Sold for grain. Sold for debt. Sold for a winter's worth of coal." Her jaw did something complicated. "There are four hundred and six names in this pile alone, and this is one pile of nine piles."

Four hundred and six. I thought of my congregation in Cinderhollow, all forty of them, and how proud I'd been when attendance swelled to fifty-one the winter of the fever. Four hundred and six souls in one pile.

"What do you want to do?" I asked.

"I want to find them homes." She said it fast, defensive, the way she said everything that mattered. "Not — I mean, their villages are gone, mostly, or worse, their villages are *there*, full of people who signed the papers. You can't send a woman back to the brother who sold her. Believe me. I've read the contracts." Her voice cracked slightly on *believe me*. "But they need somewhere. Someone. They can't just walk out of a mountain after sixty years with nothing."

"No," I agreed. "They need a church."

Pip looked at me like I'd started speaking Tallyman. "There's no church down here."

"There is now."

I wish I could tell you I deliberated. That I weighed my options, prayed for guidance, consulted the wisdom of my vanished order. The truth is dumber and truer than that: I looked at the four hundred and six names, and I looked at the great cathedral-cavern where brokers had worn silk gloves over horror, and I thought, *this building has excellent acoustics and no one is using it,* and that was that. Thirty years of theology, and God finally called me through real estate.

Vael, I suspect, appreciated the pragmatism. Gods who trade their voices for other people's souls tend not to stand on ceremony.

* * *

The work was not glamorous. Let me be clear about that, because somewhere in the years ahead someone will write songs about the fall of the Hollow Court, and the songs will have trumpets in them, and none of the trumpets will be about scrubbing soot off a former slave-market floor while a soul-woman from the famine winter asks you for the fourth time whether her husband survived the fever, and you have to say *I don't know, but we'll find out,* and she nods, and asks again an hour later, because sixty years in a lantern does something to a mind that no theology I ever studied prepared me for.

We found out about her husband, eventually. He'd died two winters after her sale, in his sleep, kind fate. She took the news strangely — relief first, then fury that she'd been robbed of even her grief, then a long silence, then: "He always said the mountain was listening. Everyone laughed at him."

"The mountain was listening," I told her. "It just couldn't answer. Its voice was being spent elsewhere."

She looked at me for a long moment. "You believe that."

"With my whole ridiculous life," I said.

Her name was Maret. She became my first parishioner, though she preferred the term *first critic*. She attended every service I held — and yes, I held services, within a week, because the freed souls kept drifting toward me when they were frightened, the way moths decide a candle is home — and she corrected my liturgy mercilessly, since her childhood church predated mine by decades and apparently I'd been doing the opening blessing wrong my entire career.

She was right. I checked against the fragments of memory the warden's toll had left me. There's a particular humiliation in learning your whole tradition from a woman who remembers it better than you do because your order erased your memories as punishment for telling the truth. I recommend it, actually. Humility is easier when it's administered regularly by a grandmother with sixty years of grievances and perfect recall of the hymnal.

* * *

Oscar came up from the vault on the fifth day, and I knew before he spoke that Lise was gone.

Not dead — nothing so clean. Gone the way a bird leaves a branch. She'd been rematerializing slowly, more ghost than girl, more girl than ghost, and each day she was a little more solid, a little more *present*, asking questions in a voice that hadn't aged past nineteen. Where was the village? What year was it? Who were these people, the thief-girl with the quick hands and the priestess with the tired eyes and the crow that watched her like she was the last page of a book?

On the fifth day she woke solid. Fully solid. Nineteen years old in a world twenty years older than the one she'd left, her brother gray-streaked and mortal, her god a bird on a throne of antlers.

"She asked me what happened to her," Oscar told me. We were in what I'd begun calling the vestry, a side chamber that still had scorch marks shaped like hands on the walls, from Sereth's last workings. "Not — she knows. She remembers everything, the lantern, the waiting, all of it. She asked me what happened to *her*, meaning — what am I supposed to do with a life that stopped and started again? And I didn't have an answer." He rubbed his face. He looked older than his years now, and younger too, in some way I couldn't name. Lighter, maybe. Mortality had done him the favor of making his exhaustion honest. "So I told her the truth. Nobody knows what to do with a life. We're going to figure out hers together, on the road, slow."

"You're leaving."

"We're leaving." He almost smiled. "Lise wants to see the sun properly. Not through stone. And I—" he glanced toward the throne room, where the crow had taken up residence and where pilgrims, word already spreading, had begun to arrive at the mountain's foot, drawn by dreams of antlers and dawn, "—I've spent enough time under mountains."

"What will you do up there?"

"Honestly? Guide caravans. It's the only work I'm good at." He paused. "But visible, this time. Known. Vael says—" and here he did that thing he did whenever he quoted the crow, this small helpless huff of disbelief that the silence of his whole adult life had ended in a talking bird with opinions, "—Vael says a shepherd who hides from the flock isn't humble, he's just useless."

"That sounds like Vael."

"It's insufferable is what it is." But he said it warmly. He said everything warmly now, this man I'd met as a locked door. "What about you? Pip says you're staying. Pip says it like she's quoting scripture, actually. 'The priestess is staying.' She seems surprised anyone stays anywhere."

"Are you surprised?"

"A month ago? Yes." He considered me with those mercenary's eyes that missed less than he pretended. "Now I think it's the only thing you could do. You spent your whole life preaching that bargains with the Court were theft. Turns out you were right, and being right cost you everything, and instead of getting bitter about it you're going to stay in the theft's basement and hand back what was stolen, one name at a time." He shrugged. "That's not surprising. That's just you, finally in the right building."

I confess I turned away to fuss with the candles so he wouldn't see what that did to me. A defrocked sister should have a better defense against kindness. We're trained for persecution, not for being seen.

"When do you go?" I managed.

"Tomorrow. Dawn. Lise wants to see it from outside." He hesitated at the door, and for a moment I saw the boy from the famine village, the one who'd eaten while his sister starved, the guilt he carried like a second skeleton. "Anwen. The service you held yesterday. For the ones whose villages are gone, the ones with nowhere."

"Yes?"

"Lise was in the back. She stayed the whole time." His voice went rough. "She said it was the first thing in twenty years that felt like it was *for* her. So. Whatever you think you lost when they took your order from you." He knocked twice on the doorframe, a smuggler's farewell. "Don't you dare go looking for it."

* * *

Pip left three days later, and she left like Pip did everything: at speed, with contingencies, and pretending it meant less than it did.

She'd spent those days in the Court's archives with the fervor of a woman looting a library, cross-referencing indenture records, debt-bonds, the criminal ledgers of half the underworld's lords. Because here is what the fall of the Hollow Court gave her, beyond freedom: leverage. Every crime lord who'd ever bought a soul-lantern as an investment, every guildmaster whose fortune was built on contracts the Court had underwritten — their paper was ash now, or worse, evidence. And Pip Marrow, who'd been sold at twelve for the price of her siblings' bread, owned the only complete copies.

"I'm not going to hurt them," she explained to me over our last supper, a genuinely alarming stew Maret had contributed to. "Mostly. Mostly not going to hurt them. I'm going to *audit* them. Their debts are void. Their collateral is void. And every kid they've got indentured — every single one — I've got the paperwork to walk them out the front door." She grinned, and it was a fierce, sharp, wonderful thing. "Turns out the best weapon against the underworld is the underworld's own filing system. Who knew."

"You did," I said. "You always knew. That's why you're terrifying."

"Flatterer." But she glowed. Then she looked down at her bowl, and the glow guttered the way it did when she brushed against sincerity. "The kids. Some of them won't have anywhere either. Same problem as yours. Sold kids, freed kids, no homes." She stabbed a turnip with unnecessary violence. "I'm going to open houses. In Cinderhollow first, because your old town owes the world some repairs, and then wherever they're needed. Places where nobody asks a kid to sign anything, ever, for any reason. I'll need—"

"Money," I said. "Staff. Legal cover. Blessings."

"Blessings?" She looked suspicious.

"You'll want them eventually. Not for the gods' sake. For the kids'. Children like ritual, Pip. They like knowing someone stands at a door each night and says *you are safe, you are counted, you are known.* It doesn't matter if the words are holy. It matters that they're said the same way every time." I leaned back. "My order taught me that much before they took it away. It's the one piece I got to keep."

Pip was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Cinderhollow. The old tannery on the river road. Big, ugly, nobody wants it. I can get it cheap because I know exactly how to make the owner desperate." She pointed her spoon at me. "You'll come dedicate it. When it's ready. That's not a request, priestess. First house gets a proper blessing or it doesn't count."

"I'll come," I promised. "Bring the children. All of them. My mountain has room."

"My mountain," she repeated, and snorted, and then, quietly, testing the weight of it: "Your mountain. Huh." She stood, slung her pack — already packed, of course, she'd probably packed the night the Court fell — and hesitated at the door in a way that was so unlike her that I nearly spoke. "Hey. Anwen. If I'd stayed sold — if you guys hadn't—"

"Pip."

"Yeah."

"Go free some children. That's the best thank-you there is. Anything you say will ruin it."

She laughed — really laughed, once, bright as a struck flint — and was gone down the tunnel before the echo faded.

* * *

They were all gone within the week. Oscar and Lise at dawn, walking east into light that Lise stopped, halfway across the valley, to stand in with her face tipped up like a plant. Pip south with a satchel of voided contracts and a list of names. Even the crow went quiet for days at a stretch, attending to the business of being a god again, whatever that entailed — I suspect, from the tremors in the deep galleries and the sudden abundance of clean water springing from old stone, that it involved a great deal of repair work, and that mountains, like churches, are mostly maintenance.

And I stayed.

I want to report that it was easy, that purpose filled me like light fills a lantern, that I never once lay awake in the vestry missing my order, my bells, my small warm church in Cinderhollow where I'd preached comfortable lies for thirty years. Some nights were like that. Other nights the holes in my memory ached like teeth, and I mourned people whose names I couldn't recall for reasons I couldn't remember, and Maret found me at the door at midnight and didn't ask questions, just stood beside me looking at the dark until the dark got bored and left.

But the congregation grew. That's the truth I'll set against the hard nights. Four hundred and six names became three hundred and ninety found, became families reconnected across impossible distances, became letters coming up the pilgrim road from a world that was learning, slowly, clumsily, what had been done beneath Thornfell and who had paid for it. Freed souls wrote to us. Some thanked us. Some cursed us — one man, sold willingly, furious at losing the strange cold peace of the lantern; I keep his letter pinned above the altar as a reminder that rescue is not always experienced as rescue, and grace does not wait for permission.

We rebuilt the great hall without the masks. We kept the acoustics. Maret insisted on restoring the original hymns, and I insisted on new verses, and we fight about it weekly, joyfully, in front of everyone, because a church where the liturgy is argued over openly is a church where the truth has somewhere to sit down.

Yesterday a courier came up the mountain road — a living courier, flesh and breath and snow in her boots, which still startles me sometimes — carrying letters. One from Pip, ink-smudged, triumphant: the tannery was theirs, eleven children installed, the owner had signed away the deed in exchange for her discretion regarding certain ledger discrepancies, and *tell the priestess the first blessing is scheduled for the equinox, bring the good incense, and tell the crow if it sends dreams to the kids again I'm charging it rent.* One from Oscar, shorter, warmer, Lise's handwriting crowded into the margins adding corrections and commentary, describing a pass, a caravan, a tavern where the innkeeper had recognized him by name and he hadn't minded.

And beneath both, a third letter, unsigned, in handwriting that made my hands shake:

*Sister Anwen. The Order of the Quiet Flame has heard what you built under the mountain. They are sending representatives. They wish to discuss your reinstatement.*

I sat with that a long while, in my vestry, in my stolen cathedral, with my mismatched congregation singing Maret's hymns badly in the hall above.

Reinstatement. As if I'd ever left anything. As if the church were a house with a door they could open or shut.

I got out fresh paper, and I began my reply, and I found I was smiling.

*To the Order of the Quiet Flame: You are welcome here, under the same terms as everyone else. The mountain keeps no ledgers anymore, and neither do I. Come and see what a church costs when no one is buying.*

Below the mountain, the world was mending. Above it, dawn struck the crown of Thornfell every morning like an answered prayer, and the passes were filling with travelers who'd heard the roads were safe now, watched over by something antlered and kind.

Among them, I would learn soon enough, was a caravan guided by a man the mountain remembered — and the Order's representatives were traveling with it.
