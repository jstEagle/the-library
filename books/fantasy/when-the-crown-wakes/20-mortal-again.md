## 20. Mortal Again

The mountain was still singing.

I don't know how else to put it. The stone itself had a hum in it now, low and patient, like a hearth that would never go cold. You could feel it through your boots if you stood still long enough, and I had been standing still a lot lately, because for the first time in twenty years there was nothing chasing me and I hadn't yet learned what to do with that.

We'd been given rooms in the Court itself — or what had been the Court. Sereth's brokers were gone, unmade with their ledgers, and the great cathedral-cavern stood empty except for dust, drifting soul-light, and Anwen, who had already claimed the altar as hers and was arguing with masons about where the pews should go. Pip had commandeered the vault, on the grounds that she'd bled for it, which was fair. Lise slept in a chamber off the nave that still smelled faintly of candle smoke, sleeping the way only someone newly returned to a body sleeps — deeply, greedily, like a debt being repaid.

And me? I sat on the steps of a dead god's throne and waited to be told what came next.

That was the thing nobody warns you about miracles. They end. There's a moment when the light stops streaming and the screaming stops and the mountain finishes shaking, and then it's just you and the wreckage and the terrible ordinary question of breakfast.

"You're brooding," said the crow.

It landed on the throne's armrest — my armrest, technically, since I was leaning against it — and folded its wings with the smugness of a creature that had recently turned out to be an entire god.

"I'm resting," I said. "There's a difference."

"Brooding has a particular slouch. You're doing it." It cocked its head at me, one black eye catching the last of the lantern-light. "Twenty years of watching you, Joubert. I know your slouches."

"Most people would call that stalking."

"Most people weren't hiding inside a bird waiting for the right moment to save your life. Repeatedly. At considerable cost to my dignity."

I almost smiled. Almost. My face had gotten out of practice at it somewhere along the smuggler roads, and it kept coming out crooked. "You could have just told me. Any time in the last three weeks. 'Hello, Oscar, I'm actually Vael, also your sister's soul is in a jar.' Would have saved us all some bruises."

"And you'd have believed me?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. The crow made a sound that I swear was laughter.

"That," it said, "is why gods keep their own counsel. Also because the last time I spoke plainly to a mortal, he sold his voice to buy back a child, and look how well that went."

The hum in the stone shifted slightly when it said that — not a flinch exactly, but something near one. Even restored, even vast again, Vael apparently couldn't talk about that winter without the mountain noticing. I decided not to push. We were both of us creatures with old wounds we'd learned to walk around.

* * *

Lise woke at dusk, and I brought her bread and honey because it was the first thing I could think of that she'd never had. She ate it sitting cross-legged on the floor like a child, which she wasn't anymore — she was a grown woman with our mother's jaw and hands that kept turning over and over, examining themselves, astonished to exist.

"The sun," she said, between bites. "Tell me about the sun. I remember it from before, but it's like remembering someone else's dream."

"It's yellow," I said. "It's warm. It comes up too early and sets too soon, and half the time the clouds ruin it anyway."

She laughed, and the sound went through me like a blade going through butter — no resistance at all, straight to the bone. Twenty years I'd carried her death around like a stone in my chest, and it turned out she'd never died. She'd waited. In the dark, in a lantern, in a child's small forever, she'd waited, and the first thing she did with her freedom was laugh at my terrible description of weather.

"Oscar." She set down the bread. Her eyes were serious now, and older than they should have been; whatever else the Court had done, it hadn't spared her the years entirely. "The god. The crow. It bought me. With its voice, with everything it had. Why?"

Because of me, I didn't say. Because I prayed so hard and so selfishly that a god bankrupted itself answering. Because you starved while I ate, and I've spent two decades pretending I didn't remember, and apparently I remembered loud enough for heaven to hear.

"Because it's kind," I said instead. "Turns out that was the whole problem."

* * *

Vael came to me on the third night, when the others were asleep and the cavern was quiet except for the mountain's slow song.

It didn't come as the crow. It came as the crow-shaped absence of darkness — a silhouette cut out of the world, wings spread wide enough to shadow the whole nave, antlers branching up into the dark like winter trees. My knees tried to buckle. I let them, mostly. A man ought to show some respect when the divine drops by, even if the divine used to steal his jerky.

"Oscar Joubert." Its voice filled the stone the way water fills a cup. It was my sister's voice and my mother's voice and, underneath, something vast and patient that had watched this mountain since before there were names for it. "You are owed a debt."

"I'm really not—"

"You are." The antlers tilted. "I took your prayer and spent it badly. I let you believe yourself abandoned for twenty years. I let you believe *her* dead. These are wounds I gave, and wounds given by gods do not close on their own. So. Name your price."

And here's the thing. Here's the honest, shameful thing. For one heartbeat — maybe less — I wanted it. All of it. The power I'd felt flickering at the edge of me since the ravine, the strength that had let a crow drive off Tallymen and burn through wards, the voice that could command stone and storm. I thought about every contract I'd ever taken, every pass I'd crossed hungry and alone, every night I'd prayed into silence and gotten nothing back, and I thought: *never again.* Never again small. Never again invisible. Never again the man whose name nobody bothers to learn.

One heartbeat. Then I looked down the nave toward the room where my sister slept, breathing, real, learning the weight of her own hands, and the wanting curdled and drained away like snowmelt.

"No," I said.

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, water dripped in the dark.

"No?" said Vael.

"No. I don't want it back. I don't want any of it." I got to my feet, because this next part deserved standing. "You want to know what twenty years of being a mercenary taught me? Power's just a bigger bill to pay. Every gift comes with a tally, and I've spent my whole life watching people sign things they didn't read. This whole place—" I gestured at the empty Court, the scarred walls, the ghost-faint scorch marks where ledgers had burned. "This is what happens when somebody thinks power will fix things. It doesn't fix things. It just moves the bill to someone poorer."

"You would refuse restoration," Vael said slowly. "Strength. Purpose. A place at my side, as my hand in the world. Mortals have burned kingdoms for less."

"Mortals are idiots. Present company included — you sold your voice, remember."

The great dark shape went very still. For a moment I thought I'd finally managed to offend a god past all forgiveness, and I braced for lightning, or at least a strongly worded omen.

Then the mountain laughed.

Not the crow's dry chuckle. The whole mountain — the hum in the stone rising and breaking, rolling through the cavern like spring flood, and I realized with a start that Vael was laughing, properly laughing, for the first time in who knew how long, and that I had done it, by insulting it, which figured. That had always been our relationship.

"Oh, shepherd's bones," it said, wiping at nothing, gods presumably having eyes to wipe. "Twenty years I worried what I'd find when I woke. A man hollowed out. A man drunk on grief. And instead I find a smuggler who insults deities in their own cathedral." The vastness gathered itself down, down, until it was the crow again, perched on the throne's arm, regarding me with one bright eye. "Very well, Oscar Joubert. No restoration. No crown. But the debt stands, and debts to gods are not forgiven merely because the debtor is stubborn. I will give you a gift whether you want it or not. It is the only thing I have left that's truly mine to give."

"Which is?"

"Listen."

And I listened. To the cavern, to the drip of water, to the mountain's song fading toward morning. And underneath it all, faint as a pulse, I heard — nothing. The same nothing I'd prayed into for twenty years. The same silent, empty sky.

My chest tightened the way it always did. Old habit.

"Yes," Vael said softly. "That. Only now, when you reach for me and find silence, you will know it isn't absence. It's me, choosing to be quiet beside you. There is a difference, little guide. Absence leaves you alone. Company can be silent and still be company. Walk your ordinary roads. When the silence feels heavy, remember: I am there, keeping it, the way a friend keeps quiet watch. That is my gift. Silence that holds you instead of abandoning you."

I stood there on the steps of a dead court, and I'll be damned if my eyes didn't sting. Twenty years of praying into a hole in the world, and the cure wasn't an answer. It was a reinterpretation. Typical god. Typical mercenary, too, honestly — we'd both made a living reframing bad terrain as passable.

"That's a terrible gift," I said, voice rough.

"It's the best I have."

"...Thank you."

"Don't mention it." The crow hopped closer and pecked gently, once, at my knuckle — the closest thing to an embrace either of us knew how to manage. "Literally. Don't. If word gets out that Vael gives sentimental gifts, every pilgrim from here to Cinderhollow will be asking for them, and I have a church to run now. Your priestess is very demanding."

"She's not my—"

"She prays to me using your name as an example. 'Make him less stubborn, like your servant Oscar.' You're canonized, Joubert. Congratulations."

* * *

We left on a grey morning, the kind I'd always liked best for travel — no glare, no shadows, just the world laid out plain.

Anwen walked us as far as the tunnel mouth, robed now in undyed wool, looking younger and older at once. She'd found more of her memories in the wreckage of the church archives, including, she admitted, the parts she'd rather not have. The order that defrocked her had served the Court willingly once. She meant to build something that never would.

"Somebody has to keep the mountain honest," she said, gripping my forearm hard. "And somebody has to keep *you* honest, and since you won't stay, I'll settle for letters."

"You hate writing letters."

"I hate lots of things. I do them anyway. It's called faith." She kissed my cheek like a sister, which was strange and good, and then she looked past me at Lise and her whole face changed — softened into something fierce and tender. "Walk slowly," she told her. "The world's been waiting. Let it introduce itself properly."

Pip's goodbye was shorter. She punched my arm hard enough to bruise, declared that she never wanted to see my crooked card-playing face again, and then hugged me hard enough to crack a rib, which I counted as fair. "I'm freeing the indentured kids," she said, chin up, daring me to make it sentimental. "Somebody's got to teach them which locks are worth picking. Write if you learn anything useful. Don't write if it's just feelings."

Then it was Lise and me and the crow, walking out of Thornfell into a morning that smelled like pine and thaw, and I won't pretend my eyes were entirely dry, but nobody saw, so it doesn't count.

* * *

The road down was long, and Lise asked questions the whole way.

What is bread made of. Why do towns have walls if everyone hates them. What is money, precisely, and why does everyone lie about having it. Why do you walk on the outside of the path, Oscar. Why do you check the treeline every eleven steps, Oscar. Why does the crow keep stealing from my pack, Oscar.

"He's a god," I said.

"He's a nuisance with feathers."

"They're not mutually exclusive," the crow said from her shoulder, where it had relocated itself within an hour of leaving the mountain, traitorously. "Ask your brother. He's an expert on nuisances."

Lise laughed — she laughed easily now, hoarding laughter like a woman who'd been rationed joy for twenty years — and I walked on the outside of the path and checked the treeline and let the questions come, every one of them, glad of each. Every question was proof she was here. Every answer was a brick in the road back to the world she'd missed.

At the first waystation, the keeper squinted at us and said, "Joubert, isn't it? The guide? Heard you cracked the Hollow Court open." He said my name. Out loud. Like it was a name worth saying. I waited for the old itch to hide, the old instinct to shrink, and it didn't come. I just nodded and paid for three beds and soup, and it was fine. It was more than fine.

That night, Lise fell asleep mid-sentence, mid-question — something about whether rivers ever got tired — and I sat by the window with cheap wine and listened to the silence.

It was there, of course. It was always going to be there. But I reached into it, the way I'd reached a thousand times on a thousand cold passes, and this time the silence reached back. Not words. Not answers. Just presence, steady as a banked fire, keeping the quiet with me instead of leaving me alone in it.

"Terrible gift," I murmured.

The crow, roosting on the bedpost, opened one eye. "Heard that."

"Meant you to."

It ruffled its feathers and settled, and outside, far above, dawn was still hours away, but I could feel the mountain behind us holding its new warmth, and ahead of us the long road unwinding through country I suddenly, absurdly, wanted to see — all of it, every mile, at walking pace, with my sister asking questions the whole way.

We had a world to catch up on. Neither of us was in any hurry.

But at the edge of the yard, just visible in the starlight, a rider stood beside a horse that stamped and steamed, watching the waystation with the stillness of someone who had ridden a long way and wasn't sure of his welcome — and Lise, asleep, murmured a name I hadn't heard in twenty years.
