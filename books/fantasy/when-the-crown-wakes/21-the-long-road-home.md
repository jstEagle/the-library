## 21. The Long Road Home

The first thing you should know about being dead for twenty years is that nobody warns you about the walking.

I had assumed — insofar as I assumed anything, insofar as I *was* anything, a warm little light in a cold little lantern — that resurrection would come with fanfare. Trumpets, maybe. A chorus of the redeemed. Instead I got my brother's coat, which smells like snow and horse and a decade of other people's secrets, and a road that goes up and up and then, mercifully, down.

The second thing you should know is that your legs forget everything.

"Left foot," Oscar said, for the hundredth time that morning, catching my elbow before I could introduce my face to a boulder. "Then the right one. It's not complicated, Lise. Children manage it."

"I was a child when I last managed it," I said, "and I'd like to see *you* walk after two decades of being a lamp."

He had the grace to look abashed, which was new. The Oscar I remembered from before — the thin boy who got the bread because I told him to take it, who carried that obedience like a stone in his chest ever after — had been a boy who flinched from his own shadow. This one was a man who'd argued with a god and a broker-queen and a mountain, and won, mostly, and now he caught my elbow on mountain roads and looked embarrassed about caring.

The crow rode his shoulder through all of it, riding the sway of his stride like a sailor on a deck it had personally christened. It had been vast once — I'd seen it, that night under Thornfell, when it unfolded out of its small black body into something with wings like weather and a voice that unmade ledgers. Now it was small again, and it had *chosen* to be small, which the others told me was significant and which I privately thought was the smartest thing any god had ever done. Big gods get worshipped. Small gods get to sit on a warm shoulder and eat crumbs.

"You're staring at me," the crow said. It had Vael's cadence still, that deep-bell resonance in a body the size of a loaf. "People stare. I'm told it's flattering. It isn't."

"I'm deciding if you're my god or my brother's pet."

"Both. Neither. I'm the part of Vael that liked walking." It preened once, decisively. "The rest of me is busy being a mountain. Mountains are terrible conversationalists. All *geology* this and *tectonic patience* that."

I laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate that hadn't swung in twenty years, but it came out, and Oscar looked at me when it did — quickly, sideways, the way you look at something you're afraid might be taken back.

That's the other thing nobody tells you about coming back from the dead: the living treat you like you might evaporate. My brother had spent twenty years being invisible and I had spent them being a light in a vault, and now we were both learning to be seen, and neither of us was any good at it.

* * *

We took the smuggler roads, because they were the only roads Oscar knew, and because he said — in that flat way he had, the way that meant the sentence had cost him something — that he wanted to see them one more time before he stopped needing them.

They were beautiful, in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. Narrow tracks stitched along cliff-faces, hidden stairways behind waterfalls, waystations carved into the rock where the soot of a hundred fires blackened the ceilings. Oscar named them all as we passed like a man naming his sins. *The Kettle. Widow's Step. The Long Stair, don't look down.* He knew which ledges held weight and which held snow and which held the bones of people who'd guessed wrong.

"Did you bring people through here?" I asked. "Smugglers?"

"Cargo, mostly. Sometimes people who paid to not be asked about." He stepped over a gap in the path without looking, an old habit worn smooth as river stone. "I had rules. Never ask names. Never take sides. Never be the most interesting thing in the room."

"What happened to the rules?"

"You happened." He said it lightly, but his hand found the map-case at his belt where the bloodstained map used to live, an old gesture with nothing left to hold. "Turns out I was always going to break every one of them. It just took the right reason."

I wanted to tell him then — about the lantern, about the twenty years of watching from inside the glass, about how I'd felt him the day the courier died, a tug on a thread I hadn't known I had. I wanted to tell him I'd heard every prayer he'd ever sent up to a silent god, that they'd drifted past my lantern like snow past a window, that I'd pressed my hands — my *light*, whatever hands are, inside — against the glass and screamed soundlessly *she's here, Oscar, I'm here* for two decades.

I didn't. Not yet. Some things you have to walk downhill first, so you have momentum.

Instead I said, "The bread."

He stopped walking. The crow shifted on his shoulder.

"The famine winter," I said. "You think I don't remember. You think because I was small and starving and then I was a lantern, that it's a blur. It isn't. I gave you the bread, Oscar. I put it in your hands and I told you to eat it and I'd have fought you for it if you'd refused. That was *my* choice. You've been carrying my choice around like it was your crime for twenty years, and I'd like it back, please. My shoulders are broad. Yours are terrible."

He stood very still on the mountain path with the wind pulling at his coat, and for a moment he was the thin boy again, and my heart broke in the old familiar place. Then he said, "You always were bossy," in a voice that wasn't steady at all, and we walked on, and neither of us said anything else for a while, and it was the best silence I'd ever stood in.

* * *

At the Kettle — a waystation like a stone bottle buried in the pass — there was a letter waiting, which is a sentence I never expected to construct, because I did not know I had friends yet, plural, and certainly not ones who wrote.

Pip's handwriting looked like it was running from something. *Oscar* — it began, and then, crossed out, *Oscar & the dead one* — and then, decisively, *Oscar and Lise.*

*Business is thriving. That's a lie. Business is existing, which for me is thriving. I found four of the indenture ledgers from the Court's Cinderhollow branch before Sereth's people burned what they could reach, and the names in them are worth more than the paper. I'm returning souls to their owners the way I used to steal bread: quickly, at night, and with an exit planned. Marrow & Associates, Reclaimers. I'm the only associate. The siblings are the associates-in-training. The youngest ate a ledger page. We're working on it.*

*Anwen says the beacon can be seen from Cinderhollow on clear nights. She's wrong, it can be seen from everywhere, I've checked, I climbed the tannery roof. If you're reading this and you're anywhere in Thornfell, look up at the mountain at dusk. There's a light in the old pilgrim tunnel mouth that burns the color Anwen's faith used to be before the church broke it, which is to say: stubborn.*

*Come visit when the roads allow. I owe Lise a drink she's legally too dead to buy and I intend to exploit that loophole.*

*— P. Marrow, Proprietor*

Oscar read it twice and then folded it with the care of a man handling something explosive. "She freed the indentured children," he said. "All of them?"

"She's freeing them," I said. "There's a difference. The second one's harder." I had been a soul in a ledger; I knew the arithmetic. Getting bought back was one night's work. Learning to be a person again was the rest of your life.

The crow hopped down onto the table and considered the letter with one bright eye. "Tell her the mountain approves," it said. "Tell her the mountain is *watching* the tannery roof, specifically, because that child has no fear response and it worries me."

"You're a god," Oscar said. "You have a mountain."

"I have *one* mountain and approximately four thousand new souls learning to walk in it, and one of them is a former acolyte who keeps trying to rebuild the church from the inside, which is either beautiful or a fire hazard." The crow ruffled its feathers, which I had learned was the divine equivalent of a sigh. "Tell Pip: approved, watched, and the youngest sibling should see someone about the paper-eating."

I wrote the reply myself, my first letter in twenty years, my handwriting coming back the way my legs were coming back — badly, then less badly. I told Pip about the roads. I told her the drink was accepted and that I'd find a loophole of my own. I did not tell her that I'd stood in the lantern vault and heard my own name spoken by a brother I thought had forgotten me, because some things belong to the person you say them to, and Pip wasn't there yet. She would be. The letter promised it.

* * *

We came down out of the high passes as autumn came down with us, the larches going gold in bands like the mountain was putting on jewelry. And in the border towns, I learned what my brother had been, and what he was becoming.

In the first one, a carter squinted at Oscar across the market square and said, "Joubert? Oscar Joubert?" — and instead of the flat *never heard of him* I'd been told was his lifelong liturgy, Oscar said, "That's me," and shook the man's hand, and was asked, in front of God and everybody, to guide a wool convoy through Thornfell in spring. He said he'd think about it. He was smiling when he said it, badly, like a man trying on a coat that fit better than he'd expected.

"You hate this," I said that night, at the fire.

"I don't hate it." He turned his hands toward the flames. They were scarred — rope burns, frostbite scars, a long white line I didn't ask about. "I spent twenty years being a shadow with good footwork. Turns out being known is..." He hunted for the word.

"Terrifying," I offered.

"I was going to say *loud.*" But he was smiling. "Terrifying works too."

The crow, roosting on a saddle-horn with its head tucked, said drowsily, "For what it's worth, I preferred you invisible. Fewer people asking me to bless their goats."

"People ask you to bless their goats?"

"Constantly. Word travels. I'm a *small god of safe passage* now, apparently, which is the divine equivalent of being a beloved minor civil servant." One eye opened, gleaming. "I have never been happier."

* * *

The nights were the hard part. I'll say that plainly, because everyone who ever writes about resurrection writes about the mornings, the light, the second chances, and skips the nights.

The nights, I was a lantern again. Not truly — my body was warm and heavy and mine — but the dreams came from the vault. The dark, the rows of lights, the whisper of brokers counting. Some nights I woke up reaching for glass that wasn't there. Some nights I woke up and couldn't remember having a body and lay there cataloguing my own breathing like a merchant checking inventory, *yes, lungs, yes, heartbeat, yes, hands,* until the panic receded.

The third time it happened, I found Oscar already awake at the fire, and I realized he'd been awake every time. He didn't say *are you all right.* He just moved over and made room in his blanket the way he had when we were children and the roof leaked, and I sat down, and we watched the coals.

"I hear them sometimes," he said, after a while. "The lights. In the stone, on the way down. I've been hearing them for years without knowing what they were. I thought it was the mountain settling."

"What do they sound like?"

"Like a house where everyone's home but nobody's talking." He fed a stick to the fire. "I used to think the silence of Vael was the worst thing in the world. A god that doesn't answer. Then I found out the silence had a shape — that it was a *purchase*, that my god spent its voice on you, coin by coin, and never told me because I would have done something stupid." He glanced at me. "Stupider."

"You did plenty stupid anyway."

"I did. I lost a rigged card game to a teenager. I let a defrocked priestess walk me into a hole in the world. I argued with a broker-queen while holding a stolen lantern behind my back like a drunk hiding a bottle." He shook his head. "Best decisions I ever made."

Above us, through a break in the clouds, Thornfell's highest peak caught the moon, and at its crown — I had to squint, but it was there — a faint antlered shape stood against the stars, and beneath it, far down in the rock, a light the color of stubborn faith. Anwen's beacon. The mountain, keeping its promises.

"Oscar," I said. "The bread. And the lantern. And the twenty years. And the crown you didn't take." I counted them off on my fingers, newly mine, scarred already from the road. "You keep a ledger of your own. Debts you think you owe. I want it burned. All of it. You didn't fail me. You *found* me. There's a difference, and I'm the one who was dead, so I get to rule on it."

The crow opened both eyes. "For the record," it said, "the mountain agrees with her. I checked. It's very hard to argue with, being mostly granite."

Oscar laughed — a real one, startled out of him, the kind I remembered from before the famine, from before everything — and the sound went up into the cold air and the mountain took it the way mountains take everything, and kept it.

* * *

We were three days from the lowlands when the travelers passed us going the other way: a wool merchant, two pilgrims, a family with a cart and a crying baby. Ordinary people, on ordinary roads, in a pass that had been a graveyard of the desperate for a century.

The pilgrims stopped when they saw the crow.

"That's him," one whispered to the other. "The antlered one's messenger. We heard it watches the passes now."

"It's a crow," Oscar said.

"It's *Vael*," the pilgrim said reverently, and bowed, to a bird eating a piece of my travel bread.

The crow swallowed, considered, and said, in its deep-bell voice that made both pilgrims sit down right there on the path, "The passes are safe. Walk in daylight. Be kind to each other, it's less work than you'd think." Then it hopped back up onto Oscar's shoulder and muttered, "I miss being nobody."

"No you don't," I said.

"No," it admitted. "I don't."

We walked on, down and down, the three of us — the mercenary who was learning to be known, the god who was learning to be small, and the sister who was learning, foot by aching foot, left then right, to be a person in a world that had gone on twenty years without her and was, to its credit, trying to make it up to her.

Behind us the mountain wore its crown. Ahead of us the road ran gold with autumn, and somewhere at the end of it were a thief with a drink owed, a priestess with a light burning, and the rest of a life neither of us had ever gotten to live.

"Left foot," Oscar said, as the path steepened, and I took it, and then the right, and the road went on.
