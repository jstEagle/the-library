## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The trouble with talking to sheep is that they never have anything interesting to say.

I want to be clear about that, because the ballads—if there ever were ballads about people like me, and I doubt it, since we tend to end up as smoke—would have you believe beast-speech is a grand and mystical thing. Whispers of ancient pacts. Communion with the wild heart of the world. In practice, it is a lame ewe named Doris telling me, for the fourth time this morning, that her leg hurts.

"I know it hurts," I said, kneeling in the wet grass with her hoof in my lap. "That's why I'm holding it."

*Hurts,* said Doris, with the deep satisfaction of a creature who has located the one word that works and intends to wear it out.

"Yes. Hurts. And if you'll stop wriggling, I'll make it hurt less."

She considered this. Sheep consider everything at roughly the speed of continental drift, but they do consider it, which is more than I can say for most of the men who drink at the Salt Latch in Emberhollow. She let me work the rot out from between her cloven halves while she narrated her suffering in a voice like a sack of gravel dragged over gravel. The rain had gotten into the crack weeks ago, back when I was still pretending I couldn't hear her limp up to me every dawn and complain, and now the whole hoof smelled like a bad decision.

There's a trick to it—the listening. It isn't words, not exactly. It's more like standing in a doorway between two rooms, feeling the shape of what's on the other side. A dog's mind is all edges and loyalty. A goat's is a weather system. Doris's mind, God help me, is a small warm room with one chair in it, and the chair faces the memory of grass.

I finished the hoof, packed it with the ash-and-tallow paste old Tam the shepherd swears by—Tam thinks it works because of the ash; I know it works because I told Doris to keep the weight off it, and against all ovine precedent, she does—and sat back on my heels in the mud.

"There," I said. "You'll be dancing by spring."

*I don't dance,* said Doris.

"You danced at the solstice feast. I saw you."

*That was falling.*

"It looked joyful."

Everything I said to her, I said under my breath, barely moving my lips, the way a man prays or lies. Twenty-three years of practice will teach you to talk without talking. My mouth made the shapes of silence, and Doris heard me anyway, because the hearing isn't done with ears either. That's the part nobody would believe: the gift doesn't live in the throat. It lives somewhere behind the breastbone, in the place where you keep the things you've never told anyone.

And in the Ember Valley, nobody has ever told anyone anything worth burning for. That's rather the point of us.

* * *

You have to understand what kind of place this is, and the only way to explain it is to tell you about the stones.

There are eleven of them, great grey leaning slabs set in a rough ring where the valley narrows to the pass road, older than the kingdom, older than the chapel, older than any name anyone uses for them. The folk here call them the Quiet Stones, and they don't mean quiet the way a library is quiet. They mean it the way a held breath is quiet. Nothing crosses those stones that carries magic. Nothing. A hedge-witch from the lowlands once rode through on a dare and came out the other side unable to light a candle with a snapped finger, and she laughed about it for an hour until she realized it wasn't coming back, and then she stopped laughing and moved into Emberhollow and took up midwifery instead. She's dead now. She died of age, which in most of the kingdom is considered a suspicious achievement for someone with her former profession.

So the gifted come here. Not many, and not often, and never saying why. A woman with a green thumb so strong her roses strangled her husband's hunting dogs. A boy who could find lost things until his father beat it out of him, or thought he did—I never learned whether he'd actually been gifted or just unlucky, and neither, I think, did he. They arrive with their gifts already folded up small inside them, and the valley presses those folds flat, and after a few years most of them can't remember whether they ever really heard roses grow or only dreamed it.

I am the exception, and I intend to die of it, preferably of age, ideally in bed, surrounded by goats who are complaining about the quality of the hay.

Because here's the joke, the cruel little joke at the center of my life: the valley should have silenced me too. Whatever runs through my blood didn't come through the stones—it was already here when I crossed them, carried in a basket on my aunt's hip when I was three days old, and it put down roots in me before the Quiet Stones could get their fingers around it. Or maybe the stones simply don't recognize what I am as magic at all. Maybe to them, beast-speech looks like nothing. Like breathing.

Either way, I hear them. Every morning. And every morning I pretend I don't, and the valley pretends along with me, and that mutual pretense is the closest thing to a covenant we have.

The people here are good people. That's the worst part. Marta Brenner, two fields over, would give you the coat off her back and then apologize for it being damp. Old Tam taught me to shear and to swear and to tell which ewes were lying about being pregnant.
