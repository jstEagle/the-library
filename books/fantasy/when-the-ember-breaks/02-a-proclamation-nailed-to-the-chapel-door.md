## 2. A Proclamation Nailed to the Chapel Door

The crier came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were market days, the one day a week when Ember Valley pretended at bustle, and so there was a crowd already gathered in the square when he rode in — a thin man on a tired horse with a brass horn and a scroll case, looking as though he'd rather be anywhere else, including dead.

I was there because of cheese. I want that on the record. I had gone down for cheese and a length of rope, and if my life had gone differently I would now be remembered, if at all, as a person who once bought too much cheese.

Instead I stood in the crowd with my basket on my hip and listened to a stranger read out my death warrant in a voice trained to carry across courtyards.

"Hear ye! Hear ye! By order of His Majesty King Aldous, sovereign of the realm, protector of its borders, keeper of its peace—" the titles went on for a while; kings collect them the way magpies collect buttons "—a census shall be taken of every soul in every village, hamlet, and holding. And let it be known: the crown seeks those who bear the taint of giftcraft. Sorcerers. Hedge-witches. Those who traffic with beasts beyond the speech of honest men."

My hands went still on the basket handle.

"Any who harbor such persons share their guilt. Any who denounce them shall receive from the crown a reward of fifty crowns, and the gratitude of a king who will suffer no corruption in his house."

Fifty crowns. I did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way you flinch without meaning to. Fifty crowns was two years of goat's milk and wool. Fifty crowns was a new roof. Fifty crowns was what my neighbors — good people, kind people, people who had brought me soup when I had fever three winters back — would receive for saying my name aloud to a man with a brass horn.

Around me, the crowd made the sound crowds make when they don't know yet what they feel. A low murmur, curious, not frightened. Not yet. Old Tomas asked the crier whether the census meant more taxes, because Tomas believed every proclamation was secretly about taxes, and honestly, history has rarely contradicted him. The crier said no, only names. Only names, he said, as though names were nothing, as though a name weren't the handle by which a person gets carried away.

Someone laughed. Someone else shushed them. And I stood very still among my neighbors and thought: *none of you know that you are standing next to the thing he is describing.*

That is the particular loneliness of my life, if you're wondering. Not the hiding itself — hiding becomes habit, like anything. It's this. Standing in a warm knot of familiar bodies, smelling bread and horse and the river mud everyone complains about, and knowing that between me and every single one of them stands a door, and behind the door, a fire.

* * *

Let me tell you about the valley, since it may matter later, or may not, since most things don't turn out to matter the way you think they will.

Ember Valley sits high in the eastern hills where the kingdom runs out of ambition. There's a line of old standing stones along the southern pass, grey and lichen-eaten, and the folk here say they mark where a battle was fought a thousand years ago, though nobody can say who fought whom. What I know is this: magic does not cross those stones. Whatever the valley is, it is a hole in the world's weave, a place where the strange stuff simply drains away like water through sand.

I learned this young, though I didn't have words for it until later. When I was six, I told my mother that the neighbor's dog was sad because his boy had gone to sea. My mother went white as flour and never spoke of it again, but she watched me after that, the way you watch a crack in a dam. She died when I was fourteen and left me the goats, the croft, and one piece of advice, delivered with her last strength: *never cross the stones, Rafa.*

I thought she meant it as grief. She meant it as armor. Outside the valley, the gift doesn't just work — it *grows*. Inside, it stays small and quiet, a candle kept cupped. Every animal within the stones speaks to me in the flat, simple way animals do anywhere, I suppose: hunger, fear, the ewe wants her other side scratched. But past the border — and I tested this exactly once, at nineteen, walking twenty paces beyond the stones and immediately regretting it — the voices come flooding in like a burst levee. Everything speaks. Everything has *opinions*. I ran back inside shaking and didn't try again.

So I stayed. Twenty-three years of staying. I sold cheese and wool, fixed fences, attended chapel, and was known throughout the valley as a decent enough sort, quiet, a bit odd about animals, unlikely ever to marry. People said *that Rafael keeps to himself* in the fond tone you use for harmless eccentrics. Harmless eccentric. I have been called worse, but rarely more accurately.

And every day I talked to the goats, and the goats talked back, and it was the smallest possible version of a life, and some mornings it was enough, and some mornings it was like breathing through cloth.

* * *

The crier left by midday, riding south toward the next valley with his brass horn and his list of villages. The crowd dispersed into market-day business, and I heard the fragments I expected to hear: *about time someone did something*, from Bren the smith, whose barn burned two years back under circumstances he still blamed on a lightning strike despite there being no storm. *Fifty crowns is fifty crowns*, said old Halvord, laughing, and then not quite laughing. And from Marta Ostrow, buying apples two stalls down, nothing at all.

Marta. I need to tell you about Marta, and I need you to understand that everything wrong with me starts here.

Marta lived on the holding below mine, a widow with a boy named Tam, eight years old, all elbows and questions. Tam had been born with fire in his palms. Not fire the way poets mean it — actual fire, small and stubborn, little blue-gold flames that curled off his fingers when he cried as an infant, when he dreamed hard, when he got excited about frogs. Marta had spent eight years teaching him to hide it the way I'd taught myself: gloves, cold water, never surprise him, never frighten him, keep the boy calm and the boy dark. She knew about me. Not the specifics — she'd never seen me speak to anything — but mothers in valleys like ours develop a sense for other people's secrets, the way dogs smell weather. Once, years ago, she'd found me in my field talking to a lame ewe, and instead of recoiling she'd just looked at me for a long moment and said, "Well. It takes all sorts," and brought me bread the next day, and we never discussed it again. That was our treaty. Two households keeping each other's silences.

Tam called me Uncle Rafa. He'd sit on my fence and ask me what the goats were saying, as a joke, because he didn't know it wasn't a joke, and I'd make up answers — *this one says your hat is stupid, this one wants to know if you've got any apples* — and he'd laugh like it was the best game in the world, and every time he laughed I felt the crack in the dam widen by one hair.

Because here is what I never told Marta, never told anyone: I could have taught him. Not to be rid of the fire — I don't think gifts can be rid of, any more than you can be rid of your own heartbeat — but to hold it. To quiet it. To live around it the way I'd learned to live around mine. I'd worked out pieces of it over the years, half by instinct, half by watching how the valley's dead air pressed against whatever I was. I could have given that boy ten years of calm he wouldn't otherwise get.

I didn't. Because teaching him meant admitting what I was, to Marta, to Tam, eventually to the valley, and I was a coward in the specific way that quiet people are cowards: politely, gradually, one unspoken thing at a time, until the cowardice is indistinguishable from the landscape.

So on Tuesday, in the market, when Marta caught my eye across the stalls — and she did catch it, I saw her look flick toward the chapel door where the proclamation hung, then to me, then away, quick as a fish turning — I looked down at my cheese and did nothing.

She was asking me, with that look, *what do we do?*

And I answered her, with my silence, *nothing. We do nothing. We hope.*

Remember that. Whatever comes after, remember that I was asked, in the wordless language of two people who shared a secret, and I chose the cheese.

* * *

They came that night.

I wasn't there. That's the sentence I've turned over a thousand times since, and it never once has made me feel better: *I wasn't there.* I was home, a mile up the hill, doing the evening rounds, milking and barring the door and lying awake listening to the wind, and I heard — this is true, this is the part I can't forgive myself for — I heard the ewe, Daisy, restless in the far paddock, and I went out into the cold to check on her, and she looked at me with those flat yellow eyes and said, in the plain wordless way of valley animals, something like *wrong, down-valley, wrong,* and I stood in the dark and told myself it was foxes.

It was not foxes.

Four riders, the crier's escort doubled with men from the county garrison, come up the valley road with lanterns and a paper bearing the king's seal. They knocked at Marta's door polite as tax collectors. Marta must have known the moment she saw the lanterns; there's no other reason a woman bars her door at nine o'clock on a Tuesday. They talked a long while at the threshold. Then they broke it.

I know the shape of what happened from the aftermath, pieced together over days, and from one other source I'll come to. The garrison men found the gloves first — children's gloves, singed at the fingertips, hidden in the woodbox, because Marta had burned the rest weeks ago when rumors of the census started and missed these. A mother's one oversight. Eight years of perfect caution undone by a pair of gloves.

They dragged Tam out in his nightshirt. He was screaming — of course he was screaming, he was eight years old and men with iron were carrying him into the cold — and when he screamed, the fire came, little blue-gold blossoms up his arms, lighting the yard like a festival, and I am told the whole valley saw it, every window lit with faces, every face wearing the same expression.

Here is the part that lives behind my ribs now. The horses screamed too. Horses hate fire, it's in their bones, and four garrison horses reared and shrieked in Marta's yard while their riders cursed and hauled at the reins. And screaming carries in a valley at night. Carries uphill. Carries a mile, easily, to a paddock where a woman stood in the dark beside a nervous ewe, hearing horse-terror and human terror braided together and telling herself, telling herself, telling herself: *foxes.*

I could have run. One mile downhill. Four minutes at a dead sprint, maybe five. I'd have arrived to find it done or nearly done, yes — but I'd have arrived. I might have spoken to those horses, turned them, bought Marta thirty seconds of chaos. I might have stood in the lantern light and said *take me instead, I'm the bigger prize,* and maybe it would have changed nothing, and maybe — this is the thought that keeps me up — maybe it would have changed everything, because a valley that watches a beast-speaker give himself up for a burning child is a different valley than one that watches a child carried off alone.

I stood in the paddock until the screaming stopped and the hoofbeats faded south, and then I sat down in the wet grass, and Daisy the ewe came and leaned against my shoulder, because that is the whole of what a sheep can offer, and that night it was almost enough.

* * *

In the morning, Marta was gone. Taken with him — harboring, they call it, as though a mother housing her own child is a crime with a tidy name. Her house stood open, door broken, hearth cold. The neighbors drifted through it in ones and twos all that day, touching things, murmuring. I heard Bren say, heavily, "Boy always seemed a bit strange," and I heard Halvord say, "Fifty crowns, though. For a word. You could live on that." Nobody said *we should have warned them.* Nobody said *this is wrong.* The valley had already begun the work of making it normal, the way a body makes a scar over a wound, smooth and numb and slightly raised, so you can run your thumb across it forever and feel that something happened there without remembering what.

I did not go inside Marta's house. I couldn't. I stood at her broken gate for a long time instead, and Tam's gloves — no. Not the gloves. On the gatepost, someone had left Tam's hat, the floppy one he wore everywhere, the one my goats allegedly insulted. Just set there in the frost like a small brown flag of surrender. Whether a neighbor put it there or Marta herself, leaving in the dark, I never learned.

I took it. I'm not proud of why. Partly to keep it safe for him — and I held onto *him*, singular, *when he comes back*, because the alternative thinking was a hole with no bottom. But partly, I'll admit it, because taking the hat felt like penance. Cheap penance. A hat costs nothing compared to a voice raised in the night.

On my walk home, the animals were quiet around me. Even the goats. Even the jays, who are never quiet, who narrate the entire valley's business from dawn to dusk like feathered gossips. Silence, from the whole living world, as if they knew what I'd done and what I hadn't, and were giving me room to sit with it.

Only Daisy spoke, that evening, in her flat sheep way, pressing her head against my knee: *cold coming. Cold coming. Cold coming.*

She wasn't talking about the weather. I understood that much. The valley's animals aren't deep thinkers, but they read the sky better than any of us, and something in the sky had changed.

* * *

Three nights later, I woke to scratching at my door.

Not the wind-scratch of branches, not the mouse-scratch in the walls. A deliberate scratch, patient, rhythmic. Scratch. Pause. Scratch. Pause. I lay in the dark and listened, and my heart was doing something complicated, because valley animals don't knock, and valley animals especially don't knock at midnight three days after the king's men carried a child out of the valley in a wagon.

I opened the door with a poker in my hand.

A fox sat on my step. Red, lean, one ear torn and a long pale scar running down its flank like a signature. It looked up at me, and its mouth opened, and what came out was not a bark.

"You're Rafael Almeida," it said. "We need to talk about the king."

I shut the door.

I stood with my back against it, poker clutched to my chest, breathing like a man who has just seen the river flow uphill. Because foxes don't talk. Foxes *can't* talk — not here, not inside the stones, not where magic cannot follow. Whatever was sitting on my step had crossed the one border in the world that I had built my entire life upon, the wall I had trusted with everything, the reason my mother died whispering *never cross the stones.*

And it had crossed carrying words.

From the other side of the door, muffled and infuriatingly calm, the fox said:

"That was rude. Also, the census riders reach the north holdings by week's end, and you, specifically, are on a list. So perhaps let's continue this conversation indoors?"

Outside, somewhere down the dark valley, a dog began to howl, and then another, and the sound rolled up the hills like a warning being passed hand to hand.

I opened the door.
