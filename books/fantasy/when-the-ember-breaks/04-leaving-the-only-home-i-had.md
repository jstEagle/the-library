## 4. Leaving the Only Home I Had

I had always assumed that leaving the valley would feel like something out of a ballad. A last look at the rooftops, a tear on the wind, maybe a verse or two about the road ahead.

Instead I spent my final hour in Ember Valley arguing with a fox about cheese.

"You cannot bring an entire wheel," Quill said, sitting on my kitchen table as though he paid rent there. "You look like a woman fleeing a plague with her good silver."

"It's not *silver*, it's food, and food is the difference between living and dying stupidly on a hillside." I wrapped the wheel in cloth for the third time. "And I'm not a woman."

"Then you look like a man fleeing a plague with his good silver. The point survives translation."

I threw the cloth over the cheese harder than necessary. It was a small wheel, honestly. A modest wheel. But it was the last thing in this house that was mine—made from milk of goats whose names I knew, aged in a cellar my father dug, and I would be damned if I walked into whatever waited beyond the stones with nothing but fear in my pack.

The house was already dead around me. That's the thing nobody tells you about leaving: the leaving starts days before you go. I'd stopped chopping wood two days ago because what was the point. I'd let the garden's weeds have the beans. Last night I hadn't banked the fire properly, just let it burn down to gray, like a sentence trailing off.

My mother's spinning wheel sat in the corner where it had sat for twenty-three years. I didn't touch it. If I touched it, I would sit at it, and if I sat at it, I would spin until the riders came up the lane, and then all of this—the fox, the plan, the terrible arithmetic of survival—would have been for nothing.

"Rafael." Quill's voice had changed. Less sardonic. "The bell."

The chapel bell had been ringing since before dawn. Not the usual call to service—a slow, deliberate tolling, one note at a time, the sound the crier used to gather the valley to the green. By now Marta would be there. Old Tomas. The miller's family. All of them standing in the cold listening to a man in royal colors read out names and prices, and every one of them would be doing the same silent arithmetic I'd done three nights ago: *who do I know? Who might I be worth something for knowing?*

"They'll notice I'm gone by midday," I said.

"They'll notice you're gone when someone checks your fence line, which, given your neighbors, is Thursday." Quill hopped down. "Grief later. Geography now."

*Grief later.* As though grief were a chore you could schedule, like milking. But he was right, which was the most annoying thing about him, so I shouldered my pack, took up my staff, and stood in the doorway of the only home I had ever known.

I looked back once. Just once. The hearth cold, the table bare, the crooked fence Quill kept mentioning visible past the goat pen, leaning into the morning fog like a drunkard against a wall. Somewhere in the pen, Bramble bleated once—not words, just a goat-sound, ordinary and dumb and safe—and I closed the door.

***

The standing stones stood at the valley's northern pass, and they were older than any story anyone told about them. Seven of them, gray and lichen-eaten, arranged in no pattern anyone had ever satisfactorily explained. Children dared each other to touch them. Farmers left offerings at their feet in dry years—bread, mostly, and once, memorably, a boot. And every soul in the valley knew without being taught: magic does not cross them. Whatever runs wild in the world outside stops dead at that line like a dog meeting an invisible fence.

I had lived twenty-three years inside that fence.

We came up through the birch wood in the gray half-light, frost crunching under my boots, Quill trotting ahead with his tail low and moving. He hadn't spoken much since the house. Foxes, I was learning, went quiet when things mattered.

"Stop here," he said finally, at the edge of the trees. Beyond him, maybe forty paces off, the stones rose against the paling sky.

"Why?"

"Because what happens next is not gentle, and I'd rather you fell on grass than stone." He turned to face me, and his amber eyes caught the light strangely. "Listen to me carefully, because I will only say this once and then we are going through. Everything you have felt your whole life—the silence, the flatness, the sense that the world is a room with the windows painted shut—that ends at those stones. On the other side, the gift you've spent your life strangling doesn't just wake up. It wakes up *hungry*. Every creature within earshot will pour into you at once. Birds. Deer. Things in the earth. Things you don't have names for. It will feel like drowning in voices."

"How long?"

"I don't know. An hour. A day. For me it was three days, but I was young and foolish, and also a fox, so take that with appropriate salt." He sat back on his haunches. "There is a way through it. There is a door in your mind—I know you can feel it, even here, even muffled. The one you've kept shut your whole life. On the other side of the stones, you're going to want to throw every door open at once, because the noise will seem like it's coming *through* you, and opening doors feels like relief. Don't. Open one. Learn its weight. Close it again. That's the whole art, Rafael. Not hearing everything. Choosing what to hear."

"And if I can't choose fast enough?"

"Then you'll spend some hours on the ground wishing you were dead, and then you'll get up, and we'll continue." He said it without cruelty, the way you'd tell someone the bridge has a hole in it. "Ready?"

No. Obviously no. I was a goatherd with a cheese wheel and a staff, walking out of the only place in the world where I wasn't a criminal, toward a kingdom that wanted people like me dead, guided by a talking fox who claimed the king was a corpse with something wearing him. Every rational bone in my body voted to turn around, go home, bar the door, and hope the census missed me.

But I thought of Marta's face in the market when they took Tam. I thought of the boy's small hands, hands that had once warmed my tea at a winter fair while everyone laughed, thinking it a trick. I thought of the bell tolling behind us, counting out a life I would never get back.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm going anyway."

Quill's ears flicked—approval, or amusement, or both. "Good. That's the correct answer. The brave ones who say yes frighten me."

We crossed the forty paces.

I remember the stones themselves least of all—gray shapes at the edge of my vision, frost on their shoulders, one of them wearing a crown of ivy some romantic had planted years ago. What I remember is the moment after. The moment the valley let go of me.

It began as a hum. Deep, below hearing, below feeling—a vibration in my teeth, in the bones of my feet, as though the ground itself had cleared its throat. Then the hum became a ringing, and the ringing became—

Sound. Not sound. *Voices.*

They hit me all at once, and Quill was right, it was not gentle, it was a wave breaking over a man who had never seen the sea. Every bird in the birch wood spoke at once—dozens of small bright minds chattering about worms and territory and the cold, overlapping, urgent, *loud*. Beneath them, deeper, slower voices: a badger somewhere under the hill grumbling in its sleep, a hare's sharp simple terror as it ran from something, an old oak's slow creaking awareness that was barely language at all and terrified me more than anything else because it was so *vast*. Worms. Beetles. A fish in the stream below, thinking fish thoughts in a wet green murmur. A crow overhead screaming not a scream but a *word*, a real word, a warning—

I went down. My knees found the frost and my staff rolled away and I pressed my palms against the earth as if I could hold the world shut with my hands, and the world poured through me anyway, a river through a broken dam, and I understood in one white-hot instant that everything I had ever felt of my gift in the valley had been a candle seen through smoked glass, and the glass was gone, and the fire was everywhere.

"*Doors,*" Quill's voice, cutting through, sharp as a whip crack. "*Rafael. Doors. Find one and close it.*"

"I can't—" The hare's terror was mine now, my legs wanting to run, run, run. The crow's warning shrieked through my skull. The oak groaned its vast slow thoughts and I felt a hundred years of seasons pass through me like cold water. "There are too many—"

"There are always too many! That is what it *is!* One door! Pick the nearest voice and imagine a door between you and it, and *shut it!*"

I did the only thing I could think of. I grabbed for the nearest voice—the hare, still running, still afraid—and I imagined a door. A plain door, oak, iron-hinged, the kind my father had built for the cellar. And I slammed it.

Silence. A small, round, perfect silence, exactly the size of one hare.

Oh.

Oh, it was like coming up for air.

"One," I gasped. "One's shut."

"Again. The crow."

Door. Slam. The crow's alarm cut off mid-word, and beneath the remaining roar I felt something I had no word for yet—a handle, a hinge, a *shape* I could grip. Again. The songbirds, all together, one door for the whole flock. Slam. The badger. Slam. The fish. Slam. I knelt in the frost with my eyes squeezed shut, building and closing doors one after another, faster, my father's cellar doors, my mother's cupboard doors, the chapel door, the gate door, and each slam bought me a pocket of quiet, and slowly, slowly, the flood became a river, and the river became a stream, and the stream became—

Wind. Distant wind, and birdsong far away, and the ordinary sounds of a morning.

I opened my eyes.

The world looked the same. That was the strange part. Same birches, same frost, same pale sky going gold at the edges. But it *felt* different—fuller, somehow, like a room I'd only ever seen by candlelight suddenly shown in daylight. And underneath the quiet, I could feel them waiting: all those voices, patient behind their doors, a whole city of them, and me holding the keys.

"Well," I rasped. My throat hurt. My whole body hurt. I was soaked in sweat despite the cold. "That was horrible."

"Yes," Quill said. He was sitting very close, watching me with an expression I couldn't read. "And how do you feel?"

I took inventory. Terrified, obviously. Shaking. My head rang like the chapel bell. But beneath all of it, rising up through the fear like a spring through rock, was something else—something I hadn't felt since I was a child and hadn't known the name for.

"Alive," I said, and heard the wonder in my own voice and hated it a little and loved it more. "God help me, Quill. It's *loud*. It's awful. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard."

"That," said the fox, "is the world. You've been living in its foyer for twenty-three years." He rose and shook himself, frost flying. "Come on. We have miles to cover before dark, and you'll want to practice your doors as we walk. Start with the near ones. Leave the oaks shut for a decade or so; they talk terribly slowly."

I retrieved my staff. I settled my pack—the modest cheese wheel riding comfortably against my spine, thank you very much—and I took one last look north, back toward the stones, back toward the valley where a bell was still tolling and a life was ending without ceremony.

A part of me stayed there. I knew it even then. Some piece of Rafael Almeida would always be kneeling in that frost, hands pressed to the earth, drowning in the first true sound of the world. You don't leave a sanctuary without paying a toll, and the toll is that you carry the sanctuary with you, forever, and it never quite fits inside you, and it aches on cold nights.

But another part of me—larger, louder, hungrier—was facing south for the first time in twenty-three years.

I followed the fox down out of the hills.

***

We walked all morning, and I learned three things.

First: doors tire. By midday my head ached dully, and Quill made me stop and simply *listen*—openly, freely—to a field mouse going about its business in the stubble. Small mind, small concerns, seeds and shelter and a vague distrust of hawks. Listening to it was like dipping a cup in a stream instead of standing under a waterfall. "This is the point," Quill said, washing his paw with obscene elegance. "Not walls. Windows. You don't live behind locked doors, Rafael. You live in a house with windows you open when you choose."

Second: animals outside the valley talked differently. Not just more—in the valley, the ewes' speech had been simple, homely, a dialect of needs. Out here, a kestrel we passed traded actual jokes with Quill, quick and sardonic, and a cart horse at a crossroads complained to me at length about the philosophy of bridles, and I stood in the road laughing until tears froze on my cheeks, because I had gone twenty-three years thinking my gift was a party trick, and it was a *language*, a whole wide tongue with poetry in it, and everyone else had been speaking it fluently my entire life while I mouthed along like a child.

Third: the world knew I was there.

I noticed it first with the birds. Wherever we walked, small wings went up ahead of us—not frightened, just attentive. A fox trotted parallel to us along a hedgerow for a quarter mile, watching. When we paused at a stream, a heron stood across the water and stared at me with one yellow eye, and its mind touched mine, gently, questioningly, like a stranger touching your shoulder in a crowd.

*What are you?*

"I don't know yet," I answered aloud, and the heron considered this, and flew off satisfied, apparently.

Quill had gone quiet again. When I asked him about it, he only said, "Word travels. It always has. Now it has somewhere new to travel *to*." And he wouldn't say more, and I remembered that he claimed to serve a king, and that kings, even dead ones, have enemies, and I walked a little closer to him after that.

Near dusk we crested a long ridge, and the land opened below us, and I stopped walking entirely.

I had seen maps. I had heard travelers' tales in the tavern, secondhand and watered down. Nothing had prepared me for the simple fact of distance—the valley had held my whole life, and here was a country spilling out to the horizon, rivers threading silver through dark woodland, a town smoking peacefully in a fold of hills, roads unspooling like dropped thread toward places I couldn't name. Somewhere out there, the capital. Somewhere out there, a throne, and a corpse wearing a crown, and a boy named Tam in a cage somewhere, and four months on a clock I couldn't see.

The wind came up the ridge and carried voices with it—faint, layered, endless. Dogs in the distant town. Sheep on far slopes. Something huge and slow turning in its sleep beneath the mountains, dreaming thoughts too large for me to catch more than the edge of.

Four months ago I had been a goatherd. Four months from now, if Quill was right, I would stand in front of a hungry god and sing.

"Rafael." Quill sat beside me on the ridge stone, small and red against the darkening sky. "Last chance to turn around. The valley's an hour back. You could be milking by midnight, and maybe the census passes you by, and maybe you grow old and die having never once been honest about what you are. Plenty do. No shame in it."

I thought about it. Honestly, I did. I gave it the full weight of a man weighing his own grave.

Then, from the valley behind us, faint with distance but clear as a bell, I felt a voice rise—one of my ewes, old Daisy, calling into the dusk the way she did every night: *Where are you? Where are you? Come home.*

She wasn't calling me. She called it every night of her life. But tonight it landed like an arrow.

"No," I said. "Keep going."

Quill nodded once, and started down the ridge, and I followed.

We were perhaps halfway down when he stopped so abruptly I nearly trod on his tail. His hackles rose. Ahead of us, in the blue shadow of the valley's mouth, something lay across the road—something that had not been there a moment before, something too large for a dog and wrong-shaped for a wolf, and it was breathing.

And it was screaming.

Not with its throat. With its *mind*—a raw, ragged cry pouring straight into my newly opened ears, a voice in agony saying, over and over, in broken beast-speech:

*Help me. Help me. Oh gods, somebody—help me.*
