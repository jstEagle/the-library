## 5. The Knight in the Ditch

I had been a knight for eleven years and a fugitive for three days, and I want to report that the second profession suits me far worse.

The trouble with being stripped of your rank is that nobody hands you a pamphlet. There should be one. *So You've Refused to Burn a Child: A Practical Guide.* Chapter one: everything you own now belongs to the crown. Chapter two: everyone you trained will hunt you. Chapter three: see chapter two.

My name was still on the bounty boards, though they'd gotten my nose wrong. Sera Vane, formerly Knight-Captain of the Third Hunt, wanted for treason, desertion, and — my personal favorite — "corruption of the realm's moral order." I'd corrupted the moral order by standing between a torch and an eight-year-old girl whose crime was making flowers bloom out of season. The girl got away. I got a hearing that lasted the length of one bell toll, and then I got the road.

Three days out of Corvenna, riding a horse I'd bought with the last honest coin I had, I made my first mistake as a free woman: I took the main road, because it was faster, because I was tired, because part of me — the stupid, sentimental part that had spent eleven years believing institutions meant what they said — still half-believed that no one would actually come for me. That Vane was a name too long in the service to be discarded like a cracked shield.

They came at dusk, in the low ground where the Ashford road dips through alder scrub, and there were six of them, and I knew every face.

* * *

"Vane." Dain Marrek said it the way he used to say it across the training yard, easy, almost fond. He'd been my lieutenant for four years. I'd taught him how to fall off a horse without breaking his neck, which he was about to demonstrate anyway. "Walk away from the horse. Hands where I can see them."

"Dain." I didn't dismount. My mare, Bess, shifted under me, reading my spine the way horses do. "Six of you. For me. The crown must be flush."

"The crown's paying," he said, which was the truer answer, and I respected him slightly for not dressing it up. "Writ says alive if possible. Dead's fine too. Bonus either way."

"For corruption of the moral order?"

"That one's new." He shrugged. "Scribes are having fun with it."

Behind him, the others fanned out with the loose competence I'd drilled into them myself, which is its own special misery — watching your own lessons turned against you, like hearing your mother's scolding come out of your own mouth. Pellor on my left with a net. The twins behind, cutting off the ditch line. Young Osric at the rear, sixteen maybe, holding his crossbow like it might go off in sympathy with his conscience. I noted Osric's grip and filed it away. Conscience is a door left unlocked; you just have to know which house.

"Sera." Dain again, softer. He always went soft right before the hard part; it was his tell, and I'd never told him he had one. "Come in. Plead the king's mercy. They'll give you the wall instead of the rope. Maybe even just the gaol, if the mood's right."

"The king's mercy," I said. "You mean the mercy of the men who burned Tam Wicker's barn with Tam Wicker inside it last month because his daughter could whistle birds down out of trees."

"That's not—" Pellor started.

"It's exactly what it is." I swung down off Bess, because staying mounted only meant they'd take her shot-ridden and screaming, and I have limits, even now. Especially now. "And you all know it. You just haven't let yourselves know it yet. Give it a year. It arrives eventually, usually at night."

"Last chance, Captain."

"I'm not your captain."

"No," he agreed, and something in his face closed like a ledger. "You're not."

The fight lasted ninety seconds, and I lost it in eighty-five.

I'm not ashamed of those numbers. Six against one, in the open, with no armor but a leather jack — losing was the arithmetic. What I'm not proud of is how I lost: badly enough to need saving, which is a word I hadn't needed since I was nine years old, and from a stranger, which is worse, and from a stranger who arrived the way this one did, which is the worst thing of all.

Because here is what happened. Dain took my sword arm with a pommel strike I should have read coming. Pellor's net came down. And Bess — brave, stupid, loyal Bess — screamed. Not the whinny of a spooked animal. A scream, high and human-shaped, the sound of a creature crying murder in a language she'd never been given words for.

And someone answered her.

Out of the alder scrub came a figure — slight, hooded, moving like someone who had never been in a fight in their life and knew it — shouting, and I mean *shouting*, at the horses. All six of the hunters' mounts turned at once. Turned, and bolted, carrying two of my hunters off into the dark with their stirrups flying, while the remaining four stood in a sudden confusion of empty reins and shouted curses.

Then the hooded figure did something to the ditch water — I still don't fully understand it — and the frogs along the bank began a croaking so loud and so precisely aimed that Pellor dropped his net to cover his ears, and I got an arm free, and after that it was mostly noise and elbows and Osric, bless his uncertain heart, firing his crossbow into a tree.

When it was over, three hunters were gone into the brush after their horses, Dain was retreating with a bleeding lip and a look I'll be turning over for years — not hatred, worse, *hurt*, the look of a man whose teacher has disappointed him — and I was sitting in a ditch with a split brow, holding my sword wrong-end-up, staring at my rescuer.

Who crouched at the edge of the ditch, keeping a careful distance, and said, in the voice of someone asking permission to exist:

"Are you — I'm sorry — are you dying? Your horse was very loud about it."

* * *

Their name was Rafael. That was all I got for the first hour, and most of that under protest.

I'll say this for myself: I did not trust them, and I was right not to, and I told them so, repeatedly, while they cleaned the cut above my eye with a rag that smelled of some herb I didn't recognize and hummed when they thought I wasn't listening.

"You could have let them take me," I said. "Why didn't you?"

Rafael's hands paused. Up close, in the light of the small fire they insisted on building in a hollow they swore was invisible from the road — and was; I checked twice — they looked younger than their voice and older than their hands. "Your horse asked."

"Horses don't ask."

"This one does. She has opinions about you. Mostly favorable. She thinks you feed her too late, but she respects your judgment in ditches."

"You're mocking me."

"A little." They wrung out the rag. "Hold still. This will sting."

It stung. I held still, because I am good at holding still, and used the time to study them properly, the way I'd been trained to study anyone I couldn't categorize. Hood back now: ordinary face, watchful eyes, the calluses of a laborer rather than a soldier. No weapon worth the name. Clothes mended with care by someone who valued things lasting. And underneath all of it — under the hunched shoulders and the flinch whenever I moved too fast — something coiled and careful, like a person who had spent a whole life practicing being smaller than they were.

I knew that posture. I'd seen it on gifted children in holding cells. I'd seen it on myself, once, in a polished breastplate, before I learned that a knight's armor lets you stand however you like.

"What did you do to their horses?" I asked.

"Talked to them."

"Talked to them."

"They were frightened. Frightened animals listen well, if you keep your voice low." Rafael tied off the bandage with a neatness that annoyed me. "The gray one was the easiest. He wanted to go home regardless. The bay needed convincing."

"You convinced a war-trained bay to throw a crowned hunter off his saddle by *talking* to him."

"He mostly threw himself. I just suggested the timing."

I sat with that. Around us the fire ticked, and somewhere down the ditch the frogs had resumed a normal, non-military croaking, and Bess grazed at the edge of the light with the serene entitlement of a creature who believed she had personally won the battle.

Beast-speech. I'd heard the word whispered in barracks and read it in interrogation reports, always filed next to words like *aberration* and *contamination*. The hunt I'd commanded for four years had taken eleven people for it. I had signed the transport orders. I had told myself each time that the writs must be sound, that the crown knew things I didn't, that the law was a wall and my job was to be a stone in it.

Then they'd handed me a torch and pointed me at a child, and the wall developed a very clear opinion about what it was made of.

"You should go," I said.

Rafael blinked. "Go?"

"Away. From me. Tonight." I gestured at the dark beyond the fire with my good arm. "Those men will report what they saw. A stranger who commands horses. By morning there will be riders on every road within twenty miles looking for exactly your face. Being near me is a death sentence with legs."

"So is having a gift, from what I hear." They said it lightly, but their hands had gone still in their lap, and their eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — somewhere with a valley in it, I'd learn later, and goats, and a life built carefully over a hole. "Everyone's a death sentence with legs these days. At least yours walks fast."

"That's not funny."

"No," they agreed quietly. "It isn't."

We sat in the fire-noise for a while. I should have pressed it. I should have stood up, saddled Bess, and put five miles between us before moonrise, because every instinct I'd ever honed said *trust no one, need no one, owe no one* — the fugitive's catechism, three lines, easily memorized.

Instead I heard myself ask, "Where are you going? When you leave here."

Rafael looked at the fire. "I don't know yet. Away from home. Home stopped being safe." A pause, and then, with the air of someone deciding to hand a stranger a knife handle-first: "There was a proclamation. A census. They're hunting the gifted. All of them. Rewards for reporting your neighbors."

"I know. I read the proclamation before they nailed it up. That's why there's a warrant with my nose drawn wrong on it."

That got a flicker of a smile — quickly smothered, the way people smother smiles when they've practiced not having them. "What did you do?"

"Refused an order." I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "There was a girl. Eight years old. Flowers out of season, nothing more dangerous than that, and they meant to burn her for it in a market square so the town would learn its lesson. I stood between the torch and the woodpile and informed my commanding officer that I would sooner be burned myself." I shrugged, and my shoulder reminded me it had opinions. "The crown found this insufficiently knightly."

Rafael was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had changed — lower, less guarded, the voice from under the hunch. "Did they burn her anyway?"

"No. In the confusion — my confusion, deliberately caused — she ran. Last I heard she was north, and free, and I intend to keep not hearing otherwise."

"Oh," Rafael said. Just that. But the way they said it carried weight, the way a small coin can weigh more than a big one if it's the last one you have.

And there it was, the thing I'd been circling all evening without wanting to land on it: we were the same animal, this goat-tender and I. Two creatures who had believed in a structure — theirs a valley, mine a vow — and watched the structure turn out to be a mouth. We sat on opposite sides of a small fire, both freshly exiled, both pretending we weren't studying each other, both knowing perfectly well that we were.

I broke the silence first, because silence is a luxury for people who aren't being hunted. "You can't come with me."

"I didn't ask to."

"You were going to."

"I was *not*—" They stopped. Took a breath. Started again with terrible dignity. "I was going to ask which road you're taking. So I could take a different one."

"The east road goes to Ashford Bridge. Which means the east road is crawling with checkpoints by now. The west road goes through Thornhollow market, which is worse — markets are full of eyes and everyone in them is spending someone else's secrets." I pulled my bedroll over with my foot, wincing. "Every road is bad. That's the current situation with roads."

"Then why did you ask?"

Because I wanted to know if you'd lie to me, I didn't say. Because I've spent eleven years reading soldiers and three days learning to read fugitives and I wanted to see which one you are. Because your horse likes you and Bess is a better judge of character than I've ever been.

"Professional habit," I said.

Rafael nodded slowly, and I saw them arrive at some decision the way you'd watch someone step off a ledge and discover, mid-fall, that they'd meant to. "The fox said I'd find you."

I stopped moving. "The fox."

"There's a fox. He talks. Not the way I talk to animals — actual words, sentences, opinions. Too many opinions." They rubbed their face. "He told me to walk east until I found a knight in a ditch. I thought he was mad. I thought *I* was mad. Then your horse started screaming and here we are, and I find I'm hoping you'll tell me foxes don't talk, because if they do, then everything else he said might also be true, and everything else he said is—"

"Is what?"

Rafael looked at me across the fire, and their eyes in the firelight were the eyes of someone standing at the edge of a story much too large for them, saying it anyway.

"He said the king is dead. That the thing on the throne wearing Aldous's body isn't Aldous at all. And that the hunt — the census, the burnings, all of it — isn't fear or policy." Their voice dropped. "It's feeding."

The fire popped. Down the ditch, Bess lifted her head, ears forward, listening to something none of us could hear.

I thought about telling them they were exhausted and concussed and grieving, and that talking foxes were the mind's way of filing unbearable news in a drawer it could survive. I thought about it for perhaps three seconds. Then I remembered Tam Wicker's barn. I remembered the girl with the flowers, and the flat hunger in my commanding officer's eyes when he gave the order — not zealotry, not duty. Hunger. I had seen it and named it nothing, because naming it would have cost me everything, and I hadn't been ready to pay.

I was ready now. Ready costs nothing when you've already lost the purse.

"East road," I said, lying back against my saddle with my split brow throbbing and my sword finally the right way round. "At dawn. If a talking fox sent you to me, then either we're both mad, or we're both exactly where something wants us, and frankly I've survived worse odds than that."

Rafael stared at me. "You believe me? Just like that?"

"No. I think you're possibly a spy, definitely a liability, and certainly going to get us both killed." I closed my eyes. "But I've been wrong about whom to follow before, and the correction nearly burned a child. Dawn, Rafael. Don't be late."

Above the alders, thin and white and indifferent, the moon was beginning its slow climb — and if I had known then how many turns of it remained, and what waited at the end of them, I might have rolled over and gone back to sleep in that ditch forever.

Instead I slept, and dreamed of bells.
