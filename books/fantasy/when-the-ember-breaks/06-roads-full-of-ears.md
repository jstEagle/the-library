## 6. Roads Full of Ears

*Rafael*

The first bounty board I ever saw was nailed to a pear tree outside the village of Hallowmere, and it had my face on it.

Not my face, precisely. A face like mine — young, dark-haired, unremarkable in the way that makes a description useless and terrifying at once. *GIFTED PERSONS: REPORT ALL SIGNS. SPEECH WITH BEASTS. FIRE IN HANDS. DREAMS THAT COME TRUE. REWARD: TEN CROWNS PER HEAD.* Below the words, a woodcut of an eye, wide open, staring down the road in both directions.

"Ten crowns," Sera said, reading over my shoulder. "You're worth less than a good mule."

"That's hurtful."

"It's useful. Nobody robs a mule's master for a mule. Stay worthless." She tore the notice off its nail, folded it into quarters, and put it in her coat like she was collecting them. She was. By the fourth village she had seven, and she'd started grading them on accuracy the way a schoolmistress grades sums.

Quill, trotting ahead with his tail at a deliberately fox-like angle — he was very insistent about looking like an ordinary fox when people were watching, which is its own kind of performance, if you ask me — fell back to my heel as we passed under the tree.

*They spelled "beasts" wrong,* he said. *B-e-a-s-t-e-s. Ten crowns for your head and they can't spell.*

*I noticed.*

*It doesn't inspire confidence in the crown's clerisy.*

*It doesn't inspire anything in me except the desire to be somewhere else.*

We walked on. Behind us, the eye on the notice watched the empty road, and I felt it watching anyway. That was the thing about the hunt that no one warns you about: it isn't the checkpoints that wear you down. It's the boards. It's walking through country where every tree has grown eyes, where the pears themselves seem to lean in and listen, and understanding that the reward isn't really ten crowns. The reward is being the kind of person who gets to feel safe again, and the kingdom has decided that's worth more than gold.

I had been safe for twenty-three years by saying nothing to anyone but animals. Now I wasn't even allowed to say nothing properly. Sera's plan — her word, delivered with the flat certainty of a woman who had commanded men and missed it — was that I should be mute.

"You'll be Tam," she'd said, our second morning out. "My servant. Deaf-mute from a childhood fever. It explains why you don't speak, why you look at the ground, why you flinch when spoken to suddenly. People fill silences with their own explanations. Give them a good one."

"And what do I call you?"

"Mistress Vane. A freeholder's widow traveling to Corvenna to claim her late husband's debts." She said it without blinking, which I later learned was how she did everything untrue.

"Why a widow?"

"Because widows are invisible and pitied, and no one searches a pitied woman thoroughly. Also because I've been one before." She said that last part lightly, the way you set down something heavy so you can pick up something heavier, and I didn't ask. Not then.

So I became Tam. I learned to keep my mouth soft and slightly open, learned the small dumb nodding that servants do, learned to let my eyes go vague. It was the hardest acting I have ever done, harder than the throne room, harder than the song, because it required me to spend whole days being less than I am, and I discovered I'd spent twenty-three years practicing exactly that and hated how good I'd gotten.

The valley had taught me to hide among people. The road taught me to hide inside myself, and there is less room in there than you'd think.

* * *

*Sera*

I have marched roads before. I know what an army looks like from the saddle and what a country looks like when it's afraid, and I will tell you plainly: they look the same. Same dust. Same shutters closing. Same dogs tied short and children pulled indoors. The only difference is that armies eventually march past, and fear moves in and pays rent.

Aldous — or whatever wore Aldous — had done in four months what no invader could have done in forty years. He had turned every kitchen into a garrison. Every village we passed had its census list pinned somewhere public, names in a clerk's hand, ages, trades, and a column at the end marked only with a symbol I didn't recognize until Quill told me it meant *under question*. Under question. As though a person could be a sum that wouldn't balance.

At the crossing at Millbrook there was a checkpoint. There were checkpoints everywhere, but this one was run properly, which is to say badly in all the ways I recognized, because I had trained the man running it. Corporal Dennet. Good lad. Terrible at cards, honest to a fault, and now wearing the black sash of the Crown Hunt over armor I remembered fitting him personally.

I took Rafael off the road twenty minutes early and made them practice.

"Look at my boots," I said. "Not my face. Servants watch boots because boots tell you where you're allowed to stand."

Rafael looked at my boots. "Like this?"

"Don't talk."

*Like this?* their hands asked instead, and I realized I would need to teach them the servant's grammar too — the half-bow, the waiting stillness, the way you make yourself furniture. They learned fast. That frightened me more than the checkpoint did. No one learns to disappear that quickly unless they've had practice.

At the barrier, Dennet waved us toward the line of carts and kept his eyes moving. I gave him the widow's face — grief worn smooth by time, a little confusion, a little money showing just enough to be respectable. He glanced at Rafael, standing behind my shoulder with their eyes fixed obediently on the mud.

"The servant?" he said. "Why's he not look up?"

"Fever took his speech when he was six, corporal, and his mother always said it took his nerve with it. I keep him close because the road's no place for simple folk alone." I let my voice catch on *simple*, just slightly. Grief and pity, the two oldest keys, and both of them true enough to ring.

Dennet softened. I watched him decide not to look at Rafael's hands, or teeth, or eyes — watched him choose kindness over procedure, because I had trained that into him too, back when the crown wanted soldiers instead of informants. He waved us through.

Half a mile on, Rafael spoke — quietly, only to me, only when the road bent and the trees closed. "He knew you."

"He knew my old rank. Not my face. There's a difference, and it's shrinking."

"What happens when it closes?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was: then I stop being a ghost and become a prize, and everything I taught them gets used to catch people like you. Instead I said, "Boots, Tam," and they went quiet, and we walked on through a country full of ears.

That night, camped in a cut hayfield, I heard them talking to the hedgehogs. Low, under their breath, a language like water over stones. I didn't mean to listen. But I lay there listening anyway, and what struck me wasn't the strangeness of it. It was the gentleness. Twenty years around the gifted-as-enemy, and the first one I ever traveled with was whispering goodnight to a hedgehog.

I turned over and pretended to sleep, and told myself the tightness in my chest was just the cold.

* * *

*Rafael*

Crows, it turns out, are terrible gossips and excellent correspondents, and the crown employs both kinds without knowing it.

I learned this on the fifth day, when a crow followed us for two miles and Quill finally lost patience and went up a tree after it. What came down was this: crows remember faces, and men in black sashes pay bread for reports of unusual birds, sick livestock, children who stare at nothing, travelers who travel too quietly. The crows weren't spies. They were simply paid in bread, and a crow will sell anything for bread, including you, including me, including, Quill assured me bitterly, *him, and I thought we had history.*

"So every crow on the road—"

"—is a pen for hire, yes." Quill sat on my knee, licking his paw with the air of a creature deeply wronged. "Which is why we walk at dawn and dusk, when crows commute, and why you will stop making friends with things that fly."

"I wasn't making friends. A wren was telling me about a cat."

"A wren will tell a black-sash anything for a thorn hedge to nest in. Everyone's bought, Rafael. That's the shape of this hunt. It isn't soldiers. Soldiers are expensive. It's neighbors." He looked up at me with his amber eyes, and for a moment the flippancy dropped away and something old looked out. "I watched a kingdom fall once because everyone was certain someone else would report them first. Fear doesn't need to catch you. It only needs to make you catch each other."

I thought of Marta's son, born with fire in his palms, taken in the night while the valley slept and said nothing. I had said nothing too. I was still saying nothing, most hours of the day, as Tam the mute. Silence had been my whole life, and now silence was the enemy's favorite weapon, and I didn't know yet how to make the difference between them audible.

Sera solved it in her way, which was drill. Every evening she ran us through it: the story of the widow Vane, the dead husband's name (Aldric, a wool factor, died of a fever in Corvenna), the route, the debts. If questioned separately, we must match. If separated, meet at the next milestone east. If either of us is taken—

"If either of you is taken," she corrected herself, "the other walks away."

"I'm not walking away," Rafael said.

"Yes, you are."

"No."

She stood up. Firelight made her scarred cheek into something carved. "Listen to me. I burned one child already because I followed orders and hesitated one breath too long. One breath. I will not hesitate again, and I will not bury another person I—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "The mission matters more than either of us. That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic."

"The ewe in my valley had a better opinion of arithmetic," Rafael said, "and she ate a boot."

For a moment — one moment — Sera laughed. It came out of her like a bird startled off a wire, and she looked almost as surprised as we were. Then she banked it, the way she banked everything, and said, "Sleep. Dawn march."

But Quill caught my eye across the fire, and his tail curled, very slightly, the way a fox smiles.

* * *

*Sera*

On the ninth day we came within sight of Thornhollow, and I stopped liking the road at all.

Thornhollow was a market town, which meant it was a throat — everything going to the capital passed down it, and throats are where you post the guards. Two bounty boards at the gates, a black-sash pair at the well, and, worst of all, a handler with a hound working the crowd near the cheese stalls. I knew the breed. Gift-hounds. Bred from wolf-stock and something older nobody documented, noses tuned not to scent but to *wrongness* — to the smell, if you believe the handlers, of power. I'd seen one point at a girl of nine once, and I had refused the order that followed, and here I was, three years and one lifetime later, walking my own gifted charge straight past another.

"We go around," I said.

"There's no around," Rafael said. "Quill checked. The fen road floods; the north pass has a toll fort. It's Thornhollow or four days lost, and we don't have four days."

"We don't have a choice either." I looked at the town a long moment, doing the arithmetic I hated. "New rule. In there, you're deaf as well as mute. You hear nothing from any animal, you answer nothing, you look at boots. And if that hound turns its head toward you, you drop the basket, fumble, apologize with your whole body, and give me time to lie. Can you do that?"

Rafael was quiet. I'd learned their silences by then — tired silence, scared silence, and this one, which was the silence of someone deciding whether to argue. "And if the hound points at some other child in that crowd? Some Marta's son?"

"Then we keep walking."

"That's arithmetic," they said, and there was no heat in it, which was worse than heat.

"It is," I said. "It's the arithmetic that keeps you alive long enough to matter. I've buried enough people who mattered early."

They picked up the basket. We went in.

The market swallowed us — fish and bread and wet wool, a hundred voices, and above it all the hound's handler calling his beast to heel. I kept Rafael at my right shoulder, bought turnips I didn't want, haggled badly on purpose, played the widow down to the crease in my forehead. For a hundred paces it worked.

Then, near the birdmonger's row, Rafael stopped.

I saw it happen — saw their head turn a degree, saw their eyes fix on something I couldn't see, saw the stillness come over them that I'd learned meant *listening*. Following their gaze: a cage. A songbird, brown and ordinary, beating itself against the bars in the particular frantic rhythm I had seen before in creatures that had more in them than feathers.

"Tam." I said it low. "Tam. Boots."

They didn't move. Their lips parted — and I saw, with a clarity that has never left me, that they were going to speak to it. Right there. In front of the handler, the hound, the whole listening town.

I did the only thing I could think of. I slapped them.

Open-handed, across the ear, hard enough to stagger — the blow a mistress gives a thieving servant, theatrical and loud, and every eye in three stalls swung to us. "Clumsy fool!" I shouted, loud enough for the handler, loud enough for the hound, whose head had begun to turn. "Daydreaming when there's work! Forgive me, goodman — the boy's simple, fever took his senses, I only bring him along because he's strong—" And I bowed and scraped and apologized us both backward out of the row, my heart going like a drum, Rafael's cheek blooming red under their hair, the hound's nose swinging away at last toward a cart of eels.

We didn't speak until the north gate was behind us and the road bent into trees. Then Rafael stopped, and touched their cheek, and looked at me with something I hadn't seen in them before.

"You hit me."

"I did."

"You could have—"

"There was no other lie fast enough." I heard my own voice go flat, soldier-flat, and hated it. "I won't apologize for keeping you alive."

"That's not what I was going to say." They flexed their hand, opened it, closed it. "I was going to say thank you. And also that the bird got out. When the monger knocked the cage catching it — the latch broke. It's free."

I stood there on the road with my palm still stinging, being thanked by someone I'd struck, for a rescue I hadn't intended and a mercy I'd tried to prevent, and I understood suddenly that I was in the presence of a discipline I didn't have. Mine was obedience, once, and then refusal, and both had cost. Theirs was something else. Something that opened cages even while it was being dragged out of the market by the ear.

"Thornhollow has a bounty officer," I said finally, because feelings are for people with safer lives. "He looked at you two beats too long. We camp far tonight, and tomorrow—"

Tomorrow. I never finished the sentence, because that was when the crow landed on the milestone ahead of us, tilted its head, and looked at Rafael with an attention no bird has ever given me.

And beside me, very quietly, Quill said a word in a language I didn't know, in a voice I'd never heard him use — and the crow flew off west, fast, toward the capital.

Toward the palace.

"Well," said the fox. "That's going to cost us."
