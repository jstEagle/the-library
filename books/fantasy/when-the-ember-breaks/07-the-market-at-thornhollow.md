## 7. The Market at Thornhollow

Thornhollow smelled of onions, wet wool, and money changing hands too quickly for anyone to count honestly.

I had been in markets before—Ember Valley held one every quarter-day, and I'd sold cheese and goat milk at it since I was old enough to carry a pail—but Thornhollow's market was a different animal entirely. It sprawled across three streets and a cobbled square like something that had eaten the town and grown too big to digest. There were fishmongers shouting over each other, a man selling knives with the confidence of someone who had never been stabbed by one, a woman whose entire stall consisted of pickled things in jars of varying menace. Children ran between legs. Dogs wove through the crowd on private errands. Somewhere a fiddler played badly enough that people paid him to stop, which I suspected was his business model.

And everywhere, under all of it, voices.

Not human voices. The valley had taught me silence; three days past its stones had taught me that silence was a luxury of the magic-dead. Out here, every creature within earshot of my gift had an opinion, and most of them were sharing it. The horses at the far rail complained about their bits. A cat on a windowsill narrated her contempt for pigeons in language I won't repeat. A pig being led toward the butcher's pens made a sound I felt in my teeth—not words exactly, more like a long, low *no* stretched out over half a mile.

Sera walked ahead of me with the particular posture of a woman who wanted very much not to be recognized: shoulders squared, chin level, eyes moving in the old soldier's rhythm—doorway, rooftop, crowd, doorway. She'd bought me a servant's smock in the last village and told me to keep my mouth shut, which was, I'll grant her, reasonable advice given that my mouth was precisely the problem.

"Mute," she'd said, pinning the collar herself with two hard tugs. "Deaf-mute, if anyone asks. It explains why you don't answer and why you stare. Stare away. People expect it."

So I stared. And I listened. And I kept my hands folded and my face blank while the whole living world chattered at me, and I thought: *this is what it costs to be safe. You have to pretend the world is empty.*

Quill rode in the basket on my back, which he had declared undignified and then filled with stolen bread crusts.

"Left," he murmured against my ear, so quietly only I could hear him—which is to say, in fox-speech, pitched below human hearing, a trick I still didn't fully understand. "The officer by the well has been looking at us since the bridge. Not at Sera. At you."

My stomach did something complicated. "Why me?"

"Because you walk like a man listening to a sermon no one else can hear. Because your eyes go to every animal on this street before they go to anything else." A pause, and then, gentler: "Because you do, little speaker. I've watched you for days. You can't help it."

He was right, and I hated that he was right. In the valley, my habit of watching animals had just been eccentricity—a goatherd who talked to his goats, charming, harmless, the sort of thing neighbors forgave because everyone needs a quirk. Out here, where bounty boards grew on posts like fungus and children were dragged from their beds for having fire in their palms, it was a confession written on my body for anyone literate enough to read it.

I fixed my gaze on the middle distance, on nothing, on the honest commerce of onions.

* * *

We had come to Thornhollow for three reasons, according to Sera: salt pork, news, and the road east out of town before dark. We would split up—she to the provisioner, me to the far stalls where a mute servant buying thread wouldn't be noticed—and meet at the fountain when the bells rang noon.

The bells rang noon while I was haggling—badly, mutely—with a thread-seller, and the sound went through me like a hook through a fish.

I hadn't known Thornhollow had a bell tower until it used it. The note was enormous, brazen, indifferent, and underneath it every animal in the square flinched. The horses tossed their heads. The dogs stopped mid-stride. The cat on the windowsill swore with real feeling. And I stood there with thread in my hand feeling as though someone had rung a bell inside my skull, because sound that loud doesn't just hit the ears—it hits whatever it is in me that hears, and it rang *that* too, a great dull bronze clangor drowning everything.

For a moment I couldn't hear any of them at all. The square went silent in the only direction I ever truly listened, and the silence was worse than noise. It was like going deaf in one eye.

Then the bell finished dying, and the world came back, and that was when I heard the bird.

It came from the row of stalls along the south wall—cages stacked three high, finches and larks and one magnificent, furious rooster who was composing insults I intend to use someday. But above all of them, thin and clear and desperate:

*"Open. Open. Open the door. Please. Please. Open—"*

A songbird. Small, brown, unremarkable except for the voice, which even in terror was beautiful the way a struck bell is beautiful. It wasn't begging the way the pig had begged—that long low animal *no*. It spoke almost in words. Caged birds do that sometimes, Quill had told me; they live so close to human mouths that they learn to shape their need into our shapes. *"Open. Open. Open the door."*

I should have kept walking.

I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps of my failures: I knew I should have kept walking. Sera's rules were simple and I could recite them in my sleep. *Don't look at the animals. Don't touch the animals. Don't, under any circumstances, speak to the animals.* Three rules, and I had held to them for three days, and here was a small brown voice saying *please* in a market full of ears, and I stood there holding thread I didn't need and felt something crack down the middle of me.

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding for twenty-three years: it isn't one decision. It's ten thousand decisions, one after another, forever, each one small enough to justify. *Don't help the ewe with the stuck gate, someone might see. Don't sing back to the lark. Walk past the cage.* Every single time, the arithmetic is the same—the risk against the mercy—and every single time, in the valley, mercy had lost, and I had called that survival, and survival had felt like virtue because I was still alive to feel it.

But the bird said *please.*

I crossed the square. I remember my feet deciding before the rest of me agreed, the way feet do.

The cage-seller was a broad man arguing cheerfully with a customer about the price of a lark, and the songbird hung on the end of the rack in a wicker cage with a door that closed on a simple wooden latch. Simple. The kind of latch a child could open. The kind of latch a mute servant could open without breaking stride, without saying a word, without doing anything that a witness could later describe as *magic.*

That was the lie I told myself as my hand came up. That there was a version of this that cost nothing.

The latch clicked.

The door swung, and the bird sat there a heartbeat, stunned—as if freedom were a trap it hadn't been warned about—and then it burst upward through the gap and the whole rack of cages erupted into screaming, wings battering wicker, the rooster delivering what I can only assume was a eulogy or an anthem, and the seller turned around mid-haggle to find his merchandise attempting a mass breakout.

"Oi!" he bellowed. "Oi! Who—"

And the songbird, rising over the stalls, spiraling up into the gray morning light, sang.

Not fear. Not anymore. It poured out everything it had—every note it had swallowed in that cage, months of them, maybe years—and the sound cut across the whole market like a blade through cloth. People stopped. Faces tilted upward. For three seconds, four, the entire roaring machinery of Thornhollow went quiet to listen to one small brown bird say *thank you* the only way it knew how.

It was the most beautiful thing I had heard since leaving the valley.

It was also, I understood with a cold, slow horror, the most conspicuous thing I had ever done. Because I was standing at the open cage. Because my hand was still on the latch. Because I was smiling—I could feel it on my face, wide and helpless and utterly damning—and every instinct I had spent a lifetime sharpening was screaming at me that a man does not smile like that at a bird unless the bird is talking to him.

The seller grabbed my wrist. "You! Did you open—"

"I did."

The words were out before I remembered I was mute. Two words, plain speech, my voice cracking with disuse, and the seller blinked at me, and I watched the confusion on his face begin its journey toward suspicion—

"—because he's simple," said a voice behind me, flat and bored and carrying the weight of rank. "Poor lad. Doesn't understand doors. Or property. His mistress will box his ears for it, believe me."

Sera.

She had appeared from nowhere the way soldiers do, and she had hold of my shoulder now, grip like a manacle, and her face wore an expression I'd never seen on it: a kind of weary, public patience, the face of a woman long-suffering with an idiot servant. She pressed a coin into the seller's palm. "For the bird. He'll pay for his sins in other ways. Come along, boy."

The seller looked at the coin, looked at me, looked at Sera—and here was the thing, the thing I would turn over in my mind for nights afterward—he looked at her *badge-side*, at the faded outline of a hunter's warrant she still carried folded in her coat because burning it felt like a step she wasn't ready to take, and something in his face smoothed over. A knight's coin. A knight's word. Whatever strangeness there'd been in the mute servant's smile got filed away under *not my business,* and he grunted, and turned back to his lark, and that was that.

Sera steered me through the crowd with her hand iron on my neck, past the fountain, down a side street, around a corner, into the shadow between a cooper's yard and a wall, and only there did she let go and turn on me.

Her eyes were terrible.

"Do you know," she said, very quietly, "what I just did?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. My voice had already betrayed me once today; I wasn't eager to give it another chance.

"I lied," she said. "In the open. On a market street. I used my name—what's left of it—to buy a stranger's disbelief, because that's the only currency I have left, and I just spent it on a *bird.*" Her breath came out slow through her nose, the way it did when she was choosing not to shout. "Do you understand what that means? If that man talks—if he mentions a knight and her simple servant and the freed bird to the wrong ears—there are officers in this town who will pull the thread. They'll ask who the knight was. They'll describe me. And then, Rafael, they will come."

"I'm sorry," I whispered.

"Sorry." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of exhausted wonder. "Sorry is for stepping on feet. Do you know what they do to the gifted? Have you actually seen it, or have you only heard about it from the safety of your valley?" She stepped closer. Up close I could see she wasn't angry, not really—she was frightened, and her fear had nowhere to go, so it had dressed itself as anger because that was armor she knew how to wear. "I refused one order. One. A child, Rafael, a child with fire in his hands, and I said no, and I lost everything—my rank, my company, my name, twenty years of my life—in an afternoon. And you, after three days on the road, cannot walk past a *songbird.*"

"It asked me," I said. It sounded pathetic even as I said it. "It said please."

"And the pig at the last farm said worse, and you walked past. What was different?"

I didn't have an answer that would survive daylight. The truth was that there was no difference, none at all, except that the bird's voice had found some door in me that the pig's hadn't, and that door had no lock, and I didn't know how to build one, and I was suddenly, enormously tired of trying.

"The difference," I said finally, "is that I've spent twenty-three years walking past things. And I'm good at it. I'm so good at it that I built a whole life out of it, and I told myself it was humility, and it was just—" My voice caught. "It was just fear wearing humility's clothes. And that bird sang, Sera. After all those months in the cage, the first thing it did with its freedom was sing. And I stood there and I thought: *that's what I want. That's all I've ever wanted.* Not safety. To make one sound that's mine, in the open air, where anyone can hear it."

The street was quiet around us. Somewhere beyond the wall, the market resumed its roaring, indifferent to our little apocalypse.

Sera looked at me for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes—recalculation, maybe, or recognition, the grim arithmetic of a woman realizing that the person she'd promised to deliver alive to a revolution might get them both killed first, and was trying to decide whether that changed anything.

"You smiled," she said at last. "At the cage. That's what he saw. Not the latch—the smile. Mute men don't smile at birds like that." She rubbed her face with both hands, and when she lowered them she looked older, and very tired, and something else underneath the tiredness that I couldn't name yet. "From now on you don't go near the animals. Not near. You look at the ground, you count your steps, you think about thread. Can you do that?"

"Yes," I said.

It was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and she nodded anyway, because apparently that was the bargain: she would give orders neither of us believed in, and I would accept them, and we would call the arrangement trust.

* * *

We met Quill at the fountain, as arranged, though technically Quill had never left the fountain—he'd spent the morning on its rim being admired by children and robbing them of pastry, which he defended as "reconnaissance."

He took one look at our faces and his ears went flat.

"What happened?"

"A bird," Sera said.

"Ah," said Quill. "One of *those* birds."

"There's a bounty officer," I said quietly. "By the well. Brown coat, gray beard. He's been watching us since the bridge—Quill saw him. And the cage-seller saw my face, and heard Sera call me her servant, and if he talks—"

"He already talked." Quill licked a crumb off his paw with elaborate calm, but his eyes stayed on the crowd. "I've been listening since the bell. A man in a brown coat bought an onion he didn't want, purely for the privilege of standing near the cage-seller. They spoke for the length of a prayer. Then the onion man walked toward the guardhouse, unhurried, the way men walk when they're being paid either way." He looked up at me, and his amber eyes were ancient and sorry. "They'll check the roads by dusk. Checkpoints on the east road, likely, and riders on the north. Our friend the officer thinks he's hunting a knight and her mute boy." A pause. "He doesn't know yet what he's actually hunting. But he'll write it down tonight, and paper travels faster than we do."

Sera had gone very still, the stillness of a soldier hearing the shape of a battle before it starts. "The east road's out. North is back toward the hunters we already dodged. South is marsh country."

"South is *wet* country," Quill corrected. "Which is different, and better, and I hate it. Marshes hide scent, and sound, and foxes who despise getting their feet damp." He sighed the sigh of the profoundly put-upon. "There's a drover's track south of town. Cattle path. No checkpoints, because no one with sense takes cattle through a fen."

"Then we leave now," Sera said. "Before dusk. Before the description gets copied and posted." She was already moving, counting coins for the innkeeper, gathering our bundle, becoming motion itself—and then she stopped, and looked back at me, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from Sera was more alarming than shouting.

"Rafael. The next town, the next cage, the next voice that says *please*—there will be a next one, I know there will. When it comes, I need you to remember that man in the brown coat. Remember that mercy has a price tag now, and the crown is collecting." She hesitated. "I'm not telling you to stop caring. I'm telling you that out here, caring loudly gets people killed, and lately I've started—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, "—I've started having people I'd rather didn't die. So. Quietly. Both of us. Quietly."

I nodded. And I meant it, mostly.

But as we slipped out of Thornhollow by the south gate, as the fen rose up ahead of us gray and whispering, I heard them—the birds in the hedgerows, the frogs in the ditches, the whole hidden choir of the ordinary world, singing their small songs to no one. And behind us, faint on the wind, the market bell began to ring again, and beneath it, if I strained, I could almost hear the sound of a pen scratching paper, writing down a description of a girl who smiled at birds.

The fen would hide us. The fen would hide our scent and our sound.

What it could not hide—what no fen, no valley, no silence in the world would ever hide again—was the thing that had cracked open in me in that square, the thing that had heard *please* and answered. I had spent twenty-three years building a door and keeping it shut. Now it was open, and the whole world was pouring through it, and somewhere behind us a man with an onion and a ledger was learning to read the shape of what I was.

Quill trotted at my heel, feet damp, dignity abandoned.

"South," he muttered. "Toward the marshes. Where, if my information holds, we'll find people who've been hiding what you just showed the whole of Thornhollow." His tail flicked. "Pray they're better at it than you are."
