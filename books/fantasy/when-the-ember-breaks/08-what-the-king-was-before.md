## 8. What the King Was Before

I have told many lies in my long and undignified life, most of them to chickens, but I had not lied to Rafael Almeida until that night, and it sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

The lie was small, as lies go. Sera asked me where I'd learned the roads through the hill country, and I said, "A fox picks things up." Which is true the way a knife is true. It cuts what you point it at.

We had made camp in the hollow of a dead oak's roots, off the shepherd's track, with a fire small enough to hide under a hat. Sera kept watch with her sword across her knees because she was the sort of woman who slept the way a portcullis sleeps—technically down, entirely ready to come up sharp. Rafael sat close to the flames with their arms around their shins, and Pip—no, not yet, Pip came later; forgive an old fox his chronology, grief tangles time like brambles—it was only the three of us that night, and the dark beyond our little ring of light was full of the ordinary night noises of an ordinary kingdom, which is to say it was lying too.

Rafael asked the question the way they asked everything: sideways, quietly, as if they didn't much care about the answer.

"You knew the roads before," they said. "You knew the valley stones were failing before we crossed them. You knew the census riders' schedule." A pause, in which the fire popped like a small rude guest. "Who are you, Quill?"

"Charming," I said. "Handsome. Excellent at finding mice."

"Quill."

And there it was. The tone. I have been addressed by kings in that tone—not this king, the old one, the good one, though he was never mine by blood—and I knew there was no more dodging to be done. A fox can outrun a hound, a hunter, a bad winter, even, if he's clever and lucky, a crown. He cannot outrun a question asked kindly by someone who has earned the answer.

So I sat up on my haunches, wrapped my tail over my paws the way I do when I mean business, and I told them the truth for the first time since the valley line.

* * *

"My name isn't Quill," I began, "but it will do, because my real name isn't a word so much as a smell and a memory and a debt, and your tongue couldn't pronounce it if I lent it to you. I was whelped in the palace kennels of Corvenna, in the reign of Aldous's grandmother, and I am one hundred and forty years old, and before you say foxes don't live that long—you're right. Foxes don't. Companions do."

Sera's hand had gone very still on her sword. She was listening the way soldiers listen, which is to say she was already planning how to kill me if the story went wrong. I respected her for it. I've always liked people who plan my death carefully; it shows attention.

"The royal house of Corvenna has an old bargain with the wild," I said. "Older than the kingdom itself. When the first king made peace with the forest rather than burn it—a novel idea, I know; most of his successors forgot it—the forest gave him a companion. Not a pet. Not a familiar, whatever your priests mutter. A witness. An animal bound to the royal bloodline, one at a time, down the generations, to see what men do when they think no one is watching. We are not permitted to advise. We are not permitted to interfere. We are permitted only to remember. It is," I said, "a miserable job, and I have done it for three reigns, and I loved every minute of it except all the minutes that mattered."

Rafael hadn't moved. Firelight moved on their face instead, shadows climbing and falling like slow weather.

"Aldous was crowned twenty-two years ago," I said. "He was thirty. He cried during the coronation, you know. Not from joy. His father had died four months before, badly, coughing his life out into a silk handkerchief, and Aldous knelt on those cold marble steps with two hundred pounds of gold on his head and wept for a man who had never once told him he was adequate. I sat behind the throne—I always sit behind the throne; it's the best seat in any room and the worst view—and I thought: this one will be kind. This one remembers what it is to be small."

My voice did something then. I'm told by those who speak human words that voices can crack. Mine didn't crack. It bent, the way a green branch bends, and held.

"He was kind. That's the thing none of the ballads will ever get right, because the ballads haven't been written yet, and when they are, some minstrel with a weak chin will make him stern and distant and tragic, and he wasn't. He was funny. He laughed at his own jokes before he finished them. He named all the palace cats after his political enemies so he could say he'd finally got Lord Hessel on his lap and purring. He learned the names of scullery boys. He—" I stopped. Cleaned my ear with a hind foot, which buys a fox several seconds and looks insouciant. "He talked to me. Not at me, the way his grandfather used to, using me as a furry confessional. With me. He knew what I was, of course; every heir is told. And he'd sit in the garden after council meetings that had gone badly and say, 'Quill, tell me honestly,' and I would, because advising was forbidden but honesty has never once been against the rules, and he'd listen to a fox's opinion on grain tariffs with more seriousness than he gave his chancellors. I watched him for eleven years. I watched him marry a woman he adored and lose her to a fever and grieve like a man and not like a king, which is to say messily, at night, in the garden, talking to me. I watched him hold the realm together through the drought years with his bare hands and his own treasury. I watched him be, without competition, the finest man I have known in a century and a half of knowing men."

"And then?" Rafael said. Softly. They already knew the shape of the answer; you could hear it in the way they asked. People always ask softly when they already know.

"And then came the crown," I said.

* * *

"It was reforged for his coronation. Did you know that? The old crown of Corvenna had been damaged—cracked, they said, in some accident of storage, though I have my suspicions about the storage, and the suspicions involve a chancellor with cold eyes whose name I will give you eventually, when it's useful. So they reforged it. Melted the old gold down and remade it, and brought in a smith from the eastern mountains, and there was a ceremony, and everyone agreed the new crown was beautiful. It was beautiful. That was the first warning. Things that old should look their age. A crown two hundred years old that comes out of the forge looking new is like a man of eighty with a baby's skin. Something has eaten the years and left the shape.

"I smelled it before I saw it. Under the gold-smell and the fire-smell there was another smell, and I want you to understand that I have hunted carrion in plague pits, and this was worse, because carrion is honest. This was old. Old the way deep water is old. It smelled of hunger that had been hungry so long it had forgotten there was ever anything else.

"I tried to warn them. Understand that. I stood in the antechamber while they carried the crown in on its cushion, and I barked, and I bit the master of ceremonies on the ankle, and I was dragged out by three footmen and locked in the mews, and Aldous laughed about it afterward—laughed!—and said, 'Quill disapproves of the new hat.' And I scratched the door of the mews until my claws split, and I howled, and no one listens to an animal on the day of a coronation, because a coronation is a day built entirely out of not listening.

"So they put it on his head. And I felt it happen. From across the palace, through the bond, I felt it—the way you feel a door slam somewhere in a house you love. Something went *in*. Through the gold, through the scalp, down along the spine of him, into the place where a man keeps himself. It didn't take him all at once. That's the part I need you to hear, Rafael, because you'll need it later, and because I need to say it to someone who won't flinch. It didn't take him all at once. It took a year. A year of small thefts. First it took his sleep, then his laughter—he still laughed, but late, a beat behind, like a man remembering the steps of a dance he used to know. Then it took his mercy, a spoonful at a time. I watched him sign the first edict against the gifted and I sat on his desk and screamed in his face and he looked at me with his father's eyes—his father's, not his—and said, 'Quill, off the documents.'

"That was the last thing he ever said to me. Off the documents. Eleven years of garden conversations, and the last thing."

The fire had burned low. Sera fed it a stick without being asked, which from Sera was practically an embrace.

"I did everything wrong," I said. "That's the confession, if you want it. I was bound to witness, not to act, and I hid behind that rule like a coward hides behind a wall. I told myself: the bond forbids interference, the forest made the law, who am I, a fox, to fight what even the forest seems to permit? And meanwhile the thing in the crown grew fat and patient and started building its bells." I looked at Rafael across the flames. "Do you know why the bells ring, in Corvenna? Everyone thinks it's piety. It isn't. It's noise. Constant, deliberate, holy-sounding noise, poured over the city day and night, so that no beast-speaker can hear clearly anywhere inside the walls. It built itself a muffler around the one kind of creature that might hear the king screaming underneath."

Rafael went white. I watched the color leave their face in the firelight, and I knew they'd understood before I said the rest, but I said it anyway, because it deserved saying plainly.

"Aldous is alive. Buried alive in his own body, behind his own eyes, and he has been screaming for a year, and I have heard every hour of it through the bond, and I could do nothing, because I am a witness, and witnessing is just another word for watching, and watching is just another word for failing slowly."

* * *

Silence, then. The good kind and the bad kind mixed together, the way they usually are around fires.

It was Sera who broke it, because Sera cannot abide silence the way other soldiers can't abide disorder. "Why us?" she said. "Why now? If you've been carrying this for a year, why bring it to a goatherd and a disgraced knight instead of an army?"

"Because armies are what it eats," I said. "It feeds on the gifted, yes—but it feeds best on power. Give it a knight's oath, a mage's art, a king's authority, and it drinks them down like spring water. No. What unbinds a thing like that isn't force. It's the binding song, and the binding song doesn't answer to swords." I turned back to Rafael. "It answers to beast-speech. To the oldest language there is. And you, goatherd, are the first beast-speaker I have found in a year of searching who crossed the valley line alive, sane, and stubborn enough to keep walking."

"You said the valley stones were failing," Rafael said. "What did you mean?"

"I meant that the Ember Valley was built as a vault, long ago, by people who wanted to hide something from magic—and I suspect, now, that what they were hiding was a person like you, or a line of them, or perhaps a weapon. The wards are old and they are dying, and things are leaking through from both sides. Your gift leaked out. My voice leaked in. And the hunters' lists leaked toward you." I let that settle. "Your sanctuary was never a sanctuary, Rafael. It was a shelf. And shelves are where you keep things until you need them."

They didn't answer that. They put another branch on the fire instead, and watched it catch, and I let them have the quiet, because I remembered what it was to learn that the walls of your world had a door in them all along.

But Sera leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her shadow fell long across the camp. "Say the rest," she said. "There's a rest. You've got the face of a fox with a bone still buried."

"There is a rest," I admitted. "Two parts. First: the thirteenth moon. The spirit's grip thins when the moon makes its thirteenth turn—old lore, older than the kingdom, and true, or true enough. Four months from now, give or take a night. On that night, in that place, sung by the right voice, the binding song can pull the thing out of the king like a splinter from a thumb. Second part." I hesitated, and hated myself for hesitating, and said it anyway. "The right voice must be one it cannot devour. And I don't yet know if that's you. I believe it might be. Belief, in my experience, is what hope calls itself when it's trying to get you killed."

"And if it can devour me?" Rafael asked. Simply. As if asking whether it might rain.

"Then we'll know by the screaming," I said, "and I'll bury what's left, and I have buried kings before, and I am, if nothing else, practiced."

Sera made a sound that was almost a laugh and entirely disgust. But Rafael—Rafael looked into the fire for a long moment, and then at me, and what was in their face wasn't fear. It was the other thing. The thing I'd seen once before, in a young man kneeling on cold marble with a crown coming down, the thing I had spent a year mourning because I thought I'd never see it again.

"All right," Rafael said. "Then teach me the song."

And far off to the east, past the sleeping hills, the bells of Corvenna rang the midnight hour—faint, at that distance, more felt than heard—and through the bond, thin as thread, I heard the thing beneath them pause.

Just for a moment. Just the way a diner pauses when he fancies, somewhere in the taste of his soup, an ingredient he wasn't expecting.

It had heard the word *song*, carried on a goatherd's breath, sixty miles away.

It was already listening.
