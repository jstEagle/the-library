## 9. The First Thirteenth Moon

I have killed men for less than what the fox said that night, and I have believed worse things from better liars, so I suppose it was inevitable that I would end up sitting on a cold log, turning a stick in the fire, explaining the end of the world to a goat-herder and a talking fox as though I were reciting a supply inventory.

That's the soldier's curse. When the truth is unbearable, you make it boring. You flatten it into lists. You pretend that if you say it in the right order, calmly enough, it won't reach inside you and take hold of something soft.

It doesn't work. It never works. But you do it anyway.

"Start at the beginning," Rafael said.

They had built the fire small — Rafael always built fires small, a habit of people who have spent their lives being overlooked and intend to keep it that way — and Quill sat just outside its light, because foxes are creatures of edges and he seemed to find comfort in never being fully claimed by anything, including warmth.

"The beginning," I said, "is a hundred years ago. Before Aldous. Before his father. Back when the crown was just a hat with delusions."

Quill made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter had been run through a woodchipper. I ignored him.

"There was a king then too. Aenric, they called him. And there was a thing beneath the mountain at Corvenna's back — old, hungry, patient. The histories call it a spirit. The priests called it a devil. The beast-speakers—" I glanced at Rafael, whose hands had gone very still around their cup, "—called it by name, which is more than anyone else dared."

"And someone bound it," Rafael said quietly.

"A beast-speaker did. One person. Alone." I turned the stick in the fire. "The song cost her everything. That part isn't in the histories — the histories prefer their heroes tidy — but the old soldiers' lore keeps it. My grandmother sang me fragments of it as a lullaby, which tells you something about my childhood. The binding held for a century. And bindings, like all things, wear thin."

"And when it wore thin," Quill said from the dark, "it went looking for a door."

"It found one," I said. "A crown is a door. Gold shaped into a circle, set on the head of a man who rules by divine right — there's no stronger invitation in the world for a thing that wants to be worshipped. It crawled into Aldous through the crown a year ago, and it has been wearing him ever since."

Rafael set down the cup. "The proclamation. The census. The hunt."

"Yes."

"That's not hunting," Rafael said, and their voice changed — flattened, careful, the way voices go when someone is walking toward an idea they don't want to reach. "Hunting is fear. Fear wants the gifted dead or driven out. But the decree didn't say *drive them out*. It said *deliver them to the capital*. Alive. Unharmed. There are rewards for denunciation but double rewards for live capture. Why would a king who hates magic want us breathing?"

I let the silence do some of the work. It's better at cruelty than I am.

"Sera," Rafael said. "Why?"

"Because a spirit is a fire," I said. "And a fire needs feeding. You've seen what a year of wearing a king has done — the bells, the decrees, the hunger growing louder. It eats the gifted. Their gift, specifically. Their magic. That's what sustains it, what keeps the binding from reasserting itself, what keeps it fat and strong inside stolen flesh. Every gifted soul delivered to Corvenna is a meal."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the slope, a night bird called and got no answer.

"Marta's son," Rafael said.

I didn't ask who Marta was. I'd heard the name once already, on the second night, spoken into a bedroll in the dark, and I knew better than to make people repeat their wounds.

"The boy with fire in his hands," I said. "Yes. He's in Corvenna now, or he will be soon. They keep them somewhere under the palace. Feeding them to it slowly, I'd guess — a whole gift lasts longer than a drained one, and Vhalor—"

"You said its name," Quill said sharply.

"I'm saying it so we all know what we're dealing with. Names are power only if you're afraid to use them." I looked at the fox. He looked back with those amber coin-eyes, unimpressed. "Vhalor. The Hunger Beneath. There. Now it knows we know, and it can lose sleep over it, if spirits sleep."

"They don't," Quill said. "I've watched it not sleep for a year."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't end. You don't serve a term and come out clean. You carry it the way you carry an old wound in winter — fine until the weather turns, and then it aches in places you'd sworn had healed.

My disgrace has a name too. Her name was Tam. She was eight years old, and she could make flowers grow in her footprints, and the order came down in Aldous's — in *its* — voice, in the third month of the new reign: all gifted within the garrison's jurisdiction to be taken alive and conveyed to the capital.

I had commanded hunters for six years. Good ones. Loyal ones. Men and women who'd followed me into floods and fevers and two border wars, who trusted me the way soldiers trust — completely, stupidly, with their lives folded into mine like letters into envelopes. And I stood in that muddy yard with the order in my hand and Tam crying behind her mother's skirts, and I said no.

Not eloquently. Not heroically. I said it small, actually — a single word, barely above the wind — and then I said it again, louder, and by the third time it had become the only word I had left.

They stripped my rank in front of the same yard. Took my badge, my colors, my right to wear the grey. Let me keep my sword, which was either mercy or an insult; I've never decided which. And then I walked out past my own hunters, every one of them looking anywhere but at me, except young Corporal Denny, who saluted, the absolute fool, and got a week's latrine duty for it.

So when I tell you I know how the hunt works, understand that I don't know it from a book. I built it. Its roads, its checkpoints, its bounty boards, its little economy of neighbors selling neighbors — I designed half of it myself, back when I believed the crown served the realm instead of feeding on it. Every trap we'll meet on the road to Corvenna, I have either sprung or set.

Which is why, when Rafael asked me that night, "How far is it? Truly?" — I gave them the honest answer, and the honest answer was ugly.

"Four hundred leagues," I said. "Through three checkpoints belts, two mountain passes that close with the first snow, and the Thornwood, where the crown's rangers shoot first and fill out paperwork never. On foot, with no papers, no colors, and a bounty notice that will eventually describe all three of us — four, counting the fox, though gods help whoever tries to write his description — four months if nothing goes wrong."

"And something will go wrong," Rafael said.

"Something goes wrong every day. The trick is choosing which wrong things you can afford."

Quill trotted a slow circle around the fire, tail low, thinking. Foxes think with their whole bodies; it's disconcerting. "There's another problem," he said. "The moon."

"The moon?"

"The thirteenth turn." He stopped at the fire's edge. "Bindings thin on a cycle. The old binding on Vhalor was woven into time itself — that's why it lasted a century. It loosens once every thirteen months, on the thirteenth turn of the moon, when the count runs out and begins again. For one night, the spirit's grip on its anchor — the crown, the body, whatever it's holding — slackens. That's the night it can be unbound. Any other night, you might as well sing at a stone wall."

"How long?" Rafael asked.

"Three nights past the last full moon, the count began again. Which means—" Quill's ears flattened, doing arithmetic he clearly hated, "—the next thirteenth turn is four months away. Nearly to the day."

"Four months," I repeated. Four hundred leagues in four months, through a kingdom turned into a snare, carrying the one kind of passenger every hunter in the realm is paid to find. I laughed, and it came out wrong, and I stopped. "Good. I was worried it would be impossible."

"Sera." Rafael's voice was gentle in the way that annoyed me most — gentle like they pitied me, gentle like they thought I needed protecting, which was absurd, I was the one with the sword. "You don't have to come."

"No," I agreed. "I don't."

I poked the fire harder than the fire deserved.

"But the hunt is mine," I said. "I mean that literally. I drew its maps. I trained its officers. Every mile of it is a debt with my signature on it, and I am going to walk the length of it until I've paid. Besides—" I nodded at Rafael, at the fox, at the ridiculous shape our company had already become, "—you'll die without me. Both of you. Within the week. Probably sooner, knowing you."

"That's fair," Rafael admitted.

"It's not a compliment."

"I know."

* * *

Rafael didn't sleep that night. Neither did I, but I pretended to, because pretending is most of soldiering, and Rafael — well. Rafael sat up past the coals with their knees drawn up, watching the dark the way people watch a door they're afraid someone will come through.

Near dawn, they spoke without preamble, the way people do when they've been rehearsing a sentence for hours and finally surrender to it.

"The harvest," they said. "That's what it is. All of it. The census, the bounty boards, Marta's neighbors lighting candles in their windows to show they're loyal — it's not persecution. Persecution wastes what it destroys. This is a *larder*. The king is filling his pantry, and everyone in the valley lit the way for the cart and called it duty."

"Yes," I said.

"My ewe — the lame one, Brindle — she asked me once why I never sang to her properly. Full voice. I told her songs attract wolves." Rafael's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I spent twenty-three years teaching myself to be quiet. Quiet hands, quiet eyes, quiet gift. And the whole time, the quiet was the point. Not safety. *Fattening.* Things kept in the dark grow tender."

I should have said something comforting. I'm told normal people have comforting things stored away for moments like this, little cushions of phrase. I've never owned any. What I said instead was:

"Then stop being quiet."

Rafael looked at me across the dead fire, and in the grey pre-dawn light their face did something complicated — fear and fury and something underneath both that I recognized because I'd worn it myself in a muddy yard two years ago, facing my own hunters with my badge in the dirt.

"Purpose," I said, because apparently I'd decided to be eloquent after all, at the worst possible hour. "Dread sits in your chest like a stone. Purpose takes the same stone and puts it in your hand. Same weight. Different work. You get to choose which one you carry."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"Then you choose again tomorrow. That's the whole secret of it. Nobody gets it right once and forever. You just keep choosing until the choosing becomes you."

Quill chose that moment to stand up from the dark, shake himself thoroughly, and announce, in the tone of a creature who has been listening to human nonsense all night and has reached his limit:

"The border stones are half a day behind us and the census riders are half a day ahead. If you two have finished your philosophy, I suggest we discuss which wrong things we can afford."

Rafael stood. Brushed off their knees. And when they spoke, the flat carefulness was gone from their voice, and what remained was quieter and much more dangerous.

"The Thornwood first," they said. "You said the rangers shoot first. So we go through it before they expect anyone mad enough to try. And Sera — when we reach Corvenna, I want to see where they keep them. The larder. I want to see it before the thirteenth moon."

"Why?"

"Because Marta's son is in there," Rafael said, "and I intend to be the wolf."

We broke camp before sunrise. Three days later, at the treeline of the Thornwood, we found the first bounty board — fresh paper, new ink, and a charcoal sketch that was unmistakably, damningly, a goat-herder with kind eyes and a price on their head.

Rafael looked at their own face nailed to a post for the first time in their life, and smiled.

It was not a comforting smile.
