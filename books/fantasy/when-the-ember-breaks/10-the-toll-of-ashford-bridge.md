## 10. The Toll of Ashford Bridge

### SERA

I had crossed Ashford Bridge eleven times in my life, and ten of those crossings had been boring. That was the thing about checkpoints nobody tells you when you're young and stupid and wearing a shiny badge: they are almost always boring. Men get bored. Bored men stop looking at faces and start looking at their boots.

The eleventh crossing was not boring.

From a hundred paces out, I could read the toll like a ledger. Four hunters in crown gray, one bored to the point of slouching, two doing it properly, and one — the tall one with the hound — doing it the way I used to do it, which is to say he was actually paying attention. The hound was a black-and-tan, heavy through the chest, the kind they breed in the northern kennels for scenting *something* rather than someone. Useful dogs. Honest dogs. I'd handled three of them in my captaincy and liked them better than most of my men.

"Rules," I said, not turning my head. "You're my mute servant from the fen country. You don't talk, you don't look at anyone's eyes, you keep your hands busy. Mute servants stare at their hands. It's the whole tragedy of the profession."

Beside me, Rafael nodded. They'd gotten better at this over three weeks of road — the downcast eyes, the shuffling gait, the way of making themselves smaller than a person had any right to be. Too good at it, sometimes. It turned something in my stomach to watch them practice being nothing.

Quill had gone ahead an hour ago, trotting across the bridge in broad daylight like any fox with business on the far bank. Nobody looks twice at a fox. That is the great advantage of being a fox, though Quill would tell you the disadvantages at length if you gave him an opening.

The bored hunter waved us toward the table. The attentive one didn't move. He just stood there with his hand resting on the hound's ruff, watching us come, and I felt the old cold walk up my spine and introduce itself.

"Toll's two coppers," said the bored one. "Business?"

"Going to my sister's," I said. "Past Corvenna."

He waved us on. And that should have been that. Two coppers, a wave, eleven boring crossings out of twelve.

Then the hound lifted its head.

I saw it happen the way you see weather coming. The dog's nose swung toward Rafael, caught, and held. Its ears came forward. It made a sound low in its chest, not quite a bark, not quite a question, and the tall hunter's hand tightened on its collar, and his eyes went from the dog to Rafael with the particular brightness of a man whose week has just improved enormously.

"Hold there," he said.

### RAFAEL

Here is what nobody understands about hearing animals speak: it isn't words. Not usually. It's more like standing next to a river and realizing the river has opinions.

The hound's voice hit me before its handler finished saying *hold there*. It came pouring across the checkpoint like water finding a crack — and it wasn't threat. That's what nearly undid me, right there in front of four crown hunters and God knew who else. It wasn't *intruder* or *prey*. It was confusion, and hurt, and a kind of desperate earnestness that went straight through my ribs:

*Wrong. You smell wrong. Not wrong-bad. Wrong-wrong. Like home but not. Like the den but outside it. Why do you smell like the den?*

The den. It meant me. My gift, my — whatever this thing was that lived under my skin and apparently smelled like every listening creature's oldest memory. Twenty-three years of burying it in the valley where magic couldn't follow, and the moment I stepped into the wide world, every animal within scenting distance could tell what I was by smell alone.

And this dog wanted an answer. That was the worst part. It wasn't accusing me. It was *asking*, tail giving one uncertain sweep, because as far as the hound knew, its handler's commands were the shape of the world, and I did not fit the shape, and that frightened it.

"Where are you from?" the tall hunter asked. His voice was pleasant. Pleasant voices were worse.

"The fen country," Sera said, flat and bored, the accent she'd been building all week sitting perfectly on her tongue.

"Mm." He let the hound's collar slacken a hand's width. The dog strained toward me, nose working. "Ginger here's got a nose for a certain kind of vermin. Crown pays well for what she finds." He smiled at Sera, then at me, and his smile had the warmth of a hook. "Open your mouth."

My heart went so loud I was sure everyone could hear it. *Open your mouth.* The mute-servant gambit, dead in the water, because a mute servant can't prove they're mute except by staying silent, and silence now looked exactly like guilt.

Sera shifted her weight. I'd learned her shifts by then — that one meant she was calculating distances and how many bodies stood between her and the bridge rail, and that she didn't love the arithmetic.

And the hound took another step toward me and said, closer now, its voice filling my skull until the hunter's questions sounded like they came from underwater:

*I'm sorry. I have to tell him. I don't want to tell him. Last time I told him about the fire-handed boy he gave me liver and the boy screamed and I still dream about the screaming. But he feeds me and he says sit and I sit. Why do you smell like the den?*

Something in my chest broke loose.

I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now: I had spent twenty-three years using my gift the way you use a candle in a cellar — small, careful, mostly for myself. A word to the ewe. A comfort to a foal. Nothing that mattered. Nothing anyone could see. Hiding had been the whole architecture of my life, and hiding does something to a person. It makes every instinct into a lock.

But the dog was afraid, and it didn't want to do what it was going to do, and it had dreamed about a screaming boy, and I thought of Marta's son with fire in his palms being dragged past my window while I kept my face very still behind the shutter.

"No," I said — not aloud.

I spoke the way the river speaks. I let my whole self lean toward the hound, and I answered its question.

*Not wrong,* I told it. *Different. You're right — I'm like the den. I'm like the den and the pack and everything with ears. Your nose is true. Your nose has always been true.*

The hound stopped straining. Its tail went still. In the world of men, perhaps half a second had passed; between us, something long and slow had happened, the way it happens between animals.

*True?* it said, and there was such naked hope in it that my throat closed. Nobody had ever told this animal its nose was true. I understood that immediately, the way you understand things about creatures, whole histories arriving in a single syllable. Its handler praised its *finds*. Nobody had ever asked what it found like.

*What happens,* I asked it carefully, *when you tell him about me?*

*Liver,* said the hound. Then, after a pause in which a dog's entire moral universe passed across its dark eyes: *The boy screamed.*

*Yes,* I said gently. *And what happens to you, after?*

It didn't answer. It didn't have to. Dogs don't lie to themselves; it's one of the many ways they're better than us.

Behind me I heard the hunter say, sharper now, "I said open your mouth. Or I'll have Ginger here sniff you proper, and if you're clean you've got nothing to—"

*Your handler,* I said to the hound, *is going to make you find people. People like the boy. People who scream. And you will get liver, and you will dream about screaming, until the dreams eat the liver and there's nothing left but the dreams.* I let the truth of it settle, soft as snowfall. *Or you could sit down. Just sit. Sit and be confused. Confused dogs can't point. Even he knows that.*

The hound sat.

It sat the way soldiers fall — abruptly, all at once, as if its legs had been waiting years for permission. It looked up at its handler with its head cocked, wearing the expression dogs have perfected across ten thousand years of domestic partnership: the expression that says *I have no idea what you want and I am deeply sorry about it.*

"Ginger?" The hunter's pleasantness cracked down the middle. He yanked the lead. "Ginger, *seek!*"

The hound sneezed. It was, I am fairly certain, a deliberate sneeze. It scratched its ear with tremendous thoroughness. It lay down, put its chin on its paws, and gazed off at the middle distance with the serene vacancy of a dog contemplating absolutely nothing, ever, in its life.

"She's off today," the hunter snarled, hauling at the lead. "Heat, probably. Get up, you useless—"

"Sir." The bored hunter had straightened off his slouch, and there was something in his voice I recognized — the wariness of a man who has worked with dogs and trusts them more than officers. "She doesn't false-alert. If she won't point—"

"If she won't—" The tall one's eyes came back to me, and now they weren't bright, they were narrow, and I watched the arithmetic turn over behind them: *mute servant, wrong-smelling servant, dog that refuses.* "Search them," he said to the others. "Both of them."

Sera moved.

I will spend the rest of my life admiring how fast she moved. One moment she was a tired traveler with a fen-country accent; the next her elbow had "slipped" into the toll table and sent the ink bottle spinning across the ledger, and she was swearing in three dialects and grabbing the collector's sleeve to steady him, apologizing with such fluent, furious incompetence that all three hunters' attention snapped to the spreading black puddle and the ruined census records and the woman loudly ruining her own trousers kneeling in the mess.

It bought seconds. Seconds were all we needed, but only because of what I did next.

Because the mule. There was always a mule at these places, packed with the checkpoint's supplies, standing in the shade of the toll house. And the mule had been watching me since we arrived with the dawning interest of an animal that smells the den, and I opened the last door in my mind and spoke to it plainly, the way I'd spoken to goats for twenty-three years, except not plainly at all, because I'd never once asked an animal to help me lie.

*Friend,* I said. *Your lead rope is tied to that post. It would be very funny — no harm, no anger, just funny — if you walked away from the post right now, dragging your rope, all the way down the road, as fast as a mule can go without being blamed for it.*

Mules, I discovered, have a magnificent sense of humor and no loyalty whatsoever to crown checkpoints.

The rope snapped taut, the post groaned, the tally bell above the toll house began ringing wildly as the mule clattered through it, and every hunter in the place turned to watch forty pounds of stubborn equine opinion drag government property down the Corvenna road. The tall one shouted. The bored one ran. Sera grabbed my sleeve and we walked — *walked*, gods help us, she made us *walk*, which I maintain to this day was the hardest thing either of us did that morning — across the rest of Ashford Bridge with our backs prickling, past the bounty board with its fresh-pasted notices, past the fourth hunter who never even looked up from his lunch.

At the far end, in the shade of an alder, a red fox sat washing one paw, wearing the smug expression foxes reserve for humans who have finally done something interesting.

"*Walk*," Sera murmured through her teeth. "Do not run. Running is a confession."

"I'm aware."

"You spoke to it." Her voice stayed low and even, which somehow made it worse. "The hound. I watched your face. I've seen beast-speakers work before, Rafael. Handlers use touch and whistle and it takes minutes. What you did took a breath."

I said nothing. There was a hole in my chest where the hiding used to be, and wind was coming through it, and I didn't yet know if the wind felt like freedom or like loss.

"You turned a crown hound against its handler," she went on, "in front of a checkpoint, to save your own skin. Which I'm grateful for. And which, if one other soul saw it, gets us both a cell and then a pyre." She glanced sideways at me, and beneath the soldier's iron in her eyes there was something else, something almost wounded. "I need to know which one you are. The person who hid for twenty-three years, or the person who does things like that. Because they can't both fit in one body on this road."

"I don't know," I said honestly.

She exhaled through her nose. "That," she said finally, "is the first useful answer you've given me in three weeks."

### SERA

We made six miles before I called a halt, and I chose the spot with a professional's paranoia: high ground, two exits, a stream Rafael could pretend to drink from while actually eavesdropping on the fish, which I tried very hard not to think about too closely.

Quill arrived at dusk, trotted into camp, and delivered his report lying on his back with all four feet in the air, which I had learned was his version of lounging and also, possibly, a personal insult aimed at me specifically.

"Checkpoint's in an uproar," he said. "They think the mule was trained by rebels. There's a wager pool. Also your description is on the board already — 'woman, soldierly bearing, traveling with a mute servant of suspicious odor.'"

"Suspicious *odor*?"

"I may have embellished. For balance." He rolled upright and fixed Rafael with those amber eyes. "The hound, though. That's the part worth discussing. Black-and-tans are kennel-trained against beast-speech. There's a sound they play to the pups, a recording of it, and it frightens them so badly they soil themselves. This one didn't flee. It sat down. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I'm very persuasive," Rafael said, in the light joking voice they used whenever anything important approached, and I had catalogued that voice weeks ago.

"It means," Quill said, "that whatever you are, you're more than a parlor trick. And the crown will reach the same conclusion the moment that handler files his report, which he'll do tonight, drunk and furious. *Hound refused. Servant suspected. Recommend inquiry.* Words like that travel to Corvenna faster than we do." His tail flicked. "We move at first light. We stop sleeping near roads entirely."

Rafael stared into the fire. I watched them carry something heavy behind their eyes — I was getting good at reading that face, which was its own complication I refused to examine — and finally they said, quietly, "The dog dreamed about the boy. The fire-handed boy. It remembered the screaming." A pause. "Nobody ever told it its nose was true. Twenty years of service and nobody ever — " They stopped. Pressed their knuckles to their mouth.

I should have said something practical. *Feelings later, miles now* — that was the speech I'd prepared, the captain's speech, the one that keeps people alive. Instead I heard myself say, "You did a kind thing back there. And a reckless one. On this road those are usually the same thing, and you'll have to learn which moments are which, because I can't teach you that. Nobody can."

Rafael looked up at me across the fire, and for a moment neither of us performed anything. No mute servant, no disgraced knight, no careful distance. Just two people who had each been thrown away by the world they'd served, warming the same terrible fire.

Then Quill's head snapped toward the road, ears forward, and every hair on my arms stood up.

"We have a follower," the fox said. "Small. On foot. Been trailing us since the bridge — badly, I might add, like a hedgehog trying to shadow a hawk." His eyes gleamed. "It's a child."

I was on my feet with a knife in my hand before he finished the sentence, because in a kingdom running a hunt for the gifted, a child following travelers away from a crown checkpoint is either bait or a witness, and I had personally watched both kinds die.

Rafael stood too. They weren't looking at the road.

They were looking at the culvert below the old mill lane — at the exact spot, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the night air, where a pair of small dirty feet had shown themselves for half a second that afternoon, tucked under the bridge's stone lip while two crown hunters searched a mute servant twenty feet away.

Half a second. I'd missed it. The fox had missed it.

Rafael hadn't.

"They were under the bridge," Rafael said softly, to no one, to all of us. "They saw everything."
