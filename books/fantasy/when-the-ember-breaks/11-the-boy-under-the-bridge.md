## 11. The Boy Under the Bridge

He caught up to us at dusk on the third day, which was two days longer than I'd expected and one day longer than I'd hoped.

I had been hoping he'd give up. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger the gods keep of my small hypocrisies: I hoped the boy would turn around, find a warm kitchen somewhere, and forget he'd ever seen a woman talk a war-hound into sitting down like a scolded puppy.

Instead he came stumbling out of the hedgerow like something the hedge had rejected, all elbows and mud, with a satchel bouncing against his back and an expression of ferocious determination that would have been more impressive if his chin weren't trembling.

"You walk fast," he accused, as though we had personally conspired against him.

"We walk *away*," Sera said, without breaking stride. "There's a difference, and it's the whole point."

"I'm coming with you."

"No," Sera and I said together. We glanced at each other. It was becoming a habit, agreeing at the worst possible moments.

The boy planted himself in the middle of the road, arms crossed, feet wide, the way children do when they've decided that geography itself can be argued with. "You'll have to make me go back."

Sera looked at him the way she looked at broken fences and bad intelligence—assessing, unimpressed, already calculating costs. "Where's back?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

"Then you're wasting your time," he said, "because there's nothing there."

Quill, trotting at my heel, made a low sound in his chest that I had learned by then to translate as *oh, this one's going to be trouble*. Foxes have a whole vocabulary of foreboding. Most of it is about chickens, but some of it is about people.

* * *

His name was Pip, and he was eleven years old, or twelve, or possibly thirteen depending on which answer he gave to whom—a flexibility about his own history that Sera noted aloud with the flat approval of a former soldier recognizing a useful skill.

We let him walk with us as far as the crossroads because it was getting dark and even Sera has limits on how far she'll throw a child out of camp. He talked the entire way. Not nervously—there was nothing nervous about him—but constantly, the way water fills every crack in a wall. He told us about the bridge, and the hound, and the checkpoint, in the tone of a man recounting a battle he'd survived through superior tactics rather than luck.

"I saw the whole thing," he said. "Under the bridge. There's a gap where the stones fell out, big enough for me and my bag. I've slept worse places." He shot a look at Sera, gauging whether this was the kind of thing that impressed her. It wasn't. He pressed on. "The hound stopped. Right in front of the officer. And it just—*sat*. Like someone had asked it nicely. And the officer got so angry, and you—" this to me, with the naked curiosity of a child who hasn't yet learned curiosity is dangerous, "—you didn't say anything. Your mouth didn't move. But the dog heard you anyway."

"The dog was tired," I said.

"Dogs don't sit for tired."

"They do if they're very tired."

"That's not—" He stopped walking. In the middle of the road, at dusk, with bounty boards nailed to trees a mile behind us, he stopped walking and stared at me with an expression I recognized from the inside. It was the expression of someone who has spent their whole life thinking they were the only strange thing in the world, and has just seen proof otherwise. "You're one too," he whispered. "Aren't you. You're *gifted*."

"Pip—"

"I knew it. I *knew* it. The way you looked at the horses. The way Quill follows you like—" He stopped again, recalculating. "Wait. Does the fox talk? Does the fox *talk to you*?"

"The fox talks to everyone," Quill said. "It's called conversation. You should try listening sometime; it pairs well."

Pip made a sound like a kettle achieving its life's purpose.

Sera closed her eyes briefly, the way she did when she was praying to a god she no longer believed in for patience she no longer possessed. "Keep your voice down."

"He *talks*—"

"*Down*, boy."

And Pip—credit where it's due—went down. He clamped both hands over his own mouth and stood there vibrating, eyes enormous above his fingers, and I thought: *oh no.* Because I knew that look. I had worn that look for twenty-three years. It was the look of a person who has just discovered that the world contains one other person who speaks their secret language, and who will follow that person off the edge of the earth rather than lose them.

I had done exactly that, three weeks ago, to a fox.

* * *

We made camp in a hollow under a stand of alders, close enough to the road to hear the occasional cart but far enough that firelight wouldn't show. Sera built the fire small and screened, out of habit, and cooked the rabbit Quill had bullied out of the bracken. Pip ate like the food might be reclaimed at any moment—one arm curled around his bowl, shoulders hunched, watching all three of us between bites.

I remembered eating like that. I remembered being eight years old in Marta's kitchen, taking bread from her hand while making sure my fingers never quite touched hers, because touch invited questions and questions invited answers and answers, in the valley, meant the pyre. Even in the Ember Valley—even in the one place magic couldn't reach—I had eaten like a thief at my own table.

"So," Sera said, in the voice she used for interrogations she wanted to seem casual. "Your family. Where are they?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Just gone." Pip shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "It's just me now."

"How long?"

"Awhile."

"Pip."

"Eight months," he said, to his bowl. "Since the mill broke."

I set down my cup. "The mill?"

"Our village mill. The wheel axle snapped—cracked clean through, and there's no smith within three days' walk, and we'd have starved before spring, everyone knows what a broken mill means, the whole harvest sitting there going rotten." He said it fast, rehearsed, the story of a thing he'd told himself many times until it wore smooth. "So I fixed it. Put my hands on the axle and it just—it knitted. Like the metal remembered what it was supposed to be. And the mill turned, and everyone ate, and then old Harl looked at me funny, and his wife looked at me funnier, and by morning there were men from the reeve's house asking questions, so I left." He lifted one shoulder. "That's all. That's the whole crime. I fixed a mill."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope, an owl asked its question of the dark and got no answer.

"And your parents?" Sera asked gently, which for Sera meant she dropped perhaps ten percent of the iron from her voice.

Pip's jaw worked. "Da said I was a devil's trick. Ma cried. Then Da said maybe the crown would pay for news of me, and I thought—I thought it was better to be worth money to them gone than worth shame to them staying." He looked up, defiant, daring us to pity him. "So don't ask again. That's the whole story. Fixed mill, lost family, end of chapter."

*Fixed mill, lost family.* Eight months old, and he'd already learned to file his own heartbreak under headings, like a clerk. I felt something in my chest crack along a seam I'd thought was long since scarred over.

Because here is what I hadn't told anyone—not Sera, not Quill, not the ewe whose lame leg I'd mended with words alone for six years: I had run too. At nineteen, when the gift first started leaking past the valley's dead border, when I could suddenly hear horses begging on the trade road half a mile outside the stones, I had packed a bag and gotten as far as the border marker and stood there for a full day, and then I had gone home. Because home was dull and safe and suffocating, but it was *home*, and I had a mother who loved me clumsily and neighbors who'd have burned me and a ewe named Sorrel who needed me, and I chose it. I chose the smaller life. I chose it every single day for four more years, until the choice was taken from me.

This boy had stood at his own border marker at eleven years old and walked through it alone, in winter, with a satchel and a talent the crown was paying silver to report.

And I had been lying awake for three nights planning how to send him back to it.

* * *

Later, when Sera took first watch and Pip finally slept—he went down mid-sentence, mid-word almost, the way children and soldiers sleep, all at once and without dignity—I sat by the low fire and Quill settled beside me with the particular weight of a fox who intends to have a conversation.

"You're going to tell him no," Quill said.

"I'm going to tell him yes-no. I'm going to tell him there's a town south of here, a day's walk, with a widow who takes in laundry girls and stable boys. I saw the notice board. She pays in soup."

"Mm."

"He's eleven."

"He says twelve."

"He's eleven. I know eleven when I see it; I was eleven for years, I'm an expert." I poked the fire harder than it deserved. "He goes to the widow. He mends her pots, quietly, with his sleeves pulled down. He grows up. Nobody burns him."

Quill watched me with those amber coin-eyes that always made me feel audited. "And you grew up quietly with your sleeves pulled down. How did that suit you?"

"That's different."

"Is it."

"I had a valley. I had walls. Magic can't cross the valley line—the gift sleeps there, it's safe, it's the one place in the world—"

"—where you spent twenty-three years talking to goats and pretending you were ordinary," Quill finished. "Yes. I've heard the brochure. Tell me, Rafael, in all those safe years, how many friends did you have? Real ones. Ones who knew you."

The fire cracked. I didn't answer.

"There it is," Quill said softly. "You want to save him from the road. But what you'd actually be saving him from is *you*. From being known. Because being known is the dangerous thing, isn't it? Not the hunters. Hunters only come for people who've been seen. And you were seen once, tonight, by a boy under a bridge—and instead of running, he followed you. Do you know why?"

"Because he's a fool."

"Because he's lonely," Quill said. "Lonely the way you were lonely. The way the king was lonely, before the crown. Loneliness walks toward any voice that sounds like its own. You of all people should recognize the animal."

I looked across the hollow at Pip, a small mound of borrowed blanket, one hand even in sleep curled protectively around his satchel. Inside it, I happened to know, were three chipped cups and a wooden bird with a broken wing—all things he'd mended on the road, all things he couldn't fix entirely, kept anyway. A boy who carried broken things because fixing them was the only part of himself anyone had ever punished.

"I'm not his father," I said.

"No," Quill agreed. "Worse. You're his mirror. And mirrors don't get to look away."

* * *

In the morning I tried anyway. I want that in the ledger too.

I sat him down on a fallen log with a bowl of porridge and laid it out plainly, the way you'd explain weather to a child: the road ahead was hard, the hunt was real, the company was hunted, and there was a place—a good place, a safe place—half a day south. I even had the shape of the lie ready: *we'll take you to the edge ourselves, we'll make sure you arrive.*

Pip listened to the whole speech with his spoon suspended, wearing the patient face of someone letting a fool finish.

Then he set down the bowl, reached into his satchel, and brought out the wooden bird. Its wing had been shattered—snapped in four places, the kind of break that means firewood to any sensible person. He held it out to me.

"Look at it," he said.

I looked. The wing was whole. Not new—there were pale seams in the grain where the breaks had been, thin lines like old scars—but whole. Strong. It would fly.

"I found it on the road the second day," he said. "Somebody's toy, thrown away. And I held it, and it fixed, and I thought—" His voice did something complicated. "Everyone tells me the gift is a curse. Da said it. The reeve's men said it, with ropes. The whole kingdom's saying it right now, on every board in every square: *gifted means dead.* But it fixed a bird. It fed a village. It's the only thing I've ever had that was *good*, and everybody wants me to bury it, and you—" he pointed the bird's scarred wing at me, and his chin had stopped trembling, "—you're the first person I ever met who *heard* it. Who heard what things say. So no. I'm not going south to hide in a widow's kitchen holding my hands still for the rest of my life. I'd rather die on the road being what I am than live in a ditch being nothing. And if you try to drag me south, I'll just follow you after. I followed a war-hound's tracks for two days. I can follow yours."

Silence. Even Quill had nothing, which from Quill was a standing ovation.

Sera, from across the cold camp where she was checking the girths, said without looking up: "He out-argued you."

"He out-argued me."

"Give it a year, he'll out-argue me." She swung up into the saddle, and I braced for her veto—for the soldier's arithmetic, the liability, the extra mouth, the child on a road that ended in pyres. Instead she looked down at Pip for a long moment and said, "Rules. You keep watch when told. You run when told. You lie when told, and you lie *well*. And if I say leave someone behind, you leave them, because dead heroes feed no one. Agree to that, and you walk with us."

Pip stuck out his hand with tremendous formality. Sera shook it, gravely, and I saw—only because I was learning to read her, the way I read everything—the corner of her mouth betray her.

"Welcome to the company," Quill sighed. "Gods help us all. We're four now. Four is a *committee*."

* * *

We walked. The road bent north toward the river country, and the morning was gray and clean, and Pip fell into step beside me and immediately began asking questions—about the fox, about the hound, about whether I could hear earthworms (I could not, and told him so, and he seemed to regard this as a personal failing of mine)—and I answered, and somewhere in the answering I realized my chest had stopped hurting.

That's the thing nobody tells you about exile. You spend years mourning the belonging you lost, and then one morning a ragged child falls into step beside you carrying a mended bird, and you understand that belonging was never a valley. It was never walls, or a chapel, or neighbors who'd have fed you to the fire while lending you salt. Belonging is being *heard*. It's as small as that and as large as that, and I had just spent twenty-three years starving in a room full of food.

"So," Pip said, around a mouthful of the bread Sera had grudgingly allotted him. "If the king's really dead, like the fox says—who's going to believe us? Who's going to *help* us? It's four of us. Against a whole kingdom."

"Four," Sera said, "and a fox."

"One very capable fox," Quill said.

I didn't answer. Because I was looking at the road ahead—at the dust hanging over the far bend, where something was moving. Riders. Six of them, maybe seven, moving fast, and beneath the wind I could hear the horses before I could see their colors, and the horses were saying a word I'd learned to dread in three short weeks on the open road.

They were saying *hunted*. They were saying *run*.

And they were saying it about a settlement—dozens of voices, frightened, hidden, downstream, in the marshes—of people I had not yet met, who were about to meet us whether they liked it or not.
