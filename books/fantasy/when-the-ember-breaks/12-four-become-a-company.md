## 12. Four Become a Company

I want it on the record that I objected.

Not loudly—loud objections get you killed on the road—but I said my piece by the fire the night after we took Pip in, while the boy slept like a dropped sack two feet away and Rafael sat across from me with that maddening, patient look they wear when they've already decided something and are merely waiting for me to catch up.

"He's eleven," I said. "He can't mend his way out of a checkpoint. Every mile he travels with us makes all of us slower and more visible."

"Every mile he travels alone makes him dead," Rafael said. "You saw his hands."

I had. Small hands, cracked knuckles, one finger crooked from an old break that had healed wrong because there was no one to mend it right. The irony of that had apparently escaped no one, least of all Pip, who'd held the crooked finger up when he asked to join us and said, "See? This is what happens when nobody finds me."

It was a good argument. I hated it.

"I'm not a nursemaid," I told Rafael. "I'm not anything anymore. That's rather the point of being disgraced."

"You're a soldier," Rafael said. "Disgrace is just a word for a soldier whose king was wrong."

I threw a pinecone at them. They caught it. Of course they did. Twenty-three years of hiding teaches a person reflexes like a cat's, and Rafael Almeida has the reflexes of a cat who has spent twenty-three years practicing.

So: Pip stayed. And because I am, at core, a fool with a sword, I decided that if the boy was going to be dead weight, I would make him less dead and less weight, and that is how I came to be standing in a frost-killed meadow at dawn teaching an eleven-year-old how to fall down properly.

* * *

"You're going to hit the ground your whole life," I told him. "Everyone does. Soldiers, farmers, kings. The only question is whether you do it on purpose or by accident. On purpose, you get up. By accident, sometimes you don't."

Pip looked at me the way children look at adults who say true things too plainly—with suspicion and a dawning, unwilling respect.

"That's grim," he said.

"That's Tuesday," I said. "Now fall over."

He fell over. Badly. Arms windmilling, chin tucked wrong, a graceless collapse like a puppet whose strings had been cut mid-dance. I made him do it again. And again. Forty times before breakfast, until the falling stopped being falling and became something controlled—a drop, a roll, a rise. His palms were raw. He didn't complain, which I noted, and which worried me more than complaining would have. Children who don't complain have learned that complaining costs them something.

"Again," I said.

"Why?" he burst out, finally, on the fiftieth fall. "I'm not going to fight hunters. You said so. You said running is the whole job."

"It is. But runners get caught, and caught people need ten seconds. Ten seconds between the hand on your collar and the knife at your throat. I'm going to teach you where those ten seconds live." I crouched to his level. "Eyes. Throat. Instep. Knee. You don't need to win a fight, Pip. You need to make winning expensive enough that the other fellow decides you're not worth it."

He thought about that with his whole face, the way he does—all of him leaning into the idea like a plant toward light.

"And then I run," he said.

"And then you run," I agreed. "Fastest thing you'll ever learn, and the only one I hope you never use."

We drilled every morning after that. Strikes with the heel of the palm—never the fist; a child's fist breaks against a man's jaw, but a palm driven up under the chin works on anyone. How to bite through a rope gag if it comes to that, which I taught him quietly and without demonstration, and which he received with a solemnity that made my chest hurt. How to throw sand. How to scream *fire* instead of *help*, because crowds come to watch fires and ignore cries for help—I learned that one in Corvenna, years ago, from a city guard who'd seen too much and drunk too much and told me the truth anyway.

Rafael watched these lessons from the edge of camp, arms crossed, expression unreadable. I expected disapproval. What I got, on the third morning, was worse: approval, delivered dry as tinder.

"You're enjoying this," they said.

"I'm training a liability into a survivor. Enjoyment hasn't entered into it."

"You smiled twice."

"The sun was in my eyes both times."

"Mm," Rafael said, in the tone of someone filing evidence away for later use, and went off to do whatever it is beast-speakers do at dawn, which turned out to be sitting very still in the wet grass having conversations with worms, presumably. I try not to think about it too hard.

* * *

The listening lessons were stranger, and I will admit—if only here, in the privacy of my own skull—that I eavesdropped on them shamelessly.

Rafael didn't start with animals. They started with Pip himself.

"Close your eyes," I heard them say, on a gray afternoon beside a stream where we'd halted to let Quill scout the road ahead. "What do you hear?"

"Water," Pip said. "Wind. Sera sharpening her sword."

"Good. Now listen past the water. Under it. What's underneath?"

Silence. A long one. Then, hesitant: "There's... a sort of scratching? In the bank?"

"A vole," Rafael said, and I could hear the smile in it. "She has three pups in a nest under the alder roots. She's telling you she doesn't like us being here. Can you hear what she says about me specifically?"

Another silence. Then Pip laughed—a startled, delighted bark of a laugh. "She thinks you smell like the valley! Like—like stone that's been in the sun!"

"Twenty-three years of sun-baked stone," Rafael confirmed gravely. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said about me in a month."

I kept sharpening my sword and pretended my throat wasn't tight. Because I knew what I was watching. I'd grown up in a garrison; I knew what it looked like when a master took an apprentice, the particular gravity that forms between them, the transfer of a whole self from one pair of hands to another. Except Rafael wasn't transferring a skill. They were transferring a self—the one they'd spent twenty-three years burying—and doing it gladly, watching it take root in a boy, as though planting it in Pip might somehow justify its existence.

Maybe it did. Maybe that's what gifts are for. I wouldn't know. Mine was swinging steel at people, and the realm had recently informed me that even that was unwelcome unless aimed correctly.

By week's end, Pip could pick out individual birds in the dawn chorus and tell you which ones were gossiping and which were keeping watch. He learned the difference between a crow's ordinary complaint and a crow that had been paid in bread to remember faces—he'd nearly been taken once because of such a crow, he told us, in a village whose name he wouldn't say, and after that he treated crows with the wary courtesy one extends to tax collectors.

And Rafael—Rafael began to laugh. Not often. But it happened. A short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut, and every time it happened I found myself looking away, busy with the fire, the horses, the map, anything.

* * *

Quill appointed himself Pip's third tutor, in a curriculum consisting entirely of cynicism, larceny, and history as told by someone with strong opinions and no institutional loyalty.

"Lesson one," I heard him announce one evening, perched on the boy's bedroll like a small orange magistrate. "Every official proclamation contains at least one lie, usually the part where they say who benefits. Lesson two: never trust a man who shakes your hand with his left. Lesson three: cheese stolen tastes better than cheese bought. This is not superstition. It is science."

"That's not science," Pip said.

"It is fox science. We have our own academies. Very exclusive."

"What do you actually teach at fox academy?"

"Mostly how to be underestimated," Quill said, "which is the most valuable education in any kingdom, and the reason your king is currently a corpse wearing a crown while I sit here eating his realm's excellent mice."

The boy absorbed all of it—the cynicism, the thievery, the casual treason—and repeated it back at dinner with the unfiltered precision of a child who has not yet learned which truths are socially load-bearing. It was Rafael who gently taught him the difference between knowing a thing and saying it, and I watched that lesson land and thought: there goes another brick.

Because that's what it was. I'd been a soldier long enough to know companies, squads, mess-tables, the whole architecture of people bound together by oath and shared danger. This was not that. This was slower and stranger. This was four misfits discovering, brick by deliberate brick, that they were building something none of them had asked for and all of them, apparently, needed.

* * *

The rain caught us in the foothills above the Marrow Road, three days out from Thornhollow, and drove us under a shelf of rock that some forgotten shepherd had walled into a rough shelter generations ago. We were pinned there for two days. Two days is enough time for a fire to become a ritual, for a deck of stolen cards to circulate, for a boy to ask questions.

It was the second night, fire low, rain hammering the world into a single gray sound, when Pip asked me about the badge.

I keep it wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my pack. I don't know why. Sentiment, probably, or spite, or the simple animal refusal of a soldier to discard the last thing that ever made her legible to herself. Pip had seen me unwrap it once to check the oiling—old habit; silver tarnishes even when you've renounced it—and now, in the firelight, he said, "Why do you still have it? If they took it away?"

The honest answers were several and none of them suitable for children. I gave him the truest one I could manage.

"Because I earned it," I said. "They can take the rank. They can't take the earning."

"But they said you broke your oath."

"I did." I fed a stick to the fire. Across the flames, Rafael had gone very still, listening the way they listen—not just to words but to everything underneath them. "My oath said protect the realm's people. All of them. The order that day said burn a child. When the two disagree, you don't get to keep both, boy. You choose which promise was real. I chose. I'd choose it again tomorrow, and the day after, until they run out of days to give me."

Pip considered this with his whole face again, that total lean of his.

"So the oath was real," he said, "and the order was fake."

"In a nutshell."

"Then you should keep the badge. It's proof you won."

I had no answer for that. I looked at Rafael over the fire, and they raised their eyebrows slightly, in the universal gesture of *the child has bested us both*, and Quill, curled against Pip's side, said smugly, "Fox academy accepts him. First year, honors."

* * *

Here is what I know about families, having failed to have one in the conventional manner: they are not made of blood. Blood is just the excuse most people use. Families are made of accumulated small surrenders—the extra blanket given up without comment, the watch taken so another can sleep, the story told at last because the listener has earned it by simply staying.

Ours accumulated slowly, then all at once.

Rafael started humming while cooking. Badly. Some valley tune, half-remembered, that made the ewe-speech cadences of their beast-talk bleed through into human song, so that it sounded like a person and a goat singing a duet in which only one party knew the words. Pip mocked it mercilessly. Rafael hummed louder. Within a week Pip was humming it too, wrong in exactly the same places, and I caught myself humming it on watch at midnight and had to stop and stare into the dark and renegotiate certain terms with myself.

I taught Pip to whittle. He made a fox. It was terrible. Quill reviewed it at length and pronounced it "a slanderous caricature, though the tail shows promise." Pip carved a second one. Less terrible. There are now two terrible wooden foxes riding in my pack, because apparently I am a person who carries a child's carvings across a hunted kingdom, and I have decided not to examine that.

Pip mended things. That was his gift and his joy, and once he understood that we weren't going to flinch from it, he couldn't stop. He straightened the bend in Rafael's cooking pot. He fixed the loose stitching in my sword-belt with stitches finer than a saddler's. He took Quill's old scar—not the flesh, but something about the way the fox carried his left shoulder, some old hurt in the joint—and worried at it until Quill admitted, grudgingly, that it ached less.

"Annoying," Quill said. "Useful, but annoying. Like most of this company."

He said *company*. Not *these humans I am temporarily tolerating*. Company. A military word. A word with structure in it. I let it pass without comment, but I marked it, the way you mark a wall that's gone up overnight where yesterday there was bare ground.

* * *

On the sixth night, Rafael found me on last watch and didn't say anything for a long time. That's one of the things about them I've stopped fighting: their silences aren't empty. They're full, like held breath.

"Pip asked me today if we'd keep him," they said finally. "After. Whatever 'after' turns out to be."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I'd ask the person whose opinion mattered most."

I looked at them. Firelight, the boy asleep between us with a wooden fox in his fist, the rain gone soft, the whole dark world holding its noise down around our little island of light.

"You realize," I said, "that I have spent my entire adult life in units. Sworn units. Chained-together-by-oath units. And this—" I gestured at the shelter, the boy, the fox, the pot with its mended belly, the humming, God help me, the humming— "this is the first one I'd have chosen."

Rafael was quiet a moment. "Chosen," they repeated, tasting the word. "Is that what we are? A chosen unit?"

"Don't make it strange."

"Too late. I'm making it strange. I'm a goat-whisperer making a family with a disgraced knight, a fugitive child, and a fox with delusions of rank. It was always going to be strange."

"Delusions?" Quill said, without opening his eyes. "I'll have you know I served the crown for nine years. My rank outranks yours. I simply decline to wear a uniform, because unlike some people I understand dignity."

"Go to sleep, Quill."

"Foxes sleep lightly. It's how we survived the last bad king. Take notes."

But he slept. And eventually Rafael slept, sitting up the way they do, as if ready to run even in dreams. And I sat my watch with a sword across my knees and a boy's breathing at my back and a wooden fox in my pocket, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the bridge.

Four of us. Four months to the capital. A dead king, a living hunger, a hunt that was a harvest, and a song that Rafael hadn't yet learned existed. The odds were bad. The odds had been bad since the day I refused a lawful order, and I had walked into them anyway, alone, because walking alone into bad odds was the only kind of courage I knew.

This was different. This was worse and better at once. Now there were people behind me when I turned around. People who hummed badly and carved slanderous foxes and fell down fifty times before breakfast because I told them to. People I would burn the realm down to keep warm.

Belonging, I thought, watching the coals breathe. So that's the trap. All those years I thought exile was the price of my choice. Turns out the price was paid up front, and belonging was waiting on the other side of it, patient as a debt collector, and it collects in the only coin that matters, which is fear. Fear for them, now. Forever. That's the toll.

I paid it gladly. That's the humiliating part. I'd have paid double.

Dawn came gray and clean over the Marrow Road, and I woke the others, and we moved. South, always south, toward Thornhollow and its market stalls and its bounty boards, toward the first crack in the careful life we were building.

I didn't know yet what waited for us in that town. Only that Rafael's gift was growing stronger by the day, that cages were getting harder for them to walk past, and that a boy who could mend broken things was traveling with a woman who was, herself, considerably broken—and that sooner or later, one of us was going to test whether that worked both ways.
