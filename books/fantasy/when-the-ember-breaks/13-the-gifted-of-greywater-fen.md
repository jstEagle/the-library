## 13. The Gifted of Greywater Fen

The fen found us before we found it, which I suspect was the point.

We had been three days in the marsh country, following a road that kept apologizing for itself—dissolving into peat, reassembling on the far side of black pools like it wasn't sure we deserved it—and Quill had stopped scouting ahead and started scouting *around*, doubling back, sitting on hummocks staring at reeds as though they owed him an explanation.

"There's something here," he said finally, on the morning of the fourth day.

"A fen," Sera said. "Well spotted."

"Something *under* the fen." He licked his paw with the air of a scholar consulting his notes. "I can smell woodsmoke that isn't ours. Bread. And—" he paused, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its usual dryness, "—goats. A lot of goats. Goats who are talking to each other in words."

Pip's head came up so fast he nearly fell off the log he was balancing on. "Talking how?"

"The way Rafael talks," Quill said. "Except all at once. Except dozens of them."

I felt it then, or maybe I'd been feeling it for a day without naming it: a low murmur at the edge of my gift, like a conversation through a wall. Out in the world beyond the valley, animal voices came at me loud and unfiltered unless I shut my doors, and I'd gotten good at shutting them. But this wasn't a crow complaining about weather or a horse reciting its grievances. This was *structured*. Voices layered over voices, and beneath them something almost like singing.

"They're hiding," I said. The certainty arrived whole, unasked-for, the way the gift always gave me things. "The animals are keeping their voices down. On purpose."

Sera's hand had already gone to her sword. Old habit. "Hiding from what?"

"From everyone," I said. "But especially from you."

* * *

They let us find them because they decided to, which we learned later, and which still gives me chills when I think about it. We were walking a causeway between two sheets of black water when the mist ahead of us thickened—not weather-mist, but the other kind, the kind that has opinions—and out of it came a woman with a heron standing beside her like a bodyguard.

She was old in the way that oak is old: bent but not broken, gray but not finished, with eyes the pale green of marsh water and a face that suggested she had been disappointed by experts. She carried a staff of black alder and she looked at each of us in turn, and I mean she *looked*, the way a customs officer looks, cataloguing everything you'd rather keep folded away.

"Hunters?" she asked.

"No," Sera said.

"That's what hunters say."

"I'm Sera Vane," Sera said, and there was a beat where she clearly considered lying about the rest and decided against it. "Formerly Captain Vane. Of the crown hunt."

The heron made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling. The old woman raised one hand and it went quiet, which told me more about her than anything else could have.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," she said to Sera.

"People tell me both. Usually in the same sentence."

"And these?"

"Pip," said Pip, who had never once in his life considered the strategic value of anonymity. "I fix things."

"Rafael," I said, and hesitated, and the old woman's eyes sharpened like she'd heard the hesitation as clearly as a snapped twig.

"Rafael *what*?" she said softly. "Finish it. Everyone has a second half to their name out here. Mine's Maren. My second half changes depending on who's asking."

I looked at Quill. Quill looked at me with the expression of a fox washing his paws of the entire affair.

"Beast-speaker," I said.

The mist moved. That's the only way I can describe it. The mist *moved*, and every bird within earshot went silent at once, and somewhere behind the woman, deep in the reeds, something large shifted its weight.

"Well," Maren said at last. "You'd better come in, then. Before the fen decides you're interesting enough to keep."

* * *

Greywater Fen wasn't a village. Villages have streets and chimneys and dogs that announce strangers. Greywater Fen was a rumor wearing a village's clothes—a scatter of houses built on stilts above the black water, connected by plank walkways that had been deliberately designed to look rotten and weren't, reed screens everywhere, and goats. Dozens of goats, moving along the causeways with the self-important stride of creatures who know they are load-bearing infrastructure.

And the voices. Walking into that place was like walking into a cathedral after years in the open field. Every animal voice I'd been shutting out for months was suddenly *organized*—a sentry-heron reporting our approach in clipped formal phrases, a dog explaining to another dog exactly why we were probably fine, geese arguing about rotation of watch duties with the petty venom of geese everywhere. They had a system. They had *shifts*.

"They've been expecting someone," Quill murmured at my shoulder, and for once he didn't sound smug. He sounded unsettled.

"Expecting us?"

"Expecting *a* someone. There's a difference."

Maren led us along a walkway to the largest house, built on the widest hummock, with smoke rising from its chimney in a thin, disciplined line—the smoke of people who had learned to burn wet wood slowly because dry smoke draws the eye. Inside it was warm and dim and smelled of bread and tallow, and there were perhaps thirty people inside, and I want to be honest about what happened to me in that doorway, because it matters.

I stopped. Just—stopped, one foot on the threshold, like a man who has walked into a glass door. Because the room turned toward us, and I saw it, and I don't know how else to say this: I saw *myself*, multiplied. A boy whose hands glimmered faintly when he was nervous, like heat-shimmer. A girl sitting very close to a cat, closer than cats allow, and the cat looking content about it. An old man with river-silt swirling in the basin of water beside him, shaping itself into little obedient spirals at his whim. A woman whose shadow didn't quite match her movements—it lagged, or led, I never worked out which, and I'm not sure she had either.

People like me. People like the thing I had spent twenty-three years being careful never to be.

"You'll want to sit," Maren said, not unkindly. "Everyone does, the first time. It's a lot like grief, seeing this. Nobody warns you about that part."

* * *

They fed us fish stew and dark bread, and the children stared at Pip until he showed them how he could mend a cracked bowl, and after that he disappeared into a swarm of small admirers and I didn't see him properly again until nightfall. Sera sat with her back to the wall, as she always did, and answered questions in short sentences, and I watched three separate people decide, over the course of the evening, that they trusted her anyway—which was more than she'd managed to make anyone believe in four months of road.

Quill sat on the rafters and listened to everything and pretended to be asleep, which fooled no one, least of all the fen's cats, who took turns sitting near him with the pointed patience of creditors.

It was near midnight when Maren came for me.

"Not you," she said to Sera, before Sera could rise. "Just the speaker. No offense meant."

"None taken," Sera said, meaning enormous amounts of offense, and lay back down with the particular stiffness of a woman planning to listen through the wall anyway.

Maren walked me out along a causeway to a platform at the fen's edge, where the water opened onto the sky and the moon laid a long white road across it. She sat on the platform's edge and gestured for me to do the same, and for a while neither of us said anything. Around us, the fen breathed. Frogs. Reeds. Somewhere far off, a goat complaining about philosophy.

"Do you know what you are?" she asked eventually.

"A beast-speaker."

"That's what you *do*." She turned those pale green eyes on me. "I asked what you *are*."

"I don't know that I've ever had a safe enough place to ask."

"Mm." She nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some kind of examination by failing to lie. "Then let me tell you a story, child, and you can decide afterward whether you wanted to know it."

She told it plainly, the way old women tell stories they've told themselves many times in the dark. About a hundred years ago—ninety-eight, if her dreams counted right, and her dreams did count right, that was the whole trouble with her—a king named Alderic had ruled this realm, and under Alderic the gifted had lived openly. Not comfortably; openly. There had been beast-speakers at court. There had been a college in Corvenna where children with fire in their palms learned to hold it. Her grandmother had been born there, before everything changed.

"And what changed it?" I asked.

"A hunger came up out of the old places," Maren said. "Out of the ground under the mountains, the elders used to say, though I've dreamed deeper roots than that. It was starving. It had starved a long time, and it learned that gifted souls taste richer than common ones—that a fire-handed boy will feed it for a season, and a speaker for a year. It crawled into the world wearing whatever it could wear. And the king of that day was proud, and proud men leave doors open."

"What kind of door?"

"A crown," she said simply. "A crown is a promise that never stops being spoken. Power answering power, head to head, every hour of every day. Something hungry can ride a promise like that all the way into a man's skull." She looked out across the black water. "It took the king. Slowly, the way rot takes a beam—from the inside, so the outside looks sound. And it began the hunts, child. Yes. You heard me. The hunts are not new. Your King Aldous did not invent them. He remembered them."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the fen.

"It's happened before."

"Ninety-eight years ago, it began before, and it would have swallowed the whole realm, except—" Maren smiled, and there was no comfort in the smile at all, only a kind of grim tenderness. "Except a beast-speaker stood up. A shepherd, by trade. Unremarkable. The histories—if any survive—would call her nothing. But she learned the spirit's true name, and she made a song of it, a binding song, sung in the speech of beasts because that is the one language hunger cannot counterfeit. Hunger only knows how to take. The song knows how to *hold*."

"And it worked?"

"She bound it into the deepest stone she could find and she died doing it. That's usually the part people skip, so let me not skip it: the song costs the singer. Holding a thing that size closed takes a hand on the lid from the inside." Maren's voice was steady. Her hands, I noticed, were not. "The realm got eighty good years. Then the binding thinned—as bindings do, as all lids do—and something hungry found a crack in a proud king's crown, and here we are."

Here we are. I looked down at my own hands, ordinary hands, goat-tending hands, hands that had spent a lifetime learning to be quiet.

"You're telling me this because—"

"Because three nights ago I dreamed of you," Maren said. "Before your fox ever set foot in my fen. I dreamed of a figure walking out of dead country where magic cannot follow, carrying silence like a bundle in their arms. I dreamed the old song stirring, the way a sleeper stirs. I dreamed the lid, child. I dreamed it *rattling*." She turned fully to face me, and the moonlight made her eyes look like holes cut in the dark. "There has never been but one kind of soul that can sing that binding. One kind in all the history of this realm. And one of them just walked into my kitchen and ate my stew."

* * *

I don't remember walking back along the causeway. I remember standing at the edge of our borrowed room, watching Sera sleep—or pretend to sleep—with her sword in reach and her boots on, and I remember thinking, with a strange floating clarity:

*I am twenty-three years old and I am the last hand on the lid.*

Quill found me there. He climbed into my lap, which he almost never did—he was a fox, and foxes have reputations—and put his chin on my knee.

"She told you," he said.

"You knew."

"I suspected. I hoped." His amber eyes caught the moonlight. "Rafael, I served a king who was eaten because nobody who could have stopped it knew what was happening until it was too late. I have spent a year looking for the one kind of soul that could make it not be too late this time. If I seemed to be leading you somewhere, well." He flicked an ear. "Foxes lead. It's what we're for."

"You could have *said*."

"And you'd have believed me? A talking fox tells the goatherd they're the prophesied lid-holder? You'd have gone back to your ewes and your sensible life and you'd have been *right* to." He sighed, a small dry sound. "Some truths need to arrive on their own feet. Hers did. Now they're yours."

"They're heavy," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

"Yes," Quill said. "That's how you know they're real. Lies are light. That's how they travel so fast."

Inside, Sera turned over in her sleep. Or pretended to. And far away to the north, past the fens and the roads and the checkpoints, in a city of bells, something ancient shifted against the walls of its prison and dreamed of eating—and for the first time since I'd crossed the valley stones, I understood that it might already know my name.

Because Maren saved the worst of it for last, standing on her causeway as I left, her voice following me over the black water like a hand on my shoulder:

"One more thing, child, and then you can hate me in peace. The dream doesn't end with the lid. It ends with the lid *opening*—from the outside, at the turn of the thirteenth moon. Whatever you decide to do with all this..." She spread her hands at the fen, the sleeping gifted, the listening dark. "Decide quickly. Hunger keeps its own calendar, and it is not waiting for you to be ready."
