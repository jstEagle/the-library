## 14. The Binding Song

The elder's name was Maren, and she smelled of peat smoke and river clay and something older underneath, something I couldn't put a word to until she taught me the word itself. She was small. She was bent like a question mark that had given up on ever standing straight again. And when she spoke, she spoke slowly, the way water speaks to stone—patiently, with the absolute certainty that it will win.

"Sit," she said, on my second morning in Greywater Fen. "You came here carrying a question you haven't asked yet. I can hear it rattling in you like a stone in a tin cup."

I sat. I didn't ask it. I'd spent twenty-three years not asking things; I was very good at it.

She waited anyway. That was her trick, I think. Everyone else in my life had either demanded answers or flinched away from them. Maren simply made room, the way you make room for weather.

* * *

The fen had taken me in the way fens take everything—gradually, without announcement. Sera had been skeptical from the moment we crossed the causeway. *A settlement of the gifted,* she'd said, *in one place, all at once. That's not sanctuary. That's a larder.* She wasn't wrong to think it. The hunt had found larger communities than this one. But the marsh did something to pursuit that open country never could: it swallowed tracks, drowned scent, turned every road into a choice between three worse roads. The hunters who came to Greywater Fen historically left with wet boots and nothing else, if they left at all.

There were forty-one people living there when we arrived. Forty-one. I counted, because counting was how I kept myself from weeping. Forty-one people who could speak to beasts, or mend what was broken, or find water under stone, or dream true dreams—and every one of them had done the same arithmetic I had done my whole life. *Hide. Shrink. Be ordinary. Be so relentlessly, aggressively ordinary that no one looks twice.*

And then there was Maren, who had never hidden anything in ninety years except her grief.

"You're wondering," she said, ladling nettle soup into a bowl that had been mended three times—"you're wondering why I'm the one who teaches you. You're wondering why an old woman in a marsh knows anything about the thing wearing the crown."

I hadn't been wondering that, actually. I'd been wondering whether the soup would kill me. Nettle soup has a flavor I can only describe as *aggressive chlorophyll*. But I let her have her question, because hers was better than mine.

"I know because I was there," she said. "Not for the binding. My grandmother's grandmother was there for that. But the song doesn't live in books, child. It lives in throats. It's been passed mouth to mouth down four generations of women who were told, each of them, the same thing: *one day someone will come who needs this, and you will know them by their ears.*"

"My ears?"

She looked at me the way you look at a door you've been waiting to open. "Your ears," she said, "and the fact that the fox led you here. Quill doesn't bring just anyone across my causeway. He brought you because he was told to bring you, and he was told by something older than he is." She paused, and her face did something complicated—the face of a woman deciding how much truth a person can carry before it becomes a burden instead of a gift. "So. Do you want the song, or do you want to keep being safe? Because you cannot have both. Not anymore. That door closed behind you somewhere on the road, and I'm sorry for it, but I won't pretend otherwise."

I thought about the valley. I thought about the ewe with the lame leg whose name I still refused to say out loud even in my own head, because saying it felt like admitting I'd left her. I thought about Marta's son, born with fire in his palms, dragged off in the night while I stood at my window and did nothing.

Safe had stopped meaning anything months ago. Safe was just the word for the part of the fear you'd gotten used to.

"Teach me," I said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about learning a language of the body: your body is a terrible student. It has spent its whole life doing whatever it wants, and now some old woman in a marsh is asking it to hold a note in its sternum, and your body responds with the enthusiasm of a goat being asked to do arithmetic.

"The song isn't sung," Maren said, on the first day. "That's the first lie everyone brings me. They open their mouths and make sounds and wonder why the reeds don't answer. The song is *held*. It's a shape you make with everything you are—breath, bone, blood, the small animal pulse of you—and the sound that comes out is just the shadow of the shape. The shape is the song. The sound is only its footprint."

She demonstrated. Or rather, she didn't demonstrate, because demonstration was impossible; she simply *did* it, once, briefly, and I understood immediately why she only did it once.

She stood at the edge of the water as the sun went down, and she held the first fragment of the binding song, and the fen *answered*. The herons went silent—not startled-silent, which is a sharp thing, but a deep silence, a listening silence. The water stilled. The frogs, which had been performing their evening racket since noon, stopped mid-croak, hundreds of them, all at once, as though someone had closed a great door. And underneath everything, underneath the silence, there was a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard—a low, vast hum, like the fen itself clearing its throat after a century of disuse.

It lasted perhaps ten seconds. Then Maren let it go, sagging against her walking stick, breathing hard, and the frogs resumed as though nothing had happened, and the herons went back to their fishing, and I stood there with the hair on my arms trying to climb off my body.

"What was that?" I asked.

"That was the first fragment," she said. "The Calling-Down. There are five fragments. The Calling-Down, the Naming, the Circling, the Holding, and the Letting-Go. My grandmother's grandmother used all five to bind the Hunger Beneath inside a cage of song, and it cost her everything—her voice, most of her years, and eventually, they say, her life, because a binding must be maintained, and she maintained it alone until the day she couldn't." She looked at the water. "The cage held for a hundred years. Then a king went digging where kings shouldn't dig, and the cage broke, and the thing got loose, and it found a crown waiting for it like an open mouth."

"And you're going to teach me all five fragments."

"I'm going to teach you what I have," she said. "Which is less than all five. Time eats songs the way rust eats iron. We have the Calling-Down whole. The Naming, mostly. The Circling, half. The Holding—" she grimaced, "fragments of fragments. And the Letting-Go—" she shook her head. "The Letting-Go was never passed down. It was never *meant* to be passed down. You don't teach the unbinding to just anyone. You teach it to the one person, in the one generation, who will need it. And then you pray you picked right."

No pressure, then.

* * *

I learned the Calling-Down over six days, and I would like to report that I was a natural, that the gift flowed through me like spring water, that Maren wept with pride. What actually happened is that I was appalling.

The problem was exactly the problem she'd named on the first day: I kept trying to sing it. My whole life, my gift had lived in my throat—I talked to animals, I *conversed*, words going back and forth like two people leaning over a fence. Words were my native country. And the binding song wanted nothing to do with words. It wanted me to stop talking and start *being*, which is a much harder assignment than it sounds.

"Stop narrating yourself," Maren said, roughly the ninth time I failed. "You're doing the thing every gifted child does—you're translating. You feel the shape, and then you rush to turn it into sound, into sense, into something you can check. Don't check. Hold. The song is not a sentence. It's a *stance*."

"A stance," I said. "Like standing."

"Exactly like standing. Now stand wrong."

I stood wrong for another hour. Then, somewhere in the failing—somewhere around the point where I'd stopped trying so hard and started just being tired and honest in my body—something shifted. I felt it in my chest first, a widening, like a door unbolting. Then the reeds nearest me began to lean. Not bend in the wind—there was no wind—but lean *toward* me, gently, the way plants lean toward light. And the water at my feet went glass-still, and a moorhen paddled over and looked at me with an expression I can only call *recognition*, and I held the fragment for four full seconds before it collapsed, and when it collapsed I nearly collapsed with it.

Maren watched all of this with her hands folded on her stick, and when it was over she said, "There. Did you feel it? That wasn't sound. That was stance."

"It felt like—" I searched. "It felt like being listened to by the ground."

"Yes," she said. "Now imagine being listened to by the ground for a hundred years, and you'll understand what my grandmother's grandmother did to the Hunger Beneath. And you'll understand why it hates us." Her eyes were bright and hard. "It doesn't hate the gifted because we're dangerous, child. It hates us because we are the only ones who can put it back in the dark. Everything else it can eat."

* * *

Sera found me that evening sitting on the causeway with my legs in the cold water, staring at nothing.

"You look like a man who's been hit with a plank," she said, settling beside me. She'd taken to sitting near me in the evenings without ceremony—one of the many things about her I was trying very hard not to read too much into, and failing at, daily.

"I learned the first piece of the song today."

"And?"

"And it turns out the weapon that kills the thing wearing the king's face requires me to stand perfectly still in front of it and be completely open." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Maren says the spirit feeds on the gifted. On our gifts. That's what the hunt is—it's a harvest, it's stocking a pantry. And the way to defeat it is to walk into its throne room and offer myself up as the main course, trusting that the song will hold before it finishes eating."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Water lapped at our ankles. Somewhere out in the reeds, a bittern boomed its strange, hollow note, like someone blowing across the top of an empty well.

"You're afraid it'll eat you," she said finally. Not a question.

"I'm afraid it'll *want* to eat me. Maren says the binding only works if sung by one the spirit cannot devour. One it can't swallow. And I keep thinking—" I pulled my knees up, hugging them like a child. "I keep thinking about what I am. I spent twenty-three years starving myself. Hiding the gift, rationing it, pretending it wasn't there. A soul that's been starved that long—maybe I'm exactly the kind of hunger it recognizes. Maybe it looks at me and sees a cousin. Maybe devouring me wouldn't even be hard. Maybe I'd *let* it."

The bittern boomed again. Sera didn't reach for me, because Sera didn't reach for people; she stationed herself near them, which was her version of reaching, and I had learned to read it.

"When I was a knight," she said, "they trained us that the shield is not cowardice. Standing behind it isn't hiding. But there's a moment in every fight—the moment before contact—where the shield comes down, because you cannot strike and cover at the same time. Every soldier knows it. That half-second where you're completely exposed, and the whole fight lives or dies on whether your blow lands true." She looked at me sideways. "What Maren's asking of you is that half-second, stretched across a throne room. It's not stupidity. It's the only geometry that works. You can't bind a thing from behind armor. It has to see you to be caught by you."

"That's deeply comforting, thank you."

"I'm not finished." Her voice went quieter. "You asked whether you're the kind of soul it can devour. I've watched you for two months, Rafael. I watched you free a caged bird in a market square with hunters ten feet away. I watched you talk a war-hound into betraying its handler because you couldn't bear its fear. I watched you take in a stray boy because you remembered being one." She paused. "Whatever that spirit is, it's made of appetite. Of taking. And you—" she shook her head, almost annoyed, as if the observation offended her professionalism, "—you are the least hungry person I have ever met. You give things away like it's a medical condition. If it tries to swallow you, I genuinely believe it will choke."

I didn't say anything. I'm not sure I could have. Some sentences you just have to sit inside for a while, like a warm room.

Quill arrived as the last light went, trotting along the causeway with his tail low and his gait businesslike, and flopped down between us as though he'd been invited, which he hadn't, which had never once stopped him.

"The old woman wants you at dawn," he said. "All of you. She says she has something to show you that she should have shown you the first night, and she's been putting it off because she's a coward, her words, and foxes don't get to judge." His ears flicked. "Also there's fish."

"There's always fish with you."

"There is *always* fish," Quill agreed solemnly. "A fox must have principles."

But his joke had a hairline crack in it, and I heard it. Quill had known Maren longer than any of us—he'd guided other fugitives here, over years—and something about what she wanted to show us had gotten under his fur. Foxes hide worry badly. It comes out the ears.

* * *

Dawn in the fen is not a gentle event. The mist lifts all at once, like a sheet pulled off furniture, and the world is simply *there*, revealed, no transition. Maren was waiting for us at the far edge of the settlement, past the last hut, where the solid ground gave up and became a maze of black water and reed islands. She had her stick and a lantern she didn't need, because the sky was already doing gold-and-grey things overhead, and she was standing before a low mound covered in rushes that I had walked past twice without seeing.

"This," she said, "is the grave of Edith Marrow-Singer. My grandmother's grandmother. The one who bound the Hunger Beneath."

We stood in a row before it—me, Sera, Pip fidgeting, Quill sitting with unusual stillness. Even the fen seemed to hush, though that may have been my imagination, or may have been the beginning of learning what stance meant.

"She bound it," Maren said, "and the binding cost her her voice within the year, and her strength within five, and she spent her last fifteen years unable to speak to so much as a sparrow. Imagine that. Imagine being a beast-speaker and going deaf to the whole living world, deliberately, as the price of the cage. She said—" and here Maren's voice did something I hadn't heard it do, something younger, "—she said the worst part wasn't the silence. The worst part was knowing the cage would break someday, because all cages break, and hoping whoever came after her would be ready, and knowing she wouldn't be there to see."

She turned to me. The dawn light caught her face and made every line of it legible, every year accounted for.

"So here is what I have put off telling you, child. The fragments I can teach you will unbind a king. They will loosen Vhalor's grip enough that the crown can be lifted and the man beneath can breathe. But they will not destroy it. They will not even fully cage it. For that—for the *Holding*, the true Holding, the complete song—you need the fifth fragment, the Letting-Go, and the Letting-Go exists in exactly one place in this world." She nodded at the mound. "Edith took it with her. Deliberately. She wrote it nowhere and taught it to no one, because a binding song in careless hands is a key, and she'd seen what keys do. She hid it the only place she trusted."

Pip, bless him, said what we were all thinking: "Where?"

Maren smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman handing someone a heavy package and stepping back.

"In the grave," she said. "Beast-speakers bury their songs in their bones, child. That's the oldest law of the gift. And Edith Marrow-Singer has been holding the last fragment of the binding song under this fen for a hundred years, waiting for someone with ears to come and ask her for it."

The bittern boomed out in the reeds, once, long and hollow, and I swear on every god I'd ever pretended to worship that it sounded like an introduction.

Maren looked at me over her folded hands. "You'll need to go down to her," she said. "And you'll need to ask nicely."
