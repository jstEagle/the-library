## 15. Hunters at the Fen Gate

I had forgotten how much I hated being right.

It's a strange thing, a soldier's instinct. You spend years sharpening it—learning to read smoke on the horizon, to feel when a village goes too quiet, to notice that the dogs have stopped barking—and then one day it tells you something you don't want to know, and you realize you built this instrument out of your own ribs.

Three days after we'd gone down into the fen and come back up with a song buried in Rafael's bones like a splinter made of music, I woke before dawn with my hand already on my sword hilt. No reason. No sound. Just the old cold arithmetic: *we've been here too long, and someone has been counting.*

Quill confirmed it an hour later, dropping through the reed-curtain of Maren's doorway with mud on his paws and his ears flat as a whipped dog's.

"Riders," he said. "Eight of them. Maybe nine. Crown badges, hunter's grey, moving up the causeway from Ashford way." He paused, and his voice went smaller than I'd ever heard it. "They've got hounds."

Rafael went white. I didn't need the gift of beast-speech to know why. A hound had scented them once before, at Ashford Bridge, and Rafael had done something to that hound that still kept me up at night—not because it was wrong, but because it had looked so *easy*.

"How long?" I asked.

"Half a day," said Quill. "Less, if they find the boy's tracks." He glanced at Pip, who was sitting cross-legged by the fire mending a cracked bowl—one of his little parlor tricks, fixing broken things, done absently the way other people chew their nails.

Pip set down the bowl very carefully. "The settlement," he said. "Maren's people. They can't run fast, can they? Old ones, little ones—" He stopped, because he was twelve and he already knew the shape of what he was describing.

"They can't run at all," I said. "That's the point of a marsh."

* * *

Here is what you should understand about Greywater Fen, and why I stood in Maren's doorway that morning feeling the ghost of a badge I no longer wore settle onto my shoulders like wet wool.

Marshes are where hunted things go because marshes are where armies drown. The paths move. The ground lies about itself. A company of horsemen in armor has exactly two options: go slow and loud, or go fast and die. So the gifted had hidden there for generations, the same way hermits hide in deserts—not because deserts are kind, but because they're honest about being cruel, and cruelty you understand is easier to survive than kindness that turns.

But hunters aren't armies. And I knew—I knew in my marrow, because I had trained them—the way crown hunters worked a fen. You burn the edges first. Drive everything toward the center. Then you burn the center and pick through the ash for whatever screams.

"They'll want the whole settlement," I told Maren, Edith, and the handful of fen-folk who crowded into the elder's hut. "Not just us. Us they want alive, mostly—Rafael especially. But the rest of you are witnesses, and the crown has stopped spending coin on keeping witnesses."

Old Edith Marrow-Singer sat by her brazier with her hands folded, looking for all the world like a woman discussing the price of eels. "Then we do what the fen does," she said.

"And what's that?"

"Flood." She opened one eye. "You think these waters sit where they sit by accident, girl? My grandmother's grandmother moved the first channel. There are gates under the east bank, older than the crown, older than most sins. We open them, the shallows go deep, and the causeway becomes a rumor." She sniffed. "It won't save everything. It will save enough."

"It will buy time," I corrected, because I'd been paid to be precise once and the habit hadn't died with my rank. "A flooded causeway slows eight riders for half a day. It doesn't stop nine hounds."

Edith looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in her face that wasn't unkind but was worse than unkind—it was *knowing*. "No," she agreed. "It doesn't stop hounds. That would take a soldier." Her eyes flicked to my hip, where my sword hung, and to the faded scar across the back of my hand where a badge-pin had been torn out rather than surrendered politely. "And we haven't got one of those anymore. Have we?"

I didn't answer. Some questions you answer by standing up straighter.

* * *

We spent the morning doing the work soldiers hate most: preparing to lose.

Maren's people moved through the stilt-houses with a terrible quiet competence, packing what could float, sinking what couldn't, carrying children toward the deep channels where the water-walkers—the fen's own small magics, folk who could stand on the surface like it was gossiping about them—would ferry them to the far islands. An old man gave me his grandmother's copper kettle to carry and thanked me as if I were doing him a favor instead of failing him in advance.

Rafael worked the animals. That was the thing I watched, that morning, while pretending to organize a defense I didn't believe in. Rafael moved among the fen's herons and otters and half-wild marsh ponies, hands moving, throat making low sounds that weren't quite words, and everywhere they went the creatures *listened*. By mid-morning the herons had formed loose lines along the eastern channels like sentries. The ponies stood saddled-ready though nobody had touched them. Even the eels, Quill reported with grudging respect, had agreed to be disagreeable.

"You're going to ask them to fight," I said, finding Rafael crouched at the water's edge, forehead nearly touching the nose of a marsh pony the color of old pewter.

"I'm going to ask them to help," Rafael said. "Fighting is their choice." A pause. "Same as yours, Sera."

I should have known better than to hope a beast-speaker wouldn't see straight through me. I walked away before my face could commit treason.

Because here was the truth I'd been circling all morning: the riders coming up that causeway weren't strangers. I knew the grey cloaks. I knew the cadence of their signals, the spacing of their file, the way they'd use the wind. And I knew—God help me, I *knew*—the man who'd be leading them, because there was only one captain the crown trusted with a fen hunt, and his name was Dorian Hale, and for six years he had been the closest thing I had to a brother.

He'd been the one who read the order aloud, the day I refused it. The day a child with fire in her palms knelt in a courtyard and I said *no* out loud, in front of forty men, and my life ended at the speed of a sentence.

* * *

The floodgates opened at noon, and the fen drank its own borders.

It was beautiful, in the way that terrible things are sometimes beautiful—a groan from under the earth, a shiver through the reeds, and then the shallow flats blooming into black deep water, swallowing paths that three hundred years of feet had worn. The causeway became a spine of stone in a drowned world. On the far side, faint as a headache, I could hear the riders stopping, regrouping, arguing.

For half a day, we had bought ourselves half a day.

We spent it badly, the way everyone spends borrowed time. Pip ferried bundles until his arms shook. Odile—no, Odile was far away in Corvenna; forgive me, the memory crowds—Maren organized the elders. Rafael sat with the children who couldn't stop crying and did something with their gift that made even the crying quieter, some animal lullaby passed hand to hand like bread.

And I stood on the western walkway and watched the treeline, and hated myself with the focused discipline of a professional.

Because part of me—the part I keep locked in a box labeled *later*, which is where I keep all my worst furniture—was not thinking about the settlement at all. Part of me was thinking: *Dorian is coming. Dorian, who taught me to throw a knife. Dorian, who carried me two miles with an arrow in my thigh and joked about the scenery.* Part of me wanted to step onto that causeway and say his name and watch his face, and I could not tell you honestly whether it was to fight him or to beg him.

That's the thing about oaths. When you break one, you don't get to stop belonging to it. You just belong to the breaking.

"Sera." Rafael beside me, quiet. They'd gotten good at finding me when I was busy not existing. "Whoever's coming. You know them."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't treat it like one. "Captain Dorian Hale. Crown Hunter, First Order. Twenty-two years' service. He once went three days without sleep dragging a wounded recruit out of the Thornwood, and that recruit was me." I kept my eyes on the trees. "He's fair. Careful. He counts his arrows." A breath. "And he burned a village last spring because the bounty list said to."

"You're sure?"

"The man I served under wouldn't have." I finally looked at them. "But the man I served under died somewhere between that courtyard and here, and whatever's wearing his cloak now, it wears it well. Hunters always do."

Rafael studied me with those too-seeing eyes of theirs, and then did the thing I hated most about them, which was believe in me without checking whether I deserved it. "When they come," they said, "you don't have to be anything you used to be. But if you still remember how they think—that's not your oath's ghost, Sera. That's just a map. Maps don't owe loyalty to anyone."

I laughed. It came out wrong, rusty, but it came. "Did the fox write that for you?"

"He wishes."

* * *

They came at dusk, because of course they did. Dorian always attacked at dusk—he called it the honest hour, when everyone's too tired to pretend.

The flood had turned the causeway approach into a killing ground, and Dorian, being Dorian, declined to die in it. Instead they came around, through the treeline on the north bank—on foot, single file, hounds cast loose ahead in silence. Eight shapes in hunter's grey moving through the last light like the fen itself had learned to walk. I counted nine hounds and felt my stomach drop, because nine hounds meant they expected quarry worth nine hounds.

Which meant the crown knew what was sleeping under this fen. Knew about Edith. Knew about the song.

The alarm came not from our watchers but from the birds—herons rising off the water in ragged sheets, screaming, and beneath their cries the hounds began to sing that low, eager bell-note that every hunted creature in the world knows in its bones.

"Maren!" I shouted. "West channels, now! Everyone to the boats—go!"

What followed, I will set down plainly, because plainness is the only honor left for it.

The fen-folk ran for the deep water, and the water-walkers took them, gliding out across the black surface with children bundled against their chests like contraband mercy. The marsh ponies broke north on Rafael's signal and drew six of the nine hounds baying after them—sacrificing their own safety, God forgive me for asking it of them, though Rafael swore to me later the ponies understood and chose it, which was somehow both better and infinitely worse.

And the hunters came on, because hunters always come on, and at the edge of the burning reeds—someone had fired the dry stands, orange light rolling up into the darkening sky like the fen's hair catching—stood a broad figure in a grey cloak with a captain's knot at the shoulder, and my heart performed the entire history of my disgrace in the space of one breath.

"Dorian Hale," I said, stepping out of the smoke with my sword drawn, because apparently my body had decided things without consulting me. Apparently it always had.

He stopped. Ten yards of burning light between us. His face under the hood was older than my memory of it, lined around the eyes, and when he saw me his expression did something complicated—surprise, then recognition, then something I refused to name because naming it would have cost me the fight.

"Sera Vane." My old name in his mouth sounded like a coin someone had bitten and found counterfeit. "They said you were dead."

"People say a lot of things for reward money."

His gaze went past me—to the boats on the black water, to the children, to Rafael standing between the flames and the fleeing settlement with their arms slightly raised like a conductor between movements. Something shifted behind his eyes. Duty, or its corpse.

"You know what's under this fen," he said quietly. "You know what the old woman holds. Step aside, Vane. This isn't your fight, and the crown pays double for you brought in breathing. I'd rather it were that way. You were the best of us."

"The best of us burned a child's family in Thornhollow, Dorian. Is that the standard, or the cautionary tale?"

Something flinched at the corner of his jaw. For one absurd moment I thought—*he's still in there, the man who dragged me out of the Thornwood, one sentence, one crack, and he'll lower his sword*—

"Hold the line," Dorian said to his men, and it was steady, and it was final, and the man I'd hoped for stayed dead. "Vane. Last chance."

"No," I said. And this time it didn't feel like breaking an oath. It felt like keeping one—an older one, the one underneath, the one they never put on a badge. *Protect. That was always the word under all the other words.*

He came at me the way he'd taught me to come at people: no flourish, no anger, pure geometry. And I met him with everything he'd ever given me, returned with interest, and we fought through the burning reeds while behind us the fen screamed and sang and saved what it could.

I won. Barely, ugly, with a cut along my ribs I'd pay for later—but I won, and I stood over him with my sword point trembling at his throat and the firelight turning us both the color of the things we'd each chosen.

"Do it," he said, and closed his eyes. He wanted it. That was the worst discovery of the whole night—that I could give my oldest friend a mercy and he'd thank me for it.

"No," I said again, and it cost more than the fighting had. "You're going to live, Dorian. You're going to live and report that the fen was empty, that the flood took everything, that there was nothing left worth hunting. Because if you don't—if you bring the crown back here—" I leaned close, and my voice came out in a register I didn't recognize. "There isn't a hole in this kingdom deep enough. I know where all the holes are. I dug half of them."

I left him in the ashes with his men regrouping around him, and I ran for the boats, because the settlement needed counting and I was afraid—truly afraid—of what the count would say.

* * *

The count said: not enough.

Forty-one souls lived at Greywater Fen when the sun set. Thirty-three reached the far islands. We pulled eight bodies from the shallows over the next two days, and burned them according to fen custom on little rafts with reed-flowers, because Edith Marrow-Singer insisted that the dead of a free place deserve a send-off no king could tax.

One of them was the old man with the copper kettle. I carried that kettle to the raft myself. I have it still. I expect I always will.

Rafael found me on the second night, sitting apart with the fire making shadows of everything I'd failed to prevent. They sat down without asking, which was another thing about them I'd stopped resenting and started needing.

"You chose," they said simply.

"I chose late."

"You chose." They let that sit. "Sera—eight people died today because the crown is rotten to the load-bearing walls. Not because you hesitated. Not because you weren't enough. If you'd run from this fight the way you almost ran from yourself, the number would be forty-nine."

I stared into the fire for a long time. "Dorian's going to report it empty," I said. "Or he's going to report my name, and then every hunter in the realm knows exactly where we are and exactly what we carry. Either way, we leave tomorrow. Either way, the road to Corvenna just got shorter and darker." I finally turned to look at them—at this person who spoke to beasts and carried a dead king's song in their bones and somehow still flinched when strangers raised their voices. "Rafael. Whatever happens next, I need you to hear me say it once, plainly. I'm not running from my past anymore. I'm walking toward it, sword out. And I'd rather do it next to you than anywhere else in this broken kingdom."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, a bittern boomed, and Rafael smiled like someone translating.

"They say," Rafael murmured, "*the ember remembers what lit it.*"

And then Quill came out of the reeds at a dead run, mud flying, and the look on his face retired every joke he'd ever told.

"Riders on the south road," he gasped. "Not hunters. Palace guard. And they're carrying Odile's colors." He stopped, chest heaving, and delivered the rest like a knife laid flat on a table. "She's been taken. And they're announcing it in every town between here and Corvenna—she sings the binding song, they say, or she burns. They've given her until the thirteenth moon."
