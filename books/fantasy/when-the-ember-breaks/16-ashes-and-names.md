## 16. Ashes and Names

Nobody tells you that digging graves is mostly arithmetic.

Two feet down for a child. Three for an adult, if you want the foxes to leave them be. One grave takes about as long as it takes to say a name properly, if you say it slow, and there were eleven names in the fen when the smoke cleared, and I had hands that could mend a cracked bowl or a broken fence post but couldn't mend a single thing that mattered.

I counted anyway. It gave my arms something to do while my head was busy being useless.

The marsh water had gone gray with ash. The reeds were burned back to black stubs along the eastern side where the hunters had come through, and the peat itself had caught in places, so the ground still smoked faintly underfoot, like the fen was sulking about what had been done to it. Which was fair. I was sulking too. Sulk is easier than the other things.

"You're digging crooked," Rafael said, kneeling beside me at the fourth hole.

"It's a crooked world. I'm matching it."

"That's very clever. Make it straight."

I made it straight. When you're twelve—well, thirteen, I'd decided to round up since nobody was in a position to check—you learn quickly which adults will tolerate your mouth and which won't. Rafael tolerated most of it. But there was a tone they got when they were tired all the way down to their bones, and this was that tone, and so I dug the grave straight and said nothing else until we lowered old Bramwen into it.

Bramwen had been the fen's beekeeper. He'd shown me his hives on my first morning there, three days and one lifetime ago, and told me the bees knew him by voice, and that he'd sing to them every dusk whether they needed it or not. "Everything wants to be spoken to kindly," he'd said. "Even the things with stingers." Then he'd laughed like a door creaking, which is to say, warmly, and slightly broken.

He hadn't run when the hunters came. He'd stood in front of the children's hut with a smoker in each hand and told them, in a voice the whole fen heard, exactly what he thought of King Aldous's men. The bees had done the rest. Eleven of the hunters' number had gone screaming into the water, and Bramwen had taken two crossbow bolts doing it, and I think—I hope—he died pleased with himself.

I put his hive-tool in the ground with him. It seemed wrong to bury a man without his tools. Nobody argued.

* * *

By midday we had nine of the eleven settled. The last two were the hard ones: a woman named Ysolde whose body we found in the reeds near the water gate, and a boy called Tam who had been ten years old and who had hidden under a floorboard the way I would have hidden, the way I had once hidden under a bridge watching strangers do magic, and the floorboard had not been enough.

Sera dug those graves herself. She didn't ask for help, and she didn't talk, and her face did the thing it does where everything goes flat and soldierly, which I used to think meant she felt nothing, and which I now understood meant she was holding everything down with both hands like a lid on a boiling pot.

Quill sat on a fence post—the fence was burned, but posts are stubborn—and watched us work with his ears low.

"Tam," he said at one point. "His name was Tam."

"I know," I said.

"He liked it when you mended his sandal strap. He talked about it for an hour. 'Pip fixed it like it had never broke.' Those were his words. Terrible grammar."

"Yeah," I said. "Terrible."

My throat did something complicated after that, so I went and hauled water from the clean well on the far side, the one that hadn't tasted of smoke, and I carried buckets until carrying buckets was all there was in the world.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about three days. Three days is long enough to learn people.

In three days you learn that Ysolde took in strays the way other people take in laundry—without ceremony, without asking why you'd ended up damp and crumpled on her doorstep, just: hang yourself by the fire, child, there's broth. In three days you learn that the fen's elder, Mother Wren, hummed while she worked and always got the tune slightly wrong, and that when someone corrected her she'd say "I know," with tremendous dignity, and keep humming it wrong. You learn that Tam was afraid of frogs and pretended not to be, and that he'd followed me around the settlement like a duckling because I could do something with my hands and he wanted, more than anything, to have something too.

You learn all of it in three days, and then the king's hunt arrives with fire and crossbows, and everything you learned becomes a list of names, and the list goes in the ground, and you stand over it wondering how much a person can shrink into a name before they stop fitting.

That was the arithmetic I was really doing. Not feet and inches. How much a person weighs, afterward.

* * *

Rafael found me at dusk sitting on the water gate, skipping stones badly. My hands were blistered from the shoveling, which was its own kind of joke, because I could heal a split fence rail with a touch and I couldn't heal my own palms, or maybe wouldn't—it's strange, the things your gift decides it's allowed to touch. Or maybe I just wanted the blisters. Maybe some part of me thought I owed the dead a little pain, since pain was the only currency I had that they could use.

"Pip." Rafael sat down next to me. Close, but not touching-close. They'd learned that about me already. "You haven't eaten."

"I ate."

"A crust isn't eating."

"It's technically eating. Ask any lawyer."

They didn't laugh. That was how I knew this wasn't going to be one of our normal conversations, the ones where Rafael pretends to be exasperated by me and I pretend not to notice that being exasperated at means being fond of. Instead they looked out at the water, where the last light was doing something orange and mournful, and they were quiet for so long I almost forgot they were there.

Then I asked it. I hadn't planned to ask it. It had been sitting in my chest since morning like a swallowed stone, and it just came up the way stones do when you've been swallowing them too long.

"Is it worth it?"

"Worth what?"

"The king. Saving him." I kept my eyes on the water. "The hunt's his. His seal was on the bounty boards. His criers read the proclamation. Whatever's wearing his crown, it's wearing *him*, and he let it—or he couldn't stop it, or whatever—but all these people are dead either way. Bramwen. Ysolde. Tam." My voice cracked on Tam's name and I hated it for cracking. "We're walking four months to save the man whose soldiers killed them. So I'm asking. Is it worth it?"

And here's the thing I want written down somewhere, in case anyone ever reads this and thinks I was being cruel: I wasn't trying to hurt Rafael. I loved Rafael. Still love them, if we're keeping records. But I was thirteen years old and I'd buried a boy younger than me, and I needed somebody older than me to say something true, because everything else I'd been told since I ran away from home had turned out to be either a lie or a half-truth, and half-truths are just lies with better posture.

Rafael didn't answer right away. They pulled their knees up and wrapped their arms around them, and Quill, down the bank, went conspicuously quiet, which for a fox is a form of courtesy.

"No," Rafael said finally. "There isn't a clean answer."

"That's not an answer."

"I know. It's the only honest thing I have." They turned to look at me, and their face in the dying light was tired in a way I'd never seen on them—not road-tired, not hiding-tired. Something deeper. "You want me to tell you it's worth it. That Tam's death buys something. That if we reach Corvenna and unbind the king, somehow it evens out. And I can't tell you that, Pip, because I don't believe it. Nothing evens out. Dead is dead. Tam doesn't get to grow up because we saved Aldous. Bramwen's bees don't care whose crown sits on whose head."

"Then why are we doing it?"

"Because the alternative is worse." They said it quietly, without any of the fire I expected. "Because if we stop—if we decide the king's not worth saving, that the hunt makes him our enemy forever—then Vhalor wins without a fight. It keeps feeding. It finds another valley full of people like me, another Marta's son dragged out of bed, another fen burned. Not because we failed. Because we chose to let it." A pause. The water lapped against the pilings. "But don't confuse that with worth, Pip. Worth is a different question. We're not doing this because it balances the books. The books are ruined. We're doing it so no one has to bury another list of names. That's all. It's small. It's not clean. It's the best I've got."

I sat with that. I skipped another stone, and it skipped twice, which was a personal record.

"It doesn't feel like enough," I said.

"It isn't." Rafael's voice did something soft and terrible. "It never will be. That's the fracture, Pip. The one you're feeling right now? It doesn't close. You carry the mission in one hand and the names in the other, and neither hand lets go, and you walk. That's what it is. Anyone who tells you it feels righteous is selling something."

I thought about Tam saying *Pip fixed it like it had never broke*. I thought about how Rafael had spent twenty-three years pretending to be ordinary so that no one would burn them, and how the first time they'd used their gift freely—for me, actually, back at Ashford Bridge—they'd done it knowing it might kill them, and they'd done it anyway, and now here they were, offering me the truth instead of comfort, because comfort was a lie and they knew I'd catch the lie.

"Okay," I said.

"'Okay'?"

"Okay. I'll carry both hands' worth." I wiped my nose on my sleeve, which was disgusting, and didn't care. "But I want it written down that the answer was bad."

"Noted," Rafael said. "Witnessed by a fox and a marsh."

"Foxes make poor witnesses. They're known liars."

"I'm *right here*," Quill said from the bank, with enormous dignity, "and I heard every word, and I'll be mentioning this at your trial."

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out wet and cracked, but it came out. Rafael put an arm around my shoulders then, brief and fierce, and we sat there until the light went, three exiles on a burned water gate, holding our ruined books open between us.

* * *

We left the fen two days later, when the survivors had scattered to safe houses Mother Wren named in a whisper—places in the hills, places under cities, places even she seemed unsure still existed. Before we went, I did one last thing. I walked the row of graves and I said every name out loud, slow, the way you dig a grave: slow, and straight.

Bramwen. Ysolde. Tam. And the others, whose three days had taught me less but cost the same.

Sera stood apart, as she always did, cleaning a sword that didn't need cleaning. But when I finished, she said, "Good," in a voice like a struck bell, and that was Sera for you—one word where other people needed a eulogy, and somehow it landed harder than a eulogy would have.

We took the south causeway out of the marsh. Behind us the fen steamed and healed in its own slow way, because fens do; water forgets faster than people do, and maybe that's mercy, or maybe it's just water being shallow.

It was Quill, trotting ahead, who stopped first. His tail went stiff as a poker.

"Company," he said. "On the road ahead. A lot of it. And—" he tilted his head, ears swiveling, and when he spoke again his voice had gone strange and careful, "—it smells like palace."

Sera's hand went to her blade. Rafael's face closed like a door.

Ahead, around the bend of the causeway, dust was rising off the road. A lot of dust. More dust than merchants make.

More dust than anything good makes.
