## 17. The City of Bells

I heard Corvenna before I saw it.

That is not a figure of speech, though I understand why you might think so. Most people see a city first—its walls, its smoke, its sprawl—and hear it after, as an afterthought. I heard it for two days before the towers came into view, because I had spent twenty-three years learning to listen past the ordinary world, and what I heard coming off that plain was not ordinary at all.

It was ringing.

Not one bell. Not ten. Hundreds of them, thousands, layered over each other in a wash of bronze and iron that never resolved into anything so merciful as silence. They rang out of time with each other, which was somehow worse than if they'd rung together. A great bell tolling the hour here, a small handbell there, a carillon somewhere stumbling through a melody that never quite finished, as if the ringer kept forgetting how it went.

"Is it always like this?" Pip asked. He'd climbed onto a milestone to squint down the road, as if distance would help him make sense of it.

"Always," Sera said. She rode ahead of us with her hood up and her shoulders set in the particular way she did when she was approaching something she hated. "I garrisoned here for six years. You stop hearing it after a while."

"You stop hearing it," I said, "or you stop *minding* it?"

She didn't answer, which was its own answer.

Quill trotted along beside my horse's hooves, his tail low, his ears flat. He had been quiet since dawn, and Quill quiet was like a hearth gone cold—you noticed it most because of what should have been there.

"It's on purpose," he said at last. His voice, when he spoke aloud where travelers might pass, was pitched low and rough, the growl of an ordinary fox pretending to be an ordinary fox. "The bells. It's not piety. It's not celebration. It built them."

"Built them?" Pip laughed. "Foxes don't build bell towers, Quill."

"This one did." Quill didn't look up. "Or it made men build them, which comes to the same thing. Think about what a bell does. It fills every corner of a city with sound. It drowns out whispering. It drowns out—" he glanced up at me, quick and apologetic, "—it drowns out everything else. Every voice that isn't rung in bronze."

I felt the understanding arrive in my chest before my head caught up, cold water spreading through warm cloth.

Beast-speech isn't sound. Not really. The throat makes noise, yes, but the language itself lives underneath—in the body, in the pulse, in the small electric truth of a living thing saying *here I am* to another living thing. I had spoken to goats across a valley without raising my voice. I had heard a lame ewe's complaints from a hundred paces. The words traveled on something no bell could ring over.

Except.

"Can it drown it out?" I asked. My mouth had gone dry. "The ringing. Can it actually—"

"Ask me again when we're inside the walls," Quill said.

* * *

The gates of Corvenna were tall enough to swallow the sky, or that's how they seemed from the queue of carts and pilgrims and refugees waiting to be let in. The hunt had emptied whole villages; people had come to the capital because it was enormous and anonymous and because even a hunted animal will run toward the biggest shadow available. We joined the line behind a tinker's wagon, and I did what I had done at every checkpoint since Ashford Bridge: I became furniture.

A mute servant doesn't look at guards. A mute servant keeps their eyes down and their hands busy and their face arranged into the mild, vacant patience of someone whose entire inner life has been politely removed. Sera had drilled me on it until I could do it half-asleep. *You're not hiding anything,* she'd said. *You're just not interesting.* It was good advice. It had kept us alive through three towns and two bounty boards with my face sketched on them, badly—the artist had given me a jaw like a shovel—but the advice assumed the danger would come from outside.

The first bell struck while we were still fifty yards from the gate.

It wasn't loud, exactly. It was *total*. The sound didn't pass through me so much as replace me, the way a flood replaces a streambed, and for one horrible instant I understood what Quill had been trying to tell me on the road: the bells weren't noise. They were a wall. And I had spent my whole life standing outside walls, listening to everything that moved behind them, and now I was walking into the one place in the world where the wall ran all the way around and all the way down.

Then the second bell answered the first, and the third answered the second, and the whole city began to speak in bronze, and my gift—

My gift went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

I want to be precise about this, because people imagine losing a sense as darkness, as absence. It wasn't absence. It was *static*. Imagine hearing every heart in a village beat, every bird's small hungers, every dog dreaming, the slow green murmur of grass deciding to grow—and then imagine all of it smeared into one shrieking smear of interference, close enough to touch and impossible to parse. That was Corvenna. The animals were still there—I could feel them, hundreds of them, thousands, dogs and rats and pigeons and horses packed into the streets ahead of us—but the bells stood between us like a crowd of shouting men, and every attempt to reach toward even one small voice came back shredded.

I gripped the saddle horn. My vision whited at the edges.

"Rafael." Sera's voice, very close, very level. She knew. Of course she knew; she'd watched me listen to her horse complain about its bit for three weeks. "Breathe. Eyes down. You're swaying."

"I can't hear anything." My own voice sounded strange, flattened, as if the bells had gotten inside my skull and were ringing there too. "Sera, I can't—it's like being deaf, except worse, because I can feel them all and I can't—"

"Rafael." Her hand found my knee under the cloak, brief and hard. "Later. Right now you are a mute servant having a headache, and mute servants don't cry in checkpoint lines. Later I'll find you a dark room and you can fall apart properly. Deal?"

It was such a Sera thing to say—brusque, practical, and underneath it, unmistakably, *I've got you*—that it worked. I breathed. I put my eyes down. I arranged my face into vacancy, and the line moved, and a guard with a bored mouth and tired eyes waved our little party through the gates without a second glance, and the City of Bells closed over us like water.

* * *

Inside the walls it was worse and better. Worse, because the bells were everywhere, pouring out of every tower, and there were more towers than I could count—on churches, on granaries, on guildhalls, on private houses, little bells and monstrous ones, some ancient and green with verdigris, some so new their bronze still shone. Better, because the streets themselves were so loud with ordinary human life that my other senses had something to hold onto. Vendors shouted. Carters swore. Children ran underfoot. If I kept my attention on the human racket, the static in my head became almost bearable—a toothache instead of a knife wound.

Pip walked close to my elbow. He had gone quiet in the way he did when he was frightened and determined not to show it, chin up, hands jammed in his pockets.

"It's beautiful," he said, in the tone of someone reporting for duty. "Isn't it? From far away it must be beautiful."

"Everything's beautiful from far away," Sera said. "That's what far away is for."

We turned off the main thoroughfare into streets that climbed and narrowed, and the crowds thinned, and that was where I made my mistake. Because in a quiet street, with fewer human voices to anchor me, my gift surged back up on its own—reaching, grasping, desperate for any clear signal—and what it found was a dog.

It was tied outside a cooper's shop, an old brown bitch with grey at her muzzle, and she was lonely. That was all. Just lonely, the way dogs get, left on a short rope through a long day. Her loneliness came through the bell-static in fragments, like a song heard through a storm: *alone, alone, alone, the rope, the rope, the rope.*

And I couldn't help her. That was the thing that broke me, right there on a cobbled street in the enemy's own city. In the valley I could have sat with her for five minutes and she'd have settled. Here, every word I tried to shape dissolved before it reached her. I stood in front of a lonely dog with the one gift I had ever been glad of, and the bells ate it out of my mouth, and she looked up at me with her brown eyes and wagged her tail anyway, because dogs forgive us everything, even the things we can't help.

"Come on," Sera said quietly, and steered me by the shoulder, and I went.

* * *

We sheltered through the afternoon in the loft of a dye-house that Sera remembered from her garrison days—an old contact, a woman named Bree who took coin without questions and asked none, which in Corvenna was the highest form of hospitality. From the loft window I could see perhaps nine bell towers, and I counted the intervals between their ringings, and gave up when the pattern refused to hold.

"They don't stop at night," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd been dreading the answer since noon.

"No," Quill said.

He was curled on a bolt of undyed wool, and he looked, for the first time since I'd known him, genuinely old. Foxes age strangely—they carry their years in their eyes rather than their bodies—but in the grey light of that loft, Quill's eyes held centuries.

"When Aldous was crowned," he said, "there were bells, of course. Every city has bells. But Corvenna had forty-one towers, and they rang for services and weddings and fires, and between those times, the city was quiet enough to hear yourself think. Quiet enough to hear *other* things think." His ear twitched. "The king used to sit in the palace garden at dusk with me on his knee, and he'd listen to the rooks going home, and he'd say, *Quill, what are they saying tonight?* And I'd tell him. Gossip, mostly. Rooks are terrible gossips. He loved it. He said it was the only honest news in the kingdom."

Pip had stopped fidgeting. Even Sera, who polished her sword when she didn't want to feel things, had gone still.

"A year ago," Quill continued, "the ringing started. New bells commissioned in every quarter. Old bells recast larger. Ringing schedules extended—first to midnight, then past it, then continuous, tower relieving tower so the sound never died. The guilds were told it was for piety. The people were told it was for victory over plague, over rebellion, over whatever fear was fashionable that month. And the people of Corvenna, being people, adjusted. They learned to sleep through it. They learned to stop hearing it." His muzzle lifted toward the window, toward the sound. "But it was never for them. It was a cage built of sound, and the thing it was meant to keep silent was the king himself."

"The true king," Pip said softly. "You said he's still alive. Still in there."

"He is." Quill's voice frayed at the edges. "And he is screaming, and has been screaming for a year, and the bells were raised so that no one—not his guards, not his priests, not the beasts of his own city—would ever hear him." He looked at me then, and his eyes were wet, and a crying fox is a sight I hope never to grow accustomed to. "I heard him once. Through everything. One night when the wind was right and a tower fell silent to change its bell, I heard him, Rafael. One word. He said *help*, in the old speech, the way his grandmother taught him when he was a boy, before anyone told a prince that talking to beasts was beneath the crown. One word. And then the bells swallowed it."

I sat down on the wool bolts because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

All day the static had been agony. All day I had thought of it as my suffering, my loss, the muffling of *my* gift. And underneath the bells, all along, had been a man. A drowning man, screaming in the only language left to him, into a wall of bronze built specifically so that no one like me would ever hear.

"How am I supposed to find him in this?" I asked. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "Quill, I can't hear a dog three feet away. How does anyone—"

"That," said Quill, "is why we are not doing this alone. There are people in this city who hate the bells as much as we do. Some of them for the same reason." He rose, shook himself, and his old sharpness came back over the grief like a mask sliding home. "Bakers. Lamplighters. A priest, if you can believe it, though he's nervous enough to faint at his own shadow. They hide the gifted beneath the city. They've been waiting a long time for someone who could hear what's under the ringing."

"And you trust them?" Sera asked.

"I trust that the enemy of the thing that wears the crown is, provisionally, my friend," Quill said. "In Corvenna, that's the best guarantee on offer."

* * *

We went at dusk, when the shift-change of the bells made the streets briefly chaotic, and Sera led us down through the city's bones—cellar stairs, coal chutes, a culvert that smelled of the river and older secrets. Pip held my sleeve the whole way. He was fourteen and brave and terrified, and I remembered being fourteen and brave and terrified, and I let him hold on.

The static changed as we descended. I noticed it first as a lessening—the bells thinning above us, stone swallowing bronze—and then, in the last stretch of tunnel, as something else entirely. A clarity. Faint, fractured, but real: the warmth of bodies behind a door, the quick hearts of hidden animals, and threaded through it all, so faint I might have invented it, a voice.

Not a beast's voice. A man's. Far away, deep in the city's heart, under a thousand tons of ringing bronze, saying something over and over in the old speech.

I couldn't make out the words. But I knew the shape of them. Anyone who has ever listened to a drowning creature knows the shape.

*Help. Help. Help.*

Quill stopped at the door and looked back at me, and whatever he saw in my face made him nod slowly, as if I'd passed a test neither of us had named.

"You heard him," he said.

"Barely."

"Barely is how it starts." He touched his nose to the door, once, twice—a knock with meaning in it, a rhythm I suspected had been agreed upon long ago. "Wait until you meet the woman behind this door. She'll know what the song fragments mean. And Rafael—" the fox's eyes gleamed in the dark, "—wait until you tell her what you just heard. Because if the king can reach you through all that bronze, even barely, even broken—"

The door opened inward, spilling lamplight and the smell of bread and the sound of many hushed voices, and Quill finished his sentence with his whole body, stepping through:

"—then the bells aren't as strong as it thinks. And neither are we."

I followed him into the light, carrying a drowned man's voice in my chest like an ember I refused to let go out.
