## 18. A Cellar Full of Strays

I have been a fox for a very long time, and in that time I have learned that the surest way to find a hidden door is to follow someone who looks like they are trying not to look like they're following you.

Corvenna was full of such people.

The city had changed since I last walked its streets—changed the way a face changes when grief moves in and never leaves. The bells rang, as they always rang now, a low bronze mutter from every tower, every quarter hour, as if the city itself were being kept awake on purpose. Which it was. But beneath the bells, beneath the checkpoints with their bounty boards and their bored men with sharp eyes, there ran another Corvenna: a city of glances held a half-second too long, of bread left where bread had no business being left, of cellar doors that were never locked and never opened either.

I knew how to read that city. I had helped build it, in the small ways a fox can build things—with teeth, with theft, with an unerring instinct for which lamplighter fed strays and which one kicked them.

The trouble was getting my companions through it without anyone dying of fright.

* * *

"Explain to me again," Sera said, for the fourth time since we'd entered the city at dusk through the tannery district, "why we're trusting a fox."

"Because," I said, trotting backward ahead of her so I could enjoy her expression, "if I wanted you dead, I've had four months of opportunities, several of them while you slept with your mouth open."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be accurate. There's a difference, and knights used to know it."

Rafael laughed—that quiet, startled laugh of theirs, still new enough to surprise them when it happened—and Sera shot them a look that would have curdled milk. Rafael, to their credit, did not stop laughing. Four months on the road will do that. It wears the polish off people and shows you what's underneath, and what was underneath Sera Vane, beneath the suspicion and the soldier's posture and the sword she checked eleven times a day, was a woman who was desperately tired of being alone and would have bitten out her own tongue before admitting it.

Pip, meanwhile, was pressing his nose against everything. He was fourteen, he was gifted, and he had never seen a city larger than Thornhollow, so Corvenna hit him the way a wave hits a sandcastle.

"There's so many *people*," he whispered.

"And so many ears," Sera said. "Head down. Walk like you're bored."

"I don't know how to walk like I'm bored."

"Then walk like me."

"You walk like you're about to kill someone."

"Bored *and* about to kill someone. It's called efficiency."

We moved through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath it all, something sweeter and wronger—the smell I had been dreading since the valley. Magic, everywhere, thick as river fog, but magic under strain. Every gifted soul in this city was holding themselves shut, clamping down on what they were, day after day, the way you clamp your hand over a wound. I could smell the effort of it. It smelled like iron and fear and old candle wax.

Rafael felt it worse than any of us. I watched them walk with their shoulders creeping up toward their ears, flinching at nothing visible, because to Rafael every mind behind every window was a voice, and the bells were screaming over all of it, and there were thousands upon thousands of voices here, more than they had ever borne at once. They had learned, on the road, to close the doors. But a thousand doors is not the same as ten, and I saw the sweat on their brow despite the cold.

"How much farther?" they asked through their teeth.

"Two streets," I said. "Possibly three. The baker moved his entrance after the winter arrests."

"The baker has an entrance?"

"Everyone has an entrance, cub. Most people just don't know what their own house is for."

Here is what I had not told them, because telling them would have meant admitting how long it had been since I'd set paw inside these walls, and I am vain enough to want my competence taken on faith: I was navigating by smell and by memory of a city that had been rearranged twice since Aldous stopped being Aldous. Streets renamed. Gates closed. A whole quarter pulled down for the spirit's new bell tower, its families scattered like seed from a shaken pod. I had loved this city the way you love a person who doesn't know you exist, and walking through what Vhalor had made of it was like visiting a friend who'd been ill for a year and pretending not to notice the hollows in their cheeks.

But two streets later—three, if you counted the alley I refused to count—we arrived at a bakery that was not a bakery.

It was a bakery, technically. Bread was sold there. But the ovens ran half the night baking loaves that never reached a counter, and the cellar below held more people than flour, and the man who owned it, Bramwell, was built like his own ovens and cried as easily as bread rises.

I scratched the third brick to the left of the cellar doors. Twice. Pause. Once.

Above us, a shutter creaked. A slot slid open, and an eye appeared, and the eye found me, and the eye went wide.

"No," said the eye, in the voice of a large frightened baker. "No, no, no. You're supposed to be dead."

"Hello, Bramwell," I said. "Lovely to see you too."

* * *

Bramwell got the door open with hands that shook so badly he dropped the bar twice. He was broader than I remembered and grayer, and when he looked at me his eyes did something complicated—grief and relief fighting for the same ground.

"They said the palace hounds got you," he managed. "Three years ago. After the king—" He stopped. Swallowed. "After the king changed."

"The palace hounds got a fox," I said. "It was a very convincing fox. It was, in fact, me, running in a highly convincing manner." I sat back on my haunches and looked up at him. "Bramwell. These people need the cellar. And I need Odile. Wake her."

"She'll be furious."

"Wonderful. Fury means she's alive. Fetch her."

He fetched her—or rather, he led us down, because once the door was open there was no putting the moment back in the bottle, and Sera had already drawn her knife and positioned herself between Rafael and the dark stairs with the reflexes of a woman who trusted no cellar in any kingdom. Pip went down first, eager as a puppy. Rafael went slowly, one hand on the wall, jaw tight, drowning quietly in the noise of a hundred minds stacked above and below them like plates.

And then the cellar opened around us, and Rafael stopped drowning, and stared.

I had told them, on the road, in my way—which is to say obliquely, and only when pressed—that they might not be the only ones. I do not think they believed me. I do not think I fully believed myself until I stood in that cellar again and counted.

Forty-one souls. Forty-two counting the baby.

They lay on pallets along the walls, sat at trestle tables, hung washing-lines of bandages between the ceiling beams. A girl no older than Pip sat cross-legged in the corner making a dead sparrow's feathers ripple as though wind moved them that did not. An old man mended shoes with his eyes shut and his hands moving faster than hands should. Two sisters who glowed faintly in the dark, banked down to embers, the way Marta's son must once have learned to bank himself. A lamplighter whose lamps lit themselves a heartbeat before he touched them. A woman with river-green eyes who could hold her breath for a quarter hour and speak to fish, and who was currently arguing with a carp in a barrel, judging by her expression.

Every one of them looked up when we came down the stairs. Every one of them went still, the particular stillness of prey that has practiced being furniture.

And Rafael stood on the bottom stair and made a sound I had never heard from them—not a word, not a sob, something between a laugh and a wound opening.

"They're—" Rafael's voice cracked. "Quill. They're all—"

"Yes," I said gently. "Welcome to the resistance. Mind the carp."

* * *

What happened next took perhaps a minute and undid something in Rafael that four months of road had not touched.

A child came first—they always do; children have not yet learned that wonder is dangerous. A boy of six or seven detached himself from a woman's skirts and toddled straight up to Rafael, staring, and said, in the way children say things, far too loudly: "You're loud."

"Pip—" Sera started, reaching for him.

"He means my gift," Rafael said. Their voice shook. They crouched down to the boy's level. "Yes. I'm sorry. Am I hurting you?"

The boy considered this with great seriousness. "No. You sound like the goats at my nan's farm. Before." His face did the thing children's faces do when *before* arrives—a small collapse, quickly patched. "Nan says I have to be quiet all the time now. Quiet inside too."

"I know," Rafael said. "I was quiet inside for twenty-three years."

The boy nodded, satisfied, as though this established a firm acquaintance, and took Rafael's hand, and Rafael let him, and I turned away to give them the privacy of it, because some moments belong to the two creatures having them and no one else, even a fox.

Around them, the cellar was waking. Word moved down the rows of pallets like fire down a fuse: *a listener, a real one, a beast-speaker.* The shoe-mender stopped working. The glowing sisters brightened despite themselves, then caught each other's eye and dimmed again, trained. The woman with the fish leaned over the barrel and shushed it, which I mention only because the carp, apparently, had opinions about our arrival.

Sera stood apart from all of it, arms crossed, doing her statue impression, but I caught her watching Rafael with the boy, and her face did something it almost never did in company. She looked, briefly, like a woman remembering why she'd burned her own life down rather than burn a child instead.

Pip, naturally, had already found the girl with the feathers and was showing her his hands. "Watch," he was saying. "Watch, watch—I broke this cup on the road, right, completely broke it, and look—" He produced a cup from his pack that was whole except for a hairline seam, a scar rather than a wound, and the feather-girl's eyes went round as coins.

Within the hour, I suspected, they would have organized the entire cellar into games. Children are efficient that way.

* * *

Odile came down the stairs at midnight.

I heard her before I saw her—hear her the way you hear weather: a pressure change, a hush. The cellar's noise dimmed as she descended, not because she commanded it but because everyone in that room owed her their lives and knew it, and love and fear make the same silence.

She was smaller than I remembered. Court mages are supposed to be imposing; Odile had always defeated expectation by looking like someone's maiden aunt, gray-streaked hair pinned carelessly, ink stains on her fingers, spectacles pushed up into her hair and forgotten there. Disgrace had added lines to her face and subtracted nothing from her spine. She crossed that cellar like a ship crosses a harbor—unhurried, certain of her draft.

Her eyes found me first.

"You," she said.

"Madam Mage."

"Don't 'madam mage' me. Three years, Quill. Three years I've told them you were dead because the alternative was telling them the crown's creature was loose in my city, and now you stroll in with—" she counted, lips thinning, "—a knight, two children, and a stranger, and expect bread."

"You're welcome."

"*Expect. Bread.*"

"There's also the matter of the end of the world," I said, "but I find it goes down easier after bread."

That, at least, won a twitch at the corner of her mouth. Then her gaze moved past me to Rafael—to the stranger standing in her cellar with a baker's boy's hand in theirs and forty-one pairs of eyes fixed on them—and I watched Odile do what Odile does, which is assess. I watched her take in the road-worn clothes, the valley accent carefully flattened, the way Rafael's head tilted as though listening to something no one else could hear.

Because Rafael was listening. Even muffled by the bells, even drowned, Rafael was hearing this room—all those small banked gifts, all those held breaths—and I saw the exact moment they stopped trying to close the doors and simply stood in the doorway instead, letting it wash through them like standing in warm rain.

"You're hearing them," Odile said quietly.

"Some," Rafael said. "The bells—it's like hearing through wool. But yes. They're all so *tired*. Holding it in. All of them, all the time." Their voice frayed. "I thought I was the only one. My whole life, I thought—"

"You were never the only one," Odile said. "You were only the one who hadn't been found yet."

* * *

Bramwell fed everyone, because Bramwell's answer to every crisis including his own death would be to feed everyone. Fresh bread, real butter hoarded against regulations, stewed apples. Pip ate enough for three boys and fell asleep sitting up halfway through his second bowl, mid-sentence, in the middle of explaining to the feather-girl how bridges worked. Sera ate standing, near the stairs, near the exits, and I did not tease her for it, because I had seen what hunters' checkpoints did to her composure and I knew the vigilance was not paranoia but scar tissue.

And Odile took Rafael to the far corner, to a table beneath a hanging lantern, and sat them down across from her, and said:

"Now. Tell me why a beast-speaker walks into my city four months before the thirteenth moon, escorted by a fox bound to a dead king's bloodline."

So Rafael told her.

I listened from the shadows by the wine racks, as is my habit and my pleasure, and I will admit freely—for who else would I admit anything to?—that I watched her face the whole time, because I wanted to see the moment it landed. The moment the fragments of the binding song, hummed softly in Rafael's careful, unpracticed voice, in the speech-of-the-body that is no language at all and every language, reached her ears.

I expected recognition. I expected gravity.

I did not expect the color to leave her face entirely, so that in the lantern light she looked carved from tallow.

"Where," she said, and her voice had gone thin as wire, "where did you get that?"

"A fen in the east," Rafael said. "An elder named Maren. She said it was only part of it—fragments. That the rest was lost."

"It isn't lost." Odile's hands had gone flat on the table, pressing down as though the table might otherwise float away. "It isn't lost, it's *locked*. In the palace archive, under triple seal, in a case warded by three generations of court mages—including, briefly, and I say this with lingering shame, me." She took off her spectacles, which she did not need, and polished them, which she did even less. "I catalogued that case when I was nineteen years old and ambitious and thought the seals were ceremonial. Do you know what I was told it contained?"

"No."

"A hymnal. Old royal funeral verses. Nothing." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "I was nineteen. I didn't ask why a hymnal needed wards that could boil the blood of an unauthorized reader. I didn't ask why the archive ledger listed it under a false title. I was *nineteen*, and the king was kind, and the realm was at peace, and I filed it away between a book of orchard grafting techniques and a treatise on drainage."

"Odile," Rafael said carefully. "Those fragments. What are they?"

She put the spectacles back on. Looked at Rafael over the top of them for a long moment—looking, I think, not at a stranger but at the shape of a story she had dreaded for a century without knowing its name.

"They're the binding song," she said. "The true one. The one that caged the thing wearing the king's face. And if you're carrying fragments of it in your head, child, then somewhere in the last hundred years, someone taught beast-speech to someone they shouldn't have, and the oldest secret in this kingdom has started leaking." She rose, abruptly, and began pacing, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a woman building a cathedral out of panic and architecture. "Which means the spirit knows. Or will know. Or—" She stopped. Turned. Stared at Rafael with an expression I had last seen on the face of a surgeon reading an X-ray. "Sing it again. The fragment. Slowly."

Rafael sang it again. Slowly.

And this time I heard it too, really heard it, and understood why the fen elder had needed four days to teach what filled less than a minute: it was not melody. It was meaning worn so deep it had become music, the way a path becomes a road. Notes that were also gestures, gestures that were also commands, and underneath all of it a single repeated instruction, patient and terrible, the verbal equivalent of a hand closing:

*Stay. Stay. Stay.*

The cellar had gone silent around us. The glowing sisters had brightened without noticing. The carp, I am told, had gone still.

"That's a cage," Odile whispered. "That's not a song, that's a *cage*, and whoever wrote it wrote it to be sung by—" She stopped pacing. Sat down. Very carefully removed her spectacles again and set them on the table, aligned parallel to the table's edge, which is what Odile does instead of screaming.

"By a beast-speaker," Rafael finished quietly.

"By a beast-speaker who was willing to die," Odile said. "Because that's the price written into the structure itself. I can read the grammar of it, child, even in fragments. The song doesn't close around the spirit. It closes around the singer and the spirit both. It's a lock that requires the key to stay inside the door." She looked up, and her eyes behind the smudged lenses were fierce and wet at once. "Whoever sang this last time did not survive it. And you're planning to sing it in four months, in the throne room, in front of the thing it was built to bury."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above us, muffled by floors and stone and forty sleeping strangers, the bells rang the hour—one, two, three—and Rafael flinched at every stroke, and I saw them thinking what I was thinking, which was: *the bells. The bells that drown all voices. The bells that never stop.*

But that revelation belonged to a later night. That night, Rafael only reached across the table and covered Odile's ink-stained hand with their own, and said:

"Then I'd better learn it properly. Will you teach me?"

Odile looked at their hand on hers for a long moment. When she spoke again, the archness had gone out of her voice, and what remained was older and wearier and, I think, relieved beyond her own permission.

"Child," she said, "I've been waiting three years for someone to ask me that."

* * *

Later—much later, when the cellar slept and only the lamp over the wine racks still burned—I climbed the cellar stairs and let myself out through the bakery's back door into the blue-black cold before dawn, because foxes sleep poorly in cellars, and because there was something I needed to check.

I sat on the loading step and looked east, toward the palace hill, where the great tower stood black against the stars, crowned with its ring of bells.

They were ringing. Of course they were ringing. They rang at every hour now, and sometimes between hours, and sometimes for no reason the bell-keepers would admit to. Tonight they rang slow and low, a sound more felt than heard, rolling down through the sleeping city like a tide.

And beneath the bronze, beneath the hunger in the sound, so faint that even I might have imagined it—

—a voice.

Not Vhalor's voice. Not the smooth, warm, devouring patience that wore the king's throat. Something thinner. Something ragged. Something that had been screaming into the inside of a bell for a very long time and had not yet learned to stop.

I sat very still on the cold step, an old fox with an old failure sitting heavy in his chest, and listened to a dead man beg.

Then I went back downstairs, because dawn was coming, and dawn would bring questions, and I had spent three years deciding whether the truth about Aldous was a kindness or a cruelty.

Four months, I told myself. We have four months.

The bells rang on, and did not agree with me.
