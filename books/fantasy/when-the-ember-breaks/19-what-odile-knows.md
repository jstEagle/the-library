## 19. What Odile Knows

I had rehearsed the speech for eleven years. In the cellar of a baker's shop in Corvenna, with flour dust sifting down between the floorboards like the world's most melancholy snow, I finally gave it.

It did not go as planned. It never does.

Let me begin where all my troubles began: with a name I had not spoken aloud since the night they took my staff and my rank and my good standing at court, and left me only my wits, which were, I am told, considerable, though you would not know it from how I spent them.

"Vhalor," I said.

The word dropped into the cellar like a stone into a well. The baker's boy—Tomas, who kept watch at the trapdoor and pretended he wasn't terrified every hour of his life—flinched above us. Even the rats went quiet, and the rats in that cellar chattered through everything, including the time I accidentally set my own sleeve alight demonstrating a cantrip to three hiding children.

Rafael Almeida sat across from me on an upturned flour barrel, hands folded in their lap like a student awaiting a lecture. Sera Vane stood by the wall with her arms crossed, which I gathered was her resting posture, her thinking posture, and possibly her sleeping posture. The fox sat on the table itself, which no one had objected to, so I decided it was permitted.

"The Hunger Beneath," I continued, because once started, I have never learned to stop. "That is what the old texts call it. Not a demon, precisely. Not a god, thank all the quiet stars. Something older than either category, from before we drew tidy lines around such things. And one hundred and six years ago, it was bound beneath this city by a beast-speaker named Ysolde Marr."

The fox's ears pricked forward. "You know the binding," Quill said. It was not quite a question. Foxes do not ask questions when they already suspect answers; they simply hand them to you and watch what you do.

"I know *of* it," I said. "There is a difference, and the difference is most of a library."

* * *

You should understand something about disgrace. It is not a single event. People imagine it as a door slamming—one bad night, one accusation, done. But real disgrace is a long erosion. First they take your chambers in the palace. Then your students. Then your correspondence privileges, then your access to the archive, and each loss is small enough that you convince yourself you can live without it, right up until you are living in a baker's cellar organizing smuggled children and realizing you cannot remember the last time anyone asked your professional opinion about anything except whether the stew needed more salt.

(It did. It always did. Salt was the one area where my expertise remained unquestioned.)

So when Rafael Almeida walked into my cellar three days ago—a stranger smelling of goat and mountain air, humming fragments of a song under their breath without knowing they were doing it—I felt something I had not felt in eleven years.

Recognition.

Not of the person. Of the *song*. I had read its notation exactly twice, in the restricted folios, before the archive was closed to me. A binding-song, the archivist had said, waving me off. Pre-dynastic. Beast-speech rendered into musical script, which is itself an absurdity, like writing down the smell of rain. Nobody sings it. Nobody *can* sing it. It is a curiosity, a dead language's dead letter.

Except someone was humming it in my cellar, badly, the way you hum a thing you heard once in a dream.

"You sang at the fen," I had said to them, the first night. "The elder there—the dreamer, Wrenna. She taught you."

"She taught me what she had," Rafael said now, in the present, in the floury dark. "Fragments. Her grandmother's grandmother carried pieces of it out of the city a century ago, when the binding was made. She said the whole of it was split. Half stayed here."

"It did," I said. "In the palace archive. Restricted folio, third vault, catalogued under a number rather than a title, because whoever shelved it understood exactly what it was." I paused. "I know the shelf. I know the vault. I know the rotation of the guards, or I knew it, eleven years ago, before—" I gestured at the cellar, the children's cots, the barrel of smuggled turnips that served as my throne. "Before all this."

Sera Vane spoke for the first time. "How do we get into the palace archive?"

And there it was. No preamble, no weighing of risks, no polite interval in which reasonable people agree that the plan is insane. Eleven years of careful, invisible, grindingly patient work—hiding the gifted, moving families, keeping the network alive one loaf of bread at a time—and this goatherd and this disgraced knight wanted to walk into the hungriest building in the kingdom and steal a song.

I should have refused. Let the record show that some part of me, the part that had kept forty-three people alive beneath a city that eats its own, knew refusal was correct.

Instead I heard myself say: "Tell me everything you know about the bells."

* * *

Here is what I told them, and I will set it down properly now, because if I am to be hanged for treason I would like the charge sheet to be accurate.

One hundred and six years ago, King Aldous the Third—no relation to our current occupant, or perhaps the very closest relation, depending on how you define a man—sat on the throne while something beneath Corvenna grew restless. Vhalor had been bound in the deep foundations, in the old crypts that predate the palace, bound by Ysolde Marr with a song sung in beast-speech, the language of the body, the language that predates crowns.

Ysolde died doing it. That is the part the histories omit, or rather, the part they record as a footnote: *the beast-speaker perished of exertion.* As if she had run a race. As if singing yourself inside-out to cage a hunger older than kingdoms were comparable to a summer festival footrace.

The binding held. But bindings are not walls. They are doors held shut, and doors require a hand on them, always, forever, and Ysolde Marr was ash within the year. So the mages of that generation did what mages always do when faced with a problem too large to solve: they built a machine to hold the door instead.

They called it the Ring of Bells.

Corvenna's bells—you have heard them, everyone has heard them, they ring at dawn and dusk and every hour between, they ring for coronations and funerals and Tuesdays—they are not bells. They are anchors. Each tower a nail, each peal a re-tightening of Ysolde's dying grip. The city has lived its entire modern life inside a song it mistook for civic pride.

"And the crown?" Sera asked.

"The crown is newer," I said. "A century of holding the door wore the mages down, generation by generation, until the last of them died and there was no one left who understood what the bells were *for*. The knowledge rotted. The ritual became routine. And a door held shut by habit, with no hand behind it—" I spread my hands. "Something beneath found the hinge."

Quill had gone very still on the table, in the way of animals deciding whether to fight. "The king," the fox said. "When did it take him?"

"A year ago, by my count. The bells changed first. Did none of you notice? They began ringing at night. Off-hours. Off-pattern. Everyone thought it was the new Master of Ceremonies being eccentric." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bitter. "Eccentric. I sat in that court for nine years and I noticed the bells and I said nothing, because saying something meant admitting what the bells meant, and admitting that meant admitting I had been sitting atop a cage my whole career reading poetry to it."

"That's not fair to yourself," Rafael said quietly.

"Nothing is fair to myself. That is why I am useful." I stood, because sitting still during confessions has never been among my talents. "Here is what matters. The spirit wears Aldous. It feeds on the gifted—it harvests them, ships them to the palace, and I have seen the ledgers, I know the numbers, and I will not repeat them tonight because Tomas is listening and he is fourteen. The feeding sustains it, but the *bells* contain it. Turn the binding inside out—that is what the song does, that is what it has always done—and you do not destroy Vhalor. You unmake its anchor. You give it back its hunger and take away its shape."

"And the true king?" Rafael asked. "Aldous himself?"

I looked at them for a long moment. This goatherd who had crossed half the kingdom on rumor and a fox's word, who hummed dead songs without knowing it, who asked about the *man* before asking about the victory.

"If the body lives," I said, "the man may live in it. Buried. Caged in his own flesh the way Vhalor was caged beneath the city. Whether anything of him remains after a year of that—" I shook my head. "I don't know. No one knows. There is no text for it. Believe me, I have looked. I have read everything, and the honest answer is that we are attempting something that has never been attempted, based on a song half of which is locked in a vault guarded by men who would burn every one of us, sung by someone who learned it from fragments passed down through marsh-water generations."

Silence. The good kind, the kind where people are actually thinking instead of merely waiting to talk.

Then Sera Vane uncrossed her arms—the first time I had seen her do that—and said, "The vault. Third one. What's the guard rotation?"

And I understood, with the particular vertigo of a woman watching her own ruin approach at a comfortable walking pace, that I was going to tell her.

* * *

We talked until the candle burned to a puddle. I gave them what I had: the vault's location behind the false shelving in the west archive, the clerk rotations, the fact that military credentials still opened certain doors because the palace bureaucracy had the survival instincts of a fat carp and updated nothing. Sera absorbed it all with the flat attention of a soldier memorizing ground. Rafael asked questions I did not expect—about the song's structure, about whether beast-speech could be *written* truly or only approximated, about whether the archive copy might differ from the fen fragments.

"Differ how?" I asked.

"If the mages transcribed it," Rafael said, "they wrote it down as music. Notes on a page. But the song isn't notes. Wrenna said the words aren't words—they're movements. Breath and bone. If the archive version is just the skeleton, the fen fragments might be the blood."

I stared at them. Eleven years in exile, and the first person to hand me a genuinely original scholarly insight was a goat-herder from a magic-dead valley.

"I could kiss you," I said. "Professionally."

"Please don't," said Sera.

"Noted."

Quill leapt down from the table and paced the length of the cellar, tail low, thinking fox-thoughts. When he turned, he looked at me with those amber eyes that see rather more than is comfortable.

"You said the last mages died not knowing," the fox said. "But you knew. You figured it out alone, from books. Why didn't the spirit come for you?"

Ah. There it was. The question underneath the evening, the one I had been waiting for since Rafael first hummed that fragment.

"Because I'm not worth eating," I said. "Vhalor feeds on the gifted. On magic. I was court mage, yes, but my talent is bookkeeping, essentially—categorizing, preserving, knowing where things are shelved. Useful. Nourishing? No. It wants the ones like Ysolde Marr. The ones like—" the fox's gaze flicked sideways, and I finished the sentence because someone had to, "—like Rafael."

Everyone looked at Rafael. Rafael, to their enormous credit, did not flinch. They looked back at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors, years ago, the expression of someone recalculating the price of a thing they had already decided to buy.

"So the song needs me," they said. "And the song is the one thing that can kill me."

"Singing it *completely*, at the moment of unbinding, in the presence of the thing itself—yes. Ysolde Marr's death was not incidental. It was structural. The binding takes the singer's—" I searched for the word and hated every candidate. "Presence. Everything that makes the gift a gift. Some part of you goes into the cage and does not come out. Or all of you. The texts disagree, and the texts were written by survivors, and survivors are unreliable narrators of their own mercy."

"Cheerful," said Sera.

"I'm a librarian at heart. We deal in what's true, not what's pleasant."

Rafael was quiet a long moment. Then: "The fen fragments. The elder said the song must be sung by one the spirit cannot devour. Do you believe that's possible? Can anyone be indigestible?"

I thought of Ysolde Marr, whose portrait I had once found in a sealed folio—a plain woman with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who had already said goodbye. I thought of the ledger numbers I would not speak aloud. I thought of the bells ringing off-pattern over my head for a year while I organized turnips.

"No," I said honestly. "I think the elder was offering comfort, and comfort is a fine thing but poor engineering. But—" and here I leaned forward, because this was the part that mattered, the part that had kept me awake since Rafael arrived, "—Ysolde bound it *alone*. One voice, one gift, one death. You are not alone. You have a knight, a fox, a fen's worth of fragments, and a cellar full of people who owe this kingdom nothing and will give it everything anyway. If the song can be shared—if the burden can be distributed across many throats, animal and otherwise—then perhaps the price is paid in portions. Perhaps no one pays all of it."

"Perhaps," said Sera.

"I am a mage, not a prophet. 'Perhaps' is the strongest word in my vocabulary and I use it with dignity."

* * *

It was near dawn when we finished—near dawn, which in Corvenna means the bells rang the hour, and we all flinched, and I saw Rafael hear something in the peal that I could not, their head tilting, their face going strange.

"What is it?" I asked.

Rafael was silent a moment. When they answered, their voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices go when they are carrying something fragile up a long stair.

"The bells," they said. "Underneath the ringing. There's a voice."

I felt the cold arrive in my spine and travel downward, the old scholar's cold, the cold of a hypothesis confirming itself.

"A voice," I repeated.

"Faint. Like someone shouting through water." Rafael's hands had closed around each other, knuckles white. "Odile. Is there any chance—any chance at all—that the king is still in there? Still alive under it?"

And I, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of the survivable lie, who had lied to hunters at doors and to children in cellars and to myself in mirrors, found that I could not produce one.

"There's a chance," I said. "Small. Unproven. The kind of chance that gets people killed chasing it."

Rafael nodded slowly, and I watched them make a decision—not with drama, not with a speech, but the way decisions are actually made, in the quiet behind the eyes.

"Then we'd better get the other half of the song," they said. "Before whatever's left of him runs out of voice."

Above us, faint through the floorboards, the bells finished their peal and the silence afterward seemed, for the first time in my hearing, less like rest and more like a held breath.

I began, mentally, drafting the plan for the archive job, and noted—with the detached professionalism that has been my sole consistent virtue—that the first line of any such plan must be: *assume we do not all come back.*

The second line was worse. It read: *decide who.*
