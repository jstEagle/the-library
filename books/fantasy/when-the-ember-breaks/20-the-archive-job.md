## 20. The Archive Job

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a palace at night. I learned it as a young knight, walking the corridors on watch, and I had forgotten how much I hated it. It isn't peace. It's the silence of a held breath, of a house where something enormous is sleeping and everyone tiptoes so as not to wake it.

I was about to go stomping through its bedroom.

"You're certain the credentials still work," Rafael whispered. We crouched in the shadow of a tannery two streets from the palace's western wall, and they were doing that thing with their hands again—turning an invisible object over and over, worrying it like a stone.

"They'll work," I said. "That's rather the problem."

"Explain that to me one more time."

"The archive requires a serving officer's seal to enter after dark. My seal hasn't been struck from the rolls because—" I paused, choosing the honest version, "—because no one wanted to be the clerk who formally erased Sera Vane. I was well-liked. I was decorated. Erasing me meant admitting the crown had been wrong about me, and bureaucracies would rather die than admit that. So my name sits in their books like a splinter under the skin. Tonight I use it to open a door. Tomorrow, if anything goes wrong, it becomes the rope they hang me with."

"And you're smiling about this."

I hadn't realized I was. "It's a good rope," I said. "I tied it myself."

Rafael looked at me for a long moment in the dark. There was lamplight from the tannery yard catching the edge of their face, and I saw something in their expression I couldn't name—worry, maybe, or the particular tenderness people get when they've decided not to argue with someone they love. Then they reached over and straightened my collar, which had gone crooked under the servant's smock I'd wear until the last possible moment.

"When the bells stop," they said, "you have until the bells start again."

"I know."

"If you're not out—"

"I'll be out." I caught their hand before they could fuss with the collar a second time. "Rafael. I have walked into worse rooms than this one wearing worse lies than mine. Go make your noise."

They went. And I stood alone in the alley for three heartbeats, letting myself feel the fear properly, all of it, every cold finger of it—because Odile had taught me that much, at least: a soldier who pretends she isn't afraid makes stupid mistakes. Name the fear. Let it sit in your chest like a stone. Then pick up the stone and carry it.

I picked it up and went to work.

* * *

The diversion began, as far as I could tell, with pigeons.

I heard them first—a rising clatter of wings from the rooftops along Tanner's Row, hundreds of them lifting at once, wheeling in a great grey spiral against the moon. Then came the dogs. Not barking, exactly. Singing. A low, wavering chorus from a dozen different alleys, joined by rats pouring out of the granary drains in a river of small bodies, and somewhere above it all a cat screaming like a soul in torment.

The city guard would call it a plague omen. The priests would call it a judgment. What it actually was, I knew, was Rafael standing very still in a square half a mile away, opening every door in their mind at once and asking, politely, for help.

Gods, but they were magnificent.

The guards came running, of course. Two patrols from the west gate, then a third from the barracks, torches bobbing, officers shouting about rabies and witchcraft—the second word landing in the night like a dropped knife, because witchcraft was a word that got people burned these days, and every guard who shouted it was thinking of the bounty boards, thinking of the reward. They converged on the square. They left the western postern, the little door in the palace wall reserved for carters and corpse-bearers, watched by exactly one bored sentry.

I put on the servant's smock, picked up a basket of what appeared to be linens, and walked toward him whistling.

"Ho there." He raised his lantern. Young. Barely shaved. The kind of young that made me feel ancient. "Laundry? At this hour?"

"Feast preparations," I said, in the flat accent of the lower city, which I could do in my sleep because I'd grown up speaking it. "His Excellency wants the guest chambers turned by morning. You want to be the one who told me to come back at dawn?"

He considered the basket, the hour, the distant animal racket, and his own profound lack of interest in being blamed for anything. "Go on, then."

The postern closed behind me, and I was inside.

* * *

The archive sat in the oldest wing of the palace, down a stair that predated the kingdom itself, behind a door of black oak bound in iron. I remembered it from my years of service—every officer did, because the archive was where careers went to be buried. Petitions denied. Reports filed and forgotten. The paper graveyard of Corvenna.

My seal opened the outer door. My hands shook doing it—not from fear now, but from the sheer strangeness of it, the sound of iron accepting a disgraced woman's mark as though the last four years hadn't happened. The past as key. I'd told Rafael that was the plan. I hadn't said aloud the other truth: that the past was also a noose, and I was putting my neck in it voluntarily.

Inside, the archive breathed dust. Rows of shelves marched away into darkness, taller than two men, loaded with ledgers and scroll-cases and the bound confessions of dead traitors. A single lamp burned at the registrar's desk, per protocol—a light left burning so the darkness couldn't hide anything, or so the story went. Really it was so the archivist on duty could doze without waking disoriented.

The archivist's chair was empty.

For one wild moment I thought the gods were being kind. Then I heard the small sounds from the back rows: the shuffle of slippers, the mutter of a man talking to himself, which old men who worked among dead paper always eventually did.

I moved away from the desk, deep into the stacks, and let him find his way back to his chair on his own schedule. While he settled, grumbled, and poured himself wine from a flask he thought no one knew about, I counted shelf rows toward the royal records. Odile's instructions were precise: the binding song, the original transcription, had been catalogued a century ago under ecclesiastical holdings—music commissioned by the crown, deemed dangerous, sealed. Sealed documents went to the restricted vault. Row forty-one, third case from the end, marked with the sunburst of the old church court.

Row forty-one was in the darkest corner of the archive, and getting to it meant passing within ten feet of the registrar's lamp.

I waited for the man to nod off. Old men and wine made it a short wait. His breathing went long and slow, his chin dropping toward his chest, and I slipped past him through the pools of shadow between the shelves, silent in soft-soled boots, carrying the basket like a talisman. If anyone stopped me, I was delivering linens. If anyone looked too closely, I was dead. These were the terms of my evening.

The restricted vault was a cage of iron mesh built into the corner of the archive, locked with a key I didn't have—but the lock was old, and Odile had pressed a strip of soft metal into my palm before we parted, along with a look that said *if this doesn't work, don't come back and complain to me.* It worked. Of course it worked. Odile had been the court mage; she'd probably helped design the damned lock.

Inside, the sealed cases stood in ranks like coffins. Third from the end. Sunburst stamp, faded almost to nothing. I lifted the lid.

Dust, and paper, and there—wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age—a bundle of pages. I unwrapped them with fingers that refused to be steady. Musical notation, yes, but underneath the notes, in a cramped antique hand, words in no language I knew—and beneath *that*, in red ink, a marginal note in some long-dead cleric's writing:

*Sung in the beast-tongue. No human throat may carry it. The singer must be one the Hunger cannot swallow.*

One the Hunger cannot swallow. I stared at those words and felt the floor tilt slightly, because Rafael feared—had said it around the fire, quiet, the way people say the things that scare them most—that they were exactly the kind of soul Vhalor fed on best. And here was a hundred-year-old priest agreeing.

Or disagreeing. I couldn't tell. I folded the pages into the oilcloth, wrapped them tight, and slid the bundle into the false bottom of my linen basket, beneath a layer of genuinely excellent napkins.

That was when the lamp behind me went out.

Not guttered. Extinguished—one clean motion, deliberate, the way a person pinches a wick. In the sudden dark I heard footsteps crossing the archive floor, unhurried, familiar, and my whole body went cold before my mind caught up to why they were familiar.

I knew that stride. I had drilled beside that stride for six years. I had followed it into battle twice.

"Sera." The voice came soft through the stacks. "I can hear you breathing back there. Come out where I can see you."

I stood very still in the vault, one hand on the mesh door, and did the arithmetic. Options: fight, run, or answer. Fighting Elara Vance in a paper coffin would end with one of us dead and the alarm raised either way. Running meant abandoning the song. Answering—

Answering meant showing her my face.

I stepped out of the vault into the dark.

She stood by the registrar's desk, sword drawn but lowered, her lantern relit and turned down to a coal-glow. She looked older than I remembered, which was unfair, because I'd seen her four days ago across a checkpoint yard and she'd looked exactly the same. But here, in this room, with the years stacked around us like evidence, she looked older. Tired in a way uniforms can't hide.

"Hello, Elara," I said.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. "Or in irons. Or at least very far away. Instead you're robbing the crown's archive at midnight with a laundry basket." Her eyes flicked to the basket, then back to my face, and I watched her arrive at the obvious conclusion and decide, visibly, not to say it. "Those napkins better be worth dying for."

"They're excellent napkins."

"Sera." Just my name, and underneath it, twenty years of shared drills and shared tents and the winter we'd frozen together on the northern wall and she'd given me half her blanket without ever once mentioning it. "Walk out of here. Put the basket down and walk out, and I'll report a rat problem and a dozing archivist, and no one will ever know."

"No."

"Why?" And there it was—the real question, the one under all the others. "What could possibly be in a sealed church case that's worth your life? You already threw your life away once, for a child nobody else would save. I stood at your trial, Sera. I said nothing. That's the largest kindness I had in me. Don't ask me for a bigger one."

"I'm not asking." I held her gaze. "But I'll tell you anyway, since you asked why. Because the king is dead, Elara. Has been for a year. The thing on the throne wears him like a coat and eats gifted children to keep the coat warm, and the only thing that can pull it off is a song locked in this vault, and I am not leaving without it."

The silence that followed was long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

"That's madness," she said finally.

"Yes."

"That's treason."

"Also yes."

"That's—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. And I saw something move behind her eyes that I recognized, because I'd carried it myself for four years: the shape of a thing known and unspoken, a splinter under the skin of a person. "If I believed you," she said, very quietly, "then everything I've done for the past year has been feeding children to it. Every checkpoint. Every arrest list I signed. Every prisoner I escorted to the tower." Her voice didn't break, but it leaned hard against something. "So tell me again that it's madness. Convince me."

And I understood, suddenly, why she'd extinguished the lamp instead of raising the alarm. Why she'd come alone. Why she was standing in a paper graveyard at midnight asking a traitor to convince her of treason.

Because she already knew. She'd known for longer than me, maybe. She just needed someone else to say it out loud first.

"It's not madness," I said gently. "You know it's not. You've seen the prisoners' faces when they come out of the tower. You've heard whatever you heard that made you pinch that lamp instead of shouting."

Her hand tightened on the lantern until the metal creaked.

"There's a boy," she said, so quietly I nearly missed it. "My brother's son. Eight years old. He warms his hands in winter and the candles never burn him. My brother hides him in a root cellar and signs my patrol reports and we pretend, both of us, every single day, that the lists I sign will never reach that cellar." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Is that enough? Is that the confession you wanted, Sera Vane? Does it buy your napkins safe passage?"

"It buys more than that," I said. "It buys you a place in this, whenever you're ready. Not tonight. Tonight, walk me to the postern, and forget you saw me."

She stared at me for a long time. Then she laughed—one short, broken sound with no humor in it at all—and turned, and led me through the stacks, past the snoring archivist, up the stairs, through corridors she cleared with a word and a shoulder-board, all the way to the little door in the wall.

At the threshold she stopped.

"The bells," she said. "When they ring tomorrow, and the day after—they're not celebration, are they. I've thought that for months. I've thought I was going mad."

"You're not mad," I said. "We're going to prove it. Both of us."

She nodded once, sharp, a soldier closing a matter. Then, as I slipped through the door into the night, her voice followed me, low and rough:

"Sera. Whoever's singing that song you stole—you tell them the Hunger doesn't leave survivors. Whatever it offers them in there, it's a lie. Tell them."

"I'll tell them," I said.

And I ran, basket and all, toward the square where the dogs had gone quiet and the pigeons were settling, toward the shadow where Rafael waited—and found, even before I reached them, that my hands wouldn't stop shaking, and that the shaking wasn't fear anymore.

It was the feeling of a noose turning, link by link, into something else.
