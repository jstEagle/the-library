## 21. The Friend Who Didn't Raise the Alarm

I have been recognized by better people in worse places, but never by a better friend in the worst one.

She came around the shelf of sealed writs with her lantern held high and her sword already drawn, and for three full seconds we stood there in the dust-smelling dark of the palace archive like two figures in an illumination, frozen mid-scene, waiting on the artist to decide which of us dies.

"Sera Vane," Elara Vance said.

"Captain Vance."

"Don't." The word came out cracked down the middle. "Don't stand there and *captain* me."

I hadn't moved my hand from my belt knife. I want that recorded, because later—much later—I would be asked whether I trusted her in that moment, and the honest answer is no, not even a little. Trust is a luxury for people whose names aren't on bounty boards. What I felt was something older and stupider than trust: eleven years of drilling beside her, of sharing watch-fires and bad wine and the particular silence between soldiers who've seen the same thing die. My body remembered her before my mind could object.

"You're supposed to shout," I said. "There's protocol. You shout, six guardsmen appear, I get dragged to Vhalor's altar in chains. Very tidy. Everyone gets a pension."

"There it is." She lowered the lantern slightly, and the light caught the gray coming into her temples—new since I'd seen her last, or maybe I'd just never looked properly. "The jokes. You always did joke when you were about to do something unforgivable."

"I'm not doing anything unforgivable. I'm browsing."

"In the sealed archive. At night. With stolen credentials." She stepped closer, and now I could see what the light had been hiding: she was afraid. Not of me—of something behind her eyes, some arithmetic she'd been running for months and couldn't make balance. "Your seal was struck from the rolls two years ago, Sera. The moment you presented it at the outer door, a bell should have rung somewhere. It didn't. Do you know why?"

Because Rafael's rats had chewed through the alarm wire an hour earlier, and every pigeon in the district was staging the most convincing riot in ornithological history. Because a beast-speaker with a lifetime of practice at being invisible had made sure my way in stayed invisible too. But I said, "Enlighten me."

"Because I silenced it." She said it flatly, the way you report a casualty. "When your name came up on the entry log, I sent the duty clerk away and cut the signal myself."

The archive went very quiet. Somewhere above us, faint as memory, the bells rang the hour—and beneath them, if you knew how to listen, that other sound the city had learned to ignore: the true king's voice, thin as thread, screaming into the bronze.

"Why?" I asked.

And Elara Vance, captain of the Crown Hunters, first sword of the palace guard, the woman who had once made me recite the hunter's oath until I got it right, set down her lantern on a reading desk with the deliberate care of someone disarming herself, and said:

"Because my brother is in the walls of this city, and if you've come for what I think you've come for, then you are the most useful criminal in Corvenna."

* * *

Her brother's name was Tam.

I'd met him once, years ago, at a midsummer feast when he was fourteen and all elbows and bad poetry, trailing his sister through the barracks like a devoted shadow. He'd shown me a trick where he could balance a knife on its point on his fingertip without touching it. I remember laughing and telling him he'd make a better juggler than a soldier.

He wasn't juggling. He was gifted, and he hadn't known the word for it yet, and neither of us had known what the word would cost.

"He hid it well," Elara said. We were sitting among the sealed writs now, on either side of the reading desk, the lantern between us like a border stone. She had not yet asked why I was here, and I had not yet told her, because there was an order to these things, a ritual, and both of us needed it. "After the proclamation. After Marta's boy was taken—yes, I know about that, Sera, I was the one who processed the report and hated every line of it—he ran. Sixteen years old. He's seventeen now, if he's alive."

"If?"

"The lower city purge took forty-one people in the spring. I signed off on thirty-eight arrests I believed in and three I didn't. Tam wasn't among them, so either he's hidden well or—" Her jaw did something complicated. "He sends signs. A chalk mark here, a mended shutter there. Little impossibilities. Things broken that shouldn't be whole. He wants me to know he's alive without telling me where he is, because he knows that knowing would kill us both."

"And you let him stay gone."

"I let him stay *alive*." She said it fiercely, then heard herself, and the fierceness collapsed into something wearier. "Do you understand what it's like? Every morning I put on this uniform and hunt people like him. Every night I go home to a house where I keep his favorite bowl on the shelf, dusted, as if he's just away at market. And the thing wearing the king's face smiles at me across the council table and asks after my family, and I smile back, and I am *so tired*, Sera. I am tired down to the marrow."

I thought about the oath. *To root out the taint, wherever it flowers; to spare neither kin nor friend.* They'd made us swear it with our hands on the crown's law-book, and I'd choked on the words even then, and she hadn't. She'd swallowed them whole and lived inside them for two years, and I finally understood that she hadn't swallowed them because she believed them. She'd swallowed them the way you swallow a key—so no one would find it.

"You knew I'd come eventually," I said.

"I hoped. I prayed, actually, which is a strange thing for me." She turned the lantern a quarter-turn, and shadows swung across the shelves of sealed truth. "When your name appeared on the log tonight, I thought: either she's here to sell us out to save her own neck—which I would almost respect—or she's here because someone finally told her the truth about what sits on the throne. And I decided I'd rather know which. Even if knowing ends me."

"It's the second one."

"I know. Your boots are wrong."

I looked down at my boots. "What?"

"Hunters who sell out dress well. They want the coin to show." Almost, almost, she smiled—the ghost of the grin that used to get us both extra kitchen duty for insolence. "You look like a woman who slept in a ditch and liked it. That's a free woman's posture, Sera. I've missed seeing it."

* * *

Then I told her. All of it, in the low flat voice we use for reports: the fox, the valley, the boy under the bridge, the fen that burned, the song half-stolen and half-remembered, the thirteenth moon nine days out. I watched her face while I talked, cataloguing tells the way she'd taught me, and what I saw was not surprise. At the word *Vhalor* her hand closed on the desk edge until the knuckles went white, but it was the white of confirmation, not revelation.

"You already suspected," I said when I finished.

"I've stood close to it. In the throne room, presenting quarterly reports." She stared into the lantern flame. "It doesn't breathe right. Kings sigh, fidget, age visibly over a year of war-councils. This thing is *still*. Like a pond that's learned to pretend to be water. And twice—twice—it looked at me, just a beat too long, and I felt like a larder being appraised." She exhaled slowly. "You don't forget that feeling. You spend your whole career learning to read fear in other people. Nobody teaches you what to do when the fear is yours."

"So you'll help us."

"I didn't say that."

"Elara."

"*Don't* use the name like that. Like we're in a ditch outside Ashford sharing the last apple." But her voice shook on the apple, on the ditch, on the shared everything, and we both pretended it didn't. "If I open anything—a door, a gate, a mouth—if one thread of this pulls back to me, they won't just kill me. They'll take me to the feeding chamber while I'm still breathing, because that thing likes to eat loyalty fresh. And if there's even a chance it learns about Tam before I do—"

"It won't. We're careful."

"You were careful getting caught in my archive."

"That was the plan working, actually. Long story involving rats."

"I'm certain it is." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. When she dropped them, she'd made whatever decision she'd been circling all night, and it sat on her face like armor donned in a hurry, straps still loose. "Here's what I can do. No doors opened—not yet. But the servants' passage into the east gallery hasn't been walked by guard patrols in four months; I control the roster, and I'll keep it that way. On the night itself, if your fox's plan holds together longer than wet paper, I will hold the gate with men I'd trust with my life and am about to test with yours."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, when your beast-speaker stands in front of that thing—" her voice caught, steadied, "—when your king breathes again, if he breathes, he ends the hunt. Publicly. In law. Pardons for the gifted written into the charter where no frightened successor can scrape them out. Every one of them. Including mine."

"Rafael will swear it."

"Not Rafael. I want it from the one holding the knife. I want *you*, Sera Vane, oath-broken and bounty-priced, to swear to me that the kingdom we build on the other side of this night has no more lists, no more censuses, no more mothers keeping bowls dusted for children who can't come home." She leaned across the desk, and eleven years fell away between us like a dropped cloak. "Swear it on whatever you still believe in. I know it isn't much. Neither is mine."

I thought of Pip's hands mending a cracked bowl by firelight. Of Rafael listening to a lame ewe like she was a senator. Of Odile's face going pale over fragments of song. Of the fen's ashes and the names Pip had insisted on learning.

"On the ember," I said, using the old soldier's oath, the one from before the crown taught us better words. "On the fire we warm ourselves by and the fire we're made of. If Aldous lives, he swears it in law, in public, in blood-ink if he must. And if he doesn't live—if all we pull out of that throne room is a corpse—then we burn the hunt's machinery anyway, and I'll stand trial for it gladly, because some things are worth the rope."

Elara closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was a captain again, but a different one—one serving a garrison of one, in a war only she had known she was fighting.

"Then listen carefully," she said, "because I'm only saying this once, and then you need to be out of my archive before the clerk returns at fourth bell."

* * *

What she gave me was the shape of the palace at night: which stairs sang, which guards drank, which corridor smelled of lamp-oil and meant a change of watch. She drew nothing—that would be evidence—but talked, and I memorized, and the archive's dust settled around us like snow that refused to melt.

At the end she stopped, and her voice changed, became smaller.

"The bowl on my shelf," she said. "Blue glaze, chipped at the rim. He broke it the day he left—threw it, actually, when I told him running was cowardice. God forgive me, I told my terrified little brother that running was cowardice, and then I spent two years running in place myself." She laughed, a terrible sound, brief as a spark. "If he's alive, Sera. If your company finds strays the way people say you do—"

"We do," I said. "It's practically our coat of arms."

"—then when this is over, tell him the bowl is dusted. Tell him I was never angry about the bowl."

I gripped her forearm, soldier-fashion, the grip that says *counted and counted-on*, and she returned it hard enough to leave marks, which was the point.

"Fourth bell," she whispered.

I went out the way I'd come, past the shelf of sealed writs, down the stair that didn't sing because Elara had rostered a deaf man onto that post a year ago—she told me that too, and I understood then how long she'd been building escape hatches in the walls of her own prison, patient as a prisoner digging with a spoon.

Outside, the bells rang the hour, and beneath their bronze thunder, thinner than ever, the true king screamed. Nine days. Nine days, and one captain inside the walls with loose straps on her armor and a dusted blue bowl on her shelf.

I slipped into the dark toward the burnt granary, carrying the palace's bones in my head and a promise on the ember, and I allowed myself—for exactly ten steps, no more—to believe we might actually win.

Which was, of course, precisely when the granary door stood open, and Quill came flying out to meet me, fur bristled, saying words I'd spend the next chapter wishing I'd misheard:

"They've taken Odile."
