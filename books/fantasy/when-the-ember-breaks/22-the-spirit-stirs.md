## 22. The Spirit Stirs

The bells began at midnight, and they did not stop.

I want to be clear about what I mean by that. Not rang and faded, not tolled for some funeral or festival, not the ordinary clatter of a city that has forgotten how to be quiet. They *began*. All of them, at once, every tower in Corvenna, as if someone had seized the ropes of the city's whole throat and pulled. And then they kept going. Through the small hours. Through dawn. Through the moment when Odile appeared in our cellar doorway with flour still on her sleeves from the bakery above and said, in a voice scraped down to wire, "It knows."

We had been back from the archive four days. Four days of Sera sleeping with her boots on, of Quill vanishing into the walls to listen, of Pip practicing his mending on everything with a crack in it—chairs, bowls, a chipped cup he'd named Regret because, he said, it had been dropped enough times to earn the name. Four days of learning the song in fragments, my mouth full of a language that wasn't words.

And then the bells.

* * *

"You have to understand what you're hearing," Odile said, spreading a map of the city across the cellar table, weighting the corners with candle stubs and a heel of bread. "Corvenna has forty-one towers. Under Aldous, they rang at fixed hours—the dawn peal, the noon bell, vespers. A rhythm. A heartbeat. The city could set its days by it." Her finger traced the map. "For the past year, the ringing has been wrong. Irregular. Sometimes silent for days, sometimes hammering through the night. I told myself it was neglect. That the ringers' guild was understaffed, that the new king didn't care for ceremony."

"And now?"

"Now I think the bells were never meant to tell time." She looked up at me, and I saw something in her face I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but the particular gray exhaustion of a person who has been right about something terrible for a long time and is tired of being right alone. "They're a cage, Rafael. Or part of one. The binding turned inside out, remember? The song that once held Vhalor down, reshaped into something that holds the king down instead—and drowns out everything else. Every beast-speaker in this city has been living inside a wall of noise for a year."

"Then why can I hear anything at all?" I asked. Because I could. Faintly, under the iron avalanche, the rats in the walls were muttering about grain, and somewhere above us a cat was complaining about the cold in the particular aggrieved tone cats reserve for weather they consider a personal insult. Muffled, yes. Painful, like listening through a door. But there.

Odile went very still.

"That," she said quietly, "is either very good news or very bad news, and I genuinely don't know which."

* * *

The purge started on the second day.

It began politely, the way these things do. Criers in the lower city announcing a "census correction"—the crown had discovered irregularities in its records, and certain households would need to present themselves for verification. Then came the less polite version: soldiers going door to door in the tanner's quarter and the lamplighters' rows, asking questions that weren't questions. *Do your neighbors keep strange company? Have you heard animals speak where no animal should? Is anyone in this house quiet in the wrong way?*

Sera stood at the top of the cellar stairs with her ear against the trapdoor, cataloguing bootfalls the way other people read weather. "Two patrols on Candlemaker's Row," she reported. "One circling the well square. They're not searching, they're *herding*. Pushing everyone toward the checkpoints." She came down the ladder and crouched by the map, all business, jaw tight. "This isn't a sweep. It's a net."

"For us specifically?"

"For whoever took those pages." She tapped the oilcloth packet where the stolen song lay hidden inside a false bottom of Pip's mending basket, wrapped in wax like a wound. "Vhalor felt the theft the way you'd feel a hand in your pocket. It doesn't know who. So it's going to shake the whole city until something falls out."

Quill arrived then, dropping through the coal chute in a puff of soot and indignation, his coat dusted gray. He shook himself, sneezed twice, and delivered his report with the crisp diction of a courtier pretending he hadn't just fallen down a chimney.

"The dogs are being leashed," he said. "All of them. Every stray in the lower quarter rounded up overnight—officially, rabies precautions. Unofficially—" his ears flattened "—the crown doesn't want ears it doesn't own. There are soldiers at the granary, the tannery, and both bridges. And Rafael, the pigeons are gone. Someone's been clearing roosts off the towers. The sky over Corvenna is empty in a way a sky should never be."

He said it like a man describing a room with the paintings taken down. I understood him. An empty sky over a city is a kind of silence, and silence, we had learned, was Vhalor's native language.

* * *

On the third night, they nearly took Pip.

He'd gone up to the bakery after dark—we were short on bread and long on nerve, which is a bad trade—and he was coming back down the alley behind the chandler's shop when a patrol turned into it ahead of him. Six soldiers and an officer with a lantern and a list. No way forward, no way back but past them.

Pip did what Pip does, which is become furniture. He pressed himself into the shadow of a doorway, made himself small, made himself dull, made himself nothing. And it might have worked. It would have worked, except that one of the soldiers carried a hound.

Not a hunter's hound, trained to scent the gift—that would have found him instantly. Just a garrison dog, big and bored, trotting along on a lead. But a dog is a dog, and Pip had spent three weeks learning to listen, and listening runs both ways if you're careless. He'd been practicing in the cellars, reaching out to the mice in the walls, the moths around the lamps, little conversations, little kindnesses. And the dog caught the edge of it. The way you'd catch a whisper through a wall. It stopped. It turned its head. It looked straight into Pip's shadow and made a sound halfway between a whine and a question.

The officer noticed the dog noticing.

"What's got into Brutus?" he said, and swung the lantern toward the doorway.

Pip told me afterward that in that moment he did the stupidest and bravest thing available to him, which was to speak—to reach out, deliberately, and say to the dog, *Run.* Just that. *Run.*

Brutus ran. Bolted down the alley, dragged the soldier two yards, knocked the lantern spinning, and in the shouting and scrambling dark Pip went over a fence, across a yard full of angry geese—who, I am proud to report, joined the chaos without being asked, because geese require no encouragement—and down the coal chute into our cellar, bleeding from one palm and laughing in the high, cracked way people laugh when the fear arrives late.

"They know the strays are ours," Quill said grimly, licking a smudge of soot off his foreleg. "That's why they're clearing them. Every animal in this district that might carry a message or raise a cry. We've been fighting a war with the city's small folk as our army, and the enemy has noticed the army."

Sera was already repacking her kit. "Then we move tonight. This cellar is known to too many people, and one of them—" she paused, and none of us said the name, because saying it would make it true "—one of them may already have sold us."

But I wasn't listening anymore. Because while they argued routes and safehouses, I had done the thing I do, the thing I had spent twenty-three years hiding and three months learning to use, and opened my doors wide.

Through the bells.

* * *

You cannot imagine the sound unless you have stood inside it. Forty-one towers, and the great bell of the cathedral—the Ember Bell, they called it, cast from the melted armor of some old war—leading the rest like a bully leading a choir. The noise came down through stone and earth and filled the cellar until my teeth ached in my jaw. Most people hear bells as sound. I heard them as voices, because everything is a voice if you listen long enough, and metal remembers the hands that shaped it, and grief carries in bronze better than anywhere.

Under the ringing, I found the pattern. Not rhythm—*pattern*. The bells weren't tolling randomly. They were repeating something. A phrase, over and over, slightly wrong each time, like a word said by a mouth that couldn't quite shape it.

And underneath the phrase, fainter than faint, threaded through the iron like a hair through amber—

A voice.

A man's voice. Screaming.

I stood up so fast I knocked over the candle, and Sera caught the flame before it died, and everyone was looking at me, and I couldn't speak, because what I was hearing was impossible and I needed it to be impossible, I needed it to be my gift misfiring, my fear making music out of noise the way fear makes faces out of shadows.

"Rafael." Sera's hand on my arm. "What is it?"

"It's—" My voice came out wrong. Small. "There's someone in the bells."

"Don't be absurd," Odile started, and then stopped, because she was a mage before she was a baker, and she knew better than most how absurd the world was willing to be.

"The bells aren't just drowning sound," I said. "They're *holding* something. The way the valley stones held magic out, the way a dam holds water. And things held somewhere can be heard through the walls if you press your ear close enough." I looked at their faces, at Sera's sharpening eyes, at Quill gone utterly still on the table, at Pip clutching his bandaged hand. "It's the king. The real one. He's screaming. He's been screaming for a year, and the bells are the shape of his scream, and nobody in this city knows they're listening to it every hour of every day."

Quill made a sound I had never heard from him. He had told us about watching the crown take Aldous; he had described it with a fox's flat, terrible clarity. But hearing was different from telling. He put his head down between his paws, and his tail wrapped around himself like a bandage, and he said, barely audible under the ringing:

"I used to sleep in his rooms. Before. When the bells rang properly, at dawn and noon and vespers, he would hum while he dressed. Badly. Off-key. He thought no one heard him." A pause. "He was wrong. Foxes hear everything. And now he's in the bells, and the bells never stop, and he can't even hum."

Nobody spoke. The candles burned. Somewhere overhead, boots crossed the street above us, six men marching in step, guarding a city that was itself a prison.

* * *

Later—I don't know how much later; time goes strange in cellars during purges—Sera sat down beside me on the grain sacks and didn't say anything for a while, which is her way of saying a great deal.

"You heard him," she finally said. "Actually heard him."

"Yes."

"Can you reach him? Speak back?"

"I don't know." I turned my hands over, looked at them like they belonged to a stranger. "Everything I've ever spoken to wanted to talk. Animals are lonely; they lean toward a voice. But he's not leaning. He's drowning. Talking to someone drowning isn't conversation, Sera. It's throwing rope into deep water and hoping their hands are free to catch it."

"And if they're not free?"

"Then I'll have learned something I don't want to know."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her do the arithmetic she always did, the cold soldier's sum of odds and costs, except that this time the numbers wouldn't sit still. "The plan hasn't changed," she said at last. "Thirteenth moon. Throne room. Song. But it's changed anyway, hasn't it. A week ago we were stealing a king back from a monster. Now—" she gestured upward, at the ceiling, at the bells, at the whole ringing weight of the city "—now we're pulling a man out of the water, and the monster is holding him under, and it knows we're here."

Above us, the bells surged, some new tower joining the chorus, and through the surge, faint and frantic, the voice again—and this time I caught something in it that undid me completely. Not words. Not yet. But cadence. The cadence of a name, repeated, repeated, repeated, screamed into bronze until bronze learned to carry it.

A name. His own name, maybe. Or—

Or a fox's name.

I looked at Quill, and Quill looked at me, and neither of us said anything, because some recognitions are too large for speech.

"We move tomorrow," Sera said, standing, and her voice had changed—it had the sound of a decision already made, which is the only sound that matters in a siege. "Whatever it costs. Whatever route. We get to that throne room, and we get him out." She looked around the candlelit cellar at the four of us—a disgraced knight, a runaway boy, a soot-gray fox, and a goatherd who talked to beasts—and something almost like a smile touched her mouth, brief and hard as a struck spark. "The spirit wanted to shake the city until we fell out of hiding. Fine. It's shaken us loose. Now it gets to find out what we do in the open."

The bells rang on. And beneath them, thin as a thread, patient as a heartbeat, the true king of Corvenna screamed his fox's name into the dark, waiting for someone to answer.
