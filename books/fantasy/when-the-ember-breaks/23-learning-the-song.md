## 23. Learning the Song

The first thing you should know about beast-speech is that it isn't speech at all.

I had spent twenty-three years believing I was fluent in it. I spoke to goats. I spoke to ewes and merchant horses and the occasional offended goose. I thought of myself as a man—or a whatever-I-was—with a gift for conversation, the way some people have a gift for carpentry or lying to tax collectors.

Then Odile put the binding song in front of me and told me I'd been mumbling my whole life.

"You speak *at* them," she said. She was pacing the cellar, candlelight making her shadow loom and shrink on the wine racks like something deciding whether to attack. "Words, images, intentions—shoved across the gap like bricks through a window. It works. It's crude, but it works, because animals are patient and generous and they meet you halfway. The song is not like that."

"And what is the song like?"

"The song is *becoming*." She said it the way a priest says *damnation* or a baker says *sourdough*, with the weight of a thing that cannot be rushed. "You don't send the song. You are the song. Your body sings it before your mind knows what's happening. If your throat tries to lead, you'll fail. If your fear leads, you'll fail worse. Vhalor was bound by a woman who sang it while it ate her alive, Rafael. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I should probably stretch first?"

Odile closed her eyes. Somewhere above us, the bells rang the hour, and I felt the sound go through the floorboards and into my teeth, that muffled cotton-wool deadness that Corvenna pressed down over everything I was. Even here, even underground, the city smothered me. Twenty-three years of hiding, and now the hiding had a soundtrack.

"No," she said, opening her eyes again. "I'm telling you that if you do this wrong, it will not kill you quickly. Now. Again. From the beginning."

* * *

The beginning, as it turned out, was not a beginning at all. It was a memory.

"Close your eyes," Quill said. He sat on a flour sack in Father Ambrose's cellar, tail wrapped around his paws, looking insufferably comfortable. "What's the first thing you ever heard? Not with your ears. The other way."

I closed my eyes. "A goat," I said. "Bramble. I was four. She told me the grass by the fence was sweeter than the grass in the middle, and I didn't believe her, so she showed me." I smiled despite myself. "She was right. Goats are usually right about grass."

"What did that feel like? Not what did it mean. What did it *feel* like."

I reached for it. And here is the strange thing: I found that I couldn't describe it in words, which should have been my first clue about everything that followed. There was no word for the sensation of Bramble's voice arriving—not arriving at me but *opening in* me, the way dawn opens in a room without anyone lighting a lamp. Warmth behind the sternum. A loosening, somewhere under the ribs, like a knot I hadn't known I wore slipping free.

"Like being believed," I said finally. "Like someone believed me before I said anything."

Quill's ears tilted forward. "Yes," he said softly. "That's it. That's the door. The song lives there."

He stood, and shook himself, and did something I had never seen him do. He stopped being a fox talking and became, for one moment, simply a fox—the whole animal, unarmored, every muscle and intention legible, a creature saying *I am here, I am fox, this is true* with his entire body. It wasn't language. It was more honest than language. It went into me through the skin.

"Now you," he said, returning to himself. "Show me the day you left the valley. Don't tell it. Be it."

I tried. I summoned the image: the border stones, the grey light, the terror. I pushed it outward the way I always pushed things outward—

And Quill yawned.

"Not that way," he said. "You're mailing me a letter again. Rafael. When you crossed those stones and the whole world came shouting in—you didn't understand it with your head. Where did it hit you?"

"In the chest," I said. "It felt like drowning in music."

"So drown." His amber eyes caught the candlelight and held it. "Stop trying to say the song. Let the song say you."

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that my first several attempts were catastrophic.

The third night, I managed to open myself fully while thinking about nothing in particular, which meant I opened myself to the cellar mice, who were in the middle of an argument about a crust of bread, and their tiny furious voices flooded me so completely that I fell off my stool and bit my tongue. The fifth night, I achieved something almost like singing—a single sustained note of pure feeling, grief-shaped—and every rat within three streets came to the cellar door to listen, which Odile said was either very promising or very dangerous, depending on whether any of them worked for the crown. She was not entirely joking. In Corvenna, these days, you assumed the rats had informants too.

The eighth night, I cried.

It wasn't sadness, exactly. It was that when I finally let the song come from the place where Bramble's voice had opened in me instead of from my clever, careful throat, what poured out was twenty-three years of silence—all of it at once, every conversation swallowed, every impulse strangled, every morning I had woken up in the valley and performed ordinariness like a role in a play that never ended. My body sang it because my mouth never could. I sat on the flour-sack floor of a stranger's cellar in a city that wanted me dead and I wept like a drain unblocked, and Quill leaned against my knee and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the song wasn't already saying.

When it passed, I felt scraped clean. Lighter. Like a house after everything has been carried out of it.

"Well," I croaked. "That was humiliating."

"It was necessary," Quill said. "You can't sing a binding until you've unbound yourself. That's why the last one died well and the ones before her failed. She had already lost everything. Nothing left to hold shut."

"I haven't lost everything."

"No," he agreed, and his eyes flicked upward, toward the floors above where Sera was drilling escape routes with Pip. "Which is why yours will be harder. Hold on to it anyway. Love isn't a crack in the song, Rafael. It's the bellows."

* * *

Here is what changed everything: the choir.

It began by accident. One of Odile's lamplighters—a round, anxious man named Tobb who fed every stray cat between here and Tanner's Row—mentioned that the cats had started following him on his rounds in a way he couldn't explain, sitting on walls at intervals like stations of some feline cross, watching him work. Odile went pale in her particular way, which involved going slightly *less* pale, and asked him exactly where each cat sat.

They sat along the route from the cellar to the lamplighters' hall. A line. A relay.

"They've been listening," she breathed. "Since the rat night. They've been *practicing*."

And so it grew, the way resistance grows in a frightened city: quietly, in the dark, one small courage at a time. Tobb's cats first. Then the pigeons of the corn market, who roosted on the bakery roof above us and cooed the song's opening phrase in their throats, garbled but earnest, like children mangling a hymn. Then the rats—I made peace with the rats, we had a long talk, they were less sinister than advertised and mostly wanted assurances about the bread situation. Dogs came next, led by a one-eared mastiff named Duchess who belonged to the baker and considered herself management. Every night, in cellars and rooftops and the spaces between, the hidden animals of Corvenna gathered around our hidden voices and learned the binding song a phrase at a time.

None of them could sing it properly. That wasn't the point. Quill explained it to me on the bakery roof, under a sky bruised purple with smoke and bells, while forty pigeons murmured the first phrase overhead like a congregation rehearsing.

"A single voice binds," he said. "But the song was written for a chorus—it was stolen from a chorus, from the old herds and packs and flocks, back when the world listened better. Vhalor cut the animals out of it when it took the crown. Silenced the singers, kept the song. So when you sing it alone in that throne room, you'll be singing a broken instrument." He looked up at the pigeons. "Unless the chorus remembers itself."

"They'll die," I said. "If Vhalor notices them. It feeds on—" I stopped. On the gifted. On voices like theirs. "It will notice."

"Yes," Quill said simply.

I looked at him. "You're asking them anyway."

"I'm not asking them anything." He settled beside me, shoulder warm against my arm. "Rafael. You spent a lifetime deciding for other creatures what was safest for them. How did that feel?"

It felt like the valley. It felt like a half-lived life. It felt like Marta's son being dragged away while I watched my own hands for something useful to do and found nothing, because I had taught my hands to be empty.

So the next night, I climbed onto the bakery roof, opened every door in me at once, and asked. Not ordered. Asked. I showed them all of it—the throne room we'd never seen, the spirit wearing a kind face, the king screaming inside the bells, the song that needed a thousand throats because one would not survive. I showed them the cost. I held nothing back, because they deserved nothing held back.

And then I waited, feeling foolish, kneeling on shingles in the dark of a doomed city, having asked pigeons to save a kingdom.

Duchess the mastiff answered first. She stood at the roof's edge, looked out over the sleeping streets, and sang—one low, cracked, imperfect note of absolute willingness, the sound of a dog who has decided, once and for all, whose side she is on.

The pigeons took it up. Then the cats along Tobb's route. Then, faint and far, from directions I couldn't name, other voices—rooks from the tower, mice from the walls, something vast and patient in the river that I suspected was a pike and chose not to examine closely. Phrase by broken phrase, they gave the song back to me stitched with their own imperfections, and it sounded nothing like the elder's version from Greywater Fen, nothing like the archive pages Odile had copied, and it was more beautiful than both, because it was *theirs*.

I laughed. I actually laughed, out loud, on a rooftop, in the most dangerous city in the realm—laughed until I had to sit down, because the gift I had spent twenty-three years burying like contraband had just become a choir, and nobody had warned me that joy would be the hardest part to bear.

Quill watched me laugh with an expression I'd never seen on a fox before. It took me a moment to recognize it as tenderness.

"There it is," he said. "There's the voice that binds kings."

* * *

Sera found me after midnight, sitting on the roof edge with my legs dangling over four stories of dark.

"You're supposed to be asleep," she said.

"You're supposed to be drilling escape routes."

"Pip fell asleep mid-drill. He was answering questions in his dreams. Apparently our fallback position is 'the big spoon.'" She sat down next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a while neither of us said anything, which had become its own kind of conversation between us. Below us, the city rang its ceaseless bells, and beneath the bells, if I opened myself just a thread, the king still screamed. We had not yet figured out how to answer him. We were getting closer every night.

"Odile says I might die doing this," I said.

"She says that about breakfast."

"She means it about this."

Sera was quiet a moment. "Do you want the soldier's answer or mine?"

"Yours."

"My answer is that I've watched you learn a dead woman's song in nine nights because a fox told you to stop talking with your mouth." She turned and looked at me, and the bell-light moved in her eyes. "I've commanded men who couldn't learn to march in nine months. Whatever happens in that throne room, Rafael Almeida, you will not fail for lack of becoming. You're the most *become* person I've ever met."

I laughed again—quieter, this time. "That's not a real word."

"Neither is beast-speaker. Neither is *queen of the valley goat-girl*, which is what Marta used to call you, according to Pip, who talks too much." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Sing me something. The real way. I want to hear what I'm fighting for."

So I opened the doors, all of them, and let the song come up from the place where Bramble had shown me sweet grass twenty years ago, and I sang it for Sera—not the binding, not yet, just the heart of it, the phrase that meant *you are here, you are true, this is real*—and I felt her stop breathing, and then start again differently, and the pigeons above us stirred and murmured it back, and somewhere below a dog added her one cracked note, and the city's bells rang on overhead trying to drown us out and failing, failing, failing.

We stayed until the sky went grey. Neither of us slept. Neither of us mentioned the throne room, or the moon turning, or how many nights might be left. Some things you carry best by not naming them.

It was Quill who broke the spell, trotting up the stair with his fur full of chimney soot and his ears flat with urgency.

"Trouble," he said. "Tobb didn't come home tonight. And the lamplighters' guild says the crown took him at his rounds—no charges, no papers. Just four hunters and a covered cart."

The joy in my chest curdled, fast and cold. Tobb, who fed the strays. Tobb, whose cats had learned the first phrase of the song.

"How many nights until the moon turns?" I asked.

"Three," Sera said, already standing, already reaching for her sword. "Maybe two, if the clouds break early."

Below us, along Tobb's lamplighter route, forty cats sat on their walls in the dark, waiting for a man who wasn't coming—and singing, soft and ragged and stubborn, the only part of the song they knew.
