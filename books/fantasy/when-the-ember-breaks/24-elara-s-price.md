## 24. Elara's Price

I had been a soldier long enough to know that the worst decisions of my life would all be made in cellars.

There was something about cellars. The dark made honest, or it made reckless—sometimes both in the same breath. I'd signed my first commission in a cellar under the garrison at Vell's Crossing, drunk on cheap wine and seventeen years of certainty. I'd refused my captain's order to burn a child in another cellar, though that one was more of a crypt, and I hadn't been drinking anything but fear. And now here I stood in a third one, under a tannery that smelled like a crime against the nose, listening to a fox explain treason.

"Three turns left," Quill said, pacing the length of a flour barrel as if he owned it. Foxes pace when they're nervous. He'd deny it to his last whisker. "The moon wanes toward its thirteenth turn. We have the song. We have the passage—*if* our friend keeps her word."

"I keep my word," I said.

"You kept your word once already," he said, not looking at me. "In an archive. At considerable cost to yourself, I might add. I find I'm curious what you'll charge this time."

Everyone looked at me then. Rafael, sitting on an upturned crate with their hands folded like a penitent. Sera beside them, arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore whenever someone else was about to make a decision she could blame later. Pip, who was supposed to be asleep and was instead pretending to be a sack of grain against the far wall, badly. Odile had gone back up to her lamplighter's rounds an hour ago, but her shadow seemed to linger anyway, the way shadows do in places where people have been afraid for too long.

"What I want," I said, "is a promise."

Quill stopped pacing. "Foxes don't deal in promises."

"Not from you." I turned to Rafael. "From all of you."

Sera shifted. "Name it."

"When Aldous comes out of this—if he comes out of it, if your song works and your spirit unravels and the man inside is still breathing—I want the hunt ended. Not quietly wound down. Not 'reviewed.' Ended. Every bounty torn down, every denunciation burned, every gifted soul pardoned by name and by law, proclaimed from the palace steps where every ear in Corvenna can hear it. Publicly. In writing. Sealed under his own hand and witnessed before the realm." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the voice I'd used for orders I expected to be disobeyed. "That is the price of the door."

The cellar went quiet in the particular way cellars go quiet—sound dying into stone, nothing echoing, everything absorbed.

"And if he doesn't live?" Sera asked.

"Then we've traded one dead king for another, and the question answers itself."

"And if he lives," Sera pressed, "and he says no? He's been a prisoner in his own skull for a year. You think his first act will be statesmanship?"

"I think," I said, "that if we open that door and put Rafael in front of that throne, and the only thing that changes is which body sits in the chair—then everything we bury in the fen, everyone the hunters dragged off in chains, every name Pip has learned to say over fresh graves—it all meant nothing. The crown just changed hands. The hunger just got a new face." I looked at Rafael. "You've seen it eat people. Do you believe mercy survives that throne unattended?"

Rafael didn't answer right away. That was one of the things about them—the silence wasn't cowardice; it was weight-bearing. They let questions sit on them until they found out how much they could hold.

"No," Rafael said finally. "Not unattended."

"Then it needs attending. In law. Law is the only thing in this kingdom that outlives kings."

Pip made a small sound against the wall. I pretended not to hear it, because acknowledging him would mean acknowledging that a twelve-year-old was party to high treason, and there are lies a person needs to keep their footing.

"The passage," Quill said, drawing us back. "Describe it."

I crouched and scratched a map into the dirt floor with a nail: the palace's lower ring, the servants' corridors, the old laundry stair. "There's a passage behind the wine cellar on the east range. Built two hundred years ago so the king's household could move between kitchens and hall without crossing the state rooms. It's been sealed since Aldous took the crown—Vhalor doesn't trust servants, doesn't trust anyone moving unseen—but the seals are wax and iron, not magic. Magic can't pass the bells' radius cleanly, which is why the thing uses bells at all. Iron and wax I can cut." I tapped the map. "It opens forty feet from the throne room doors. On the night of the thirteenth moon, the guard rotates at the change of watch. Ninety seconds, maybe less, with Elsbet covering the corridor."

"Elsbet," Sera repeated. "Your co-conspirator grows."

"My co-conspirator is one woman who owes me her brother's life and knows it. Don't push her luck or mine." I stood, brushing dirt from my knees. "Here's what you need to understand about what I'm offering. If this fails—if any part of this fails—the passage leads back to me. My name is on the roster of the east range. My seal opened the archive. When they trace it, and they will trace it, there is no version of this where I walk away. So understand that I am not selling you a door. I am spending my life on it, and I intend to get a receipt."

"A receipt," Quill murmured. "How very human."

"The pardon," I said. "Sworn. Now. All of you, or none of you use my door."

* * *

They argued, of course. Good. I'd have distrusted them if they hadn't.

Sera argued longest, and hardest, and I understood why. She'd worn the badge I used to wear. She knew better than any of us what oaths cost when they collide with each other. "You're asking a man coming out of a year of possession to legislate on the worst night of his life," she said. "What if he's not capable? What if he needs months? Are you going to hold a knife to a convalescent until he signs?"

"I'm going to hold witnesses to him until he signs," I said. "You. Rafael. The whole company, plus Elara Vance, captain of the palace guard, who watched the hunt happen from inside it and said nothing until now. There will be six swords' worth of memory standing in that room. Kings forget. Witnesses don't."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then we learn what kind of king we bled for," I said, "while he still has a city full of animals and one very motivated beast-speaker standing in his throne room. I don't think refusal will come easily."

Pip, who had given up pretending to be grain, raised his hand like we were in school. "What if—" he started, and stopped, and tried again. "What if the pardon isn't enough? Marta's boy. The people from the fen. A pardon means they stop being hunted. It doesn't give them anything back."

The kid had a way of putting his finger directly into the wound. I felt Rafael flinch at the names.

"It gives them tomorrow," I said. "Tomorrow is not nothing. Ask anyone who didn't get one."

He nodded slowly, chewing on that, and I thought: someone should write down what this boy says before he grows up and starts saying sensible things instead.

Quill had gone still on his barrel, tail curled around his feet, amber eyes moving from face to face like a man counting coins. "You understand," he said at last, to Rafael, "that you cannot swear this alone. A promise made by one is a wish. The company swears together, or the words are wind."

"I know," Rafael said.

"Do you?" Quill hopped down, and even now, after months on the road with him, the sudden grace of it startled me—a creature built entirely of intent. "Because I have watched humans swear things in cellars before. It is usually the wine talking. There is no wine here. Only you, and the dark, and a woman with a nail." He glanced at me. "No offense."

"None taken."

"Say what you'll swear," he told Rafael, "and mean it in your bones, because the bones remember longer than the tongue."

* * *

Rafael stood. They'd gotten steadier on their feet since Greywater Fen—there was a way people carried themselves after they'd buried strangers, a settling, like ballast taken aboard. They came to the middle of the cellar where my map was still scratched into the dirt, and they looked down at it for a long moment, at the little lines that stood for corridors and stairs and the forty feet between a hidden door and a hungry throne.

"If he lives," Rafael said, "we swear he ends the hunt. Completely. No bounty boards, no censuses, no lists. Every record of the gifted gathered and burned where the people whose names are on them can watch."

"Yes," I said.

"We swear he pardons them. By name, publicly, under law—and not just the ones we know. Anyone denounced, anyone fled, anyone born marked. Their families too. No reprisal for the hiding, ever. The hiding was survival, not crime."

My throat did something complicated. I thought of Tomás, my brother, eleven years old and setting the curtains alight with his palms, crying because he thought he'd done something wicked. Twenty years since, and I still woke some nights smelling smoke. "Yes," I said. "Go on."

"We swear he does it in the open. From the palace steps. With the bells silent, so everyone can hear—not just Corvenna, the messengers carry it to every town that nailed up a bounty board. And—" Rafael hesitated here, and I watched them decide whether to spend something. They spent it. "And we swear he asks forgiveness. Not grants it. Asks. On his knees if he can manage it, in words if he can't. Because a pardon handed down from above is just the hunt changing direction. They deserve a king who knows what was done in his name, even while his mouth was full of someone else's hunger."

Silence again. This time it was the good kind, the kind that means something landed.

"That's not in my price," I managed.

"I know. It's extra."

Despite everything—despite the fen's ash still under all our fingernails—I almost smiled. "Accepted. Extra included."

Sera uncrossed her arms and stepped up beside Rafael. "I witness it. And if the man won't keep it, I'll remind him. Loudly. With my sword hand if necessary."

Pip pushed off the wall. "Me too. Witnessing." He said it solemnly, the way children swear oaths they don't fully understand and somehow keep better than adults.

Quill sat back on his haunches. "And I," he said, "who was bound to the royal bloodline before any of your grandmothers drew breath, witness it also. Which means, I should tell you, that it binds. Old law, older than your kingdoms. Spoken in a cellar, in darkness, freely, by a beast-speaker and those who stand with them—" His ears flicked. "Congratulations. You've just founded a covenant. I'd have mentioned the paperwork earlier, but frankly I wasn't sure you'd go through with it."

"What paperwork?" Pip asked, alarmed.

"The kind written on your soul," Quill said cheerfully. "Very tidy. No ink required."

Rafael laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, the way they laughed in those days, as if joy were rationed. But they turned to me then, and the laughter drained into something level and grave.

"Elara. If we die in that palace, the oath dies with us."

"I know."

"If we live and fail—"

"You won't fail from lack of witnesses," I said. "That's the entire point of me."

They put out their hand. I took it. Their palm was rough from goat-work and road-work, warm, steady. Around us the others closed in—Sera's gauntlet on Rafael's shoulder, Pip's small hand grabbing whatever it could reach, Quill pressing his forehead briefly against Rafael's knee with a gravity that foxes reserve for coronations and funerals and apparently both at once.

"So sworn," Rafael said. "In the dark, where no one sees but the dark."

"So sworn," we answered, and the cellar held the words the way cellars hold everything: completely, and without echo, waiting for someone to dig them up.

* * *

Afterward, while the others settled into their blankets among the barrels, I stayed by the map I'd scratched and studied it like it might apologize for what it asked of me.

Sera appeared at my elbow with two cups of something that claimed to be tea. She handed me one. Neither of us drank.

"You could have asked for less," she said.

"You could have argued for less. You argued for more time, not smaller terms."

"He's a broken man, Aldous. Maybe worse than broken. Maybe there's not enough left to legislate with."

"Then the realm deserves to see that," I said, "instead of pretending a rescued king fixes everything by existing." I turned the cup in my hands. "I spent a year watching that hunt from inside the uniform. Do you know what I told myself? That I was more useful inside. That I could soften it, slow it, save one here and there. And I did—Tomás, and two others I got across the border, and I told myself the arithmetic balanced." I looked at her. "It never balances. You just get used to carrying the remainder. This—" I nudged the map with my toe. "This is the remainder."

She was quiet a moment. "Your brother. The fire-hands. That's why the terms."

"That's why I'm alive to offer terms," I said. "The rest is arithmetic."

Across the cellar, Rafael had fallen asleep sitting up, chin on chest, one hand still loosely curled as if holding something invisible—an oath, maybe, or a song. Quill had arranged himself across their feet like a sentry who happened to be fur. Pip was actually asleep now, really asleep, mouth open, young enough that his body still believed mornings were safe.

"Elara," Sera said quietly. "The passage. Forty feet from the throne room. Have you walked it?"

"Once. Years ago, delivering dispatches. Why?"

"Is there a lock on the far end?"

I hadn't wanted to think about that. I made myself think about it now. "Iron bar. From the inside. Someone will have to lift it from within the throne room side—which means someone goes in early and hides, or—" I stopped.

"Or," Sera said, "one of us goes in through the front, gets caught on purpose, and gets thrown exactly where we need to be."

We looked at each other across the tea we weren't drinking.

"I'll draw straws," I said.

"Don't bother," she said, and there was that terrible calm soldiers get when they've already decided and are just waiting for the world to catch up. "I've been meaning to have a word with the crown anyway."

Above us, faint through the stones, the bells began the midnight count—all thirteen towers, one after another, drowning every whisper in the city, exactly as the thing beneath the crown intended.

Twelve strokes. Thirteen. Silence.

Three turns left, I thought, and started planning how to hide a knight inside a hunger.
