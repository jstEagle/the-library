## 25. The Moon Wanes

The cellars beneath the lamplighter's house smelled of tallow, dust, and old onions, which Odile claimed was a security feature. "No one searches for heroes in a place that smells like this," she said, and I chose to believe her, because the alternative was believing we were hiding in an onion cellar like rats, and I'd spent enough time around actual rats lately to know they had better accommodations.

Two nights until the thirteenth moon.

I want to tell you I spent those days in noble calm, sharpening my resolve like a good sword. What I actually did was count things. The number of barrels against the far wall (eleven). The number of times Sera checked the escape tunnel before noon (four). The number of hours Pip slept, total, across two days (three, and badly). Counting is what my mind does when it's frightened. In the valley I counted goats. Now I counted exits and heartbeats and the slow, grinding turns of a moon that seemed to be taking its sweet time waning, as if it too had heard what was waiting for us at the end of it.

Quill lay on a sack of flour near the lamp, chin on his paws, eyes half-closed in the way foxes pretend to sleep when they are actually listening to everything. He hadn't made a joke in two days. When a fox stops joking, you know the hour is late.

* * *

It started, as most of our worst moments did, with Pip.

He was sitting on an upturned crate, whittling—Pip whittled constantly now, little shapes he never finished, a bird here, a boat there, all of them abandoned mid-stroke—and he said, without looking up, "I want to fight."

Sera didn't even pause her inspection of the tunnel door. "No."

"You haven't even heard the plan."

"I don't need to hear the plan. Any plan where you fight is a plan I've already rejected." She tested the hinge, found it acceptable, moved to the brace. Sera could reject an idea the way other people breathed—involuntarily, continuously, without visible effort.

"I mended a door with my hands," Pip said. His voice had that edge it got when he was scared and covering it with stubbornness, which, if I'm honest, described about ninety percent of his sentences since the bridge. "I held off hunters on the stairs. I'm not a child anymore."

"You're fifteen," Sera said.

"Fifteen and useful."

"Fifteen," Sera repeated, turning now, arms crossed, "and the reason we're doing any of this. You understand that, don't you? Not Rafael. Not me. You. A boy who can mend broken things, standing in front of a spirit that eats the gifted alive, holding a knife." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the terrible thing about Sera Vane; she could dismantle you entirely at conversational volume. "If you fight, you die, and then everything we've buried people for becomes nothing. You stay behind me. You mend what needs mending. That's your fight."

"My fight," Pip said bitterly, "is watching."

"Watching is how I survived twenty years in armor," Sera said. "Most of war is watching. The rest of it is graves."

The silence after that had weight. Pip looked down at his half-carved bird, and I saw his jaw work, and I thought about saying something kind, and then decided kindness would only make it worse, because sometimes what a person needs is not comfort but the truth delivered by someone who isn't their own fear wearing a friendly face.

So instead I sat down next to him and said, "When I was your age, I wanted to fight too."

He glanced at me sideways. "You? You talk to sheep."

"Sheep have opinions worth defending," I said, and got the ghost of a smile out of him, which had been the point. "But I mean it. There was a winter a wolf came into the valley—I told you about the valley, how magic can't cross its stones. Well, hunger crosses anything. This wolf was starving, ribs like a harp, and every farmer in the valley grabbed a pitchfork, and I stood there with them thinking, *let me be brave, let me be the one who drives it off.* And do you know what happened?"

"What?"

"The ewe talked me out of it. Bramble—the lame one, I've mentioned her, I mention her more than is probably dignified. She said, *the wolf doesn't want your courage, it wants the goat, and if you give it the old billy from the far pen, everyone lives.*" I turned the carving over in my memory the way Pip turned his knife. "I hated it. I wanted to matter loudly. Instead I mattered quietly, and the billy lived another four years, and so did I. Quiet matters, Pip. It just doesn't get songs written about it."

"That's because no one writes songs about mending doors," he muttered.

"Someone will," Quill said from the flour sack, without opening his eyes. "Bad ones. But someone."

Pip huffed—a real laugh, small and surprised out of him—and went back to whittling, and though I doubt he'd surrendered, he'd at least stopped charging. With Pip, that was victory enough for one morning.

* * *

Sera drilled escape routes all afternoon, and I use the word *drilled* precisely, because there was a sergeant hiding under all that disgraced-knight melancholy and she came out fully armed whenever she had chalk and a floor.

She'd drawn the palace approaches on the cellar stones in white lines—servants' passage, guard rotations, the bell tower, the throne room marked with a circle she'd pressed so hard the chalk had crumbled. Elara's route in. Odile's guild silencing the towers, one by one, west to east. Quill and the animals. And then, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: *IF ALL ELSE FAILS.*

"If all else fails," Sera said, tapping the tunnel mouth, "we go up the cistern stair, through the cooper's yard, over the wall at the blind corner where the watch changes, and out the south gate before the third bell. Everyone will know this cold. Pip, walk it."

Pip walked it. Then I walked it. Then Quill walked it, which mostly consisted of him trotting the line while making sarcastic remarks about human architecture—"a wall with a blind corner, how generous, did they design it for fleeing specifically?"—until Sera snapped that foxes didn't need escape routes because foxes were the reason *other* creatures needed escape routes, and Quill looked so delighted by this that I suspected he'd provoked it deliberately.

Then she made us do it again. And again. By the fourth repetition my legs ached and my patience had worn through to the wood underneath.

"Sera." I caught her elbow at the top of the cistern stair. "We know it."

"You don't. Knowing a thing and knowing it in your bones are different countries, and you can't cross between them except by walking." She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the dark mouth of the tunnel, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't drilling us at all. She was drilling herself. Every route she memorized was a route she might have to take alone, carrying news, or not carrying anything at all.

"And if the plan works?" I asked. "What's the route home?"

Her jaw tightened. "One disaster at a time, Rafael."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." And she went back down the stairs to draw the palace again, because drawing it was easier than answering, and I let her, because I loved her, and love is mostly letting people build their walls in peace until the night comes when you finally ask them to open the gate.

* * *

Odile found me at dusk, sitting on a barrel with the binding song spread out on my knees—not the pages themselves; those were hidden in three separate places that would take a small novel to explain—but my copy of it, transcribed in my own hand, the beast-speech rendered as best ink can manage, which is poorly. Beast-speech is a language of the body. Ink catches maybe a tenth of it, the way a map catches a country.

Odile lowered herself onto the barrel beside me with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. She was older than I'd first taken her for—disgrace ages you strangely, some parts faster than others—and up close her eyes had the flat shine of someone who had read too many dangerous books and remembered all of them.

"May I?" She held out her hand for the pages.

I gave them to her. She read in silence for a long time, lips moving slightly, and I watched her face do something complicated—recognition, yes, but also grief, the way you grieve hearing a song your mother used to sing.

"Where did you learn this?" she asked finally.

"The fen. Elder Maren. Before the hunters burned it."

"Maren." Odile closed her eyes briefly. "I knew her name. We all knew her name, those of us who studied. The last keeper of the binding." She handed the pages back carefully, the way you hand back something holy. "Rafael. I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide how afraid to be."

"That sounds promising."

"The song unbinds Vhalor from Aldous. That part Maren taught you truly. But the song requires a vessel—it must be sung *through* a soul the spirit cannot simply devour, and the singing opens the singer to it completely. Every door in you, open, while a hunger stands inside." She met my eyes. "Maren's predecessor died singing it. Maren always believed she would too, if it ever came to her. And the histories say the beast-speaker who bound Vhalor a century ago—Corvane, his name was—he survived the binding itself. It was the aftermath that killed him. The spirit's hunger, tearing loose, took him with it as it fled into the crown."

"So the song may kill me."

"The song may kill you," Odile said, with the gentle precision of a surgeon naming a tumor. "Or it may not. There has never been a singer who had what you have—an entire city's beasts to carry the chorus, a company to anchor them, a fox bound to the bloodline standing at their shoulder. Corvane sang alone in a locked room. You will not be alone. That may be the difference. I genuinely do not know." She paused. "I would rather you hate me now than thank me later."

"I don't hate you," I said. And found, examining myself, that it was true. "I spent twenty-three years waiting for something to kill me for what I am. At least this death would be on purpose."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. "That," she said, rising with another groan, "is either very brave or very stupid, and history will sort out which." At the foot of the stairs she paused. "For what it's worth, I hope it's neither. I hope it's Tuesday."

* * *

Night came down through the cellar grate in thin gray ribbons. The others slept—Pip curled against Quill's flank, both pretending this arrangement was for warmth; Odile in her corner with a book she wasn't reading; Sera nowhere, which meant Sera was on watch, which meant Sera couldn't sleep, which meant—

Which meant I found her at the top of the cistern stair, sitting with her back to the stone, sword across her knees, looking at nothing.

"There's room," she said, without turning. Of course she'd heard me. The woman could hear a moth change its mind.

I sat down beside her. For a while neither of us spoke, which was its own kind of conversation, the kind we'd been having for months—the comfortable silence of two people who had each decided the other was safe and were both too proud to say so out loud.

Above us, faint through the stones, the bells rang the second hour. They never stopped anymore. Vhalor kept them going day and night now, a fever in the city's ears, and I felt it the way you feel a toothache—distant, constant, wrong.

"I keep thinking about the valley," I said.

"Don't."

"Bramble. The ewe. Whether Marta is feeding her right, whether anyone's talking to her, because she gets lonely, and no one else in that valley speaks sheep, they just shout at them." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "Twenty-three years, Sera. I built a whole life out of being almost-happy. Almost was fine. Almost was survivable. And then you handed me a bowl of stew on the road and called me 'mute' to a checkpoint officer with a straight face, and somewhere between that and the fen I stopped being able to go back to almost."

"Rafael—"

"Let me finish, because if I stop I won't start again." The words were coming fast now, the way water comes through a cracked dam. "Tomorrow—or the night after, whichever way the moon falls—we walk into that throne room and I sing a song that might unmake me, and you lift a crown off a haunted king's head, and the odds that we both walk out are—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Odile says the song may kill the singer. So before I possibly die in a room full of gold and hunger, I am going to say the true thing, which is that I love you. Not the way comrades love. Not the way exiles love out of shared damage. I love you the specific way, the embarrassing way, the way where I memorized how you hold your sword and how you lie to officers and how you look at Pip when you think no one sees you being soft. I have loved you since Thornhollow, probably, and I said nothing for months because I am a coward who talks to animals because animals can't leave you, and tomorrow I might die, and I refuse—do you hear me, Sera Vane—I *refuse* to die with that still folded up inside me like a letter I never sent."

Silence. The bells finished their hour. Somewhere below, Quill shifted in his sleep.

Sera was very still. Then, quietly, in a voice I had never heard from her—unarmored, stripped to the skin of it:

"I refused to burn a child." She stared straight ahead. "That's why they broke me. Everyone thinks it was some grand principle. It wasn't. It was that the boy looked at me the way my brother used to look at me, and my hands wouldn't move, and I have regretted nothing less in my life. I lost everything that day. Rank, name, the only shape my life had ever had. And then a mute servant with hay in their hair pulled me out of a ditch because a horse cried for help, and I thought, *there it is, the new shape, and it's this ridiculous person who apologizes to goats.*"

"You don't apologize to goats."

"I apologize to one goat. It earned it." Her hand found mine in the dark—calloused, sure, shaking slightly, which undid me more than any words. "I love you too. I have been trying not to, because loving someone in this kingdom is handing the crown a knife and showing them where you keep your heart. But I'm done trying. If we die in two nights, I die having said it. If we live—" her breath caught, and steadied, "—if we live, I'd like to find out what people like us do with mornings. All of them. As many as we can steal."

"What do we do right now?" I asked.

"Now," she said, and leaned her head against my shoulder, sword still across her knees, knight to the last, "now we sit here until the third bell, and then we wake the others, and we win. Because I'll tell you a secret, Rafael Almeida. I've planned retreats my whole career. Tonight is the first time I've planned to come back."

We sat. The moon, above the grate, wore itself down to a nail-paring of light.

And that was when Quill's voice came up the stairwell, sharp and awake and utterly without humor:

"Rafael. Sera. Wake the others. There are torches in the street above the lamplighter's house—and the man who lives here is standing in his doorway, talking to them."
