## 26. The Betrayal

I want to tell you that I saw it coming. I want to tell you I smelled the rot on Tam Wicker three days before he did it, that I noticed the way his hands shook when he passed the bread, the way he stopped meeting Rafael's eyes, the way his laugh went thin as winter ice.

I didn't. Or rather — I noticed all of it, and I explained it away, because explaining things away is a skill I learned young and practiced often. His hands shook because he was old. He didn't meet your eyes because he was shy. His laugh was thin because the cellar was cold and bread was scarce and everyone's laugh had gone thin by the second week.

That's the thing about fear. It doesn't announce itself. It moves in quietly, like damp through stone, and by the time you see it on someone's face it has already been living in their chest for months, eating the good rooms out of them one by one until there's nothing left but the part that does arithmetic — the part that counts your children against your conscience and finds the children heavier every single time.

Tam Wicker had two daughters. He'd hidden gifted folk under his bakery for eleven years before we ever came down those stairs. Eleven years of flour-dusted courage, and it took the crown four days to unmake him.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

Let me back up to the morning it started, which looked exactly like every other morning, which is how disasters always dress.

We'd been in the cellars beneath Corvenna for nine days. Nine days of learning the binding song in fragments, of Odile pacing the length of the candle-lit tunnel with her ink-stained fingers laced behind her back like a general reviewing troops she didn't trust yet, of Quill sitting on the one dry crate and making comments that were funny right up until they weren't.

The cellar network ran under half the lower city — old smugglers' tunnels, mostly, brick and slime and the smell of a hundred years of other people's secrets. There were maybe forty of us down there. Gifted folk who'd fled the purge, sympathizers who carried baskets of food down through their own cellars and pretended they were storing turnips, and one very nervous priest named Father Aldric who kept apologizing to God under his breath and then apologizing to us for apologizing.

Rafael spent most of each day singing. Not with their throat — with their hands, their shoulders, the set of their spine, the language of the body that Quill called beast-speech and I was only beginning to hear properly. The song was strange. When Rafael sang it right, the air in the tunnels felt *thinner*, like something heavy had been lifted off the world for a moment. When they sang it wrong — which was often — the rats got agitated, and Quill made a face like he'd bitten a lemon, and Odile wrote another note in her little book.

I was learning too. My gift is mending — broken cups, cracked beams, torn skin, given time and my two hands and a stubbornness I inherited from nobody, being as I inherited from nobody at all. But Rafael said listening was a gift too, and they were teaching me, and some nights I could almost catch it: the hum of the city above us, thousands of animals going about their lives, dogs dreaming, pigeons arguing about rooftops, cats pretending not to care about any of it.

It was the closest thing to a home I'd had since I ran. Which should have been the first warning. Homes, in my experience, come with an expiry date printed somewhere small.

* * *

Tam Wicker brought our bread every second day. He was a round man with flour in his eyebrows permanently, even though I'm fairly sure eyebrows don't hold flour, and he had a way of talking that made everything sound like either a joke or a complaint, usually both. He complained about the price of grain. He joked about Father Aldric's nervousness. He complained about the king, then immediately joked that complaining about the king was the safest hobby left in Corvenna since it required no equipment.

He loved his daughters. That was the whole of him, really — everything else was decoration around that fact. Lissa was seven and Maren was ten, and he talked about them the way other men talked about harvests or victories. Maren had her mother's temper. Lissa had drawn a picture of a fox and asked if foxes could really talk, and Tam had laughed and said no, of course not, sweetheart, and then come down into the cellar and stared at Quill for a long moment with an expression I can only describe as theological crisis.

Quill, to his credit, pretended to be an ordinary fox whenever strangers were present. He was very bad at it. Ordinary foxes do not sigh.

On the ninth day, Tam came down with the bread and his jokes were wrong. Not absent — wrong. They landed a half-beat late, like a dancer counting music only he couldn't hear. And when Rafael thanked him, Tam flinched.

Flinched. At thanks. From a person he'd been feeding for over a week.

I noticed. I swear on whatever orphaned children swear on, I noticed. And then Sera asked him if something was troubling him up top, and Tam said no, nothing, all quiet, quiet as church, and smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a borrowed coat fits — correctly, and not at all.

Sera noticed too. She was a knight; noticing was her trade. But what do you do with a noticing? You can't arrest a man for smiling badly. You can't interrogate your own baker because his laugh came out thin. The whole architecture of trust is built on agreeing not to treat every wrong note as a confession.

So we let it go. We ate his bread. We slept in his tunnels. And somewhere above us, in a house that smelled of yeast and fear, a man sat at his table doing the arithmetic I told you about, and every night the children weighed a little more.

* * *

It happened on the eleventh night. I know it was the eleventh because Odile had just declared that Rafael would be ready "soon," and when I asked soon for what, exactly, she gave me a look that suggested I was not cleared for the shape of the word, only its direction.

I was asleep in a side alcove with my back against warm brick, Quill curled against my feet like a hot water bottle with opinions. Rafael was a few yards off, breathing slow. Sera had first watch, because Sera always took first watch and last watch and considered sleep a personal failing she permitted herself out of courtesy to the rest of us.

I woke because the dark changed.

I don't know how else to say it. The dark in the tunnels had a texture — close, soft, familiar, the dark of a place that knew you. This dark had edges. Boots have a particular sound on old brick, a flat knock that stone floors don't make, and there were a lot of boots, and they were coming from more than one direction, which meant somebody had known the map.

"Up," Sera said, and her voice wasn't loud but it went through me like a nail. "Everyone up. Now."

Then the screaming started — human screaming, from deeper in the network, where the families were. And under it, worse, the bells. All of them. Every bell in Corvenna ringing at once at midnight, a sound like the sky tearing, and I remembered what Rafael had told me about the bells, that they were built to drown out listening, and I understood in one sick drop of the stomach that this was not a raid. This was a harvest.

Lantern light came around the corner of the main tunnel, and shapes came with it — hunters in grey and black, the crown's badge on their chests, and with them dogs. Big ones. The kind bred for this, for finding people who smell of magic.

And here is the thing I will carry until I die: the dogs were crying.

Not barking. Crying. A high, miserable whine threaded through their growls, and I didn't need Rafael's gift to understand it, because I'd been learning, and what they were saying was *we don't want to, we don't want to, why are you making us.* They could smell the gifted down the tunnels like blood in water, and they hated it, hated the taste of it, hated their handlers, hated the leash. Some of them were shaking.

One of them looked straight at me. Brown eyes, wet nose, misery written plain across a face built for violence. And I did the thing Rafael taught me without deciding to do it — opened the door in my mind, the way you open a window onto a storm — and said, in the body-language of beasts, *run.*

He ran. Straight backward, dragging his handler three yards before the man got him under control, cursing, and in those three yards of chaos Sera moved.

I have seen Sera Vane fight twice now, and both times I understood that the word "fight" was too small. What she did was closer to weather. Two hunters came at her in the narrow tunnel and stopped existing as threats — one disarmed, one folded around her knee and deposited on the floor like laundry. She fought the way she did everything: efficiently, furiously, and with the expression of a woman settling very old accounts.

"Rafael!" she shouted over the bells. "The song pages — get the pages and go!"

Because that was the arithmetic of the moment, the terrible kind, the kind where you count what you can carry. The song — the binding song, the only weapon in the kingdom that mattered — was written across six pages in Odile's cramped hand, and they were wrapped in oilcloth inside Rafael's coat, and Rafael was already moving toward them, and so was I, and so was Quill, running low and fast along the wall like a fox-shaped shadow with a grudge.

Which is when I saw Odile.

She was at the far end of the main chamber, near the tunnel mouth that led up toward the chandlery, and she wasn't running. She was standing in front of three frightened families — a tinker with burn-scarred palms, a woman whose eyes went silver when the moon was full, two children — and she had her arms spread like a gate.

"Go," she was saying to them. "Down the water passage, go, I'll hold them."

"You can't hold them," the tinker said. "Odile, you can't—"

"I was court mage," she said, and her voice had iron in it, the voice of a woman who had once stood in palaces and been thrown out of them and had decided, finally, what she was for. "I have held worse than this. Go."

They went. She turned to face the hunters alone, raised her hands, and did something with the air that made the lantern flames bend sideways and the nearest hunter stagger like a drunk. For ten seconds — I counted, because counting is what I do instead of crying — she was magnificent.

Then a hunter she hadn't seen came out of the side passage and put a weighted net over her, and the flames bent back, and the magnificence ended the way magnificence always ends, suddenly and on the floor.

"No!" I shouted, and started toward her, and a hand closed on my collar and hauled me backward so hard my feet left the ground.

Sera. Her face was next to mine, close enough that I could see the scar through her eyebrow, close enough to see that she hated what she was doing.

"She's taken," Sera said. "You go back there, you're taken too, and then the song dies with all of us. *Move.*"

"But—"

"*Move.*"

I moved. I want you to know I hated moving. I want you to know that leaving her there, netted and struggling while the hunters bound her wrists, is a weight I expect to carry alongside the memory of the crying dogs, and that I am keeping both weights, because I think a person who sets down weights like that stops being a person worth writing about.

* * *

The water passage was exactly as pleasant as it sounds. Knee-deep, black, cold enough to make my teeth file complaints, and smelling of every century that had ever poured anything into it. We went single file — Sera first with a stolen hunter's lantern, then Rafael, then me, with Quill riding on my shoulder like a furious red scarf, dripping tunnel water and dignity in equal measure.

Behind us the bells rang and rang. Somewhere in that ringing, muffled by distance and stone, I thought I heard voices — the true king's voice, Rafael had said, faint and screaming inside the bells — and I pressed my hands over my ears and it didn't help, because the worst sounds don't come in through the ears.

We walked for the better part of an hour. Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the water slapping against the walls wouldn't have said better.

When we finally climbed out through a collapsed grate into a burnt granary near the river — Quill's idea of shelter, and I use "idea" generously — Rafael sat down on a scorched beam and unwrapped the oilcloth, and the six pages were there, intact, ink smeared only slightly where the water had found a corner.

"We have the song," Rafael said, in a voice like a report filed by someone else.

"We have the song," Sera agreed. She was cleaning her sword with a strip of cloth, methodical, mechanical. "We don't have Odile. We don't have the network. We don't have Father Aldric, or the tinker, or half the people who fed us." The cloth paused. "We don't have anywhere to be tomorrow."

"The granary," Quill said, "is available."

"Thank you, Quill."

"You're welcome. I also accept gratitude in the form of dried fish."

I laughed. It came out wrong — thin, the way Tam's laugh had gone thin — and halfway through it turned into something else, and I sat down hard on the granary floor and cried the way twelve-year-olds cry, gracelessly, with hiccups, in front of people I respected, which is the worst venue for it.

Rafael came over and sat beside me. Didn't say anything clever. Just put an arm around my shoulders and let me be disgusting for a while, which is a form of love I intend to write down someday and pass on.

"It was Tam," Sera said eventually, into the quiet. "Wasn't it."

Nobody answered, which was an answer.

"I saw him flinch," I said, when I could talk. "Days ago. I saw it and I — I made excuses. Everyone made excuses. Because he was *kind*, because he brought bread, because—" I wiped my nose on my sleeve with tremendous dignity. "Because wanting him to be safe felt easier than being right about him."

"He had daughters," Rafael said quietly. "Two girls. Seven and ten."

"And the hunters have those girls now too, probably. If he sold us to save them, it didn't even work." I heard my own voice go bitter and didn't stop it. "That's the trick of it, isn't it? The whole kingdom runs on it. They don't need to torture anyone. They just need everyone to have someone. A daughter, a brother, a mother. Make love the lock and fear the key and you don't need many soldiers at all. People will deliver each other gift-wrapped."

The words hung in the smoky air. Sera stopped cleaning her sword. Rafael looked at the floor. And Quill — Quill, who had a comment for everything, who had narrated our entire journey like a cynical chorus in a play — said nothing at all for a long moment, and then:

"I watched a king be loved," he said, "by everyone who had something to lose. It didn't save him." He licked a paw, fastidious, wounded. "Being loved is not protection. It never was. It's just the reason the losing hurts."

* * *

We didn't sleep much. Toward the small hours, Sera laid out the situation with a stick in the ash on the floor, drawing the city in rough strokes: the palace, the bell towers, the river, the granary marked with an X.

"They'll move Odile to the palace," she said. "Publicly, probably. A captured court mage is a trophy. Vhalor will want her where it can feed slowly." Her stick tapped the palace. "Which means she's not dead. Which means there's a version of the next few days where we get her back."

"And the timeline?" Rafael asked. "The moon turns in—" They counted on their fingers, then stopped, because counting it made it real. "Soon."

"Soon," Sera agreed grimly. "And now we've lost the cellars, the guild contacts, the priest, the safe houses, and every friendly face in the lower city — because every face Tam knew is a face the hunters know now. Whatever we do next, we do it with no floor under us."

There was a silence after that, the kind that has plans growing in it like mold.

I broke it, because apparently I'm that sort of boy. "So we get her back," I said. "Before the moon turns. We get Odile out of the palace, and then Rafael sings the song, and we win, and it's fine."

Four faces turned toward me — well, three faces and one fox, whose face is harder to read but whose ears had gone very still.

"Pip," Sera said, and her voice had gone gentle, which from Sera is more alarming than shouting, "do you understand what you just proposed?"

"A rescue. Then a song."

"A rescue of a prisoner held by an immortal hunger-spirit, inside the most guarded building in the kingdom, by four people, one of whom is twelve, one of whom is a fox, one of whom has been dead to the crown for years, and one of whom has to stand still and sing while the spirit tries to eat their soul." She rubbed her eyes. "Yes. Fine. Simple."

"Well," I said, "when you describe it like that it sounds difficult."

"It *is* difficult."

"Good." I pulled my knees up and looked at the ash-map, at the little X marking the granary, at the palace sketched like a child would draw it, because a child had. "Difficult things are the only ones left. Everything easy got sold for bread."

Rafael laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of them, tired and cracked and alive. Sera almost smiled, which for Sera is a religious experience. And Quill stood up on the beam, shook the ash off his tail, and delivered the verdict of the company:

"Then it's settled. We do the impossible thing, in the impossible place, in an impossible amount of time." He paused. "I'll need more dried fish."

But underneath the joke, underneath the ash-map and the exhausted laughter, I could feel the truth of the night settling over all of us like the cold coming up through the granary floor. We had trusted a baker, and the trust had been used as a knife. We had trusted a home, and the home had been mapped and sold. Every name we'd eaten with was a name on a list now, and Odile — brilliant, bitter, brave Odile — was in a cage being carried toward the thing that eats souls, and the moon was turning whether we were ready or not.

Somewhere across the sleeping city, the bells rang again. Shorter, this time. Almost like laughter.

Almost like a countdown.
