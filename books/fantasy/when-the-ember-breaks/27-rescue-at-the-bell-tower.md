## 27. Rescue at the Bell Tower

I want to be able to say that I made a noble decision, that I stood up in the burnt granary with firelight on my face and declared we would not abandon Odile. The truth is less dignified. The truth is that I couldn't sleep, and every time I closed my eyes I saw her face — that dry, unimpressed face — and heard her voice saying *don't be stupid, Rafael* in exactly the tone she'd use if she knew what I was about to do.

Which is how Sera found me at midnight, already dressed, standing over Quill's sleeping form like a thief deciding whether to rob a church.

"No," she said.

"You don't even know what—"

"I know that look. That's the look of someone about to do something heroic and irreversible." She crossed her arms. She was sitting against the granary wall with her sword across her knees, because Sera Vane did not sleep so much as she took shifts at being awake. "The answer is no."

"Odile taught me half the song," I said. "She's the only one who's read the archive pages besides me. She knows Vhalor's true name in the old tongue. If the spirit feeds on her before the thirteenth moon—"

"Then we've lost an ally. If we go after her now, we lose everything. You, me, Pip, the song, all of it." Her voice was low and flat, the voice she used for tactics. "Rafael, I have planned retreats before. This is not a rescue. It's a funeral with extra steps."

And that should have been the end of it, because Sera was usually right, and I usually knew it. But Quill opened one eye — foxes can do that, open one eye as if the other one is still making up its mind — and said:

"The bell tower."

Sera and I both looked at him.

"The great tower of Corvenna," he said, sitting up and shaking ash off his coat with tremendous dignity. "You're both thinking of it as a building. I'm asking you to think of it as a cage full of birds. And I'm telling you that I have spent the last year, in my small and humiliating way, learning every crack and rafter of this city from creatures who live in it rent-free. There are passages through that tower that no man has walked since the masons left. Rat roads. Rook roads. A gap behind the bells where pigeons roost and nothing larger than a cat has ever bothered to look."

"So?" Sera said.

"So the prisoners taken in tonight's purge will be held there. In the ringing chamber and below it. Waiting for the feast." Quill's tail went very still, which for Quill was the equivalent of a man going pale. "I watched them carry the cages in at dusk. I counted."

"How many?"

"Eleven." He looked at me. "One of them argued with the guards the entire way. Loudly. About their grammar."

I felt something in my chest loosen and break at the same time. Odile would argue about grammar. Odile would correct a hunter's pronunciation of a death sentence.

"We're going," I said.

"Rafael—" Sera started.

"Sera." I turned to face her, and I found that my hands were steady, which surprised me. "A year ago I stood in a valley and listened to a child being dragged away in the night, and I told myself the valley stones kept me safe and there was nothing I could do. I am done telling myself that. I am done being safe in ways that cost other people everything." I swallowed. "Come or don't come. But I'm going."

There was a long silence. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the burnt rafters, and the whole granary creaked like an old ship.

Then Sera swore — a soldier's oath, comprehensive and almost loving in its thoroughness — and began checking the straps on her sword.

"Pip stays here," she said.

"Pip stays here," I agreed.

"He's going to hate that."

"He's going to be furious. He'll sulk for a week. And he'll be alive to do it." I looked down at where Pip slept, one arm flung over his eyes, his bandaged shoulder rising and falling. Thirteen years old and he'd already bled for us once. "That's the point."

Quill stretched, yawned enormously, and said, "Well. If we must die in a tower, at least it's a tall one. I've always wanted my last words to have acoustics."

* * *

We went in through the corpse road.

That's what the rats called it — the rats had names for everything, most of them better than the ones humans used. It was a channel in the palace foundations, built three hundred years ago to carry away rainwater, abandoned since, and colonized since then by every small thing in Corvenna that preferred the crown not know where it lived. We walked bent double through the dark while Quill narrated turns in a whisper, and the smell was exactly what you'd expect from a road maintained by rats, and I loved it desperately, because it was the first time in my life I had ever entered a fortress through the back door of the small and overlooked.

That's the thing nobody tells you about beast-speech. Everyone imagines it's about power. Commanding wolves, calling eagles, whatever the ballads say. But mostly it's about listening to the ones nobody listens to. And the small and overlooked see everything.

The rat who met us at the foundation wall was old and gray-muzzled and missing an ear, and she regarded us with the weary patience of someone who has been asked for favors by larger creatures many times.

"You're the speaker," she said. Not a question. "Quill vouched for you. That means nothing to me, but he pays his debts, which is more than I can say for most of your kind."

"What's your name?" I asked, because Marta's son had once told me that the first thing you owe anyone is their name.

Something flickered in the old rat's whiskers. Surprise, maybe. "Bristle," she said. "It's been forty years since anyone asked."

"Bristle, we need to reach the bell tower without being seen."

"I know. I was told." She turned and led us into the dark. "Follow exactly where I walk. Some of these boards hold. Most don't."

* * *

The bell tower rose out of the palace's eastern flank like a stone finger pointed at the moon — which was nearly full now, two nights short of its thirteenth turn, fat and white and wrong-looking. Even from outside I could hear it: a hum in the bronze, low and constant, like a hive of bees made of metal. The bells never stopped ringing anymore, but up close I could feel that they weren't ringing *now*. They were waiting. Holding their breath.

"They're hungry too," I murmured.

"Everything in this city is hungry," Bristle said. "This way. The pigeon gap is behind the third buttress. After that, you're beyond my roads. My people don't go up. Too much iron. Too much..." She hesitated, and for a moment the weary old scavenger sounded genuinely frightened. "Too much *listening*. Something in that tower listens back."

Then she was gone into the dark, and I thought: I will remember your name, Bristle. Whatever happens tonight, someone will remember it.

The pigeon gap was exactly as advertised — a seam in the masonry wide enough for a thin person sideways, running upward between the outer wall and an inner shaft. Quill went first, flowing up the crack like water running backward. Then me. Then Sera, swearing under her breath in a continuous mutter that I suspect was actually a prayer in disguise.

Halfway up, my gift came back.

It hit me like cold water. All the way through the city it had been muffled — the bells drowning everything, that constant agonizing pressure behind my ears — but here, in the gap, pressed against ancient stone, I suddenly heard the tower itself. And the tower was *full*.

Fear. That was the first thing. Fear in layers, human fear from below and animal fear all around, pigeons packed into the rafters too terrified to coo, rats frozen in the walls, even the spiders holding still in their webs. And underneath the fear, threaded through it like wire through rope:

Voices. Human voices, faint, praying, cursing, weeping. Eleven of them.

And one voice arguing about grammar.

"There," I breathed. "Sera. She's alive. They're all alive."

"For now." Sera's voice came from below me, tight. "Move."

We came out of the gap onto a maintenance ledge in the belfry's upper dark, and I got my first look at the ringing chamber below through the gaps in the floorboards. Torches. Six hunters in crown black. Cages — actual cages, iron-barred, the kind used for hounds — arranged in a row beneath the great bells. And in the cages, eleven people. An old man clutching a wooden charm. Two sisters holding hands through the bars. A boy no older than Pip, rocking himself. And at the far end, gray-haired and upright despite everything, arms crossed, mid-sentence:

"—and furthermore, if you're going to misquote the Edict of Cages, at least misquote it *correctly*, it's Article Nine, not Seven, you absolute boot-licking—"

A guard struck the bars with a halberd shaft. Odile didn't even flinch.

"That's her," I whispered. "That's Odile."

"I see her." Sera was beside me, and I felt rather than saw her counting — doors, guards, distances, weights. Old habits. "Six guards. Two exits we know of, probably a third we don't. The cages are locked with padlocks; I can open those if I get close. The problem is getting close."

"The problem," Quill said softly, "is the bells."

I had been trying not to listen to them. Now I let myself, and immediately wished I hadn't. Up close, the hum wasn't a hum. It was a voice. Or the memory of a voice, worn smooth, like a word repeated ten thousand times until it stops meaning anything. And beneath it, fainter, almost lost:

*Singing.*

No. Not singing. *Counting.* Something in the bronze was counting down, patient and vast, and every ring of every bell across the city was a syllable in the count.

"Quill," I said. "What are the bells for? Really?"

He was quiet a long moment. When he answered, his voice had none of its usual flippancy.

"Aldous had them cast in his first year. Twelve great bells, one for each moon. I always thought it was vanity. Kings love the sound of their own names rung in bronze." His ears flattened. "But I stood in this tower the day they first rang, and I watched Aldous's face, and he wasn't proud. He was *listening*. Like a man tuning an instrument. Like a man testing a lock from the inside." He looked at me. "I think the bells aren't the spirit's celebration, Rafael. I think they're its *leash*. And I think it's been tightening them for a year."

Below us, a door banged open.

* * *

The man who entered the ringing chamber wore no crown.

I want that recorded clearly, because everything else about him said king. He wore plain traveling clothes, gray wool, soft boots — the dress of a man who has stopped needing to impress anyone. He was perhaps fifty-five, broad through the shoulders, with a beard going silver at the edges and deep lines around his eyes, the kind of lines that come from laughing a great deal or grieving a great deal, possibly both. The six hunters went to their knees. The prisoners shrank against their cage bars.

And I felt Quill stop breathing beside me.

"*Aldous*," the fox whispered, and the name came out of him cracked down the middle.

Because it was him. It was Aldous's face — I'd seen it on coins, on the proclamation nailed to our chapel door, painted stern and golden on every wall in the kingdom. But the paintings had shown a man in his prime, hard-eyed and crowned. This face was tired. This face was *kind*. He walked down the row of cages slowly, and when the old man with the wooden charm began to weep, the king reached through the bars and — gently, the way you'd touch a frightened animal — rested his hand on the man's head.

"Hush," he said. His voice carried in the great stone space, warm and resonant, a voice made for a throne room. "Hush now. It won't hurt. I promise you it won't hurt. Nothing hurts, in the end. That's the mercy of it."

"You lie," the old man choked.

"Yes," the king agreed pleasantly. "I do."

And that was when I understood, in my skin and my bones and the place behind my ribs where my gift lived, that I was looking at Vhalor. Not a shadow wearing a shape. Not a monster in a mask. *This* — the tired eyes, the gentle hands, the warm voice — this was what a devouring spirit looked like after a year of wearing a good man. It had learned his smile. It had learned his tenderness. It wore them the way a magpie wears stolen silver, and somewhere underneath all that borrowed kindness, the real Aldous was screaming.

My hands were shaking. Sera's hand closed on my wrist — a knight's grip, meant to anchor.

"Don't," she breathed. "Whatever you're thinking. *Don't.*"

But the king's head turned.

Not toward us. Toward the wall. Toward the pigeon gap, toward the dark where three small bodies hid among the rafters — and I realized with a lurch that the birds had gone silent, utterly silent, and silence in a roost is a shout.

"Ah," the king said softly. "Mice in the walls."

He didn't call the guards. That was the horror of it. He simply raised one hand, and the six hunters rose and filed out through the far door without a word, without a glance at the cages, leaving their king alone in the torchlight with eleven prisoners and whatever mice had come to visit.

"Come down," he said, conversationally, to the rafters. "Or stay up there and fall asleep and drop. Either way, we'll talk. I've been looking forward to this." He smiled, and it was Aldous's smile, worn soft with use. "The little speaker from the dead valley. Do you know, you're the only interesting thing to happen in this kingdom in a hundred years?"

Quill's claws dug into my shoulder — either warning or plea, I couldn't tell. Below us, Odile had gone very still, her sharp eyes lifted to the rafters, finding us there with uncanny accuracy. She gave the smallest shake of her head. *Don't.*

I thought about the ewe in the valley, lame and trusting, the first creature I ever spoke to. I thought about Marta's son, dragged away in the night while I stayed silent. I thought about Bristle, whose name no one had asked in forty years. I thought about a boy under a bridge, bleeding, telling me to run.

Every time in my life I had ever hidden, someone had paid for it.

I climbed down.

The stone was cold under my boots. The torchlight made long shadows of both of us — the king in his gray wool, and me, a goat-herder with straw still somehow in my hair, standing in the belly of the enemy's tower. Behind me I heard Sera descend in silence, blade loose in its sheath, and Quill flow down the wall like a ribbon of fire, and none of it mattered, because the king's eyes had found mine and would not let go.

"There you are," Vhalor said gently.

Up close, the wrongness was worse. His eyes were kind and empty at once, like windows of a house where every lamp has been lit but no one is home. And beneath the warmth of his voice, so faint I might have imagined it, I heard something else — a second voice, buried deep, hoarse from screaming, saying words I could not make out but whose *shape* I knew.

Beast-speech. The true king was speaking beast-speech, down in the dark where the spirit couldn't hear him. Or couldn't be bothered to.

"Your Majesty," I said, because my mouth apparently had its own plans.

The king laughed — a real laugh, delighted, Aldous's laugh. "Oh, *good*. Manners. Do you know how long it's been since anyone called me that honestly? The court says it like a password. The hunters say it like a spell. But you—" he stepped closer, and I forced myself not to step back, "—you mean it the way the old speakers meant it. With fear and courtesy together. That's proper. That's *old*." He tilted his head. "Tell me your name."

"Rafael."

"Just Rafael?"

"Just Rafael."

"Mm. And the fox is Quill, who served my bloodline and failed it. And the woman behind you is Sera Vane, who broke her oath to save a child and has regretted it every day since, though not, I think, in the way her commanders hoped." His gaze moved past me, mild as milk. "Good evening, Ser Vane. Your sword hand is trembling. You may want to breathe."

Sera said nothing. Her silence had edges.

"And you've come for the mage." The king turned, considering Odile through the bars the way a man considers a horse he intends to buy. "Understandable. She's worth ten of the others. A century of court magic in that skull, and such a *flavor* to her gift — bitter, disciplined, excellent vintage." He sighed. "But I'm afraid the cellar is closed, Rafael. The feast is in two nights. Everything must be fresh."

"Then take me instead."

The words were out before I'd decided to say them, and I felt Sera go rigid behind me, and Quill make a sound like a bitten-off snarl, and Odile close her eyes as if praying for patience.

The king went still. Truly still, for the first time — and in that stillness I glimpsed the thing underneath, vast and cold and *hungry*, turning its attention on me like a searchlight.

"You would offer yourself," it said, and the warmth drained out of the voice, and what remained was older than the tower, older than the bells, older than the kingdom. "For eleven strangers. For a mage who mocks you. For a king you've never met."

"For everyone you've put in a cage," I said. My voice didn't shake. I don't know how. "Yes."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, the great bells hummed their countdown, and the torches guttered, and the boy in the last cage had stopped rocking to stare at me.

And then Vhalor smiled — slow, and terrible, and almost tender — and said:

"No."

I blinked. "...No?"

"Not yet. Not like this. A gift given in fear spoils the flavor." He leaned in, and his voice dropped to a murmur meant for me alone, and it was the kindest sound I had ever heard and the worst. "In two nights, little speaker, you'll stand in my throne room, and you'll sing your little song, and you'll fail — because the song needs a chorus, and I own every throat in this city. And when you fail, you'll understand that you were never here to save anyone. You were here to be *harvested*. The finest gift in a generation, walking itself to my table." He straightened, and the warmth returned to his face like sun coming back from behind a cloud. "Guards will show you out. Do try to survive until the moon turns. I'd hate for the story to end early."

He turned away. Dismissed us. And as he walked toward the stair, passing so close I could have touched the gray wool sleeve, I heard it again — that buried voice, ragged and desperate, and this time, this once, the words came through:

*"...the bells... it's not the crown... the bells are the binding, turned inside out... silence them... silence them and..."*

The voice cut off like a snapped thread. The king paused at the door. He did not turn around.

"Two nights," Vhalor said pleasantly. "Do be punctual."

The door closed behind him. The torches burned. Eleven faces stared at me through iron bars, and Odile's eyes were wet and furious and alive, and Quill was shaking against my leg, and Sera's hand found my shoulder and gripped it hard enough to bruise.

"Rafael," she said quietly. "Tell me you heard it too."

I looked at the great bells hanging silent and humming above us, twelve bronze moons waiting for their thirteenth turn, and I heard the true king's voice still echoing in the place behind my ribs where all the voices of beasts and broken men come home.

"He told us how to kill it," I said. "We have to silence the bells."

Above us, as if it had heard, the nearest bell began — softly, sweetly, horribly — to ring.
