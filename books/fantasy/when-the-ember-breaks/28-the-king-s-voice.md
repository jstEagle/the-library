## 28. The King's Voice

The bell tower of Corvenna was not a building so much as a throat.

That was my first thought as Quill and I came up out of the animal passages into the ringing dark—nine hundred feet, give or take, of stone and rope and bronze, all of it shuddering with the bells above us like the inside of some enormous struck drum. The floor trembled. My teeth trembled. Somewhere below, Sera was drawing hunters through the palace halls by being loudly, magnificently herself, and I prayed to whatever gods listened in bell towers that her talent for making noise would keep her alive.

"Odile should be here," Quill shouted against the din. He had to press his muzzle nearly against my ear to be heard at all. "She said the prisoners would be held in the ringing chamber!"

"She also said the guild would have two towers silenced by now!" I shouted back. "How many do you hear?"

He cocked his ears, that quick fox arithmetic. "All of them."

All of them. So much for sabotage. The bells rang on and on and on, and I understood suddenly why Vhalor had built this city this way—why every street corner sprouted a belfry, why the ringers' guild was the richest in Corvenna, why even the beggars hummed the carillon's pattern without knowing they did it. It wasn't music. It had never been music. It was a wall of sound thrown up around a prison, and somewhere inside it a man was screaming.

I just didn't know it yet. Not then. Then, I only knew we had maybe minutes before the hunters regrouped, and a cellar full of strays counting on us, and an old mage in a cell below who'd looked at me through the bars and said, *You're the singer? Gods help us all.*

* * *

The ringing chamber was exactly as wrong as everything else in the palace, which is to say it was beautiful.

Bronze bells in ranks, each one taller than me, their mouths open toward the floor like flowers turned wrong-side-up. Ropes thick as my wrist hanging in patient rows. And along the walls—cages. Not the iron kind. Wooden ones, well-made, padded, the sort of cages a thoughtful person builds for something they intend to keep alive. Each held a prisoner. Each cage had a slot for food and a small brass plaque, because apparently even horror appreciates good record-keeping.

I found Odile's cage third from the window. She was sitting cross-legged, hands folded, looking less like a disgraced court mage and more like a woman waiting out a dull sermon. Her eyes found mine and she didn't smile, but the line of her shoulders dropped half an inch, and from Odile that was practically weeping with relief.

"You're late," she said.

"The roads were busy."

"The roads are always busy when you're fleeing a hunt you started." She jerked her chin toward the far wall, where a heavy oak door stood shut. "Keys will be on the warden. The warden will be wherever wardens go when they hear fighting downstairs. Go."

I went. The warden was, in fact, exactly where predicted—a broad man huddled behind the stairwell door with a cudgel and a face full of regrets—and he gave up his keys faster than any man I'd ever robbed, mostly because a scarred red fox trotted out of the shadows, sat down in front of him, and said, in perfect courtly speech, "I wouldn't, if I were you."

The man made a sound like a hinge and ran down the stairs. Quill watched him go with his tail curled in satisfaction.

"One day," I said, unlocking Odile's cage, "that trick is going to stop working."

"And on that day," Quill said, "the world will be a poorer place."

We got the others out—seven prisoners, six of them gifted, one of them simply a lamplighter who'd been in the wrong cellar on the wrong night. They moved slow and quiet, the way people move who've learned that noise is a currency spent best never. Odile organized them with little gestures, herding them toward the animal passages, and I hung back to count heads, because counting heads is what I do instead of panicking.

That was when the king spoke.

Not Vhalor. Not the hollow, honeyed thing that had addressed the city from its balcony, the voice like warm oil poured over a wound. This was different. This was underneath. For a moment I thought it was the bells themselves—the great bourdon bell, the monster called the Father, whose lowest note you feel in your sternum rather than hear. But it wasn't a note. It was words.

And they weren't in the tongue of men.

*Please.*

One word. In beast-speech. The language I had spoken for twenty-three years to goats and ewes and merchant horses, the language with no letters and no lies in it, the language that lives in the body—in the set of the shoulders, the drop of the breath, the angle of the head. You cannot fake it. You cannot perform it. And no human throat can make it, because humans stopped carrying it centuries ago, the way they stopped carrying tails.

But someone was making it. Someone was speaking it from inside the Father bell's deepest note, muffled and cracked and worn thin as a coin rubbed between fingers for years, and it said: *please*, and then, after a silence that lasted four heartbeats: *please, whoever you are, please.*

My knees did something unbecoming.

"Rafael." Quill's paw on my boot. "What is it? Your face has gone strange."

"He's in there," I whispered. "Quill. The king. He's *in there*. He's talking."

Quill went very still. Foxes are good at stillness—it's how they hunt—but this was a different stillness, the stillness of an animal hearing something it had spent a year telling itself was impossible. "Say that again."

"*Please.* That's what he said. Over and over. In beast-speech." I pressed my palm flat against the cold bronze flank of the nearest bell, feeling the vibration crawl up my arm like something climbing. "How is that possible? Humans don't speak it. Aldous couldn't—"

"No," Quill said slowly. "No ordinary man couldn't. But Aldous wasn't raised ordinary. He was raised *royal*." His ears flattened. "Rafael, the old royal houses—the bloodline goes back past the founding, back to the marsh kings, the fen kings, the ones who kept beast-speakers as counselors because they *were* half beast-speakers themselves. The gift runs in that blood like water in a well. Most kings let it run dry. Some have it beaten out of them by tutors who call it peasant magic." He looked up at me, and his green eyes were wet in the bell-light. "Aldous didn't let it run dry. I know because—I taught him. When he was a boy. Before I understood what wearing the crown cost him. He used to talk to me in it. Properly. Beautifully. Like a riverbank in summer."

I stared at him. Twenty-three years I'd hidden my gift in a valley where magic couldn't follow, believing myself the last ember of a dying fire, and the whole time the king himself had carried the same coal—and had it smothered under a crown, under a spirit, under a year of bronze noise designed specifically to drown him out.

"It's not just drowning him," I said. The understanding came all at once, ugly and complete. "It's not just covering him. The bells—they're *made* of him somehow, aren't they. Or bound to him."

"I don't know," Quill admitted. "Ask him."

So I did what I had spent a lifetime doing, except this time in reverse. I closed my eyes. I let the breath drop low into my body where the language lives. And I answered him—not with words, not at first, but the way you answer a frightened animal. A softening. An opening. *Here. I hear you. You're not alone in there.*

The response was immediate and devastating.

The Father bell's note changed. Just slightly—so slight that the ringers, if any remained at their ropes, might not have marked it. But I felt it shudder through the floor and up my spine, and inside that vibration was a voice breaking apart with relief, a voice that had been screaming politely into bronze for a year and had finally, finally been heard.

*Who—* it said, stumbling. The beast-speech was rusty, degraded, full of gaps where the crown had starved it. *Who speaks? Truly speaks? Not the hunger. Not the hunger wearing voices.*

*Rafael,* I told him. Name-gesture, the one you make with your whole chest. *Beast-speaker. From Ember Valley. I've come for you.*

A long trembling pause. Then, with a bitterness so sharp it cut through the bronze itself: *Come for me. Come for the crown, you mean. Everyone comes for the crown. Break the crown and the hunger falls. That is what the wise ones say. That is what the songs say.* A shudder ran through the great bell, and I felt something beneath the bitterness—something rawer. *They are wrong.*

*Then tell me what's right,* I said. *Tell me where it holds you. We have minutes, perhaps. Tell me true.*

*I cannot show you. I can only tell you what I feel, from inside my own bones.* And then the king of Corvenna, the Hollow King, the tyrant whose proclamations had burned Marta's son's name onto a bounty board—he spoke to me the way the lame ewe spoke to me about her bad hip. Plainly. Painfully. With the terrible honesty of a creature describing its own wound.

*The crown is a door,* Aldous said. *It is how the hunger entered me. But a door is nothing once the house is built. The hunger does not live in the crown. The crown is only the keyhole it crawled through, and it keeps the crown polished and feared because fools like me believe the stories. Its hold is elsewhere. Its hold is in the sound.*

*The sound,* I repeated, and my eyes went up, involuntarily, to the ranks of bronze towering over us. *The bells.*

*Every bell in this city was cast in the year it took my body,* Aldous said, and now the grief in him was so large it barely fit inside the vibration. *Nine hundred and sixty-one bells, cast from ore quenched in my blood—or near enough; do not ask me to explain sorcery, I lived it and I still don't understand it. Each bell carries a thread of the binding. The binding that should have caged the hunger forever, twisted inside out, so that instead of caging it, the sound feeds it. Instead of silencing it, the sound drowns me. Every hour of every day, the city rings, and every ring tightens the weave. It is why the bells never stop. Ask your fox. Ask him if they ever stopped. Even in mourning. Even in plague. Even the night the queen died, when every ringer begged leave to fall silent—did they stop?*

I looked at Quill. Quill's ears were flat to his skull.

"No," he said quietly. "They never stopped. I thought it was grief. His grief. I thought the king ordered it out of mourning." His tail dragged the floor. "It was the hunger ordering it. And I—" He stopped. Foxes don't apologize easily; pride is fur-deep with them. But he tried. "I failed him. I knew the bells were wrong. I smelled the wrongness and I told myself stories, because stories were easier than war."

*The thirteenth moon,* Aldous said, urgent now, and I felt his strength guttering like a candle in a draft. *The hunger thins then. The binding loosens. If the song is sung while the bells ring, it fails—the sound swallows the song, turns it, uses it. But if the bells fall silent—if the city's voice is taken from it, even for the length of one song—then the weave hangs loose, and the hunger can be unwound from me like thread from a spool.* A pause, and when he spoke again there was something almost like laughter in him, the awful laughter of a man who has been dead a year and just heard he might live. *You see the joke, beast-speaker? To save the king of the bells, you must silence every bell in Corvenna. All nine hundred and sixty-one. At once.*

* * *

We got the prisoners out through the animal passages—narrow brick channels built a century ago for the palace cats, wide enough for a stooped human and exactly the width of Odile's temper when she had to use them. The lamplighter cried a little. One of the gifted, a girl of maybe fifteen with frost in her fingertips, kept touching the walls as though asking permission. Pip would have liked her. Pip, who was currently below with Sera, bleeding or not bleeding, alive or not alive, and whom I could not think about or I would stop walking.

At the mouth of the passages, where they opened onto the night air behind the palace kitchens, Odile caught my sleeve.

"You spoke to someone in there," she said. Not a question. Odile didn't ask questions; she issued conclusions and waited for you to confirm them. "Before we left the chamber. The Father bell changed pitch. I felt it in my teeth. What did you do?"

"I talked to the king."

She stared at me. Behind her, the rescued strays shuffled in the dark, and the city roared its endless bronze above us, and somewhere in the halls we'd left behind, Sera was still singing her own particular song of violence and retreat. "The king," Odile said, "is a hollow suit filled with hunger. I served that court for twenty years. I watched the change happen. There is nothing left inside."

"There's a man inside," I said. "Starved down to almost nothing. Speaking beast-speech so rusted he stumbles on his own childhood. Odile—he's been screaming into the bells for a year and nobody heard him because nobody alive speaks his language anymore except me." My voice cracked on the last word, and I hated it, and I let it crack anyway, because some things deserve cracking. "He told me how to kill the hunger. And you're not going to like it."

"Tell me anyway."

"The bells. All of them. The binding isn't in the crown—it's in the ring. Nine hundred and sixty-one bells, cast from his captivity, ringing his prison tighter every hour. To free him, the whole city has to go silent. On the thirteenth moon. While the hunger is weakest and the song is sung."

Odile was silent for a long moment. Above us, the bells rang on, indifferent, enormous.

"Do you know," she said at last, "what happens in Corvenna when a single tower misses its peal? Riots. Panic. The ringers' guild calls it the Silence-Sickness—the common folk believe the bells hold back the dark, literally, that the city exists inside the sound like a chick inside a shell. And you want to silence all of them. Simultaneously. In a city of two hundred thousand souls, most of whom would burn a beast-speaker for a copper reward."

"Yes."

"And the plan for accomplishing this impossible thing is?"

"That's the part," I admitted, "where I was rather hoping you'd come in."

She laughed—one short, hard bark with no joy in it, the laugh of a woman recalculating every number of her life. "The bell-works," she said. "The mechanisms. Clappers, wheels, the great driving shafts. A bell doesn't ring itself; something strikes it. If the guild seized the towers—if we jammed the works, broke the escapements, tied the clappers still—we could silence the city by hand. It would need every ringer sympathetic to our cause, coordinated to the minute, on the very night the hunger will be fighting hardest to keep its song alive." She rubbed her eyes. "It would need Elara Vance holding the guard. It would need the animals. It would need—" She stopped, looked at me properly, and something in her face shifted from contempt to something older and sadder. "It would need you, standing in the throne room, singing a dead king free while every soul in Corvenna holds its breath in the first silence this city has known since the hunger put its crown on. Do you understand what you're proposing? If the bells fall silent and the hunger survives, it will know exactly who did it. There will be no valley deep enough."

"I spent twenty-three years in hiding," I said. "I'm done with deep valleys."

* * *

We found Sera at the rendezvous—a burnt granary three streets from the palace, its roof open to the sky like a broken jaw. She was leaning against a beam with her sword across her knees and blood on her collar that I prayed wasn't hers, and when she saw me she stood up too fast, caught herself, and pretended she hadn't.

"Well?" she said. "Did you get the mage?"

"We got the mage. We got seven." I crossed the granary floor and took her hands, because I'd earned the right somewhere on the road and because my own were shaking. "Sera. Everything changes tonight. Everything we planned—it's wrong. Or rather, it's bigger than wrong."

She listened. She was good at listening; it was one of the things I loved about her, the way a soldier listens—not just to the words but to what's underneath them, testing each plank before she puts her weight on it. I told her about the voice in the bells. About Aldous, the boy who'd spoken beast-speech until the crown starved it out of him. About the nine hundred and sixty-one bells cast from his captivity, the binding turned inside out, the city's song as the hunger's anchor.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long time. Outside, the bells rang on, and I watched her flinch at them—really flinch, for the first time, hearing them not as background but as the sound of a man being buried alive in bronze.

"A year," she said finally. "A year I served the thing that did that. I trained its hunters. I wrote its patrol rotations." Her jaw worked. "I refused to burn one child and I told myself that bought back something. That I'd washed my hands." She looked up at me, and her eyes were bright and furious and terrified, all three at once, which is the most honest expression a soldier ever wears. "The whole city, Rafael. Silent. On the night of the thirteenth moon. You know what we're asking? We're asking two hundred thousand people to spend one night inside the hunger's silence, trusting that what comes after is better. And half of them will fight us. And the other half will panic."

"Then we'd better start recruiting the other half."

Something happened to her face then—not a smile exactly. More like a door opening in a wall she'd built a long time ago. She squeezed my hands once, hard, and let go, and became again the knight, the planner, the woman who turned despair into logistics.

"All right," she said. "Elara holds the guard—that's already sworn. Odile takes the ringers' guild; she'll need names, and I'll get them from Elara's records. Quill rallies the animals—every dog, rat, and raven in Corvenna, and gods help anything that stands between that fox and a mission. And you—" She pointed at me, and her finger was steady now. "You learn that song until it lives in your bones. Because on the thirteenth moon, in the silence, you'll be the only voice in Corvenna. The whole city will hear it, whether they understand it or not. The first true voice this kingdom has heard in a year."

Above us, the bells thundered on, burying a king in sound.

"Not for long," I said, to them, to him, to the whole ringing dark. "Hold on. We're coming. And this time, we're bringing quiet."

Somewhere high overhead, beneath the bronze, beneath the hunger's roar, I felt—or imagined I felt—an answer. Faint as a heartbeat through a wall.

*Hurry,* said the king.
