## 29. A Plan Made of Desperation

The granary smelled of char and old wheat, the way grief smells when it's been sitting in a room too long. Burnt beams leaned against each other like drunks holding one another up, and through the holes in the roof I could see the moon—two nights shy of her thirteenth turn, fat and pale and indifferent to what we intended to do beneath her.

We had no better place. The cellars were gone, betrayed and flooded with hunters; Odile was in a cage somewhere in the palace, being kept alive the way you keep a pig alive before a feast; and every safe house we knew of was either burned or watched. So we sat in the ruins of a granary on the edge of Corvenna's poorest quarter, on sacks that had half-melted into slag, and planned a revolution with four people, a fox, and a song none of us could finish.

I've laid siege plans on finer tables. I've never laid them with less hope.

"Say it again," Pip said. He was propped against a beam with his arm bound tight, the wound from the palace door still seeping through the linen despite everything Rafael's gift could spare for him. "All of it. Slowly. I want to hear how insane it sounds out loud."

Rafael looked at me. I nodded. Someone had to say it, and I'd spent twenty years saying terrible things to rooms full of armed people, so it might as well be me.

"Elara holds the guard," I said. "She has maybe forty soldiers she can trust not to ask questions—the ones whose brothers and sisters and children are gifted, or hidden, or simply decent. When the bells start ringing tomorrow night, she seals the palace gates from the inside. Not open rebellion—that would get her hanged by dawn. She just follows orders *slowly*. Confusing orders. Contradictory ones. A gate held shut because the paperwork isn't right. Hunters given directions to the wrong courtyard. She buys us time, hour by hour, lie by lie."

"And if she breaks?" Quill asked. He was curled on the highest intact beam, tail wrapped over his paws, looking like the only creature in Corvenna who'd slept recently.

"She won't," I said. "Her brother is under this city. She let me walk out of an archive she'd have died for defending. She's already broken, in the way that matters." I paused. "But if she's caught, she hangs alone, and she knows it, and she came anyway. Write that down in whatever ledger you keep."

Quill's ear twitched. He didn't write anything down. Foxes keep their ledgers elsewhere.

"Odile," I went on. "If we get her out—and we will, because Rafael won't hear otherwise and I've stopped wasting breath arguing—she goes to the bell-ringers' guild. Half of them hate Vhalor already; the bells have rung them deaf for a year, and ringers know better than anyone what those towers are for. She leads them tower by tower. Wedges, hammers, cut ropes, jammed wheels. They don't need to silence the whole city at once. They need silence spreading, quarter by quarter, while the moon turns. Every tower that falls quiet is a tooth pulled from the thing's mouth."

"The spirit will feel each one," Rafael said quietly.

"Yes."

"It'll send everything it has."

"It will," I agreed. "That's why Elara's part comes first, and why yours—" I turned to Quill— "is the maddest of all."

He preened, which for a fox is a genuinely unsettling sight. "Go on."

"Every animal in Corvenna. Dogs, rats, ravens, cats, the horses in the guard stables, the pigeons roosting on the cathedral. You told us they can hear Rafael. Tomorrow night, they don't just listen—they carry. The binding song needs voices, and Rafael can't sing it alone; we learned that in the tower. So when the moment comes, you gather them. Thousands of throats. None of them human. All of them singing in a language the crown spent a year trying to drown."

* * *

Quill was quiet for a long moment, which from Quill is like another man weeping.

"You understand what you're asking," he said finally. "Not the gathering. That's easy—Corvenna's animals are angrier than any army I've ever scouted for. It's after. If this works, the binding dissolves. My binding." His tail flicked once. "I was bound to the royal line before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. When the song finishes, whatever holds me to this shape, this speech, this—*purpose*—goes with it. I may be an ordinary fox by morning. Or nothing at all."

Nobody spoke. Pip's hand found his sling and gripped it.

"Then you shouldn't come," Rafael said softly. "You've done enough. More than enough."

"I didn't say I wouldn't come," Quill snapped. "I said you should understand the price. There's a difference between sacrifice and ignorance, and I refuse to die ignorant—it's undignified." He licked a paw with tremendous dignity. "Besides, someone has to show the rats which end of the song they're singing. They're enthusiastic but pitchy."

A laugh went around the ruined granary, small and startled, the kind of laugh people make when the alternative is screaming. I let it live for three seconds, then killed it, because that was my job.

"Rafael sings in the throne room as the moon completes its turn," I said. "That's the spine of it. Everything else—Elara, Odile, the animals, all of it—is scaffolding around one person standing in front of a hungry god and opening their mouth."

"And me?" Pip asked.

I'd hoped he wouldn't. He always did.

"You stay here."

"No."

"Pip—"

"No." He stood up, swaying, and his voice cracked but held. "You all keep deciding things for me. Sera, you taught me the knife work. Rafael, you taught me to listen. I'm not a stray you rescued anymore. I'm company." He looked around the circle, daring us. "I'll hold doors. I'll run messages between Elara and the guild. I'm small and fast and nobody looks at boys with bandaged arms. Use me."

I wanted to tell him no. I wanted it so badly my teeth ached. But I remembered being fifteen and being told I was too young to matter, and I remembered what it did to me, and I looked at this boy who'd bled holding a door so we could climb, and I knew that sending him away would break something in him that no safety would mend.

"Messages only," I said. "Between Elara's post and the guild towers. You run, you don't fight, and if a hunter so much as looks at you sideways, you vanish. Swear it."

"I swear," he said, too fast.

"Pip."

"*I swear.*" Slower this time. Whether he meant it was a debt I'd collect later or never collect at all.

* * *

When the plan was said, we did the thing soldiers do and civilians don't: we counted our odds out loud.

"One chance in ten," I said. "Maybe less. Elara's timing has to hold, the guild has to move fast enough, the animals have to arrive, and Rafael has to sing a song they've known for three weeks against a spirit that eats souls. Any link fails, we all hang."

"That's generous," Odile would have said, if Odile had been there. "I'd have said one in twenty."

"Then why are we doing it?" Pip asked. Not bitter. Genuinely asking, the way children ask, which is the only honest way.

Rafael answered before I could, and I was glad, because my answer would have been about duty and theirs was better.

"Because the odds don't change whether we go or not," they said. "Vhalor feeds either way. The hunt continues either way. Aldous drowns either way. The one-in-ten isn't the chance of winning. It's the chance of *anything* being different afterward. Staying here is also a choice. It just doesn't look like one."

I looked at them—at this goat-herder from a magic-dead valley who'd walked into my life bleeding other people's wounds and refusing to leave—and felt the thing I'd been carrying since the ditch where we met shift its weight again. Love, if I named it. I'd named it two nights ago, in the dark, badly, and they'd named it back, worse, and we hadn't had time since to be anything but terrified together.

"Courage isn't feeling brave," I told Pip, because he was watching me and waiting. "It's arithmetic done in the dark. You count what it costs, you count what it buys, and then you stand up anyway. Everyone in this room has already done the standing-up part. The rest is just tomorrow."

* * *

We slept in shifts, though sleep is a generous word for it. I took first watch at the granary's broken door, sword across my knees, watching the city burn its thousand lights against the dark. Somewhere in there, Elara was lying beside her husband pretending to snore. Somewhere, Odile sat in a cell counting the hours until we came or didn't. Somewhere, a king who didn't know he was a king screamed inside a cage of bells.

Quill came and sat beside me without being asked. For a while neither of us spoke. That's the trouble with animals—you can't lie to them, and they can't lie to you, and so silence is the only conversation left when the truth is too heavy.

"You loved him," I said at last. "The king. Before."

"I was a young fox and he was a lonely boy in a palace full of flatterers," Quill said. "We understood each other. Then I got old and slow and clever instead of wise, and I was scouting the marshes the season the crown came down on his head, and when I got back there was something wearing my friend's smile and calling it mercy." His ears flattened. "Yes. I loved him. Why do you ask?"

"Because tomorrow you might get him back, or you might lose the last piece of him you have left, and either way you're walking in first." I glanced at him. "That's not a soldier's courage. That's something older."

"Don't sentimentalize me, knight. I bite." But he leaned, very slightly, until his shoulder touched my knee, and stayed there until my watch ended.

Before dawn, Rafael woke and came to the doorway, and the three of us watched the sky go grey over a city that had forgotten the sound of its own name. Rafael's hand found mine. Their palm was cold. Mine wasn't much warmer.

"When the moon turns," they said quietly, "if the song starts taking me—like it took the last beast-speaker—don't let me become it. Whatever I say, whatever I promise, whatever I look like. Don't let me."

"I know," I said.

"Swear it."

"I swear."

They nodded, satisfied, and went back inside to wake Pip, and I stood in the cold and did the arithmetic one more time, honestly, the way I'd taught the boy to do it.

One chance in ten. Four people, a fox, a song, a city of enemies.

And then I turned back toward the granary, toward the fragile, stubborn family we'd built out of exiles and strays, and started sharpening the plan's dull edges, because courage is a decision made with full knowledge of the price—

and the price, I decided, was acceptable.

Tomorrow night, the moon turns.

And we would be ready—or we would be a story someone else had to finish.
