## 30. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

### Sera

I had stood watch a thousand nights in my life, and I had never once been afraid of the quiet.

Tonight I was afraid of it. The whole city held its breath like a man pretending to sleep while burglars move through his house. Corvenna's streets were empty by decree of no one—Vhalor hadn't posted curfew orders, hadn't needed to. A year of bells and purges had taught every soul in the lower city that after dark, indoors was the only honest place to be. And so the city lay dark and silent, and beneath that silence, in cellars and granaries and the bell-ringers' guildhall, it waited.

The thirteenth moon rose fat and white over the palace spires, and I thought: *come on, then.*

Elara stood beside me at the palace gates with forty guards she'd sworn were loyal and I privately estimated at thirty. She'd dressed them in plain mail without crest or color, which was either brilliant or damning depending on how the night went. She kept flexing her sword hand the way she used to before inspections, back when we were both young and stupid and the worst thing either of us feared was each other.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring at my guards like you're going to steal them."

"Only the good ones."

That got half a breath of a laugh out of her, which I counted as a victory, because her other hand hadn't stopped gripping the hilt since moonrise. "They'll come from the river side first," she said. "Vhalor's hunters like the water gate. It's dark, and the current covers sound."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I trained them." Her jaw tightened. "Because for six years I taught men and women how to slip into places quietly and drag people out of them, and now I get to stand on the other side of the lesson. There's a poetry to it. I hate poetry."

Somewhere across the city, the great bell of the Cathedral of Saint Aldous—the first Aldous, the real one, dead two hundred years and probably spinning in his grave—shivered once against its yoke. Not rung. Just shivered, the way a dog shivers before a storm.

Then it rang.

Not struck by any hand. It simply *rang*, one deep bronze note rolling out over the rooftops, and a heartbeat later every bell in Corvenna answered it—the cathedral, the watchtowers, the little chapel bells, the cracked old thing in Tanner's Row that hadn't rung clean in a decade. All of them at once, all of them unswung, all of them screaming the same note into the night.

*He knows,* I thought. *He's known all along, and he's telling us.*

"He knows," Elara said, and drew her sword.

The gates came under attack within minutes. They came exactly the way she'd said they would—from the river side, quiet shapes in the dark with hunter's badges and hunter's knives—and they found forty guards who had spent the last three days being told, in Elara's flat drill-sergeant voice, precisely what was coming and precisely where. It was not a battle. It was a very well-prepared argument, and the hunters lost it.

But the bells kept ringing. That was the thing. The fighting was noise and blood and close work, and underneath all of it the bells went on and on, that single grinding note, and I could feel it in my teeth, in my chest, trying to be the only sound in the world. Trying to drown everything else out.

Trying to drown *him* out.

"Hold the gates," I told Elara. "Whatever happens, whatever you hear—hold them until dawn."

"Where are you going?"

"To make sure a fox keeps an appointment."

### Odile

I had been the court mage of Corvenna for eleven years, and in all that time I had never once been inside the bell tower of the Cathedral of Saint Aldous. This is because the court mage's duties are largely ceremonial, largely decorative, and entirely above climbing ladders, whereas the bell tower of Saint Aldous has four hundred steps and a smell like old rope and pigeon regret.

Now I climbed those steps with nine members of the bell-ringers' guild, a satchel full of iron wedges, and the distinct sensation of a woman about to commit high treason against a god-shaped hunger wearing her former employer's face. My knees objected. My conscience did not. Twenty-three years of service to a crown, and it turns out treason feels a great deal like relief.

The guildmaster, a barrel-shaped woman named Hesk with forearms like ship cable, reached the belfry first and stopped so abruptly I walked into her back.

The bells were ringing themselves.

I don't mean swinging. I mean the great bell of Saint Aldous hung motionless in its frame, unmoved by wind or rope, and it *rang*, the bronze shuddering with a note that had no source, that came from inside the metal the way a scream comes from inside a throat. Around it the smaller bells answered, and the ringers' ropes hung slack and swaying, untouched.

"Well," said Hesk, in the tone of a woman who had rung bells for forty years and just learned they'd been humoring her the whole time. "That's rude."

"It gets ruder," I said. "Wedges. Now. The big one first."

Here is what nobody tells you about silencing a haunted bell: you cannot stop it with your hands. Tam the youngest ringer tried, pressed both palms flat against the bronze, and was thrown across the belfry hard enough to crack a beam. What stops a bell is physics. You wedge the clapper still, you jam iron into the yoke, you give the vibration nowhere to go—and if the vibration comes from somewhere else entirely, from something old and hungry pouring itself through the metal—

—you give it nowhere to go anyway, and you hope.

We worked in pairs, wedging and hammering while the note ground on around us, and I will confess that my hands shook for the first three bells and steadied after that, because there is a peculiar mercy in having a task. Fear is a luxury for people with idle hands.

The fourth bell fought us. When Hesk drove the wedge home, the note didn't stop—it *moved*, leapt out of the bronze and into the stone of the tower itself, and the walls began to hum, and every hair on my arms stood up, and in the humming I heard—not words, nothing so kind as words—a pressure, an attention, turning toward us like a head turning.

It knew where we were. It had always known. It simply hadn't bothered, until now.

"Faster," I said. "It's noticed us. When it finishes noticing, things will arrive."

"What things?" asked Tam, from the floor, holding his ribs.

"The kind of things the crown sends when it wants someone silenced," I said. "Which tonight is everyone. Wedges, children. Move."

We silenced five bells in that tower. Five out of eleven. And as the fifth note died, far below us, boots began to thunder up the stairwell—many boots, unhurried, certain, the tread of soldiers who have been told the building is already theirs.

Hesk looked at me. I looked at Hesk. Four hundred steps down, one narrow way, and between us and it, the vanguard of the Hunger Beneath.

"Guildmaster," I said, drawing the knife I'd sworn I'd never need again, "how are your stairs?"

### Rafael

Quill woke me by putting one paw on my cheek, claws sheathed, and saying, "Up. It's time. Also you're drooling on the song pages, which is either blasphemy or good luck, depending on the scholar."

I came awake with my heart already going. In the granary's dark, Pip sat up too, bandage fresh on his side, and Sera's bedroll was empty—she'd been gone an hour already, gone to hold the gates. Outside, the sky over Corvenna glowed faintly with the last light leaving, and the thirteenth moon was rising, huge and white and patient.

And then the bells began.

Every one of them at once. The sound came through the granary walls like water through a cracked hull, and I felt it hit the gift in me like a fist hitting a drumhead. For twenty-three years I had lived with animal voices in my head, a hundred small songs layered under the world, and the bells were built—I understood it now, understood it in my bones—to bury all of that. To drown the whispering. To drown *him*, the true king, screaming under a year of bronze.

"Rafael." Quill's muzzle was very close to my face. "Breathe. Doors. Close the doors."

I closed the doors. Most of them. Enough. Through the ones left open, thin as knife-cuts, I could feel the city's animals waking—not frightened, though the bells terrified them, but *waiting*. Dogs standing up in yards. Rats pausing mid-route along their walls. Ravens lifting off the cathedral roof in ones and twos, circling, silent. A tide deciding which way to run.

Or which way to rise.

"Pip," I said. "You don't have to—"

"Don't." He was already pulling his boots on, jaw set in a way that made him look suddenly older than his fourteen years. "Don't do the thing where you try to leave me somewhere safe. There's no somewhere safe. There's just here, and I'm coming." He paused, and his voice cracked, and he pushed through it. "You listened to me under a bridge when nobody else did. Let me carry the door."

There was no arguing with him. I've raised goats; I know when a creature has decided something.

We went out through the granary's back, into streets lit only by the moon, and the bells rolled over us the whole way, and the city rose around us without a sound. That was the strangest part—that a city could rise silently. But everywhere I looked, doors opened a crack and shut again; shadows moved along rooftop lines; and through my half-open doors I felt them gathering, hundreds of dogs flowing through alleys toward the palace district, rats pouring along the gutters like dark water running uphill, ravens thickening overhead until the stars went out in patches. No barking. No cawing. Nothing the hunters could hear and name.

Just a silent tide, moving on the Hunger Beneath's own city, on the night it had planned to feed.

Quill trotted at my heel, scarred ears forward, and I heard him murmuring as we ran—not to me. To them. Instructions in the beast-speech, that language of the body, the one I'd hidden my whole life and was about to sing in front of a kingdom: *the postern, the drain, the gap in the wall by the tannery. Hold the streets. Hold them till dawn.*

At the mouth of the servants' tunnel—the passage Elara had promised, the one that ran under the outer wall and up into the palace's bones—we stopped, all three of us, breathing hard in the shadow of the wall. Above us the palace loomed black against the moon, and somewhere inside it, behind all that stone, a tired kind king was drowning in his own body, and the thing wearing him was listening to its bells.

One of the bells, far off, went silent. Then another. Odile's guild, doing its work, tower by tower.

The remaining bells changed. The note bent, warped, went ragged with something that in anything else I'd have called panic—or fury.

"It feels them dying," Quill said softly. "Its anchors. Good. Angry things make mistakes." He looked up at me, and his eyes caught the moonlight, amber and ancient and more afraid than he would ever admit. "The song, Rafael. You'll have to start it alone. Before we're all in place. You know that."

I knew that. I'd known it since Greywater Fen, since the elder put the fragments in my hands like handing someone a live coal. The song had to begin while the moon turned, and the moon waited for no choir.

Pip pressed something into my hand. A button. His mother's, the one thing he'd carried out of his old life, polished smooth from worrying. "For luck," he said. "Bring it back."

The tunnel mouth gaped in front of us, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and old fires and, faintly, underneath everything, of hunger.

I closed my eyes. Opened every door in my mind, all at once, for the first time in twenty-three years—and the city poured in, ten thousand voices, none of them words yet, all of them waiting for mine.

I took a breath, and stepped down into the dark, and began to sing.
