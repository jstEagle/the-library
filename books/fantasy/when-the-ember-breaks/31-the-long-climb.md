## 31. The Long Climb

The animal passages of Corvenna Palace were not built for people. They were built, as far as I could tell, for cats with grudges and rats with somewhere to be, and the architect had clearly never imagined a grown human crawling through them on hands and knees while the bells of the city screamed overhead.

"Left at the rat-run," Quill said from ahead of me, his voice pitched low under the bell-noise that even here, in the stone guts of the palace, came through like a headache given architecture. "Then straight up until your shoulders regret everything."

"My shoulders regretted everything an hour ago," I said.

"Then you're on schedule."

Pip laughed behind me — a small, breathless sound, quickly swallowed. He'd been quiet since we'd left Sera at the junction below, since she'd turned down the wide corridor with her sword drawn and her old captain's stride, drawing Vhalor's hunters after her like a lure drawn through dark water. She hadn't said goodbye. She'd said *don't wait for me*, which in Sera's language was the same thing, and then she'd walked into the noise of the bells and the torchlight and been gone.

I tried not to think about it. I failed, thought about it anyway, and kept climbing.

The passage narrowed where it bent left, exactly as promised, and my shoulders filed their formal complaint. Above us, through gaps in the masonry no wider than a hand, the city poured its sound down like rain: bells upon bells upon bells, layer over layer, the thirteenth moon's eve ringing itself hoarse. Vhalor knew we were coming. Of course it knew. It had known since the archive, since the song pages vanished from its own locked shelves, since the night the whole lower city rang with its fury and I heard Aldous screaming inside the bronze.

*Not the crown,* he'd told me, in that voice like a candle guttering. *The bells. The bells are the binding turned inside out.*

So tonight we climbed toward the throne room while Odile's guild sabotaged the bell-works tower by tower, while Elara held the gates with whatever guards still remembered whose king they served, while every dog and raven and rat in Corvenna gathered in the dark at Quill's calling. And while all of that happened above and around us, three people crawled through a hole in the wall meant for cats.

There were worse plans. I couldn't name one at the moment, but I was confident they existed.

* * *

The rat-run opened into a vertical shaft, brick-lined, breathing cold air down at us like something curious. Rungs of iron studded the wall, rusted to the color of dried blood. Somewhere far above, a faint gray light marked our exit.

"Hold," Quill said, and went still in the way only a fox can go still — as if his bones had simply agreed to stop.

I listened. Not with my ears; with the other thing, the thing I'd spent twenty-three years pretending wasn't there. The shaft was empty of anything larger than mice, and the mice were saying nothing worth hearing except that it was cold and they wanted cheese, which felt honest enough.

"Clear," I said. "Mice only. They're complaining about the cheese situation."

"There's always a cheese situation," Pip said, and started up the rungs before either of us could tell him not to.

He was sixteen and made entirely of stubbornness and healing hands, and he climbed like a boy who'd learned on rooftops because the ground belonged to other people. His palms glowed faintly when he gripped the iron — not fire, never fire, just that soft gold warmth of his gift, the mending light. He'd offered, back in the granary, to mend the rusted rungs as we went. Sera had vetoed it. *No glow in the walls*, she'd said. *You shine, you die.* Pip had taken it without argument, which told me how much the last few days had changed him too.

We climbed. My arms burned. The bells hammered. And halfway up, Quill froze again, and this time what I heard through the stone made my stomach drop.

Voices. Human voices, below and behind, moving fast.

"The junction," I breathed. "They found Sera's trail."

"They found her trail because she *left* them a trail," Quill said, already descending past me with infuriating grace. "That was the point. But hunters are like water — they find every crack." His eyes caught the gray light, flat and bright. "One crack leads here."

* * *

They came up the shaft behind us faster than I liked and slower than I feared, which is the arithmetic of being hunted: you count seconds against footsteps and lose either way. We gained the top of the rungs and shouldered through a grate into a gallery — a narrow servants' corridor, high-walled, hung with dusty banners of some dead duke — and ran.

Or rather, I ran and dragged Pip by the wrist, because he'd stopped.

"No," he said. "Rafael. No. They'll follow us up. Someone has to hold the—"

"You're not holding anything, you're coming *with*—"

"There's a door." He pointed back the way we'd come, to where the gallery met the shaft head-on: a low wooden door, old, iron-banded, set into the wall beside the grate we'd just broken through. Beyond it, I could hear the hunters' boots on stone, and worse, beneath the boots, the thin wrong whine of hounds — not true hounds, nothing with a heartbeat that steady, things Vhalor wore the way it wore the king. "It opens onto the stairwell they're using. If I drop the bar—"

"If you drop the bar you're standing in front of it when they break it down!"

"I can mend wood, Rafael. I've been mending wood since I was nine." His chin came up, and for a second he looked so much like the boy under Ashford Bridge — filthy, terrified, absolutely immovable — that my chest hurt. "I'm the fastest mender you've ever met and you know it. Bar breaks, I mend it. Bar breaks again, I mend it again. You need maybe ten minutes to reach the throne room. I can give you ten minutes."

Quill had gone very still by the wall, ears flat. "He's right about the door," the fox said quietly. "And he's right that we don't have time to argue. The moon doesn't wait for family meetings."

"Then you stay with him," I snapped at Quill.

"I can't." Flat. Final. Grief under it. "The animals answer *me* tonight, Rafael. Every ear in this city is tuned to my call. If I stop singing, the tide stops rising, and Sera dies in those halls alone. You know this."

I knew it. That was the worst part. I stood in a dusty gallery with the bells screaming through the stone and did the arithmetic I'd been dreading since the granary, since the plan, since the moment this stopped being a story about a king and became a story about everyone I loved standing in front of doors.

Ten minutes. A boy with golden hands. A door.

"Pip—"

"Go." He was already moving, dragging the bar across, and his smile was the crooked one, the one that meant he'd decided something and the world could file complaints later. "Hey. Beast-speaker. When this is done—" the bar settled, his palms flared gold against the iron, "—you owe me a goat. A whole one. Roasted."

"I'll owe you two."

"Deal." The first blow landed on the far side of the door, and the wood jumped in its frame, and Pip pressed both hands flat against it and *sang* — not with his throat, with his gift, that low warm hum of making-things-whole — and the splintering blows began to heal as fast as they landed.

I ran.

* * *

I want to say I didn't look back. I looked back. Twice. Both times I saw the same thing: a small figure silhouetted against a shuddering door, hands glowing like a hearth, holding the line the way Sera would have — feet planted, jaw set, refusing the ground an inch.

The third time I looked back, the passage had curved and there was nothing to see, and the not-seeing was worse than any of it.

Quill ran ahead of me through the dark, a red streak in the torchless corridors, calling directions in that clipped way he had. "Stairwell. Then the peregrine gallery — the birds nested in the rafters for a century, there are ways. Then the last climb." He glanced back, and even winded, even running, his voice carried the weight of everything unsaid. "He'll hold. Menders always hold. It's the ones who mend others who forget to leave themselves a way out."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to be *true*. Comfort is above my station."

The stairwell spat us out onto a landing where the bell-noise hit like a physical thing — the great bells of the keep directly overhead now, and Vhalor's fury in every stroke. I staggered. My gift, muffled for months in this city, writhed under the bronze thunder like a fish on a hook. Through the din I caught fragments: Elara's guards clashing steel at the gates, far off; the bell-works groaning as Odile's guild jammed another tower; and everywhere, everywhere, the tide of small voices Quill had raised — dogs flowing through alleys, ravens blackening the sky, rats pouring along the walls like a second river — all of it converging on the palace, all of it singing the song's first phrases in a thousand throats.

And underneath, faint as a pulse, the true king's voice. Still screaming. Still drowning.

*Hold on,* I told him, with everything I had, knowing he might not hear. *Hold on. I'm coming.*

* * *

The peregrine gallery was a horror and a mercy. A mercy because it bypassed three guarded floors. A horror because it was a rafter-walk forty feet above the throne room's antechamber, planked, swaying, open on both sides to a long fall, and the falcons who'd built the original paths had opinions about visitors.

"One of them remembers me," Quill said, as a shape dropped out of the dark and screamed past my ear close enough to part my hair. "That's Biter. She's pleased to see me. That's how she says it."

"She has *teeth*—"

"She's expressing joy! Show some gratitude!"

We crossed the walkway in a storm of wings and insults, and came at last to the final climb: a chimney of stone behind the throne room's eastern wall, rising straight up into darkness, with a hairline seam of moonlight at the top. The thirteenth moon, nearly full, nearly turned, waiting for us like a held breath.

I put my hands on the first hold. And then the sound came.

It came up through the stone, through my palms, through the place where my gift lived — a sound I knew, a sound I'd taught myself to hear across a valley and a kingdom. Wood breaking. Iron giving. And beneath it, thin and getting thinner, a voice I'd have known anywhere, calling my name the way he'd called it from under a bridge.

*Rafael—*

The bar had failed. Or the door had. Or the hounds had found another way, or the hunters had flanked him, or ten minutes hadn't been ten minutes, because time never is when someone you love is holding a door.

My hands stopped moving on the stone.

"Rafael." Quill's voice, sharp as winter. "Climb."

"He's hurt. Quill, I can *hear* it, he's bleeding, he's—"

"Climb."

"I can heal him." The words came out of me before I could weigh them, hot and desperate. "My gift — the song needs beast-speech, needs everything I have, but if I turn back, if I go to him, I can pour what's left into the wound, I can—"

"And then?" Quill turned on the stone below me, and in the moon-seam light his scarred face was terrible and gentle at once. "Then you stand in the throne room empty. Then Vhalor looks at what's left of you and finds a cup with nothing in it. Then the king dies, the hunt goes on, and Pip bled for a door that bought nothing." His voice cracked, just once, just slightly. "I watched a good man be eaten because everyone who could have helped him arrived too late or too empty. Do not make me watch it twice."

Above us, the moonlight widened. Below us, fainter now, Pip's voice had stopped calling my name and started saying something else, over and over, in the tone of someone who knows exactly what he's asking:

*Run. Run. Run.*

He'd told me this would happen. Back in the granary, when Sera laid out the odds, Pip had said it plainly, sitting with his bandaged hands in his lap: *If it comes down to me or the song, you pick the song. Promise me. Because if you pick me and fail, then I held that door for nothing, and I'd rather it bought the world.*

I had promised. I'd promised the way you promise children things you hope never come due.

The choice stood in front of me now, in the dark, wearing a sixteen-year-old's voice. Stop and heal him with the last of what I had — and arrive at the throne room hollow, a cup with nothing in it, a song half-sung, everything lost. Or climb, and let him bleed, and carry the sound of it up every remaining foot of stone.

There was no third option. I looked for one. I tore the dark apart looking for one. There was none. This is what they don't tell you about the cruel choices: they aren't cruel because they're hard to see through. They're cruel because they're perfectly clear.

*I'm sorry,* I said, down through the stone, with everything in me, in the language of beasts that needs no throat. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm coming back for you, I swear it on the ewe, I swear it on the valley, I swear it on every morning after this one — live, Pip. Live, and I'll owe you the goats.*

For a moment — one moment — I felt him hear me. Felt the faint gold flicker of him, hurt and furious and laughing all at once, answering in the same tongue: *Two goats. Whole ones.*

Then I climbed.

I climbed with his voice in my ears and my gift burning unspent in my chest like a coal I refused to spend, and Quill flowed up the stone beside me, and the moonlight opened above us like a door, and the last thing the dark gave me before it let me go was the sound of the bells beginning, one by one, tower by tower, to fail.

Odile's guild was working. The binding was loosening. The throne room waited.

And somewhere below, a boy with golden hands held a broken door against the hunger of a god, and trusted me to be worth it.

I intended to be. Whatever it cost.

I pushed through the seam of moonlight into the throne room's shadow — and stopped, because the Hollow King was already waiting, seated on his throne in the silver dark, and he was smiling at me like he'd heard every word.
