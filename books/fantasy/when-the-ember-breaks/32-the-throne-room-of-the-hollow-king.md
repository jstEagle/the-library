## 32. The Throne Room of the Hollow King

The doors opened the way I had always imagined a king's doors would open: slowly, with ceremony, with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

What I had never imagined was that I would be the one walking through them bleeding soot and pigeon feathers, with a fox at my heel and my heart going like a drum in a parade I hadn't agreed to march in.

The throne room of Corvenna was long enough to have its own weather. Banners hung from rafters lost in shadow, and between them hung the bells—smaller ones, silver-throated, swaying on chains from the ceiling beams like strange fruit. Even here, even now, they rang. A low, constant hum, felt in the teeth more than heard. Quill's ears flattened against his skull.

*It knows we're coming,* he said. *It's singing to itself.*

*Then it's about to learn what happens when you sing at me,* I said, which sounded much braver in beast-speech than it felt in my chest.

The hall was empty. That was the worst part. I had expected guards, hunters, Elara's people fighting at the gates below—but Vhalor had cleared the stage. It wanted an audience of one.

At the far end, beneath a window of colored glass that turned the moonlight into a pool of drowned jewels, sat the throne. And in the throne sat the king.

King Aldous had been described to me a hundred times on the road: a broad man gone soft, a kind face gone tired, hands that had once been famous for gentleness. The thing wearing him had kept all of it. That was the horror. It looked *exactly* like the paintings. It looked like every coin in the realm. It sat with its hands folded on its knees, patient as a stone, and when it saw me it smiled—and the smile was warm, and tired, and human, and utterly wrong, the way a painted door is wrong when you walk into it and find brick.

"Rafael Almeida," it said. My name in a king's mouth. "Of the Ember Valley. Keeper of goats. Speaker to sheep." The smile deepened. "I've so looked forward to meeting you."

I stopped halfway down the hall, because stopping felt like the last free decision I would get to make tonight. Quill pressed against my ankle, a small warm weight, a reminder that I was not alone even when the architecture said otherwise.

"You know my name," I said.

"I know everything about you." The king rose. It moved well—too well, smooth as poured honey, no stiffness anywhere, as if it had practiced being a body for a year and finally gotten good at it. "I know you talked a lame ewe through her worst winter. I know you cried over a merchant's horse and lied to a census man and ran from the only home you ever loved. I know you freed a songbird in Thornhollow because you couldn't bear its begging." It began to walk toward me, unhurried, robes whispering. "I know what it cost you, Rafael. Every single day of twenty-three years. I know because I have spent a century knowing what that costs."

"Stay where you are," Sera's voice said in my memory, sharp as a whetstone. *Don't let it talk. Talking is how it hunts.* But there was no one else in the room, and the distance between us was shrinking whether I walked or not, and so I did the only thing left: I listened.

"I could show you," the king said softly, and raised one hand.

The world changed.

* * *

I was standing in the Ember Valley.

Not remembering it—standing in it. The morning fog lay along the river like a cat that had decided to stay. The valley stones stood grey and patient at the border, and beyond them the world went quiet in that particular way I had spent my whole life mistaking for peace. My goats were on the hillside. Marta's chimney smoked. And there, by the fence, lame leg tucked up, chewing with the air of someone who has opinions and sees no reason to share them, stood Clover.

My ewe. My stupid, stubborn, wonderful ewe, who had once told me—in the flat, unimpressed way of sheep—that if I was going to sit in the rain feeling sorry for myself, the least I could do was stand where she could lean on me.

"Clover," I said, and my voice broke on it, and I didn't care.

She looked up. She spoke, and it was her, it was *her*, the dry warmth of her, the exact texture of her mind: *You're late. You're always late lately. The grass doesn't wait and neither do I.*

"This isn't real," I said. To the valley, to the sky, to whatever was watching me watch this.

*Isn't it?* said the voice behind me—not Clover's voice, the other one, the honeyed one. *Feel it. The fog smells right. The stones are cold. This is your valley, Rafael, exactly as it was the morning before the crier came. I can give it back to you.*

And oh, it could. That was the terrible thing. It wasn't a lie dressed up as a dream; it was a *memory* dressed up as a promise, rendered so perfectly that my feet knew the mud, my lungs knew the damp. Somewhere under my ribs, the homesickness I had carried for four months like a stone in my boot turned over and wept.

"You burned the fen," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Greywater Fen. Old women and children. You burned it."

"The hunt was not mine," the king said gently. "The hunt was fear. Fear belongs to men; I merely harvest what they plant. But hear me—" and it moved around me, circling slow, and the valley turned with it like a scene on a wheel, "—hear what I am actually offering. Not fire. Never again fire. A kingdom where the gifted need not hide. Where a child born with fire in her palms is a wonder, not a warrant. Where you, Rafael Almeida, walk down the street of any town in this realm and speak to any animal you please, and the crowd *applauds*."

The valley shimmered and became a city square—I didn't know which one, some place bright and clean—and I was in it, and people were smiling at me, and a hawk landed on my shoulder like it had known me all its life, and someone in the crowd said *there he is, that's the King's Speaker*, and the words went through me like warm wine.

*I could make you beloved,* Vhalor said. *I could make you safe. I could give you back every morning you lost to hiding. All I ask—*

"All you ask," I said.

"—is that you stop fighting me. That is all. Stand aside. Let the moon turn. Sing nothing. And live the life you were owed." The king's face, Aldous's face, leaned close, and behind the kindness in it something vast and starving shifted like a whale under ice. "You never wanted a crown, Rafael. I know. Crowns are heavy and I should know better than anyone. You wanted a home. I am offering you the largest home ever built."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't look like a devil. It looks like relief.

I stood in a false valley holding a true grief, and every part of me that had been afraid since the night Marta's son was dragged screaming from his mother's house—the parts of me that flinched at checkpoints, that rehearsed lies, that woke at night listening for hounds—all of those parts reached toward the offer like frost reaching toward sun. Say yes. Stop running. Go home. Be loved out loud, for once, for the rest of your life.

Quill's tail brushed my ankle. Once. Deliberate.

And I thought about what the fen elder had told me, in the marsh reeds, with her true-dream eyes: *It was bound by a beast-speaker long ago. It will offer you everything, child, because everything is what it took from someone else.* And I thought about what I'd seen in the bell tower three nights ago, the prisoners in their cages, waiting to be eaten, each of them somebody's Pip, somebody's Marta's son. And I thought about the real king, buried alive inside his own body, screaming in the language of his childhood—a language he'd been taught as a boy, before the crown shut it away, which meant somewhere, once, there had been a king who let his children talk to animals. There had been a court where the gift was not a crime.

That kingdom had existed. It had just died. And this thing was wearing its corpse and selling me its ghost.

"No," I said.

The valley flickered.

"It's a lovely lie," I said, and my voice shook, and I let it shake, because honesty was the only weapon I had brought that couldn't be taken from me. "The fog's right, the stones are cold, you got every detail. Except one." I looked at Clover—at the perfect, hollow copy of Clover—and my chest hurt so badly I could barely get the words out. "She'd never say the grass doesn't wait. She'd say the grass waits fine, and it's *me* who doesn't. You gave me her voice but you never understood her. Because understanding was never the point, was it? You don't want to give me a home. You want me to trade the only real one I've got for a painting of it."

The valley cracked down the middle like an egg.

For half a heartbeat I saw through the split—not the throne room, something older, something dark and endless and hungry, a hunger with a shape, a hunger that had once been small and caged and *afraid*—and then it sealed itself, and I was back in the hall of bells, and the king's face was still kind, and that was somehow worse.

"You refuse," it said. Not angry. Curious, almost. "After everything. After the valley, after the ewe, after the girl you love who will die on these stairs tonight beside you." It tilted its head, studying me the way I had once studied ants, before I learned they were people too. "Why?"

And there it was. The question underneath everything, the one the whole road had been asking me since the border stones, and I found—to my enormous surprise—that I knew the answer. I had known it since a cellar full of strays shared bread with a nervous priest. Since a boy with broken-knuckle hands had asked me if saving the king was worth it and I'd had no clean answer and he'd stayed anyway.

"Because you keep saying *kingdom*," I said. "And I keep hearing *home*. And they're not the same word. A kingdom is a thing you take. A home is a thing you build—with other people, out of truth, brick by brick, even when it costs you. You can't give me one. Nobody can. You can only help build one, or burn one down." I lifted my chin. "You burned yours down. I'm going to go build mine."

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Something old, and tired, and—for one unguarded instant—*sad*, the way only the truly damned can be sad. Then it was gone, folded away like a letter, and the smile returned, and the smile was hungry.

"So be it," said Vhalor, the Hunger Beneath, wearing the gentlest face in the realm. "Then we do this the old way. The way it was done to me."

The bells above us began to ring.

Not the low ceremonial hum—*all* of them, every silver throat in the ceiling, a wall of sound designed to drown speech, to drown thought, to drown the small clear voices of beasts and the smaller still voice of a man buried in his own body. The floor shuddered. The stained-glass window flexed like water. And the king sat back down upon his throne, folding his hands, settling in to watch me break.

I opened my mouth to sing, and the bells ate the first note whole.

Beside me, Quill stepped forward into the ringing dark, planted his paws, and threw back his head—and out of that scarred little body came a sound I had never heard him make, a call that was not a bark and not a word but an opening, a door thrown wide. And from somewhere below us—from the streets, the cellars, the rooftops, the granaries and gutters of Corvenna—something answered.

Thousands of somethings.

The song had begun.
