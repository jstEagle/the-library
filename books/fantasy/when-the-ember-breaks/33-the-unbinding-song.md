## 33. The Unbinding Song

The first thing you learn about singing with a thousand animals is that they do not wait for your cue.

I had spent four months learning the binding song the way you learn a language by drowning in it—fragment by fragment, Odile's cracked voice in a cellar, Quill's corrections sharp as thorns: *No, not there, you're shaping it like a human word, it isn't a word, it's a weight.* I had practiced until my ribs ached and my dreams were full of syllables that had no letters. And now, on the night of the thirteenth moon, with Vhalor wearing a king's body twenty feet away and every bell in Corvenna screaming, I opened my mouth to begin—and the rats of the lower city got there first.

It shouldn't have surprised me. Rats are terrible audiences and worse choirboys. They came in under the song like water under a door, a low chittering drone that was supposed to be my part, the root note, the foundation. Then the dogs took it up—somewhere below us in the great hall's throat I heard them, dozens of them, hounds and mongrels and one yapping lapdog who had no business being brave but was anyway. The pigeons arrived from the rafters in a gray rush of wings. Ravens on the broken windowsills. Cats, who had clearly negotiated their own terms and would be joining when they pleased.

And through all of it, threading them together like a hand through puppet-strings, Quill's voice—that impossible opening call, sustained far past what any fox's lungs should hold, because magic does not care about lungs.

*Good,* I thought wildly. *Excellent. Now what am I supposed to do?*

Because here is the thing nobody warns you about when they hand you a destiny: the binding song was never meant to be sung *at* something. It was meant to be sung *around* something—a circle, a cage, a shape with the singer standing at one point of it and everything else at the others. A beast-speaker alone could never finish it. That was the flaw in the old design, the reason the last singer died with the work half-done. The song needed a chorus. It had always needed a chorus. It had simply waited a hundred years for someone to gather one.

Vhalor understood this before I did.

---

The king moved.

Not fast—nothing Vhalor did was fast, that was the horror of it. Aldous's body crossed the throne room floor with the unhurried gait of a man walking to greet an old friend, robes whispering on stone, crown catching the red light of the burning city beyond the windows. His face was kind. That was the worst part, the part I would carry afterward like a stone in the chest: it was a tired, gentle, *reasonable* face, the face of a grandfather listening to a child explain why the goat ate the laundry.

"Rafael Almeida," he said, and his voice was warm and deep and wrong, wrong the way meat is wrong when it has gone off—you couldn't say what was missing, only that everything had. "Of the Ember Valley. Do you know how long I have watched the valley? How long I have stood at its border like a beggar at a locked gate, smelling what I could not reach?"

I kept singing. Or rather, I kept my mouth moving while the song poured through me, because by then it wasn't entirely mine—it was coming up through the floor, through the stones, through whatever thread connected me to every listening throat in Corvenna. My part was to be the still point. The hinge. The place where the circle closed.

"You think you have come to fight me," the king said, stopping an arm's length away. Close enough that I could see the fine lines around his eyes, the stubble on his jaw, the small scar on his chin that some real man had earned doing some real human thing. "You have come to feed me. All of you do, eventually. The gifted ones especially." He tilted his head, and behind those eyes something ancient leaned forward against the glass. "You cannot imagine what a century of starvation tastes like. Come. Let me show you what I can offer instead."

And he showed me.

---

It was not claws. It was not even touch. It was hunger reaching into me the way cold reaches into a wet coat—gradually, then all at once, then everywhere.

He showed me the valley. Not a memory—an offer. The ewe with the lame leg standing in the high pasture, her breath steaming in morning air, turning her head toward me with that look she had, the one that said *you again, good*. Marta's kitchen with bread rising under cloth. The valley stones warm in evening sun. My goats. My crooked fence. My whole small life laid out before me like a table set for one, safe inside the magic-dead border where nothing could ever follow, where I could grow old tending beasts and speaking to them softly and pretending, right up to the end, that half a life was a whole one.

*Stay,* said the hunger, wearing the valley's voice. *You never wanted any of this. Crowns, songs, dying kings. You wanted a fence and a flock and someone who knew your name. I can give you back the only home you ever had.*

And here is what I learned about hunger, in that moment, from the inside: it lies beautifully, but it lies *specifically*. It knows exactly which door in you is unlatched. Mine was the ache—the twenty-three years of it, the life half-lived, the neighbors who'd have burned me, the silence I'd kept while Marta's son was dragged away in chains. Vhalor poured itself into that ache like water into a cup, filling it, promising to fill it forever.

For one terrible heartbeat, I wanted it. I want to be honest about that, because everyone who tells this story later will want to make me a statue, and statues don't admit things. For one heartbeat I stood in the pasture with the ewe and felt the sun and thought: *yes, oh yes, take the song, take the king, take all of it, just let me go home.*

Then the ewe turned her head and spoke, and it wasn't Vhalor's voice at all.

*"You're late,"* she said, in her flat, chewing, eternally unimpressed way. *"The grass won't wait and neither will I."*

It was a memory. Just a memory. But memories made of love don't bend, even under hunger, and hers had never once asked me to be anything other than exactly what I was. Vhalor's counterfeit valley shimmered—and in the shimmer I saw the seams. The grass didn't move. The sun didn't warm. The ewe's eyes were painted on.

"You can't build a home," I said, around the song, around the fear. "You've never had one. That's all you are. An empty thing, so hungry you've forgotten hunger was ever for anything."

Something flickered across the king's borrowed face. Rage, maybe. Or grief. With Vhalor I would learn—they were the same expression.

*Then I will take your gift and wear IT as a home,* the hunger said, and stopped offering, and began to eat.

---

I will not describe it fully. Some things belong to me. But understand: it did not attack my body. It went for the gift itself—for the beast-speech, the listening, the thread that had run through my whole life like a silver wire. It found the place in me where the gift lived and clamped down and *pulled*, the way you'd pull a tooth, the way you'd pull a heart.

The song faltered. Below us, in the streets, I heard the dogs lose the melody, confused, casting about. The rats' drone stuttered. Somewhere a pigeon panicked into flight.

Quill's voice held. Barely. He was shaking—I could see it, the whole small scarred body trembling with effort, blood on his muzzle where he'd bitten his tongue—but he held the opening note like a man holding a door against a flood, and through gritted teeth he did the most Quill thing imaginable:

He insulted me.

*"Boy,"* he snarled, in beast-speech, in front of God and the hollow king and several thousand animals, *"if you let this overgrown appetite swallow you, I will haunt your grave personally, and I am VERY tedious, ask anyone—"*

And underneath the insult, wrapped inside it, the actual message, the one he'd been carrying since the border: *you are not alone, you have never been alone, sing.*

So I stopped trying to be the whole song.

That was the secret, and it had been the secret all along, and I had been too proud and too frightened to see it. Every time I'd practiced in the cellars I'd tried to carry the melody entire, solo, the lone hero, because that was the story I'd been told and the story I'd believed: the gifted one stands apart, hides, endures, saves the day alone or dies alone. Hiding was the only shape my gift had ever known.

But the gift had never been for hiding. It had been for *this*.

I let go of the melody and gave it away.

---

I reached out—not with my hands, with the listening, flung wide as I'd never dared fling it in the city of bells—and I handed the song to Corvenna.

To the dogs first, because dogs give everything away gladly: *here, this line is yours, carry it.* They took it up in full-throated chorus, and the melody steadied. To the rats, who had the patience for the long low drone beneath everything, the part nobody hears that holds the rest up. To the pigeons for the high keening descant, to the cats—who arrived precisely then, because cats—for the sharp grace notes that cut like knives. To the horses in the palace stables, stamping the rhythm. To the oxen and the mules and the half-starved stray mutt Odile fed behind the bakery, who had the honor of the final phrase and would carry it, I swear, like a knight carries a banner.

And to Quill, the conductor, the bridge, the fox who had walked out of a dead king's service and across a kingdom on three good legs and sheer stubbornness—Quill took the burden of holding us all together, thousands of scattered voices in a ringing stone city, and he did it without dropping a note.

The song became a thing with a thousand throats.

I felt it turn beneath me like a great wheel beginning to move—the binding song, the true one, complete for the first time in a century, sung not by one doomed hero but by an entire city's hidden population, every creature that had ever been kicked or caged or ignored, rising together in the dark. The bells above us shrieked louder, Vhalor throwing everything into the din—but the bells were only metal, and metal can be drowned. The song went through the bells. The song went through the walls. I heard it come back changed from the far side of the city, carried by voices I'd never touched, lamplighters' cats and dockyard rats and one enormous cathedral rook who had apparently been waiting his whole life for this.

The king staggered.

Aldous's body—one man's body, sixty years old, mortal—put out a hand and caught himself on the arm of the throne, and the sound he made was not a roar. It was thinner than that. It was the sound of a thing that has fed for a century suddenly finding the food fighting back, organized, *united*, and discovering that hunger, for all its teeth, has never once built anything.

*"What are you,"* Vhalor said, and for the first time the warmth was gone from the voice, and what remained sounded almost young. Almost lost. Almost like something that had once been small and caged itself.

I should have answered with triumph. Instead what came out of me, woven into the song, was the truth:

"I'm the valley's beast-speaker. I'm Sera's fool. I'm Pip's friend. I'm the chorus of Corvenna. I'm nobody alone—which is the one thing you've never been able to devour."

The wheel of the song turned faster. The circle was closing. I could feel the shape of it now, vast and bright, the same cage Vhalor had been bound in a hundred years ago rebuilt from living voices—and at its center the king's body shook, and the crown seemed to darken, and behind the king's eyes something enormous pressed its face against the glass of the world—

—and the glass began to crack.

But cracks, I was about to learn, open in both directions. Because as the song tightened and Vhalor thrashed against the closing circle, its hunger tore a hole in the space between us, and through that hole I fell—not with my body, which stayed kneeling on the throne room stones, but with the listening, the deepest part of me, the part the spirit had grabbed and refused to release.

I fell *into* Vhalor.

And what I saw there, in the dark at the bottom of a hundred years of hunger, was not a monster.

It was a beast-speaker, starving in a cage, singing to keep itself company, waiting for someone—anyone—to answer.

* * *

The song was still turning. The city was still singing. But somewhere very far below the music, in the dark where I now hung, something that had once been small and caged itself turned toward me with the king's stolen eyes and whispered, in a voice like my own:

*"Now you know. Now you must choose what I chose—or prove you are better than me, little singer, while I am still hungry enough to test you."*
