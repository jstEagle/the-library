## 34. What the Spirit Showed Me

Here is what nobody tells you about being devoured: it is not like being eaten. It is like being *read*.

The hunger went through me the way a clerk goes through a ledger—patiently, line by line, looking for the column where the numbers don't add up. It found the valley. It found the ewe with the lame leg whose name was really Blossom, though I'd never told a living soul that I'd named her. It found twenty-three years of lying by omission, every careful silence, every time I'd laughed at a joke about beast-speakers so no one would wonder why I laughed too easily.

And then, because hunger is greedy and greed is stupid, it kept reading past my defenses into the thing itself—the song—and for one terrible, wonderful moment, Vhalor and I were singing the same words from opposite sides of the same throat.

That's when it showed me.

I don't know if it meant to. I think hunger doesn't mean anything; it only takes, and in taking, it gives you everything it has ever swallowed. And Vhalor had been swallowing for a very long time.

* * *

I was standing in a courtyard I did not know, under a sun that was too white, in a body that was not mine.

A boy stood at a well, maybe ten years old, barefoot on stones worn smooth by centuries of buckets. He was talking to a crow. Not talking *at* it—talking *with*, the way I talked with Blossom, head cocked, listening as much as speaking. The crow said something that made him laugh, a bright unguarded sound, and I felt my own chest ache with recognition so sharp it was nearly pain.

*I know you,* I thought. *I am you.*

The boy's name—I learned it the way you learn things in dreams, without anyone saying them—was Vhalor.

He lived in a king's city, in the shadow of a palace, and he hid his gift the way I had hidden mine: badly, joyfully, in stolen moments with animals who asked nothing and told everything. He had a mother who sold fish. He had a friend, a girl with flour on her hands, who knew his secret and kept it because she loved him. He wanted, more than anything, to be let inside the palace—not for gold, not for title, but because he had heard the royal stables held a horse who could sing, an actual singing horse, and he wanted to meet her the way other boys wanted to meet knights.

He never met the horse.

What happened instead was this: the old king died, and the new king heard rumors. A fish-boy who spoke with crows. And the new king—kings are frightened creatures, always have been, always will be—decided that a gift he could not command was a knife pointed at his throat.

They came for Vhalor at night. They came for his mother too, though she had no gift at all, because soldiers are efficient and questions are expensive. The girl with flour on her hands watched from a window and did nothing, because she was eleven years old and terrified, and I do not blame her, and neither, in the end, did Vhalor, which somehow made everything worse.

They put him in a cage.

Not a cell—a cage. Iron bars, a stone floor, and around it, carved into every surface, binding-script so dense it looked like frost. Because the new king had a use for a beast-speaker after all. A gift that could call every animal in a hundred miles could find things. Could flush out other gifted souls hiding in the kingdom's corners. Could be a hound, if you starved it enough.

So they starved him.

I stood in that cage with him—I *was* in that cage with him—for what felt like lifetimes, because it was lifetimes. They fed him just enough to keep the gift working. They brought him animals in smaller cages and made him interrogate them, and when he refused, they hurt the animals instead, and so he learned that his voice was a weapon whether he wanted it to be or not. Every creature he called to him, he called to its ruin. Every word out of his mouth became someone's death warrant.

Do you understand what that does? Do you understand what it does to a boy who once laughed with a crow at a well?

His gift began to curdle. That's the only word for it. Beast-speech is a language of listening—you open doors in your mind, Quill had taught me, and you let the world walk through. But Vhalor's doors had been nailed shut from the outside for so long that when they finally broke, they broke inward, and everything that came through came through *hungry*. The animals stopped being neighbors and started being food. Not meat-food. Soul-food. The warmth in a sparrow's breast. The long slow patience of an ox. He drank them because drinking them was the only touch left to him, and touch withheld becomes appetite, and appetite fed becomes a bottomless well.

The king grew old. The king grew frightened of the thing in the cage, which no longer looked entirely like a boy. So the king did what kings do with problems they've created: he buried them. There was a ritual. There was a deep place beneath the palace. There was a binding sung by court mages who wept while they sang it, because even they understood they were not ending the horror, only postponing it, and passing the bill to their grandchildren.

Vhalor lay in the dark under the palace for a century. Then another. He counted the heartbeats of rats to stay sane, and then he stopped counting, and then he stopped being sane, and what was left down there in the dark was hunger wearing the memory of a boy like a coat two sizes too small.

And then, a year ago, a king named Aldous—who loved animals, who had been a gentle man, who kept a fox companion and spoke softly to his hounds—went looking in the deep places for old treasures. Kings do that. They go digging in the dark for power their grandfathers buried, and they never once ask themselves *why* it was buried.

The crown was already waiting. Vhalor had spent a century pushing his hunger up through the stone like roots through a coffin lid, and when Aldous lifted the crown onto his own head—

Well. You know how that story goes. You've seen the bells. You've seen the hunt. You've seen the harvest.

* * *

The vision released me—or rather, the song pulled me back, because the song was still going, still pouring out of me in a language older than throats, and somewhere far away Quill was screaming my name in the voice of a thousand animals.

But something had changed in me, and Vhalor felt it change, and the hunger hesitated.

Hesitation, from a thing like that, is an earthquake.

*You saw,* it said. Its voice was everywhere now, in the throne room's stones, in my bones, in the space between heartbeats. Not Aldous's tired kind face anymore—something vast and ragged, a shape made of every animal it had ever drunk, all of them screaming in chorus. *Now you understand. Now you know what they do to us. What they have ALWAYS done to us.*

"Yes," I said. Or sang. Or both. "I saw."

*Then stop,* it said, and here is the horrible thing—it sounded reasonable. It sounded like reason itself, worn smooth by centuries of solitude until every edge was logic. *Stop singing. Join me. We are the same, you and I. Two voices the world tried to silence. Two cages. I have simply been in mine longer. When I wear this kingdom, no beast-speaker will ever be caged again. No child will laugh with a crow and die for it. I will make a country where our kind is the crown and the crown is our kind, and every soldier who ever carried iron toward a gifted child will kneel in the ash of their own banners.*

And the worst part—the part I will carry to my grave—is that some part of me *wanted* it.

Not the hunger. Never the hunger. But the promise underneath it. I thought of Marta's son dragged into the night with fire in his palms. I thought of Pip bleeding in a doorway below me because the world had never once made room for what he was. I thought of Sera, stripped of everything for one refusal, and Odile in chains, and the fen burned black, and every bounty board in every village square with its neat rows of names.

Wouldn't a kingdom of our own be justice?

Wouldn't it be *fair*?

For one heartbeat—one long, ringing, thirteenth-moon heartbeat—I let myself imagine it. Me on a throne. The hunters hunted. The valley stones torn up and magic allowed to flood home. Blossom living in a palace stable, fat and adored, and every animal in the realm safe because I had made them safe, and everyone who had ever made me afraid made afraid instead.

It would have been so easy. All I had to do was stop singing.

*You see it,* Vhalor whispered, tender as a mother. *You see how right it would feel.*

"I see it," I said.

And I kept singing.

Because here is what I saw in the vision that Vhalor had not shown me on purpose—what leaked through anyway, the way grief leaks through walls. I saw the boy at the well. I saw him laughing. And I understood, finally, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Vhalor had made one mistake I must never make, one mistake that had turned a child into a famine:

He had believed the cage was the whole world.

He had looked at the bars for so long that when his gift finally broke free, it broke free *as a cage*—it took everything it touched and locked it inside itself. Hunger is just love with nowhere to go. Devouring is just belonging turned cannibal. He had wanted, his entire existence, exactly what I wanted—to be heard, to be held, to matter to something—and the world had answered by teaching him that the only way to hold a thing was to swallow it.

If I took his bargain, I would not be defeating him. I would be *continuing* him. A new cage, gilded, with me inside it and everyone else outside. The cycle doesn't care whose face it wears. Justice that runs on vengeance is just hunger with better manners.

"You were a boy," I sang to it, and the song bent around the words, made them true in the old language, made them land like hands on shoulders. "You talked to a crow. She told you a joke. You laughed."

The vast ragged shape of it *flinched*.

"Someone should have opened the door," I went on, and my voice cracked, and the crack went into the song and made it stronger, because beast-speech is honest or it is nothing. "No one did. That is the crime. That is the real crime, and it belongs to kings, dead ones, and I will not pay their debts by becoming them. I will not build my freedom on your hunger. I will not take a kingdom as loot."

*Then you will fail,* Vhalor snarled, and the tenderness dropped away like a mask, and the hunger came down on me like a collapsing roof. *Then you will die small, in a room full of stone, singing to no one, and I will drink what's left of you and wear your kindness like a fur.*

"Maybe," I said.

But I was smiling. I couldn't help it. Because I had heard something, underneath the roof-fall, underneath the rage—faint, furious, unmistakable.

Footsteps. Running ones. And beneath those, closer than breath, a red-fox heartbeat I would have known anywhere in the world, carrying with it—threaded through it, the way a chorus threads through a solo voice—every dog and rat and raven and sparrow in Corvenna, climbing, running, gathering, coming.

Vhalor had spent a century learning that the only way to hold a thing was to swallow it alone.

It had never once imagined that someone might simply *ask for help*.

The throne room doors burst open behind me, and I heard Sera Vane's boots on the stone, and I sang louder.
