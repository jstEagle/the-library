## 37. The King's Oath

I had been king for nineteen years before I learned what my own voice sounded like.

That is not a figure of speech, though it sounds like one. I mean it plainly. For a year of those nineteen, my voice was not mine at all—it belonged to something that wore me like a coat and spoke through my mouth with my accent and my cadence and none of my soul. And for the eighteen years before that, I had spoken so carefully, so diplomatically, so much the way kings are taught to speak, that I am not sure any of it was mine either.

So when I stood in the great hall of Corvenna on the first morning of my restoration—three days after a beast-speaker sang me out of my own skull, three days after a knight lifted the crown from my head with hands that shook only once—I did not begin with a proclamation. I began by clearing my throat like an ordinary man with a dry mouth, and I said:

"My name is Aldous. I have been gone for some time. I intend to tell you where."

The hall was not full. That was deliberate. Odile had advised me—she who had spent a year cataloguing the spirit's cruelties from exile—that a court learns nothing from spectacle except how to perform agreement. So instead of the full peerage I summoned forty people: the captains of the guard, including Elara Vance, who stood at parade rest as though she had never in her life committed treason, which she had, twice, gloriously; the masters of the guilds, including the bell-ringers, who looked as though they had not slept in a month, because they hadn't; two priests, one of them the nervous one from the cellars, who kept touching his collar as if checking whether it was still there; and a scattering of lords whose faces I had learned to read over nineteen years, faces that said *we will follow whoever is holding this room*.

And at the back, uninvited but unsurprising, sat a fox.

Quill had taken up residence on the windowsill like a gargoyle with opinions. No one had dared remove him. One of the lords—a fat, comfortable man named Harrow, whose loyalty I had never once doubted because he had never once displayed any—kept glancing at the fox and then at me, waiting for permission to be alarmed. I gave him none.

"I will tell you the truth in the order it happened," I said. "First: I died. Or nearly. A year ago, something entered me through the crown—an old thing, older than the crown, older than the kingdom—and it wore me. Every proclamation of the hunt, every bounty, every child dragged from a bed in the night—that was its voice, using mine. Second: it fed on the gifted. It gathered them the way a farmer gathers wheat, and it ate them to keep itself whole. Third: it is gone now. Not destroyed. Released. There is a difference, and I will explain the difference another day, when I understand it better myself."

A murmur went around the hall. Lord Harrow's hand drifted toward his sword, remembered that one does not draw steel on a fox's windowsill, and returned to the table.

"Fourth," I said, "and this is the part you will want to argue with: the hunt is ended. Today. Not wound down. Not reviewed. Ended. Every bounty rescinded, every warrant burned, every prisoner released. And further—" and here I made myself pause, because the next words were the ones Elara had demanded in the dark of a servants' passage while the city screamed above us, the words I had sworn with a beast-speaker's hand on my shoulder and a fox watching from the shadows, "—the gifted are hereby named under the protection of the crown and the law of the realm. Their gifts are not crimes. They never were. Any who raise hand or law against them answers to me."

The silence afterward had a texture. I have heard silences of fear, silences of boredom, the particular silence of men calculating. This one was all three braided together, and underneath them, faintly, something else—the sound of a room discovering that the floor might hold.

It was Lord Cassel who broke it, because of course it was. Cassel had chaired the council sessions during my absence—the *thing's* absence, I corrected myself, though the distinction grew harder every day—and he had signed, in my name, orders that would take him the rest of his life to live down. He was not evil. He was worse: he was reasonable.

"Your Majesty," he said, in the tone of a man approaching a horse he suspects may bite, "the realm has just endured a year of terror. The people were told the gifted were dangerous. They were told it by your voice. You cannot simply—"

"You are entirely correct," I said. "I cannot simply. Which is why I am doing it thoroughly." I let that sit. "Lord Cassel, you signed those proclamations because you believed they came from your king. I do not blame you. But consider what that means. It means the hunt was always possible. The voice was mine; only the hunger was borrowed. If we end the hunt and call it an aberration, we leave the machinery standing, oiled and waiting for the next hungry thing to climb into the next tired king. So no. We do not wind it down. We break it. We write into law what should never have needed writing: that a child born with fire in her palms is a child of this realm, full stop, and the full weight of the crown stands between her and anyone who says otherwise."

"Words in a charter," Cassel said quietly, "did not stop the hunters."

"No," said Elara Vance.

She had not spoken until then. She stepped forward now, and she carried a satchel, and I confess my stomach dropped, because I knew what was in it. She had told me. She had asked my leave to say it publicly, and I had given it, and knowing a thing is coming does not stop it from arriving like winter.

"But ledgers did," she said. "For the past year, Captain—my captain—kept records. Every denunciation, every payment, every prisoner, every transfer to the feeding. He kept them because he was proud of them. Because he thought he was building something." She set the satchel on the table and drew out ledgers, and the sound they made hitting wood was heavier than their weight. "These are in his hand. These are countersigned by three members of this council. And these"—her voice did not change at all, which is how I knew what it cost her—"are the names of four hundred and six people taken. Two hundred and eleven confirmed dead. The youngest was six."

She looked at Cassel, and then, deliberately, at each face in the room.

"The city remembers," she said. "The city watched the bells ring while their neighbors vanished. You can argue with a king. You cannot argue with four hundred and six names read aloud, which is what will happen in the square on Sunday if this council wavers. I have the list. I have memorized it. I will stand in the cold and read every name, and I will begin with my own brother's."

Nobody argued after that. There was arguing in the corridors afterward—I could hear it through the doors, the low urgent music of men rearranging their loyalties—but in the hall there was only work. Charters drafted. Warrants burned in a brazier while the nervous priest watched with an expression I can only describe as liturgical relief. Orders sent to every garrison in the realm: the hunt is ended, the gifted are free, here is the seal that proves it.

It took six hours. Nineteen years of kingship had taught me that most justice is paperwork done quickly by someone who means it.

* * *

Rafael came to see me on the fourth day.

They arrived without announcement—which is to say, Quill announced them by trotting into my study ahead of schedule, sitting down before the fire, and saying, "He's coming. Try to look regal. It won't help, but try."

I had wondered, in the strange quiet hours since my restoration, what I owed Rafael Almeida. Everything, obviously. My body, my mind, my name, my memory of my daughter's laugh—all of it pried loose from a thing that had swallowed them whole. But debt between a king and a commoner is a dangerous currency; it inflates into titles, and titles become chains, and I had spent a year watching what happens when power tries to repay what it cannot understand.

So I had prepared a speech. I want that recorded somewhere, for honesty's sake: I, Aldous, King of the Realm, sat up half the night composing a speech about gratitude and service and the realm's need, and Rafael dismantled it in under a minute without raising their voice.

They came in dusty from the road—they had delayed their departure east by four days, they told me later, specifically to see this done—and they stood in my study looking at the crown, which I had placed on a side table. Not worn. Placed. Like a tool set down mid-task.

"Your Majesty," they said, and started to bow, and stopped halfway, visibly deciding against it.

"Please don't," I said. "I've had enough of people bowing to the shape of me. Sit. There's wine."

"There's always wine," said Quill from the hearth. "Kings are mostly wine and paperwork. I've seen inside both."

I poured it anyway. Three cups, because I had learned that refusing the fox a cup only meant he drank from yours.

"I owe you a debt the treasury cannot express," I began, "and so I have prepared—"

"Don't," Rafael said. Gently. The way one stops a friend from stepping on ice. "Please. Whatever it is—a title, land, a place at court—I'll say no, and then we'll both feel awkward, and Quill will make it worse."

"I will make it *much* worse," Quill agreed. "I have material. I saw him eat a pigeon in the tower. Kings shouldn't know about that."

"*You* told me to eat it. You said it was traditional."

"It was traditional for *me*."

I set down the wine and laughed—actually laughed, the first time in over a year, and the sound startled me, rough and rusty as an unused hinge. Then I did the thing I should have done first, the thing beneath the speech, the true thing.

"Then tell me what you want," I said. "Anything within my power. Name it plainly, and I will not dress it up in ceremony."

Rafael looked at the fire for a long moment. When they spoke, it was quiet, and it was the plainest sentence ever spoken to a king.

"Write it down," they said. "Not for me. Write the protection into law so deep that no hungry thing can dig it out again. Make it boring. Make it permanent. Make it the kind of law that schoolchildren memorize and complain about, because it's always just been there. That's what I want. I don't want a title. I want it to be *ordinary*."

"And after?"

"After," Rafael said, and something eased in their shoulders, "I'm going home. To the valley. There are people there who knew me for twenty-three years and don't know me at all. I have to go stand in front of them and be true, and some of them will turn away, and I have to survive that." A pause. "Sera's coming with me. And Pip. We're going to build something at the valley's edge. A school, maybe. A place where the listening children can learn what they are before someone teaches them to be afraid of it."

I looked at this person—this goat-herding, ewe-talking, kingdom-saving person who had been offered everything a crown could give and had asked instead for a law and a home—and I felt the full weight of my own smallness, and it was, strangely, a comfortable weight. Like a coat that finally fits.

"Then I'll ask something of you," I said. "Not a debt. A request. When you build this school—when the frightened ones come, and they will come—teach them what I learned too late. That a gift is not a weapon and not a wound. It's a language. And languages exist to be spoken *to* someone."

Rafael smiled. "That's the first lesson," they said. "We'll carve it over the door."

Quill raised his cup. "To boring laws," he said, "and interesting people. May the realm always have more of the second than it deserves."

We drank to that. All three of us. And when Rafael rose to go—they had a long road east, and a truth waiting at the end of it—I walked them to the door myself, past the guards, past the bowing clerks, and at the threshold I did something no chronicler will believe.

I bowed. First. To a beast-speaker in road dust, in front of my entire court.

Quill trotted at Rafael's heel, and just before the door closed, the fox looked back at me with those amber eyes that had loved me across two lifetimes, and said, softly, so only I could hear:

"He'd have liked that, you know. The man you were before. He'd have liked you very much."

The door closed. I stood alone in my study with the crown on the table and the taste of wine and pigeon-feathers and grace, and outside, far to the east, a valley waited to learn the name of the person who saved it.

I picked up my pen. There was a law to write, and I intended to make it very, very boring indeed.
