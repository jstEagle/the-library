## 38. What Belonging Costs

The Ember Valley looked smaller from the ridge. That was the first cruelty of homecoming: not rejection, not torches, just geography reminding you that the place you'd built your whole life inside was a cupped hand of green between two walls of stone.

I stood there a long time with Sera's shoulder against mine and let it be small.

"You could wait until spring," she said. She'd said it four times since Greywater Fen, in various forms. *You could send a letter. You could send Quill. You could never go back at all, plenty of people live entire lives without confessing to their neighbors.* She was offering me exits the way she offered them to Pip before a hard ride — not because she expected me to take one, but because she wanted me to know they existed.

"I spent twenty-three years taking exits," I said. "I'm told I'm done."

Quill, sitting on my pack like a fox-shaped judgment, said nothing. He had been saying nothing about the valley for three days, which from Quill was practically a eulogy.

We came down through the shepherd's path because I wanted the valley to see us coming. No sneaking. If I had learned anything in Corvenna — in the throne room, under the thirteenth moon, with a dead king's voice in my throat and ten thousand animal voices carrying my song — it was that hiding is just a slow way of disappearing.

The border stones passed on either side, and the world went quiet.

That's the thing nobody tells you about the valley's silence. It isn't peace. It's a held breath. For twenty-three years I'd called it sanctuary, and it was, but it was also a kind of deafness I had mistaken for calm. Crossing back into it now felt like walking into a room where all the clocks had stopped. My gift didn't vanish — it never vanished, it was *me* — but it went muffled, distant, a voice calling from across a river. The ewe would hear me. Everything else would be guesswork.

"Gods," Sera muttered beside me. "It's like cotton in the ears." She wasn't gifted, but a year on the road will teach anyone to feel magic the way you feel weather. She flexed her sword-hand, uneasy. "How did you stand this?"

"It stood me," I said. "There's a difference."

Marta's farm was the first one on the road, which felt less like coincidence and more like the valley having opinions. Her gate was still broken where the hunters' wagon had gone through it a year ago. Nobody had fixed it. Some things a community leaves broken as a kind of accusation.

She was in the yard beating a rug, and when she saw me she dropped the rug entirely, which for Marta constituted open weeping.

"Rafael Almeida." She crossed the yard at speed and took my face in both floury hands like I was twelve years old and had fallen in a creek. "They said — the criers said you'd run. They said you were one of—"

"I know what they said." I gently removed her hands and kept them. "Marta. I need to talk to everyone. Tonight. At the chapel green, if people will come."

Her face did something complicated. Marta had always been quick; that was why the hunters had come for her boy and not her. "Everyone," she repeated. "And what will you tell them?"

"The truth."

"That's rarely what people want at gatherings," she said. But she was already looking past me, at Sera, at the scarred fox sitting on my pack with the composure of a minor noble, and whatever arithmetic she was doing behind her eyes came out in my favor. "I'll ring the bell."

*

They came. That surprised me more than anything else that day. Not all of them — maybe sixty of the valley's two hundred souls, standing awkwardly among the chapel gravestones as the light went amber, farmers holding their hats, the miller's wife holding her husband's arm like he might bolt. Old Tomas the smith stood at the very back with his arms folded, which I noted and filed away. The crier who had nailed up the proclamation was not among them. Neither were perhaps half the faces I'd known my whole life, and their absence was its own message, delivered clearly and without the bother of words.

Sera stood at my left in her old knight's coat, stripped of insignia but unmistakable. Quill sat on the low wall like he owned the chapel and possibly the god inside it. I had asked him to stay out of sight. He had declined with a look that suggested I'd confused him with someone who took instruction.

I had rehearsed a speech on the road. Standing there, I threw it away.

"My name is Rafael Almeida," I said. "Most of you have known me since I was born. You know I keep goats. You know my fences lean. What you don't know—" and here my voice did something traitorous, wobbled, and I gripped the graveyard wall and started over, "what you don't know is that I talk to animals. Not in the way shepherds pretend to. In the way you're talking to each other right now. Words. Meaning. They answer."

Silence. The particular silence of people deciding whether to be afraid.

"The king's hunt was for people like me. Is that what you're saying?" That was Tomas, from the back, arms still folded.

"Yes."

"And you stayed here. Among us. All this time."

"Twenty-three years."

"Why?" A woman near the front — I couldn't see who, the sun was behind the chapel — and it was a fair question, an honest question, and it deserved better than the speech I'd thrown away.

"Because I wanted to belong somewhere," I said. "And I thought belonging was something you could sneak into. Wear the right clothes, say the right things, never let them see the door in you. I thought if I was quiet enough and useful enough, the part of me that was different would stay small enough to hide forever." I looked down at my hands. Goat-keeper's hands. Beast-speaker's hands. Same hands. "It doesn't work. Hiding isn't belonging. It's just loneliness with good manners. And while I was being polite, Marta's son got taken off this green in a wagon, and I said nothing, because speaking might have cost me my hiding place. That's what my caution bought. I've come back to pay what I owe."

Nobody moved. Somewhere a rooster made an observation, and I couldn't understand a word of it, and grief for that small loss hit me sideways, absurdly, in the middle of everything.

Then Marta stepped forward. She had put on her good shawl, the one with the embroidery, the one she wore to weddings and funerals, and I understood that she considered this both.

"My Tam," she said, to the crowd, not to me, "was born with fire in his palms. Six years old. Six." Her voice cracked and steadied itself, the way voices do when they've been practicing. "And every soul standing here knew it, and every soul standing here said nothing either, so I'll thank no one to throw stones at Rafael Almeida tonight. We were all quiet. Quiet is how they took him."

Tomas unfolded his arms. Folded them again. "The boy's dead," he said flatly. "Everyone knows what happens to the ones they take."

"He's not."

Every head turned. I hadn't planned to say it yet. I'd planned to build up to it, ease them toward it, manage the moment — more hiding, even now, even here, dressed up as kindness. I was apparently going to spend the rest of my life catching myself at it.

"The crown fed on the gifted to keep its spirit alive," I said. "When the spirit unbound, everyone it held was released. The ones who died, died. The ones who didn't—" I took a breath. "Tam didn't. He's been in Corvenna since the moon turned. He's been asking after his mother. The king himself wrote to him. There's a pardon under law now, signed, sealed, read aloud in every market square whether the lords like it or not. Tam can come home."

Marta made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a word. Something older than words, the sound a hinge makes when it's been rusted shut for a year and finally gives.

"When?" she managed.

"A cart leaves Corvenna in nine days. He'll be here by month's end."

She sat down on a gravestone, abruptly, like her legs had resigned, and several women gathered around her, and the shape of the crowd changed — softened — the way a fist becomes a hand.

Not everyone. Let me be honest, since honesty was the entire evening's business. Old Tomas looked at me a long moment and then walked away, unhurried, and did not come back. The miller and his wife followed. Three families in all turned and went down the lane, and none of them shouted, and somehow that was worse than shouting. Shouting at least admits you're worth the breath. Their doors simply closed, quietly, the way doors do, and I understood that they would stay closed, and that this too was part of the price, and that no song, however many voices carried it, could sing a door open that had decided to be shut.

Sera's hand found mine at the edge of my sleeve. She didn't say anything. She had learned, over a long road, when I needed words and when I needed weight.

Quill, on his wall, addressed the remaining crowd in a clear, dry voice that made two children gasp and one woman drop her basket. "For the record," he said, "the fox also talks, the fox served the true king, and the fox would like it noted that he warned this valley about the census riders a full day before they arrived, and was pelted with a turnip. History should be accurate."

"We thought you were a rabid dog," someone said weakly.

"I am frequently mistaken for lesser animals," Quill said. "It builds character."

And then, because the evening had reached that fragile tipping point where it could become a wake or a festival, the baker's youngest daughter — bold as brass, seven years old, missing a tooth — marched up to the low wall, planted herself in front of Quill, and asked him if he knew any stories. And Quill, who has never once in his life declined an audience, began telling one, and the crowd stayed, and someone's mother sent for cider, and by full dark the chapel green had stopped being a confession and started being something I didn't have a name for yet.

I stood apart from it, the way I always had, and then caught myself doing it, and walked into the middle instead.

Belonging, it turns out, is not a thing you find. It's a thing you claim, out loud, knowing some of it won't hold. You pay for it in closed doors. You pay in full.

*

Nine days later, the cart came.

I heard it before the valley did — or rather, I felt it, a smudge of noise at the edge of my muffled gift, hooves and wheels and one small bright voice chattering at the driver. I was at Marta's gate before the cart cleared the bend, standing next to her, and her hands were shaking so badly she'd given up trying to fold them.

He was taller. Of course he was; a year will do that to a child, especially a year like his. His hair had been cut short — prison cut, grown out ragged — and there was a new carefulness in how he moved, the wariness of someone who'd learned that hands are dangerous things. But when he saw the gate, the broken gate, the crooked farm behind it, the mother in the good shawl running down the lane with no dignity whatsoever, the carefulness fell off him like a coat.

"Mam!"

What happened then belongs to them. I turned away, and found Sera beside me, and Quill on the fence post, and the three of us stood in the lane like a committee of gravediggers at a wedding, which is to say: necessary once, glad to be finished.

Later, Tam came to find me. He walked up with his fists in his pockets and studied his boots, and I waited, because I remembered being a child with a secret and knowing exactly how heavy the first sentence is.

"They said in the city you're the one who sang it down," he said finally. "The beast-speaker. They said the king calls you friend."

"The king calls me stubborn. Friend came later."

"Is it true? That it doesn't go away? The—" he opened his hands. Faint heat shimmered above his palms, like air over summer stone. He snapped them shut fast, guilty, trained.

I reached out and gently pried his fingers open, one by one, and held his small burning hands in mine. "It doesn't go away," I said. "And one day it's going to be the best thing about you. Not despite the fire. The fire itself."

His eyes filled and he blinked it away furiously, six years old and thirteen at once. "Can I—" He stopped. Started again. "The goats. Mam says you keep goats. Could I help? With them, sometimes?"

Something in my chest that had been clenched since the ridge, since before the ridge, since twenty-three years of clenched, let go about a quarter turn.

"Every morning," I said. "Bring your fire. The goats admire ambition."

*

I went up to see the ewe on the last evening.

She was in the far pasture, the high one, where the grass grows sweet and stupid, and she was lame in the same leg, because some things persist, and she lifted her head when I came through the gate and stared at me across forty yards of evening with the flat golden insolence only sheep can manage.

*I wondered when you'd get around to me,* she said. Her voice came thin through the valley's hush, a whisper across the river, but it was her, unmistakably her, the first voice I ever heard that wasn't human and the last one I'd feared losing.

"I had errands."

*Nineteen days.* Sheep count poorly, but they count grievances precisely. *Nineteen days in my own pasture and not a word.*

"I'm sorry."

*Hm.* She limped toward me, which she does for no one, and pushed her forehead against my chest, hard, the way she did when I was nine years old and crying behind the barn because I'd understood for the first time that I could never tell anyone what I was. *You smell like cities,* she complained. *And smoke. And a dog. Why do you smell like dog?*

"There's a dog coming. Long story. Also a boy with fire in his hands, a knight who snores, and a fox who believes he's royalty."

*The fox was here last week,* she said. *Came ahead of you. Sat on my wall and insulted my grazing.* She pulled back and looked at me with one rolling eye. *You're staying, then. After everything. Here.*

"Not here exactly." I looked west, toward the border stones, where the valley's silence met the world's roar, the seam between the two halves of my life. "At the edge. Where I can hear both."

*That sounds like you,* she said. *Never able to choose a pasture.* But she leaned into me again, and stayed leaned, and the sun went down over the valley that had made me and nearly unmade me and was learning, slowly, door by door, some opening and some closing, to know my name.

Behind us, up the lane, lamplight bloomed in Marta's kitchen window, and Tam's laugh carried thin and bright across the fields, and somewhere below the ridge Sera was arguing with Quill about whose turn it was to cook, and I stood in the middle of all of it, claimed and claiming, and thought: *this is what it costs, and this is what it buys, and I would pay it twice.*

Then the ewe bit my sleeve, because dinner was late, and some conversations, however profound, must eventually yield to a sheep's schedule.

Tomorrow we'd start building.
