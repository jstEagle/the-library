## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Hollow woke the way it always did on a birthday: slowly, grudgingly, and smelling of bread.

Vesper Crane knew this because she was already awake, had been since before dawn, lying in her narrow bed above Maren's shop listening to the valley perform its morning rituals. The rooster three doors down crowing off-key. Old Tam Wicker arguing with his goats in a voice that carried. The millwheel turning with its familiar wet creak, like an old man clearing his throat before telling the same story he'd told every morning for forty years.

Nineteen years of that creak. She could have timed her life by it.

She got up, dressed, and braided her hair while looking out at the mountains that ringed the valley like cupped hands — beautiful hands, protective hands, the kind that never let go. The peaks caught the first light and turned it gold, and Vesper thought, as she thought most mornings, that it was very hard to resent something so lovely.

She managed anyway. She'd had practice.

* * *

"Vesper! The feverfew won't weigh itself!"

"It weighs nothing, Maren, that's the entire point of feverfew!"

Maren Odile ran the only herb shop in the Hollow, which meant she ran the only medicine, which meant half the valley's health passed through her hands and the other half through her opinions. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a bundle of kindling wrapped in wool, with eyes that had missed exactly nothing in nineteen years of watching Vesper grow up.

Today those eyes were suspiciously festive. There was a honey cake hidden somewhere. There was always a honey cake hidden somewhere, and Vesper always found it within the hour, because Maren hid things the same way every year — behind the dried lavender, where no one who actually worked in an herb shop would ever look.

"The sign needs turning," Maren said, thrusting the wooden board at her. "And mind you don't scowl at Mrs. Petch when she comes for her joint salve. She'll buy less out of spite, and then she'll ache all week, and then I'll have to hear about both."

"I never scowl at Mrs. Petch."

"You scowl beautifully. It's practically a talent."

Vesper took the sign and stepped out into the street, and the valley received her the way it received everything: warmly, completely, and without one single surprise.

That was the thing about the Hollow that visitors — the rare peddler, the rarer pilgrim — never quite understood. It wasn't just pretty. It was *sealed*. Vesper had felt it all her life without having words for it, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. A hush under everything. The mountains didn't just hold the valley; they held something *out*. When she pressed her palm flat against the border stone at the western pass — a habit Maren pretended not to know about — she felt nothing, and the nothing had edges, and the edges hummed.

Magic couldn't cross into the Hollow. Everyone said it matter-of-factly, the way they said rain came from the west. The ward kept out curses, wild spirits, the hungry old things of the deep roads. It also kept out wonder, apparently, because in nineteen years Vesper had never once made a candle flame dance or heard a stone speak or done any of the small impossible things the peddlers described with wine-wet eyes.

Other people seemed fine with this. Tam Wicker thought magic was overrated and said so loudly. Mrs. Petch believed in it the way she believed in taxes — real, distant, and someone else's problem.

Vesper had spent nineteen years feeling like a word missing from a sentence.

* * *

The morning passed in the comfortable rhythm of work. She turned the sign, ground valerian, argued cheerfully with Mrs. Petch (who bought extra salve out of spite, which Vesper chose to count as a victory), and ate her birthday honey cake behind the lavender at midmorning, to Maren's theatrical despair.

"Nineteen," Maren said, watching her lick her fingers. "A grown woman. Legally accountable for her own scowling now."

"Mm." Vesper dusted crumbs from her skirt. "Maren. Can I ask you something I've asked you a hundred times?"

"If it's about your parents—"

"Why can't I do anything?" She kept her voice light, the way you keep a blade light so the cut doesn't show. "Outside the valley. The peddler last autumn swore he saw a woman light her hearth with a word. Children in the lowlands sing notes that make bread rise. And I've stood at the border stone until my hand went numb and felt *nothing*, Maren. Nothing at all. If it's just the ward, fine. But what if it isn't? What if it's me?"

Maren was quiet long enough that the mortar and pestle in her hands went still.

"You're asking the wrong question," she said finally.

"There's a right one?"

"There usually is." Maren set down the pestle and looked at her — really looked, the way she did perhaps twice a year, with an expression Vesper had never been able to name. Something like fear wearing love's clothing. "But it isn't your birthday question, and I'm not answering it today."

"You say that every year."

"And every year I'm right to." Maren smiled, and it almost reached her eyes. "Go take the comfrey delivery to the Fenwicks. Walk the long way. It's your birthday; be young at it."

* * *

Vesper walked the long way, because she was obedient in all things except the ones that mattered.

The long way went past the western pass, past the border stone, up the sheep track to the ridge where the whole valley spread out below like something painted by someone who loved it too much to leave anything out. Green fields stitched with streams. Smoke rising straight from chimneys. The millwheel creaking its eternal creak.

Her valley. Her whole world, fitted inside a bowl of mountains, humming at the edges with the nothing that kept the world out.

She sat there until the shadows grew long, doing sums she'd been doing since she was small. Nineteen years. One valley. Zero answers. Somewhere beyond those peaks was a court beneath a mountain — everyone knew the stories, told them to children as warnings — a place of cold fire and older bargains, where souls were currency and kings were patient. Nonsense, probably. Or true, and far away, and none of hers.

Either way, it wasn't hers. Nothing outside the stone circle was hers. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

She went home in the blue dusk, let herself into the shop, and found Maren already gone up to bed, which was unusual — Maren closed late on principle, claiming early closers died sooner. A lamp was left burning on the counter beside the back-room door, and beneath the lamp, a note in Maren's cramped hand:

*Don't.*

One word. No explanation. Aimed, Vesper realized slowly, coldly, at the back room — at the oak chest in the corner that Maren kept locked, that Maren had kept locked for nineteen years, whose key hung around Maren's neck and had never once, not once, left it.

Vesper read the note twice.

Then she did what any reasonable person does when told *don't*: she looked at the chest, and the chest seemed suddenly, enormously loud. All these years she'd taken it for furniture, the way you take a scarred old table for granted. But sitting alone in lamplight with a note forbidding her, the chest stopped being furniture and became a door.

She tried the lid. Locked, naturally.

The key was around Maren's neck, upstairs, attached to a sleeping woman who had raised her, lied to her in small daily ways for nineteen years, and hidden a single word under a lamp like a warning nailed to a door.

Vesper stood in the quiet shop a long time, honey-cake sweetness still on her tongue, and felt the day tilt beneath her.

Then she went upstairs, waited until Maren's breathing went slow and deep, and lifted the key from around the old woman's neck with hands that barely shook.

Maren slept on. She would understand later — or she wouldn't, and Vesper would carry that too. But the chest had been waiting nineteen years, and Vesper had spent all of them being patient, and patience, she decided, was a coin she was done saving.

* * *

The lock turned with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, the chest smelled of cedar and dust. On top lay ordinary things — letters bundled with string, a christening gown gone yellow, a lock of dark baby hair tied with ribbon. Vesper's chest tightened. Her mother's, presumably. Her parents, dead before memory, cause unspoken, subject closed for nineteen years with the gentle iron efficiency of Maren's love.

Beneath the letters, a book.

It was old — old the way the border stones were old, old the way the mountains were old. Leather gone soft and dark as plowed earth, pages swollen with age, corners worn round by a century of handling. There was no title. There didn't need to be. Vesper knew prophecy when she held it; the valley children were raised on the shape of such things, even if the valley itself admitted no such power.

She opened it with the reverence people reserve for graves.

The early pages were dense, faded script — visions dated across decades, names she half-recognized from valley history, warnings fulfilled and forgotten. A book of the Hollow's secrets, kept by some keeper long dead. She turned pages faster, skimming, the lamp guttering, her heart keeping strange time—

And stopped.

The entry was dated a hundred years and more past, ink brown with age, written in a hand that had crumbled to dust before Maren was born. But the words themselves sat on the page sharp and black and unbearably clear:

*In the nineteenth year of her forgetting, on the eve of her nineteenth birthday, the Crane girl will open what was locked. Vesper, born under the sealed sky, daughter of the sold name. The ember remembers what the valley cannot. Let her read, and let the counting begin.*

Vesper read it once.

She read it again.

The lamp hissed. Outside, the millwheel creaked, and creaked, and went on creaking, and the valley slept on around her, warm and sealed and safe, humming its edgeless nothing at the borders, and Vesper Crane sat in a pool of lamplight holding a hundred-year-old book that knew her name.

Not Crane-girl-in-general. Not some future child yet unborn. *Vesper.* Her exact name. Tonight's date. Written before her grandmother drew breath.

Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside was not steady at all. Inside was the feeling of standing on the border stone pressing her palm against the nothing — except now the nothing was everywhere, and it had her name written on it.

*Nineteen years of her forgetting.* Forgetting what?

She looked at the chest. At the christening gown, the letters, the lock of hair. At the note that said *don't*, written tonight by a woman who had known, who had always known, who hid a honey cake behind the lavender every single year because she knew exactly how many birthdays Vesper had been counting.

Upstairs, the floorboards settled. Maren, turning in her sleep.

Vesper closed the book gently, as though it might feel pain, and held it against her chest in the dark shop, and listened to the valley creak.

The invisibility was over. Whatever came next, it had already started — a hundred years ago, in ink, in someone else's hand, with her name in it.
