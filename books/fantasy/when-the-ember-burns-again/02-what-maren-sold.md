## 2. What Maren Sold

Vesper did not close the shop.

That was the first strange thing—the way her hands kept moving through the ordinary motions of an ordinary evening while the rest of her stood very still somewhere behind her own eyes. She bundled feverfew. She labeled jars. She swept the floor of the back room where she had swept a thousand floors before, and all the while the prophecy book sat on Maren's desk upstairs like a lit coal in a paper house.

*Vesper Crane.* Her name, inked in brown-black script that had dried a century before her mother ever dreamed of her. *On the nineteenth year, the ember wakes.*

She was nineteen today. There had been cake at breakfast—honey cake, because Maren believed birthdays required honey the way wounds required poultices—and Maren had laughed at something Vesper said about the neighbor's goat, and her eyes had done the thing they sometimes did: gone bright and wet and far away, like a woman looking at a door only she could see.

Vesper had thought it was sentiment. Nineteen years of knowing someone teaches you to read their weather, but it also teaches you to explain away the storms.

She set the broom against the wall with more care than the gesture deserved, and went upstairs.

Maren's study smelled of lavender and old paper and, beneath that, something faintly scorched—a smell Vesper had never been able to place and had long ago stopped asking about. The key to the prophecy book hung on its hook by the window, exactly where it always hung, exactly where it had never once been within Vesper's reach, because Maren wore it around her neck for thirty years and then, three days ago, without comment, moved it to the hook.

Three days ago. Two days before Vesper's birthday.

Maren had known Vesper would find the book. Maren had *left the door open*, which was its own kind of confession, and Vesper's chest ached with the understanding even as her hand closed around the key.

The lock turned with a sound like a held breath released.

The pages were vellum gone soft as cloth at the edges, and the handwriting shifted down through the decades—three different hands, maybe four, each picking up the thread where the last had dropped it. Prophecies about harvests. About a flood that never came. About a child born under a waning moon who would mend a bridge, and there in the margin, in cramped pencil: *did not happen, bridge fell anyway, prophecy is a rumor the future spreads about itself.*

Vesper almost smiled. Almost.

Then she found the page near the end, and the room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with drafts.

*The Crane child. Sold before the quickening. The price of the ward, paid in full. On the nineteenth year, the ember wakes, and the mountain sends for what it bought.*

Below it, in Maren's handwriting—younger Maren, the loops still confident, not yet worn down into the careful scrawl Vesper knew—someone had written a date. Tonight's date. And beside the date, a single word:

*Due.*

"Vesper."

She hadn't heard the stairs. Maren stood in the doorway with a candle in one hand and the other pressed flat against the doorframe as if the floor might pitch. She was small. Vesper had never noticed how small she was, or how gray her face had gone, or how her thumb worried the fabric of her sleeve the way it did when a patient's fever turned.

"You left the key out," Vesper said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "You left the book unlocked. You wanted me to see."

"I wanted you to be ready." Maren set the candle down with a hand that was not quite steady. "There's a difference."

"Ready for what, exactly?" Vesper lifted the book. "Because I've read this page eleven times, and I keep hoping I'm misreading the part where my soul was *sold*. Before I was born. Like a—like a lamb. Like a bolt of wool." The level voice cracked down the middle. "Who sold me?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the evening bell rang from the chapel, and somewhere below, old Tam Wicker shouted cheerfully at his mule, and the Hollow went on being the Hollow—safe, sleepy, magicless, wrapped in its invisible blanket—and Vesper understood, with the terrible clarity of a bone setting wrong, that the blanket had a price tag.

"Sit down," Maren said.

"No."

"Vesper—"

"*Who sold me?*"

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked older than Vesper had ever seen her—not old the way grandmothers are old, but old the way houses are when the foundation shifts. "I did."

* * *

The story came out in pieces, the way bad news always does, in the order the teller can bear rather than the order it happened.

It began thirty years ago, before Vesper existed, when the blight came up the valley road eating everything green and the Hollow's borders were just lines on a map that hunger ignored. It began with Maren young and desperate and gifted in ways the Hollow had no use for, walking out through the pass to bargain with the Undercourt—the court beneath the mountain, the ones the old stories warned about, who dealt in souls the way merchants deal in salt.

"They weren't monsters," Maren said. "That's the worst of it. They were *dying*, Vesper. Their king's fire was going out, and their whole court runs on it, and they were hungry the way starving things are hungry. Not cruel. Hungry." She wrapped her hands around the cold teacup she'd poured and never drunk from. "They offered a ward. A border nothing crossed uninvited. Blight, plague, war, wild magic—all of it stopped at the stones. In exchange—"

"In exchange they got me."

"A soul not yet formed. Unclaimed, unbaptized by any god, unwoven into any fate. The rarest coin there is." Maren's mouth twisted. "Your parents couldn't have children. They came to me for a blessing. I gave them one, and I—" Her voice failed, steadied. "I told myself a soul unborn wasn't anyone yet. I told myself I was saving four hundred lives. I have told myself every version of that lie for nineteen years, and none of them has ever once let me sleep."

Vesper sat very still. The candle had burned down a finger's width. She watched the flame do its small, stubborn dance and thought: *that's what I am to them. That size. That brief.*

"The ward runs on me," she said. "That's what the page means. The ember wakes. My soul isn't sitting in some vault—it's *fuel*."

"It's the hearthstone of the border," Maren whispered. "Every day you've lived inside the Hollow, your soul has been holding up the sky for everyone in it. Every day you've spent outside these stones, it burns faster." She leaned forward, urgent now, gripping Vesper's wrist with fingers surprisingly strong. "Which is why you have to listen to me. Tonight. The mountain will send for you tonight—I've felt it coming for weeks, the way you feel a storm in a healed break. If you run, run *now*, before midnight, and don't stop until—"

"Until what?" Vesper pulled her wrist free, gently. "Until where? Name a place, Maren. Name one place in the world where I can go that doesn't have a mountain in it somewhere."

"There are places—"

"And every day I hide, the ward thins, and the blight comes back, and Tam Wicker's mule, and the chapel bell, and the neighbor's horrible goat—" She was laughing, she realized, or something adjacent to laughing, the sound cracked and awful. "Four hundred people. You said four hundred people. You sold one soul to save four hundred, and now you want me to spend those four hundred to save one. That's just your arithmetic again, Maren. Only this time I'm the four hundred."

"You're the *one*," Maren said fiercely, and her eyes were streaming now, unhidden. "You are the one, and you are mine, and I have loved you since the hour you were born like a penance and like a prayer, both, and I am telling you—begging you—*run*."

And there it was. The betrayal, laid out plain: not the selling itself, though that was a wound Vesper would carry for years, but this—that Maren's answer to the ruin she'd made was still, after everything, to ask Vesper to carry it alone. To flee into the dark with a stolen soul and a guilty conscience and let the valley fend for itself.

Nineteen years of honey cake and herb-lore and lullabies about the moon. None of it false. All of it true. And underneath it, the whole time, a debt with Vesper's name on it.

Vesper stood up.

"Where are you going?" Maren's voice pitched high. "Vesper, the sun's already down—if you mean to run, we take the north path, I have coin hidden—"

"I'm not running." Vesper climbed the stairs to her little room under the eaves, the one with the crooked window and the quilt stitched from three generations of outgrown dresses. She pulled her travel bag from under the bed—the one she'd packed in secret at fifteen, dreaming of the world past the border stones, and unpacked in secret at sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, each time telling herself next spring, next spring.

Tonight she packed it properly. Spare shirt. The good boots. Dried apples and the honey cake saved from breakfast, wrapped in cloth, because whatever waited for her under the mountain, she refused to meet it hungry. Her mother's ring. The stub of pencil she'd owned since she was nine.

Last, she took the prophecy book from Maren's desk. Maren watched from the doorway, wringing her apron, and did not try to stop her.

"You'll die down there," Maren said quietly. "Or worse. They'll keep you forever, Vesper. Souls don't get returned."

"Then I suppose," Vesper said, slinging the bag over her shoulder, "I'd better negotiate."

She kissed Maren's cheek—salt and lavender, the smell of her entire childhood—and walked down through the dark shop, past the bundled feverfew and the labeled jars, past the whole small warm life that had been built on top of her like a house on a grave.

At the door she paused. Behind her, Maren stood in the stairlight, hands pressed to her mouth.

"The border stone at the south pass," Vesper said. "If the mountain sends someone for me, that's where they'll watch. So that's where I'll be."

"Vesper—"

"And Maren?" She looked back once. "When I come home—and I *am* coming home—we're going to talk about the fact that you let me believe, for nineteen years, that the worst secret in this valley was where you hid the licorice."

Then she stepped out into the night, toward the border, toward the mountain, toward whatever had bought her and was coming to collect—

—and saw, at the edge of the lamplight, leaning against the fence post as if he'd been waiting all evening, a man in a knight's travel-worn armor with no crest on it anywhere, watching her with tired, appraising eyes.
