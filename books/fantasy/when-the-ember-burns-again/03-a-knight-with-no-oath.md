## 3. A Knight With No Oath

The border stone stood where it had always stood, gray and patient as a gravestone, at the narrow mouth of the Hollow where the road squeezed between two hills and left the valley behind forever.

Vesper had walked past it a thousand times. She had never once crossed it.

She stopped now, three paces away, her bag heavy on her shoulder with everything she owned: two shirts, Maren's good knife, a heel of bread, and the prophecy book she'd stolen back off the shelf where Maren kept it locked, as if a lock could hold down what was already written.

*Vesper Crane. The nineteenth of Harvestmoon.* Inked a hundred years before she was born. Her name in a dead man's handwriting, waiting for her like a trap sprung slow.

"You could still turn around," she told herself out loud, because the valley was empty and no one would hear her being a coward.

The wind came down off the hills and touched her cheek — just wind, just air, nothing else. That was the whole trouble with the Hollow. Magic couldn't cross the border stone. It pooled outside like rain against a roof, and inside everyone lived dry and safe and small, and Vesper had spent nineteen years pressing her palm against that invisible ceiling without knowing what she was pressing against.

Maren's face when Vesper had asked her — *what did you sell, and what did it buy?* — was a face Vesper would carry to her grave. Not guilt exactly. Worse. Certainty. *I'd do it again,* those eyes said. *I'd sell you again, girl, and sleep soundly after.*

So. The border stone.

Vesper set one foot past it.

And a voice behind her said, "That's far enough."

She turned so fast her bag swung into her hip. A man stood ten paces up the road, and she hadn't heard him coming, which should have been impossible on the Hollow's packed dirt unless he walked like snow falling.

He was big — not fat, but built like something meant to hold a line — with a sword at his hip worn enough to be honest and armor plain enough to be desperate. His cloak had once been fine. You could tell by the way it hung, even faded to the color of old ash. His beard was going gray at the jaw, and his eyes were tired in the specific way of a man who has been awake to his own mistakes for years.

"I know you," Vesper said slowly. "You've been in the shop. You bought willowbark in spring."

"Ser Aldric Vane." He didn't bow. Exiles didn't bow; there was no one left to bow to. "And you're the reason I bought it. Headaches. Yours is a loud soul, miss. It carries."

"My what?"

He sighed, and it was the sigh of a man who had rehearsed this speech on a long road and hated every draft of it. "The Undercourt hired me. To find you. To bring you to the mountain. Alive and willing, if it could be managed."

"Willing," Vesper repeated. Her hand found Maren's knife at her belt. "And how does a knight make someone willing, exactly?"

"With words, ideally." He held up both hands, palms out, empty. "I'm not your gaoler, girl. I'm your escort. There's a difference, and the difference is that an escort can be dismissed."

"Then consider yourself dismissed."

"I would," Aldric said, "except you're about to step over that stone, and everything I know about what happens after says you'll need someone watching your back within the hour. So here's my offer, and I'll only make it once: walk on, and I'll let you go. Truly. I'll sit on this rock, eat my lunch, and report that I lost you. But I'll follow anyway. At a distance. Because I owe the Undercourt a debt, and I owe myself worse ones, and neither debt gets paid by letting a nineteen-year-old with a kitchen knife walk alone into a world that's been waiting a century for her."

Vesper stared at him. "That's the worst offer I've ever heard."

"It's the only one I have."

"Why?" The word came out sharper than she meant. "Why follow? What am I to you?"

Aldric looked at the border stone for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone quiet and rough, like a boot dragged over gravel.

"Twenty years ago I was given an order. Burn a village that sheltered a runaway. I refused. They took my oath, my name from the rolls, and my right to wear silver, and they sent me out into the world to be nobody." He shrugged, a heavy motion. "I've been nobody a long time, miss. And nobodies notice things. I noticed that the court wants you found *before* the king dies, which means whatever you are, you're a clock ticking down. And clocks that tick that loud always break somebody. I'd rather it weren't you."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Maybe the answer is I'm tired of being paid to do wrong things, and this time the wrong thing happens to be standing still while you walk into the dark."

Vesper's hand eased off the knife. Not all the way. But some.

"If you follow me," she said, "I won't go to the mountain. Whatever the court wants—"

"I know what the court wants. I don't care what the court wants. The court exiled me, remember? We're not friends, me and the mountain." He almost smiled. It was a rusty motion, like a gate long unopened. "Go on, then. Cross your stone. I'll be along."

"And if I say no? If I say stay here?"

"Then I'll stay here," Aldric said, "and starve politely, and watch the road, and the moment your back disappears over that hill I'll get up and follow anyway, because I already told you I would, and a man with no oath has to keep the promises he makes himself or he's got nothing at all."

It was, Vesper thought later, the most honest thing anyone had ever said to her. Maren loved her and lied to her for nineteen years. This stranger owed her nothing and told her plainly he'd disobey her to her face.

She stepped over the border stone.

Nothing happened. Everything happened. She couldn't tell, yet — but the air felt different on her skin, thinner somehow, as though she'd been breathing through cloth all her life and someone had finally pulled it away.

She didn't look back. Looking back was for people who expected to be called home.

Behind her, she heard Ser Aldric Vane settle onto his rock, unwrap his lunch, and begin — very audibly — to eat.

* * *

Ser Aldric watched the girl crest the hill and disappear, and allowed himself one full minute of feeling like a fool.

He chewed. He swallowed. He counted the minute out honestly, because he was a man who kept accounts, even the humiliating ones.

Then he got up, brushed the crumbs from his cloak, and followed.

It wasn't duty. He'd buried duty twenty years ago in a burning village that wasn't burned, in the faces of people who'd lived because he'd said *no* to a crowned man in a silver chair. Duty was a coin the mint stopped making when they struck his name from the rolls. What he felt now was something older and less respectable — the itch a hound gets in its teeth, the certainty that somewhere ahead something was about to go badly wrong and he'd rather be near it than not.

The girl was a lit candle walking through a dry field. That was the whole of it. He'd felt her soul from across the market square in spring, bright and strange and utterly unaware of itself, like a bell that doesn't know it rings. The Undercourt wanted her. The Undercourt *needed* her, on a deadline. And the last time the mountain had needed something on a deadline, Aldric had been handed the torch.

Never again. But also — not standing idle. Those were the only two commandments he had left, and they pulled against each other like bad harness.

He caught sight of her a mile on, stopped at a stream, drinking from cupped hands. She was talking to herself again. He'd noticed that in the valley too — a habit of narrating her own courage, as if she had to argue herself into every next step. It should have made her seem weak. Instead it made his chest ache, because he recognized it. That was the voice of someone who'd been kept small on purpose and was finding out how much room there was.

Something flickered ahead of her. Just at the edge of seeing — a spark, a red-gold shimmer above the water, dancing.

Aldric went very still.

He knew what magic looked like. He'd served men who wore it like jewelry. This wasn't that. This was smaller and stranger — a mote of ember-light, drifting toward the girl like a moth that had finally found its flame after a very long winter.

She froze. She reached out one hand.

The spark settled onto her palm like a bird landing, and even at a mile's distance, even without a drop of magic in his own gray soul, Aldric felt it — a warmth rolling down the road like the first day of spring after a killing frost.

The girl laughed. He couldn't hear it, but he saw it hit her whole body, saw her double over with it, saw her sit down hard in the grass holding a handful of fire that didn't burn her, laughing like someone who has just remembered a word she lost years ago.

"Well," Aldric said to no one. "There it is."

He adjusted his pack, checked his sword out of habit, and kept walking.

Somewhere behind them, he knew, others were on the road too. The court wouldn't send only him. He was the polite option — the escort who offered words. Somewhere back along that road rode or walked or crawled whatever the mountain sent when words failed.

And the girl had just lit herself up like a beacon for every one of them to see.

Aldric lengthened his stride.

"Hold on, miss," he muttered. "Help's coming. Whether you want it or not."
