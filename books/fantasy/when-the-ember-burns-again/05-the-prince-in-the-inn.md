## 5. The Prince in the Inn

The inn went quiet the way a forest goes quiet before something large moves through it.

Vesper felt it happen in stages: the dice-players stopped shaking their cups, the barmaid froze mid-pour, and somewhere near the hearth a fiddle player's bow slid off the strings with a sad little *squeak*. The young man at the corner table sat very still, cards fanned forgotten in his hand, staring at her as though she were a ghost he'd been paid to believe in but had privately hoped was fictional.

He was not what she expected. She wasn't sure what she had expected—something armored, probably, something with teeth—but this was a lean, tired-looking man of perhaps twenty-five, dressed in traveling clothes too fine for the road and worn badly, with dark circles under his eyes and ink stains on his fingers. His hair was black as coal-seam. He looked, frankly, like someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks.

Then he did the last thing anyone in that room could have predicted.

He put down his cards, stood up, bowed—low, formal, court-perfect—and said, in a voice pitched to carry to every listening ear:

"Lady Vesper Crane. I've been looking everywhere for you."

* * *

Kaelen's POV

Three months. Three months of riding through country he'd only read about in maps that lied about distances, following rumors that turned out to be drunkards' jokes and a prophecy that turned out to be true, which was somehow worse.

And now here she was, standing in the doorway of the Stag & Thorn with ash on her sleeves and an expression on her face that suggested she'd already decided how this evening would go, and none of the available outcomes involved him winning.

*Say something,* he told himself. *You're a prince of the Undercourt. You've addressed ambassadors.*

"Your soup is getting cold," said the woman beside her—a knight by her build, gray-streaked braid, one hand resting on a sword hilt with the casual authority of long habit. Ser Aldric, if the descriptions held. Exiled. Dangerous. Currently radiating the specific menace of a person deciding whether killing him tonight would be inconvenient.

"I'm not hungry," Kaelen heard himself say. "I'm—" *terrified, exhausted, out of options* "—delighted. Genuinely. Do you play cards?"

Vesper blinked. "...What?"

"Cards." He gestured at his abandoned hand. "I've been playing solitaire against a cheating deck for six days because my escort deserted at the ford and I needed to keep my hands busy so I wouldn't think about the fact that my father is dying and everyone I answer to believes you're the answer to a prayer we stopped saying out loud years ago." He paused. "That came out wrong."

"It really didn't," said the knight.

"There's no version where it comes out right," Vesper agreed. But she crossed the room anyway, pulled out the chair opposite him—the one facing the door, he noticed; she'd chosen the seat where she could watch his hands—and sat down like a queen accepting a surrender. The ember-spirit on her shoulder cast the table in a warm orange light and regarded Kaelen with what he could have sworn was contempt.

"You're the prince," she said. Not a question.

"Kaelen." He offered the name like a small gift, because it was all he had left to give freely. "And before you ask: yes, I'm under orders to bring you home. Yes, I intend to fulfill them. And yes," he added, gathering the deck and shuffling it with hands that only slightly shook, "I am about to lose to you at cards, because I have never once won anything in my life that mattered, and it seemed important to establish the pattern early."

Something flickered across her face—not a smile, but the place where a smile might live someday, given better weather.

"One game," she said.

"One game," he agreed, and dealt.

* * *

Vesper's POV

The thing about playing cards with a prince, Vesper discovered over the first hour, was that he cheated.

Not well—he cheated the way a child cheats, with elaborate misdirection and a guilty flush creeping up his neck—but he cheated, and when she caught him palming the queen of spades for the third time she simply looked at him until he sighed and set it down.

"My father taught me that trick," he admitted. "He said a king who can't cheat honestly deserves to lose."

"That's terrible."

"He also said a prince should never lie to someone whose eyes are that color, because they'll always know. Which is why I'm going to stop lying to you now, approximately four hours later than wisdom dictates." He set down his cards. Around them the inn had emptied by degrees; the barmaid kept refilling their mugs with the resigned air of a woman who recognized trouble and had decided to charge extra for it. "The bargain that bought the valley its ward—it's failing. My father made it before I was born. Before *you* were born. The magic that holds the borders runs on..." He hesitated.

"On me," Vesper said quietly. Cindral stirred on her shoulder. "I know. Maren told me. My soul, sold before I drew breath, to pay for stone walls around people who never asked for them."

"They asked," Kaelen said. "That's the part nobody tells you. There was a plague year, ninety years back. The Hollow petitioned the court for protection. Your mentor carried the request herself—she was younger then. My father named his price, and Maren Voss paid it, and I don't think either of them understood what they were trading until it was done." His voice roughened. "My father has spent nineteen years pretending the debt doesn't sicken him. Now he's dying, and when he dies, the bargain passes to whoever holds the throne. And the man next in line is not—"

He stopped. Drank. Started again.

"The man next in line collects things," he said. "Souls. Grudges. He's been waiting for the ember of your soul to come home so he can burn brighter than Father ever did. If I bring you back under the old terms, I save my father's peace and deliver you into his hands forever. If I don't bring you back at all, the bargain collapses when Father dies, and the ward falls, and everything your mentor traded herself hollow to protect—" He spread his hands. "There's no clean ending, Lady Crane. There's just the least dirty one. I've spent three months trying to find it and mostly I've found blisters."

For a long moment Vesper said nothing. The fire crackled. Cindral's light pulsed slow, like a sleeping heartbeat.

"You look terrible," she said finally.

Kaelen laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, rusty from disuse. "I know."

"No, I mean—you look like you haven't slept since the ford."

"I haven't. Every time I close my eyes I see two endings and neither one has you walking free in it." He rubbed his face. "I'm telling you this because tomorrow I have to decide, and whichever way I choose, someone gets broken, and I wanted—at minimum—for the person most likely to be broken to know I hate it."

Vesper studied him across the cards: the ink stains, the exhaustion, the crown-shaped worry he wore like a wound. Somewhere behind her, Aldric pretended not to listen and failed completely.

"Play another hand," she said.

* * *

Kaelen's POV

They played until the candles burned low.

Somewhere past midnight the games stopped being games. They talked instead—or she talked, mostly, in short fierce bursts between deals: about the valley that forgot her, about the locked book with her name inked a century ago, about the moment beyond the border when magic flooded into her like drowning in reverse. He told her things in return, small treasons of honesty: that he'd memorized her description at nine years old off a document he wasn't supposed to read; that he used to sneak up to the mountain gate and imagine the girl the bargain held; that he'd requested this assignment hoping she'd be insufferable, because it would be easier to do his duty to someone he disliked.

"And?" she said. "Am I?"

"Insufferable?" He considered her—mud-spattered, sharp-eyed, holding an ember-spirit like a candle she refused to be afraid of. "You're the worst possible outcome. You're someone I'd rather fail with than succeed without."

The words hung there, too honest, too late to take back. Vesper looked at him for a long time. Then she reached across the table and took the queen of spades out of his sleeve, where he'd hidden it without noticing he'd done it, and laid it gently on the pile.

"You win," he said.

"No," said Vesper Crane. "But I might, yet."

It was nearly dawn when she stood, gathered her cloak, and paused beside his chair. Close enough that he could see the ember-light reflected in her eyes, and something underneath it, banked and patient, like coals waiting for a reason.

"If you come for me tomorrow," she said, "come *with* me. Not after. Not against. With."

She walked toward the stairs. At the landing she stopped, and without turning around: "The man next in line collects things. What does *he* call you? When no one's listening?"

Kaelen stared at his empty hands. "Traitor," he said. "He's called me traitor since I was twelve."

"Good." And now he heard it, clear as morning: she was smiling. "Then you'll know the way."

Above, her door closed softly. By the window, Ser Aldric met his eyes across the darkened room and gave a single nod—the first thing the knight had given anyone freely in years. On the table, the little ember-spirit flared bright once, twice, like a lamp being lit in a house long shut.

Kaelen sat alone among the dead candles and realized, with the calm of a man watching the last bridge burn behind him, that he had already decided.

Outside, far off along the road they'd ridden in on, torches moved in the dark. Many of them. Moving fast.
