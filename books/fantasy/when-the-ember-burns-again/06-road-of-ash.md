## 6. Road of Ash

**Vesper**

The first thing Vesper noticed about the land beyond the valley was that it had stopped pretending to be alive.

They had walked three days out of the hills, and the green had thinned by degrees—first the wildflowers went, then the moss, then the grass itself, until they were crossing a plain of gray ash that crunched underfoot like old bread. Trees stood in rows as if a forest had once been planted here and then simply forgotten to be anything else. Their branches were bare. Not dead-bare, she decided, studying one as they passed. Worse than that. *Waiting*-bare.

"It's the bargains," Kaelen said, when she asked. He said it lightly, the way he said most terrible things, as though he were describing weather. "Every soul the Undercourt takes has to go somewhere. The magic drains out through the mountain's roots and—" he gestured at the gray plain with a flourish that didn't quite hide his flinch "—this."

"This is what my soul is paying for?" Vesper asked.

"No," Kaelen said quickly. "Yours is different. Yours is—"

"Kaelen." Aldric didn't turn around. His voice was flat as the road. "Stop talking."

Cindral, riding in Vesper's hood as he had taken to doing, made a small sound like a log settling in a fire. Vesper had learned to read him well enough: that one meant *he's right to stop him*.

She let it go. But she counted how many times over the next two days Kaelen started to explain something about the Undercourt and then found something fascinating to look at instead. She was on her fourth count before she admitted to herself that she was counting at all, and what that meant—that some part of her had already decided not to trust him, and was gathering evidence like a lawyer building a case against her own heart.

That night they camped in the shell of a farmhouse whose walls still stood but whose roof had become more suggestion than shelter. Vesper cooked; Aldric cleaned his sword for the third time that day, which was how he prayed, she suspected; Kaelen told a story about a court jester who had once convinced the entire Undercourt that the moon was made of cheese, and got Aldric to make a sound that might have been a laugh if you were feeling generous.

And Cindral sat apart from all of them, on the cold hearthstone, and did not glow.

Vesper noticed it the way you notice a room gone quiet. The ember-spirit had burned steadily since the day he'd attached himself to her—a warm gold-orange, brightening when she laughed, flaring when she was frightened. Now he was the color of a coal an hour after the fire dies. Dim. Dull. Small.

"Cindral?" she whispered.

He turned his coal-dark eyes up to her. *"I'm fine,"* he said, which was exactly what someone would say who was not fine.

* * *

**Aldric**

Ser Aldric Vale had been hunted before. It changed a man, being hunted—taught him things about himself he'd mostly have preferred not to know. So when he crested the ridge at dawn on the fifth day and saw, far behind them, four riders moving in the disciplined wedge of a military patrol, his body knew before his mind did. His hand went to his sword. His stomach went cold.

He knew the wedge. He'd drilled it a thousand times under Captain Rhea Solenne, who had taught him everything worth knowing about soldiering and then, two years ago, ordered him to burn a village of soul-debtors and learned what he was willing to refuse.

"She's found us," he said.

Vesper looked up from lacing her boots. "Who's found us?"

"The best soldier I ever served with." He kept his voice even, because Vesper was watching him the way she watched everything now—cataloguing, weighing. "Captain Rhea Solenne. The Undercourt hired her company when I wouldn't do their work myself. Poetic of them, really."

"How many?"

"Four riders visible. Probably eight total." He squinted. The wedge moved with terrible patience, no hurry at all. "Rhea doesn't chase. She herds. She'll push us toward ground of her choosing and take us there."

Kaelen stood, brushing ash off his fine coat that would never be fine again. "Then we should talk about what happens when she catches us. Because she will, and I know her, and I know what she'll ask first."

"And what's that?" Vesper asked.

"She'll ask me why I'm traveling with the court's property in the wrong direction." Kaelen smiled his inn-smile, the charming one, the one Vesper had once called *infuriatingly effective*. "And I'll tell her I'm taking you home. Under orders. That I intercepted you from these two criminals and we're going to the gate together. It's clean. It keeps everyone safe."

Aldric watched Vesper's face while Kaelen said it. He watched the smile she gave back—warm, easy, agreeing—and he watched what was underneath it, which was nothing at all. A door closing quietly.

He said nothing. But he marked it, the way he marked everything now, and he thought: *the prince is lying to protect her, and she knows it, and neither of them sees what that costs.*

* * *

**Kaelen**

The lie worked for six hours.

Kaelen had rehearsed it in his head until it shone: the story was simple, plausible, and required only that Rhea believe he was still the man who'd left the court three weeks ago—dutiful, tired, desperate to get this done before his father died. Most of it wasn't even a lie. That was the trick of good lies. You built them out of true things, like building a fire out of wood that used to be a house.

Rhea caught them at midafternoon, in a shallow valley where the ash lay drifted like snow. She rode out ahead of her men with her helm off, gray-streaked braid over one shoulder, and looked at Kaelen for a long moment with eyes like a winter river.

"Your Highness." She dismounted. Bowed. Perfectly correct. "You're a long way from your escort."

"I dismissed them. Sensitive cargo." He gestured at Vesper with the weary air of a man transporting something breakable and expensive. "The Crane girl. I found her at the border with these two. They're coming along as leverage, or hostages, or whatever word makes this least awkward."

Rhea's gaze slid past him. To Vesper. To Aldric, standing with his hand nowhere near his sword, which from Aldric was its own kind of message. To the small dim ember sulking in Vesper's hood.

Then back to Kaelen. And she smiled—not unkindly, which was worse.

"Your Highness," she said, gently, "your father sent me a letter last week. It named you traitor if you failed to deliver the girl by the new moon." She paused. "It also mentioned you'd be traveling with Ser Aldric Vale, whom you were ordered to arrest on sight. And it said—" she drew a folded paper from her breastplate, and did not open it, only held it, "—that if you spoke to the girl as anything other than cargo, I was to consider my contract void and my loyalty mine again."

The silence in the ash valley was very long.

"You're lying," Kaelen said, and heard his own voice crack on it.

"No," Rhea said. "You are. And badly, Highness. You always were terrible at it—you blush." She folded the letter away. "So. Here is where we actually stand. My company is behind me, and half of them would follow me into the sea and half would sell me to the court by morning. The king is dying. Whatever bargain holds your girl's soul is unraveling with him, and every soul in that valley of hers will go with it." Her eyes went to Vesper, and softened by a degree. "I don't know what you're planning, miss. But I'd plan faster."

She remounted. Turned the horse.

"Captain," Aldric said.

"Aldric." She didn't look back. "You were right, two years ago. I've hated you for it every day since. Try not to die before I finish hating you properly."

And the wedge wheeled away into the ash, leaving four people and one dimming ember standing in a valley that had died so that a mountain could live.

* * *

**Vesper**

They didn't speak for an hour. When they finally made camp, Vesper sat down across the fire from Kaelen and looked at him until he ran out of charm.

"You lied to me," she said. "For days. Every time you 'explained' things, you were editing. Every time you went quiet, you were hiding."

"I was protecting you—"

"I know." And that was the worst part, and she made herself say it. "I know that's what you think you were doing. But Kaelen, everyone who's ever protected me has done it by deciding what I'm allowed to know. Maren. The ward. The whole valley. I have spent my entire life inside other people's merciful lies, and I am *done*." Her voice shook, and she let it. "If we're walking into that mountain together, you tell me the truth even when it's ugly. Especially when it's ugly. Or you can walk in alone."

Kaelen looked at the fire for a long time. When he spoke, the inn-charm was gone entirely, and what was underneath was just a tired young man with a dying father.

"The bargain doesn't just hold your soul," he said. "It holds *all* of them. Every soul the court ever bought, going back four hundred years. They're not spent—they're stored. The king burns them slowly to keep the court alive, the way a candle burns wax. And the valley's ward is fed from the same store." He swallowed. "When he dies, the store breaks open. Everything comes out at once. The ward won't survive it. Neither will the court. It's not theft, Vesper, it's—it's a dam, and my family built it out of people."

The fire popped. In Vesper's hood, Cindral stirred, and his light came up—just slightly, just barely, like a breath drawn in the dark.

*"Now she knows,"* the ember said softly. *"Now the road gets shorter."*

Vesper reached up and touched him with one finger, gentle as touching a wound. "Why are you dimming, little one?"

Cindral was quiet a moment. Then: *"Because I was born from the spark they cut out of you. And the closer we come to the mountain, the more the court pulls at what's left of me. It remembers what it owns."* His coal-eyes lifted to hers. *"I don't want to go back to being yours, Vesper. I like being *with* you. There's a difference. I only recently learned there's a difference."*

Behind them, far off across the gray plain, a horn sounded—one long note, then another, then a third. Not Rhea's signal. Rhea's signals had been crisp. This was ragged, urgent, wrong.

Aldric was on his feet before the third note faded, staring north toward a smudge on the horizon that hadn't been there that morning: a black line of riders, too many to count, moving fast.

"That's not a hunting party," he said quietly. "That's the court emptying out. Something's happened at the mountain."

Kaelen went white. "My father."

Vesper stood, pulled her pack tight, and felt Cindral flare against her neck—not much, not the old brightness, but enough. Enough to see the road by.

"Then we stop walking to the mountain," she said, "and start running."
