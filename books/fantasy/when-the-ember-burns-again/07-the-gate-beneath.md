## 7. The Gate Beneath

The mountain announced itself three days before we reached it, the way a judge announces himself before a trial: slowly, and with every intention of making you feel small.

Kaelen had grown quieter as they climbed. Vesper noticed—of course she noticed; she noticed everything about him now, in that way that made Aldric roll his eyes and Cindral make small crackling sounds of commentary—but she didn't press. She was busy pressing her palms against boulders as they passed, feeling for the hum of magic thickening in the stone like sap under bark.

Kaelen was doing arithmetic.

He knew the numbers by heart. He'd known them since he was nine years old, when his father first sat him down in the cold audience hall and explained what the family business actually was. *We do not rule the Undercourt, Kaelen. We keep its accounts.* Souls owed, souls held, interest accruing in the dark for centuries. His father had said it with pride. Kaelen had looked at the ledger and seen only a list of names, each one a person who had once been someone's daughter or someone's son, filed away like grain against a famine.

And at the top of the newest page, ink still sharp after nineteen years: *Vesper Crane. One soul. Held in trust.*

*In trust.* As if the mountain were a kindly uncle.

"Tell me again," Vesper said on the last morning, when the gate was close enough to smell—the air had gone mineral and strange, like licking an old coin. "What happens when we walk through."

Kaelen kept his eyes on the path. "I present you. Formally. There are words—I'll say them, you stand there looking appropriately reluctant, which won't require acting—and the gate wardens take custody. You're registered. Housed. The king gets what he paid for before he dies, the bargain holds, the valley keeps breathing." He paused. "And I get my name back."

"And if you don't say the words?"

He laughed, because it was easier than answering. "Then I'm a traitor. They strip the title, the name, the standing. In the Undercourt that's not a metaphor, Vesper. Names are *kept* there. If they take mine, there won't be anything left to call me. I'll be one of the unrecorded. People will look at me and their eyes will slide off, because there'll be nothing to look *at*."

Cindral, riding in the hood of Vesper's cloak, flared a sullen orange. "Sounds rude."

"It's efficient," Kaelen said. "Everything down there is efficient. That's the whole problem with us."

Vesper stopped walking. The others went two paces before realizing, and then it was just the four of them on a grey scree slope under a sky the color of old pewter, wind pulling at everything.

"You don't have to come in," she said. "Aldric could hold the pass. Cindral and I—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "No. That's not fair either. I'm not going to hand myself over and ask all of you to be fine with it. I just—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Every road out of this ends with someone paying. I'm so tired of other people paying for me."

Kaelen looked at her—at this woman who had spent nineteen years as a line item in his family's ledger, who had walked out of her valley with a bag over her shoulder and a spine like a drawn bow, and who was standing here worrying about *his* name.

Something settled in him then. Not peace—he suspected he'd never have that again—but certainty. The kind his father had tried to teach him with ledgers, and failed, because certainty couldn't be inherited. It had to be chosen.

"I'm coming in," he said. "But not the way they expect."

* * *

The gate was older than the court above it. That was the thing no one told you about the Undercourt: it thought of itself as eternal, but even mountains have doors, and doors have makers, and the makers were dust before the first king learned to count souls.

It stood alone in a cirque of black stone—a slab of basalt forty feet high, carved with a door that did not open, covered in script worn nearly smooth. No guards. No wall. Just the door, and the sense of being read.

Kaelen stopped ten paces out and turned to face the other three.

"When I say the words of presentation," he said, "the gate answers. It always answers. And when it does, everyone within earshot knows exactly where I am and what I've done." He looked at Aldric. "Which brings us to the hunt."

"They're close," Aldric said. He'd been saying it for a day, in the flat tone of a man reporting weather. "Commander Hale runs hounds that track oath-breaks. Mine's the loudest scent in the march. She'll have the pass by nightfall whether we're through or not."

"So you hold it."

Aldric's eyebrows went up, slow and theatrical. "Do I?"

"Aldric." Kaelen heard his own voice crack and didn't care. "You were exiled for refusing an order. One order. You've spent six years paying for a single *no*. I'm asking you for a whole sentence of them."

"I know what I'm asking," Aldric said. "That's why I'm enjoying the pause."

But he was already unbuckling his sword belt to check the straps, which was Aldric for *yes*, and always had been. He tested the blade, rolled his shoulders, and looked past Kaelen to Vesper.

"Knight with no oath," he said. "Turns out it's the best rank I ever held. Nobody can order me to leave this pass, because nobody owns me." A crooked smile. "First time being unowned has come in handy."

Vesper crossed to him. For a moment Kaelen looked away—it felt like trespassing—whatever passed between them needed no witness. When he looked back, Aldric was buckling his belt again and Vesper's eyes were red-rimmed and furious with grief, and she said, "You come through that gate after us. That's an order from someone who doesn't own you."

"Yes, *my lady*," Aldric said, with tremendous insolence, and turned to face down the pass.

* * *

Kaelen stood before the door.

The words of presentation were in Old Court, a language built for contracts, every syllable a hook. He'd learned them as a child, kneeling on cold stone, his father's hand heavy on his shoulder: *One day you will speak these words and give the mountain what it bought.*

He opened his mouth.

And found, to his complete lack of surprise, that he had rehearsed this moment too many times in too many wrong ways to say it right.

"*Hear me,*" he began, and the gate woke, script kindling along the basalt like embers under ash. "*I am Kaelen, son of the house beneath, keeper of accounts, and I bring—*"

He stopped.

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind them, faint as memory, a hound gave voice.

"*I bring,*" Kaelen said again, and then he laughed—one short, disbelieving breath—and turned his back on the door entirely, to face Vesper.

"No," he said. "I'm done bringing. Nineteen years your family paid for a soul, and my family kept the receipt, and nobody once asked the soul what it wanted. So I'm not presenting you. I'm not giving anyone anything." He dropped to one knee on the frozen scree, head bowed, the way he'd knelt to his father's crown at age twelve—and had never once meant it the way he meant this.

"Vesper Crane. I can't give you back what was taken. But I can refuse to be the hand that takes it again. My name, my title, my house—all of it, against yours. Whatever you choose in there, I choose it first."

The gate roared.

Not with the clean chime of acceptance—with a sound like a ledger tearing, script flaring white and then *red*, the word TRAITOR burning itself across the basalt in letters tall as trees. The mountain knew. The mountain would tell everyone. By morning there would be nothing left of Kaelen, son of the house beneath, but a man with no name walking into a dark place that ate names.

He stayed kneeling anyway.

Vesper came to him. She took his face in both hands and made him look up, and her expression wasn't gratitude—that would have been too easy, too small. It was recognition. One account-keeper to another, closing a book.

"Get up," she said. "We walk in together."

Behind them, up the pass, steel rang against steel, and Aldric's voice rose over the wind—not a battle cry, something better, something almost cheerful:

"*Come on, then! I've got all evening and no orders whatsoever!*"

Kaelen got to his feet. Cindral blazed up Vesper's shoulder like a small furious dawn. Hand in hand, the named and the soon-to-be-nameless turned to the burning door—

—and it opened onto darkness, and cold, and the sound of a king counting what remained of his life.

They stepped through.
