## 8. Court of Cold Fire

The mountain did not want them inside. That was the first thing Vesper understood about the Undercourt: it was not a place that had been built so much as a place that had been *argued* into existence, and the argument was still going on.

The gate had swallowed them at dusk—Kaelen's treason still fresh on his knees in the snow behind them, Aldric's silhouette still standing alone against the pass—and now they walked down a corridor of black stone that breathed. Not wind. *Breathing.* The walls expanded and contracted with a slow, patient rhythm, like the ribs of something enormous and very old that had learned to sleep standing up.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Vesper asked.

"The mountain's alive," Kaelen said. He was walking ahead of her with his hands clasped behind his back, which she had learned was what he did when he was terrified and wanted no one to know. "Everything under it is alive. That's rather the problem."

Cindral rode in the crook of her elbow, dimmed to the size of a coal in a dying grate. He hadn't spoken since the pass. When she'd asked him why, he'd only said, *I remember this place,* and gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than any of Kaelen's confessions had.

The corridor opened without warning into a hall so vast that her eyes refused at first to accept it. Pillars rose out of darkness into further darkness. The floor was smooth as a frozen lake, and their footsteps rang off it like bells in an empty church. And everywhere—everywhere—there were lights. Not torches. Not lamps. Small cold flames, blue-white as frost, hovering in the air at every height, motionless, patient, watching.

"They're souls," Kaelen said quietly. "Some of them. The ones the court holds in trust." He winced at his own word. "In *debt*, I should say. The ones the court holds in debt."

Vesper stopped walking.

She could feel them now that he'd named them—a pressure against her skin like standing too close to a window in winter, hundreds of small cold attentions all turned toward her at once. One of them drifted nearer, curious, and she felt it brush her cheek like a fingertip made of January.

"Don't," Cindral said, very softly. "Don't let them taste you, little spark. They'll know what you are."

"What am I?"

He didn't answer. The soul-light drifted away, disappointed.

They crossed the hall through a forest of pillars, past alcoves where hooded figures sat motionless—courtiers, or statues, or both; Vesper genuinely could not tell—and came at last to a set of doors taller than Aldric, carved from a single slab of stone veined with silver. The veins glowed faintly. Fainter than they should have, she thought. Fainter than anything here ought to glow, in a court built on fire.

Kaelen pressed his palm to the doors. "Whatever happens in there," he said, "remember that I chose you. Whatever my father says, whatever the law says—I knelt in the snow for you, and I'd do it again with frostbite in places frostbite has no business being."

"That's very romantic," Vesper said.

"I'm a prince. We practice."

The doors opened onto cold.

* * *

The throne room was smaller than the hall, which somehow made it worse. It felt less like a seat of power and more like a sickroom that had given up pretending otherwise. The cold fire here burned low along the walls, guttering, and the great throne at the center was less a chair than a bier—a slab of black stone on which a figure lay propped among furs that might once have been magnificent.

King Aurelian of the Undercourt looked like a man who had been handsome for three hundred years and was now simply tired. His skin was gray as ash. His eyes, when they found Vesper across the distance between them, were the color of banked coals—the last warm thing in the room.

"So," he said. His voice was dry leaves and door hinges. "The Crane girl. You have your mother's chin and your father's stubbornness, and I would know, because I held the contract on both their debts before either of them drew breath." A smile, thin as paper. "Come closer, child. I've been waiting a hundred years to look at what I bought."

Vesper did not come closer. She planted her feet on the frozen floor and folded her arms, which was, she realized a moment too late, exactly what Maren did when a customer tried to hagle over feverfew. The thought hurt more than the cold did.

"You didn't buy me," she said. "You bought a promise about me. There's a difference. Ask anyone who's ever been promised."

The king laughed—a real laugh, rusty, surprised out of him—and then coughed, long and wet and terrible. When he finished, one of the wall-flames had dimmed.

"Straight to the point. Good. I haven't time for dancing." He lifted one hand, and the cold fire in the room gathered toward him like moths, like children, like things that loved him. "You want your soul back. Of course you do. It's yours; it was always yours; we took it the way courts take everything, by paperwork and patience. But you should understand what you're asking before you ask it."

"I know about the ward," Vesper said. "Maren told me. My soul is the price of the valley's protection. Take it back, the barrier falls, everyone I love dies or worse. I did the arithmetic on the road. It doesn't balance."

"Do you know *how* it works?" The king's coal-dark eyes glittered. "Not the contract. The mechanism. Do you know what a ward needs to run for a century?"

"Magic," Vesper said. "A lot of it."

"A *soul*," the king said, "is not magic. Magic is what a soul does when it's allowed to be alive. What we hold in the vault beneath this throne is not your magic, Vesper Crane. It is the thing that makes your magic *yours*. Your name, before anyone spoke it. Your shape, before anyone wore it. And the valley's ward is woven through it like thread through cloth—" he coughed again, waved her protest aside, "—because that is how your mentor designed it. Not me. Her. Maren of the Hollow took the bargain I offered and improved upon it, because she is cleverer than I will ever be, and she bound the ward directly into the living core of you. Pull your soul free, and the ward unravels in the same instant. There is no surgery. There is no careful extraction. There is only the thread, and the scissors, and everything you love falling down."

The silence afterward had weight. Vesper stood in it and felt it settle on her shoulders like snow.

Behind her, she heard Kaelen shift his weight. Heard him start to say, "Father, there must be another—" and stop, because there wasn't, and they all knew there wasn't, and kindness sometimes meant letting a sentence die before it embarrassed itself.

"You brought me here," Vesper said finally, "to tell me it's hopeless."

"I brought you here because I'm dying," the king said simply, "and when I die, every contract I hold collapses—including yours, including the valley's, all at once, with no warning and no mercy. I wanted to look at you first. I wanted to see what a hundred years of my greed had bought the world." His gaze moved over her face, hungry and sad. "It bought a girl who argues with kings. Perhaps that's worth it. Perhaps not. The dead are poor judges of accounts."

"And if you lived?" Vesper asked. "If the cold fire burned again—if you had years instead of hours—could you release the contracts? Slowly? Carefully? Unravel the thread stitch by stitch instead of cutting it?"

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Hope, maybe, or its fossil.

"To reignite the fire of the Undercourt," he said, "requires fuel of a kind that hasn't existed in this mountain for a century. Freely offered flame. Willing warmth. No court has that. Courts *take*." He closed his eyes. "Go home, child. Live your borrowed life. Love your borrowed people. When I die, it ends, and none of it will have been your fault, which is more than most souls in this room can say."

And that was when the doors opened again, and Maren walked in.

* * *

She looked terrible. Vesper registered this with a strange, detached clarity—her mentor, her whole sky for nineteen years, gray-faced and frost-burned, one sleeve torn where something in the pass had gotten a hand on her, breathing hard from a climb no woman her age should have survived. She had followed them. Through the blighted march, through the hunt, through the gate, alone, with nothing but a herb-satchel and the same mule-headed resolve she used on customers who insisted willow bark cured love.

"You absolute *idiot*," Vesper said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it.

"Language," Maren wheezed. "There's a king present."

"There's a *dying* king present," Vesper said, "which you clearly knew, because that's why you're here, isn't it? That's why you followed us the whole wretched way." She turned back to the throne, and the pieces assembled themselves in her chest with a horrible click. "You knew what he needed. You've known for years. That's why you wrote the date in the book. That's why you told me to run—you weren't saving me from the court. You were buying time to get here first."

Maren didn't deny it. She never did; it was one of her worst qualities. She walked forward across the frozen floor, slow, deliberate, until she stood between Vesper and the throne like a hedge planted against a storm.

"Your Majesty," she said. "We met once, a hundred years ago. You'll forgive me if I don't curtsy; my knees have opinions."

"I remember you," the king said. "The clever one."

"The clever one," Maren agreed. "And the clever one has spent a century knowing this day would come, and preparing for it, and hating herself, and preparing anyway." She reached into her satchel and drew out a small glass vial, no bigger than a thumb, in which something soft and golden turned slowly, like a candleflame underwater. "My soul, Your Majesty. Whole. Unbargained. Ninety-one years old and entirely mine, which makes it rarer than anything in your vault. Freely offered, freely given—one old woman's flame for one young girl's freedom, and the ward stays woven, and nobody's valley falls down."

"No," Vesper said.

"It's not a discussion, dear."

"Maren—"

"You are nineteen," Maren said, and now she turned, and her eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely immovable, the eyes of a woman who had raised a child on stolen time and always known the bill would come due. "You are nineteen and you have never seen the sea and you argue with kings, and I have been old and tired and finished for a decade, and I will not stand in this freezing hole and watch you gut yourself to pay for my cleverness. I made the bargain. I pay the price. That is what mentors are *for*."

The vial caught the cold fire and threw it back gold.

On the throne, King Aurelian looked at the small golden flame, and then at Vesper, and then at the vial again, and Vesper watched three hundred years of hunger and three hundred years of exhaustion fight openly across his ruined face.

"It would work," he said at last, quietly. "That is the obscenity of it. It would work beautifully."

"Then take it," Maren said.

"Put it down," Vesper said, and her voice came out strange—low and even and nothing like her own. "Put it down, or I swear by every herb in your shop, I will drink it myself."

And in the crook of her elbow, so faintly that at first she thought she'd imagined it, Cindral began—very softly—to burn brighter.
