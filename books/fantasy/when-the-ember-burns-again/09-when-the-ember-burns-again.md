## 9. When the Ember Burns Again

The throne room of the Undercourt was not what Vesper had imagined, which was fitting, because nothing in her life had been what she imagined either.

She had pictured cold stone and colder faces, a court of shadows sharpened to points. Instead the great hall beneath the mountain was beautiful in the way a frozen lake is beautiful—vast and blue-lit and utterly still. Chandeliers of pale fire hung from a ceiling lost in gloom, burning without warmth. Courtiers lined the walls like statues dressed in silk, their skin faintly luminous, their eyes old beyond counting. And on a throne of black glass sat the king: a figure so thin his crown seemed to hold him upright, wrapped in robes that had once been magnificent and were now only patient.

His fire was nearly out. She could see it—the dimming behind his ribs, visible through his skin like a lantern running dry. The whole court was a room full of people pretending not to watch a candle die.

"Vesper Crane." His voice was soft as ash settling. "Nineteen years, and here you stand at last."

"I'd have come sooner," she said, "but nobody told me I was invited."

Beside her, Kaelen made a small strangled sound that might have been a laugh or a prayer. Aldric stood at her other shoulder, hand nowhere near his sword, which for Aldric was practically a declaration of faith. Cindral rode in the crook of her arm, a coal no bigger than a plum, trembling with heat he was trying very hard not to show.

"You know why you've been brought," the king said.

"I know why I *came*," Vesper corrected. "Brought implies I didn't have opinions about it. I have *many* opinions."

Maren stood off to the left, near the dais steps, grey-faced and wringing her hands, having arrived two days ahead of them by means she still refused to explain. "Vesper, please—"

"In a moment, Maren." Vesper kept her eyes on the king. "First I want to hear it from you. All of it. No more pieces handed to me one at a time like I'm a child being fed medicine in jam."

The king inclined his head, and something in the court shifted—a rustle of silk, a collective intake of breath that had been held for decades.

"The ward around your valley is woven from your soul," he said simply. "Your mentor came to me before your birth, when the Hollow's borders were failing and the blight beyond would have swallowed every living thing within a season. She offered the only currency I accept. A soul, freely given—or freely taken; the magic does not care which, though I confess I have always preferred the former. Your soul has burned in my hearth ever since. It is the last bright thing in this mountain." He paused. "And I am dying, Vesper Crane. When I die, the bargain collapses. The ward fails. Your valley dies. Unless the soul passes to my heir, and my heir holds the throne, and the throne holds the fire, forever. That is the shape of it. That is the trap."

"And if I take my soul back?"

"Then the ward unravels tonight, and everyone you love dies slowly, over years, in a valley that forgets how to protect them." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "There is no clean answer. There never was. Your mentor did not sell you out of malice. She sold you out of arithmetic."

Vesper looked at Maren then. Her mentor's eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and Vesper thought: *she carried this alone for nineteen years. She baked bread and taught me to distill feverfew and never once let it show.*

"I'm not angry," Vesper said quietly. "I want you to know that. I'm going to be, later, probably, at three in the morning for about a decade. But right now? You bought time for an entire valley. That's not betrayal. That's just a terrible price paid by terrible accountants."

"Vesper—" Maren started.

"So here's mine." Vesper stepped forward, up onto the first step of the dais, and the whole cold court leaned toward her like grass toward sun. "You want my soul. Kaelen wants to save his father. Maren wants to trade herself instead—yes, I heard her offer at the gate, I wasn't asleep. And everyone keeps treating this like a market stall. Soul for ward. Soul for heir. Soul for soul. Everyone's got prices chalked up."

She lifted Cindral into both hands. He was blazing now, too hot to hold comfortably, and she held him anyway.

"But nobody asked what *I* have to sell. Because I'm not selling anything today. I'm giving something away."

The king's dim eyes narrowed. "Magic freely given cannot be compelled back. It is the one gift with no strings. If you give me your magic—"

"—then the fire burns again," Vesper finished. "Not on stolen fuel. Not on a caged soul. On a gift. Freely given, freely burning, and when it gutters out—and it will gutter, everything does—it gutters *clean*. No debt. No heir chained to a hearth. No valley holding its breath for a century waiting for the next bill to come due."

"That is not how bargains work," said a courtier near the wall, scandalized.

"It's exactly how gifts work," Vesper said. "That's the difference, and honestly, it's the whole problem with this court. You've forgotten there *is* a difference."

Kaelen made the strangled sound again, louder.

The king was silent a long moment. The chandeliers flickered. Somewhere deep in the mountain, Vesper felt—not heard, *felt*—a vast slow heartbeat, and knew it was the fire itself, listening.

"If I accept," the king said carefully, "your magic leaves you. You will be as you were in the Hollow. Ordinary. And your soul—"

"Stays mine. You release it. You release *all* of them." She gestured at the walls, at the courtiers, at the dark doorways where she could feel other fires banked and waiting—souls held against debts real and imagined, some of them centuries old. "Every soul this court holds that was taken rather than given. That's my price. Not a bargain price. A condition. Gifts don't haggle."

"You understand," the king said, "that when my fire goes out—for it will go out, even fed by your gift—you will have given away the only power you ever had, to a dying man, for nothing you can keep."

"I'll keep plenty." Vesper smiled, and found to her surprise that it didn't shake. "I'll keep a valley full of people who get to grow old. I'll keep a knight who follows people around for free. I'll keep a prince who's terrible at cards. And I'll keep—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—the piece of myself that apparently ran off ahead of me and became a small opinionated fire. Which brings me to you, Cindral."

The ember-spirit flared. "*Yes.*"

"When the king releases my soul—it comes home. All of it. Including whatever spark you were born from. What happens to you?"

Cindral was quiet for a moment, which for Cindral was unprecedented and alarming.

"*I go home too,*" he said at last. "*Into you. Where I should have been all along. Don't make that face, Vesper Crane, I have been looking forward to this since the day I crawled out of your stolen light. You are drafty and stubborn and you hum when you cook, and I intend to spend eternity telling you so from the inside.*"

"That's the least romantic proposal I've ever heard," Kaelen murmured.

"It wasn't for you," Vesper said, and turned back to the king. "Well? Do we have a gift, Your Majesty? Because I warn you, I've read enough of Maren's contract law to know a verbal acceptance counts down here."

The dying king looked at her for a long, long moment. Then, with effort that shook the throne, he rose.

"Nineteen years I have waited for someone to walk into this hall and refuse me properly," he said. "Do you know how many souls have stood where you stand? They beg, or they rage, or they calculate. None of them ever simply *gave*." He extended one translucent hand. "Freely given?"

"Freely given," Vesper said. "No strings. No heirs. No century of interest accruing. Just light, while it lasts."

"Then I accept," said the King of the Undercourt, "and may the memory of it outlast me."

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the strangest part. She had braced for pain, for tearing, for the sensation of being emptied—but the magic left her the way warmth leaves a cup passed hand to hand, flowing up her arms and out through her palms and into the king's waiting grasp.

He lit like dawn.

Cold fire roared up through the black glass throne, through the chandeliers, through the courtiers' stunned faces, and for one impossible moment the Undercourt was the warmest place Vesper had ever known. The king laughed—a sound like a bell underwater—and threw back his head, and the light poured out of him into every corner of the mountain.

And the doors opened. All of them. Souls came streaming into the hall like birds released from a burning house—points of light, hundreds of them, thousands, spiraling upward through the vaulted dark, singing in voices too high for ears. Some paused before the king as if to bow. Some paused before Vesper. One brushed her cheek, warm as a hand, and was gone.

Her own soul came last.

It came slowly, the way a dog comes home after a long absence—circling, uncertain, checking whether it was wanted. Vesper held out her hands.

"Come on, then," she whispered. "You're late. We have a lot to catch up on."

It settled into her chest like a struck match—

—and Cindral went with it, laughing, blazing, pouring himself into the hollow place inside her that she had never known was hollow until that exact moment, filling it to the brim and past the brim, until she glowed. Until the whole court glowed. Until Maren was crying openly on the dais steps and Aldric had turned away to inspect the architecture with suspicious intensity and Kaelen was gripping her elbow as if the floor might otherwise take her.

The king sank back onto his throne, spent, smiling, dimmer already but *clean*, burning on borrowed grace instead of stolen goods.

"How long?" Kaelen asked hoarsely.

"Long enough," his father said. "Longer than I deserved. Go home, boy. Both of you. Take the knight and the herb-witch and the—" he peered at Vesper's chest, where a faint ember-glow pulsed under her skin, "—whatever that is now."

"He's a cat," Vesper said. "Or he will be. He promised."

"*A magnificent cat,*" said her own chest, indignantly.

* * *

They walked out through the mountain while the light was still rising behind them, past gates swinging open, past courtiers who touched their foreheads as she passed. At the threshold where the tunnel met the night sky, Vesper stopped and flexed her fingers, testing.

Nothing. No wild heat, no flood of power. Just her hands, ordinary and steady, and a warmth behind her sternum like a banked hearth.

"Well," she said. "That's that."

"Not quite." Kaelen pointed east, toward the distant line of the Hollow's hills. Even from here, across miles of blighted march, she could see it—the ward, shimmering faint as breath on glass.

Still standing.

But different. She could feel it, the way you feel weather coming. It wasn't running on her stolen soul anymore. It was running on something else—on nineteen years of Maren's bread and Aldric's grudging loyalty and a prince losing badly at cards, on every memory the valley had ever made.

Memory, not theft.

"Huh," said Vesper Crane, and started walking home.
