## 10. Home Is a Door Left Open

The border stone looked smaller than I remembered.

That was the strange thing about leaving the Hollow and coming back: the valley hadn't changed at all, and I had. The stone still stood where it had always stood, gray and patient, wearing its crown of moss like an old man wears his hat. Beyond it, the fields of Marrow's Bend lay gold in the late afternoon light, and the smell of bread from Tilda's ovens drifted all the way out here, which it shouldn't have, because magic couldn't cross this line and neither could the smell of anything so good.

I stopped at the stone. Old habit. You didn't step over unless you meant it.

"You're doing the thing," Kaelen said behind me. "The standing-and-staring thing. You've done it at every landmark since the mountain."

"I'm savoring."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *savoring*, your highness, and if you say one more word I'm going to remember that you cheated at cards our first night together and tell everyone in that village exactly how you did it."

Aldric snorted. He was carrying most of our luggage, because he was Aldric, and because he'd decided somewhere on the Road of Ash that carrying things was how he expressed affection now. "For the record," he said, "he cheats badly. I've watched him. He palms cards like a man who learned it from a book."

"I learned it from a *very good* book," Kaelen said with dignity.

At my feet, Cindral sat down and began washing one paw with tremendous concentration. He'd been a cat for six days now, ever since the king's fire guttered out and something small and stubborn in him refused to gutter with it. He was orange, mostly, with patches of ember-red along his spine that glowed faintly when he was pleased and flared bright when he was offended, which was often. Right now he was glowing like a forge, because we were almost home and he knew it, and because home apparently included him.

He'd asked. That was the part that undid me every time I thought about it. On the last night before the mountain pass, curled against my ribs where the ember used to burn, he'd said, *The spark came from you. Let me see where it grew.* And what do you say to that? What do you say when the piece of yourself they sold before you were born looks up at you with coal-bright eyes and asks to come home?

You say yes. Obviously. You say yes and then you cry into a disgraced knight's spare cloak, and nobody mentions it afterward, because that's what family does.

"Vesper." Kaelen's voice had gone quiet. He'd come up beside me, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched mine, and he was looking at the valley with an expression I'd learned to read over a hundred miles of bad road. It was the look of a man seeing a place he'd only heard described in prophecy books and Maren's old stories—*the Hollow, where nothing burns and nothing leaves.*

"It won't feel like anything," I warned him. "Crossing over. That's the whole point of the ward. No magic, no pull, no—" I gestured vaguely at everything the Undercourt had been made of. Cold fire. Bargains. The weight of a dying king's gratitude sitting on my chest like a second heartbeat. "Just quiet."

"Good," he said. "I could use some quiet."

And that was true, as far as it went. But I watched him look at those golden fields a moment longer, and I saw what he wasn't saying: that he was a prince of a court that no longer held any souls, that his father was three weeks dead and mourned by maybe nine people, that he'd traded a crown's worth of duty for a herb shop in a valley that would forget his name the moment he crossed its borders.

None of them belonged here. That was the plain truth of it. A prince with cold-fire blood in his veins, a knight whose oath had been struck from the rolls, and an ember-spirit wearing the body of a cat—all of them walking into a place built specifically to keep their kind out.

They were coming anyway.

I stepped over the stone.

* * *

Nothing happened. That was the miracle of it. Nothing happened, and the bread smell got stronger, and Cindral sneezed at the sudden absence of his own glow—he went dull orange, ordinary, a perfectly normal cat—and Aldric made a small sound I'd never heard him make before, something between relief and grief, like a man setting down a sword he'd carried so long his arm had forgotten its own shape.

"No more dreams," he said quietly. He'd never told us much about the dreams. I hadn't pushed. Some debts you let people pay privately.

Kaelen flexed his fingers, frowning. "It's gone. All of it. Like a—" He paused, hunting for the word, and I braced myself, because Kaelen hunting for words usually ended in poetry. "Like a room you've walked through your whole life, and someone's finally turned off the lamps."

"That's not comforting, Kaelen."

"It's *accurate*, Vesper."

But he took my hand anyway, crossing the last field toward the village, and his hand was just a hand. Warm. Human-adjacent. Holding mine because he wanted to, not because any bargain or prophecy or dying king's wish told him to. I found I liked that version better.

Tilda saw us first. She was out front of her bakery beating a rug, and she beat it one more time very slowly while she stared at us, and then she dropped the rug entirely.

"Vesper Crane," she said. "You get over here."

I got over there. She grabbed me by both shoulders and looked me over like she was pricing me at market, and whatever she saw—the road dust, the new lines around my eyes, the general air of a person who had walked into a mountain and walked back out—made her jaw go tight.

"Maren's been asking after you," she said. "Every day. Comes down for her bread, stands there, asks if you've come home yet. Every day, Vesper. For three weeks."

Three weeks. The king had died three weeks ago. Which meant Maren had followed us after all—which I'd known, I'd seen her in the court of cold fire offering her own soul like it was a purse of coins—but I'd lost track of her on the way out, in the chaos of the releasing, in the long climb back into daylight. She must have come home ahead of us. Or behind us. Or simply *around* us, the way Maren did everything, sideways and stubborn and three steps ahead of anyone giving her credit for it.

"She's at the shop?" I asked.

"Where else." Tilda's eyes flicked past me to the others—to Aldric, big and scarred and trying to look harmless, which was like a bear trying to look like furniture; to Kaelen, who was doing his best charming-prince smile and getting absolutely nowhere with Tilda; to Cindral, who sat down and began licking his paw as if he did this every day. "And who are they?"

"Family," I said.

It came out easy. That surprised me most of all. Three months ago I'd had a mentor and a valley and a locked book, and I'd called that enough. Now I had this ragged collection of people who owed me nothing and stayed anyway, and the word *family* fit them like it had been measured for them.

Tilda looked at each of them again, longer this time. Then she nodded once, the way she nodded at bread that had risen properly, and said, "Well. They'll be wanting supper, then. Come by when you've sorted yourselves out." And she picked up her rug and beat it twice more, hard, which was Tilda for *I'm glad you're alive and I will be discussing this later.*

* * *

The shop door was unlocked.

That stopped me on the step. Maren locked that door the way other people breathed—involuntarily and constantly. The lock had been the first thing I'd noticed about the prophecy book too, and the second thing, and the third, right up until the day I'd picked it and found my own name inked in a dead woman's hand a century before I was born.

I pushed the door open. The bell above it rang, tin and sweet, the same note it had rung my whole life.

Inside, everything was where it should be. Bundles of feverfew hanging from the rafters. The good mortar and the chipped mortar. The jar of honey left unstoppered because Maren had never once in her life managed to put a lid back on anything. And Maren herself, sitting at the worktable with the prophecy book open in front of her, a cup of tea gone cold at her elbow, looking like a woman who had been sitting in exactly that position for three weeks waiting for a door to open.

She looked older. The mountain had taken something out of her—I'd seen it happen in the court of cold fire, seen her face when the king refused her soul and thanked her instead, thanked *her*, the seller, the bargainer, the woman who had spent nineteen years loving a child she'd also spent. Grief and relief had carved her down to something finer and more fragile.

"Vesper," she said.

"Maren."

We stood there. Somewhere behind me, Aldric cleared his throat with the delicacy of a landslide, and the three of them filed past me into the shop—past! into!—and started putting things away. Kaelen hung his travel cloak on the peg by the door like he'd been doing it for years. Aldric picked up the fallen broom and leaned it back in its corner. Cindral jumped onto the counter, knocked over the dried lavender, and settled into the spill like he owned it.

Maren watched all of this happen with her mouth slightly open.

"They're staying," I said. "In case that wasn't clear. We're going to need more beds. And Kaelen eats like a siege is ending."

"The prince," Maren said faintly. "Of the Undercourt. Is putting away my yarrow."

"He's very good at it. Watch."

He was, too. Neat little bundles, tied properly. I'd taught him on the road, night after night by Cindral's glow, because a man who couldn't sleep needed something to do with his hands, and because teaching him felt like rewriting something. Duty rewritten. That was the whole shape of my life now, I think. Everything I'd been handed—a stolen soul, a prophecy, a valley full of people who never knew what their safety cost—everything, I'd carried back to the mountain and set down, and what I'd carried home instead was this: four people and a cat in a herb shop, putting things away.

Maren closed the prophecy book.

I hadn't even looked at it yet. I'd been avoiding it, honestly, the way you avoid a mirror when you're not sure who'll be in it. But now she slid it across the table to me, and her hands were shaking, and I understood that this was the apology she'd been unable to give on the road, in the court, in front of kings.

"The last page," she said. "Look at it."

I opened the book to the end.

Blank.

Not empty the way a page is empty before it's written. Blank the way a page is blank when whatever was written on it has been *answered*. I ran my fingers over it and felt nothing—not the prickle of old magic, not the itch of a fate waiting. Just paper. Just paper, in a valley where paper was allowed to be only paper.

"It finished," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of it. "The prophecy. When the king released the souls—it finished. There's nothing left for it to say."

"There never was anything it *had* to say," Maren said. "That's what I finally understand, Vesper. I read that page a hundred times and thought it was a sentence being handed down. It was a question. *When the ember burns again—* and then it waited a hundred years to see what you'd do with the rest." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she did everything. "You answered it better than any of us deserved."

I came around the table and hugged her. She smelled like feverfew and cold tea and the shop, and she held onto me with the desperate strength of a woman who had spent nineteen years preparing to lose everything and gotten it all back by accident.

"I'm still angry with you," I said into her shoulder.

"I know."

"I'll be angry for a while. Maybe years. There might be incidents."

"I'll deserve every one."

"But you're teaching Kaelen to make the cough syrup, because he needs useful things to do and I refuse."

Maren laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one. "Agreed."

* * *

We reopened the shop on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were market days and market days were when the Hollow came to you whether you invited it or not.

By noon we'd sold out of feverfew. By mid-afternoon, half the valley had come through under one pretext or another—to buy comfrey, to ask after my health, to stare openly at the enormous scarred man grinding herbs in the back with the focus of a monk, or at the handsome stranger who greeted everyone by name within a day because of course he did, he was a *prince*, charm was load-bearing for him. Tilda brought bread and interrogated Kaelen for forty minutes and pronounced him "acceptable, barely." The miller's boy adopted Cindral on sight and was devastated to learn the cat already had a family, and Cindral, glowing faintly with smugness that no one else seemed able to see, permitted the boy to scratch his ears for a full quarter hour.

Magicless. All of it. No wards humming, no cold fire under the floorboards, no soul of mine burning in a stranger's keep. Just a valley, holding itself up the way valleys do—by memory, by habit, by people showing up. The king's last gift, freely given: the ward rebuilt on remembrance instead of theft. *Let them keep it,* he'd said, with the last of his fire. *What is given cannot be repossessed.*

Evening came down gold through the west window. Aldric lit the lamps—no magic in it, just a match and a steady hand, and he seemed to love the match more than he'd ever loved any spell. Kaelen argued cheerfully with Maren about the correct ratio of honey to vinegar. Cindral slept in the lavender.

I stood in the doorway a moment, keys in my hand, and locked the shop behind us out of pure habit.

Then I smiled, and left the key in the door.

Some doors, I'd learned, you leave open.
