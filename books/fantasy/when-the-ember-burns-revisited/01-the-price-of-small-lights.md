## 1. The Price of Small Lights

The trick to selling magic in a city where magic is outlawed is to sell it badly.

That was the first lesson Ashmarket taught me, back when I still had lessons to learn and a spine that hadn't yet been bent into ledger-shape. A charm that works too well draws eyes. A charm that works too obviously draws worse. But a charm sold as a *trinket* — a bit of river-glass on a cord, a knotted thread "for luck," a copper disc stamped with a fish because fish are plentiful and nobody legislates against fish — a trinket can sit in plain sight on a market blanket all day while doing its quiet, illegal work.

So I sold small lights. Harmless ones. A warmth-charm to keep a grandmother's hands from aching through the winter. A sleep-knot for a colicky baby. A focus-token for a clerk whose eyes wandered off his columns. Nothing that would make the Order's wardens look twice. Nothing worth burning anyone over.

I should say here, since honesty costs me nothing anymore: I am very good at this. Seven years good. And every morning I hated myself a little more efficiently than the last, which is its own kind of craft.

* * *

Ashmarket woke the way it always did — grudgingly, smelling of coal-smoke and frying onions and the particular sourness of a city that had learned not to hope before breakfast. I had my blanket laid out by the time the first light came gray over the rooftops, my wares arranged with studied carelessness: charms in three rows, priced in coppers, each one tagged with a little paper label in my own neat hand. *Luck knot, 2 cp.* *Warmth token, 4 cp.* *Against bad dreams, 5 cp.*

And beneath the blanket's folded corner, where no one ever looked: my ledger-work for the Order of the Quiet Flame. Three years of entries in their required ink, their required hand, recording every registered scribe's daily output of *sanctioned* work — which is to say, work that did nothing at all. The Order liked its magic like it liked its theology: inert, ornamental, safely dead.

My debt sat in that ledger too. Seven years old now. It began with my sister's lungs, went through a physician who took payment in favors, and ended with me signing myself over to the Order as a bound scribe until the sum was worked off. They set the interest themselves. Of course they did. The Quiet Flame does not burn bright, but it burns *patiently*, and patience is how institutions eat people.

"Morning, Dunmore." Old Marta from the pie stall, setting up beside me. She'd been calling me by surname alone for six years, ever since I'd corrected her once about the price of a sleep-knot and she'd decided familiarity was a luxury she couldn't afford from a man who smelled faintly of temple incense. "You look like death rehearsing."

"I've been practicing," I said. "How's the hip?"

"Worse. Weather's coming." She squinted at the sky, which was doing something pale and wrong above the eastern wall — clouds the color of dirty wool, though the season said they shouldn't be there. "You got anything for weather?"

"You know I don't sell weather."

"You know I'll pay double."

I knew she'd pay triple. I also knew what happened to charm-sellers who sold weather. The last one had been a woman named Odile who'd kept the rain off the tanners' quarter for a whole summer until a warden bought an umbrella that stayed dry indoors. They'd made her stand in the Fountain Square while a Cantor read her name out of the register, and then they'd read her out of existence — every record, every memory outside the minds of those who loved her, smoothed away like a thumbprint in wet clay. I'd been eleven when it happened. I'd cried for a week and couldn't remember why.

"No weather," I said. "But the warmth tokens are fresh. Two for seven."

She haggled me down to six because that was the liturgy between us, and bought two, and pressed one back into my hand because my fingers were white at the tips and she pretended not to notice and I pretended not to be grateful. This, too, was Ashmarket: kindness conducted entirely through fiction.

* * *

The morning went the way mornings went. I sold a focus-token to a tax-clerk who needed it more than he knew, a luck-knot to a fishwife who swore by them and would swear louder if she ever learned what they actually were, and a bad-dreams charm to a woman who told me her husband hadn't slept whole through the night since the war. I gave her the one with the strongest weave and charged her four instead of five and lied that it was yesterday's stock.

That was the other lesson Ashmarket taught me: you don't charge full price for mercy. Mercy isn't merchandise. You just make sure it leaves your stall looking like merchandise, so nobody asks why your hands shook when you tied the knot.

It was nearly noon when the child came.

She couldn't have been more than eight or nine, small even for that, with soot on her cheeks and her hair cut short in the way of children whose families couldn't spare comb or care. She stood at the edge of my blanket for a long moment, studying the rows of charms with the ferocious concentration of someone counting coins in her head and finding the sum insufficient.

"They're two coppers," I said gently. "The luck knots. That's the cheapest."

"I'm not buying a luck knot." Her chin came up. Under the soot she had a stubborn jaw and eyes the color of strong tea. "What's that one?"

"That one's a—" I looked where she pointed. My stomach did something complicated. "That's a remembering-charm. River-glass. It helps you hold on to things you're afraid of forgetting."

"I want it."

"It's eight coppers."

"How much do you have?"

I blinked. "That's not—"

"My gran's forgetting things." The words came out flat and fast, the way children deliver news they've already chewed to death alone. "The physician says it's her age but it started after the fever and he won't come again without money and Da says the forgetting is a blessing because she doesn't cry about my brother anymore except she *does*, she just doesn't know why she's crying." She held out her fist. Opened it. Four coppers, warm from her grip, and a button. "That's everything. Please."

Now. Here is where I should tell you what I told her: that the charm was a trinket, that river-glass holds nothing but light, that grief is not a thing a string can carry. Here is what the law required me to tell her, what the Order required, what seven careful years required.

Here is what I actually heard myself say:

"Sit down. No — not there, here, where I can see your hands. Give me the button back; I don't take buttons, I'm not a beggar."

She sat. She watched me work with the terrible stillness of a child who has learned that watching closely is how you survive. And I will confess what I did, because someone has to confess things eventually and I appear to be the only candidate.

I wove her grandmother's name into the glass.

Not much. Just the sound of it, folded small, tucked into the lattice the way you'd fold a letter into a pocket. Remembering-charms are delicate work — you can't put memories *in* them, only give the mind a hook to catch itself on, a familiar shape to follow home when it wanders. It wouldn't cure anything. It wouldn't bring back what the fever took. But it might let an old woman surface sometimes, gasping, holding her granddaughter's face, knowing *why* she was crying — and knowing, too, that the crying had a bottom to it, that love was the thing underneath.

It was more than I'd woven for any paying customer in three years. It was, technically, the most illegal thing I'd done since Odile burned.

"There." I tied the cord myself, around and twice through, the way Wren used to—

The thought arrived and left in the same instant, a bird through an open window. I didn't chase it. I have gotten very good at not chasing it.

"Four coppers," I said. "Because the glass was chipped."

"It's not chipped."

"It's chipped *now*. Four coppers and we never speak of this again."

She paid. She held the charm up to the light and looked at me with those tea-colored eyes and said, with the awful precision of children, "You're not supposed to be able to do that, are you?"

The market noise seemed to recede. Somewhere behind my ribs, my heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as attempting to exit via the throat.

"It's glass," I said. "On a string."

"It's not."

"Sefa, is it? Sefa, it's glass."

Her eyebrows went up. "I didn't tell you my name."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. I replayed the exchange — she hadn't introduced herself, I hadn't asked, the name had simply been *there*, sitting on my tongue like it lived there, the way names sometimes arrive in this city when the wards lean close and the boundary between known and unknown goes thin as ice on a spring puddle. Small gift. Useless gift. The kind of gift that gets people read out of registers.

"Someone shouted it," I said. "Earlier. In the crowd."

Nobody had shouted anything. We both knew it. She pocketed the charm with the solemnity of a knight receiving a sword, and looked at me for a long moment, and then said the second-worst sentence anyone said to me that year:

"You have sad hands, mister. Like my gran before she forgot."

* * *

I closed the stall early. I told myself it was the weather — those wrong-colored clouds thickening over the wall like smoke that had forgotten which direction to rise. I told myself the headache building behind my eyes was the coal-smoke. I told myself a great many things, the way I always did, arranging my lies in tidy ledger-columns the way the Order had taught me.

Under the blanket, my hidden notes waited in their oilskin wrapping. Not the Order's ledger — mine. The one I wasn't supposed to keep, the one that recorded not sales but *names*: every person I'd ever charmed, every small light I'd ever sold, written down because I could not bear the thought of a single one of them being forgettable. Including, on the first pages, in handwriting younger and steadier than mine is now, a name I had not spoken aloud in four years and could not stop writing.

Wren. Ember-keeper of the Lamplighters' Hall. Sold to the court beneath the mountain in payment of debts that weren't theirs, struck from every record in the city, remembered now by exactly one bound scribe with chipped river-glass and sad hands.

I touched the oilskin and didn't open it. Some doors you keep closed during daylight hours.

Above the eastern wall, the clouds had gone the color of ash. The lamplighters were making their rounds early, lighting the street-flames against a darkness that hadn't properly arrived yet, and I noticed — distantly, the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've sung all your life — that the flames were guttering. All of them. Down the whole length of Ember Row, the little fires bending sideways, toward the mountain, as if the mountain were inhaling.

Old Marta packed up beside me, glancing at the sky, at the lamps, at me.

"Dunmore," she said slowly, "you feel that?"

I felt it. Under my feet, faint as a heartbeat through a floorboard, something vast and far below us had begun to pull.

"Storm coming," I said.

It was the only lie I told that day that I almost believed.
