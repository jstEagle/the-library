## 2. The Night Without Stars

Midnight came to Ashmarket the way it always did: badly, in stages, with a drunk singing three streets over and the lamplighters long since gone home to whatever it is lamplighters dream about. I was still awake because I was always still awake, hunched over a ledger by candlelight with my fingers stained gray from cheap ink, doing sums for an institution that owned seven years of my life and had never once said thank you.

The Order of the Quiet Flame kept its accounts the way it kept everything else—quietly. My debt sat in those pages like a stone at the bottom of a well. Seven years of scribe-work against it, and according to High Cantor Vess's most recent arithmetic, I had perhaps two more years left before I was free. Two years of copying ward-maintenance reports in a hand so careful no one would ever suspect that the same fingers had sold forty working charms out of a market stall that very week.

I want to be clear about something, since history—if it remembers me at all—will probably get it wrong: I did not love the deception. I loved what it bought. There is a difference, and on good nights I could feel the shape of it.

That night was not a good night.

The first sign was the candle. It guttered—not the ordinary gutter of a draft under the door, but a slow, deliberate lean, as if the flame were listening to something. Then it stood back up, perfectly straight, and burned blue for exactly three heartbeats.

I know what you're thinking. Three heartbeats of blue flame means nothing; candles do strange things. But I have spent my whole adult life around small magics, and small magics are like animals: they go quiet when something bigger passes nearby.

Then the singing stopped. Mid-verse. The drunk three streets over simply ceased, and the silence that rushed in behind him was not the ordinary silence of a city between songs. It was the silence of held breath.

I put down my pen. I remember thinking, absurdly, *I haven't finished the column.*

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud—I looked, I threw open the shutters and looked, and there were no clouds, because clouds need starlight to be seen against, and there was no starlight. The sky over Ashmarket was not black the way night is black. Night has texture, depth, the faint gray suggestion of the world continuing above you. This was absence. This was a ceiling.

And beneath that ceiling, the wards along the border—two hundred miles of woven light that had held since before my grandmother's grandmother—the wards went out too. I couldn't see them from Ashmarket, obviously. But every hair on my arms stood up at once, and the little charms hanging in my stall downstairs—all eleven of them, the ones that worked and the ones that didn't—rang together like struck glass. One clear note. Then nothing.

In the street below, someone screamed. Then several someones, in the particular overlapping way that tells you panic has stopped being individual and become municipal.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about catastrophe: it is mostly logistics. The screaming lasts an hour, maybe two. After that comes the knocking on doors, the hoarding, the rumors moving faster than feet—and after that, inevitably, the paperwork.

The Order's summons reached me before the second hour was out. A novice, fourteen at most, hammering on my door with a face gone the color of tallow. "All registered scribes," she gasped. "To the Chantry. Immediately. Cantor Vess's orders."

"Registered scribes," I repeated. "Meaning me."

"You're on the rolls, Master Dunmore." She said it with the flat apology of the very young delivering bad news they don't understand yet. "Everyone on the rolls."

I dressed. I took my coat, and then—because habit is stronger than sense—I took the little oilcloth packet from beneath the floorboard and slid it inside my shirt, against my ribs. My hidden notes. Wren's name, written in my own hand, over and over, dates and small observations, proof of a person the world had agreed to forget. I told myself I took them because leaving them felt dangerous. That was true. It was also true that I could not have left them, any more than I could have left a lung.

The streets were bedlam with a strange orderliness to it, the way crowds are orderly right up until they aren't. People stood in doorways staring upward with their mouths open, as if the sky were a debt collector who had come early. An old woman was nailing a horseshoe to her lintel by candlelight. A man ran past carrying a birdcage, empty, running anyway.

"The wards," people kept saying, to each other, to themselves, to me. "The wards are down."

Which was true, and also not the whole truth, though I didn't know that yet. What I knew was the feeling in my chest—that specific dread of a man whose forgery has been excellent for years and who has just heard boots on the stair. Except the boots belonged to the sky itself.

* * *

The Chantry of the Quiet Flame was built to be unremarkable, which in Ashmarket is a kind of architecture, and inside it smelled as it always did of cold ash and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where important decisions are made slowly. Vess stood at the head of the scriptorium, robed in the gray that meant formal business, and around her maybe thirty scribes, blinking, half-dressed, clutching pens like talismans.

Vess did not shout. Vess never shouted. She let the room's fear do her shouting for her, and then spoke into the quiet she'd harvested.

"At midnight," she said, "the border wards failed. All of them. Simultaneously." She let *simultaneously* sit there a moment, because simultaneous failure is not how wards fail. Wards fail one strand at a time, fraying like rope. They do not all go dark in the same instant unless the thing feeding them stops feeding them. "You will divide into assessment teams. You will travel to the nearest anchor-stations. You will record conditions, inventory remaining stores, and report back within three days."

A scribe near the front—an earnest young man named Pell, whom I liked despite myself—raised his hand. "Cantor. Failed *how*? The wards are soul-fed. The contracts—"

"The contracts are being reviewed," Vess said, in a voice that closed doors. "Your task is observation, Brother Pell. Not speculation."

But I saw her hands. Vess's hands, folded in front of her, were doing the thing they only did when the arithmetic frightened her—thumb pressing thumb, slow, rhythmic, a woman counting to some number that kept not arriving. I had spent seven years learning to read that woman the way she read ledgers. She was afraid. And Vess being afraid meant the situation was worse than *simultaneously*, worse than anything she would say aloud in a room full of registered scribes.

She assigned me to the western anchor-station at Grayfall, with two others: Pell, and a dry old sister named Omma who had assessed more failing wards than the rest of us had copied. We were to leave within the hour.

"One more thing," Vess said, as we gathered our kits. Her eyes found mine across the room, and I felt the particular chill of being *seen* by your creditor. "Dunmore. You were assigned to the ward-record audit last month. You'll recall the asset registers?"

My mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with fear of the dark sky. "I recall them, Cantor."

"Good. When you reach Grayfall, pull the station's contract ledger. Cross-reference against the central registers. If—" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do, something almost gentle, almost human, "—if the fuel records show irregularities, I want to know before anyone else does. Before the Conclave knows. Do you understand me?"

Irregularities. Fuel records. Somewhere in the back of my mind, a door I had kept locked for years rattled on its hinges, and I thought—unreasonably, absurdly, with no evidence whatsoever—of Wren. Of the way names disappear when institutions decide they should.

"I understand, Cantor."

"See that you do." She turned away, dismissing me, and added, without turning back: "Take care on the road, Dunmore. The dark is not merely visual tonight."

It was the closest thing to kindness she had offered me in seven years, and it terrified me more than the black sky had.

* * *

We left Grayfall-bound through streets that had begun, horribly, to adapt. Lanterns hung everywhere, improvised constellations strung between balconies, because people cannot abide a lightless sky and will manufacture their own. A child watched our party pass from a windowsill—a girl, maybe eight or nine, wrapped in a blanket far too big for her, holding a charm on a string. One of mine. I recognized the knot. The one that wasn't supposed to work, sold that morning to a boy, but this girl had it now, and it was glowing. Softly, stubbornly, blue as my candle had been, holding its own little circle of warmth against the enormous dark.

She wasn't scared. She was watching it like it was the most interesting thing in the world.

Something in my chest turned over. Fear, I decided later. Fear wearing tenderness's clothes, the way it usually does in me. Because if a dead man's worthless charm could burn like that tonight, then the dark outside wasn't just absence. It was hunger. And things that hungry come looking for what feeds them.

Pell walked beside me, chattering nervously about anchor-station protocols. Omma walked ahead, silent, counting something under her breath. And I walked with my hand pressed flat against my ribs, over the oilcloth packet, over Wren's name, and made myself a promise I didn't fully understand yet:

Whatever the ledgers said happened at midnight—whatever the fuel records showed, whoever had been listed as *asset, sold*—I was going to find it. Not for Vess. Not for the Order.

For the person the world forgot, whom I hadn't.

Ahead of us, the road bent toward the mountains, toward the collapsed ward-line, toward whatever waited in the unprecedented dark. And somewhere behind us, faint and impossible, the girl's little charm was still burning.
