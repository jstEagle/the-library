## 3. What the Ledger Kept

The Order of the Quiet Flame kept its records in the Undercroft, three floors below the chapel where the faithful pretended not to notice that the eternal flame had gone out.

I want to be clear about that, because it matters: the flame was out. Had been out since midnight. And the Order's response, in the great tradition of institutions everywhere, was to form a committee.

"Registered scribes will audit the ward-ledgers," High Cantor Vess had announced that morning, her voice carrying the particular calm of a woman who had already decided what the truth would be and merely needed it transcribed. "We must determine the extent of the failure. Nothing more. No speculation. No talk of souls."

No talk of souls. In an order whose entire theology ran on the premise that souls were currency. It was like a bank forbidding mention of money while its vault stood open and empty.

So there I was, third day after the sky went black, sitting in a room with no windows, surrounded by ledgers that smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath—something like a cellar where meat has been kept. The Undercroft's air had that quality. I'd noticed it my first year and convinced myself I'd imagined it. Seven years later, I no longer had that luxury.

My assignment: cross-reference the ward-contribution rolls against the soul-asset registers for the northern border line. Tedious work. Deliberately tedious, I suspected. Vess wanted her scribes buried in columns of figures so deep they wouldn't have time to wonder why the stars had gone dark or what exactly the wards had been eating all these years.

The trouble with giving me tedious work is that I am very good at it. Debt does that. When you owe an institution everything, you learn to read its books the way a pickpocket reads a crowd—looking for the loose thread, the unguarded pocket, the entry that doesn't belong.

I found the first one at midday.

* * *

The ward-rolls for the northern line were kept in a hand I recognized before I recognized the name. Neat, upright, economical—the writing of someone who resented every letter but refused to let the resentment show in the work. Wren had written those pages. Wren had sat in this same cold room, or one like it, and recorded the intake of souls into the mountain court with the same careful strokes they used for grocery lists and the margins of love notes.

I hadn't known, then. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the wound under the wound. Wren had been taken in the spring of the year we were together—called up for "temple service," which everyone understood to mean debt labor and no one asked about. I'd received two letters. Then nothing. Then, a season later, a formal notice from the Order informing me that one *Wren Ashvale, ember-keeper, third class*, had been *discharged from all obligations*.

Discharged. As if Wren had finished a shift. As if they'd walked out some evening door into the ordinary world and simply chosen not to come home to me.

I'd spent a long time being angry about that. Anger was easier than the alternative, which was understanding what *discharged* meant in the vocabulary of an institution that traded in souls. But I'd been a scribe for years by then. I knew the ledger-language. I just hadn't let myself read it.

Now I read it.

*W. Ashvale — ember-keeper, 3rd class. Obligations transferred. Asset status: sold.*

Sold. Not discharged. Sold. There was even a price, entered in the court's private notation—a string of characters that looked like accounting but read, if you knew how, like a butcher's tally. Portions. Fractions. The soul divided into shares the way a harvest is divided, or a debt.

And beneath it, in a different hand, older and colder: *Allocated to border ward, north line. Fuel grade: high.*

Fuel grade: high. Wren, who could keep a coal alive through a night of rain with nothing but their breath and their stubbornness. Wren, who once relit the whole of Ashmarket's festival lamps when the lamplighters went on strike, laughing the entire time. Fuel grade: high. Of course. Of course they burned brightest.

I put down my pen because my hands had started shaking, and a scribe who shakes spoils the page, and I have always been a good scribe.

That was when the memories came.

* * *

They came the way they always came—not summoned, not welcome, rising like silt when you stir a riverbed. The Undercroft did something to them. Or the ledgers did. Or proximity to Wren's handwriting opened some door in me that seven years of careful living had papered over.

—I am on the roof of the ember-house and Wren is showing me how they bank a fire for the night, raking the coals into a tight heart at the center, ash packed around it like a blanket. "It's not dead," they say. "People think banking a fire is killing it. It's the opposite. You're teaching it to wait." Their hands are black to the wrist. They smell of smoke and rosemary oil, which they swear keeps the sparks off, and which I have never once smelled since without—

—Wren asleep, finally, after a double shift, face slack in a way waking Wren never permitted. I am counting their breaths without meaning to. I think: this is the only arithmetic I will ever need. I think this so hard and so sincerely that it embarrasses me even in memory, and memory-me reaches over and touches their hair anyway—

—"Promise me you'll write things down," Wren says, out of nowhere, over dinner, bread and onions and cheap wine. "You're a scribe. Write me down. Everything. Because—" and here they laugh, but it doesn't land right, "—because I have a feeling this place is going to lose track of me."

I remember laughing back. I remember saying something clever. I do not remember making the promise.

But I made it.

* * *

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I confirmed twice more that afternoon, checking other names, other lines, building the pattern the way you build any case: the Order's ledgers were thorough about everything except existence itself. They recorded what a soul weighed, what it cost, what portion went to which ward. They did not record who the person had been. Names appeared once, at transfer, and were then replaced by asset numbers. The court beneath the mountain didn't buy people. It bought *subtractions*. It bought the removal of a person from the world, and the world, obligingly, forgot.

I checked the public rolls upstairs, the ones families consulted. *Wren Ashvale*: no entry. Not deceased. Not transferred. No entry at all, as though the name had never been spoken into any book anywhere.

Except one book.

That night, in my rented room above the chandler's shop, I pried up the third floorboard from the wall—the loose one, the one I'd told the landlady was rotten—and took out the packet wrapped in oilcloth.

Forty-one pages. Seven years of hidden notes, written in cipher, in the dark, by a man too much of a coward to act and too much of a scribe to forget. Dates. Descriptions. Copies of transfer entries I'd copied illegally, risking my neck for ink. Every letter Wren ever sent me, every scrap of their hand I could salvage. A pressed sprig of rosemary that had long since lost its scent but not its meaning.

Forty-one pages proving that Wren Ashvale existed. That they laughed and banked fires and made promises over cheap wine. That they were loved, specifically, by name, by me.

The only proof in the world.

I sat on the floor with the packet in my lap and understood, with terrible clarity, what I was holding. Not a memorial. A *key*. If the wards failed because the fuel ran out—if the mountain court needed new souls to forge whatever came next—then somewhere in those ledgers was the schedule. The allocations. The *when* and the *where* of the burning. And if Wren's portions were marked *fuel grade: high*, then Wren wasn't finished being used. Wren was inventory. Inventory gets consumed last, when the need is greatest.

And the need, as of three nights ago, was greater than it had ever been.

I should have felt fear. Some part of me noted, distantly, that I ought to. Descending toward the mountain court was not a thing scribes survived; it was barely a thing *anyone* survived. But what I actually felt was older than fear and simpler. It was the feeling from the roof of the ember-house, translated into purpose: *it's not dead. You're teaching it to wait.*

Seven years I had taught it to wait. Forty-one pages of waiting, pressed flat under a rotten floorboard.

I got out fresh paper and began, in cipher, to copy the allocation schedules. The northern line. The reserve stores. The dates. Somewhere in those numbers was a burning hour, and I intended to know it before anyone else did.

I was three pages in when I heard footsteps in the stairwell—heavy ones, official ones, stopping outside my door—and a voice I knew from a hundred chapel announcements say, pleasantly, through the wood:

"Scribe Dunmore. The High Cantor would like a word about your audit."
