## 4. The Debt Collector's Bargain

The High Cantor received me in the counting room, which told me everything before she said a word.

Most visitors to the Order of the Quiet Flame were shown to the chapel, all candle-smoke and hushed reverence, or to the scriptorium with its smell of ink and patience. But Vess had chosen the counting room: a low stone chamber where the Order's ledgers were kept under triple lock and where, on the table between us, a single lamp burned with a flame so steady it looked painted on. No flicker. Nothing wasted. Even the fire here was disciplined.

"Sit, scribe." She didn't look up from the ledger open before her. "You'll want to sit for this."

I sat. Seven years in this place had taught me that when someone tells you to sit, they are about to take something from you, and they'd prefer you not be standing when it happens. Easier to topple a seated man.

"The city burned forty thousand candles last night," Vess said. "Panic-candles, prayer-candles, candles lit by people who don't believe in anything but wanted to hold something warm while the sky refused to exist." She turned the page. "Every one of them is an expense. Every prayer is a debt against order. And you—" now she looked up, and her eyes were the grey of ledger-paper, "—were seen."

I kept my face as blank as a fresh page. "Seen doing what, Cantor?"

"Selling." She let the word hang there like smoke. "In Ashmarket. A charm of finding, I'm told, sold to a child. A charm that *worked*."

"It was a bit of copper and a prayer," I said. "Prayers work sometimes. That's why people keep buying them."

"Don't be clever with me, Dunmore. Cleverness is how debtors talk themselves deeper into holes." She closed the ledger and folded her hands upon it, and I saw that her fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle—the hands of a woman who had spent her life writing down what other people owed. "Here is what will happen. The Order has need of you, and so the usual penalties—confiscation, caning, the quiet room—are set aside. Instead, I am authorized to make you an offer."

"I don't have anything worth offering."

"You have something priceless. You have a *forgetting*."

* * *

She explained it the way one explains arithmetic, which is to say without mercy and with the expectation that the numbers will speak for themselves.

The border wards had not simply failed. They had been fed, and the feeding had stopped. For three hundred years the Order had purchased souls—voluntary, the records insisted, though voluntary is a word that bends easily when the alternative is starvation—and fed them into the ward-stones beneath the mountain. Each soul bought light, law, the slow reliable turning of the world. And each soul, once fed, was struck from every record in the city. Erased from tax rolls, from parish books, from the memories of parish clerks, from family Bibles, from gravestones. The Order did not merely buy souls; it bought the right to say a person had never been.

"There is a soul," Vess said, "designated Asset 4471, currently held in reserve beneath the mountain. It is scheduled for transfer to the new ward-work. Before that transfer occurs, there is a discrepancy in the files. A scribe of this Order has been keeping unauthorized records concerning this asset. Notes. Correspondences. A—a private history." Her voice went dry as chalk on the last words. "This cannot be permitted. An asset with a history is not an asset. It is a person, and persons complicate transactions."

My heart was doing something complicated behind my ribs. "And the offer?"

"The Order forgives your debt. All seven years of it. You walk out of here a free man, registered nowhere, owing nothing." She slid a document across the table. "In exchange, you surrender your notes to be burned, you submit to official forgetting—and you sign, here and here, attesting that Asset 4471 never existed at all."

I looked at the paper. It was beautifully made, vellum, the Order's seal stamped in wax the color of dried blood. Somewhere a craftsman had taken pride in it. Somewhere a clerk had ruled those lines with a straightedge and thought: *this is good work.* That's the thing about institutions. They are never staffed entirely by monsters. Mostly they are staffed by people making beautiful instruments for monstrous purposes.

"And who," I asked, "was Asset 4471?"

Vess's pen paused above the ledger. "That is precisely the sort of question you are being paid to stop asking."

"Wren," I said. Just to hear it in the room. Just to see if the name made any sound at all in that lamp-steady air.

Nothing. Vess blinked once, the way one blinks at dust. The name landed on her and left no mark whatsoever. Three hundred years of practice, and they could erase a person so cleanly that even saying their name aloud produced nothing but a small silence, like a word spoken into snow.

"They volunteered," Vess said. "All assets volunteer."

"Wren couldn't have afforded to eat that winter. Volunteering was the only currency left."

"Then it was a fair transaction." She turned the document toward me and set a pen beside it. "Sign, scribe. Be free. Forget. It is the kindest thing we do."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because it wasn't noble. There was no trumpet-blast of defiance. What happened was smaller and stranger: I looked at the vellum, and I thought about my hidden notes—the ones sewn into the lining of my coat, seven years of them, written in a cipher of my own devising because I could not bear to be the last person in the world who remembered Wren and then forget too.

And I thought: *if I sign this, then no one ever loved them. Not anyone, anywhere, ever again.*

Not Jonah Dunmore loved Wren. Not anyone. The love itself would be unmade, retroactively, tidily, the way the Order unmade everything—with good penmanship.

Seven years ago I had signed myself over to this Order to pay a debt I incurred buying medicine for my mother, who died anyway, because medicine only postpones certain conversations with the dark. I had served faithfully. I had copied ten thousand pages. I had sold charms I pretended were trinkets because some small rebellious ember in me refused to go out entirely. I had done my duty, dutifully, and I had hated every hour of it in the quiet way one hates weather.

And here was the institution that owned me, offering to forgive everything, on the sole condition that I agree to have never loved.

"Duty," I said slowly, "is an interesting word."

"It is the foundation of order."

"Is it? Because I've been thinking about duty lately. About whose duty serves whom." I picked up the pen. Vess's expression did something almost soft—not triumph exactly, but the satisfaction of a fisherman watching the bobber dip. "You know what I've concluded? Duty is like a charm. It works exactly as long as you believe in it, and the moment you stop—"

I drove the pen not into the signature line but straight through the seal, tearing the vellum, and stood up so fast the chair went over backward.

"—it's just wax."

For one heartbeat the counting room was perfectly still. Even the lamp seemed to hold its breath. Then Vess said, very quietly, "Guards," and I learned something about myself in the seconds that followed, namely that seven years of copying documents gives a man surprisingly quick hands and an encyclopedic memory for where things are kept.

The descent-keys hung on a hook behind Vess's own chair, because the High Cantor trusted no subordinate with them and because arrogance always keeps its treasures within arm's reach of its pride. Iron, six of them on a ring, each stamped with a sigil I had spent three years cataloguing during my audit work and two nights memorizing from a stolen rubbing. I took the whole ring. If you're going to commit theft, commit it thoroughly; half-measures are just inefficiency.

Then I ran.

* * *

They would tell it differently later, if they told it at all—that the scribe Jonah Dunmore fled the counting room like a thief, which is what I was, and like an apostate, which is also what I was, and like a lover, which is the part they would never understand and the part that was truest.

I went first to the novices' dormitory, where the children conscripted as ward-witnesses were being held pending assessment. The border wards had failed; the Order needed eyes it controlled at every station; and Sefa—nine years old, sharp-eyed, the same child who had bought my impossible charm two days past—had been registered as a witness because she had been near Ashmarket gate when the sky went out. The Order collected witnesses the way it collected debts: automatically, and without asking.

A novice was supposed to be watching them. The novice was asleep, which I chose to interpret as providence rather than poor discipline.

"Sefa," I whispered, and she woke instantly, the way children do when they've learned that sleep is a thing adults take advantage of. Her eyes found mine in the dark and narrowed.

"You're the charm man."

"I am. I need you to come with me."

"Am I in trouble?"

"Yes," I said honestly. "But less trouble with me than without me. The Order is about to decide that some people never existed, and you've seen too much to be safe."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, which should have frightened me more than it did. Then: "Did you steal something?"

"Why?"

"Because you're holding keys and looking guilty. Grown-ups only look guilty like that when they stole something or broke something. Sometimes both."

"Keys," I admitted. "Iron ones. To a door under the mountain."

Her eyes went wide, and then—this is the part I remember most clearly, the part I would trade much to forget—her face went strange, unfocused, as if she were listening to a voice from another room. When she came back to herself she said, quietly:

"There's a stair with teeth. I dreamed it. Except it isn't a dream, is it."

"No," I said, because lying to this child suddenly seemed both pointless and dangerous. "I don't think it is."

"Then we should hurry," said Sefa, nine years old, taking my hand, "before they remember we're gone."

* * *

We went out through the ash-gate, past the candle-shrines where the panic of the starless night had not yet burned down, past beggars and vigil-keepers and one drunk singing a song about the dark with impressive commitment. Behind us, somewhere in the Order's halls, alarm bells had begun—I heard their first thin note as we crossed the square, and then a second bell answering, and a third. The Quiet Flame was waking up, and it would be neither quiet nor merciful.

Ahead of us lay the collapsed ward-line, the scar in the world where the mountain's throat opened onto stairs that ate sound and memory. Ahead of us lay a court that traded in souls, magistrates in masks, and Wren—Asset 4471, portioned out like bread, waiting in the dark to be burned.

Sefa squeezed my hand. "Whoever we're going to save," she said, "you love them. Don't you."

It wasn't a question. Children ask questions; this was a diagnosis.

"Yes," I said. "More than my name."

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"In my experience," I said, tightening my grip on the iron keys as the bells rang louder and the dark mouth of the mountain rose up before us, "those turn out to be the same thing."

The stair swallowed our footsteps, and then our echoes, and then, one by one, the reasons I had ever obeyed.
