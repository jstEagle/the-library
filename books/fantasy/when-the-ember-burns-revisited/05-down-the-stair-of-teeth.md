## 5. Down the Stair of Teeth

**Jonah**

The ward-line had not collapsed so much as it had *unzipped*. That was the image that kept coming back to me as Sefa and I picked our way through the wreckage of the border checkpoint: a seam torn open down the length of the world, and everything that should have been held in—wind, light, law—spilling out through it.

Officially, I was a scribe of the Order of the Quiet Flame, conscripted to assess damage and file a report no one would read. Unofficially, I was a thief with two descent-keys sewn into my coat lining and a child who had seen me steal them.

"You're doing the face again," Sefa said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding whether to lie to me."

I looked at her. Nine years old, ash on her cheeks from the panic-fires, carrying a charm I'd sold her for three coppers—a charm that shouldn't have worked, and had. In Ashmarket terms, that made her an accomplice already. The Order didn't distinguish between sellers and buyers when they burned ledgers.

"I'm deciding whether to tell you the truth," I said, "which is worse."

"Tell me anyway."

So I did—or the shape of it. That the wards were fed by souls. That souls were bought by something beneath Mount Cerenne. That among them, listed in a ledger under *asset, sold*, was a person whose name I had spent seven years keeping alive in hidden notes because every other record of them had been scraped clean.

"Wren," Sefa said, trying the name out.

"Wren."

"And you're going to steal them back."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounds like the charms," she said. "The ones that work when they shouldn't." She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, the kind children learn too early. "I believe in things that work when they shouldn't."

* * *

**Sefa**

The stair went down farther than stairs should.

That's what nobody tells you about mountains: they're mostly inside. The outside is just the part that got left over after all the dark was packed in. Jonah said the old name for this passage was the Stair of Teeth, because the walls jutted out like jaws, and because once you started down, the mountain chewed slowly.

It ate sound first. I noticed it after the hundredth step or so—our footsteps going soft, then softer, like the stone was drinking them. When Jonah spoke, their voice came out flat and close, even though their mouth was a body-length away. Shouting did nothing. The mountain took loudness and gave back nothing.

Then it started eating other things.

I knew it was happening because of the smell. There was a smell I knew from home—my mother's bread, the burnt-bottom kind she always swore was intentional—and suddenly I smelled it here, in the cold dark, seeping out of the rock like the rock remembered it. Except the rock couldn't remember it. It wasn't the rock's memory.

"Jonah," I whispered. My whisper sounded like it was being read off a page. "I can smell my mum's bread."

Jonah stopped. Held up the little lamp they'd brought, the one burning ember-oil that cost more than I'd ever see in my life. Their face did the deciding-whether-to-lie thing again.

"The corridors eat memory," they said. "Old records say so. They take it out of you as you pass."

"But it's not mine leaving. It's—" I pressed my hand against the wall. Warm. Warm like an oven door. "It's mine coming *in*."

* * *

**Jonah**

This was the part I hadn't put in my report to myself, the private ledger I kept behind my eyes: I had read about the Stair of Teeth in a forbidden commentary, twenty years ago, when I was young enough to think forbidden meant *interesting* rather than *fatal*. The commentator wrote that the mountain's throat was lined with residue—that everything the court beneath had ever swallowed left a film on the walls, and travelers who walked the Stair came out wearing other people's lives like borrowed coats.

I had assumed it was allegory. Commentators loved allegory. It let them be wrong beautifully.

But Sefa stopped dead on the four-hundredth step and said, in a voice that was not entirely hers: *"Third bell. Wren, bank the embers, third bell means the wardens walk the row."*

My whole body went cold.

"That's a keeper's call," I said. "That's from the ember-house. That's—" *Wren's voice. Wren's cadence. Words Wren used to say.* "Sefa. Look at me. What did you just say?"

She blinked up at me, and she was nine years old again, frightened, holding the lamp I'd asked her to carry because I needed my hands free. "I don't know. It just came out. Like a burp but with words." Her chin wobbled. "Jonah, am I sick?"

"No." I crouched to her height, which also put me closer to the warm, breathing wall, and I hated the wall for how much I wanted to press my ear against it. "You're a chimney," I said. "Things are passing through you. They'll keep passing. You hold onto yourself and let the rest go through. Can you do that?"

"How do I know which one is me?"

"You're the one who's scared," I said. "The memories aren't scared. They've already happened."

She thought about that with her whole face, the way children do, and then nodded once, businesslike, and we kept descending.

I did not tell her that the words she'd burped had cracked something open in me that seven years of careful ledger-work had sealed shut. I did not tell her that hearing Wren's phrase in a child's mouth was the closest I'd been to Wren since the night the records went blank. I filed it instead, in the hidden place, under *evidence*: the mountain still held them. The mountain leaked them.

Leaking things could be found.

* * *

**Sefa**

We slept in a niche where the teeth of the wall curled back like a hand opening. Jonah gave me the good blanket and took the scratchy one, which I pretended not to notice, because noticing would make it a thing and then they'd stop doing it.

In the dark, without the lamp, the corridor glowed faintly. Not light exactly. More like the memory of light, the way you still see the sun if you squeeze your eyes shut in summer. And in that glow, I dreamed—not my dreams. Someone else's.

A room full of small fires in clay cups. Hands cupping each flame like water. A voice saying, patient, over and over: *smaller. Make it smaller. An ember isn't a fire, it's a promise you keep all night.*

I woke up knowing how to bank a fire. I had never banked a fire in my life. Our stove at home was gas.

"Jonah." I shook them. They came awake fast, hand already at the knife they thought I didn't know about. "I know how to do the ember thing. The keeping. I dreamed it."

Jonah sat up very slowly. In the glow-light their face looked older and younger at the same time, the way faces do when hope and fear fight to a draw.

"What else do you know?" they asked quietly.

"How to read the burn-marks on charcoal. Which woods hold heat longest. A song." I hummed it before I could stop myself—four notes, round and low—and Jonah made a sound like someone had pressed on a bruise.

"That's Wren's song," they said. "Nobody's sung that in seven years. Nobody *could*. It was erased."

"Then why do I know it?"

Jonah looked down the stair, into the breathing dark, and I watched them decide something. Not whether to lie—I'd gotten good at telling. Whether to hope. Hoping is scarier than lying. Lying costs nothing if it fails.

"Because the mountain remembers what everyone else was made to forget," they said. "And it's showing us where to dig."

* * *

**Jonah**

By the second day, Sefa was surfacing fragments faster than I could record them, and I recorded everything—scratching notes by ember-light in the margins of my false assessment report, which had become the truest document I'd ever written. Keeper's prayers. The layout of the ember-house. The names of three wardens and a dog named Bristle. The particular way Wren laughed when a spell worked on the first try—*like a hiccup with triumph in it*, Sefa said, and demonstrated, and I had to turn away and pretend to check the descent-keys for the fourth time that hour.

The keys themselves worried me. Two iron tokens, warm as coins from a pocket, etched with the Quiet Flame's sigil. High Cantor Vess had kept them in a warded case, which meant they mattered, which meant using them would announce us somewhere. Nothing stolen stays quiet forever. But there was no other way down; the court did not build doors for people it hadn't invited.

On the third day the stair ended.

Not gradually. One step there was stone, and the next step there was nothing—a threshold where the corridor simply stopped pretending to be a corridor. Beyond it lay a cavern so large the ember-lamp couldn't find its ceiling or its far wall, and rising out of that darkness, a structure: pale spires, terraces, bridges arcing between towers like frozen lightning. The Court Beneath. It was beautiful the way a jaw is beautiful—all architecture in service of closing.

And above its highest tower, faint but unmistakable, hung a light. Small. Steady. Ember-colored.

Sefa saw it too. She grabbed my sleeve.

"That's a keeper-fire," she said, in the borrowed voice again, or maybe not borrowed anymore—maybe learned, the way you learn a song. "Somebody's keeping it. Somebody's been keeping it a long time."

Seven years, I thought. Seven years, banked small, kept alive, a promise held all night in a place with no morning.

I touched the descent-keys through my coat and felt them pulse, once, like something on the far side had felt us arriving.

"They know we're here," I said.

Below us, across the black floor of the cavern, doors began to open along the court's lowest terrace—rows of them, in perfect silence, spilling out light the color of a held breath.

"Stay behind me," I told Sefa. "And whatever you remember down there—hold onto the scared one. Hold onto you."

She nodded. Then, softly, in her own voice, with her own nine-year-old fear and her own impossible faith: "The charms work when they shouldn't."

"Yes," I said, and stepped out of the mountain's throat, into the court that fed on forgotten names. "Let's go be something that works when it shouldn't."
