## 6. The Court Beneath

The stair stopped pretending to be a stair about an hour after Sefa stopped counting the steps, which was roughly when her knees filed a formal complaint and her feet threatened to unionize.

"Are we there yet?" she asked, because someone had to say it, and Jonah had gone quiet in the way that made her nervous—the way grown-ups went quiet when they were rehearsing something terrible in their heads.

"Almost," Jonah said. "Probably. The maps say—"

"You don't have a map."

"I have a very strong feeling."

"Your feelings got us chased by a corridor that ate my left shoe."

"That corridor was *hungry*, Sefa. It wasn't personal." Jonah held up a hand, and she saw it: light ahead, but wrong light—not torchlight, not lamplight, but a pale gold glow like the inside of a honey jar, seeping up from below through seams in the stone. And with it came sound. After hours of corridors that swallowed every footstep before it landed, sound itself felt like a gift. Voices. Many voices. A hum of transaction.

And beneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something singing.

Sefa's chest went tight. She knew that song. She'd never heard it before in her life, and she knew it the way you know your own name—except lately she didn't trust herself on names either, because for three days now she'd been remembering things that had never happened to her. A woman braiding rope on a dock. A dog named Turnip. Snow falling upward. She kept them in a list in her head, her not-memories, because Jonah said lists were armor.

"What is that singing?" she whispered.

Jonah's face did something complicated. "I don't know," they said, which Sefa had learned was scribe-language for *I know exactly what it is and I would rather swallow my own tongue.*

They descended the last stretch together, and then the mountain opened its throat all the way, and Sefa forgot how to breathe.

* * *

It was a city. That was the first horror of it—that it wasn't a cave or a lair or anything so honest as a monster's den. It was a *city*, carved into a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, lit by that same honey-gold glow rising from ten thousand small flames set in niches along streets of black stone. Bridges arched over canals where the water ran slow and silver and full of faces—if you looked too long at the water, faces looked back, mouths moving, saying things no one above ground had ever bothered to hear again.

And everywhere, everywhere, the market.

Stalls lined the avenues the way stalls line Ashmarket, except here they sold no charms against warts and no luck-knots for fishermen. They sold *pieces*. A vendor with a voice like warm oil held up a vial glowing faint blue: "Courage of a drowned sailor, barely used!" Another spread out shards on velvet like a jeweler: fragments of memory, Sefa realized, each one flickering with somebody's wedding day, somebody's mother's kitchen, somebody's last words.

"They're souls," Sefa breathed. "They're selling *souls*."

"Pieces of them," Jonah murmured. "That's worse."

Above the market, on a terrace of white stone, sat the court: seven magistrates behind a long desk of black glass, each masked in porcelain so smooth it had no expression at all, only suggestions—a curve like a smile, a hollow like grief—and none of them matching the hands that moved beneath, sorting ledgers with terrible gentleness.

A queue stretched toward them. Not a queue of petitioners. A queue of *goods*. Hooded figures presenting trays, and the magistrates weighing souls on scales that clicked like beetles.

"We need an identity," Jonah said quickly, pulling Sefa into the shadow of a buttress shaped like a rib. "Before anyone looks at us twice. Down here, being nobody is the most dangerous thing you can be. Everyone must belong to someone."

"So who are we?"

Jonah pressed two fingers to their temple, thinking, and Sefa watched them build a lie the way she'd watched masons build walls—one stone at a time, load-bearing first. "We are Vellin Ostra, factor of minor assets, come from the surface branch to audit the ward-fuel inventory. And this—" a pause, a flicker of guilt so fast Sefa almost missed it, "—is my apprentice."

"I'm not wearing a hood?"

"You're wearing a hood." Jonah produced one from their pack, gray and itchy-looking. "Apprentices are furniture, Sefa. Furniture sees everything and gets asked nothing. It's the best disguise there is."

She pulled the hood on, sulking only a little. "What if I'm bad at being furniture?"

"Then be a table with opinions. Quietly."

* * *

The queue for the magistrates took two hours, which Sefa spent cataloguing not-memories (a red door; a lullaby in a language with no word for goodbye) and watching Jonah rehearse under their breath. *Vellin Ostra. Factor. Minor assets. The surface branch sends its regards.* Their voice changed when they said it—flatter, colder, a voice Sefa hadn't met before, and she decided she didn't like it, even though it wasn't real.

When their turn came, the magistrate in the center mask tilted its head. The porcelain smile-curve caught the gold light.

"Vellin Ostra," the magistrate read from nowhere, from everywhere. "Surface branch. We were not told to expect you."

"The border wards failed," Jonah said, in that flat cold voice. "The surface branch thought your records might explain why. I'm here to inspect the fuel inventory. Specifically the core-shards earmarked for the new ward."

Silence. Long enough that Sefa's heart tried to climb out through her ears. Then the magistrate made a sound like paper turning.

"Auditors," it said, with something that might have been contempt or hunger. "You people always come after the collapse. Never before. Very well. The inventory is sealed until the Burning Hour. You may review the manifests." A pause. "You will find the accounting *impeccable*."

"I'm sure I'll find it horrifying," Jonah said. "That's rather the point of audits."

Something in the court laughed—not the magistrates, but the market, a ripple of vendor-chuckles, and Sefa understood suddenly that everyone down here was listening, always, and that Jonah had just done something either very brave or very stupid by making them laugh.

The magistrate gestured. A clerk-thing—gray, many-jointed, faceless—appeared with a ledger the size of a door and dropped it at Jonah's feet.

"Fuel manifest," it said. "Core-shards, portioned and pending. Sign for receipt of viewing."

Jonah signed. Sefa watched the name *Vellin Ostra* flow off the pen, and thought: *that's a person who doesn't exist signing for a person who does exist to steal a person who isn't allowed to exist anymore.* Down here, that was probably just paperwork.

* * *

They carried the ledger to an alcove off the terrace, and Jonah opened it, and Sefa watched a person come apart quietly.

At first it was fine. Jonah's finger ran down columns—shard numbers, weights, burn-rates—with professional steadiness. Then the finger stopped. Then it started trembling, very slightly, the way a candle flame trembles when someone walks past without meaning to disturb it.

"Sefa," Jonah said. Their voice had lost the cold flatness entirely. "Look at entry forty-one."

She looked. The column read:

*Shard 41 — designation: J.D. — content: childhood, river, mother's voice, 4 lbs. 2 oz. — status: SOLD — purchaser: Order of the Quiet Flame, annuity.*

"J.D.," Sefa said slowly. "Like—"

"Like me." Jonah's voice was very small for someone speaking in a cavern built for gods. "I sold pieces. Years ago. To pay down the debt. I thought—I told myself it was memories I wouldn't miss. My mother's voice. The river we swam in. Small things. *Harmless* things." A breath that shook on the way out. "They weren't harmless. They were mine. And the Order bought them, and the Order fed them to the wards, and I've been walking around for years missing something I couldn't name, telling myself it was just tiredness."

Sefa didn't know what to say, so she did what Jonah had taught her: she made a list. "Turnip," she said. "Red door. Snow going up. Your mother's voice."

Jonah looked at her—really looked, hood and all—and something passed between them that Sefa didn't have words for yet, though she suspected she'd remember it forever, if forever was still on the table.

"You keep my missing pieces," Jonah said wonderingly. "Somehow. This whole time."

"Am I supposed to give them back?"

"Not yet." Jonah closed the ledger gently, like closing a coffin. "First we find shard sixty-three."

Sefa found it before Jonah did—her eyes went straight to it, pulled like a fish on a line:

*Shard 63 — designation: W. — content: core-self, love, laughter, the naming of small true things — status: RESERVED — purpose: ignition, new ward, Burning Hour.*

Below it, in ink that gleamed wetly as though freshly written:

*Burning Hour commences at dawn.*

Dawn. Which down here meant whenever the magistrates decided it did, which meant soon, which meant—

"Jonah," Sefa whispered, and pointed past the alcove, down into the market, where clerks were lighting lamps along a processional route, where vendors were packing up their soul-shards with the brisk efficiency of people clearing a square before an execution, where the singing—that song Sefa had known without knowing—was growing louder, closer, coming up the great avenue in a procession of gray hooded figures carrying a casket of glass.

Inside the casket, something glowed like a coal refusing to die.

And beside the casket walked a figure Sefa recognized from nowhere and everywhere—from a red door, from snow falling upward, from a lullaby with no word for goodbye—and the figure turned its head, scanning the crowd with eyes like banked embers, and for one impossible moment those eyes found hers through the hood, and the singing stopped.

The figure frowned, as if trying to remember something it had been promised it never needed to remember again.

Then the procession moved on, toward the furnace, and Sefa grabbed Jonah's sleeve with both hands.

"It's Wren," she said. "Jonah—it's *Wren*, and they're taking them to burn."
