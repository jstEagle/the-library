## 7. Ash Where the Heart Was

The court beneath the mountain had a smell I wasn't prepared for.

I'd expected brimstone, or rot, or some theatrical incense designed by a magistrate with too much budget and too little taste. Instead it smelled like a library that had been left out in the rain—damp vellum, old glue, the faint char of pages burned to keep a fire going. Somewhere in the dark ahead of us, ink was drying on ledgers that recorded every soul in this place as a line item, and the mountain itself smelled like the paperwork of its own cruelty.

Sefa walked close enough behind me that I could feel her breath on my neck. She hadn't spoken in an hour. The corridors had been eating her memories the way they ate sound—she'd told me her mother's name three times before we stopped counting—and each time she came back from wherever the stone took her, she looked a little older. Not in years. In the way a soldier looks older after one bad night.

"There," she whispered finally, pointing down a corridor where the walls were veined with something pale and slow-moving, like light trapped under skin. "That's where the humming comes from."

She was right. Beneath the silence the stair had imposed on us, there was a hum—a low, patient drone, like a hive thinking about winter. It got into my teeth. It got into the space behind my eyes where I kept the things I refused to write down.

I had come down a mountain's throat, past wards built from sold souls, carrying forged papers and a stolen key-ring, to find one particular soul among thousands. And now that I was here, now that the hum was in my teeth, I discovered I had no idea what I was going to say.

*Hello. You don't know me. Seven years ago you held my hands over a candle flame to teach me how ember-glass bends light, and you laughed when I burned my sleeve, and then you sold your soul to feed a border ward because someone had to, and the Order erased you so thoroughly that even your grave-marker reads as a blank.* That seemed like a lot for a first sentence.

We followed the pale veins until the corridor opened into a gallery, and I stopped being able to think in sentences at all.

* * *

It was a workroom. That was the horror of it—it was so ordinary. Long tables under lamps that burned without flame. Scribes at the tables, masked like the magistrates above but with simpler masks, plain porcelain things, working. And along one wall, shelved floor to ceiling in niches cut into the living rock: embers.

Not coals. Souls rendered down to ember-form, each one glowing in its niche with a color all its own. Some burned white-hot and furious. Some had dimmed to a sullen orange, barely holding. A few were grey already, ash waiting to be swept, and beside those niches the shelves stood empty—the fuel spent, the records closed. Each niche bore a small brass plate with a number. No names. Names were the first thing this place took.

Sefa made a small sound beside me, and I realized she was crying without any change in her expression. "They're people," she said. "They're *people* and they're *shelved*."

"I know," I said.

"No, I mean—" She pressed her fist against her mouth. "I remember one of them. The blue one, third row. I remember his wedding. I never had a wedding. I've never been married." She stared at me with the terrible clarity the corridors had been lending her. "Jonah, the wards didn't just take their souls. They're leaking. Everything they were is leaking out through the stone, and the mountain is *drinking*."

Which meant that everything Wren had ever been—every joke, every argument, every morning—was soaked into these walls somewhere, available to a child who'd never met them, and gone from the person who'd lived them.

I found Wren by accident, which is to say I found Wren because part of me had known the way the whole time and simply hadn't told the rest of me. My feet turned left where I would have turned right. My hand rose toward a niche two rows up, third from the end, and only then did I look at what hung there.

A brass plate: **ASSET 4471. STATUS: PORTIONED.**

And below it, in a clerk's tidy hand, the word that undid seven years of careful grief: *reserved.*

"Reserved," I said aloud, because if I said it, it couldn't be a hallucination. "Sefa. It says reserved."

She climbed onto the shelf-ladder to look. "That's good, isn't it? Reserved means they're keeping it?"

"It means they haven't spent all of it yet." My voice came out flat and clerical, the voice I used for inventory. It was either that or scream in a room full of listening masks. "Portioned means they've already—"

I stopped. Because I understood then what portioned meant, and the understanding arrived not as a thought but as a physical event, a cold hand closing around the back of my ribs. They hadn't taken Wren's soul whole. They'd carved it. Sold pieces of it off to the border wards one contract at a time—*this shard for the eastern watchtower, this shard for the river crossing, this shard for the festival lights the year the harvest failed*—and what remained in the niche was what was left over. The rind. The pit. Whatever part of a person is too small or too stubborn to burn efficiently.

The ember in the niche was the size of my thumbnail. It glowed a green-gold I knew better than my own handwriting.

"They're going to use it," Sefa said quietly. She'd read the plate too; the child missed nothing anymore, having traded most of herself for the sight. "For the new ward. The big one. That's why it's reserved—they need it for the ignition. It's a good ember. Strong."

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Because I remember being strong." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Some of this one leaked into the stairs while we walked. It's warm, Jonah. Everything else down here is cold, but the pieces of this one are *warm*."

I put my palm against the niche's lip, and she was right. Warmth came through the stone like a hand returning a grip. Seven years I'd carried Wren's memory alone, the last archive of a person the world had agreed to forget, and here the mountain had been doing the same thing in its own way—hoarding the heat, letting it seep into the walls, keeping it without understanding it.

"You're real," I told the ember. It was a stupid thing to say to a thumbnail-sized scrap of someone. "You're real, and I'm getting you out, and I don't care what it costs, and I'm sorry it took me seven years."

The ember did nothing. Embers are famously bad conversationalists. But the warmth pulsed once against my palm, slow, like something turning over in sleep, and I chose—with full knowledge that I was choosing, that hope is a decision you make and keep remaking—to believe it heard.

* * *

The problem, as ever, was paperwork.

Wren's remainder sat in a locked vitrine behind glass etched with ward-script, and the vitrine answered to a ledger, and the ledger answered to a clerk, and the clerk was currently ten tables away, mask tilted down, copying numbers with the serene industry of a man who has never once wondered what the numbers are.

I am also a scribe. This is the joke the universe keeps telling: it made me good at exactly the thing that damns me.

I walked to the table with the gait I'd used for seven years in Ashmarket—the walk of a man whose business is duller than he is—and set my forged credentials on the desk. "Audit from the surface chapter," I said. "Order of the Quiet Flame. We're reconciling pre-collapse asset registers against current holdings. Your section's flagged."

The clerk looked up. Porcelain face, eye-holes showing nothing but lamplight. For three heartbeats I felt certain he could see straight through the forgery, through my skin, through to the ember-warmth still ghosting my palm, and that guards were already coming.

Then he sighed—the sigh of middle management everywhere—and turned his ledger around. "Reconcile fast. The Ignition Commission has priority access starting at the burning hour, and if your figures disagree with theirs, that's a dispute between chapters, not my problem."

"The burning hour being...?"

"When it stops being a reserve and starts being a ward. Midnight tomorrow, surface reckoning." He tapped the entry with one dry finger. **ASSET 4471 — REMAINDER — ALLOCATED: NEW BORDER WARD, PRIME SHARD.** "Strong material. Rare, honestly. Most of them fragment fine but this one holds coherence even portioned. The Commission was pleased."

*Holds coherence.* As if Wren's stubbornness—Wren, who argued with me about star-maps for six years and never once conceded a point they secretly knew they'd lost—was a quality worth noting on an invoice.

I copied figures. My hands were steady, which I will mention because I am still proud of it. I copied figures and asked dull questions and received dull answers, and underneath the clerical performance I was doing arithmetic of a different kind: one remainder, one burning hour, one chance between now and midnight tomorrow, and a court full of magistrates who would burn a thief's soul as readily as they'd burn anyone's.

There was a second problem, and it was worse than the paperwork, and I only saw it when I finally stood at the vitrine with the release-chit in my hand and the lock yielding to the stolen keys, and looked—not at the ember, but at the small inspection mirror mounted beside the niche so clerks could check the asset's condition without opening the case.

In the mirror I saw the far side of the gallery. And standing at a worktable there, sleeves rolled, copying ward-calculations in a neat left-slanted hand I would have known blindfolded across a burning city, was a person.

Alive. Functional. Upright and employed and breathing, with a face I had kissed in a bell-tower and an expression of mild concentration I had loved more than most people love anything. Hollowed out behind the eyes like a lamp with the wick pulled—but lit anyway, running anyway, the way a body runs when the soul has been sold off in installments and only habit remains to hold the shape.

Wren.

Here. Not shelved, not ash—*here*, doing inventory for the thing that ate them.

Sefa's hand found mine and gripped hard. "Is that—"

"Yes," I said.

"They don't look like a stranger."

"They aren't one." My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. There was no one down here worth performing steadiness for except the masks, and the masks had seen everything already; the masks had *invented* everything already.

I should have run then. I want to be honest about that, whoever ends up reading this: every sane calculation said run. Take the ember, take the child, take the keys, get up the Stair of Teeth before the burning hour, and count myself miraculous. The person at that table was a remainder themselves—what was left when the court was done—and restoring them might be impossible, might cost more than I had, might give me back a stranger wearing a beloved face who would look at me the way the clerk looked at figures.

But I had spent seven years being the last person who remembered, and I had learned something in that time that no ledger records: a person is not their memories the way a fire is not its fuel. Take the fuel away and the fire goes out, yes. But the *shape* of it—the way it leaned, the way it reached, the direction it always burned—that was still standing at that table, still writing left-slanted, still rolling its sleeves the same way.

You do not leave the shape of the person you love doing inventory in hell.

"Wren," I said.

Nothing. The pen moved. The lamp hummed.

"Wren. It's Jonah."

The pen paused—one heartbeat, two—then continued. Habit, answering where the person couldn't. But the pen had paused, and I was going to build a cathedral on that pause if I had to.

Sefa tugged my sleeve and pointed, and my chest went cold, because down the gallery corridor came three magistrates in their high lacquered masks, moving with the unhurried purpose of men checking on something valuable, and the nearest of them carried a case lined with velvet, sized precisely for a thumbnail of green-gold fire.

The burning hour had come early.

Behind us, in the inspection mirror, the pen stopped writing altogether—and Wren's head began, slowly, to turn.
