## 8. The Burning Hour

**Jonah**

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a court commits an atrocity. I'd imagined it would be thunderous—drums, chanting, the scrape of a thousand masks turning toward the furnace. Instead it was the quiet of a counting house. Ledgers open. Quills moving. Somewhere below us, a machine breathing.

Sefa pressed against my side in the shadow of a buttress shaped like a molar. "They're not even looking at it," she whispered. "It's just *inventory* to them."

"That's how you know it's bad," I said. "Nobody chants over inventory."

Below us, the Court of the Quiet Flame—yes, *our* Quiet Flame, the Order's founding myth made flesh and apparently made monstrous—had arranged its remaining soul-shards on iron trays like loaves waiting for an oven. Hundreds of them. Each one a dull amber coal, each one humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after you've told everyone to stop listening.

And on the third tray, nearest the furnace mouth, sat a shard that did not hum. It *pulsed*. Slowly. Stubbornly. Like a heart that had been told for seven years to keep time with someone else's drum and had never quite obeyed.

Wren.

I had found them an hour ago in the ash-gallery: alive, upright, sweeping cinders with the patient competence of someone who had done nothing else for years. Their eyes had passed over me the way eyes pass over furniture. When I said their name, they'd frowned—not with recognition, but with the mild irritation of a man interrupted mid-task—and asked if I required assistance finding the exit.

*Assistance finding the exit.* As if I were the lost one. Which, standing there with my heart coming apart in my chest, I suppose I was.

Now the magistrates gathered at the furnace dais, and High Cantor Vess stood among them—Vess, who had offered me absolution in exchange for forgetting, whose descent-keys hung from my belt like stolen teeth. He wore his mask now, bone-white and serene, and he was saying something I couldn't hear over the furnace's breath. But I could read the shape of it in the way the other magistrates inclined their heads.

Ignition at the burning hour. Midnight above; midnight below; the hour when the old wards had died and the new one would be born already hungry.

"They'll light it all at once," Sefa breathed. She'd been remembering things she never lived all the way down—the mountain teaching her, or using her, we still didn't know which—and her voice had acquired a terrible fluency in Court matters. "All the shards together. That's how you forge a ward that doesn't ask permission. You burn so many names at once that the sky can't tell them apart anymore. It just... obeys."

"Can it be stopped?"

She looked at me. Thirteen years old, hollow-eyed, carrying memories of dead strangers behind her face. "Everything can be stopped. The question is what stops with it."

I looked at the trays. At the pulsing shard. At Wren, somewhere in the ash-galleries below, sweeping cinders from a floor that would soon be the foundation of a tyranny.

"I don't care what stops with it," I said, and discovered I meant it, which frightened me more than the furnace did.

* * *

**Sefa**

The furnace room smelled like the inside of a candle. Not burning—*about* to burn, the waxy hush of a thing holding its breath. Sefa had never liked candles since the night the stars went out. Now she understood why. Candles were just small furnaces, and furnaces were where names went when nobody wrote them down anymore.

Jonah moved along the maintenance channel like a man who had spent seven years sneaking around temples, which, Sefa supposed, he had. He kept muttering things under his breath—"left at the flue, mind the drip, Vess always was too proud to fix the drainage"—and she realized he wasn't praying. He was doing ledger-work. Reciting the building the way he recited debts.

"You've been here before," she whispered.

"No." A pause. "But I've read everything the Order ever stole from here. Seven years of filing. Turns out filing is just burglary you do sitting down."

They came out onto a gantry above the furnace floor. Below, the trays waited. Magistrates in their bone masks stood in a ring, and at the center, a figure Sefa recognized from memories that weren't hers—a Furnace-Warden, robed in grey, hands folded, waiting for the word.

Jonah's hand found hers. His palm was sweating. Somehow that helped. Gods and monsters were frightening; sweating scribes were manageable.

"The sabotage point," he murmured. "The slag-channel. If the drainage fails—if water gets into the firebox at ignition—the flame gutters. Burns uneven. A ward forged uneven won't hold the border. It'll hold *nothing*."

"And then?"

"Then we grab Wren's core-shard and run very fast and hope very hard."

"That's your plan?"

"It's a seven-year plan," Jonah said. "I'm compressing the timeline."

She almost laughed. Almost. Then the great bell beneath the mountain began to toll, and every mask below turned upward at once, and Sefa felt the memories that weren't hers rise up screaming, because she knew this moment. She had *been* this moment, once, in someone else's life—a soul-shard watching the furnace doors open, understanding, in the last amber second, exactly what was about to happen and exactly how little anyone cared.

"Go," she said, shoving him toward the channel. "I'll watch the ring. Go *now*, before I remember anything else sad."

* * *

**Jonah**

The slag-channel was exactly as the stolen schematics promised: narrow, wet, and lined with a residue that ate the light off my lamp until I was crawling by touch and memory. Appropriate. Half this mountain ran on memory; the other half ran on the absence of it.

Behind me, the bell finished tolling. Voices rose below—liturgy, or accounting, with this court the two were indistinguishable. I heard Vess's voice lift above the rest, smooth as oiled paper:

*"By flame, remembrance. By remembrance, law. What is burned cannot argue."*

What is burned cannot argue. There it was. The whole philosophy in one line. The Order taught it as comfort—grief fades, pain passes. Down here they'd simply stopped pretending it was a kindness.

I reached the junction marked on the schematic as SLUICE 9 and found the wheel. Rusted, naturally. Everything down here was rusted or beautiful, nothing in between. I put both hands on it and thought, absurdly, of Ashmarket, of the child's charm that shouldn't have worked and did, of the small illegal warmth of magic leaking through the cracks of a lawful world.

Maybe that was the whole problem. Maybe wards built on burned souls held too well. Maybe the world needed more leaks.

I threw my weight against the wheel.

It gave with a shriek that the sound-eating corridors mercifully swallowed, and the sluice-gate dropped, and the water I'd been hearing drip for an hour became a flood—black, freezing, mountain-cold, pouring down the channel and into the firebox vents in a roaring sheet.

Below, the liturgy stumbled.

The furnace screamed—a sound like a name being pronounced wrong—and the amber glow through the grate guttered, steadied, guttered again. Steam erupted in white geysers. And in the sudden chaos, every masked head turned toward the drowning fire, which meant no masked head turned toward the scribe dropping from the gantry onto the tray-platform, landing hard enough to rattle every soul-shard on the iron like dice in a cup.

I grabbed the third tray's pulsing shard.

Warm. That was the first thing. After seven years of cold ledgers and colder nights, it was warm, and it pulsed against my palm like something that remembered being held.

"Wren," I said, out loud, stupidly, to a lump of amber. "Hold on. I'm terrible at rescues but I'm excellent at paperwork, and I have *plans*."

"Plans," said a voice behind me, "are subject to revision."

Vess stood at the end of the platform, mask gone, face bare, and somehow that was worse. Behind him, four magistrates, their bone faces streaming with steam. Beyond them, the furnace choked and drowned and the whole court's grand ignition sputtered toward farce—but none of them were looking at the furnace anymore.

They were looking at me.

"High Cantor," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You're late for your own apocalypse."

"You flooded it." He sounded less angry than *fascinated*, which from Vess was infinitely more dangerous. "A debt-scribe. A clerk. You walked into the court beneath the world and broke its furnace with plumbing." He stepped forward. The magistrates fanned out along the platform edges, cutting off the gantry, the stairs, the ash-gallery beyond. "Give me the shard, Jonah Dunmore, and I will still honor my offer. Your debt erased. Your notes surrendered. A clean ledger. You could forget all of this by morning."

"You keep offering to let me forget," I said. I backed toward the platform's edge, the shard pressed to my chest, warm as a sleeping bird. "Don't you understand? Forgetting is the *furnace*. You're not selling mercy. You're selling fuel."

"Everything is fuel," Vess said gently. "That is the first law. Even defiance. Especially love—it burns brightest of all. Why do you think we kept *his* shard for the core? Sentiment has caloric value, scribe. We are counting on yours."

And that was when I understood the trap inside the trap. They hadn't left the core-shard reachable by accident. The whole night—the keys, the descent, the flooded furnace—they had let me come, because a rescue was worth ten surrenders. Because a man who fights for something will pay anything for it, and the court's price was never coin.

The magistrates closed in, unhurried, steam curling around their bone masks. Sefa shouted something from the gantry—I couldn't make out the words over the dying furnace. The shard pulsed against my heart, once, twice, stubborn as ever.

Seven years of ledgers, and it came down to this: one exit, four magistrates, and a price I already knew I would pay.

"All right," I said, and lifted the shard like a lantern. "Let's talk terms."
