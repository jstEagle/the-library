## 9. A Name Given Back

The furnace was still screaming when I understood the shape of the trap.

Not a trap in the ordinary sense—no portcullis dropping, no masked magistrate rising from the floor with a writ in one hand and a knife in the other. The magistrates were doing something worse. They were being *reasonable*.

"An exchange has been made," said the one called Ilvane, her porcelain mask tilted at an angle that suggested either curiosity or contempt. With those masks it was impossible to tell. That was rather the point of them. "A core-shard of the ember-keeper's soul has left its ledger-line. The court does not object. The court merely requires *payment*."

Behind me, Wren stood very still in the way of someone who has recently been handed back their own heartbeat and isn't yet sure what to do with it. Their eyes—Wren's eyes, warm brown, creased at the corners from years of squinting into forge-light—moved from my face to the magistrates to Sefa, who had planted herself in front of us both with her small fists balled like she intended to fight the entire mountain.

"The stair is closed," Ilvane continued. "It closed behind you, scribe. It closes for everyone. Nothing leaves this court without a debt discharged. You know how we work. You've been working for us your whole life, in your small way."

She wasn't wrong, which was the humiliating part. Seven years of selling charms, seven years of ledger-work for the Order of the Quiet Flame, and all of it—the whole grubby architecture of my life—had been built on the same foundation as this place: *something owed, something paid.* I had just never asked what currency the deepest ledgers used.

I looked at Wren.

Here is what nobody tells you about loving someone whose soul has been portioned out like bread: you spend so long imagining the reunion that you never plan past it. I had rehearsed finding them. I had rehearsed carrying them out, fighting for them, bleeding for them if it came to that. I had not once rehearsed standing next to them while they looked at me with polite, distant confusion—the look you give a stranger who is crying for no reason you can see.

Because that was the other thing. Wren didn't remember me. The court had burned those years for ward-fuel along with everything else. What I'd pulled from the furnace was their core-shard—the self itself, the ember beneath the ash—but the memories had gone up the flue years ago, spent keeping the border wards humming while the city above slept soundly and complained about the price of candles.

So when I looked at Wren, I was looking at the person I loved, whole and alive and utterly unacquainted with me.

And when I looked at myself, I thought: *well. There's a symmetry there.*

* * *

"You're not seriously considering this," Sefa whispered. She had dragged me aside, into the shadow of a ledger-pillar carved with ten thousand names, each one glowing faintly as it was spent somewhere in the dark. "Jonah. Look at me. You're not considering it."

"I'm considering it," I said. "That's literally what I'm doing."

"It's a trick. It's always a trick with these people."

"Of course it's a trick. Everything is a trick. The charm you bought in Ashmarket was a trick—a bit of tin and a lie I told it until it believed me." I crouched down to her level. She was eleven years old and had followed me down a mountain's throat because I hadn't been fast enough to stop her, and somewhere in the corridors that eat memory she had started remembering things that never happened to her, which meant the mountain had already taken its toll on her too, and she had kept walking anyway. Braver than me. Better than me. "But here's the difference, Sefa. A trick only works if you don't know it's coming."

"That doesn't make any sense."

"No," I agreed. "Most important things don't."

Ilvane waited with the patience of an institution. Behind her, the other magistrates had resumed their work—souls being weighed, portioned, entered into columns. The new ward they meant to build would need fuel for a century, and they had a furnace, and they had time, and they had every reason to believe I would eventually do the arithmetic their way.

Except I had done the arithmetic. That was the thing. I'd been doing it since the night the stars went out.

Here is what I knew: Wren's soul was back in their body, but the court held the *deed*. The ledger-line could be redrawn. And the price of release—one soul's freedom, fully discharged—was a name-memory of equivalent weight. Not a life. Not blood. The court didn't deal in blood; blood was cheap and messy and stopped being useful. They dealt in *names*, in the accumulated weight of a self, in everything a person had ever been to everyone who ever knew them.

And mine, I realized, had a certain market value. Because mine was braided through with Wren's. Seven years of grief, of hidden notes written by candlelight, of a love that had survived being erased from every record but one. If they took my name-memory, they wouldn't just be taking Jonah Dunmore, temple scribe, debtor, minor smuggler of harmless charms.

They'd be taking the person who loved Wren.

Which meant—and here was the joke, the beautiful, terrible joke—that the payment and the purchase were the same thing. They were taking exactly what I'd come to give. Wren would walk out of this mountain owing nothing, holding nothing of me, free. And I would walk out too, technically, breathing, upright, a person with a face and hands and a talent for small lights—but without the thread that tied me to them. Without knowing why my chest hurt when they looked at me. Without knowing why I'd come.

"They'll let us go?" I asked Ilvane. "Both of us. All three of us. No interest. No follow-up collections."

"The court honors its contracts," she said. "It is the only thing we honor."

"And the memory? Where does it go?"

"Into the reserve. Names are never destroyed, scribe. Only held." Something in her mask's tilt shifted—almost gentle, if porcelain can be gentle. "Held things can sometimes be found again. We do not promise it. But the mountain keeps what it keeps."

I thought about my hidden notes. The little book sewn into the lining of my coat, the only record in the world that Wren Oyelaran had ever lived—every entry in my own handwriting, dated, detailed, stubborn. *They laughed at their own jokes before the punchline. They burned the flatbread every single time. They hummed when they worked the forge, always off-key, always the same song.*

If I forgot, the notes would still exist. Someone would have to read them to me. Someone would have to teach me myself.

Someone would have to want to.

"Wren," I said.

They came closer, wary as a cat near water. Up close I could see the hollow places where the years had been scooped out—not empty, exactly, but *unfurnished*, rooms waiting to learn what belonged in them. "You keep looking at me like that," they said quietly. "Like you're memorizing me."

"I am," I said. "One last time. Then I'm going to do something incredibly stupid, and I'd like you to remember that I did it on purpose."

"Don't," Sefa said behind me. Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Jonah, *don't*—"

"Sefa. Listen. When we get up top—if I'm strange, if I don't know you—you tell me about Ashmarket. Tell me about the charm that shouldn't have worked. Make it a good story. Embellish. I won't mind."

"You're really going to pay them." She wasn't asking anymore. She was eleven years old and learning, far too early, that love is a thing people spend. "You're going to give them *you*."

"I'm going to give them the part of me that's already given away," I said. "Honestly, they're getting a bargain. The rest of me is mostly bad posture and unpaid debts."

Wren caught my sleeve. Their grip was strong—forge-strong, even hollowed, even now. "Whatever you think I was to you," they said, low, urgent, "I can't be it. I don't have it. You'd be paying everything for a stranger."

"No," I said. "I'd be paying everything so that you get to decide who you are. Including whether you ever decide to be anything to me at all. That's not a purchase, Wren. That's the opposite of a purchase. That's the first free thing I've ever given anyone."

And God help me, I meant it. That was the strangest part. Seven years bound to the Order, seven years of small crimes and smaller lights, and the freest moment of my entire life was the moment I chose to hand over the self that had done all the loving. Duty says: protect the institution. Love says: protect the person. But there's a third thing, quieter than either, that says: *set them down gently, and let go, and mean it.* I had never been able to afford that third thing before.

I turned to Ilvane. "Draw up the contract."

* * *

The court's paperwork, I will say this for it, is immaculate.

A desk rose from the floor—or perhaps it had always been there, waiting; the mountain has opinions about furniture. A quill of grey feather. A page that drank ink the way sand drinks rain. Ilvane recited the terms in a voice like a closing door: *the name-memory of Jonah Dunmore, complete and undivided, tendered in discharge of the debt against the soul designated ember-keeper; upon execution, all parties depart by the stair; no further claim shall attach.*

"No further claim," I repeated, and signed.

Signing felt like nothing. That's the cruelty of it—I'd braced for pain, for some grand tearing sensation worthy of the sacrifice, and instead the quill simply scratched, and the ink sank, and Ilvane said, "Executed," and for three full seconds I thought maybe I'd gotten away with something.

Then the mountain collected.

It didn't take the memory all at once. It took it the way tide takes a sandcastle—from the edges inward, politely, thoroughly. The Order's ledgers went first, dissolving into general grayness. Then Ashmarket, the smell of it, the noise. Then faces—Sefa's going soft and unfamiliar, a child, a witness, a stranger who seemed oddly upset. My mother's laugh. My own reflection at various ages. And last, because the mountain saves the heaviest coin for last, Wren: the flatbread burning, the off-key humming, the night at the ember-shrine when they'd taken my hand and said *stay* and I'd said *always* and we'd both been lying, because nothing stays, nothing stays except—

Except the notes. Somewhere above us, in a coat lining, in my handwriting, in ink that the mountain did not hold: *they laughed at their own jokes before the punchline.*

I felt the words leave me and not leave me, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. Gone, and yet *pointing* at where it had been. A hole shaped exactly like a person.

The magistrates withdrew. The stair unsealed with a sound like a long-held breath released. Light—real light, starved and gray and glorious—poured down from somewhere impossibly far above.

"Up," said a voice, and it took me a moment to recognize it as mine, because whoever I was now, I still apparently gave orders in a crisis. "All of you. Before it changes its mind."

Sefa took my hand without hesitation, which confused me deeply, because I was quite sure I'd never seen her before in my life. Wren walked beside me, silent, watching me with an expression I couldn't read—which was fair, since I couldn't read my own face either.

We climbed. The stair of teeth let us pass. The mountain, true to its contract, kept what it kept and released what it released, and I ascended out of the dark carrying nothing but a body, a coat with a suspicious heaviness in its lining, and a hole in my chest shaped precisely like someone I couldn't name.

Above us, the sky was turning the color of a fresh bruise healing—the first dawn after the long dark. And the first stars were coming back, one by one, like lamps relit in windows down a long street.

Wren stopped on the last step, staring up at them, and then stared at me, and reached slowly into my coat—my coat, though neither of us knew why that felt natural—and drew out a small, battered notebook sewn into the lining.

"What's this?" they asked.

I opened my mouth to say *I have no idea.*

What came out instead, from somewhere deeper than memory, was: "Read it to me. Please. I think—it's mine. I think I'm in there."

Wren looked at the first page. Their breath caught. And above us the stars came home, and below us the mountain kept its silence, and between us lay a book full of a person I used to be, waiting to be introduced.
