## 10. When the Ember Burns Revisited

The first thing I learned about being alive again was that it itched.

Not metaphorically—though there was plenty of that too—but literally, in the skin, like wearing a coat someone else had tailored and then left folded in a drawer for seven years. My hands didn't feel like my hands. My name sat in my mouth like a coin I'd been handed by a stranger: *Wren*, yes, fine, a good shape for a mouth to make, but whose?

Jonah's, apparently.

They were asleep when the stars came back. I know because I was watching them—not out of devotion, but because they were the only fixed point in a world that had recently been reassembled around me from spare parts. The mountain had let us go at the hour before dawn, and we'd climbed the Stair of Teeth with Sefa chattering the whole way about things she remembered that she'd never lived, and Jonah walking half a step behind me the entire time, careful as a man following a dog he wasn't sure wouldn't bite.

Then we'd come out into Ashmarket, and the sky had done its trick.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the only currency I have left: I don't remember the stars. I remember *that* there were stars, the way you remember a word in a language you no longer speak. But when the sky cracked open above the rooftops and all those cold little fires came back on, one by one, like lamps lit down an endless street—I stopped on the cobbles and made a sound I'm not proud of. Something between a laugh and a wound.

And Jonah looked at me looking up, and their face did something complicated, and I understood, without knowing how I understood, that this person had paid something enormous for me. That the price had been themselves.

That was four days ago.

* * *

We live now in a room above a dyer's shop on Tanner's Row, which smells permanently of vinegar and mordant, and which Sefa has declared "haunted but in a friendly way." She sleeps by the window and talks in her sleep in a language neither of us knows, and in the mornings she remembers our names better than we do, which is its own kind of haunting.

This morning Jonah woke late, which is new for them—the notes say they used to wake before the temple bells, always, out of habit or fear—and sat across the little table from me with two cups of chicory and the expression of someone about to defuse something.

"I found them," they said. "Your notes. Mine, I mean. About you."

"You said."

"I think—" They turned the cup. Turned it back. "I think you should read them. Not me. I can't. Whatever I wrote in there, I wrote it as someone who—" A breath. "He doesn't exist anymore. The man who loved you. He traded himself away so you could walk out of that mountain whole. And I keep reaching for him and finding ash where he was, and it's like reading letters from a dead brother, except the dead brother is also sitting right here drinking terrible chicory."

"That's a very specific grief," I said.

"I contain multitudes," Jonah said, wry even now, even hollowed, and something in my chest—which I am told is called a heart, and which I am told once belonged entirely to this person—turned over like a page.

So they gave me the book.

It was small, oilcloth-wrapped, hidden—according to a note tucked inside the cover—in the false bottom of a ledger box in the Order's scriptorium, which meant Jonah had stolen it back sometime in the last four days, along with everything else they'd stolen, which is apparently a longer list than either of us expected. The cover was soft with handling. Whoever had written this book had held it often. Held it the way you hold a thing that is the last of its kind.

I opened it.

* * *

Here is what I know about myself, as of this morning, compiled from sources:

I kept embers. Not poetically—actually. There was a hearth-shrine on Coalgate Street, one of the old ones, pre-Order, the kind that burned a true fire instead of a licensed one, and I was its keeper. I fed it at dawn and dusk. I talked to it. The notes say I believed—believed, past tense, though the belief appears to have survived the soul-stripping better than most of me—that a tended fire remembers everyone who ever warmed their hands at it, and that keeping one alive was a way of keeping *them* alive.

I laughed easily. There are three separate entries that begin "Wren laughed at" and none of them explain the joke, which I resent deeply.

I sang badly and often.

I once spent an entire festival night teaching Jonah the constellations using bread rolls on a tavern table, and got six of them wrong, and refused correction on principle. ("You're wrong." / "The stars are wrong. I'm consistent.")

And on the last page, in handwriting gone shaky, dated the night before the wards fell:

*I sold nothing today. Wren bought a charm off me for a copper just so I'd have made one sale, and the charm does nothing, and we both know it, and I love them so much it feels like a debt I'll never finish paying. If anyone ever reads this: his name is Wren. He kept the fire on Coalgate. Remember that if the Order makes me forget. Remember it even if I beg you not to.*

I read that last line three times.

Then I looked up, and Jonah was watching me with the particular stillness of a person waiting to find out whether they've been robbed.

"He begged you not to remember," I said. "Himself. And you did it anyway."

"It seemed rude to obey," Jonah said, and their voice cracked on the second word, and I got up, because some things the body knows even when the self doesn't, and I put my arms around them, and they shook against me like a door in wind.

* * *

That was this morning. Now it's evening, and the stars are out again—all of them, even the ones I got wrong—and I've decided on a course of action, which is this:

I am going to teach Jonah who they were.

Not out of duty. Duty is the Order's religion and I've seen what it builds. Out of something less orderly. Because I read a man's whole heart in a notebook today, and he's sitting across from me eating bread like it's a chore, and it seems obscene that the only person who knew him is gone, and the only person who could know him is me.

"Entry forty-one," I said. "You once bribed a temple cat with dried fish to sit in High Cantor Vess's chair during convocation. It took the entire congregation twenty minutes to notice. You were never caught. You consider this your masterpiece."

Jonah blinked. "Did I write that?"

"You wrote that. You also wrote, quote, 'Vess deserved it, the man blesses his own inkwell.'"

"I do bless my inkwell," Jonah said automatically, and then stopped, hand halfway to their mouth, staring at nothing. "I—yes. I do. I didn't know that until I said it." They looked at me with something between terror and wonder. "What else?"

"Entry fifty-eight. You hate the sound of bells but you get up early to hear them anyway, because—" I checked the page, because I wanted to say it exactly right, because it deserved exactness. "Because 'the day should start with something you chose to endure.'"

Jonah was quiet a long moment. Outside, Sefa was arguing with the dyer's cat, loudly, in two languages.

"That's me," they said finally, sounding surprised. "That's—genuinely me. How is that me? You read it to me and it's more mine than anything in my own head."

"Because he wrote it down," I said. "That's what keepers do. We feed the fire so it remembers." I closed the book gently. "So here's the arrangement. Every day, I read you one entry. Just one. You get to decide if you keep it. Some of them you might not want—they weren't all kind, and he was afraid a lot of the time, and afraid people are cruel in their private pages. But they're yours. All of them. And I'll be here while you sort through them, for as long as it takes, and after, if you'll have me."

"And what do you get out of it?" Jonah asked. Not suspicious—wary, the wariness of someone who has learned that gifts from institutions come with ledgers attached.

"Nothing," I said. "Or everything. I genuinely don't know yet. I woke up eleven days ago with someone else's memories leaking into my head and a stranger who ruined their life to save me, and I don't remember loving you, Jonah. I want to be clear about that, because you deserve clarity: I don't remember. What I have is the record. The evidence. Seven years of entries that say I loved you, and a body that leans toward you when you talk like iron filings toward a magnet, and a hole in my chest shaped exactly like your name."

They had gone very still.

"So I can't give you back the man who wrote that last page," I said. "He's gone. He paid himself out like coin and the mountain spent him. But I'm here. I'm new. And I would like—" and here my voice did the thing voices do when they mean what they say, "—I would like to fall in love with you on purpose. From scratch. With witnesses. Starting with entry one, tomorrow at dawn, if you'll allow it."

Jonah looked at me for a long time. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, the city went on with the business of being saved and not knowing it.

"You know what he'd say," Jonah said at last, and the corner of their mouth moved. "He'd say something insufferable. Something like—'the stars are wrong, I'm consistent.'"

"The stars are wrong," I agreed. "I'm consistent."

And Jonah Dunmore—whoever they had been, whoever they were becoming—laughed. Really laughed, startled out of them, bright and cracked and alive, and I thought: *there*. There's the ember. That's the one I keep now.

"Entry one," they said. "Tomorrow. Dawn."

Outside the window, above Tanner's Row, the stars burned on—wrong, probably, misnamed, miscounted, tended badly by everyone who'd ever tried.

But burning.

And I intended to keep them that way.
