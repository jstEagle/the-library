## 3. What the Smugglers Know

The snow had stopped by morning, but the wind had not, and Thornfell's lower streets wore a skin of ice that made every footstep an argument with the ground. Juniper left Ash asleep beneath three blankets in the loft above the shop, a charcoal stub and a scrap of paper within the boy's reach in case he woke wanting to talk — because he did talk now, in that language like river-stones knocking together, even when no one answered. Juniper had filled eleven pages of it overnight. The syllables repeated. That was the worst part. Language was supposed to wander; this one circled, patient as water finding a crack.

He told himself he was going out for bread. He bought the bread. Then he kept walking, down past the tanneries where the reek of lime cut through the cold, past the customs house with its fat yellow windows, to the Crooked Eel — a tavern so far into the town's shadow that the sun touched it perhaps four days a year, and those grudgingly.

Brannoc Hale would be there. Brannoc Hale was always there, in the same corner seat by the peat fire, in the same coat with its elbows gone to velvet, nursing the same cup of black barley beer as though it were a friend who'd disappointed him but not enough to abandon. Twenty years Juniper had known him. Brannoc had carried more contraband through the Thornfell passes than any three men alive, and he knew the mountains the way Juniper mapped them: from the outside, honestly, with respect for what they could do to you.

Except for one winter. The winter Brannoc went up through the Grey Saddle with a load of uncut garnets and did not come back until the thaw. And when he came back, something about him came back wrong — or rather, incomplete. He'd lost things no wound explains. He could no longer remember his mother's face, though he remembered her funeral. He'd forgotten the taste of honey entirely, and once, mid-story about his boyhood, had stopped and stared at his own hands and asked Juniper, quietly, whose hands they were. The forgetting came back in patches after a season, like feeling returning to frostbitten flesh. Mostly. Not all.

He had never said what happened up there. Juniper had never pressed. Smugglers earned their silences honestly.

The Eel was warm inside the way old wood is warm, holding heat the way it held smoke and spilled beer and forty years of arguments. Brannoc sat where he always sat. His head lifted when Juniper crossed the floor, and something flickered across the smuggler's seamed face — recognition first, then a wariness that arrived a half-beat too late to be hidden.

"Cartographer." Brannoc said it like a diagnosis. "You've got your walking face on."

"I need to ask you about something, and I need you not to decide I'm mad before I finish."

"That's a tall order before noon." But Brannoc nudged the bench opposite with his boot, and Juniper sat, and set his satchel on the table between them.

He laid out the pages of Ash's speech. Eleven sheets of syllables rendered in his careful cartographer's hand, cross-referenced against the rubbings he'd taken years ago from a cave wall high on the eastern shoulder of the mountain — markings no scholar at Lowmarsh would touch, dismissed as shepherd graffiti or older-than-useful nonsense. He laid out the map too. The one whose ink had crawled and rearranged itself under a child's fingers, opening a road through solid rock to a door marked with a crest Juniper had worn on his signet since he was nine years old and had never once thought to question.

Brannoc looked at all of it for a long time. He did not touch anything. Juniper noticed that — noticed how the smuggler's hands stayed flat on the tabletop, deliberately still, the way a man keeps his hands still near a snake.

Then Brannoc looked up, and his eyes were the grey of slate under meltwater, and very tired.

"Where's the child?"

"Asleep. Above my shop." Juniper paused. "Why does everyone ask that first? Vael—"

"Because it matters whether he's asleep," Brannoc said. "Keep him sleeping. Keep him fed and warm and *bored*, cartographer. A bored child is an ordinary child. It's when they start dreaming loud that the Court hears them."

Juniper felt the words go through him like cold water through a boot seam. "What court?"

Brannoc took a long pull of his beer. Set it down. Turned the cup a quarter-rotation, aligning the handle with some private meridian. Buying time, Juniper realized. The man who talked his way past border guards in three duchies needed time to answer.

"There are two kinds of roads through the Thornfells," Brannoc said at last. "There's the kind I ran. Garnets, brandy, untaxed wool, fugitives with coin. Roads made by feet. And then there's the other kind." He tapped the edge of the rearranged map without quite touching the ink. "Roads made by *want*. Every soul that ever wished itself elsewhere, every debtor who prayed for a way out of what he owed — the mountain drinks all that. Down deep, it pools. And pooled want gets... organized."

"The Ember Court," Juniper said. Vael's word. She had said it the way you name a disease.

"Is that what she called it?" Brannoc's mouth twisted. "Pretty name. They'd like that, actually. They like being pretty. Here's what my grandmother called them, and her mother before her: *the ones below who keep accounts*." He leaned forward, and the firelight caught the missing piece of his left ear, long ago bitten off in some port brawl, and Juniper understood suddenly that he was about to be given something Brannoc had never given anyone sober.

"They buy souls, Juniper. Not metaphor. Not priest-talk. They buy them the way you'd buy a horse — with paper, with terms, with witnesses. Only their currency isn't coin. Their currency is debt. Real debt — the famine-year kind, the gambling-kind, the kind that has a family eating bark-bread and watching the youngest turn grey. Somebody comes to you in a bad winter wearing a friendly face and says: we can make it all go away. Your debts, your hunger, your grief. Everything owed to you and everything you owe. All settled. You just sign here. And what you're signing away seems small, because they let it seem small. A memory. A name. A voice. Something you'll tell yourself you can live without."

"And people sign."

"People are starving," Brannoc said, gently, which was worse than harsh. "People have always been starving. That's not a flaw in the design, cartographer. That *is* the design."

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them a keg was tapped and the smell of fresh beer briefly overrode the peat smoke. Juniper found he was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and made himself let go.

"Ash talks," he said. "A language nobody's recorded. It matches markings on a cave wall I found twelve years ago. Tell me what that means."

Brannoc was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register Juniper had only heard him use twice before — once reporting a death, once confessing a theft that had gotten a man hanged.

"It means the boy is sold," Brannoc said. "That tongue — the circling one, the one that repeats — it's the speech of the taken. The Court teaches it to everything it owns. It's not a language for saying new things, see. That's the horror of it. It's a language for *reciting*. Contracts, mostly. Terms of sale. Clauses. The sold walk around inside it like prisoners pacing a cell, saying their own captivity over and over because it's the only words left in them." He nodded at the pages. "Read yours again. Listen to the shape of it. That's not a child chatting. That's a child reciting the bill of sale for his own soul, in the only tongue he's been permitted to keep."

Juniper's stomach turned over, slow and sick. Eleven pages. The boy had been reciting for hours, patient as tide, and Juniper had sat there transcribing it like a scholar, charmed by the novelty of it. Charmed.

"He remembers," Juniper managed. "His parents. Being chosen. He thinks they're coming for him."

"Don't tell him they aren't."

"I know."

"Do you?" Brannoc's eyes were steady and pitiless with kindness. "Because here's the second thing you need to hear, and you're going to want to hit me for it. Those contracts — they mature. Nothing the Court buys is collected right away. They wait. Let the child grow attached to whatever warmth it's found. Let the buyer grow attached too, if there is one. Attachment makes collection easier. A soul that's loved comes along quieter than a soul that's hunted." He turned the beer cup another quarter-rotation. "And the Court's claim doesn't expire. It ripens. There'll be a date written somewhere, in that language of his, and when the date comes, they collect. Or—" his eyes flicked to the map with its impossible road and its crest he plainly recognized, "—someone settles the debt another way."

"What other way?"

"You tell me. You're the one carrying a Ravensworth seal into my tavern asking about doors under the mountain." Brannoc leaned back, and for a moment he looked old — old the way the mountain is old, weathered down to what can't be worn away. "I told you I vanished for a winter. I'll tell you what I never told you, and then we're square, and you never bring this into the Eel again. I owed money. Bad money, to bad men, and I took a shortcut through the Saddle that I'd been warned about since I was a boy, and I met someone on the ice. Friendly face. Offered to settle everything. I signed." He spread his hands, palms up, and Juniper saw they were trembling very slightly, twenty years later. "They took the winter. Just the winter — that was the term, and I've learned since that they honor terms the way a knife honors a sheath. Exactly, and only so far as it cuts. I walked their roads for a season. I carried loads for them. And I heard things, and forgot things, and when they let me go I'd lost my mother's face and the taste of honey and a few other pieces I didn't find out about until later."

"Your debts," Juniper said quietly. "Were they settled?"

"Oh, aye." Brannoc smiled, and it was the bleakest thing Juniper had seen in years. "Settled beautiful. The men I owed were dead within the month — all three, accidents, nothing traceable. That's the other thing you need carved into you, cartographer: *they keep their side*. Perfectly. That's why people keep signing. Nobody cheats you down there. They just own you."

* * *

They sat awhile without speaking. The tavern filled slowly around them — a drover, two quarrymen, a woman mending a net by the window — ordinary lives, ordinary debts, and Juniper found himself looking at each of them wondering who among them had signed something, who was walking around a half-piece short and calling it age.

"How do I get him free?" Juniper asked finally. "Not outrun. Free. If the claim ripens, running just delays collection."

Brannoc laughed — short, hollow, no humor in it. "You don't. Nobody gets free. The Court's whole architecture is built so nothing leaves. Debts don't die, contracts don't lapse, and the doors—" he shook his head. "There are more ways into the deep places than out of them. That's not accident either. Ask your priestess friend. She'll know more than me; she was inside the trade, if I'm any judge. Ember-priestesses draw up the papers." He drained his cup and set it down with a click that ended something. "But if you're fool enough to try anyway, then hear the rules. Three of them. Write them on your heart, since you're a man who trusts ink."

Juniper waited.

"One: they cannot take what is freely given, but what is freely given they take *completely*. Never offer them anything. Not a promise, not a kindness, not a name in greeting. Every gift is an opening. Two: they must show you the true price before you pay it — that's law older than their law, older than the mountain. Make them say the cost aloud, in full, every time. Liars hate daylight and they're bound to it. Three—" Brannoc hesitated, and Juniper watched him wrestle with whether to say it, and lose. "Three. The Court honors bloodlines. Old blood, old service. If your family crest opens doors under that mountain, cartographer, then your family didn't just stumble past them. They built them, or served those who did, and the Court will know your name the moment you speak it down there. Maybe before." He stood, dropping coins beside his cup. "So don't speak it. Whatever happens below, however angry, however frightened — do not give them your name freely. Names are handles. They pull handles."

Juniper rose too. At the door, Brannoc caught his sleeve — quick, almost furtive.

"The boy," he said. "When the date comes — and it'll come sooner than you think, spring-thaw soonest — they'll come for him polite. Knocking at your door with borrowed faces. Don't open. Don't argue. Arguing is engagement and engagement is invitation." His grip tightened. "And Juniper. If they offer you a bargain for him — and they will, they *love* a bargain — make them say the whole price. All of it. Even the part they leave till last. Promise me."

"I promise."

Brannoc searched his face a moment longer, then nodded once and pushed out into the cold, a bent silhouette swallowed by the white street.

Juniper stood alone in the warmth of the Eel, and around him the tavern noise went on — laughter, the clink of cups, a song starting badly in the corner — all of it suddenly sounding to him like the surface crust over very deep water. Eleven pages of a child's recitation weighed in his satchel like stones. Somewhere above the town, Ash slept, dreaming, perhaps, in a tongue that circled.

And somewhere beneath all of it, beneath the snow and the stone and every honest road Juniper had ever drawn, something patient turned its ledgers toward spring.

He was halfway home before he saw the figure waiting by his shop door — a woman in ash-grey robes, hood back, standing in the trampled snow as though she had grown there.

She smiled, and it did not reach her eyes.

"Master Ravensworth," Vael said. "We should talk before the boy wakes."
