## 8. The Village Without Names

The pass narrowed for the better part of a day, and then, without ceremony, it opened.

Juniper had mapped these mountains his whole life. He had walked the smuggler roads with Brannoc Hale before Brannoc's winter of vanishing; he had surveyed the high cols in summer and the low caves in thaw; he had drawn, in careful ink, every ridge and riverbed between Thornfell and the far side of the range. And yet the valley that spread below him now appeared on none of his charts — not even on the ones Ash had rewritten, not even on the pages that rearranged themselves like living things under the boy's small fingers.

It was a shallow bowl of land cupped between two shoulders of grey stone, floored with old pine and newer birch, threaded by a stream that ran pale as milk where snowmelt fed it. Smoke rose from perhaps thirty chimneys, thin and unhurried. Fields lay fallow or half-planted in rows too neat to be abandoned and too sparse to be prosperous. A bell tower stood at the village's center, its bell long since removed — Juniper could see the empty yoke from here, a dark mouth where sound should have hung.

"There," Vael said.

He had learned, over the days since the Hollow Stair, to read her silences as well as her words. This was not one of them. She stood with her hood back, ash-grey hair loose around a face that had gone very still, and her eyes were fixed on the village with an expression he had no name for. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"You know this place," he said.

"I know what it is." Her voice was flat, scraped clean. "I did not know any still lived."

"Escaped ones?" He kept his voice low out of some instinct he couldn't explain, though there was no one within a mile to hear him. "You said some broke free. Ran before their debts came due."

"Some ran." Vael shouldered her pack and started down the switchback trail without waiting for him. "Some were let go. The Court does not always chase what it has already emptied. A soul sold and reclaimed is profit. A soul sold and fled is a lesson. They leave the lessons where others can find them."

Ash tugged at Juniper's sleeve. The boy had not spoken — could not speak anything but that old tongue, that language of sold souls that no scholar had ever recorded — but he had developed a whole grammar of gesture: a pull on the sleeve meaning *look*, a tap on the shoulder meaning *wait*, both hands pressed flat against his chest meaning something Juniper had not yet decoded but suspected meant *me* or *mine* or *afraid*. Now he pulled twice, urgent, and pointed down into the valley, and then pressed his palms to his chest and held them there.

"I see them, lad," Juniper said. "We'll ask shelter. That's all. Shelter, food, a night's rest, and whatever you can learn from them about your own tongue."

Ash's hands stayed pressed to his chest a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, the way a soldier nods before a wall must be scaled.

* * *

They met the first villager on the outskirts, and Juniper would spend the rest of his life trying to describe what that meeting was like.

A woman stood among the half-planted fields, bent over a row of seedlings. She wore homespun dyed the brown of undyed wool, and her hair was bound back with plain cord, and everything about her said *farmer, widow, ordinary*. But when Juniper's eyes went to her face, they slid off. Not refused — he could look, if he tried, if he concentrated — but they slid, the way a boot slides on wet stone. He knew she had eyes because he could see light catch in them when he angled his head. He knew she had a mouth because her voice came from somewhere near it. But the moment he stopped forcing himself, his gaze drifted away and found the fence post beside her, the furrow, the distant bell tower, anything else.

"Good day," he managed.

She straightened. Looked at him — he felt the weight of it, actual and deliberate, the way he'd never felt a stranger's gaze before — and said nothing.

"We're travelers," Vael said, stepping forward. "Road-worn. We ask nothing but a fire and a floor."

The woman's head tilted. Her mouth moved, and words came out, but they arrived strangely, as if she were speaking through water: "Travelers. Yes. I see — I am trying to see — you are three. One small. One who smells of cinders."

Vael flinched. It was a small thing, quickly mastered, but Juniper saw it, and saw too how the woman's blank face turned toward that flinch with something like hunger.

"You smell of the Court," the woman said. "Ember-priestess. Even disgraced, even burned-out. The ash doesn't wash off."

"No," Vael said quietly. "It doesn't."

Another figure had come up the row while they spoke — an old man, or Juniper thought old, though age sat strangely on him too, less like years than like weathering, the way stone weathers without ever having been anything but stone. He carried a hoe he wasn't using. When he looked at Juniper, Juniper felt the same terrible sliding, the gaze finding no purchase, and understood with a lurch that it went both ways: these people could barely hold him in their attention, and he could barely hold them.

"They can't see us properly," the old man said to the woman. His voice was clearer than hers, worn smooth like a river stone. "That's how it works. We lost our names, so the world lost its grip on us. You get used to being a rumor."

"We're looking for shelter," Juniper said. "And—" He hesitated, then made himself say it. "And my companion here—" he touched Ash's shoulder— "speaks a language. Old. We've been told it belongs to those who were sold."

Everything changed.

It was not dramatic. No one cried out. But the woman set down her basket, and the old man leaned his hoe against the fence with great care, and other figures rose from doorways and field-rows all across the valley — Juniper counted eleven, then fourteen, then gave up counting because his eyes kept losing them — and converged, unhurried, silent, until the three travelers stood in the middle of a ring of people whose faces he could not hold in memory for longer than a breath.

The old man spoke first, and his voice had changed. There was something underneath it now, something that trembled.

"Say a word," he said. "Boy. Say any word of it."

Ash looked up at Juniper. Juniper nodded. And Ash, in his clear, high, unbroken voice, spoke a sentence of that flowing, syllable-rich language that had haunted Juniper since the first snowbound night — the language that matched the cave-wall markings, the language of the ledger, the language beneath all languages of the mountain.

The effect on the villagers was like watching frost form in fast motion. Faces Juniper could not remember visibly remembered themselves for a moment — mouths shaping the sounds along with Ash, hands rising, an old woman at the ring's edge beginning to weep without any change in her blurred expression, tears simply falling from somewhere in the blur.

"That's the market-tongue," the old man whispered. "That's what they speak to you when they weigh you. Boy — boy, where did you hear that? Who taught you?"

Ash pressed his palms flat against his chest again. *Me. Mine.*

The silence that followed was worse than any wailing could have been. Fourteen blurred faces, all turned toward a child, all understanding at once what he was telling them: that he belonged to the Court already, that his sale was written and waiting, that the spring equinox was coming whether any of them willed it or not.

"He's claimed," someone said. "Matured debt. Equinox."

"How long?" the old woman asked, sharp as a blade despite her blur.

"Days," Vael said. "Weeks at most. We mean to go under before it falls due."

"Go under." The old man laughed, and it was the worst sound Juniper had heard yet, because there was no bitterness in it at all, only a vast tired tenderness. "Priestess. Cartographer. Listen to me. We went under. That's how we got here. Every soul in this valley bought their way out with the only coin they had left after the Court took its price." He spread his arms, indicating the fields, the bell-less tower, the blurred faces. "Our names. We paid our own names for the road out. That's why you can't see us. That's why your maps don't show this place. A nameless village doesn't hold ink."

Juniper felt the ground tilt under a truth he hadn't known he was standing on. All his life he had trusted his surveys, his instruments, his careful triangulations. And here was a whole valley, thirty chimneys' worth of lives, that had simply never existed on paper because paper required names and these people had traded theirs away.

"The Hollow Stair let you out," Vael said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The Stair lets everyone out who's willing to arrive as nobody." The old man studied her, or tried to; his gaze slid from her face the same way hers slid from his. "You'd know that, priestess. If you'd ever been willing."

* * *

They were given the meeting-house — a long low building of timber and stone near the bell tower, with a hearth and benches and walls hung with objects instead of pictures: a rusted key, a child's shoe, a wooden bird, a strip of cloth embroidered with letters that trailed off unfinished. Juniper understood slowly, watching the villagers move among these things with casual familiarity, that each object had once belonged to a name. They were kept the way other communities keep portraits of ancestors.

Supper was barley soup and dark bread, and it was good, and it was strange, because Juniper could not afterward have said who had served it to him. Hands had passed the bowl. A voice had told him where the salt was. But the faces would not stay, and when he closed his eyes to try, he found he could not even remember how many people had sat at the table.

Ash, though — Ash they could see.

"It's because he isn't empty yet," the old man explained. He had introduced himself three times, and each time the name had slipped from Juniper's mind like water from a tilted hand, until finally the man had laughed and said, "Call me Elder. It's what they call me. It's close enough to a name to be useful and far enough to be honest." Now he sat across the hearth from Juniper while Ash slept curled against Vael's side — the boy had chosen her, of all people, and neither she nor Juniper had remarked on it aloud. "The Court hasn't finished with him. His name is still in their vault, whole and waiting. So the world can still see him. We're the ones who paid in advance, you understand. We're spent."

"Do you regret it?" Juniper asked. "The trade?"

Elder was quiet a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the stream ran milk-pale over stones.

"I regretted it for the first year," he said at last. "Every morning I woke reaching for my name the way you reach for a limb that's been cut off. Then the second year, I noticed I wasn't reaching anymore. And now—" he turned his hands palm-up, weathered and steady— "now I'll tell you something no priestess will like. It's peaceful, being no one. The Court took my name, but it also took my debts, my grudges, my father's expectations, my shame. What's left is just — work, and neighbors, and bread. We can't be seen proper, but we see *each other*. That's worth more than you'd think. When you're a blank to the world, you learn that being held in someone's attention is the only belonging there ever was. We give it to each other daily. Deliberately. Like feeding a fire."

Juniper looked into the flames and thought of his shop above Thornfell, of the maps pinned wall to wall, of the decades he'd spent believing that to belong to no one was freedom. He had built a life on being unmapped. And here were thirty households of people who had been unmapped by force, and they had built, in the space where identity used to be, a community more deliberate than any he had ever stumbled into by accident of birth.

"Your language," he said, shaking himself. "Ash's language. You recognized it."

"Market-tongue. Court-speech. We called it the weighing-words." Elder's voice dropped. "I was a clerk, before. Sold over a gambling debt my brother incurred — the Court takes kin-debts, didn't the priestess tell you? I worked the sale-floors for six years before I ran. I know the tongue. Not all of it. Nobody knows all of it; some of it isn't meant for the sold to understand. But I know enough."

"Teach me," Juniper said. "Please. He can't tell me what happened to him. He can't tell me what he wants, what he fears, whether he—" He stopped, because his throat had closed. Whether he hates us for what we're about to do to him, taking him down toward the very court that owns him. "He's been talking at me for weeks and I've understood nothing. Teach me."

Elder looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached up to the wall behind him, took down the strip of embroidered cloth with its unfinished letters, and spread it across his knees.

"This was mine," he said. "My wife started stitching my name before I was taken. She died before I came back, and I found this in her things, and I sat with it for a season trying to remember what the letters sounded like." His thumb moved over the trailing thread. "So yes, cartographer. I'll teach you. Not for free — nothing in this valley is free, we learned that much from the Court — but the price is only this: you write it down. All of it. Every word I give you, in your fine surveyor's hand, with your fine surveyor's precision. Because we are thirty households of people the world cannot see, and when we die, the last speakers of that tongue die with us, and no one will ever have recorded it." His blurred face turned fully toward Juniper, and for a moment, through sheer force of will, Juniper held his eyes — old, brown, unbearably patient. "You make maps. Make this one. Make us exist on paper, once, before we're gone."

Juniper took out his notebook. His hands were not entirely steady.

* * *

They worked through the night and into the grey of morning, and it was the hardest cartography Juniper had ever done.

Not because the language was difficult — though it was, a music of open vowels and doubled consonants, a grammar that seemed to build meaning the way coral builds reefs, layer upon layer, each word carrying sediment from every word spoken before it. It was hard because Elder taught him the way a drowning man describes water: precisely, obsessively, with the urgency of someone who knows the knowledge dies with him.

"*Vessha,*" Elder said. "It means *to be weighed*. But the root, *vess*, means *to be held in the balance*, and the ending *-sha* means *willingly*. So the word for being weighed contains, inside it, the word for consent. The Court built that into the tongue itself. Every time they speak to you of your debt, they are speaking the word for your agreement. You cannot discuss what they did to you without saying yes."

Juniper wrote it down. *Vessha. To be weighed, willingly.* His pen pressed hard enough to nearly tear the page.

"*Sennu.* Home. But the root means *the place that holds your shape*. Not house. Not family. The place that holds your shape. A bowl holds water's shape. Do you understand? In the market-tongue, home is defined by what contains you. That is why the sold do not say *I miss home*. They say *sennu-vess* — *my container weighs on me*. Homesickness and debt are the same word-family. The Court did not steal that from us. We brought it with us. It was always in the language."

Outside, dawn was coming up grey over the fallow fields. Ash had woken and sat listening on the bench beside Vael, utterly still, his eyes moving between Elder's mouth and Juniper's pen. And as the translations accumulated — *vessha*, *sennu*, *korrath* (to burn a contract), *ilume* (the moment of forgetting) — Juniper watched the boy begin, very slowly, to lean forward. To mouth the words along. To glance at Juniper with an expression that was no longer the sealed, patient waiting of a child who expects never to be understood.

It was hope. Juniper had mapped enough faces to know it anywhere.

Near midmorning, Elder paused, rubbed his eyes, and said, "There's a word you'll need. Before you go under. I'll give it last, because it's the heaviest."

He set down the embroidery. He folded his weathered hands.

"*Ravensworth*," he said — and Juniper's whole body went cold, because that was not a word of the market-tongue, that was his own name, his family's name, the name Vael had shown him burning in the Court's ledger. "In the weighing-words, that name is a word. Did you know that? Your family's name entered the language centuries ago. It means *the hand that draws the door*. The hand that draws the door, cartographer. Every soul that ever walked down into that court walked through a door your bloodline drew. When the sold speak of Ravensworth, they spit the word. When the Court speaks it, they say it softly, the way you'd say *beloved*."

The fire had burned to embers. Ash was staring at Juniper with those clear, seeing eyes, and Juniper realized the boy had understood every word of this exchange — had probably understood it for weeks, in his own tongue, waiting for Juniper to catch up.

"I didn't know," Juniper said hoarsely. "Until Vael showed me the ledger, I didn't know any of it. My maps — I thought I was mapping the world. I was maintaining their doors."

"I believe you." Elder said it simply, without absolution, which somehow made it bearable. "But belief won't matter below. What matters below is the word itself. So learn its true form, the old form, before the Court softened it. *Ravensworth* in the first speech — the speech before the weighing-words — means something else entirely. Something the Court buried." He leaned forward, and his blurred face came into focus for just a moment, as if the gravity of what he was about to say anchored him in the world. "*Ra-vens-worth*. *The hand that closes what it opens.*"

Juniper stared at him.

"A door-drawer and a door-closer," Elder said. "Your family drew every door into that place. But the first of them built in a closing, too. Buried it in the root of the name, where the Court's clerks — men like me, men who copied without understanding — never thought to look." He tapped the notebook in Juniper's lap, the pages dense now with the first written record of the market-tongue the world had ever held. "You're going down there in a few days' time, cartographer, to bargain with a court that owns a child. Learn the rest of this tongue while you're under my roof. Because I think — I have thought since the boy first spoke — that the answer to your riddle isn't written in any vault."

He nodded at Ash.

"It's in what he says. Children repeat what they're made to memorize. And the Court makes its sold children memorize their contracts *in the old speech* — the speech before the softening — because contracts in the old speech cannot be altered." Elder's eyes, suddenly and terribly clear, went from Juniper to the sleeping-wakeful boy and back. "Ask him to recite his sale. Word for word. Write down every syllable. And then bring it to me, and we will read together what the Court has spent three hundred years making sure no one reads."

Ash, hearing his own fate discussed in a language he thought no one else alive could parse, did not look afraid.

He looked, for the first time since the snowbound night at Juniper's door, like a boy with a secret.

He patted the notebook twice — *look, look* — opened his mouth, and began to recite.
