## 9. Ash Speaks

The key came to me as a strip of birchbark.

Sera-who-was-called-nothing — the elder of the village without names, though I had begun privately, guiltily, to think of her as Grandmother Birch for the pale strips she peeled and smoked over the fire — had spent three nights teaching it to me. Not teaching, exactly. That was too generous a word for what happened in her smoke-darkened house at the edge of the blank village. She would press the bark into my hands, and on its surface were scratched marks like bird tracks and falling rain, and she would tap each one with a cracked yellow fingernail and watch my face until I understood.

The language of the sold was not spoken so much as *remembered*. That was her first lesson, delivered in the flat voice of someone who had forgotten how to want things. The tongue beneath the mountain had no word for *I* that stood apart from *we*, no future tense that did not imply debt, no way to say *mine* without also saying *owed*. Every syllable was a small contract. To speak it was to sign something.

"No wonder they took it from us," she said, or rather, this is what I understood her to say, through gestures and the fragments of common speech the nameless ones still clung to like coats gone threadbare at the elbows. "No wonder it sounds like singing when the children say it. A song is just a promise you make to the air."

* * *

Ash sat by the hearth in Sera's house with his knees drawn up and his bare feet, at last, wrapped in wool. Vael had traded two silver buttons off her priestess's coat for them — the coat itself she refused to sell, and I did not ask why. The boy watched the flames the way he always did, with the patient attention of someone waiting for a fire to tell him something important.

In my lap lay the notebook where I had copied out every sound he'd made since the night he appeared at my door. Pages of it. My surveyor's hand recording syllables the way I'd once recorded elevations: *this ridge runs northeast; this vowel falls at the end, like something set down gently.* Beside the notebook, the birchbark key, and beside that, the translations I'd been building all morning while snow ticked against the shutters.

I had checked the work eleven times. I checked it a twelfth time anyway, because what the words said frightened me more than any unmapped chasm ever had.

"Vael," I said quietly.

She was sharpening a knife on a whetstone by the door, a habit of hers I'd come to read like weather. When her hands went still, trouble was coming. When they moved in slow even strokes, she was simply keeping herself together. Now the strokes were slow and even, and she didn't look up. "You've finished it."

"I've finished enough."

"Then don't." The whetstone whispered against steel. "Some doors you find are better left drawn shut."

"He's a child, Vael. He's been talking at me for weeks and I've been nodding like an idiot. He thinks I understand him. Maybe worse — maybe he thinks I *agree* with him."

Now she looked up. Her eyes in the firelight were the color of cooling coals, that gray-gold that ember-priestesses' eyes became after years of tending contracts, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before. Not warning. Pity. It was aimed at me, and I didn't yet know why.

"Read it to yourself first," she said. "All of it. Then decide what the boy needs to hear. Those are two different documents, Juniper Ravensworth, and every priest learns the difference long before they learn anything else."

* * *

So I read.

The boy remembers being chosen.

That was the heart of it, repeated across a dozen pages in a dozen grammatical shapes the old tongue could build around a single idea, because the old tongue loved one idea the way a river loves its bed. Ash remembered standing in a doorway during the famine year — our famine year, the winter the Thornfell passes closed and the grain wagons didn't come and half the villages down-valley boiled pine bark for soup. He remembered his mother's hands, chapped and shaking. He remembered his father kneeling to look him in the eye, which his father never did, his father being a man who spoke to children over their heads as though they were furniture.

He remembered the words: *You are the strong one. You are the one who will not starve.* And he remembered believing it — believing it the way only a six-year-old can believe, whole and unguarded, that being given away was not being thrown away but being *sent*. Sent somewhere warm. Somewhere under the mountain where the fires never died and the tables never emptied. Somewhere his parents could visit, when the roads opened. Somewhere he would be waited for.

Because that was the part that undid me, sitting there with the notebook trembling in my hands while the fire popped and Vael pretended to study her knife. Ash believed they were coming for him. Every page of his small grammar said it. The future tense of the sold language implied debt — and Ash had built entire sentences, day after day, week after week, from that single implication. *They owe me a homecoming. They promised. A promise is a debt and debts are collected below and above alike.* He had worked it out with a child's merciless logic: if the Court kept its ledgers perfectly, if every soul was tallied and every contract honored to the letter, then his parents' promise was written down somewhere too. Ink did not forget. Ink did not grow poor. Ink did not boil pine bark in the dark and weep.

He had been waiting, all this time, not to be rescued.

He had been waiting to be *collected* — by the right people.

* * *

I must have made some sound, because Ash turned from the fire and looked at me, and his face did the thing it always did when he caught me studying him: a quick brightening, guarded hope held carefully behind his teeth like a coin he didn't dare spend. Then he signed — spoke — asked.

The key let me follow it now. *Did you read it? Do you know what I said?*

"I'm reading it," I said aloud, uselessly, and then caught myself and tried the shapes Sera had drilled into me. My accent must have been atrocious. My mouth kept trying to make the vowels mean *mine* instead of *owed*. But the boy's whole body changed. He uncurled. He slid off the bench and crossed the floor and put both small cold hands on either side of my face, the way you'd steady a map that wanted to curl at the corners, and he looked into my eyes from a hand's breadth away and began, slowly, deliberately, to speak. For me. At last, for me.

*I knew you would learn,* he said. *The map-man. Mother said the mountain sends helpers to those who wait well.*

"Ash—"

*You found the markings on the cave wall before you found me. That means the mountain was already telling you about me. That's how it works. That's how everything works.* His hands tightened fractionally on my jaw, urgent now. *When the equinox comes, the Court opens the high gate. That's in the songs. And my mother and father will walk down through the gate and collect me, because a promise is a debt, and the Magistrate keeps all debts, even theirs. Even theirs.* His voice — that strange chiming voice, the voice of the sold — cracked on the repetition. *Even theirs.*

What do you say to that?

Understand what I was holding. Understand precisely what I had in my hands at that moment, with the firelight moving on his face and his fingers at my jaw and Vael watching from the doorway with her knife stilled and her pity already spent on me. I was holding the truth: that his parents had taken payment. Grain, mostly — Vael had told me that much in the shop, in Thornfell, a lifetime ago. Two sacks of barley, a side of salt pork, and a copper kettle. That was the price of a soul, in a famine year. That was what a child weighed. They had signed the ledger page and taken the food and eaten it, most likely, all of it, gone the way food goes in a starving winter, and the promise Ash treasured — *they will come for me, they will come for me* — existed nowhere except in the shape a desperate six-year-old had pressed onto the memory of a doorway.

And I understood, suddenly and sickeningly, why the Court's language had no clean word for *chosen*. Why *chosen* and *sold* wore the same coat in that tongue, distinguishable only by context, only by who was speaking. The Court had not corrupted the word. The Court had merely declined to lie about it.

But I could lie. Lying was a skill of the surface world, of mapped countries, of men like me who drew borders through other people's lives and called it honest work.

*Do you see?* Ash was saying. *It's arithmetic. The Magistrate showed me the arithmetic once, in the counting room, before—* he faltered, and his eyes flicked toward the shuttered window, toward the north, toward the deep places we had fled and would have to enter again. Before whatever had happened down there that he would not speak of, the weeks between his sale and his escape, the gap in his story that neither Sera nor Vael nor anyone in the nameless village had been able to pry open. *Before. And debts are debts. She cannot refuse to honor a debt. Even she is bound by the ledger. So they will come. They have to come.*

"Ash." I took his hands from my face and held them. Small hands. Burn-scarred across the knuckles in patterns I now recognized as contract-marks, the Court's receipt stamped into its merchandise. "Listen to me."

He listened the way he did everything — completely, trustingly, with his whole self, holding nothing back, and God forgive me, that trust was the heaviest thing I carried on the whole journey, heavier than the ledger page, heavier than the crest on the door beneath the mountain.

"Your mother and father love you," I said.

Every word of that sentence was true. I have thought about it many times since, and I will tell you now what I told myself then: every word was true. Love is real even when it is worthless. Love is real even when it can be bought for two sacks of barley. Perhaps especially then.

"They love you," I said again, and the old tongue gave me the shapes for it awkwardly — *we-you-love*, because there was no *I* apart from *we*, no love apart from debt, and so what I actually said, what the language forced me to say, was closer to: *you are owed our loving.* "And the road is long, and the passes are cruel, and people who love sometimes move slowly. Slower than waiting. Slower than hoping. But the debt is real. What your parents promised is real. No one can take that from you."

Which was true. Which was all true.

I simply did not add that debts can be dishonored. That the debtor can die, or forget, or choose starvation's opposite and live with the taste of it forever. That a ledger entry is not a heartbeat. I let him pour his certainty into the space I left, and he filled it to the brim, and he smiled — Ash smiled, fully, for the first time since the night he arrived barefoot at my door with snow melting in his dark hair — and the smile went through me like a blade goes through a ledger page, clean and total, ruining everything it touches.

*Then we go down,* he said. *We go down and wait at the gate. You'll stay with me? Map-men are allowed at the gate. It's in the songs.*

"We'll go down," I said. "And I'll stay with you."

Behind me, very softly, Vael exhaled. I heard in it everything she wasn't going to say: that I had just lied to a child in the language of the sold, which might be the one language in creation where lies are impossible — where every syllable is a contract, where speaking is signing. If that were true, then I had not lied at all. I had made a promise. *You are owed our loving.* *The debt is real.* *I will stay with you.*

Three signatures. Given freely, in a tongue older than kingdoms, in front of a disgraced priestess and a burning fire and a boy who believed in ink the way other children believe in mothers.

* * *

Vael found me later on the porch of Sera's house, where I sat sketching the valley by moonlight out of pure habit, my hands needing to draw the way other men's hands need to drink.

"You told him they're coming for him," she said. Not a question.

"I told him the debt was real."

"That's not the same thing, and you know it. You're a cartographer, Juniper. Precision is your religion."

"Precision." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You want precision? Here's precise. Two sacks of barley. A side of salt pork. A copper kettle. I've done the sums, Vael. I've priced a child to the ounce and I know exactly what his parents got for him and I know they ate it. Every word I said to him was true and every word was a lie and I don't know how to hold both anymore. So yes. I chose the mercy. Sue me for it. Take it out of my ledger."

She sat down beside me on the step. For a while neither of us spoke, and the snow made its small endless whisper against the world, and somewhere in the blank village a dog that might not have existed barked at a moon nobody could quite perceive.

"My first year as a priestess," Vael said at last, "I recorded forty-one sales. Famine year. I wrote the contracts in fair hand, I witnessed the signatures, I collected the payments into the tally-room and I told myself the same thing you just told him: the debt is real, the ink does not forget, the Court honors its paper. It was easier than the alternative, which was to stand up in the cathedral of cinders and call it murder in front of the Magistrate wearing her borrowed faces." She turned the silver-buttonless cuff of her sleeve between her fingers. "Mercy isn't lying to him, Juniper. Mercy would be if the lie were kind. What you did tonight was give him a reason to keep walking into the one place designed to eat reasons. That's not mercy. That's fuel."

"And what's the alternative? Tell him the truth and have him refuse to go? Have him run? We have—" I stopped. Calculated. The equinox was nine days off. Eight, perhaps, by the time we reached the Hollow Stair. "We have days, Vael. Days. He walks down on his own feet or we carry him, and either way he arrives. The only question is what he believes when he gets there."

"The only question," Vael said quietly, "is what you believe. Because you signed three contracts tonight in a language where speaking is binding, and one of them was *I will stay with you*. The Court heard it. The Court hears everything said in its tongue, even up here, even in the snow." She rose, brushing ash — real ash, from Sera's fire, from the burned contracts the villagers fed it nightly like a sacrament — from her coat. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we buy our passage across the toll bridge. And I'd think hard, before then, about which of your memories you can bear to lose. The bridge takes small things. It always takes small things first."

She went inside. I stayed on the step a while longer, looking at my sketch of the valley, at the little X I had unconsciously marked where the Hollow Stair went down into the dark.

Then I noticed what my traitor hand had drawn in the margin, without my permission, in the shorthand I used for hazards and warnings:

*A promise is a debt. Debts are collected below.*

I tore the page out and fed it to the stove.
