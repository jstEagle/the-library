## 10. The Toll Bridge of Small Things

The road stopped pretending to be a road on the third morning.

Vael had warned Juniper it would. Roads below a certain depth were not built but remembered, and the mountain's memory was a jealous thing—it kept only what pleased it, and what pleased the mountain was rarely kind to travelers. So when the packed earth beneath their boots frayed into threads of gray mist and the mist frayed into nothing at all, she did not slow her pace. She only tightened her grip on Ash's small hand and said, in the tongue of the sold, *Walk where I walk. Count your steps. Do not count anything else.*

Ash counted.

Juniper watched them both from half a stride behind, the way he had watched everything since the Hollow Stair—with the wary attention of a man who had spent his life trusting paper and was learning, slowly and painfully, that paper could lie. He carried his satchel of maps against his chest like a shield, though Vael had told him twice already that no chart he owned would serve him past this point. The mountain would redraw itself around their memories faster than any pen could follow.

*Then we will walk by memory,* he had said, with the stubbornness of a man who did not yet understand what that would cost him.

Now he understood. They all stood at the edge of it.

The bridge appeared out of the mist the way grief appears—suddenly, and as if it had always been there, waiting. It spanned a chasm that had no bottom they could see, only a faint orange glow far below, like embers raked over in a hearth left to die. The bridge itself was made of stone so pale it looked bleached, and it was narrow—narrower than any cart-road, narrower than sense—and along its balustrade, at intervals of perhaps ten paces, sat small stone figures no taller than a candlestick.

They were shaped like people. Hunched people, kneeling people, people with their heads bowed and their hands cupped open before them, as if begging or offering. Their faces were smooth. Blank as eggs.

"The Toll Bridge of Small Things," Vael said quietly, in the trade-tongue, because some words should not be spoken even in the old language. "Beyond this is the Court's outer march. Nothing crosses without paying."

Juniper came up beside her. His breath made ghosts in the cold air. "Pay with what? We have no coin worth anything down here."

"Coin." She almost smiled. It felt like a crack running through ice. "No. The toll is smaller than coin and heavier than gold. Each traveler gives up one small memory. Something dear but not essential. A taste. A scent. A moment. The Court collects them the way a miser collects buttons—it cannot use them, but it cannot bear to leave them unowned." She nodded at the little kneeling figures. "Those are the payments. Every stone beggar on this rail was once something someone carried in their heart—a first snowfall, a grandmother's kitchen, the sound of rain on a specific roof in a specific summer. Given up freely, they harden. That is the price of crossing: you leave behind a piece of yourself and walk on lighter and poorer and unable to say exactly what you lost."

Ash tugged her sleeve. In the old tongue, the boy signed and mouthed his question: *Does it hurt?*

Vael crouched to his height. "It hurts the way a splinter hurts," she told him. "Not while it goes in. After. When you reach for something and find the place where it used to be empty."

She saw Juniper watching them, his jaw working. "And if someone refuses?" he asked.

"Then the bridge refuses them." She rose. "There is another toll. Older. The bridge will take blood instead of memory—but it takes a great deal of blood, and it takes it slowly, and it does not care whether you have enough to give. The sold used it, in the escape years, when they had no memories left worth taking. The nameless ones down there still call it the Red Crossing." She met his eyes. "I do not recommend it."

"You've crossed this way before," Juniper said. It was not quite a question.

"I have crossed everything there is to cross between here and the Court," Vael said. "That is what disgrace means. You keep walking after everyone else stops."

* * *

They paid in order of age, Vael decided. Herself last, so that if the bridge took more than it should—if the toll-figures grew greedy and grasped for something larger than a button-memory—she would be standing between the Court and the others, as she had failed to stand between the Court and anyone for thirty years.

Juniper went first.

He stood at the near end of the bridge for a long time, wind pulling at his coat, and Vael watched him turn his memory over in his hands the way he must once have turned over maps, looking for creases, judging value. She knew what he was doing. He was searching for something he could bear to lose. And she knew, with the sick certainty of long acquaintance with the Ember Court, that whatever he chose would hurt him more than he expected, because men like Juniper Ravensworth—who had spent their whole lives belonging to nowhere and no one—carried their small treasures deeper than other men did. Buried treasure. Hard to dig out. Harder to replace.

He knelt at last before the nearest stone figure and cupped his hands the way the statue's hands were cupped, an ugly mirror, and spoke aloud: "I give my mother's lullaby."

The mist went still.

Vael had seen souls pay this toll before, long ago, in her priesthood days, when she'd stood at the bridge's far side as an officer of the Court and watched the desperate cross toward it. She had forgotten, until now, how quiet it got. How the whole chasm seemed to lean in and listen.

Juniper began to hum.

His voice was not good. It wavered, cracked, found itself again—a rough, uncertain thread of melody, six notes rising and falling, rising and falling, the shape of a song sung to a child in a dark room in a cold house in a life he had never spoken of to either of them. As he hummed, the melody became visible. That was the horror of the toll; Vael had never decided if it was mercy or cruelty that the payment showed itself. The notes turned to faint golden light in the air above his cupped hands, coiling like smoke, gathering into a small warm knot of glow that pulsed gently, in rhythm, like something breathing.

Like something alive.

"It doesn't feel like mine anymore," Juniper whispered. He was crying without seeming to know it. "I can see it sitting there and it doesn't—I know it's the song, I remember knowing it, but it's like reading about someone else's mother." His hands shook. The light-pulse trembled with them. "Is this what it's like? Is this what the sold feel all the time?"

"Yes," Vael said. "This is a splinter of it. Now let go."

He opened his fingers.

The knot of light drifted down into the cupped stone hands of the figure, and settled, and dimmed, and hardened, and where a blank egg-face had been, a face now pressed faintly outward against the stone—a woman's face, young, tired, singing—the features blurred as though behind frosted glass, but there. Present. A mother's lullaby, wearing stone.

Juniper knelt there a moment longer. Then he stood, wiped his face with the back of his hand in the rough efficient way of a man who resents every tear, and walked onto the bridge without looking back.

He made it across. Of course he made it across. But halfway over he stumbled—not on the stones, Vael saw, but on nothing, on absence, on the sudden hollow where a thing had been—and he caught himself against the balustrade beside the little figure that wore his mother's face, and he stood there a long moment with his head bowed, and then he walked on.

Ash went next, and Vael's chest tightened, because the boy had so little. What does a child own? A few winters of fear, a door, a fire, voices through a wall, hunger, the smell of his mother's hair. The Court would take any of it gladly.

But Ash surprised her. The boy knelt and gave up, of all things, a game—a clapping rhyme two village children had taught him one bright afternoon before everything darkened, four nonsense syllables and a pattern of palms. Small. Bright. Useless. Wise. The boy understood instinctively what adults never learn: give the bridge something true but shallow. Give it a pebble, not a cornerstone.

Even so, when his small light-knot sank into the stone and a child's laughing face surfaced briefly in the figure's blankness, Ash stared at his own open palms as if they had betrayed him, trying to remember the pattern of the game and finding only the shape of clapping with nothing inside it. Then he crossed, quick and light, counting his steps as Vael had taught him, and did not look back at all. Children are better at loss than we are, Vael thought. They have not yet learned to build their houses out of it.

* * *

Which left Vael alone on the near side, and the bridge waiting, and the choice she had made thirty years ago in a different form, standing before her again in stone.

She had known, walking down out of the passes, that this moment was coming. She had rehearsed refusals the way soldiers rehearse dying. Because here was the truth she had never spoken aloud, not to Juniper, not to the nameless elder, not to the dark of her own tent on the worst nights:

Vael did not trust herself to choose a memory.

She knew what the bridge would ask for. Not ask—take what she offered, yes, but memories were not honest currency from her hands. If she knelt and opened her hands, what would she give? She knew too well what she would give. She would give the wrong thing. She would give something cheap and call it dear, or something dear and pretend it was cheap, and either way she would walk off this bridge having cheated, and cheating this bridge was how the Court got its hooks into a soul forever. The toll forgave poverty. It did not forgive lies.

Or worse—the thought she could barely look at, the thought that had been following her for three decades like a dog she couldn't lose—worse, she would kneel, and she would search herself honestly, and she would discover that the memory dearest to her, the one her hands would open around without her permission, was the memory of recording Ash's sale. The scratch of her stylus. The parents' faces. The exact weight of the silence after she read the contract aloud. Thirty years of penance, and her deepest treasure—the thing she had polished and carried and refused to set down—was her guilt. She had built her whole self around it. It was the only room in her house.

Let the Court have that? Let the Court file her guilt away among its buttons, owned, catalogued, held? Never. Her shame was the one thing she owned outright, bought with her own hands, paid for in full. She would not surrender it to a Magistrate who collected suffering the way other women collected jewelry.

So. The other toll.

"There is blood enough in me," she said aloud, to the bridge, to the figures, to the orange glow below. "Take it and be damned to you."

She drew her knife—the old priestess's blade, its edge worn thin from years of uses it was never forged for—and she cut across her left palm, deep, the way the rite demanded, and she pressed her bleeding hand against the cold forehead of the first kneeling figure.

The bridge drank.

There was no gentleness in it. Memory-toll was a transaction; blood-toll was a feeding. She felt the pull begin in the wound and travel inward, into the wrist, the arm, the chest, a slow patient suction like the mountain itself nursing at her, and she knelt down on the stone and gave it what it wanted and did not cry out, because she had made a vow of stubbornness years ago and stubbornness was, she had come to understand, simply armor worn so long you forgot it was armor and believed it was skin.

The world went gray at the edges. The chasm-glow below seemed to brighten, or her eyes seemed to fail; she could not tell which. She thought absurdly of Juniper's maps, of clean lines and confident borders, of a man who had spent his life drawing the world as if it could be known. The world could not be known. The world could only be paid for, over and over, in pieces, and the paying never ended, and still you walked on, because the ones behind you needed the road to hold.

Somewhere far away, Juniper was shouting her name. She heard it as if through water. She thought: *don't come back across, you fool, the toll takes twice from those who turn around.* She tried to say it and had no voice.

She lost time. She did not know how much—an hour, a day. When she came back to herself she was lying on the far side of the bridge with her hand bound in strips of her own shirt hem, the knot clumsy, done by someone else's fingers, and Juniper was crouched over her with a face like winter, and Ash was pressing his small cold hands flat against her cheeks, one on each side, as if he could hold her together by touch alone.

"How much did it take?" she rasped.

"Too much." Juniper's voice was raw. "You were gray. You were gray for hours. There's a—" he stopped, steadied himself. "There's a trail of it. Across the bridge. Where they dragged you. Your blood froze on the stones."

"Good," Vael said. "Let it mark the way. Blood remembers longer than ink."

She meant it as a joke. It came out as scripture, and Juniper flinched, and she realized, distantly, that she had frightened him—that he had spent the whole crossing watching her bleed and been unable to do anything, and that helplessness sat on him badly, the way it sits on all men who have only ever saved themselves with pens.

"Ash translated for me," he said quietly. "While you slept. He said—" Juniper hesitated, and Vael saw him decide, deliberately, to say the true thing instead of the kind one, and loved him for it despite herself. "He said you do this. Refuse. Pay more than you have to rather than give up one single thing you don't have to give. He said the nameless ones have a word for it. He said it isn't a compliment."

"No," Vael agreed, staring up at the colorless sky. "It isn't. Stubbornness is just fear wearing boots. I know precisely what I am."

"And yet you're still here."

"And yet." She sat up slowly. The chasm yawned beside them, patient as ever. Somewhere below, the ember-glow pulsed once, twice, like a sleeping heartbeat—or like something turning over in its sleep, beginning to notice.

She got to her feet. Her legs held. Her hand throbbed in time with her pulse, and each beat reminded her of the price, and she was glad of the reminder. Pain was honest accounting. Pain never lied about what things cost.

Ahead of them, the road resumed—narrower now, descending in long switchbacks into a darkness that glimmered faintly red, as though the mountain had swallowed a sunset and kept it. The outer marches of the Ember Court. The air had begun to taste of ash and hot iron, and beneath that, faintly, almost sweetly, of burning paper.

Burning contracts.

Vael flexed her bandaged hand and started down. Behind her, Juniper gathered Ash up onto his hip—the boy was seven and far too big to be carried, and neither of them remarked on it—and followed.

They had crossed the toll. They had paid in song and laughter and blood.

What waited below would ask for everything else.

And far ahead, down in the red-dark where the switchbacks vanished, Vael saw it: a point of light moving along the lower road. Moving toward them. Deliberate. Unhurried. Wearing, even at this distance, even in that shifting ember-glow, the unmistakable silhouette of a traveler who knew exactly who was coming down to meet them—

—and who had been sent to greet them by name.
