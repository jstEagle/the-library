## 13. A Century in One Night

The Magistrate did not ask him to sit. She simply turned, and the cathedral of cinders turned with her, and Juniper understood that the chair she led him toward had been waiting for him longer than he had been alive.

It stood at the center of the Ember Court's great hall, where the ceiling of cooled lava arched overhead like the ribs of some drowned leviathan. Contracts burned along every wall — not paper, but names, each one a slow flame the color of a hearth at dusk, and each flame cast a face into the smoke above it. He had seen those faces on the way down. Some of them had wept. Most of them had only watched.

The chair was black basalt, worn smooth at the arms as if by centuries of hands. A drafting table rose from its side like a growth, and upon it lay quills of ember-feather and ink that glowed faintly, like coals banked for morning.

"Sit," said the Magistrate, in a voice made of many voices — his mother's lullaby was still gone from him, but something in her tone snagged on that absence anyway, a hook where tenderness used to hang. "And let me show you what you are refusing."

"I haven't refused yet," Juniper said. His mouth was dry. Somewhere behind him, Vael's breathing had gone very quiet, the quiet of someone holding a blade she is not permitted to draw.

"No," she agreed, and the borrowed faces across her own shifted like weather. "You haven't."

He sat. The stone was warm as skin.

* * *

The vision began with snow.

Not the snow outside Thornfell — cleaner than that, kinder. It fell through lamplight onto the cobbles of a street he knew too well to doubt: his own street, below his own shop, except the shop was larger now, its windows glowing gold, its sign freshly painted. *Ravensworth & Son.* He felt his stomach turn before his mind caught up, because he had never painted that name on anything, and yet his hands in the vision were steady on the brush, and the letters came out of him like they had always lived there.

In the vision, people stopped to greet him. The baker's widow pressed a loaf into his hands and would not take payment. Children ran ahead of him shouting his name — *Juniper, Juniper, Master Ravensworth* — and their voices did not grate the way praise always had, the way it had always sounded to him like a door closing. It sounded like a door opening. It sounded like coming home to a house where someone had kept the fire.

A century passed inside an hour.

He watched himself grow old in the vision the way one watches weather change. He saw his hair silver, his hands stay sure. He saw the maps he drew in that glowing ink become famous — *the honest charts*, scholars called them, though the vision did not trouble itself with irony. He saw himself honored by guilds he'd spent his life mocking, seated at high tables, asked to speak about the deep places, and speaking, and being listened to. Purpose poured through the vision like light through honey. Every morning had a shape. Every night ended with work well done and a name spoken warmly behind him as he climbed the stairs.

*Ravensworth. Ravensworth. Master Ravensworth.*

He had spent forty years belonging to no one, and here was a hundred years of belonging laid out like a banquet, and the terrible thing — the thing he would be ashamed of later, alone, in the dark — was how easy it went down. How hungry he was without having known it. How his throat worked around the vision's warmth like a man swallowing soup after a week of ice.

"This is what the Court offers its cartographer," the Magistrate murmured, and her voice was everywhere now, in the falling snow, in the lamplight, in the mouths of the children. "A name that opens doors. Work that matters. A place."

"And Ash?" he managed.

"Ah." The vision rippled. "Yes. Let us not forget the boy."

* * *

The scene changed, and the kindness drained out of it so gradually that at first he could not say what had altered — only that the light had gone thin, the way light goes in late autumn when the year begins dying without apology.

Ash stood at a window.

Not his window. A window in a house Juniper did not know, in a town he did not know, and the boy at the glass was taller — no, wait, the boy was the same, and the years were moving, and that was the horror of it, watching a child remain a child while everything around him aged. Snow fell outside the window. It was always snowing outside the window. Ash's breath fogged the pane and he drew shapes in it with one finger — shapes Juniper recognized with a jolt that went through him like a hook through the hand: the syllables of the old tongue, the language older than any kingdom, written over and over in condensation that vanished as fast as it formed.

Nobody came to wipe the glass. Nobody came at all.

The vision moved through Ash's life the way water moves through a cracked vessel. A foster-house where the boy slept in the cold corner and woke before dawn to haul water because children who speak no known tongue are given the chores no one argues about. A winter when he was sick and no one sat with him, because no one quite remembered he was there — the neighbors' eyes slid off him like rain off slate. A spring when he stood at the edge of the thawing pass looking north, waiting, certain, faithful in the way only the sold can be faithful, that his parents would come.

They did not come. Or they came and could not find him, or they came and looked straight at him and walked past, because —

Because.

Juniper felt it then, the flaw in the vision, the way a cartographer feels a false contour line: everything else true to scale, and one river running uphill.

"Show me my shop," he said quietly. "In your hundred years. Show me who keeps it after me."

The Magistrate hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a hairline crack in her composed face, but Juniper had spent his life reading terrain, and terrain does not lie; it only waits.

"Show me," he said again, "who remembers me."

* * *

The Court obliged, because the Court could not do otherwise. That was the thing about contracts, Vael had told him once, in the blue hour before the Hollow Stair: they are compulsions even for the ones who write them.

The vision showed him Thornfell, a hundred years on. The shop was there — *Ravensworth & Son* — but the sign hung crooked, and the paint had gone gray, and when travelers asked the innkeeper whose shop it had been, the innkeeper frowned and said a mapmaker, wasn't it, someone, the name wouldn't come. His oldest friend Brannoc Hale, ancient and half-remembered already, stared through the vision-Juniper as if through glass, and said to his granddaughter, "There was a man. Clever with lines. I can't — there was a man."

No grave bore his name. No letter mentioned him. In the vision's final years, Juniper watched a young scholar hold one of his famous charts to the light and marvel at it, and say, wonderingly, "Whoever drew this must have loved these mountains more than anyone alive," and the room agreed, and no one knew, and no one ever would know, because the Court does not pay its debts in coin.

It pays them in memory. It had taken his mother's lullaby at the bridge, a small toll, a taste. A century of service would cost everything the toll had only sampled. Every mind he had ever touched would close around the space where he had been, smooth as water, seamless as snowfall. He would be adored inside the mountain and erased outside it. Honored by no one who could keep him. Belonging, purchased at the price of every hand that might have held him.

The vision showed him Ash one last time — grown now, gray now, standing at the same window, drawing the same vanishing syllables on the same glass. And in the fog of the boy's breath, Juniper read the words at last, the ones the elder's translations had given him the key to, the ones Ash had been writing all his life:

*I was chosen. Someone will come.*

The condensation ran. The words dissolved. Nobody came.

* * *

The vision released him like a fist unclenching.

Juniper found himself still in the basalt chair, hands gripping its arms hard enough that the warmth of the stone had printed itself into his palms. The hall of burning contracts roared softly around him, ten thousand small flames, ten thousand faces in the smoke.

"Well?" said the Magistrate. Her borrowed faces had settled into something almost gentle — his mother's gentleness, though he could no longer recall his mother's face, only the shape of the feeling. "A century of purpose against a lifetime of drifting. A name against a void. You have wanted to belong since the day you were born, Juniper Ravensworth. Here is belonging, gift-wrapped in gold light. All it costs is—"

"Everything," Juniper said. His voice came out rough, scraped raw by the vision's ending. "It costs everything. That's not a price, that's a theft with better lighting."

He stood. His legs shook. He made them hold.

"You showed me a hundred years of being loved," he said, "and not one soul left above ground who can say my name. You showed me honored in the dark and forgotten in the light. You didn't offer me belonging, Magistrate. You offered me a beautiful cell with my crest on the door — the same crest, I think, that's been on that door for six generations." He looked up at her, at the shifting faces, and found, beneath all of them, something that flickered and would not meet his eye. "Your vision has a flaw. Rivers don't run uphill. And love that costs everyone else their memory of you isn't love. It's just loneliness with an audience."

The flames along the walls guttered, all together, as if the Court itself had drawn breath.

Behind him, Vael moved — one step, soft on the cooled stone — and the Magistrate's attention snapped past Juniper to her, and the gentleness fell away from her borrowed face like ash from a log.

"You should not have brought the priestess," she said, and the many voices in hers went cold and aligned, one will, one hunger. "The equinox is tomorrow night, cartographer. You have until then to choose your chair."

She raised one hand, and the doors of the hall swung open onto a corridor of dim red light, and two figures stepped through it wearing faces Juniper knew — Brannoc's grin stretched wrong on a stranger's skull, his own father's frown on another — and Juniper understood, with the clarity that comes only after a dream curdles, exactly what kind of century the Court gave to those who refused.

"Take them to the cells," said the Magistrate. "The heir needs a night alone with his inheritance."
