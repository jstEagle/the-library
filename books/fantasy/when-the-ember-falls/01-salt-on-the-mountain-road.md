## 1. Salt on the Mountain Road

The thing about being indispensable, Mara Joubert had learned by his seventeenth winter and confirmed every year since, was that it got you killed. Indispensable people were chained to their usefulness like goats to a stake — and sooner or later, something came along that wanted goat.

So he had built his whole life on the opposite principle: be useful exactly as long as it pays, then become a rumor. A name on no ledger. A man who had passed through, who might have been taller, or shorter, or a woman in a hood, depending on who was asking. He'd kept this discipline for eleven years of guiding caravans through the Thornfell passes, and it had kept his throat uncut through two border wars, one plague, and the entire career of a bandit captain named Osk who held grudges the way other men held religion.

Which made what he'd done three nights ago all the more remarkable, because there was no way to do it without becoming needed.

He had stolen from Old Wrenn.

Not coin — she kept her coin in a boot and would have noticed within the hour. Not food; she'd have been glad to be rid of him at table. He had stolen the *book*. The grimoire with the cracked leather spine and the brass clasps worn to nubs, the one she kept under a loose hearthstone and pretended not to have, the one whose pages smelled of tallow smoke and something older, something like the inside of a bell. He'd wrapped it in oilcloth and strapped it beneath his shirt against his spine, where it rode like a second shoulder blade, and he had walked out of her hut while she slept sitting up, which she did more often now, her hands folded like a woman waiting for something.

She had opened one eye as he reached the door. He was almost sure of it.

"Thief," she'd said pleasantly, to the dark, or to him, or possibly to the fire. "Run well."

And he had run well. Three days down the western slopes, taking trails that existed mostly as arguments between goats, until he'd intersected the salt road at Cindermill Ford and talked himself onto Doss Havel's caravan as guide for the high pass — because a man fleeing one kind of trouble looks less suspicious when he's visibly employed in another.

"You're quiet," said Doss Havel now, riding up alongside Mara's mule. Havel was a salt merchant built along the general lines of a rain barrel, with a voice that carried over avalanches and a laugh that had ended negotiations prematurely for twenty years. "Guides who are quiet are either thinking or dying. Which is it?"

"Thinking about how much you're paying me."

"That I can believe," Havel said cheerfully. "Because it's nothing."

"It's an insult is what it is. Half up front, half at Thornfell's saddle, and you've paid me the half that buys my silence, not my judgment."

"And your judgment says?"

Mara nodded ahead, where the trail climbed out of the pines into the grey scree below the pass. Snow sat on the heights like a debt waiting to come due. "Your judgment says take the low road around by Vess like the toll-keepers want, because it's wide and marked and safe." He let that sit. "My judgment says the low road has been closed since the thaw — rockfall at the Narrows, if you believe the shepherds, and I do, because I paid them to tell me true. My judgment also says your lead mule is favoring her near foreleg, and if we take the high road at a proper pace instead of your idea of a pace, she'll hold. If we push her the way you pushed her yesterday, you'll be carrying four hundredweight of salt on your own back, and I've seen your back."

Havel looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed — briefly, mercifully — and turned in the saddle. "You heard the man! We go high, we go slow, and anyone who complains can complain to the mountain!"

The caravan groaned in the way caravans do, a collective sound of men discovering new reasons to resent their employer, and Mara felt the familiar small satisfaction settle into place. Not gratitude. Gratitude was a chain. Just satisfaction: the clean arithmetic of a job done correctly, which would end at the saddle below Thornfell, where he would collect the rest of his pay and evaporate before anyone thought to ask him to stay for the return trip.

Never be needed twice. That was the whole religion of it.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a hollow below the tree line, a smuggler's camp Mara knew from years when guiding honest salt hadn't been the only work available to a hungry boy. It was a good hollow — hidden from the trail, sheltered from the north wind, and blessedly out of earshot of anyone's conscience.

He should have left the grimoire behind. He knew that. You didn't steal from a hedge-witch; everyone in the mountains knew it, the way they knew not to whistle in caves and not to count the rings on a felled elder-tree. But he'd spent six years chopping her wood and brewing her teas and pretending not to notice the things she muttered over the kettle, and in all that time she'd never once told him what the book was, only that he wasn't ready, that he was *not for it*, that some doors were better left carrying someone else's weight. And then last month he'd caught her looking at him across the fire with that open eye of hers, and she'd said, mild as milk:

"You'll want to know the way through soon, lad. Best I teach you the words for it."

And Mara Joubert, who survived by never being needed by anyone, had understood in his bones that whatever door she meant, she meant for *him* to be standing in it when it closed.

So he'd taken the book and run. Simple. Sensible.

Except that now, sitting apart from Havel's snoring drovers with the oilcloth bundle against his knees, he found he couldn't leave it in a streambed or sell it to a collector in the lowlands. His hands wouldn't arrange it. Every time he considered burying the thing under stones, some part of him — the part that remembered Wrenn's open eye, and *run well* — pointed out that a witch who let herself be robbed probably wanted to be robbed, and that throwing away a gift was worse than stealing one.

"Trouble sleeping?" said a voice.

One of the drovers — the young one, Pell's-age, named Tam with a nose like a broken stair — had wandered over with two cups of something steaming. He offered one. Mara took it, because refusing tea started conversations longer than accepting it.

"Sleeping fine," Mara said.

"You're holding your book like it owes you money."

Mara looked down. He had, without deciding to, unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth. He tucked it back. "Ledger," he said. "Salt accounts."

"At night. Under a blanket. With the clasps facing away from the fire." Tam grinned. He had the particular courage of young men who had not yet been robbed of anything worth guarding. "My gran read cards. She used to say you can always tell a secret-keeper by the shoulders. Yours are up around your ears, friend."

"Your gran sounds like she should guide caravans."

"She died. Fever." Tam drank his tea and gazed at the stars with the frank appetite of the very young. "Funny, though. Before she went, she said the old days were coming back. Said the mountain gods were turning over in their sleep like men before the dawn bell. Everyone laughed." He shrugged. "Gran was usually right about weather, though. And people."

"Well," Mara said, "she was wrong about gods."

"You'd know?"

"I know the mountains don't answer anybody. That's the one mercy of them." He drained the tea and handed back the cup. "Get some sleep. We climb early, and the scree doesn't care how charming you are."

Tam went off whistling, which in a cave country was practically blasphemy, and Mara lay back with the book warm against his spine and told himself, firmly, that in four days he would be over the saddle and down into the lowlands and lost among ten thousand strangers, and none of it — not witches, not books, not boys with dead grandmothers — would ever find him again.

It was a good speech. He gave it often. Lately it had begun to sound, even to him, like a prayer.

* * *

The second day was the day the road stopped being a road.

This was the point where honest guides turned back and dishonest ones earned their pay, and Mara did neither: he simply led them off the trail at a cairn that looked like every other cairn unless you knew that its top stone had been split by lightning and set back wrong-side up. From there the smuggler's path bent west through a fold of the mountain that the maps had never bothered insulting with an opinion — narrow, green-shadowed, threaded with meltwater streams that sang in a key that made the mules nervous. It added half a day. It subtracted every toll, every patrol, and every set of eyes between here and the saddle.

"How," asked Havel, breathing hard beside him, "does a track like this exist? Who cuts it?"

"Nobody cuts it. You keep it. You walk it enough times that it remembers you." Mara stepped over a fallen birch and heard, behind him, eleven men and forty mules negotiate the same obstacle with varying dignity. "Smugglers have used it since before the kingdom drew its lines. The lines moved. The path didn't."

"Hm." Havel was quiet for a stretch, which from Havel was roughly equivalent to another man's funeral silence. Then: "You know, most guides talk. All the time. Points out the eagles. Tells you the history of every rock. Makes you feel like the mountain's their cousin."

"I'm not most guides."

"No," Havel agreed. "Most guides also don't know where the patrols aren't. Most guides don't carry themselves like a man walking away from a burning house that hasn't caught yet." He smiled his barrel-wide smile. "I'm not complaining. Best money I never spent. Just noting that when we get to Thornfell's saddle, I may make you an offer to stay on for the return."

"There's no offer you can make."

"Everyone has a price."

"Everyone has a price," Mara said. "Mine is being paid already. That's the trick of it — you pay a man enough to leave, and he leaves. Pay him to stay, and now you own him, and everything that owns a man gets to watch him die defending it." He said it lightly, the way you say true things so nobody checks whether they're true. "Take the free advice. Never need anyone twice."

Havel chewed on that for a quarter mile. "Bleak trade you're in, friend."

"It's the only one where the merchandise is yourself. Bleakness comes standard."

* * *

On the third afternoon, the weather turned thoughtful.

Not bad — not yet. But the light went strange and coppery along the western crags, and the wind died all at once, the way it does when the mountain inhales, and the mules began doing the thing mules do when they know something: planting their feet and staring at nothing, ears swiveled toward the heights.

Mara had crossed the Thornfell pass thirty-one times. He had seen whiteouts, rockslides, a bridge collapse under a wool convoy, and one memorable occasion involving a bear, a honey cart, and a drover named Fitch whose screaming could be heard in two valleys. The mountain had never once frightened him. Mountains were honest. They wanted what they wanted — your caution, your respect, occasionally your corpse — and they told you so plainly.

But this stillness wasn't honest. This was the stillness of a room when someone has just entered it.

"Camp early," he told Havel. "Full camp. Double ropes on the animals."

"Sky's clear as a bell."

"Bells ring," Mara said. "That's rather the problem with them."

Havel studied him, and for once didn't laugh, and gave the order. They made camp in a fold of scree below a leaning shelf of granite, and the men grumbled about losing an hour, and the mules stood in their picket line with their heads low, and above them the pass waited in its copper light like a held breath.

Supper was barley and salt pork, eaten fast, and Tam tried again to draw Mara into conversation — "so what's actually *in* the book, then?" — and Mara said, "salt accounts," and Tam said, "you're a terrible liar, you know that? You lie like a man reading from a list," and Mara was composing a reply that would end the conversation for good when the shepherd came down out of the rocks.

He was an old man, sheepskin-wrapped, driving three ragged ewes ahead of him with a crook worn smooth as driftwood. He came into the firelight nodding to everyone, the way mountain folk did, and asked could he share the fire, and Havel said yes of course, sit, eat — because Havel was generous the way big men often were, broadly and without much thought.

And the shepherd sat, and ate, and then, with the ease of a man mentioning rain, he said:

"Heard you'd be coming over the saddle. Prayed you'd come before the snows." He smiled around the fire with red, wind-cracked lips. "Prayed hard. Lost two lambs this spring. Asked the old ones for a kindness, and here you all are, sent along like an answer."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke. Somewhere up-slope a stone ticked against another stone, though there was no wind to move it.

Mara set down his bowl. "Old ones," he said carefully, "don't send caravans. Caravans come because salt's cheap in the lowlands and dear over the pass. That's not a god. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," the shepherd agreed pleasantly. "That's what the priests in the valley used to say, back when there were priests in the valley. Then the shrine at Vess lit up green as spring grass three weeks past, and old Mother Annet prayed for her son's fever, and the fever *left* him — left him so fast it took the flesh off his arm with it, but left him, mind, left him living." He tore his bread and chewed, unhurried, watching the flames. "Gods waking, some say. Gods answering, others say. Me, I just pray, and things happen, and I've stopped asking whether it's arithmetic." He looked up, and his eyes caught the firelight and held it a beat too long, the way water holds a stone. "You've a long climb tomorrow, guide. You might say a word to them yourself. Costs nothing. Everything costs something, but the word itself is free."

Mara's hand had gone, without his permission, to the oilcloth bundle at his spine. The book was warm. Warmer than his body had any business making it. Warm the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire's out — as if something underneath had recently been awake.

"Thank you," he said, "for the warning."

"Wasn't a warning, lad." The shepherd rose, gathered his crook, clicked his tongue to his ewes. "Was a greeting." He paused at the edge of the firelight, a dark shape against darker rock, and added — kindly, terribly — "They know your name, Mara Joubert. Sleep facing the mountain. It likes to be watched."

Then he was gone up the slope, and the three ewes' hoofbeats faded, though Mara could not afterward swear the sheep had made any sound at all going up ground that steep.

Havel laughed, uncertainly. "Mountain madmen. Every pass breeds them." But he posted a double watch that night without being asked, and Mara lay with the grimoire against his back and did not sleep, and above the camp the copper light drained slowly out of the west until the peaks stood black against the stars.

Toward midnight, Tam's voice floated softly across the camp, awed and a little afraid:

"Mara. Mara, you awake? There's a light up by the saddle. Green. Like — like grass on fire."

Mara sat up. Far above, at the head of the pass where the shrine of the old road stood — a shrine he had walked past thirty-one times without so much as a nod — a pillar of flame burned straight up into the windless sky, green as spring, green as fever leaving a body, and it cast no heat that he could feel, and no shadow that he could see.

Thirty-one crossings. Thirty-one years of arithmetic.

The mountain, it appeared, had finally done sums of its own — and the total was due in the morning.
