## 2. The First Answered Prayer

The shrine at Coldbrook Bend had been there longer than the road, longer than the village it was named for, and — if you asked Mara Joubert — considerably longer than anyone's interest in maintaining it. It was a low stone arch straddling a spring, furred with moss on its north side and lichen like old paint on its south, and for as long as Mara had been walking these mountains it had done precisely nothing except drip.

Which was exactly how he liked his gods: quiet, damp, and structurally load-bearing.

"Left fork past the shrine," he told Dossan, the caravan master, pointing with the stub of cheese he'd been saving since breakfast. "Not the high road. The high road washes out below the scree this time of year, and the mules know it even if your drover doesn't."

Dossan squinted at the fork, then at Mara, in the particular way of a man who had paid good coin for a guide and was still deciding whether he'd been sold a guide or a liability. "You're sure."

"I'm from here," Mara said, which was true, and "I've walked that path since I was nine," which was also true, and left out entirely the part where he'd learned it running salt and stolen goods between villages whose headmen preferred not to be able to prove anything. Some truths were better served whole and some in slices.

"The shrine's cursed," offered Fenwick, one of the drovers, a broad man with a broad opinion about everything. "My gran said so. Said the old folk used to leave bread there and the bread never went moldy, and *that's* unnatural."

"That's dry stone and a drafty arch," Mara said. "Your gran also swore the miller's second wife was a fish in winter."

"She might've been."

Mara let that lie where it fell. Arguing with caravan men about their grans was like arguing with weather; you'd exhaust yourself and lose anyway. He swung down off the lead mule instead and walked ahead to look at the spring, because a good guide looked at water before he trusted it, and a good smuggler looked at everything twice.

The water ran clear and cold over grey stones. The moss was green. The lichen was grey-green. Nothing glowed, hummed, whispered, or otherwise misbehaved. Above them the pass rose in long grey shoulders, patched with snow that would not melt until midsummer, and the morning light came slantwise through it all, gilding the tops of things and leaving the bottoms honest.

Behind him, the caravan groaned into motion — six wagons of salt bricks, fourteen mules, eleven men, and one boy named Tam who was technically a drover's apprentice but mostly a bottomless appetite attached to two boots. They'd come up out of the eastern valleys a week ago, and in a week Mara had learned everyone's habits: who cheated at cards (Fenwick), who snored like a rockslide (Dossan), who was homesick (all of them, though only Tam admitted it).

He had also, in that week, not once opened the grimoire.

It rode against his ribs in an oilcloth wrapping, heavy as a sin, warm in a way that books had no business being warm. He'd stolen it from Mother Hessel's hut eleven days ago along with three weeks of food and her good copper kettle, and every night when he stopped he told himself *tonight*, and every night his hands found some other task that needed doing — mending a strap, checking a hoof, studying the stars for navigation purposes that did not require reading a single word.

There were reasons. Good ones. The book had been hers, and she had been — well. She had been the kind of mistress whose lessons ended with questions you couldn't un-ask, and whose hut had smelled of herbs and iron and something older underneath, like meat under snow. When he'd found the false bottom in her chest, and under it the pages written in ink that moved when you weren't looking directly at it, something in him had understood, with the clarity of a man hearing the first crack in ice, that whatever she'd been training him for, it wasn't hedge-craft.

So he'd run. It was what he was best at.

"Mara!" Dossan bellowed, echoing his name around the bend like an accusation. "Water the stock or stand around admiring rocks, pick one!"

"Both," Mara called back, and crouched at the spring to fill his skin.

That was when he heard the singing.

Or not singing. Singing has shape and intention, and this had neither. It came drifting up the lower road — the high road, the washed-out one, the one nobody sensible used — thin as thread and snagging on the wind. A voice, human, cracked, coming closer.

The caravan heard it too. Eleven men developed eleven sudden interests in their harnesses. On roads like these, a lone voice meant a madman, a beggar, a scout, or a corpse that hadn't finished its business, and none of those improved a salt delivery.

Then around the shoulder of the hill came a man leading a goat, and the tension broke like a dropped plate. A shepherd. Just a shepherd — old, stooped, wrapped in a coat that had been patched more times than Fenwick's opinions, one hand knotted in the goat's halter rope and the other pressed hard against his own side, where a dark stain spread through the wool with terrible patience.

"He's hurt," Tam said unnecessarily.

"He's bleeding," Mara corrected, already moving. Habit, not heroism. You helped people on the mountain because the mountain remembered, and because someday the man bleeding might be you, and you wanted witnesses who'd seen you be decent. He'd learned that arithmetic young.

Up close the shepherd was worse than he'd looked. Grey as ash, lips gone the color of tallow, breath whistling through him like wind through a broken shutter. A gash under his coat — boar tusk by the look of it, ragged and deep — and however long ago it happened, it had been too long. The goat pressed against the man's leg and stared at Mara with flat yellow eyes, as if filing a complaint on his master's behalf.

"Sit him down," Mara called over his shoulder. "Someone get the spirits out of the third wagon — Dossan keeps a bottle he pretends is for snakebite—"

"It *is* for snakebite," Dossan protested, already fetching it.

They got the old man onto the flat stones beside the shrine, propped against the arch where the moss made a poor cushion but the sun reached. Fenwick held the bottle to his lips. Tam hovered. The shepherd drank, coughed, and managed to focus on the circle of faces above him with the mild surprise of a man who'd expected to arrive somewhere else entirely.

"Wool," he said.

"What?" said Fenwick.

"Got a full clip in my pack." His mouth twitched toward a smile. "Good wool. Was bringing it to my daughter in Marrowdale. Someone should—" The sentence wandered off like the goat.

"You'll bring it yourself," Dossan said gruffly, kneeling. "Boar?"

"Boar." The shepherd nodded, then winced at the cost of nodding. "Big fellow. Old. Knew what he was doing better than I did." He laughed, which turned into a wet rattle, which stopped being funny to everyone including him.

Mara crouched and looked at the wound properly, and the arithmetic he did then was not encouraging. He'd seen gut-wounds on the passes. There was a narrow window where stubbornness and spirits could hold a man together, and this window was mostly shut, and everyone standing there knew it, and nobody wanted to be the first to say it aloud.

"Get him to Coldbrook village," Fenwick said. "It's an hour back—"

"He's got twenty minutes," Mara said quietly, "and the village has a healer who drinks. I'm sorry. I'm telling you true."

Silence settled over the little group the way snow settles — soft, total, cold. The goat nibbled at moss. Somewhere above, a bird complained about something.

And then the old shepherd, dying on the stones of a shrine nobody tended, did the thing that changed everything.

He prayed.

Out loud. Not the way people pray on the mountain — quick and sideways, muttered into collars, hedged with *if it please* and *no offense meant*, the way you'd address a landlord known to be violent. Not even the way Mother Hessel had prayed, all ceremony and precision, a woman laying out terms. He prayed the way children talk to mothers. Plainly. Without fear. As if it had simply never occurred to him that the thing listening might want anything from him but his voice.

"Old One of the Bend," he said. Each word cost him, and he spent them anyway. "You kept my father's flock. You kept his father's. I never brought you bread — I know it, I'm a stingy old man — but I'm bringing you wool now, see, it's in my pack, best clip in ten years, take it, take all of it." His breath hitched. "Only — my daughter. Bets. Her husband drinks and she won't leave him, stubborn girl, stubborn as me. If you can't mend me, mend *her*. Give her a reason to go. Give her—" His voice failed, gathered itself. "Give her a spring morning. That's all. One spring morning, so she remembers what leaving feels like."

He closed his eyes. The stain beneath him had stopped spreading, which was its own kind of verdict.

Nobody spoke. Tam was crying without shame or noise. Fenwick had taken his hat off, which Mara had not previously known he owned manners deep enough to require.

Mara stood very still and felt the hair rise on his arms.

Because the spring had gone silent.

Not quiet — springs are never quiet, they mutter and trickle and gossip over stones the way caravans gossip over fences. This one had stopped. Utterly. The water hung over its stones like glass, and the dripping from the arch had ceased mid-drop, and the air itself had the held-breath quality of the moment before lightning.

"Fenwick," Mara said. His voice came out strange. "Step back from the water."

"What—"

"*Back.*"

The world exhaled.

Flame stood up out of the spring.

Not leapt, not flared — *stood*, the way a person stands, unfolding to its full height with unhurried grace. It was green. Not the green of fire as any story tells it, not witchlight, not foxfire, but green like the first leaf after a killing frost, green like deep water over sunlit stone, green the way the mountainside must have been once, before anyone thought to build anything on it. The pillar of it rose thirty feet, forty, straight and silent, giving off heat that felt less like burning and more like being *noticed* — warmth pouring over them the way sunlight pours through a window you didn't know faced east.

The mules lost their minds. So did the men. Wagons lurched, ropes snapped, and the caravan scattered down the lower track like beads off a snapped string, shouting prayers in eleven different dialects of terror. Dossan was screaming about his salt. Fenwick was praying with a fluency that suggested practice. Tam stood frozen, tears still wet on his face, staring up at the flame with his mouth open.

And Mara — Mara could not move.

Not from terror. That was the thing he would carry for the rest of his life, the thing he would turn over at night like a stone with something gleaming underneath: he stayed because he *wanted to look*. Because inside the fire, if you looked — really looked, the way you look at a face across a crowded room when someone calls your name —

There was a face.

It was vast and it was near and it was neither of those things, the way a mountain is near when you stand on its slope. Features assembled themselves out of the light the way features assemble in smoke, in dreams, in the moment before waking: brow, cheekbone, jaw, eyes like pools of that impossible green, and an expression that Mara spent years afterward failing to describe, because it held everything at once — joy, grief, hunger, tenderness, recognition. It looked at the shepherd. It looked at the little bundle of wool clutched in his hands. It looked, last and longest, at Mara, and in that look was the unmistakable weight of *being known*, of having your whole shabby clever careful life read aloud in one glance by something patient enough to have waited centuries for the reading.

The god — and it was a god, there was no other word, the old stories had been true, all of them, the bread that didn't mold and the stones that listened and the hundred small superstitions the Court called heresy — the god bent toward the dying man.

The flame touched him.

It did not burn. Mara would swear that on any altar, under any torture, forever: it did not burn. The green light sank into the old shepherd like rain into drought-cracked earth, and the wound beneath his coat closed to a pale seam, and the man's face smoothed, and he sighed — a sigh of pure relief, the sound of a debt forgiven — and he was gone. Not destroyed. *Taken*, maybe. Or received. The distinction mattered enormously and Mara had no way to ask.

The pillar of fire folded itself down, gently, the way a tall man sits in a small chair, and poured backward into the spring, and the water began to mutter over its stones again as if nothing had happened.

The silence afterward was enormous.

The goat sneezed.

Mara became aware, gradually, of his own body: knees on stone, hands shaking, heart going like a drum at a festival. Of the oilcloth bundle against his ribs, which had gone from warm to *hot*, almost painful, as if the grimoire had opinions about what it had just witnessed and was insisting on sharing them.

Of the fact that he was alone.

No — not alone. Tam stood ten paces off, rooted, staring at the shrine. And the others were visible far down the switchbacks, tiny figures still running, still shouting, a wagon listing drunkenly against a boulder. But no one else had stayed. No one else had *seen*.

Had they? He looked at Tam, and Tam looked at him, and the boy's expression was the answer: awe, yes, but the blank awe of a man who has seen lightning strike and cannot say what shape it took. Whatever the god had shown its face to, it had shown to one pair of eyes only.

"Why me?" Mara whispered. It wasn't a question he expected answered. It was a complaint.

The spring trickled on, inscrutable.

Tam found his voice first, which was fitting; the boy had always talked enough for three. "Did you see — Mara, did you *see* — it answered him. It *answered*. My nan always said the old ones were just sleeping, she said the Court burned the shrines because they were scared of them waking up, she said—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at the dead man on the stones, and his voice dropped. "Is he...?"

Mara crossed to the shepherd and knelt. The body was warm still, peaceful, wearing an expression Mara had never seen on a corpse — something close to satisfaction. The pale seam of the healed wound showed at his collar. In his hands, absurdly, miraculously, the bundle of wool remained, and when Mara lifted it, it was soft and clean and utterly untouched by fire.

Inside the pack, tucked beneath the wool, he found a folded scrap of cloth — a child's dress, tiny, carefully mended, smelling faintly of woodsmoke. Bets' daughter's, maybe. A granddaughter the old man hadn't mentioned, because dying men prioritize.

"Take the wool to Marrowdale," Mara said. His voice sounded scraped. "Find the daughter. Tell her — tell her a spring morning's coming. She'll either know what you mean or think you're mad, and both are fine."

"And the goat?"

"The goat comes with us. Dossan loves a bargain and we're short a mule now anyway."

Tam nodded and bent to lift the shepherd's body with a gentleness that caught Mara off guard. The boy laid him out neatly beside the shrine, folded the old man's hands — over the wool, no, over nothing, the wool was for the daughter — and stood back.

Mara lingered a moment longer than he needed to.

*Being known.* The memory of that gaze sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. Every secret he had — the smuggling routes, the stolen names, the grimoire pressing hot and impatient against his ribs right now, the false bottom in Mother Hessel's chest and the moving ink and the growing certainty that his entire life had been somebody else's plan — every one of those secrets had been *seen*, in one green instant, by something old enough to find them all very small.

He had spent twenty-two years making sure no one needed him and no one knew him, and it had worked beautifully, and it had bought him nothing, and a dying shepherd with a goat had gotten more out of the universe in four minutes of honest prayer than Mara had gotten out of a lifetime of cleverness.

The realization arrived without drama, the way ice gives way: *the gods are awake.*

Not sleeping. Not stories. Awake, and answering, and hungry — because nothing that wakes after that long wakes gentle, and the pillar of flame had held hunger in it alongside the tenderness, he'd felt it, he'd *seen* it, the wanting underneath the welcome like teeth behind a smile.

Down the valley, faint on the wind, came bells. Shrine-bells, ringing in a pattern Mara had never heard before — urgent, repeating, spreading from peak to peak like a shout passed along a wall. The mountain villages, waking up. Word would reach the Ember Court within days. And the Court, which burned heretics for whispering to stones that weren't even listening—

The Court, which had built its whole dominion on gods that did not exist—

Was about to have a problem.

"We need to catch the caravan," Mara said. "Now. Before Dossan convinces himself I summoned that thing and decides salt's cheaper without a guide."

Tam scrambled for the goat's rope. Mara swung up onto the lead mule, took one last look at the shrine — the arch, the spring, the neat-laid body of the only honest man he'd met in years — and rode.

Against his ribs, slow as a heartbeat, the grimoire began, very softly, to hum.

* * *

He caught them at the ford, and it took an hour of talking, half a bottle of Dossan's snakebite spirits administered as medicine, and one complete fabrication involving a lightning strike and a gas pocket to get the caravan moving again. Fenwick believed none of it and said so. Dossan believed all of it and thanked the saints. Tam kept looking at Mara with an expression he didn't like at all — the expression of a boy recalculating what his guide was.

By dusk they'd made camp in a hollow below the tree line, and Mara took first watch partly from duty and mostly because sleep was not going to happen. He sat with his back against a boulder, the goat dozing against his knee — traitorous animal had adopted him within the hour — and watched the peaks go violet, then grey, then black.

Somewhere up there, in the dark, the old gods were opening their eyes. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth.

He unwrapped the grimoire.

Just to look, he told himself. Just the cover. The leather was black and smooth as still water, and stamped into it, worn nearly flat, was a sigil he'd never examined closely: a doorway, and above the doorway, a flame.

Green, he was suddenly certain. It had always been green.

The book warmed under his hands like a living thing greeting its owner, and Mara Joubert — survivor, smuggler, expert in the art of never being needed — sat alone under the indifferent stars and understood, with perfect clarity, that he had stopped running somewhere on the road today without noticing, and that everything ahead of him was downhill.
