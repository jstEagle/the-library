## 3. A Country That No Longer Exists

The salt had kept Mara honest for eleven days, which was a personal record.

Not that salt was difficult work — salt was the best cargo in the world, if you asked him, because nobody wanted to steal it and everybody needed it, which meant you could walk it through a war zone and be greeted like a cousin. No, what had kept him honest was Master Odo of the caravan, who had a habit of counting burlap sacks in his sleep and could smell a pilfered ounce from across a campfire. Mara had guided the man's mules over the Thornfell pass on roads that did not appear on any map drawn by anyone with an interest in taxes, and he had done it without skimming so much as a grain, mostly because Odo paid well and remembered faces, and Mara's whole philosophy of life fit on a coin: never be owed to, never owe, never be remembered longer than the transaction takes.

"Joubert," Odo said now, squinting down at the lowlands from the last shoulder of the pass. "That smoke's wrong."

Mara looked. Smoke was one of the few things he trusted himself to read better than most men — chimney smoke meant breakfast, forge smoke meant money, forest smoke meant run. This was none of those. It rose in three fat columns from the valley floor, black at the base and greasy at the top, the kind of smoke that came from buildings full of paper and pitch and people who hadn't gotten out.

"Garrison smoke," Mara said.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure it isn't bread." He shifted his pack higher on his shoulder. The grimoire rode against his spine where he'd sewn it into the lining, and it had developed, in his imagination, a weight entirely out of proportion to its actual heft — the way stolen things do. "Could be nothing. Garrisons burn their own rubbish all the time."

"Since when do garrisons burn rubbish in triplicate?"

Odo was a fat, shrewd, fundamentally decent man, which was why Mara liked him and also why he'd never steal from him. They stood together a moment while the mules stamped behind them, breath steaming, and below them the road unspooled toward the border town of Harrowgate, or toward whatever Harrowgate was now.

"Well," Odo said. "We still need salt sold."

"That's the spirit."

They went down.

* * *

Harrowgate announced itself wrong before they ever reached its gates. A border town should have smelled of horses, tallow, and the particular despair of customs officials; instead the wind carried something sweeter and more unpleasant, like incense left burning in a room where something had died. The walls were intact. The gate stood open. And nailed above the archway, where the king's arms had hung for as long as there had been a king, was a sheet of hammered copper bearing a single embossed flame.

Mara stopped under it just long enough to feel it watching him.

Inside, the town was full and quiet in the way a room is quiet when everyone in it has agreed not to mention something. The market was open. Women were selling turnips. Children were doing the thing children do, which is playing a game whose rules involve screaming. But every third doorway had a small iron brazier beside it, cold now, waiting, and every brazier bore the same mark: the flame, worked in copper, with a little slot beneath it that Mara chose not to think about too hard, because slots like that were for offerings, and offerings were fine, and he absolutely believed that.

"Salt!" Odo called out, with the optimism of a man who had crossed a mountain range and refused to let reality cheapen the experience. "Fine white salt! Mountain-cut!"

A woman buying turnips turned and looked at them the way you'd look at someone singing in a library.

"Friend," she said, lowering her voice as though the turnips might inform on her, "you want the shrine quarter. Nobody buys anything else anymore. Salt's consecrated or it's contraband."

"It's *salt*," said Odo.

"It's whatever the Court says it is." She gathered her turnips and left, quickly, the way people leave when they've said one sentence too many.

Mara drifted along the edge of the square while Odo set up his stall with the stubbornness of his profession. He counted what he saw. It was a habit, counting — doors, guards, exits, the number of steps between himself and anywhere else. Here's what he counted: no royal writs on the notice board, only tall sheets of parchment headed with the copper flame and dense blocks of text in a hand too formal to be anything but law. No garrison colors anywhere. No tax collectors, which in any healthy town would have been the first thing you noticed, the way you notice a missing tooth. Instead there were gray-robed figures moving through the crowd with censers, unhurried, and people stepped out of their path the way water parts around a stone — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Something more organized than fear.

He stopped at a baker's stall, because a man asking about politics can be dangerous but a man asking about rolls is invisible.

"Bread?" he said.

"Consecrated," said the baker, a round young man with flour to his elbows and the eyes of someone who hadn't slept in a month. "Everything's consecrated now. You buy it, you say the words, the priest stamps your ledger. You don't—" He glanced sideways. "You don't skip it."

"And if I'm just passing through? Don't keep a ledger."

The baker wrapped two rolls without being asked and leaned across them. "Then get your business done and go. There's a reading at noon. Everyone's meant to attend. Everyone *does* attend." His voice did something complicated on the word *meant*. "You'll see."

"What happens at a reading?"

"The herald comes." The baker straightened up, transaction over, face closed like a shutter. "Two coppers."

Mara paid four, because generosity was cheaper than curiosity, and took his rolls back to Odo's stall, where Odo had sold precisely nothing and was defending a sack of salt from a gray-robed figure with a censer and a clipboard expression.

"—unconsecrated goods may not change hands within the dominion," the figure was saying. He was young, earnest, and had the slightly singed look of a man who genuinely believed in his own paperwork. "However! The Court welcomes the repentant trader. Present your stock at the shrine quarter for blessing, pay the consecration fee of one part in ten—"

"One part in *ten*," said Odo.

"A modest tithe for divine protection—"

"My protection," Odo said, "is a mule named Duchess who bites priests."

Mara got between them with the smoothness of long practice. "My master's tired from the road," he said, warm as fresh butter. "You understand. Mountain passes, bandits, weather. We'll certainly present ourselves for blessing once we've rested. Where would that be?"

The young priest brightened immediately, because zealots are only dangerous when resisted and only human when agreed with. "The High Brazier, past the old garrison. You can't miss it. Though—" he lowered his voice, sharing, confiding, awful, "—the garrison's closed now. Burned. The soldiers were given every chance to consecrate themselves, you know. Every chance." He shook his head with genuine sorrow. "Some men just prefer the dark."

"Tragic," said Mara.

"Isn't it." The priest moved on to the next stall, censer swinging, already saying the same words in the same cadence, and Mara watched him go and thought about the phrase *given every chance*, and about how many chances a man gets when the person counting them holds the matches.

* * *

They went to see the garrison anyway, because Mara had learned long ago that the thing you most want to walk past is usually the thing you most need to look at.

It sat on the low rise at the town's edge where it had sat for two hundred years — a squat stone keep flying, until recently, the blue-and-silver of House Aldery, Warden of the Marches. Now the flagpole was bare and scorched. The gatehouse was a black mouth. The roof of the barracks had fallen inward, and the three columns of smoke Mara had seen from the pass had dwindled to sullen threads rising from the wreckage, days old and refusing to die, as if the fire itself had been consecrated and told to keep working.

There were bodies. Not many, and not fresh; the townsfolk had dragged most of them out and laid them in a row along the wall, covered with blankets, because whatever else Harrowgate had become, someone in it still remembered how to be decent. Mara walked the row without meaning to. He didn't lift the blankets. He'd seen enough dead men to know that lifting the blanket changes nothing except how well you sleep afterward.

At the end of the row, a boy of maybe twelve was standing guard over the dead with a kitchen knife held the way children hold kitchen knives, which is to say like a spoon with ambitions.

"They wouldn't kneel," the boy said, unprompted, when Mara paused.

Mara considered several replies and discarded all of them. "No," he said finally. "Soldiers generally don't."

"The priest said they were given every chance."

"He would know. He wrote the chances." Mara crouched, putting himself below the knife line, which was both courtesy and tactics. "You family to any of these?"

"My da." The boy's chin wobbled and set itself. "He said the gods were stories. He said a man pays his debts to other men." The knife trembled. "Was that wrong?"

Here was the trap, and Mara felt it close around him with real admiration, because it wasn't a trap laid by the boy or even by the Court — it was the oldest trap there is, the one where a child asks you to tell him whether his father died for nothing. Every answer burns somebody.

"Your father," Mara said slowly, "kept a promise to the country he was paid to keep promises to. That's not nothing. That's the whole job." He stood. "Mind the knife. Point it down unless you mean it."

He walked away before the boy could ask anything harder, and hated himself a little for the ease of it, and noted, in the ledger he kept behind his eyes, another entry in the column marked *things the Ember Court owes*.

* * *

At noon, the whole town went to hear the herald, and Mara went with them, because a man who stands out during a mandatory reading has misunderstood his entire profession.

They gathered in the square before the old moot-hall, packed shoulder to shoulder, hundreds of them, silent in a way crowds are never silent. On the hall's steps stood a platform draped in gray, and on the platform stood the herald — a tall woman in robes the color of ash, with a voice trained to carry across battlefields, and beside her, on an iron stand, a brazier the size of a wine barrel, burning with a flame that was the wrong color.

Green.

Mara had seen green fire once before, eleven days ago, on a mountainside, coming out of a dying shepherd's prayer, and he had spent every day since telling himself he'd imagined it, or that mountain air does strange things to torchlight, or any of the hundred comfortable lies a sensible man keeps in his coat pockets for emergencies. There was no explaining this one away. The flame burned steady in the noon sun, casting no heat that Mara could feel from thirty feet away, and the crowd flinched from it as one body, and the herald smiled at the flinching the way a farmer smiles at rain.

"People of Harrowgate," she said, and her voice rolled out over them like a lid closing. "People of the dominion of the Ember Court. Hear the writ."

A functionary unrolled a scroll the length of a table. The herald read it in a flat, liturgical drone, and the drone was worse than shouting, because shouting ends:

"—by authority of the Flame Eternal and its Hierophant, the crown of Thornfell, having failed in its sacred duty to tend the waking gods, is declared dissolved. The borders of Thornfell are dissolved. The writs of Thornfell are dissolved. The name of Thornfell is struck from the rolls of nations, being henceforth and forever the Dominion of the Ember, one and indivisible, from the Salt Road to the Sea Gate. All oaths sworn to the crown are void. All debts owed to the crown are transferred to the Court. All persons residing within the former borders are subjects of the Flame, and will conduct themselves accordingly, under penalty of—"

She read the penalties. There were many. They were creative. Mara listened to all of them with the attention of a man memorizing the exits.

And underneath the drone, in his own head, something quieter was happening, something he couldn't have explained to Odo or the baker or the boy with the knife. It was the feeling of coming home after a long trip and finding your house gone — not burned, not ruined, simply *gone*, the lot empty, the neighbors looking at you with polite blankness when you give the address. Thornfell had been a bad country. Mara had spent his whole life exploiting its badness, dodging its tolls, slipping through its laws like water through a fence. He had never loved it. He had never once, in twenty-six years, said a kind word about it aloud.

But it had been *his* bad country, and its fences had been fences he knew every gap in, and now some priest had come along and replaced the whole landscape with a temple, and there were no gaps in a temple. There were only doors, and doors open for whoever holds the key.

The scroll ended. The herald folded her hands.

"The Flame is merciful," she said. "The Flame is hungry. Bring it what it asks, and be fed." She raised one arm toward the green brazier, and the crowd bowed — not all at once, but in a wave, like wind through wheat, starting at the front and rolling back, because courage in crowds is contagious but so is kneeling, and kneeling travels faster.

Mara knelt too. Of course he knelt. He was a smuggler, not a martyr; martyrs die of principle and smugglers die of bad routes, and he intended to die of neither. He put his knee in the dirt with six hundred others and kept his head down and his face arranged into devout blankness, and he waited, the way he always waited, for the moment when everyone would stand up again and the world would resume being navigable.

Except the world didn't resume. The herald began calling names.

"Step forward when your name is spoken. This is not punishment. This is registration." She lifted a ledger. "Every soul in the dominion will be known to the Flame. Refusal to be known is itself an answer."

One by one, people climbed the steps, spoke their names to a scribe, pressed a thumb in ink, and received — Mara craned slightly — a small copper token stamped with the flame, which they held afterward like communion coins, some kissing them, some just gripping them, all of them wearing the same expression: relief and shame in equal measure, shaken well.

The line moved toward him, name by name. *Tomas Beck. Widow Anthe. The miller's daughters.* Mara calculated distances. North gate, forty paces, guarded by two men with pikes. South alley, reachable, but the alley had a brazier in it and braziers had watchers now. The crowd around him was a wall of bowed heads, and every bowed head was a witness, and witnesses in a country of shrine-law had just learned what happened to soldiers who wouldn't kneel.

*Joubert,* he thought, with dark humor, *you have crossed mountains to escape one old woman, and arrived in a country that wants to adopt you.*

The line shortened. Ten names ahead. Five.

And then, cutting through the murmur of the square, a new sound: hooves. A rider came up the south road at a hard gallop — a woman, mud-spattered, riding a lathered horse like she'd borrowed both from someone with a grudge. She wore a travel-stained cloak over what had once been a very good uniform, and at her hip hung a sword with an inquisitor's knotwork guard, and half the crowd recognized the uniform before she reined in, because uniforms outlive the institutions that made them, the way names outlive their owners.

"Inquisitor Vane," someone whispered near Mara, with the hushed relish people reserve for famous disgraces. "The one they stripped. The doubter."

Vane swung down, shoved through the line of kneeling registrants without apology, and planted herself at the foot of the platform, looking up at the herald with the expression of a woman who has ridden three days to have an argument and intends to enjoy it.

"That writ," she said, loud enough for the whole square, "is unlawful. The Court holds no authority to dissolve a crown. I served the crown. I intend to see it restored, and every one of you—" she turned, sweeping the crowd with a finger, "—should be ashamed of how fast you knelt."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere a brazier pinged softly as it heated.

The herald looked at Sera Vane the way a cat looks at a bird that has landed voluntarily inside the cage.

"Sera Vane," the herald said pleasantly, consulting her ledger. "Stripped of office. Struck from the rolls. Unregistered." She smiled. "How convenient. We were just collecting names."

Mara, five places back in the line, did the fastest arithmetic of his life and arrived at a sum he didn't like: the only person in this square making noise was the only person in this square about to be arrested, and arrested people talk, and talking people name accomplices, and he — Mara Joubert, salt guide, unregistered soul, carrier of a stolen grimoire sewn into his own coat — was standing directly behind her.

He began, very quietly, to edge backward.

Vane's eyes swept the crowd once more — sharp, gray, missing nothing — and snagged, briefly, on the one face in six hundred that was moving the wrong direction.

She looked at him. He looked at her. Something passed between them that was not yet recognition but was already, unmistakably, appointment.

"Hold that man," said Inquisitor Sera Vane, pointing directly at Mara Joubert. "He's running."
