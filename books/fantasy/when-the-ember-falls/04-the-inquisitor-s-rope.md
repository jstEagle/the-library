## 4. The Inquisitor's Rope

Sera Vane had hanged men for lesser crimes than existing, and she had never once enjoyed it.

She crouched now at the edge of the shepherd's clearing, where the grass still bore a green scorch in the shape of a man kneeling, and watched the god-speaker pick through the ashes of the shrine like a man choosing vegetables at market.

He was younger than the reports suggested. Thin, sun-browned, with the particular slouch of someone who had spent his whole life making himself smaller than he needed to be. His pack was salt-stained. His boots were good — smuggler's boots, resoled twice, the kind that gripped wet stone. And around his neck, on a cord, hung a book wrapped in oilcloth, which he touched every few breaths the way other men touched holy symbols or old scars.

*There*, thought Vane. *That's your heretic.*

She had been tracking him for six days, which was five days longer than she should have been able to track anyone, given that she no longer technically existed. The Ember Court had stripped her office in a ceremony that lasted eleven minutes and involved more incense than dignity. They had taken her seal, her writs, her white-and-gold cloak, and her name from the rolls. They had left her, pointedly, her sword, because even the Court understood that a disgraced inquisitor walking out of the capital unarmed would raise questions, and questions were the one thing the Court could not burn.

*Doubting the miracles.* That was what they wrote. As if she had stood in the Hierophant's hall and shouted *fraud!* She hadn't. She had only asked, quietly, in the way of a woman who had spent twenty years reading ledgers of the soul: *where does the fire go after it answers?* Twenty years of miracle-reports, and not one accounting. Not one ash-weight. Inquisition work was arithmetic before it was anything else, and the Court's arithmetic did not sum.

For this they took her name.

So here she was, nameless, cloakless, squatting in a burned shrine-field outside Hallowmere, watching a smuggler count dead men's candle-stubs.

Vane rose from the ferns and said, "Don't run."

He ran.

She had expected this — hoped for it, honestly, because a man who ran was a man who told you his routes by his feet — but she had not expected him to be *good* at it. He went over the shrine wall rather than through the gap, used a grave-marker as a stepping stone without breaking stride, and was into the tree-line before she cleared the wall.

"God-speakers," she muttered, and gave chase.

The wood was birch and larch, thin-trunked and bright with afternoon. He wove through it the way water goes downhill — no hesitation, no wasted motion, always choosing the line she wouldn't have chosen and being right about it. Twice he dropped out of sight behind deadfall and came up somewhere she hadn't watched him go. Once he crossed his own trail backward along a fallen log, which was textbook, except that he did it while *running*, which was not.

*Smuggler,* she reminded herself. *Not a priest. A professional.*

She was a professional too. She simply cut the corner he couldn't see, took the deer-path he didn't know about, and stepped out onto the game trail two lengths ahead of him just as he burst through the bracken.

To his credit, he didn't scream. He skidded, turned, and got exactly one stride toward escape before her boot caught his ankle and put him face-down in the moss.

"Hello," said Vane.

* * *

She trussed him against a larch with proper inquisitor knots — wrists behind, rope to the trunk, enough slack that he could sit if he accepted his situation and not if he argued with it. He sat, tested the knots twice with the weary thoroughness of a man who already knew the answer, and looked up at her with an expression she would come to know very well: polite, watchful, entirely unimpressed.

"You're not Court," he said.

"Observant."

"Court wears white and gold. You're dressed like a widow who's given up." He nodded at her grey traveling leathers, her plain hood. "Also, Court doesn't say 'hello.' Court says 'confess.' You said 'hello.'"

"I've been demoted," said Vane. "From confession down to pleasantries."

That earned her something almost like a smile, quickly buried. "And what does a demoted inquisitor want with a humble salt-caravan guard?"

"A humble salt-caravan guard," said Vane, "who was seen at three shrines in four days, all three of which answered prayers within the hour, and who walked away from each one untouched."

"That's coincidence."

"The first one, perhaps. The shepherd at the second one burned green, and everyone within fifty paces scattered like startled birds. Except you. You walked *toward* it."

His face did something complicated and closed, like a shutter going down over a window with light behind it. "I tripped."

"Toward the pillar of divine fire."

"People trip toward things. It's a known phenomenon."

Vane crouched to his level, forearms on knees, and studied him the way she'd study a suspect ledger. Up close he smelled of road dust and tallow and, faintly, under it, of something green and cold — mountain-water smell, snowmelt smell. The same smell the scorched grass carried. She kept that observation folded away where he couldn't see it.

"What's your name?"

"Wouldn't you like to write it down."

"My name is Sera Vane," she said. "Formerly Inquisitor Second Rank of the Thornfell Inquisition, which no longer exists, attached to an Ember Court I no longer recognize, serving gods I'm fairly sure are either mad or fake. So when I ask a man his name, understand it costs me more than it costs you."

That landed. She watched it land — a small settling in his shoulders, the wariness recalibrating from *captor* to *person*. It was a gamble, telling a prisoner true things. Her old instructors would have flayed her for it. Her old instructors had also taught her that mercy was a rounding error, so she felt entitled to improvise.

"Mara," he said at last. "Mara Joubert."

"And are you a god-speaker, Mara Joubert?"

"No."

"Are you sure? Because the Court has a simple test, and I'll describe it so you appreciate the alternative you're currently enjoying. They tie a suspected speaker to a stake, they read him the Litanies of Answering, and if any god takes an interest in him, he ignites. Green flame. Very festive. The priests then declare it a miracle and take up a collection."

Mara was quiet for a moment. "You don't believe in their miracles."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway, because apparently today was a day for spending truths. "I believe in fires. I believe in prayers. I believe there's something answering them, because I've read forty-one eyewitness accounts in the last month and forty-one frightened people don't hallucinate the same shade of green." She paused. "What I don't believe is that the Court knows what it's feeding. Or why the answers come faster near certain people. People like you."

"I'm nobody."

"Nobody," Vane agreed. "A nobody who knows every smuggler path through Thornfell, if my information holds."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the quick arithmetic of a man deciding how much of himself a stranger can safely own. "Your information," he said carefully, "holds."

"Then here is where we find ourselves, Mara Joubert. The country we're both standing in has been legally dissolved. Its king is either dead or hiding. Its roads belong to shrine-law, its passes to whatever wakes next, and its people are learning that prayer is a loaded crossbow. The Court is burning heretics to keep the gods fed, and I intend to find out why — and how to make it stop. There is exactly one place left in Thornfell that might hold the answer. The Door at Thornfell's heart. The old passage. The one the first priests sealed and the last kings forgot."

Mara had gone very still. Stillness, Vane noted, was his native language; everything else he spoke with an accent.

"There's no Door," he said.

"There's always a Door. Countries are built over them like houses built over wells."

"And you want me to take you to it."

"I want," said Vane, "to propose a business arrangement."

"A business arrangement," repeated Mara, straining slightly against the rope to look down at himself. "Is this how businesses usually start?"

"I'm told partnerships begin worse."

* * *

She built a small fire — small, screened, the kind of fire a woman makes when she doesn't want to be found — and cooked barley and dried mutton in a tin pot, and after a decent interval of letting him smell it, fed him some, holding the spoon herself, which she judged he hated and therefore enjoyed doing.

"So," she said. "The arrangement."

"I'm listening mostly out of politeness."

"Guide me to the Door. Safely, by paths the Court doesn't watch. When we reach it, I release you from service, give you what coin I carry, and write — in whatever registry survives this mess — that Mara Joubert aided the lawful inquiry of Sera Vane, which may one day matter, if law survives this mess."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I leave you tied to this tree with a day's water, a knife at your feet, and my sincere best wishes. You'll cut yourself loose by nightfall. You'll vanish into the mountains, and you'll spend the rest of your short, loud life dodging priests who burn people for smelling like snowmelt." She let that sit. "Or the Court finds you first, in which case the stake and the Litanies apply. I've seen the list of suspects they're circulating. Your description is on it. *Young man, salt-stained, walks toward fires.*"

"That last part," said Mara, "is slander."

"It's testimony. Four witnesses."

"Witnesses panic. Panic distorts. One man trips and suddenly I'm a pilgrimage site." He shifted against the trunk, wincing theatrically. "Look at me. Do I look like a chosen vessel of the elder powers? I look like a man who hasn't eaten a vegetable since spring."

"You look like a man," said Vane, "who is negotiating instead of saying yes, which tells me the yes is already decided and you're angling for better terms. So let me save us both the theater. Name your term."

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When he spoke again, the flippancy had drained out of his voice, leaving something older and wearier underneath, and Vane understood she was hearing the actual man for the first time.

"When we get to this Door — if it exists — I walk away. Whatever happens there, whoever comes out, I'm not part of it. No vows, no witness marks, no names in registries. You wanted to know why I run caravans instead of staying anywhere?" He tipped his head back against the larch. "Because everywhere I've ever stayed, someone eventually decided I belonged to them. My mistress. The guild. Now the Court, maybe. I'm done belonging. Guide you to the Door and live — fine. But I live *afterward*. Free and unnamed. That's my price, Inquisitor. Take it or hang me here and lose your map."

Vane looked at him for a long moment.

Here was the thing she would not say aloud: that she recognized the shape of his wanting. She had served law her whole life because law meant you belonged to something larger than appetite — and the Court had shown her what happened when belonging curdled into ownership. This boy on the tree wanted the opposite extreme, and both of them were wrong, and both of them were human, and the Door at the heart of Thornfell did not care which of them learned it first.

"Agreed," she said. "Free and unnamed. Assuming the Door exists and assuming we survive the walk."

"Oh, we won't survive the walk," said Mara cheerfully. "But I accept your terms."

She cut him down. He rubbed his wrists, retrieved a coil of good rope from beside the tree — *his* rope, she realized; he'd been carrying it and she'd confiscated it, which meant she'd hog-tied a smuggler with his own equipment, a fact she resolved never to mention — and shouldered his pack.

They walked until dusk, Mara setting a pace that was brisk without admitting to hurrying, taking trails that weren't trails, pausing at forks to taste the air like a man reading a letter written in wind. Vane followed and memorized and asked nothing, because a good inquisitor knew when silence gathered more than questions.

At full dark he stopped at the lip of a hollow where a spring came up cold out of the rock, and made camp with the economy of long habit. While she saw to the fire he unwrapped the oilcloth bundle at his throat — just a few inches, just enough to check the book inside — and Vane saw, for half a heartbeat, the edge of a cover scarred with a sigil she knew from forbidden archives: a circle with a door drawn inside it.

The Door at Thornfell's heart. Drawn in ink older than the kingdom.

Mara caught her looking and wrapped the book away, and neither of them said anything, and the fire settled, and far off across the black shoulder of the mountain a shrine bell began to ring — ringing, ringing, ringing, the way bells ring when the hand pulling the rope belongs to something that is not a hand.

Mara listened to it with his head tilted, and then he said, quietly, in the voice of a man revising his terms:

"Inquisitor. We should walk faster."
