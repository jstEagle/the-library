## 5. What the Grimoire Remembers

The grimoire did not like being read by firelight.

Mara had discovered this on the second night after Vane's bargain, when the book — bound in grey leather gone soft as old bread, smelling faintly of tallow and thyme — had snapped itself shut on his thumb hard enough to raise a blister. He had sworn at it. It had settled back into sullen stillness, the way a cat settles after scratching you for touching its belly.

"You're a book," he told it now, cross-legged on the floor of the abandoned shepherd's hut with a stub of candle guttering in front of him. "You're paper and stitching and someone's bad handwriting. Stop acting like my mistress."

The book did not answer, which Mara chose to interpret as agreement.

Three nights now he had stolen from sleep to read it, waiting until Inquisitor Sera Vane's breathing went slow and even across the hut, until the child they hadn't yet found — the child Vane spoke of in clipped, careful sentences, as if naming it might summon the Court — was safely dreaming under a shared blanket. Three nights of squinting at Agnes Marrow's handwriting, which had been elegant once and then, somewhere around page two hundred, had simply stopped caring.

He knew that handwriting better than he'd ever wanted to. Ten years of copying labels for dried herbs. Ten years of transcribing her letters, all of them addressed to no one, sealed and stacked in a chest she never opened. Ten years of her voice over his shoulder saying *no, boy, the descender curls left, are you writing with your foot?*

He missed her so badly it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He hated that he missed her. He had stolen from her. She had presumably told the Court exactly where to find him, because the Court's riders had appeared on his trail within a day of his leaving, and only a woman who could scry smoke knew that fast.

Both things were true. Both things hurt. Mara had learned young that this was how most love worked, and had built his whole life around avoiding it.

He turned the page.

The early chapters of the grimoire were what he'd expected when he stole it: hedge-craft. Salves. Weather-sense. The proper way to ask a river's permission before crossing — *ask aloud, fool, rivers can't hear thinking* — and the way to salt a doorstep against things that walked at night. Useful knowledge. Knowledge that had kept him alive on roads where other travelers simply vanished.

But the deeper pages changed. The ink got older. And the subject matter stopped being about keeping things out and started being about putting things *in*.

*On the binding of that which prays back,* said a heading in faded brown script. Below it, diagrams: circles within circles, each ring labeled in a language Mara didn't know but recognized anyway, because he'd seen it carved into the roadside shrines of Thornfell all his life, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather and offerings. Everyone assumed those carvings were prayers.

They weren't. He could see that now. They were *instructions*.

Mara sat back and rubbed his eyes.

"No," he said quietly. "No, that's — that's shrine-keeping. That's old women tying ribbons. That's not—"

The book, helpfully, turned its own page.

He stared at it for a long moment. "I hate you," he informed it, and looked down at what it wanted him to see.

*The rite is simple,* Agnes's younger hand had written, in the neat, confident script of someone decades from becoming the crooked-lettered tyrant of Mara's childhood. *Simple, and terrible, and necessary. A god is nothing but attention given weight. Starve the attention and the weight remains — sleeping, hungry, patient. We could not kill them. Killing requires an ending, and they have none. So we built doors instead. Seven doors. Seven seals. And behind each door we shut a piece of the sky.*

*My sisters called it mercy. I call it arithmetic. One sealed god eats no one. That is the whole of my theology, and I have never been ashamed of it.*

Mara's mouth had gone dry somewhere around *we built doors*.

*We.* Plural. *Sisters.*

He turned pages faster now, past rites he didn't want to understand, past lists of names — dozens of names, arranged like a roster, each one followed by a small symbol he eventually worked out meant *sealed* or *deceased* or, in one chilling case, *unaccounted*. The names ran backward through generations. His mistress's name appeared near the bottom of the oldest list, written in her own hand but in an alphabet older than any she'd used for him:

**AGNES MARROW, KEEPER OF THE THIRD DOOR.**

Below it, a date. Mara did the arithmetic twice, because the first time the answer was impossible, and the second time it was worse.

Two hundred and eleven years ago.

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"Two hundred years," Mara whispered. "Agnes. You're *sixty-one*. You complained about your knees *yesterday*. You made me carry you up the hill to the bee-hives in *spring*."

Except — hadn't she? Hadn't there always been something wrong with the arithmetic of her? The neighbors in the valley village had spoken of her grandmother teaching their grandmothers, and Mara had never thought to ask why the stories never seemed to age. He had filed it under the same category as everything else about Agnes Marrow: strange, useful, not his business. Survival rule number one. Never be needed by anyone, and never need anyone enough to start asking questions.

He had broken rule number one the day he'd stolen this book. Now the questions wouldn't stop coming.

He read on, because stopping was unthinkable now, and because — some treacherous part of him admitted — reading her words again felt like having her voice in the room. Even her younger voice, all certainty and no complaints about her knees.

*The Court will tell you the gods woke on their own,* she wrote, in a later hand, sharper now, hurried. *The Court will be lying, which is what the Court does. They have been starving the seals for a century, feeding the doors just enough worship to keep the locks rusted shut and themselves indispensable. A priest who needs a devil will always find one. But seals are seals. Hunger wears through anything, given time. When the third door thins, the Keeper must be ready.*

*A Keeper cannot be trained. Training makes soldiers, and soldiers fight. A Keeper must be* shaped. *Taught the roads before they know why roads matter. Taught every smuggler's path, every goat track, every forgotten stair, until the mountain lives in their feet. Taught to survive alone, to trust no one, to owe no debts — because the Door does not open for armies or congregations or lovers. It opens for one person walking out of nowhere, wanting nothing, known to no one.*

*It opens for a ghost with a map in his bones.*

*I have chosen my apprentice accordingly. He will forgive me or he won't. That is also arithmetic.*

Mara did not move for a long time.

Somewhere across the hut, Sera Vane sighed in her sleep and rolled toward the wall. The child-shaped absence beside her — the child they would find tomorrow, or the day after, because the grimoire's maps said so and the grimoire had apparently decided to stop fighting him — breathed in the space between her breaths, or didn't exist yet, depending on how much of this nightmare was already written.

Mara looked at his own hands in the candlelight. Ordinary hands. Salt-cracked knuckles, a burn scar from a carelessly held pan, the particular callus of a man who'd carried packs since he was eight years old. Hands that had walked every pass in Thornfell before he turned fifteen, because Agnes had sent him — *take this to the widow Corren, take this to the ferryman, go by the low road, no, the other low road, the one that isn't a road.*

Every errand. Every delivery. Every time she'd said *you're the only one I trust, boy*, which he had treasured, gods help him, had hoarded like a miser hoards coins, because no one else in his entire life had ever needed him for anything good.

She hadn't trusted him. She'd been *building* him. Layer by layer, errand by errand, the same way you lay a foundation without telling the house.

And the worst of it — the part that made his eyes sting and his jaw ache — was that it had worked. He was exactly what she'd made. A ghost with a map in his bones. He'd been proud of it. He'd thought it was his.

*He will forgive me or he won't. That is also arithmetic.*

"I won't," Mara said out loud, and heard how unconvincing it sounded, and said it again, louder, and Vane stirred.

"Joubert." Her voice came rough from sleep, one hand already finding the hilt of the blade she slept beside like a sensible woman. "What in the nine names are you doing?"

"Reading."

"At the hour when sensible people are unconscious."

"Sensible people are currently losing their country to fire priests, so I've adjusted my standards." He closed the grimoire — it permitted this, magnanimously — and set his palm flat on its cover. "Vane. How old do I look?"

She blinked at him across the dark. Even half-asleep, her assessment was immediate and insulting. "Twenty-five. Twenty-six. Younger than your sins."

"And if I told you my mistress has been keeping a ledger that says otherwise? If I told you she helped lock the gods away two hundred years ago, and she took me in when I was eight, and every lesson, every road, every *favor* she ever asked of me—" His voice cracked on the word favor, which infuriated him, so he pushed through it. "It was all preparation. For this. For the Door you want me to find. She grew me like a crop, Vane. I was never an apprentice. I was a *harvest*."

Silence. The candle fluttered.

Then Vane sat up, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and regarded him with the expression she wore when she was deciding whether a suspect was lying — except softer, he realized, than she'd ever worn it before.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "the Church assigned me to a mentor. High Inquisitor Doss. He taught me doctrine, procedure, the correct angle for holding a confession-iron." Her mouth twisted. "When the Court fell on me for doubting their miracles, do you know what he wrote in his report? That he had *cultivated* my skepticism. Raised the question in me deliberately, so that when the moment came, my fall could be used to discredit every honest questioner in the order. I wasn't his student. I was his instrument, and he tuned me to break."

Mara stared at her. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're sitting there believing you're the only person ever built by someone else's hands." She shrugged inside the blanket. "It's a very lonely belief. I've held it. It's heavier than the truth."

"And what's the truth?"

"That she chose you because you were already *you*." Vane nodded at the grimoire. "Read her list of Keepers again. Read what happened to the ones who weren't ghosts with maps in their bones. She couldn't make a man out of nothing, Joubert. Nobody can. All she could do was sharpen what was already there — and what was already there walked out on her the first chance it got, stole her book, and survived three weeks of the worst roads in Thornfell with a priestess's bounty on his head." Something that was almost a smile touched her face. "If you were truly her creature, you'd have come when she called."

"I don't remember her calling."

"Exactly."

He wanted to argue. He wanted it so badly his teeth ached. But the candle had burned down to a coin of wax, and the grimoire under his hand felt suddenly less like a cage and more like — a letter. An apology, maybe, or the closest thing Agnes Marrow's pride would ever permit. *Here is everything. Here is why. Come find me, boy, and bring your angry questions. I'll put the kettle on.*

Or a trap. It could absolutely still be a trap.

That was the trouble with Agnes. Even two hundred years dead-or-not-dead, she kept both possibilities open, and Mara could not decide whether that was wisdom or cowardice.

"The child," he said instead, because the alternative was sitting with his feelings, and Mara Joubert had survived thirty years by never once sitting with his feelings. "Your refugee column. The one the Court wants. Tomorrow we reach the ford at Hollowmere, and if your information holds, the column camps there tonight. Which means—"

"Which means tomorrow we find out what a child sounds like when stones answer her." Vane lay back down, and her last words drifted across the dark, dry as dust. "Try to sleep, Joubert. Whatever she made you for — you'll need your feet."

He didn't sleep. He sat with the grimoire in his lap and watched the candle die, and sometime before dawn, in the grey hour when the mountains hold their breath, the book warmed under his palms — once, gently, unmistakably — like an old woman patting his hand.

He pretended not to notice.

He was getting very good at pretending not to notice. It was, after all, the one thing she'd never had to teach him.
