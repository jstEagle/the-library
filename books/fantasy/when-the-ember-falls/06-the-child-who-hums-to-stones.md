## 6. The Child Who Hums to Stones

The refugee column on the old salt road moved the way grief moves — slowly, and in a long line that refused to end.

Inquisitor Sera Vane watched it from the ridge above Millbrook Ford, her hand resting on the hilt of a sword she was no longer entitled to carry. Below her, several hundred souls trudged north through the mud: farmers with their doors strapped to their backs because they had nowhere else to put their lives, grandmothers carrying chickens like disgraced relatives, children driving goats with sticks twice their own length. Somewhere in that river of the dispossessed, she had been told, there was a child.

"A god-touched child," Mara Joubert said beside her, in the flat tone he used for everything, as though the world were a toll road and he'd already counted the cost of passage. "That's what your friend in the tavern said, anyway. Between drinks. Loudly."

"He wasn't my friend. He was an informant."

"You paid for his ale."

"Informants drink on commission." Vane adjusted her pack. "It's standard practice."

"Right. And when I steal information, it's theft, but when you buy it, it's *practice*."

"Yes," she said. "Law is like that. It's one of its most reliable features."

He gave her a look — the look he'd been giving her since she'd put a rope around his wrists outside Harrowgate and then, three days later, untied it with an apology so grudging it barely qualified as words. *You're my only map,* she had told him. *Guide me to the Door and live.* He had agreed the way a cat agrees to a bath: with his whole body communicating that this arrangement was temporary and entirely your fault.

They went down into the column.

* * *

Vane had walked refugee columns before, back when she'd worn the grey-and-ember of the Inquisition and columns like this had been evidence of something. Bandits worked the edges. Deserters worked the middle. And priests of the Ember Court — she checked every hood now, out of habit, out of the particular paranoia of a woman whose own order wanted her head — priests worked the tail, where the slow and the desperate gathered, asking gentle questions about miracles seen and voices heard.

"Third hood from the cart," Mara murmured. He didn't point. Smugglers never pointed; they spoke in a sideways language of glances and small nods, as if even their faces were contraband. "Grey robe. Been passing down the line all morning, talking to anyone who'll cry at him."

"Anyone who'll cry at him," Vane repeated.

"It's how they fish. You don't ask people if they've seen miracles. You ask them what they've lost. Then you wait."

She hated how good he was at this. She hated more that she needed him to be.

They joined the column two carts back from the priest, moving with the flow, letting the mud swallow the sound of their boots. Up close, the procession resolved into individuals: a man carrying a loom's shuttle as though it were a relic; a woman walking beside an empty cradle she could not seem to put down; two brothers arguing over whose turn it was to carry the family pig, which rode between them in a sling with the serene entitlement of royalty.

And then, near the rear, walking alone.

The child was perhaps eight, maybe nine — hard to tell under the layers of borrowed wool. Small face, dark eyes, hair cut badly by someone in a hurry or someone grieving. The child carried nothing except, clutched to the chest, a smooth river stone the size of a fist.

And the child was humming.

It wasn't much of a tune. Four notes, maybe five, looping, patient, the kind of melody a person makes without knowing they're making it. Vane might have thought nothing of it — half the column hummed to keep terror at arm's length — except that as they passed the third standing stone of the old road-marker row, the stone began to glow.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. A soft amber warmth, deep in the granite, like a banked coal remembering fire. Like something answering.

The child did not stop walking. Did not look at the stone. Just hummed, and held the river stone tighter, and kept pace with the mud and the misery.

Vane stopped.

"Mara," she said quietly. "Tell me you saw that."

"I saw it." For once, the smuggler's flat tone had cracked. Underneath it was something almost young. "I've walked this road forty times. Those markers have been dead stones since before my grandmother was born. Dead, Sera. They're *supposed* to be dead."

"Everything's supposed to be dead," Vane said. "That appears to be the season we're having."

Ahead, the grey-robed priest had reached the child.

* * *

His name, when he offered it with unctuous gentleness, was Brother Anselm, and he crouched before the child the way men crouch before things they intend to pick up.

"There now," he said. "All alone? Where's your mother, little one?"

The child looked at him. Said nothing. Hummed — and the fourth marker stone, ten feet away, glowed like a lantern behind frosted glass.

Vane saw the priest's expression change, and she knew that expression. She had worn it herself, once, standing in a shrine in the capital while a dying woman's whispered prayer ignited green fire on the altar, and the Hierophant had turned to her with exactly that look: hunger dressed up as wonder. Wonder was honest. Hunger wore wonder's clothes.

"Pell!"

A woman came shoving through the column — broad-shouldered, flour still ghosting her sleeves, a baker's forearms and a mother's stride. She swept the child up against her hip with the efficiency of long practice. "I'm sorry, brother, she wanders. She doesn't talk, but her feet never stop." She smiled the tight smile of a woman who has learned that priests are weather: best endured politely, best survived by staying indoors.

"She doesn't talk," Anselm repeated softly. His eyes hadn't left the glowing stone. "But she *sings*, doesn't she. What a gift. The Court cherishes gifted children. We have schools. Warm beds. Food." He produced, from somewhere in his robe, a honey-cake wrapped in cloth, and held it out the way you'd bait a hook.

The child — Pell — looked at the cake. Looked at the stone. Pressed her face into her mother's shoulder.

"We're fine," the baker said.

"The Court insists—"

"The Court," said a new voice, "can insist all it likes. It's not licensed to take children off the road."

Vane stepped forward, and it cost her something to do it, because the voice that came out of her was the old voice — the Inquisitorial register, iron over gravel, the voice that had made hardened smugglers confess and minor nobles sit down. She hadn't earned that voice anymore. She'd thrown away its authority in a room full of burning ledgers when she'd said aloud what no one in the Ember Court would say: *your miracles are arithmetic, and someone is doing the sums.*

Anselm straightened, and recognized her, because of course he did. Her description had been nailed to shrine doors from here to the coast. "Sera Vane," he breathed. "The heretic inquisitor."

"Retired heretic inquisitor. There's a difference. Retirement comes with fewer benefits and better honesty."

"You stand in defiance of holy writ."

"I stand in defiance of *you*," she said, "which is currently a much shorter document." She let her hand rest visibly on her sword. "This child is under the protection of her mother, under the law of the road, and — if you want a writ you can't argue with — under mine. Walk on, brother."

For a moment, the priest weighed her. She could feel him weighing her — the sword, the exile's rags, the notorious name. Then he bowed, spread his hands in a gesture of perfect harmlessness, and withdrew down the column, unhurried, unbothered, a man who had learned everything he needed and knew precisely whom to tell.

Mara drifted to her elbow. "You realize," he said, low, "that we just became very findable."

"Yes."

"And that following a marked priest out of a refugee column is the opposite of smuggling."

"I'm aware."

"Just checking. You inquisitors have such a talent for making noise." He paused. "The mother heard you too, by the way. She's deciding whether to run."

Vane looked over. The baker was watching them — Pell still on her hip, the honey-cake abandoned in the mud, the child's dark eyes fixed on Vane with an attention that felt less like a child looking at a stranger and more like a lighthouse taking an interest in a ship.

"Then don't give her a reason," Vane said, and walked over, and took off her hood, which was the most disarming thing she owned.

* * *

The baker's name was Tilde Carrow, and she talked the way exhausted people talk when someone finally asks them a question they can answer — in floods, in lists, in the grammar of survival.

From Hallowmere. Left when the shrine burned. Husband stayed behind to argue with the Court about the shrine, which is why there was an empty place beside her where a husband should be, and why she flinched whenever anyone mentioned him. Pell had been silent since birth — "not broken, mind, just quiet, like she's saving up" — and the humming had started only after the first green fires. And the stones...

"The stones started after the fires," Tilde said. They sat by the wheel of her cart, off the road's edge, while around them the column made camp in the blue dusk. Pell sat in the cart bed among bundles, humming her four patient notes, and along the old marker row, one by one, the dead stones warmed to life like windows lit at evening. "First time it happened I nearly died of fright. Thought she'd caught something. Took her to a hedge-priest — the real old kind, pre-Court — and he looked at her for a long time and then he gave me back my coin and told me to get north as fast as I could and tell no one." Her jaw set. "So naturally everyone knows."

"Naturally," Mara said. "Mothers are the worst smugglers alive. No offense meant."

"Some taken."

"The Court will come tonight or tomorrow," Vane said. "Not Anselm — he's a fisherman. They'll send nets. Riders, probably six to ten, with writs and wagons." She said it plainly because Tilde deserved plainness; sugar-coating was just another kind of hook. "If you stay with the column, they'll take her. They won't hurt her, at first. They'll be gentle. That's worse. Gentle is how you feed something without it struggling."

Tilde's hands closed on each other. "Feed what?"

Vane hesitated. This was the part she'd sworn off — the telling of truths that got people burned. But she looked at Pell, humming in the cart, lighting stones the way other children lit candles, and she found that the old vow had rotted somewhere between the capital and this muddy field.

"Something older than the Court," she said. "Older than the shrines. The gods aren't waking, mistress Carrow. They're being *fed*. Every heretic they burn, every miracle they stage — it's not worship. It's husbandry. And a child who can wake standing stones with a song isn't a student to them. She's seed corn."

Silence, except the fire crackling and, faintly, four notes looping like a knot being tied over and over.

"They'll say she'll be honored," Vane went on. "Clothed in white. Fed well. Prayed over. And one day there'll be a ceremony, and it will be beautiful, and it will be the last time anyone sees her, and they will call it an ascension." She had read the ledgers. She had seen the columns of names next to columns of yields. "I know because I audited it. That was my sin. I did the sums and I said them out loud."

Tilde looked at her for a long moment. Exhausted, sharp, unbroken.

"You're the one they burned my cousin for sheltering," she said. "The runaway inquisitor."

"I'm the one whose fault that was, yes."

"Hm." Tilde turned toward the cart, lifted Pell down, and set her on her feet facing Vane. "Well then. Here's my cousin's avenger, come to save us. Say thank you, love."

Pell said nothing, of course. She looked up at Vane with those dark, unhurried eyes. Then she held out the river stone she carried everywhere — the smooth one, the one she slept holding, the one thing in the world she owned on purpose.

An offering. Or a test. With children and gods, Vane was learning, the two were difficult to distinguish.

She took it. It was warm. Warmer than a stone had any business being on an autumn night, warm like a sleeping animal, warm like — she turned it over and felt her breath go strange — warm like a word waiting to be said.

"She doesn't give that to anyone," Tilde said. "Took me a year to be allowed to hold it."

"Why me?" Vane asked, though the question wasn't really aimed at Tilde.

Pell answered anyway, in the only way she answered anything. She hummed. Four notes, five — and this time she looked directly at Vane, and the note bent, changed, became something almost shaped, almost a syllable, almost—

Behind them, every stone in the marker row flared gold at once, and somewhere far off in the dark hills, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep and noticed.

* * *

They left before dawn, the four of them — because Vane woke in the grey hour to find Mara already packing, Tilde already harnessing, and Pell already awake, watching the tree line, humming a warning.

The riders came at full light: nine of them, grey cloaks, ember badges, a wagon with a gilded cage built into it that they had dressed up with ribbons to look like a festival cart. Vane watched them from the rocks above the ford where the party now stood, and felt the old machinery of duty grind against the new thing growing in her chest, and was surprised — genuinely surprised — at how little contest there was.

Once, she would have handed the child over herself. Once, she'd believed that the law was a wall and mercy was a crack in it, and cracks were how the dark got in.

Now she understood the wall was the dark, mortared with ceremony.

"They'll fan out at the ford," Mara murmured beside her. He had his hood up and his crossbow slung with the casual insolence of a man who claimed to be a pacifist about everything except his own survival. "Six across the water, three up the far bank. Standard net. Your former colleagues always were tidy."

"They're not my colleagues."

"No. They're your *former* colleagues. Even angrier, then." He glanced at her. "There's a smuggler's path through the fells, north of here. Narrow. Wagon-width, barely. It adds four days."

"Four days of you leading us through places you haven't shown anyone."

"That's generally what 'smuggler's path' means, yes."

Below, the lead rider dismounted at the empty camp and picked up the ribbon Pell had left tied to a branch — Tilde's idea, a false trail south. The rider stared at it, then raised his head and scanned the fells, and even at this distance Vane saw the moment his gaze snagged on the marker stones below, all of them still faintly gold, still glowing, a trail of lit windows pointing north like an accusation.

Like a signature.

"Oh," Mara said softly. "Oh, that's going to be a problem."

"What?"

He pointed at the stones. Then at Pell, riding high on Tilde's shoulders, humming her contented four notes — at which the nearest boulder, quite on its own, began gently to shine.

"She's leaving us a trail," Mara said. "Every stone she passes lights up. We're not fleeing the Court, Sera. We're a procession." He sighed the sigh of a professional watching amateurs work. "I'm going to need to teach that child about covertness. First lesson: *glowing is loud.*"

Far behind them, a horn sounded — once, twice, answered from the north. Not pursuing. Signaling. Calling ahead.

And in the pause after the second horn, from the hills on either side of the valley, came an answering sound that was not a horn at all: a low, vast, harmonized drone, rising out of the rock itself, as though the mountains had learned Pell's little tune and were humming it back.
