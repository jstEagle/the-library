## 7. Smuggler's Justice

The thing about old debts, Mara had learned early, was that they were like knives left in a wall. You remembered where they were. You could hang your coat on them, if you trusted the wall. And every so often, one fell.

He'd hung his coat on Doss Ferrow for nine years.

They came down off the high shoulder of the mountain in the grey hour before dawn, when the world was the color of old pewter and the wind hadn't yet decided what it wanted to be. Mara led, as he always led, with Vane a half-step behind and Pell riding his shoulders like a small, warm, humming sack of turnips. The child had hummed all night — a low, tuneless drone that made the frost on the stones glimmer faintly, like the mountain was purring. Mara had decided, sometime around the third watch, not to think about that. Thinking about it was a door, and he was in the business of keeping doors shut.

"There," he said, and pointed with his chin.

Below them, tucked into a fold of rock like a coin slipped under a mattress, sat the waystation of Cold Saddle — a low stone barn, a chimney breathing thin smoke, and a corral where a handful of shaggy ponies stood steaming in the cold. And leaning in the doorway, arms crossed, a barrel of a man wrapped in a coat made of at least four other coats, watching the path with the patient expression of someone who had been waiting for exactly this.

Doss Ferrow had a face like a dropped pie — broad, flat, and somehow comforting, with small eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Nine years ago, on a night Mara did not like to revisit, Doss had been about to be hanged from a bridge by three brothers who held a grudge, and Mara had cut him down, lied to the brothers about where the money was, and walked away with a scar on his forearm and a promise: *Anything, Joubert. Anything, ever.*

"Anything, ever," Mara murmured now.

"Sorry?" said Vane.

"Nothing. Praying, in my fashion." He adjusted Pell's weight. "That's Doss Ferrow. Smuggler. Salt, brandy, occasionally people. Owes me his neck."

Vane's eyes narrowed the way they did when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "And you trust him?"

"I trust the debt," Mara said. "It's the most reliable thing about him." He paused. "That's a joke. Mostly."

They went down. Doss watched them come the whole way, and when they were twenty paces out he pushed off the doorframe and spread his arms, and his face split into the grin of a man greeting a long-lost brother he'd fully intended to rob.

"Mara Joubert," he boomed. "Nine years, and not one letter. I wept."

"You wept into other men's brandy casks, I heard."

"Constantly. It's a disease." Doss's gaze slid over Vane — took in the cropped hair, the travel-worn coat that had once been very fine, the sword carried like something that had opinions — and then up at Pell, and something behind the small eyes did a quick, quiet calculation that Mara chose not to witness. "And friends. You've never had friends. This is a red-letter day."

"Doss Ferrow, Inquisitor Sera Vane." Mara slid Pell down off his shoulders; the child's boots hit the dirt with a soft *whump* and the humming stopped, which was somehow worse. "The inquisitor part is retired. The inquisitor part is a story we don't tell."

"Stories are my whole trade," said Doss warmly. "Come in. There's stew. There's always stew; it's cheaper than conversation."

* * *

The stew was, in fact, excellent, which should have been Mara's first warning. Doss was a competent man, and competent men fed you well when they were about to disappoint you — it was a form of apology issued in advance.

They ate in the barn's back room, by a fire that smoked just enough to keep the room dim. Doss talked the whole time — about the passes, about which shrine-states were burning which other shrine-states, about a cousin of his who'd married a woman who turned out to be a goat in a very good hat. Pell sat close to the fire, humming softly at a poker until it glowed a faint, gentle orange, and Doss watched this with an expression of such elaborate unconcern that it was practically a confession.

"So," Doss said, at last, when the bowls were empty. "You want the Shepherd's Cut. Through the Teeth, down to the Vale. Three days' hard walking, no Court eyes, no tolls." He scratched his jaw. "I can get you to the mouth of it. After that, the Cut's a footpath and a prayer, and you've got the footpath covered."

"And the prayer?" Vane asked.

Doss smiled. "Prayers are expensive these days. Haven't you heard?"

Mara laughed, because it was expected, and because the alternative was listening to the small cold voice in the back of his skull that had been saying, all evening, in the tone of a man reading a bill of lading: *He's asked too many questions. He's looked at the child too long. He's told you nothing about his own road.*

The voice was right. Mara had known it was right since the second bowl of stew. He'd simply been enjoying the warmth, and the company, and the dangerous luxury of being *known* — of sitting across from someone who'd seen him at nineteen, terrified and bloody, and owed him his life for it.

"Tomorrow, then," Mara said. "First light."

"First light," Doss agreed, and poured three fingers of something amber into tin cups, and raised his. "To old debts."

They drank. Pell fell asleep against Vane's shoulder, which surprised them both; Vane went very stiff, then very carefully did not move for the rest of the evening, and Mara pretended not to notice, because some kindnesses only work if everyone agrees they didn't happen.

* * *

He woke in the black hour before dawn to the sound of Doss's voice, low and urgent, coming from the corral.

Mara was a smuggler's apprentice and then a smuggler, and a smuggler learns to sleep like a cat in a room full of rocking chairs. He was up and moving before his mind finished the sentence *that's Doss, and that's the wrong tone for Doss.*

He went out the back, along the wall, into the shadow of the woodpile. The night was clear and bitter. Frost furred everything. And by the corral fence, wrapped in his four coats, stood Doss Ferrow, talking to two riders in grey.

Grey. Not the Ember Court's scarlet-and-gold — not the priests, not the burners. Grey was the Court's *civil* arm. The clerks. The ones who wrote down names.

"—and the Cut's the only way through the Teeth that isn't watched," Doss was saying. His voice was steady, but Mara had heard that steadiness before, on men standing at the edge of things. "They'll be at the mouth of it by tomorrow night. I've marked the trail — chalk on the left-hand stones, every fork. You can't miss it."

"And the woman?"

"Ex-inquisitor. Vane. There's a writ — I've seen the shape of it. And the man, Joubert, he's nobody, but the child—" Doss stopped. Mara watched him take a breath, watched the big shoulders square, watched a man talk himself into a thing. "The child's the prize. Bring the child to the Court and the amnesty's clean. Full pardon. My name comes off every list in Thornfell. My brothers come home from the shrine-galleys."

One of the riders said something Mara couldn't catch. Doss laughed — a short, awful sound.

"Joubert? Joubert cut me down off a bridge when I was worth nothing to anyone. That's the whole of it. Debts don't make you loyal, friend. They just make you *expensive*." He paused. "I'll keep them fed and slow tomorrow. You'll have a day's lead."

Mara stood in the cold and felt something in his chest do a slow, careful turn, like a key in a lock he hadn't known he was carrying.

It wasn't rage. That was the strange thing. He'd expected rage — he'd had rage ready, banked and seasoned, a lifetime's supply. But what he felt instead was a kind of terrible clarity, cold and clean as the frost, and underneath it a small, sick disappointment, the kind you feel when a wall you've hung your coat on for nine years finally falls.

*Anything, ever.* Doss had said it with his whole face. And he'd meant it — that was the worst part. He'd meant it right up until meaning it cost more than not meaning it. That was what debts were, in the end. They were only good while they were cheap.

Mara looked at the two riders, memorizing them — the tall one with the grey hood, the short one with the crossbow slung wrong, too far forward. Then he went back inside, quiet as frost forming, and lay down, and did not sleep, and listened to Doss come in an hour later and settle into his blankets with the sigh of a man who has finally, *finally* paid off a bill.

* * *

They left at first light, as agreed. Doss led them up the switchbacks toward the mouth of the Shepherd's Cut, cheerful as a wedding, pointing out landmarks, telling a story about a mule and a bishop that Mara had heard before and that Doss told slightly differently every time, which was its own kind of confession.

The Cut's mouth opened in the rock ahead — a slot of shadow between two leaning slabs of stone, narrow enough that they'd have to go single file. Beyond it, three days to the Vale. Behind it, the waystation, and the chalk marks, and the grey riders with their day's lead.

Mara stopped at the shadow's edge.

"Doss," he said. "Walk with me a moment. The others can start through."

Vane looked at him. He gave her nothing, and she was — to her lasting credit — smart enough to take nothing and go, one hand resting briefly on Pell's small back, steering the child into the dark of the Cut.

Doss came to stand beside him at the lip of the pass. Below and behind them, the waystation smoke rose thin and straight. The morning was going to be beautiful. It was the kind of morning that made you believe in second chances, which was, Mara reflected, exactly the kind of morning this had been engineered to be.

"Nine years," Mara said.

"Nine years," Doss agreed, warm as the stew. "And you haven't aged a day, you bastard. What's your secret?"

"You're selling the Cut. Chalk on the left-hand stones. Grey riders, one tall, one short with a crossbow slung wrong. They give you a pardon and your brothers back." Mara said it evenly, the way you'd read a manifest. "And you told them I'm nobody."

Doss went still. It was remarkable, really — a man that size, going that still, that fast. The warmth didn't leave his face; it just stopped being connected to anything underneath, the way a painted smile stops.

"Mara—"

"I'm not angry." And he found, checking, that it was true. "That's the strange part. I keep waiting to be angry and it's not there. I'm just—" he searched for it, "—*tired*, Doss. I'm tired of walls falling."

"Mara. Listen to me." Doss's voice dropped, and for one moment the real man was there underneath, the one from the bridge, the one who'd wept. "The Court doesn't lose, do you understand? You've seen the Vale. You've seen what answers prayers now. There is no *outside* anymore. There's the Court, and there's ash, and I've got two brothers in the galleys and a daughter in Lowmark, and I chose—"

"I know what you chose." Mara drew the knife. "I was there when you chose it. I heard you."

Doss looked at the knife, and then at Mara's face, and did the last calculation of his life. Mara watched him do it — watched him weigh the big body, the surprise, the narrow ledge, the odds. Watched him decide they were good enough.

Doss lunged.

It was not a fair fight and Mara had never intended it to be one. That was the smuggler's whole doctrine: *fair is what you call it when you've already lost.* He stepped inside the lunge — Doss was strong but he was a cask-man, a wagon-man, all shoulder and no wrist — and put the knife in under the four coats, into the soft place above the collarbone, and turned it, and stepped back.

Doss sat down. Just sat, right there on the edge of the pass, with the enormous sky behind him, looking surprised in a mild, almost polite way, as if he'd slipped on ice at his own wedding.

"Ah," he said. "Ah, Mara."

"I know." Mara crouched, because someone should. "I know."

"Debts," Doss said. Blood at the corner of his mouth. He laughed, wetly. "Debts don't make you loyal. I told them that. Told them—" a cough, "—told them you were nobody."

"You told them I was expensive."

"That too." Doss's small eyes found Mara's and held them, and there was no hatred in them, which was somehow the hardest thing of all. There was only the ordinary, human, unforgivable arithmetic of a man with a daughter in Lowmark. "The tall one's name is Corve. The short one can't shoot moving targets, panics, aims high. You'll want to know that."

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because you cut me down once," Doss said, "and I've been trying to work off the interest ever since, and I never once got it right." His breath was going short. "Mara. The girl. Lowmark. If the lists come down—"

"I'll send word," Mara said. "If there's anyone left to send it."

"Good." Doss leaned back against the stone, and the sky was very blue above him, and he looked, for a moment, like a man resting at the end of a long road. "Anything, ever," he said, almost smiling. "You see the trouble with it. Terrible contract. No exit clause."

"I know," Mara said. "I wrote it."

Doss Ferrow died on the pass with the morning coming up gold behind him, outside any law, under no jurisdiction but the old one — the smuggler's law, the mountain's law, the one that says a man who sells the road kills everyone on it, and the road collects.

Mara sat with him until it was done. Then he took the body's hands and folded them, the way you do, and stood, and found that his own hands were shaking, and looked at them with a kind of distant interest.

This was what power was, then. He'd always thought it would feel like warmth. It felt like this — like a cold clean line drawn through the world, with you on one side of it and everyone else on the other, and the line made of you, made of your own hand, and no one to appeal to, and no one to blame, and the morning beautiful, and the blood already freezing on the stone.

He wiped the knife and sheathed it and went into the Cut.

* * *

Vane read his face in about three seconds, because she was Vane, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of mercy. Pell, sensitive to weather in all its forms, pressed close against Mara's leg and hummed something low and minor, and the frost on the Cut's walls glittered faintly in sympathy, and Mara put a hand on the child's head and walked.

They made good time. The Cut was exactly as he remembered — smuggler-narrow, smuggler-quiet, the kind of path that existed only because no one official had ever admitted it did. By late afternoon they came to the first fork, and Mara looked at the left-hand stones.

There was no chalk. Of course there was no chalk; Doss had made his marks for the riders, not for Mara. But Mara knew the Cut's true way anyway — right at the fork, down through the slot the salt caravans used, the one that didn't appear on any map because it had been paid for, over the years, in exactly the currency he'd paid today.

He turned right.

They were an hour down the true path when Vane finally spoke, without turning around, her voice flat against the echo of the stone.

"You killed him."

"Yes."

"And you're not sorry."

Mara considered the question honestly, because she deserved that much. Ahead, the path dropped toward the Vale, where the burnt ground waited, and the warm dead, and everything they'd walked into this story to find.

"I'm sorry it was cheap for him," he said at last. "That's not the same thing."

Vane was quiet for a long moment. Then, in the tone of a woman filing something away that she would take out and examine for the rest of her life, she said: "No. It isn't."

They walked on. And behind them, far up the mountain, faint on the wind, came the sound Mara had been dreading all day — a horn, and then another, answering it. The grey riders, finding the waystation, finding the body, finding the empty fork where the chalk should have been.

They'd panic. They'd aim high.

Mara pulled Pell closer and walked faster, down into the dark of the mountain, toward the Vale and the Door and whatever price was waiting at the end of the road — carrying, now, in his hands, the first taste of a power he'd sworn he'd never want, and the growing, cold understanding that the Door was not the only thing on this journey learning what it cost to open.
