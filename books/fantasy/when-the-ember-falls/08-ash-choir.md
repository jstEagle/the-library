## 8. Ash Choir

The hands come in the night.

Pell knows they are coming before they come — the stones say so. The little grey stone in his pocket goes cold all at once, like a window in winter, and the humming stops in his throat on its own, the way a bird stops singing when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

Then: torchlight. Shouting that isn't words yet, just shapes of sound. Vane's voice, one shape. Mara's voice, another, lower and faster and full of words that are probably swearing, because Mara swears when he's frightened and pretends he doesn't. The third shape belongs to strangers, and it is the worst one, because it is *calm*.

Calm voices mean people who have already decided how everything ends.

Pell does what Pell always does. Pell runs small. Runs quiet. Gets behind the biggest thing nearby, which tonight is a fallen pine, and presses himself into the space between its roots where the earth smells like sleep, and pulls the grey stone out and holds it against his chest, and waits.

It doesn't work this time.

A hand closes on his ankle — gentle, which is worse than rough, because gentle means they've been told not to bruise the merchandise — and then there are more hands, many hands, and the world tips sideways, and the last thing Pell sees before a hood comes down over everything is Vane's face, white as the chalk marks on the trees, and her mouth shaping his name without any sound coming out.

*Pell.*

His name has never had a sound before. It's only ever been a mouth-shape, the way everyone talks at him, around him, over him. But in that mouth-shape, for half a heartbeat, there was something else. Something like the way the standing stones glow when he hums to them: warm from inside.

Then the hood. Then the dark. Then the road, bumping, going away from them both.

* * *

Here is what the hooded dark is like:

It is like being a stone at the bottom of a stream. Everything happens above you. Voices come through the water of it, rounded and strange. *The child. Yes, the god-touched child. The Hierophant will want — no, not want, will require —* Words like coins dropping through water.

Pell counts the bumps in the road. Forty-one. Forty-two. Counting is a kind of humming you do inside your head, and nobody can take humming away, not even with a hood. Not even with rope, and there is rope; Pell can feel it at the wrists now, soft from use, worn smooth by other wrists before these. That thought arrives and Pell puts it down carefully, like setting down something hot.

Forty-three. Forty-four.

Mama used to say — well. Mama used to say things. Mama's voice was the first music Pell ever heard, before the stones, before anything. Mama said the world was mostly kind if you kept your head down and your songs soft. Mama said the gods were far away and mostly asleep and mostly stories.

Mama was wrong about the gods.

Pell knows this now in the place under the thinking place, the deep place where knowing happens without permission. Because even here, even hooded and roped and bumping along in the dark, Pell can feel them waking up out there in the mountains — vast slow turnings-over in the earth's sleep, like giants rolling toward morning — and every one of them is *hungry*, and hunger is a feeling that travels, the way cold travels through a wall.

And worse — much worse — some small part of that hunger has already noticed Pell. Has turned toward Pell the way a sunflower turns, slow and inevitable, toward the light.

Except Pell is not the light. Pell is the bread.

* * *

They arrive somewhere that smells like ash and tallow and too many people praying.

That's the thing about the Ember Court's camps — or temples, or whatever the tall tents and the smoking braziers are — that nobody who isn't Pell would ever know: they are *loud*. Not with trumpets. With prayers. Hundreds of them, thousands, rising off the camp like heat off a summer road, and each prayer is a thread, and each thread runs down, down, down into the deep places where the old gods are turning over in their sleep, and at the end of every thread there is a hand.

Reaching.

That is what prayer feels like, from the underneath. Pell learned this three nights ago, in the refugee column, when the whole column prayed at once for rain and the rain came so hard it drowned a man's oxen, and Pell felt every single reaching hand go through him like wind through a reed. Prayers are hands reaching down a well. And the gods are at the bottom of the well. And Pell — god-touched, stone-humming, mute little Pell — is sometimes the bucket they get hauled up in.

The ropes come off for the ritual. Ropes would spoil it, someone says, through the water-sound. The god must see the vessel's hands open.

Vessel.

Pell has been called many things. Stray. Whisper-child. That odd one. Once, by a woman in a market who meant it kindly, *the mountain's own*. But *vessel* is new, and *vessel* is the worst one, because vessels are things you pour things into, and things you pour things into don't get asked.

They carry Pell — walk, really, Pell's legs work, they just aren't asked about either — into a ring of standing stones. Real ones, old ones, older than the Court's tall tents, older than the Court itself maybe. And here is a secret, the kind of secret that lives in the body instead of the head: Pell knows these stones. Has never met them before, and knows them. They are cousins to the stones back home, the ones Pell hums to sleep. They are singing very quietly, very badly, because they are afraid.

Stones can be afraid. Nobody believes that. It's true anyway.

The priests begin.

There are nine of them, in ember-orange robes, arranged around the ring, and they are not praying the way villagers pray, the way Mama prayed, small and hopeful and a bit embarrassed about wanting things. These prayers are *engineered*. They have structure. Verse and response, call and answer, a great wheeling architecture of words built for one purpose: to reach down the well and haul something up.

And the something comes.

Pell feels it the way you feel weather change — pressure first, then a smell, then the light going wrong. The braziers gutter. The stones' quiet frightened song cuts off entirely, which has never happened before, not once, not ever, and their silence is the loudest thing Pell has ever heard. Then the center of the ring begins to glow, low down, near the earth, an ember-color that is not fire's color at all. Fire is quick and orange and forgets you immediately. This color is *patient*. This color has been waiting under the world since before there were names.

A hand reaches up out of the well.

It finds Pell instantly. Of course it does. It was invited, and Pell is the door it was invited through, and doors don't get to be picky.

What it feels like — and Pell will spend a long time, later, finding words for this, and never quite find enough — is being *read*. Being read by something enormous and patient and starving, a reading that goes cover to cover, that turns the pages of you without asking, that licks its thumb. It moves through Pell's memory like a finger down a shelf. *Here: the grey stone, warm from a pocket. Here: the standing stones glowing, glowing. Here: a mother's mouth making a mouth-shape that means safe, that stopped meaning safe.* It reads all of it. It is interested in all of it. Its interest is the most frightening thing Pell has ever survived, because its interest has no cruelty in it at all.

It is simply hungry the way winter is cold. You cannot argue with winter. You can only be outside in it.

The priests' chanting climbs. The ember-light climbs with it. And Pell, kneeling in the middle of the ring with hands held open by other hands, does the only thing left.

Pell hums.

Not loudly. Not defiantly. Just — hums. The note that lives lowest in the chest, the one that matches the stones' deepest tone, the one that makes frost bloom in patterns on windows. It is not a weapon. It is not a prayer. It is the opposite of a prayer: nothing reaching, nothing asking, nothing wanted. Just a sound, given to no one, belonging to no one, a small round room of sound with Pell inside it.

The hand in the well hesitates.

Because here is the thing the priests got wrong, the thing they could spend nine lifetimes learning and never learn, sitting up in their tall tents with their engineered prayers: the old gods do not want worship. Worship is just food shaped like love. What they starve for — truly starve for, down at the bottom of the well, in the dark where nothing has answered them in generations — is the other thing. The unasked thing. The song sung to no one. The stone warmed by a pocket for no reason. The small useless kindnesses that nobody offers up, that just *are*, like warmth coming off a hearth whether or not anyone is watching.

Pell's hum is made of that. All of Pell's humming is made of that.

So the hand pauses. And the pause stretches. And in the pause — in that hairline crack of hesitation in a hunger older than kingdoms — the ember-light flickers, confused, like a flame trying to decide which way the wind blows.

The priests falter. Their wheeling architecture of words loses a brick. One of them says, in a voice gone suddenly ordinary and human and scared: *"It's stopping — why is it stopping—"*

And then the night explodes.

* * *

Later — much later, wrapped in a blanket that smells like woodsmoke and horse and, faintly, of a certain grumpy smuggler who pretends not to care about anyone — Pell will piece together what the explosion was.

It was Vane. Vane, who came alone over the mountain on a horse she'd ridden half to death, who walked into the Court's camp wearing no office and no colors and carrying nothing but a sword and a fury that had finally finished deciding what it was for. It was Mara too — because of course it was Mara, appearing out of the dark exactly where no plan said he'd be, doing the coward's trick of being brave when nobody was looking, cutting tent-ropes and spooking horses and generally being a natural disaster in human form.

But in the moment, in the ring, in the ember-light, none of that has names yet. In the moment there is only: chanting breaking apart, robes running, an orange blur, a blade catching brazier-light, someone shouting *the child, take the child* and then not shouting anymore — and then two familiar shapes crashing into the ring of stones like weather.

Vane reaches Pell first. She drops to her knees in the trampled snow and gathers Pell up, and her armor is cold and her hands are shaking, and she keeps saying the mouth-shape of Pell's name over and over, *Pell, Pell, I have you, I have you,* as if saying it could make it structurally true. Maybe it can. Maybe that's what names are for.

Mara stands over them both with a knife he holds like he's surprised to find it in his hand, breathing hard, glaring at the fleeing priests with an expression Pell has never seen on him before — fierce, and terrified, and underneath the terror, horribly, a little lit up. Like a man discovering a room in his own house he didn't know was there.

"It saw you," Mara says. His voice comes out wrong. "When it touched you. Did it — did it leave anything? Do you know what it—"

"Let the child breathe," Vane says, and then, softer, into Pell's hair: "Breathe."

Pell breathes.

The ring of stones stands quiet around them. Whatever came up the well has gone back down it, unhauled, unfed, puzzled — but not gone, never gone, only patient again. The braziers smoke. Somewhere out in the dark, the Court's camp is shouting itself into order, which means they have minutes, only minutes, before the minutes run out.

And in the middle of all of it — the cold, the fear, the shaking arms holding him — Pell opens a mouth that hasn't made a word in longer than Pell can remember, longer than Mama, longer than anything, and finds that the hum has changed. That all this time the humming wasn't silence. It was a door held shut. And doors, it turns out, open from the inside.

The word comes out cracked and small and rusted with disuse, more breath than sound, barely a word at all.

But Vane goes absolutely still. And Mara turns his head slowly, slowly, like a man hearing ice shift underfoot.

"*Door,*" Pell says again. Clearer now. Sure of it, though sure of nothing else — not what it means, not why the stones sang it into Pell's bones in the ring, not why the word arrived wearing a feeling like a map wearing a shroud.

Only that it matters. Only that everything, from here to the end of the mountains, has quietly rearranged itself around it.

Mara looks at Vane. Vane looks at Mara. Far off in the dark, a horn sounds, and then another, answering.

"The Door," Mara repeats, very quietly, the way you repeat a thing to check whether saying it twice makes it less true. It doesn't. "Kid, I think we need to talk about how you know that word."

Pell points. Not south, toward the Vale. Not toward any direction maps bother with.

Up. Into the high country, where the snow never quite melts, where the clans keep old rites and older silences — where, though neither Pell nor anyone else in the ring of stones can know it yet, a woman named Sera Vane is about to learn that everything she served was the corruption all along.

The horn sounds a third time.

They run.
