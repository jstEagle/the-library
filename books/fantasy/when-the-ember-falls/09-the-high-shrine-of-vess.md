## 9. The High Shrine of Vess

The mountain clans did not take them in so much as absorb them, the way a river absorbs a dropped stone — with a ripple, a swirl, and then no sign that anything had ever fallen at all.

Sera Vane had expected chains. She had spent eleven years in the Inquisition's service, and her professional expectation of strangers was always chains. Instead, an old woman with cheeks like wind-scoured apples had looked at Vane's torn cassock, at Pell half-asleep against Mara's shoulder, and said only, "You'll be wanting soup before questions. Questions keep better."

Her name was Maren of the Vess clans, and she was wrong about the soup. The soup was terrible — barley and mutton fat and something piney that Vane suspected had wandered into the pot by accident and been too polite to leave. But it was hot, and it came with dark bread, and when Pell woke enough to eat, the child ate three bowls of it without once stopping the low, tuneless humming that had become as constant as breathing.

The humming made the hearthstones glow.

Vane watched it happen on their first night under the Vess roof: a faint amber light rising from the stones around the fire, pulsing gently in time with the child's song, like embers remembering what they used to be. Nobody else in the longhouse remarked on it. A boy turned his porridge. A dog relocated.

"You're staring," Mara murmured beside her. He had a gift for appearing exactly when Vane least wanted company, which was always.

"I'm observing," she corrected. "There's a difference. Staring is idle. Observing has a purpose."

"And your purpose?"

Vane didn't answer. Her purpose, if she was honest — and she was trying, lately, to be honest, because dishonesty had recently gotten several hundred people burned — was that the glow frightened her less than it should have. That was the part she kept turning over like a stone looking for what lived underneath. A year ago, god-touched light in a hearth would have meant a pyre. Now it meant warmth. Now it meant Pell slept easier for it.

She was becoming someone she didn't recognize, and the worst part was that she liked her.

* * *

The High Shrine of Vess sat above the village, a day's climb up a path that switchbacked through pines gone silver with frost. It was not a shrine in any sense Vane understood. There was no altar, no icon, no offering box. There was a ring of standing stones older than the kingdom — older, Maren said, than the idea of kingdoms — and the stones were warm to the touch even in deep winter, and the clan had been keeping them warm for forty generations.

"Keeping them *how*?" Vane asked.

Maren smiled with most of her teeth. "Come up tomorrow. You'll see. Bring the little one." Her eyes went to Pell, and there was something in the look that Vane couldn't parse — not fear, not greed, but recognition. The way you look at a stranger wearing your grandmother's coat.

They climbed at dawn. Frost furred the pines, and their breath hung in clouds, and Pell rode on Mara's back with small hands knotted in his collar, humming a slow ascending phrase that made icicles chime in harmony. Vane walked behind and tried to pray, out of habit, and found she could only get four words in — *O Flame, eternal, pure* — before the words curdled in her mouth. *Eternal.* The Court had taught her the gods were eternal. The Court had also taught her the gods were hungry, and that hunger was holy, and she had fed people into it with a clear conscience and a formal blessing.

Four words. Then nothing. She kept climbing.

At the top, the stones stood in a circle perhaps thirty paces across, black and smooth as frozen water, each taller than two men. Snow lay everywhere except within the ring, where the ground was bare brown earth and soft as moss. And in the center, on a flat stone like a table, lay offerings: bread, salt, wool, a wooden toy horse worn smooth by generations of small hands, a wedding ring.

No blood. No ash. No pyre.

"They give things they love," Vane said slowly. "Not things they hate."

"What did you give?" Maren asked, from behind her. "In your courts. In your fine burning places."

Vane opened her mouth to say *sinners*, to say *the guilty*, to say *justice* — and heard, in the space before the words, how thin they all were. "People," she said finally. "We gave it people."

"Aye." Maren crouched and pressed her palm flat to one of the stones, and Vane saw the old woman's lips move in something that wasn't quite words, and the stone answered — a pulse of green-gold light, gentle as a heartbeat. "And it ate them, and it grew, and now it's waking up starving. You don't feed a fire wood and then act surprised when it wants the house."

Pell slid off Mara's back and stood at the ring's edge, swaying slightly, humming. All nine stones lit at once. The light rose through them like sap through trees, and for a moment Vane felt something move beneath the mountain — vast, slow, attentive — turn over in its sleep and listen.

Then Pell stopped humming, yawned enormously, and asked — in gestures, since the child did not speak — whether the toy horse could be ridden.

"No," said Maren fondly. "It's for giving, not taking. That's the whole rite, little one. Giving, not taking."

Vane stood very still in the warm ring of stones and felt the first true crack run through the last wall of her certainty. Because she knew the Ember Court's rites. She had performed them. And the Court's rites were taking, dressed in giving's clothing. They took the condemned. They took confessions. They took everything a person was and called it an offering, and the flame roared higher every year, and everyone called the roaring *grace*.

"Your gods," she said carefully. "When they answer prayers here. What do they ask in return?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment, wind pulling at her gray braid.

"Nowt," she said. "That's what frightens your Court about us, inquisitor. Not the rites. The price."

* * *

It was that night, in the longhouse, with the storm rattling the shutters and Pell asleep between them like a small warm coal, that Maren told them the truth about the Ember Court.

Mara had gone out to check the animals — he never fully slept under another person's roof, Vane had noticed; he slept in doorways and thresholds, one ear open, a man built like a hinge — and so it was just Vane and the old woman and the dying fire, and Maren chose that hour deliberately, Vane was sure of it. Old women always chose their hours deliberately. It was one of the many powers the Inquisition had never catalogued.

"You served them," Maren said. Not a question.

"I served the law."

"The law wore their robes."

"The law *was* the robes," Vane said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back. "I believed the miracles were real. I still believe some of them are real. That was the horror of doubting — you can't unsee a pillar of green fire. But the doctrine—" She stopped. Started again. "The doctrine says the gods wake hungry, and hunger is sin, and sin must be burned out of the world to appease them. I enforced that doctrine for eleven years. I sent—"

She had been going to say a number. She discovered she didn't know the number. That was somehow worse than knowing it.

"I sent people to the flame," she finished.

"Aye." Maren stirred the coals. "And did you ever wonder where the power went? When the flame roared? Somebody was drinking it, inquisitor. Fires don't roar for free."

"I don't understand."

"No," Maren agreed, not unkindly. "You wouldn't yet. So listen." She settled back, and began to speak the way clan women speak of weather and lineage — plainly, as established fact.

"The old rites are giving. Bread, salt, wool, things loved. Small feedings, freely given, and the gods sleep sound on them, the way a man sleeps sound after bread. Your Court does the opposite and calls it holiness. They take. They take the terrified, the condemned, the heretic — and terror seasons the offering, see? Fear makes the meal rich. A willing gift feeds a god for a night. A screaming one feeds it for a season." Maren's face was grim in the firelight. "And the Court doesn't burn the screaming ones for the gods' sake. They burn them for the *harvest*. Every pyre pours power into the priests who light it. That's the tithe nobody names. That's why the pyres got bigger every year, and the miracles grander, and the granaries emptier. They're not worshipping the gods, girl. They're *farming* them. Fattening the herd on heretics and milking the flame."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, the house timbers ticked against the cold.

"That can't be true," Vane said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "The Hierophant fasts. The Hierophant wears sackcloth. I've seen him—"

"You've seen a farmer in work clothes," Maren said. "Fasting keeps the vessel empty, aye? Empty vessels hold more. Sackcloth keeps the flock from wondering why the shepherd's barn is full. Ask yourself the question you've been afraid to ask since the rope missed your neck: where do the miracles go? Who grows richer when the flame answers? Not the burned. Not the praying. Follow the power, inquisitor. It's the one lesson your order never taught, because teaching it would end the order."

Vane sat with it. She thought of the tithe-houses in the capital, marble-faced, always warm. She thought of the Inquisitors' refectory, where the wine had been very good and no one had ever explained why. She thought of the annual accounting, the ledgers she'd been forbidden to read past the third page, the page where the column headed *RECEIVED* simply continued, unnumbered, into darkness.

She thought of Sera Vane at nineteen, kneeling on cold marble, swearing herself to the Flame Eternal, certain to her bones that she was signing on to be a sword against the dark.

"The law I served," she said quietly, "was the corruption."

"It usually is," said Maren. "Law's a fine plow. Depends what's being planted."

Vane looked down at her hands. Inquisitor's hands. They had held the rope. They had held the brand. They had, once, held a weeping woman steady at the pyre's edge while the priest read the rite, because the woman was struggling, and struggling was disorder, and disorder was sin.

She had thought she was holding her steady *for her own good*.

"I need air," she said, and went out into the storm, and stood in it until her tears froze, which did not take long, and which did not help.

* * *

Mara found her there, eventually. He was good at finding people; it was the other thing, the being-found, he'd never mastered. He leaned against the byre wall beside her and said nothing for a while, which was the single greatest kindness anyone had shown her in years.

"The old woman talked to you," he said at last.

"She talked."

"And?"

"And I'm standing in a snowdrift having a crisis of faith, Joubert, what do you think *and*."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. His breath steamed. Above them, through a tear in the clouds, the stars over Thornfell burned hard and cold, and somewhere far to the south — Vane felt it rather than saw it, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of smoke — something enormous was being fed.

"My mistress used to say," Mara offered, "that the difference between a religion and a racket is whether the priest eats last or first."

"Your mistress sounds wise."

"My mistress sounds wise because she's had eighty years to practice sounding wise. Mostly she was insufferable." He paused. "Is. Present tense. Don't ask."

Vane almost smiled. It hurt her face; she'd been clenching it for hours. "Eleven years," she said. "I gave them eleven years, Joubert. My oath, my mind, my— I branded a miller in Harrowdale for keeping an old charm over his door. A *charm*. His daughter watched. And the whole time, the men who ordered it were pouring the flame into their own cups like wine." She shook her head. "I thought doubt was my sin. That's why they stripped me — I doubted, and doubting was corruption of the vessel. But it wasn't sin. It was the only clean thing in me. Doubt was the part that was still mine."

Mara was quiet a moment. "What will you do?"

"I don't know." It was the truest sentence she'd spoken in a decade, and it frightened her more than the green fire had. "Everything I was is a lie with good posture. Everything I knew how to be is a weapon pointed the wrong way." She looked at him. "But I know this much. I'm not going back. Whatever the Court offers, whatever it threatens — I've seen behind the altar now. You can't unsee behind the altar."

"No," Mara agreed softly. "You really can't."

Inside, faintly, through the shutter cracks, they could hear Pell humming in sleep, and the hearthstones glowing, and the clan children laughing at the glow, unafraid. Warmth without a price. Giving without a ledger.

Vane stood in the snow a moment longer, letting the cold finish its work, letting the last of the Inquisitor freeze out of her bones.

"We should sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we walk."

"Where?"

"Wherever the Door is. And Joubert—" She turned to him, and the starlight caught the frost in her hair, and her voice was quiet and absolutely level. "If the Court comes for that child while we're here — and it will come, it's only a matter of when — then for the first time in eleven years, I know exactly which side of the rope I'm standing on."

Far to the south, over the black spine of the mountains, the sky flickered — once, twice — a soft orange pulse, like a pyre seen from a great distance, like a harvest coming in.

Maren, watching from the doorway, spat into the snow.

"They've started early this year," she said. "Best eat well in the morning. We've a hard road, and hungry things behind us."
