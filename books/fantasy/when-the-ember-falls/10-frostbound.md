## 10. Frostbound

The storm came down on them like a debt collector.

Mara had been watching the sky all afternoon — the particular bruised color the clouds went over the high passes when they meant business — and he'd said nothing about it, because there was nothing to say. You could not outrun a Thornfell blizzard. You could only find a hole and get into it before it found you.

"There," he said, pointing his chin at a black seam in the rockface ahead. "Old shepherd's cave. My mistress used it, once."

"Your mistress used every cave in these mountains," Vane said. She had Pell wrapped against her chest inside her cloak, the child's legs locked around her waist like a small determined vise. "I'm beginning to think you learned nothing from her but geography."

"I learned plenty of other things." He tested the snow underfoot with his staff. "Most of them I'd rather not teach you."

The cave mouth was half-choked with drift, but behind it opened into a chamber deep enough to stand in, dry enough to matter, and — Mara checked this first, always first — with only one way in. He got a fire going from the hoard of scrub-pine he kept bundled under his pack's flap, because a man who survived by never being needed by anyone still needed dry wood, and he was honest enough with himself to admit it.

Pell slid off Vane's hip the moment there was firelight and went, as Pell always did, to the stones. The child crouched at the cave wall, laid one palm flat against the granite, and hummed — two notes, low and patient, like a mother soothing something enormous. The stone did not glow here. It never did, in caves. But Pell seemed satisfied anyway, and patted the wall twice, the way you'd pat an old dog, and came back to sit as close to the fire as skin allowed.

"What is that?" Vane asked. She asked it every time. She would ask it every time until Pell answered, and Pell did not answer, and so the question just lived among them now, a fourth traveler who never carried anything.

Mara shrugged. "Maybe the rocks are listening."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to be true, which is worse."

Outside, the wind arrived properly. It didn't howl so much as *arrive* — a white wall slamming across the pass with a sound like the whole mountain clearing its throat. Snow began sifting through the cave mouth in fine gray threads. Within an hour, Mara knew, the entrance would be sealed entirely. They would be entombed, comfortably, for a day or three.

He should have hated it. A man on the run does not enjoy being trapped. But there was a part of him — a part he kept in the same locked drawer as the grimoire and every other thing he refused to look at — that felt the wind seal the door and thought: *good*. No one coming in. No one to run from. For one night, the world had been reduced to a circle of firelight and three people, and none of them wanted anything from him except warmth, which even he could provide.

Vane rationed out dried mutton and hardtack with the grim precision of a woman who had once fed a garrison on less. Pell ate with the total absorption of childhood, crumbs going everywhere, and Vane — Inquisitor Sera Vane, terror of heretics, breaker of the Kellam cell, stripped of office and hunted by her own Church — reached over without looking and wiped a crumb from the child's cheek with her thumb.

Mara watched her do it and said nothing, because saying something would have made her stop, and it was better than theater.

* * *

It was the cold that woke the book.

Not cold exactly — the fire was holding, barely — but the kind of deep, mineral chill that comes up through stone when the mountain itself is thinking. Mara had the grimoire out of its oilcloth mostly from habit; nights like this were for study, if you were foolish enough to be studying a stolen book that whispered. He'd had it three months now, since the night he'd run from his mistress's hut with it under his shirt and her voice ringing in his ears — *you'll open it when you're ready, boy, and not one page before* — and in three months it had given him nothing. The early chapters read fine: hedge-craft, weather-sense, the little binding words she'd taught him aloud anyway. But somewhere past the middle the pages simply stopped. Not blank. *Stopped.* His eyes slid off them like rain off waxed canvas. He'd held the thing over candles, soaked it, threatened it. Nothing.

Tonight, when he opened it, the pages past the seal were warm.

He sat very still. The fire popped. Pell looked up from a half-doze against Vane's shoulder, and tilted their head at the book the way Pell tilted at everything that hummed.

"Mara," Vane said quietly. She'd seen his face.

"It's warm," he said. "The pages are warm."

"Is that good?"

"You're asking me? It's your profession that deals in omens."

"My profession dealt in hanging people for omens," she said. "I've since developed opinions."

He turned the page.

It turned. That alone nearly stopped his heart — the resistance he'd felt for three months, the sense of pushing against a shut door, gone, just gone, and the paper moved under his thumb as easily as breath. The writing on the new page was his mistress's hand, but older, younger, sharper — the hand of a woman decades before he'd known her, before the stoop and the squint and the smell of valerian. And it was not hedge-craft.

It was a map.

No — it became a map as he read it, the letters rearranging themselves the way dreams do, and what they arranged into was the mountain range he'd spent his whole life learning, drawn from underneath. Every smuggler path he knew appeared on it, and then kept going, down and inward, through passages no smuggler used because no smuggler came back. The lines converged on a point beneath the roots of Thornfell itself, beneath the High Shrine, beneath everything, marked with a single word in the old tongue that his mistress had made him learn without ever saying why:

*Door.*

"Pell," Vane said suddenly. "Pell, don't—"

But the child was already crawling toward the fire, toward the book, humming those two low notes, and where Pell's shadow fell across the open pages the ink *lifted*, rising off the paper like iron filings finding a magnet, hanging in the air between them in a slow-turning constellation of words and lines. The cave filled with a sound like distant singing through water.

Then it settled. Back onto the page. Ordinary ink. The fire crackled on as if nothing had happened, which was the most frightening option available.

Mara realized he had been holding his breath and let it out. "Right," he said. "So it opens in a blizzard. Good to know. Very sensible book."

"Why now?" Vane had Pell gathered against her, one arm banded around the small body, her other hand nowhere near her sword, which was its own kind of discipline. "Three months you've carried it."

"Because it wasn't waiting for me." He turned pages now freely, whole chapters unsealed, and the shape of it rose in him with each one, cold and vast. "It was waiting for the storm. Look — here." He angled the page toward the firelight. "She wrote it in cipher, but the cipher key is the date. The date of the last great sealing. And the date is written as a *weather* — a storm like this one. The same storm. The book only counts time by the mountain's own calendar, and tonight the mountain is keeping the old date."

Vane stared at him. "Your mistress bound a book to a blizzard."

"She bound it to a *night*," Mara said. "This night. The night the gods were shut away. And the mountain keeps its anniversaries."

* * *

They read by turns after that, because some of it neither of them could bear alone.

Mara read the geography: the Door lay beneath the mountain's heart, reachable by passages that predated every kingdom built above them, and the way in ran through country the Court had not yet thought to hold because no one alive remembered it existed. Except his mistress. Except, apparently, him.

Vane read the history, because she read fastest and because someone had to say the ugly parts out loud. The Door was not a gate out of Thornfell, exactly. It was older than that purpose. It had been made — or found, the text wavered on which — as a hinge between what the gods touched and what they could not. When the old orders sealed the gods away, generations ago, they had done it *through* the Door, folding the divine hunger behind it like a man folding a letter he cannot bear to reread.

"And it opens how?" Mara asked. He asked it lightly. He was proud of how light he made it sound.

Vane read in silence for long enough that the fire had to be fed. Then she read it aloud, flat, the way she delivered verdicts.

"*The Door does not open for worthiness, nor blood, nor prayer. It opens for surrender. One who would pass must give up what they most want to keep. Not what they love — what they keep. The thing held closest in the secret ledger of the heart. The Door knows the difference, and the Door does not bargain.*"

The wind outside filled the pause, pressing at the sealed entrance like something leaning its weight against a door of its own.

"Well," Mara said. "That's fine. I don't keep anything."

"Mara."

"I don't. That's the whole point of me. Ask anyone. Ask my mistress, who trained me to it — ask her, actually, why would a hedge-witch train her apprentice to need nothing? What was that *for*?" He heard his own voice climbing and brought it down. "A man who wants nothing can't be robbed. Apparently a man who wants nothing also can't leave. Convenient."

Pell reached out and put one small hand flat on the open page, over the word *surrender*, and hummed the two notes, soft, and the ink did not move, but the fire leaned toward the child like a listener.

Vane watched the flames bend. "There's more," she said. "At the bottom. Smaller hand. Later."

Mara looked. Beneath the main text, cramped into the margin in his mistress's later hand — the stooped, faded script he knew — was a single line, added like an apology:

*"It will ask you for the wanting itself, in the end. I am sorry. I chose you because you were born empty-handed, and I have hated myself for it every day since."*

He closed the book.

For a while nobody spoke. Pell fell asleep against Vane, mid-hum, the notes dissolving into breath. The storm ground on outside, patient as a priest. Mara fed the fire stick by stick and told himself the tightness in his chest was smoke.

"When we reach it," Vane said at last, quietly, "you don't have to go through. Whatever it asks. We find another way, or we don't go at all. A door that only opens for sacrifice isn't a door, it's a toll. And I have spent my life serving institutions that charge tolls in exactly that coin, and I am *done*—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "We'll find another way."

"There is no other way. You know that. I know that." He smiled, and it worked, mostly. "Besides, I told you. I don't keep anything. Maybe the Door will be embarrassed. Open out of sheer disappointment."

"Mara."

"Sleep, Inquisitor. First watch is mine. It's always mine — you snore like a landslide with opinions."

She gave him a look that promised a longer conversation later, the kind of look that was, he was beginning to understand, the closest thing she had to tenderness. She lay down with Pell tucked into the curve of her body, and within minutes both of them breathed in the slow rhythm of the genuinely exhausted.

Mara sat with the fire and the book and the storm.

*I chose you because you were born empty-handed.*

His whole life, he had worn that emptiness like armor and called it freedom. Never needed anyone. Never kept anything. A man who could walk away from any room, any road, any person, at any moment, owing nothing. He had thought it was survival. He had thought it was *his*.

And now a dead woman's handwriting told him it had been a preparation. A shaping. Someone had looked at a child and begun, patiently, deliberately, to hollow him out — to make of him a vessel shaped exactly like a keyhole.

Except.

Except that sitting here, in the firelight, watching Vane's arm tighten around Pell even in sleep, hearing the child breathe, feeling the ridiculous warm ache of it — he was not sure the hollowing had taken. Somewhere between a roadside shrine and a refugee column and a smuggler's blood on the pass, things had gotten *kept*. Things he had not authorized. A disgraced inquisitor who wiped crumbs from a child's face. A mute kid who patted stones like old dogs. A future, maybe, a small stupid imagined future with a hut in it and a garden and no running at all.

That was the thing he most wanted to keep. That was what the Door would come for.

The fire sank low. Outside, the storm paused — a strange, held-breath lull, the kind that comes before the worst of it — and in the silence Mara heard, faint and far below the rock, through however many fathoms of stone, a sound like a vast throat swallowing.

Or like a door, testing its hinges.

He looked at the grimoire. On its cover, faint as breath on glass, new letters were surfacing — not his mistress's hand, not the old order's, something hungrier and closer, rising through the leather the way heat rises through a floor:

*IT KNOWS YOU ARE READING.*

Far off down the pass, back the way they'd come, a light bloomed against the clouds — orange, deliberate, moving. Torches. Many torches, marching in formation through a blizzard no mortal army would march in.

Mara closed the book and reached for his boots.
