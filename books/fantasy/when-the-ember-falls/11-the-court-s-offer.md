## 11. The Court's Offer

The trap closed on them at a place called Merrow's Rest, which was not a rest at all but a ruined waystation where the road narrowed between two shoulders of rock. Mara had known it was coming for three days. You learned to smell an ambush the way you smelled rain — a stillness in the air, birds going quiet, the particular silence of men trying very hard not to cough.

He'd tried to warn Vane. She'd looked at him with that flat inquisitor's stare and said, "If they wanted us dead, we'd already be dead," which was either reassuring or the most terrifying thing anyone had ever said to him, and Mara had spent the last two days deciding which.

Pell, for his part, had spent those same two days teaching stones to hum. The child sat by their fire each evening with a lap full of river pebbles, arranging and rearranging them like a merchant pricing goods, humming little rising notes until one of the stones would glow faintly green and vibrate against its neighbors with a sound like a contented cat. It should have been beautiful. Mostly it was beautiful. But sometimes, when the humming hit a certain pitch, Mara felt something lean toward them from very far away — a vast attention turning, slow as a glacier, curious and hungry.

"Maybe don't do that here," Mara had said.

Pell had looked at him with enormous, offended dignity, and put the stone away, and then taken it out again twenty minutes later when he thought Mara wasn't watching. Children were all smugglers at heart. Mara respected it enormously.

So when the horns sounded at dawn — three low notes, courtly almost, nothing like a war cry — Mara was already awake, already dressed, already calculating the three ways out of the hollow and finding all three of them watched.

* * *

They came down the road in grey and ember-orange, perhaps forty soldiers, and behind them, unhurried, a palanquin of black lacquer and gold carried by six men who walked as though the weight were a privilege. The Hierophant himself stepped out of it without assistance, which surprised Mara. He'd imagined someone vast, gilded, half-swallowed by his own vestments. Instead he got a lean man of middle years with a scholar's stoop and a burn scar crawling up the left side of his face like ivy made of candlewax. His eyes were the problem. They were warm. Warm eyes on a man like this were worse than cold ones. Cold eyes wanted something from you; warm eyes had already decided you'd give it.

"Vane." The Hierophant said her name gently, the way you'd speak to a dog that had once been a good dog. "And the guide. And the child."

Nobody answered. Pell had gone very still against Vane's side, one hand fisted in her coat, and Vane's hand rested on the boy's shoulder with a weight that Mara recognized: she was holding him in place the way you held a door shut.

"You know," the Hierophant said, strolling forward a few steps, boots crunching frost, "I've read your trial transcripts, Sera. All four hundred pages. You asked excellent questions. That was always your crime, you understand — not doubt. Doubt is useful. Questions are useful. It was asking them *out loud*, in front of people who might have started wondering too." He smiled, and the burn scar pulled. "The Court forgives so much. Curiosity isn't on the list."

"I remember my questions," Vane said. Her voice was level, but Mara, standing close enough, saw her thumb press white against her sword belt. "Do you remember your answers?"

"Oh, vividly." He turned then to Mara, and everything about him rearranged itself, the way a shopkeeper turns friendly when the right customer walks in. "Mara Joubert. Salt-runner. Grimoire-thief. The only man in Thornfell who knows every goat path, deadfall, and smuggler's stair between here and the Door." He spread his hands. "You see our difficulty. We have an army. Armies are wonderful for roads. You have a map that lives inside your skull, and skulls are so fragile."

"He doesn't know where the Door is," Vane said flatly.

"The grimoire does," the Hierophant said pleasantly, "and the grimoire travels with him. We burned a village two nights ago looking for it. Well — *part* of a village. We're not monsters." He paused. "Actually, I suppose we are. The distinction stopped mattering some time ago."

Mara said nothing. This was a skill. Twenty years of surviving had taught him that the most dangerous thing you could say was anything at all, and the second most dangerous thing was saying nothing too *well* — so he arranged his face into the expression of a man doing arithmetic, which came naturally, because he was.

The Hierophant watched him do it and clearly enjoyed it.

"There it is," he murmured. "That look. I've seen it on merchants, on priests, on kings. The look of a man counting exits." He clasped his hands behind his back. "Let me save you the arithmetic, Mr. Joubert. Here is what happens if you refuse. My soldiers take Pell — the Court has wanted that child since the ash choir incident, and yes, we know about the ash choir, we know about everything, grief is a terrible keeper of secrets — and they will take him *gently*, because damaged goods sing poorly. Then they take Vane, who will burn, publicly, beautifully, as a warning to every other inquisitor who ever loved a question more than her career. And you—" he tilted his head, considering, "—you we'll follow. Slowly. A man can't run forever on a map he refuses to read."

"And if I say yes?" Mara asked. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the whole trick of his life.

The Hierophant's smile widened by exactly one tooth.

* * *

They sat down to negotiate, which was the strangest part. Not a battle. A *negotiation*. The Hierophant waved his soldiers back up the road — far enough to be polite, close enough to be a message — and produced from his sleeve a folding table, two stools, and a flask of wine, and Mara thought, absurdly, *this man has done this before, many times, and gotten good at it,* and hated how much he recognized the craft. This was a smuggler. This was the best smuggler Mara had ever met, except what he ran was souls.

"Before we begin," the Hierophant said, pouring, "understand that I am not lying to you. Lying is for people with weak positions. Everything I offer is real. Whether you believe me is your own affair."

He laid out the terms like cards.

Wealth: a house in the new capital, whatever the new capital turned out to be, with land attached and salt rights — *salt rights*, the words landed in Mara's chest like a hook, because every runner dreamed of owning rather than carrying, of being the mountain instead of the mule on it.

Pardon: full and written, for the theft, for the flight, for every road he'd ever walked without asking permission. "You'd be astonished," the Hierophant said, "how much of the old kingdom's law still functions, provided you hold the pen."

And then the third thing, and Mara understood, watching the man's warm eyes, that the first two had been scaffolding for this one.

"A name," the Hierophant said softly. "Not the name you were born with and abandoned. A *new* name. Court name. Entered in the rolls. A man with a name cannot be erased, Mr. Joubert. Cannot be hunted, cannot be disappeared, cannot wake one morning to find that the country he stood in has been dissolved overnight." He leaned back. "You've spent your whole life being no one. Being no one is freedom, I grant you — I was no one myself, once, before the fire took my face and gave me this job. But freedom-with-nothing is just poverty wearing a costume. I'm offering you freedom *with*."

Mara sat there with the wine untouched in front of him and felt the offer go through him like cold water through cloth, touching everything.

Because here was the honest thing, the thing he would never say aloud to Vane, the thing that sat in him like a swallowed stone: he *wanted* it. Not the wealth, particularly. Not even the pardon. But the name. Gods, the name. He had been Mara Joubert, apprentice, thief, runaway, guide-for-hire, *the man who is never needed* — he had built his entire life on the principle that the moment someone needed you, they owned you, and the moment they owned you, they sold you, and he had watched it happen to better people than himself a dozen times. A name entered in rolls was a chain. He knew that. He knew it the way he knew the mountain paths, bone-deep.

And he wanted it anyway. Wanted to walk into a town and have someone say *there goes Master Joubert* instead of *who?* Wanted a door that locked from the inside and belonged to him. Wanted, with a hunger that shamed him, to stop running.

Survival had always been his religion, and here was its high priest offering him heaven.

"That's a long silence," the Hierophant observed kindly.

"It's arithmetic," Mara said.

"Of course it is." The Hierophant sipped his wine. "Take your time. I'll confess I hope you say yes. Not for the Door — well. Partly for the Door. But mostly because I enjoy talking to people who understand the world as it actually is. Vane still believes in things. It's exhausting. You and I, we believe in *terms*."

Across the hollow, Vane stood with Pell, and she was watching Mara, and her face gave away nothing, absolutely nothing, which told Mara everything: she knew. She knew exactly how good the offer was. She was a woman who had traded her career for a question; she knew what it cost to refuse, and she was standing there letting him count the price without flinching, without begging, without adding a single guilt to the scale. It was the most decent thing anyone had ever done for him, and it consisted entirely of *not speaking*.

Pell, meanwhile, had picked up a pebble and was humming to it, very quietly, under his breath. The stone glowed a faint traitorous green. And far away — Mara felt it again, that vast glacial attention leaning closer, listening through the child the way you listened through a wall.

The Hierophant felt it too. Mara saw it flicker across the burn-scarred face — hunger, quickly mastered, quickly hidden. Almost hidden.

"You should know," Mara said, and heard his own voice as if from outside, "what happens to the gods' power. Where it goes. What the Court does with what it harvests."

"I know precisely what the Court does," the Hierophant said. "I designed the system. Would you like to hear the justification? It's quite elegant. Everyone burning is guilty of something. Every prayer answered is a debt collected. The gods were sealed away out of fear; we let them eat, and they let us rule, and the country survives, which is more than it was doing under the crown." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "It's not damnation, Mr. Joubert. It's *husbandry*."

"Husbandry," Mara repeated.

"Farming," the Hierophant agreed, "with extra steps."

And that was the moment, though Mara didn't fully know it yet — that was the moment the offer curdled. Because the Hierophant said it plainly, without shame, and expected Mara to nod along the way sensible people nodded along, and Mara looked at Pell — small, silent, humming to a stone in his cupped hands like it was a bird he was keeping warm — and understood that in the Hierophant's elegant arithmetic, the child was a *crop*. A field to be rotated. Something you planted and harvested and planted again.

Mara had spent his whole life refusing to be needed. He had never once, in all his careful years, refused to *see*. And he was seeing now.

"I need the night," Mara said. "To consult the grimoire."

The Hierophant's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "A wise man reads before signing. Of course. We'll withdraw to the ridge. You'll have until dawn." He rose, folded his little table with a snap, and paused. "One courtesy, since you've been honest with me. If you run, we will catch you — the pass is closed, the snows are early, and I have forty men and you have a woman, a child, and a book. But if you run, I won't burn the woman or take the child *first*. I'll simply follow. Whatever you decide, decide it knowing I keep my terms. It's the only religion I have left."

He walked back up the road, and his soldiers flowed after him like water finding its level, and the hollow was quiet again except for the wind and the small green glow dying in Pell's palm.

* * *

That night they didn't light a fire. Vane sat with her back to a boulder, sword across her knees, watching the ridge where the enemy campfires made a crooked constellation. Pell slept curled against her side, one hand still loosely closed around his favorite stone.

Mara sat apart with the grimoire open on his thighs, not reading it. Just resting his hands on it. It was warm, the way it had been warm since the frostbound cave, warm as a sleeping animal, and lately he had begun to suspect it was warm the way a *trap* is warm — baited, patient, waiting for the right hand.

"So," Vane said quietly, not looking at him. "Wealth. Pardon. A name."

"You were listening."

"I was standing thirty feet away. He projects like a cathedral." She turned her head. "I'm not going to tell you what to do, Joubert. I've seen what happens to people who get told. I'm only going to ask you one question, and then I'll shut up."

He waited.

"If you say yes," she said, "and you lead them to the Door — do you actually believe they'll let any of us walk away after?"

Mara looked at the dark shape of the child asleep against her ribs, and at the ridge, and at the grimoire warming his hands like a heart that wasn't his.

"No," he said. "But I believed it for about ten minutes this afternoon. While he was talking about the name." He laughed, short and joyless. "Ten minutes. That's how long it takes to sell me. You should write it down. Useful intelligence."

Vane almost smiled. It was a narrow, cold, exhausted almost-smile, but it was real. "For what it's worth," she said, "I've spent my whole life believing in law, and the law turned out to be a farm. You've spent your whole life believing in nothing, and today you nearly bought a name from the man who runs the farm." She looked back at the ridge. "Maybe we're both bad at this. Maybe that's why the child keeps us."

Above them, on the ridge, a single ember drifted loose from the enemy fires and rode the wind down the dark face of the mountain — down, and down, glowing, falling past their hollow, past the frozen road, into the valley below where, Mara remembered with a chill he couldn't explain, a hundred pilgrims had gathered at the shrine of Vess to pray for the winter's end.

The ember fell. Somewhere below, faint as memory, something answered.

And in the morning, when the Hierophant sent a herald down with ink and parchment and a smile, Mara Joubert took the pen — and wrote something on the parchment that made the herald's face go grey, and made forty soldiers reach for their weapons, and made the mountain itself, very far beneath their feet, begin to rumble like a throat clearing.
