## 12. Crossing the Burnt Vale

Sera Vane had seen executions before. She had performed a dozen of them herself, back when her hands still belonged to the Court, and she had thought she understood what fire did to a human body. She had been wrong. The Court's pyres were tidy things — oiled wood, iron chains, a hymn sung in unison so the smoke rose straight and the ash could be swept into consecrated ground by noon. What lay before her now was not tidy. It was not even fire anymore. It was the *aftermath* of fire, the way a flood leaves mud, and it stretched across the whole of the valley below them like something spilled from the sky.

Mara stood at the lip of the pass with his pack straps gripped in both fists, and for once he had nothing sardonic to say.

"Dear gods," Vane said, and then heard herself, and winced. Old habits. "Forgive me. That was — not the appropriate invocation under the circumstances."

Pell, riding on Mara's shoulders with the solemnity of a small admiral surveying a fleet, patted Mara's head twice. It was the child's way of saying *I agree, this is bad.*

"We can go around," Mara said at last. His voice was careful, flattened, the voice he used when he was doing arithmetic he didn't want anyone else to see. "There's a ridge path north of here. Adds two days. Two days is food we don't have, and the Court's outriders are sweeping the high passes since the Gate garrison fell."

"And through?"

"Through is half a day." He paused. "Through is also *that*."

Vane looked down again. The Burnt Vale had been green when she was a girl — she remembered it from a childhood journey she barely trusted herself to remember anymore, because so much of what she'd believed in that girlhood had turned out to be painted scenery. There had been a shrine at the valley's heart then, a modest one, a stone well-house where travelers left copper coins and asked for safe roads. Now the valley was a bowl of black glass. Not ash — glass. Whatever had happened here last night had happened hot enough and fast enough to fuse the topsoil, and the pilgrims who had been caught in it were preserved in attitudes of prayer, kneeling, arms raised, faces lifted toward a sky that had answered them all at once and killed them in the answering.

They were still warm. Mara had checked. He hadn't wanted to; he'd done it anyway, crouching beside the first body they found inside the vale's edge, laying two fingers against the man's charred neck the way you'd check a fevered child. Warm. Not cooling-warm. *Warm*-warm, as if the bodies were still holding the heat of their own combustion the way a hearthstone holds the evening's fire long after the logs have gone to white.

"They prayed too loudly," Pell signed, or rather drew — the child communicated in quick charcoal gestures on a slate when words failed, and words failed often enough that Vane was learning to read the slate the way she'd once read writs. *Too loudly.* Then, beneath it, a smaller drawing: a hand reaching down out of a cloud, and beneath the hand, tiny figures, and beneath the figures, a line scratched deep and dark.

"Yes," Vane said quietly. "That's exactly what happened."

* * *

She had spent eleven years as an inquisitor of the Ember Court, and in those eleven years she had never once doubted the doctrine of the Balanced Ledger. It was elegant. It was comforting. Every miracle, the doctrine held, was paid for — every flame called down upon the faithful was drawn from somewhere else, some other valley, some other village, some other family's granary or lungs or luck. The gods were not generous. They were bookkeepers. And the Court, by rationing prayers, by deciding whose petitions went up the mountain flues and whose were quietly refused, kept the accounts balanced. It was theft, but it was *managed* theft, and managed theft was simply what civilization was. She had believed this the way she believed in the weight of iron: completely, without ever thinking to test it.

Then she had doubted one miracle. One. A pillar of flame in a market square that had consumed a baker's stall and left the baker himself untouched, which the Court declared a sign of divine precision and which Vane, standing in the crowd with scorch-marks on her boots, had privately suspected was a sign of nothing more than a bad wind. For that doubt she had been stripped of office, name, and standing, and set loose into a country that no longer existed to find a smuggler who might lead her to a Door.

And now she stood above a valley where the Ledger had been balanced in a single night, and she could see, finally, with terrible clarity, what the balancing actually looked like.

The pilgrims had come to the vale because word had spread — word always spread, faster than any rider, faster than sense — that the old god of the vale, the one the Court had starved for three generations, had woken hungry. And where there was a waking god, there were answered prayers, and where there were answered prayers, people came. They came in families. They came carrying their sick on litters. They came with the particular desperate hope of people who had exhausted every ordinary remedy and had nothing left but the extraordinary. They knelt at the ruined well-shrine and they prayed aloud, because everyone knew now that the gods only heard what they could *hear*, and volume was the cheapest form of devotion available to the poor.

And the god had heard. All of it. At once. Every petition stacked upon every other petition, a thousand voices asking for rain, for healing, for vengeance, for bread, for the return of sons taken by the Court's press-gangs — and the god, being neither cruel nor kind but merely vast and newly awake and *hungry*, had reached down to gather its payment, and the payment was everything within reach, and everything within reach was the vale itself.

"They weren't sinners," Vane said aloud, to no one, to the air, because she needed to say it to someone and Pell couldn't hear and Mara was pretending not to listen. "They weren't heretics. They were *faithful*. They did everything right. They came to the right place and said the right words with the right hearts, and the answer killed them." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that after everything — the defrocking, the exile, the rope around her own wrists in a smuggler's camp — it was a valley full of dead strangers that finally made her voice crack.

Mara glanced at her sidelong. "You're allowed," he said. "To be upset. I know you've been rationing it. You ration everything. It's very inquisitorial."

"I am not upset. I am *recalibrating*."

"You're upset."

"I am performing a professional reassessment of foundational assumptions, which superficially resembles—"

"Upset," Mara said, with the gentle certainty of a man diagnosing a limp, and Pell, from his perch, drew on the slate a small stick figure with water coming out of its eyes, and held it up, and Vane decided that if the two of them conspired against her dignity any further she would leave them both on the mountain, and also that she would do no such thing, and she was beginning to suspect that this was what having companions felt like, and she resented it enormously.

* * *

They went through. Of course they went through. Mara led, because Mara always led on the paths, and he picked his way down into the black glass with the unhurried precision of a man crossing a frozen river — testing each step, distributing his weight, never hurrying, because hurrying was how you died in places like this. Vane followed in his footprints exactly, the way she'd learned to during the pass crossings, and Pell rode high on Mara's back with the slate tucked under one arm like a soldier's kit.

The heat rose through their boot soles. That was the worst of it, Vane decided, worse than the bodies, worse than the smell — and there was a smell, sweet and wrong, like meat left near a candle, faint but everywhere, inescapable. The ground itself was warm. The whole valley floor was warm, a kiln cooling over days, and they were walking across its mouth.

"Keep your prayers silent," Mara said, and it took Vane a moment to realize he wasn't joking. "Whatever you're thinking up there — whatever you want, whatever you're afraid of — keep it behind your teeth. Sound carries differently here. I don't know why. Everything about this place is wrong in ways I don't have vocabulary for." He paused atop a low rise of fused earth. "But I've noticed, since the gods woke, that loud places stay loud. Grief echoes. If you start grieving out loud in this valley, I genuinely don't know what will hear you."

Vane closed her mouth on the prayer she hadn't known she was forming. It had been a simple one, childish almost — *let us cross safely* — and she swallowed it whole, and felt slightly ill at how easily she'd begun to believe in the listening again. Doubt, it turned out, was not a thing you achieved once. It was a discipline, like prayer, and hers needed daily practice, and today the practice was silence in a graveyard.

The bodies thickened as they descended toward the vale's heart. At first they passed them singly, a kneeling figure here, a sprawled one there, each preserved mid-motion like insects in amber. Then in clusters. Then in rings — concentric rings around the shattered well-shrine, hundreds of people arranged by distance from the object of their hope, the closest kneeling, the next ring standing with arms raised, the outermost simply fallen where they'd walked, face-down, mid-stride, their last step frozen in the glass. It looked less like a massacre than like a diagram. Like someone had dropped a stone into a pond of people and the ripples of devotion had been answered outward, and the answering had eaten the pond.

Near the third ring, Vane stopped. There was a woman there, kneeling, holding a litter, and on the litter was a child — a real child, small, maybe six years old, curled on its side as if asleep. The woman's arms were wrapped around the litter's frame. Her face was turned away from the shrine, toward the child, and Vane understood, with a lurch, that the woman hadn't been praying for herself at all. She'd been praying with her body between the god and her daughter, and the god had collected them together, because gods do not respect shields, only debts.

Pell saw it too. The child on Mara's back went very still, and then began, very softly, to hum.

"No," Vane said immediately. "No humming. Not here."

Pell's humming was the thing that made stones glow. Vane had watched it happen — watched a roadside menhir light up like a lamp with veins of gold when the child leaned against it and hummed the tuneless little melody that seemed to live in Pell permanently, the way a heartbeat lives in a chest. In the refugee column it had been a wonder. Here, in a valley that was one vast open wound in the world, freshly made, still warm, still *open* — here, the humming felt like striking a match in a powder store.

Pell stopped. Looked at her. And then, deliberately, slowly, laid one small hand flat against Mara's shoulder, over the knob of spine, and hummed anyway — but quieter, a thread of sound, almost nothing, a lullaby pitched below hearing.

And Mara's shoulder-blade glowed. Faintly. A coin-sized patch of warmth-light under his shirt, gold-green, pulsing gently, like a lantern behind cloth.

"What—" Vane started.

"It does that," Mara said, and his voice had gone strange, tight. "It's been doing it more. Every day, a little more. Since the vale, since—" He stopped walking. Stood very still in the middle of the burned glass, surrounded by the kneeling dead, and let Pell's hand rest on him, and the little light pulsed, and Vane watched him watch it, and something in his face frightened her more than the corpses did. Because it wasn't fear. It was recognition. It was the look of a man seeing a door he'd forgotten he owned.

"Pell says the dead aren't angry," Mara said quietly, translating from the slate, which Pell had produced and was writing on with urgent little strokes while balanced on his back. "Pell says they're *listening*. Pell says they're waiting for someone to finish the prayer they started. Pell says—" he squinted at the slate, tilted it, squinted again— "Pell says the prayer isn't finished. The god took the payment but nobody told the pray-ers. They're still mid-word. All of them. The whole valley is one sentence that got cut off."

"That's not possible," Vane said.

"I agree," said Mara. "And yet."

* * *

They crossed the vale's heart in the early afternoon, when the light came straight down and the shadows of the dead were shortest, and Vane made herself do the thing she had trained her whole life to do and had never once had to do at this scale: she counted. It was an inquisitor's reflex — tally the dead, record the names where names could be found, because the Court's whole machinery ran on records, and even stripped of office she could not walk past the dead without wanting their number to exist somewhere outside her own skull. She counted in rings. Four hundred and eleven in the inner circles where the count was clean. Beyond that the glass blurred and the bodies merged and the counting became estimate, and the estimate became guesswork, and somewhere past nine hundred she stopped, because the numbers had stopped being information and become a kind of injury.

Nine hundred. Nine hundred faithful people, killed by the exact faithfulness they'd come to offer. And the Court's heralds, she knew — she had *been* the Court's voice once, she knew exactly how the proclamation would be phrased — the Court's heralds would ride to the villages around this vale within the week and declare it a miracle. A sign of the gods' returning vigor. A call to greater devotion, greater tithing, greater care in the wording of one's prayers. And the villages would weep and tremble and send *more* pilgrims, because terror dressed as holiness was the most reliable crop the Court had ever planted, and Vane had helped plant it for eleven years, and there was no arithmetic that could make that weight bearable, so she didn't try. She just carried it. One boot-length at a time, across the warm glass, past the kneeling dead.

Mara, for his part, coped the way he coped with everything: sideways, with jokes pitched so dry you could strike sparks off them. He pointed out, as they picked through the outer field, that the vale's famous wildflowers would presumably be spectacular in spring, assuming anything could grow in glass, and that he'd always wanted to see a flower that had to be chiseled open. He observed that the fused earth made the finest road surface in Thornfell, being perfectly smooth, and that it was a pity about the toll. He noted that at least the vale was honest — no ambushers, no Court patrols, no tax collectors; nothing living wanted to be here, which made it, statistically, the safest stretch of road in the entire country. Vane found, to her surprise, that the jokes helped. Not because they were funny — mostly they weren't, quite — but because they were *his*, offered steadily, hour after hour, a handrail of irreverence bolted along the edge of an abyss, and she gripped it, and kept walking.

Pell coped by touching things. The child would not stop touching things — the glass, the stones, occasionally the hem of a dead pilgrim's robe, always briefly, always with a little pat, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal to check it was breathing. And every touched thing glowed, faintly, briefly, a breath of gold-green light rising out of the black like a bubble through tar, and then fading. Vane watched it happen perhaps forty times across the valley floor, and by the thirtieth time she had stopped flinching, and by the fortieth she had begun to understand what she was looking at.

The light wasn't Pell's. Pell was just the striker. The light was the vale's — the god's payment, the gathered heat, the devoured devotion, sitting in the ground like coin in a strongbox. And Pell's touch drew a little of it up, the way a fingertip draws flame from a candle, and released it, harmlessly, beautifully, back into the air where it could do nothing to anyone. The child was *spending* the dead men's money. Penny by penny. Lighting the dark with it.

"Their prayers are finishing," Pell wrote on the slate, unprompted, near the vale's far edge, and showed it first to Vane and then, turning, to the valley behind them — to the nine hundred, to the rings, to the cut-off sentence hanging over the black glass. The child's face was grave and calm and entirely without self-importance, the face of someone reporting weather. *Finishing,* the slate said again. And beneath it, small: *That's why I hum. Somebody has to.*

Vane looked at the slate for a long moment. Then she looked at the child. Then she did the thing she had sworn off doing since the day the Court took her name: she reached out and rested her hand, briefly, on Pell's head, the way you'd bless someone, except she didn't say any blessing, because blessings were prayers and prayers were currency and she was done paying into that account. She just let the weight of her hand mean what it meant.

* * *

They climbed out of the vale as the sun went down behind the western peaks, and the temperature dropped with the light, and the warmth of the ground faded beneath their feet until, at the northern rim, the earth was merely cold and merely earth and Vane nearly wept with relief at the plainness of it. Ordinary dirt. Ordinary cold. A world that asked nothing of her except footing.

They made a cold camp — no fire, by Mara's flat insistence; fire was language now, and they were not going to speak it within sight of the vale — and shared dried mutton and hard bread, and Pell slept between them with the slate hugged to the child's chest like a talisman, and Vane sat up a while longer than she needed to, watching the dark line of the valley below.

Somewhere in that dark, nine hundred unfinished sentences hung in the air. Somewhere in that dark, a god slept on its hoard, fed and dreaming, and would wake hungry again, because hunger was all it had and all it was. And somewhere beyond the dark, further north, further up, past ridges she had crossed as a young officer chasing heretics she now understood had been innocent — past all of it — there was a hidden hut, and a woman in it who did not age, and a boy in her company who had grown up as preparation for something he'd never agreed to.

"Mara," she said, into the dark.

He was cleaning his knife by feel, methodical, each stroke the same length. "Mm."

"The grimoire. Your mistress. The sealed pages opened in the cave — you told me what they said about the Door. But you didn't tell me everything the pages said about *her*."

"No," he agreed. "I didn't."

"Why not?"

A long pause. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then, quietly, in the voice he used for true things, which was rarer than his jokes and always cost him something:

"Because tomorrow we'll be close enough that you'll find out yourself. And because—" the knife stilled— "because I've been telling myself for twenty years that she stole my life for a reason. That there was a reason worth what it cost. And every mile north, I believe it less." He sheathed the knife. "Sleep, Inquisitor. Tomorrow we knock on a witch's door, and I promise you nothing about what answers."

Below them, far off in the black vale, very faintly, something hummed back.
