## 13. Old Debts, Old Names

The hut was exactly where Mara had left it, which was the first wrong thing.

Hidden places were supposed to drift. That was the whole point of hiding something — you buried it under years, under changed roads and fallen trees and the slow erosion of your own memory, so that even you couldn't quite find it again without paying for the finding. But the witch's hut sat in its fold of the mountain as if no time had passed at all, as if the twenty years since Mara had run from it had been a door left open only a crack, through which nothing had entered and nothing had aged.

The chimney smoked.

"She's home," Pell said. The child said it the way Pell said most things now — a word at a time, rationed like salt, each one landing with more weight than it should have carried. Pell had spoken perhaps forty words since the ash choir, and every one of them had been true, which was more than could be said for anyone else in Thornfell.

Vane looked at the smoke, then at Mara. "You said she'd be dead."

"I said I *hoped* she'd be dead." He heard how it sounded and didn't take it back. "There's a difference. There's usually a difference between what I say and what I hope. It's how I've stayed alive."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *appreciating the view*," Mara said, gesturing at the grey scree, the wind-bent pines, the hut crouched under the cliff like a stone that had decided to grow a roof. "It's a lovely view. It may be the last view I appreciate before someone turns me into a newt or worse."

"Newts aren't worse than what's behind that door," Pell said solemnly, "probably."

"Thank you, Pell. Your comfort is a balm."

But he went down the slope anyway, because there was nowhere else to go, and because — this was the part he didn't say, the part that had been sitting in his chest since the frostbound cave like a swallowed coal — because some traitorous corner of him had missed her every single day of those twenty years, and hated himself for it, and missed her anyway.

The door was oak, iron-banded, carved with the same knotwork it had always borne: a circle within a circle within a circle, worn nearly smooth by weather and by hands. Mara raised his fist to knock and found his hand shaking, which was absurd, because he had faced smuggler captains and Ember priests and a god wearing a traveler's face, and none of them had made his knuckles tremble against wood.

He knocked.

"It's open, boy," said a voice from inside, dry as old paper and just as flammable. "It's been open for an hour. You've been standing on my hill arguing with an inquisitor about whether I'm dead. You always did your grieving out loud where the whole mountain could hear it."

Mara pushed the door open.

* * *

The inside of the hut smelled the same — dried yarrow and tallow and something mineral underneath, like the inside of the mountain itself had been invited in and never left. Herbs hung from the rafters in bundles gone grey with age. The kettle hung over the fire. Two chairs sat by the hearth, and on the table between them lay a third cup, already poured, steam still rising from it.

Three cups. She had known they were three.

And there she was, in the chair by the fire, hands folded in her lap, looking up at him with eyes the color of river ice.

She had not aged.

That was the thing that stopped him in the doorway with the cold wind at his back and the warmth of the room reaching out to him like a lie. Twenty years. He had spent twenty years imagining her old — older, anyway; she had been old when he was nine, ancient by the time he ran at nineteen, and he had built her death into his plans the way other men built in a saint's blessing. And here she sat, not a line deeper, not a hair whiter, her spine straight as a young pine, wearing the same dark green dress with the mended elbow he remembered stitching himself, badly, at fourteen.

"Mistress Ilse," he said. His voice came out strange. Smaller than he wanted.

"Apprentice," she said. Then, tilting her head, considering him the way she used to consider a doubtful herb — is this worth the garden bed? — she added, "No. Not anymore. You unlearned that title the night you took my book. Sit down, Mara. All of you. The tea's for the child, mostly. Growing bodies need it, and hers has been doing rather more growing than most lately."

Pell slipped past him into the warmth like water finding a low place, took the cup, drank, and sighed — a small, whole-bodied sound of trust that made something in Mara's chest twist sideways. Children trusted too easily. Or maybe children trusted *accurately*, and it was adults who had lost the knack of reading people true.

Vane did not sit. Vane stood with her hand nowhere near her sword, which for Sera Vane was a performance of peace, and said, "Madam. You are Ilse of the High Shrine of Vess?"

"I have been called that." The witch's gaze moved to Vane and sharpened. "And you are the inquisitor who wouldn't burn the child. They sing about you in the clan holds, you know. Badly. Mountain people cannot carry a tune to save their souls, which is fitting, since it's their souls needing saving." She waved at the second chair. "Sit, Inquisitor. I don't bite. I gave that up with the rest of it."

"The rest of what?" Mara asked.

Ilse looked at him then, fully, and he saw — finally, now that he was close enough — what was wrong beneath the unaged face. Her eyes were old. Not old the way a grandmother's are old, soft and folded. Old the way a well is old. Deep, and dark, and with something at the bottom that you did not want to look at directly.

"The rest of everything, boy. Sit down. You'll want to be sitting."

He sat. He was proud of how steadily he did it.

* * *

"You stole my book," Mara said, when the silence had stretched long enough. "Twenty years ago. I stole yours — I know, I know, let me finish. You taught me letters so I could read it. You taught me the passes so I could move unseen. You taught me which plants feed and which kill and how to read weather and how to lie to soldiers, and I thought — all those years, I thought you were being kind. A hedge-witch taking in an orphan because hedge-witches do that. Because somebody did it for you once." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated her for it, hated himself more. "You were training me. Like a hawk. Like a hound. Every kindness was a leash."

"Yes," Ilse said.

Not *no*. Not *you misunderstand*. Not any of the hundred soft words she might have used, the ones he had rehearsed her using on the road north, the ones he could have fought. Just *yes*, plain as bread, and it went through him worse than any denial.

"Why?" Vane asked quietly, when he didn't.

"Because I was told to." Ilse rose, joints silent — silent, another wrongness, old women's knees announce them — and went to the shelf above the hearth, where a row of jars stood in the firelight. She took one down: glass gone cloudy, and inside it, faintly, a green glow, like a firefly drowned in honey. "Because the ones who sealed the Door needed a key, and keys must be grown, not made. Made things can be copied. Grown things can only be betrayed." She set the jar on the table. The light in it pulsed, very slowly, like breathing. "I was told to find an orphan of the pass roads. Born under the mountain's shadow. With the right blood and the right stubbornness and no family to grieve him if it went wrong."

"No family," Mara repeated. "My parents died in the rockfall at Grey Ford. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said it was the mountain shifting. It was. The mountain shifted because the gods below it turned over in their sleep, and the Door felt it, and the Door reached up and touched the nearest living thing with the right shape to its soul." She said it the way she had once told him the names of poisonous berries: levelly, completely, leaving him to do his own screaming. "You were four years old, Mara. You walked out of Grey Ford alone, downhill, through ash, without a scratch, humming a song no one had ever taught you. I was sent to collect you. I told myself I would raise you gently. I mostly managed it."

"*Mostly.*"

"I never lied to you about anything that mattered," she said, with a flash of the old temper, sudden as a struck spark. "I lied about why you were learning things. Everything else was true. The herbs were real. The passes were real. The knife-work was real — it's kept you alive through a country full of fire, hasn't it? I gave you every tool I had. I simply didn't tell you what the tools were *for*."

"And what were they for?" Pell asked softly, from the corner, cup cradled in both hands.

Ilse looked at the child for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her shoulders drop, just slightly, the way a wall drops when the siege ends.

"For the Door," she said. "The Door at Thornfell's heart opens for one person and one person only. Not the purest. Not the strongest. The one it chose, the day it chose, forever after. It chose a four-year-old boy walking out of a burning valley." She turned to Mara. "It chose you. You are the key, child. You have been the key since before you could talk. Your whole life — the apprenticeship, the running, the surviving, every road you ever walked — has been the Door keeping its key safe until the day it was needed."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound almost like voices.

Mara found that he was laughing. It came out of him low and ugly, and he couldn't stop it, and he didn't much try.

"My whole life," he said. "Every choice I thought I made. Running from you — that was the plan. Stealing the book — that was the plan. Becoming a smuggler, learning every goat-track in these mountains, never staying anywhere, never letting anyone need me—" his voice climbed, cracked, climbed again— "because a *door* picked me when I was *four*."

"Yes."

"I could have died a hundred times! I nearly did die, at the shrine, at the vale, at the Gate of Teeth—"

"But you didn't." Ilse's voice gentled, and somehow that was the worst cruelty yet. "Keys don't break easily, Mara. Not the important ones. You were never in as much danger as you believed. That was the one mercy I insisted on."

"The one mercy." He stood. His legs held, barely. "Do you know what it cost? Do you know what I *am*? I'm a man who sold his best friend's route to save his own skin and then killed him for it. I'm a man who watched a shepherd burn alive in green fire and ran. I'm a man who has spent twenty years making sure nobody loved him because love is a debt and debts get collected—" He stopped. Breathed. "Was that the plan too? Was my loneliness *load-bearing*?"

For the first time, the witch looked away from him.

"There," Vane said quietly, into the silence. "There's your answer, Mara."

"I was young," Ilse said. "Younger than I look now, though I grant that's hard to credit. The sealing order came down from women who had watched the old gods eat three generations, and they were not gentle, and neither was I. We did what worked." She lifted her chin, and there — there at last — was the woman he had run from, the iron under the kindness. "And I will not apologize for keeping the world's worst door shut for twenty years with whatever materials came to hand. One of those materials was you. I am sorry it was you. I am not sorry it was done."

"That's not an apology," Mara said. "That's arithmetic."

"I'm an old woman who does sums," Ilse said. "It's what kept the gods asleep."

"They're not asleep now," Pell said.

All three of them turned. The child had set down the cup and was looking at the jar on the table, at the green light pulsing inside it, and Pell's head was tilted in that listening way, the way that made standing stones glow.

"It's listening to us," Pell said. "Through the jar. Through her." Pell pointed one small finger at Ilse, not unkindly, the way you'd point out a bird on a branch. "She's full of it. That's why she doesn't get old. It's feeding her so she can keep feeding it. She's a *cup* it drinks from." Pell frowned, concentrating. "Except the cup is almost empty now. It's getting hungry again. It's starting to wonder why the tea stopped coming."

Ilse's hand closed around the jar, knuckles white.

"How long," she said, and her voice had changed — thinner, older, suddenly every one of its real years arriving at once, "have you been able to see that?"

"Since the choir," Pell said. "Since the Door said my name through the stones."

Mara looked at his mistress — at the unaged face and the bottomless eyes and the hand shaking around a jar of borrowed life — and understood, all in one cold flood, why she had waited on this hill for twenty years without aging, without dying, without ever once coming down to find him.

She hadn't been waiting for him.

She'd been *kept*. A warden in a cell of her own making, sustained by the thing she guarded, unable to leave, unable to age, unable to do anything but tend the lock until the key came home.

"You poor, arrogant old woman," he said softly. "You think you're the jailer. You're the last bar on the cage, and the cage is rusting through."

Ilse smiled at him then, and it was the saddest thing he had ever seen, sadder than the burnt vale, sadder than the shepherd's prayer.

"Now you understand why I needed you angry," she said. "Angry keys turn. Grieving ones just sit in the lock and weep." She set the jar down and straightened, and outside, far down the mountain, a horn blew — twice, then twice again, the Court's marching call, closer than it had any right to be. "They've found us. Of course they've found us; the Hierophant has been following the smell of my emptiness for a week. So listen fast, all of you. The Door will ask you for the thing you most want to keep, and you must decide *now*, before you stand in front of it, what your answer is — because it doesn't ask twice, and it doesn't accept lies."

"And if I refuse?" Mara asked. "If I turn around and walk south and let the whole hungry country burn?"

Ilse looked at him with those well-deep eyes, and for a moment — just a moment — he saw her as she truly was: a girl once, long ago, standing where he stood, handed a task she never chose, doing her sums while the world got worse.

"Then you'll be free," she said. "Truly free, for the first time in your life. No destiny, no duty, no debts. Just you, and the road, and whatever's left of the world after the ember falls." She glanced toward the window, toward the sound of the horns. "Choose quickly, boy. The Court is coming up my hill, and this time, they're bringing enough fire that not even a key survives it."

Vane was already at the door, sword loose in her hand, watching the treeline. "Mara," she said. "How many ways off this hill do you know?"

He opened his mouth to answer — and stopped, because Pell had begun to hum.

Low. Soft. The same song, he realized with a chill, that he had been humming at four years old, walking out of Grey Ford through the ash. The stones of the hearth glowed faintly green, and beneath the floor, beneath the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and listened.

"Door," Pell said, and pointed down.
