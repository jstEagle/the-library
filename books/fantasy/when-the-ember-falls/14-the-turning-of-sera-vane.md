## 14. The Turning of Sera Sera Vane

Sera Vane had spent nineteen years learning to obey.

She had learned it the way other people learned prayers — by repetition, in the dark, until the words wore grooves into her. *The Court is the kingdom. The law is the flame. Doubt is the first spark of heresy.* She had recited them at dawn and dusk for two decades, and she had believed them the way a stone believes in gravity: not as a choice, but as the shape of the world.

Then the Court burned her name off its rolls, and the world kept turning without her, and it turned out she had been wrong about the shape of everything.

Now she crouched behind a fallen shrine-wall in the ruins of Vess's outer village, watching four Ember loyalists pick their way through the snow toward the hut where Pell slept, and discovered that obedience, like any habit, died hard — but it did die.

"You're certain it's here?" The speaker was a tall man in scorched vestments, his collar melted into a shape like a claw. Brother Cassian. Vane remembered him from the seminary at Ashmoor: second in his class, endlessly punctual, famous for once reporting his own brother for praying to the wrong saint. He had been kind to her then, in his bloodless way. He carried a short sword now instead of a censer.

"The clansman said a woman, a smuggler, and a child who hums," said another — a woman Vane didn't know, young, frost-burned cheeks, the eager look of someone who still thought the Court was a church. "The Hierophant wants the vessel brought to the Vale. Alive if possible."

"And if not possible?"

"Then dead is also an offering." The young woman said it brightly, the way one might say *then soup is also acceptable.*

Vane's hand found her sword hilt. It was a good sword — Court-forged, blessed at three altars, paid for out of her own stipend over six years because the Inquisition had never quite gotten around to issuing her one. She had held it at executions. She had held it while heretics screamed, telling herself the screaming was necessary, that the fire fed the gods and the gods kept the borders and the borders kept children like this one safe in their beds.

Pell, asleep in the hut, humming even in dreams. A soft little tune that made the frost on the windowpane bloom into patterns like ferns.

*Alive if possible.*

"Brother Cassian." Vane stepped out from behind the wall, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Nineteen years of practice. "Walk away."

Four heads turned. Four faces went through the same sequence — recognition, confusion, recalculation. She watched it happen in each of them like weather moving across a field.

"Inquisitor Vane." Cassian inclined his head with terrible politeness. "Or should I say *former* Inquisitor. You were declared apostate in the autumn writs."

"I read the writs. I'm told they make excellent tinder."

"This isn't a jesting matter." His eyes flicked to the hut. "You're harboring the vessel."

"I'm harboring a child."

"The child is a door, Vane. You've seen what it does. Stones glow. The old powers stir toward it like moths. Do you understand what that means?" He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into the register priests used for the genuinely stupid. "It means the gods are coming back, and when they are fully awake, every prayer in Thornfell gets answered. Every one. The widow's prayer and the murderer's. The prayer for rain and the prayer for your neighbor's crops to rot. The Court is the only thing standing between this country and—"

"I've seen the Burnt Vale," Vane said.

That stopped him. Good. She wanted him stopped. She wanted all of them stopped, standing very still in the snow, listening.

"I walked it last month," she continued. "Three miles of pilgrims who prayed too loudly. Still warm, some of them. A mother holding a baby, both of them ash, and the ash hadn't scattered because there'd been no wind — the gods took them so fast the air didn't have time to move." She let that sit. "You call that mercy. You call it order. I stood in the middle of it and I did the arithmetic you taught me at Ashmoor, Cassian, and the numbers don't come out. We aren't feeding the gods to save the country. We're feeding the country to feed the gods, and someone is harvesting what comes out the other side."

Something moved behind Cassian's eyes. For half a heartbeat she thought — hoped — it was doubt.

It wasn't. It was pity.

"Oh, Sera." He said her given name gently, horribly, like a doctor delivering a diagnosis. "I heard about the High Shrine. What the clan-mothers told you. The farming of the faithful." He shook his head. "Do you know what we called stories like that in seminary? *Comfort heresies.* Lies the weak tell themselves so they don't have to carry the weight. The Court does difficult things. Someone must."

"The Hierophant harvests power from burned heretics and sells it back to us as blessing."

"Someone must," he repeated, and she understood, finally, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that he would say it forever. That there was no argument, no evidence, no valley full of ash that would ever reach him, because the alternative to *someone must* was *no one must*, and Cassian would rather burn than live in a world where nothing was required of him but kindness.

The young woman drew her blade. "We have orders, apostate. Step aside or be rendered."

"Rendered," Vane said. "Is that what we're calling it now? When I joined we called it murder and did it anyway, but at least we were honest in our hearts."

"Sera." Cassian again, patient, awful. "Come with us. Stand trial again. Plead reform. The Court forgives — you know it does. One act of contrition and you're back under the flame's protection. Bring the vessel. That's all. One errand, and everything you lost comes home."

And there it was. The offer she had been dreading, not because she feared she'd take it but because she understood exactly how much it cost to refuse.

Everything you lost comes home.

Her office. Her rank. Her place at the long table in the Hall of Judgment, the chair with her name burned into the backrest. Her pension, such as it was. The respect of strangers. The right to walk into any town in Thornfell and have doors open. Nineteen years of being *somebody*, offered back to her for the price of one sleeping child.

Behind her, faintly, through the hut's thin walls, Pell hummed in sleep. The frost ferns on the window grew a new frond.

"No," Vane said.

"Sera—"

"My name is Inquisitor Vane," she said, and drew her sword. "Court-forged. Blessed at three altars. Paid for honestly." She settled into the guard stance Master Odran had beaten into her at fourteen, the one designed for fighting multiple opponents in narrow spaces, and felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked since the day they stripped her sash. "And I am still, whatever your writs say, an officer of the law of Thornfell. Not the Court's law. The old one. The one where killing children is a crime."

"There is no Thornfell," the young woman said. "It was dissolved."

"Then I suppose," Vane said, "that makes me an outlaw."

She struck first, because Odran had also taught her that the person who waits for the formal challenge in a four-against-one loses the formal challenge.

* * *

Later — much later, sitting against the shrine wall with blood freezing in her boot seams and her sword arm shaking in a way it would take hours to admit to — Vane tried to remember how long it lasted. Moments. Minutes. Memory refused to file it properly. There was only the young woman, fast and frightened and better than Vane expected; and Cassian, who fought like a man doing paperwork, correct and joyless; and the other two, whose names she never learned, who would be names on no one's list anywhere, mourned by no institution, buried under no rite. She gave them that, at least, in the end: she dug. All four. In frozen ground. It took until well past dark, and her hands were ruined afterward, and Mara said nothing when he saw them, just handed her the salve jar and looked at her with an expression she decided not to interpret.

But before the later there was the fight itself, and the thing that happened inside it that she would carry for the rest of her life.

Cassian got past her guard. Once. Early, when she was still fighting like an officer, like a duelist, like a woman performing competence for an audience. His blade opened her forearm and hers rang off his pauldron, and they broke apart breathing white, circling, and he smiled at her — kindly, terribly, with real affection underneath it.

"It doesn't have to go this way," he said. "Truly. I liked you at Ashmoor, Sera. You were the best of us."

"I know," she said. And then, hearing herself say it, understanding it for the first time in full: "That was the problem."

Because she *had* been the best of them. She had believed hardest, doubted least, reported most faithfully, burned most efficiently. Every horror she had uncovered in the past year — the farming, the Vale, the hungry things wearing the masks of gods — she had been perfectly constructed to serve. The Court hadn't corrupted her. The Court had *completed* her, the way a key completes a lock, and what was breaking open now wasn't her faith.

It was the lock.

Cassian came at her again, and this time she didn't fight like an officer. She fought like Mara fought — dirty, economical, entirely uninterested in form. Snow to the eyes. Boot heel to the knee. The pommel, not the edge, when the edge would kill too slowly. It was ugly work and it was over quickly, and when it was done she stood alone in a churned circle of trampled snow with four bodies around her and her breath coming in sobs she refused to acknowledge as sobs.

From the doorway of the hut, Pell watched her.

The child didn't cry. Didn't run. Just stood there in the lamplight, wrapped in a blanket far too big, humming very softly — and the hum wasn't the dreamy wandering tune from before. It was low and steady and deliberate, and every stone within twenty feet of the shrine was glowing a faint, warm green, like coals under ash.

Pell looked at the bodies. Looked at Vane. Looked at the glowing stones. Then back at Vane, with an expression of grave, absolute, unmistakable approval — the look of a small magistrate confirming a verdict — and nodded once.

Vane started laughing. It came out of her sideways, cracked and helpless, and she sat down in the snow and laughed until it turned into something else, and Pell crossed the distance between them — small feet crunching — and put one small mittened hand on her shoulder, the way one comforts, the way one knights, the way one forgives.

* * *

Mara found her at midnight, sitting up with the fire, cleaning her sword with hands that wouldn't stop trembling.

He didn't ask what happened. The graves were visible from the road; anyone traveling the pass tomorrow would see four mounds and understand the sermon. Instead he sat down across from her, fed the fire a knot of pine that spat and flared, and after a while said:

"Cassian. Wasn't it? The tall one."

"You knew him?"

"Knew *of* him. My mistress kept lists." He turned the grimoire over in his hands, that damnable book, its sealed pages catching the firelight. "Everyone the Court sends after us, she has notes. He was seminary class of... I want to say the year the salt roads flooded. Top of his cohort in doctrine." A pause. "Bottom of his cohort, apparently, in walking away."

"He couldn't have walked away." Vane heard her own voice go flat. "That's not a failure of nerve. It's architecture. They build them so they can't."

"Mm." Mara poked the fire. "And what did they build you as?"

The question landed harder than he probably intended — or maybe exactly as hard as he intended; he had that quality, the smuggler's instinct for exactly where a person's cargo hold was weakest. She considered lying, considered deflection, considered the seventeen kinds of silence available to a trained interrogator.

"They built me as the best of them," she said. "And it turns out the best of them is just the one who takes longest to notice the floor is made of ash."

Mara nodded slowly, and there was no mockery in it. "Took me thirty years to notice I was built too. Apprentice. Guide. Useful man." He glanced at Pell's sleeping shape by the hearth. "They choose us early, these gods and their courts. Before we get a vote."

"Did you get a vote tonight?" she asked. "In the pass. With the captain who sold us."

The fire popped. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer.

"No," he said quietly. "And that's the part that keeps me up. Not the killing. The ease of it." He looked up at her, and in the firelight his face was younger than it pretended to be, and very tired. "So I'll ask the question you're going to be asking yourself at three in the morning, Inquisitor, and you can lie to me now and answer honestly later: when you turned your blade on them today — was it because it was right? Or because it was finally *yours*?"

Vane cleaned her sword. Took her time with it. Ran the whetstone down the fuller twice, three times, listening to the sound fill the hut.

"Both," she said at last. "God help me. Both at once."

"Good," said Mara. "Only liars and madmen feel just one thing at a time. Now sleep. Tomorrow we reach the mistress's hut, and I'd rather face her rested."

He banked the fire and lay down, and Vane stayed up a while longer, looking at her reflection in the polished blade. The woman who looked back wore no sash, bore no writ, answered to no throne and no altar and no law written anywhere in what remained of Thornfell. By every measure she had lived by for nineteen years, she was damned.

She found, testing the thought carefully, like a tongue finding a missing tooth, that she could breathe.

* * *

They reached the hidden hut two days later, in a snowfall fine as sifted flour, and the door was open, and the lamp was lit, and the woman waiting inside had not aged a day since the portraits in the grimoire's margins.

Vane saw Mara's face when he saw her — saw the whole history of the boy break across it like ice on a river — and she put her hand on her sword hilt out of pure habit.

Then the woman looked at Vane, and smiled, and said, in a voice like dry paper: "The Inquisitor. Oh, *good*. I did so hope they'd send someone worth corrupting."

And Vane understood, with the cold certainty of a woman who had finally learned to read the floor beneath her feet, that the trap had never been behind them at all.
