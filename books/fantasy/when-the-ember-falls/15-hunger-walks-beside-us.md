## 15. Hunger Walks Beside Us

The stranger joined them on the third morning out of Vess, and he did it the way fog joins a valley — no footstep, no announcement, just a man-shaped absence of solitude standing at the edge of the fire's light when Mara woke.

He was sitting on a boulder with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a ferry that had been delayed by weather. He wore traveling clothes of no particular country: a coat the color of wet slate, boots too clean for the road, a hat set at an angle that suggested someone had once told him hats were worn this way and he had never had occasion to doubt them. His face was pleasant. That was the first wrong thing about him. Roads wear faces; this one had been ironed.

"Good morning," the stranger said. "I hope I'm not intruding. I've been walking behind you since the shrine at Cold Ford, and it seemed rude to keep pretending otherwise."

Mara's hand found his knife before his mind finished the sentence. Behind him, inside the lean-to of pine boughs they'd raised against the wind, Pell stirred and hummed one low note — the warning note, the one that made stones itch.

Vane was already awake. She was always already awake; Mara suspected she slept the way cats did, with one ear on duty. She came around the fire with her blade drawn and her expression arranged into its usual verdict: guilty until proven interesting.

"Name," she said.

"Ah." The stranger looked genuinely delighted, as though she'd asked him a question he'd been hoping someone would ask for four hundred years. "Names are difficult for me. I've had so many, and most of them were shouted. You may call me Wren. It's small and harmless and I chose it myself, which I find makes it more honest than the ones I was given."

"Step into the light," Vane said.

"I'd rather not, if it's all the same to you."

"Then I'll drag you."

"You could try," Wren agreed pleasantly, "and then we would both learn something upsetting about your limits, and I would hate to start our friendship that way."

Pell emerged from the lean-to, hair full of pine needles, and looked at the stranger the way children look at thunderstorms: frightened, and unable to look away. Then the child did something that stopped Mara's heart mid-beat. Pell walked forward, past Vane's outflung arm, and sat down on the frost-burned grass directly in front of the boulder, gazing up at Wren with the solemn attention of an audience member at a puppet show.

"Pell," Vane said sharply. "Come here."

Pell didn't move. And Wren — Wren leaned down, slowly, the way you'd lean toward a bird you didn't want to startle, and studied the child with an expression that cracked the pleasantness right down the middle. Underneath it was hunger. Not the ordinary kind. The kind that has outlasted every meal it ever stole.

"Hello, little singer," Wren said softly. "You're the reason I finally worked up the courage to introduce myself."

"Touch the child," Vane said, "and there won't be enough of you left to burn."

"Everyone keeps threatening me with burning." Wren straightened up and spread his hands, wounded. "Do I seem flammable to you? Honestly. First impressions matter, and yours is *arson*."

* * *

They didn't kill him. This was not for lack of Vane trying to construct a plan, or for lack of Mara pointing out, reasonably, that a man who had walked behind them for three days without leaving prints in the snow was probably not going to be solved with a knife.

"He doesn't eat," Mara said that evening, watching their guest across the fire. Wren had produced food from somewhere — a wheel of pale cheese, a loaf still warm, honey in a clay jar sealed with wax that smelled of summer — and laid it out before them like an offering, taking nothing for himself. "Look. He gave us supper and he's not touching any of it."

"Poisoned?"

"If it is, it's the best-poisoned cheese I've ever eaten." Mara took another bite anyway. He was aware this made him an idiot. He had decided somewhere over the last two days that he was probably already dead in every way that mattered, and dead men might as well enjoy good cheese. "That's the thing about him, Vane. He's *courteous*. Gods aren't supposed to be courteous. They're supposed to be pillars of green fire and valleys full of ash. This one says 'please' and 'thank you' and complimented my fire-building."

"It was a very good fire," Wren called over, agreeably. "Small, efficient, well-sheltered from the wind. Whoever taught you understood that warmth is a discipline."

"My mistress," Mara said, before he could stop himself.

"Did she?" Something moved behind Wren's eyes — recognition, maybe, or appetite. "She sounds wise."

"She sounds like none of your business."

"Fair." Wren smiled and let it go, which was somehow worse than pressing.

Vane crouched beside Mara, voice dropped low. "We should lose him. Cut off the road at night, take the smuggler paths, be gone before dawn."

"He'll find us. He found us through three days of snow and switchbacks. And besides—" Mara turned the honey jar in his hands, watching the firelight swim in it. "He hasn't done anything. That's what I keep coming back to. Everything else that's happened to us since the shrines woke has tried to kill us, arrest us, or eat us. This one brought breakfast."

"That's how it starts," Vane said grimly. "In the Court, we had a word for miracles that arrived politely. We called them *terms*."

* * *

The gifts continued, and each one fit too well.

On the fourth day it was new boot-laces for Mara, waxed cord of exactly the length he needed, appearing coiled atop his pack while he pissed behind a rock. On the fifth it was a whetstone for Vane, fine-grained river slate, and Vane held it for a long moment before stowing it with the expression of a woman accepting a bribe she intended to report later to an authority that no longer existed. For Pell there was a wooden flute, carved from applewood, smooth as a river stone — and Pell, who had never spoken a word in all the weeks they'd traveled together, held it to the light and hummed into it, and the flute hummed back in harmony, and Wren watched with his hands pressed together like a man in prayer.

"Stop taking things from him," Vane said that night.

"They're just laces."

"They're just *hooks*. My mother used to say the devil doesn't knock. He leaves packages on the step until you open the door yourself."

"Your mother sounds like my mistress," Mara said. "Maybe they knew each other. Maybe there's a whole secret society of grim women telling people not to accept gifts, and everyone ignores them, and that's why the world's in the state it's in."

"Mara."

"I know." He sighed and lay back against his pack. "I know. But here's what bothers me. He asks questions. All day, little questions. What the salt trade paid before the borders went. Whether I ever wanted anything more than this road. Whether Vane misses having a court to serve. Whether Pell dreams. And I keep answering, because he's easy to talk to, and that frightens me more than the fire pillar did. Fire doesn't want to *know* you."

Across the embers, Wren sat awake, of course. Gods presumably didn't sleep, or slept standing up inside mountains, or something. He caught Mara looking and raised two fingers off his knee — a small salute, friendly, patient. A man with nowhere better to be and all the time in creation to be there.

* * *

It came to a head at the pass below the Burnt Vale, where the road narrowed between walls of black stone and the wind sang through cracks like a choir clearing its throat.

Wren fell into step beside Mara, alone, deliberately, the way a careful man arranges a private conversation without anyone noticing it's been arranged. Ahead, Vane walked with Pell on her shoulders, the child's flute tucked in her belt because Pell only played it now when Wren was watching, and something about that arrangement had begun to trouble even the child.

"You're angry with me," Wren observed.

"I'm wary of you. There's a difference. Anger requires me to care."

"Mm. Your mistress taught you that deflection too? She must have been quite a woman." He walked a few steps in silence, boots making no sound at all on the scree, which Mara had stopped remarking on the way you stop remarking on a limp. "May I ask you something honest? Not one of my little questions. An honest one."

"You'll ask it either way."

"I will. But I'd like your leave." When Mara said nothing, Wren took it as the yes it was. "What do you want, Mara Joubert? Truly. Not survival — survival is what animals want, and you're better than an animal, whatever you tell yourself. I mean the want underneath. The one you don't say out loud because saying it would make it real, and real things can be taken away."

Mara kept his eyes on the path. "Passage," he said finally. It cost him something to say it, and Wren heard the cost, and leaned in like a man smelling bread. "Since I was six years old. Out. Over the mountains, past Thornfell, to somewhere the gods have never heard of. Somewhere nobody knows my name or my mistress's name or what I am. A door, and me on the other side of it, and it shut behind me."

"There it is," Wren said softly, with such tenderness that Mara's skin crawled. "Thank you. That wasn't so terrible, was it?"

"Why do you want to know?"

"Because I can give it to you." Wren stopped walking. The wind kept moving through him, Mara noticed — bent his coat, stirred his collar, but never quite seemed to touch him. "Not the Door at the mountain's heart. I know about the Door; don't look so surprised, I knew about it before your mistress's grandmother was a rumor. I mean simply. I can carry you over the pass tonight. Past the Court's pickets, past the vale, past everything. One night's work for me. Less than nothing. And you'd be out, Mara. Free, unnamed, exactly as you dreamed. The child stays with the inquisitor, who will die within the year regardless — she's marked, you've seen it, she walks like a woman reading her own sentence. Or the child comes with you, if you prefer, and I'll carry you both, and ask nothing."

"Nothing," Mara repeated.

"Nothing at all." Wren smiled, and for one unguarded instant the smile was too wide, showing teeth that belonged in a larger mouth. Then it folded itself back into courtesy. "I'm old, Mara. Older than the Court, older than the sealing, older than names. Do you know what I have wanted for four hundred years? Company. Someone to walk with who isn't afraid — or rather, someone whose fear has grown into something more interesting. You interest me. That's all. Walk with me a while longer. Let me give you the world you've wanted since you were six. It costs you a kindness you'd pay a friend for free."

And the horrible thing — the thing Mara would turn over in his mind for years afterward, on the far side of everything — was that some part of him wanted to say yes. Not the greedy part. The tired part. The six-year-old part, still standing at the foot of the mountain with salt-cracked hands, looking up at a wall of stone and snow and thinking *there has to be a way through, there has to be a way out, there has to be.*

"No," he said.

Wren tilted his head. "No to which part?"

"All of it. Every part." Mara made himself meet those pleasant, ironed features. "Because I've met men like you. Smugglers, captains, priests. The ones who offer gently are the ones collecting hardest. You don't want company. You want *consent*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I think you know it, and I think that's why you need it from us instead of just taking us. Taking isn't enough for you anymore. It hasn't been for centuries. You need someone to open the door themselves." He paused. "Sound familiar? Doors. Funny thing to want, for a god."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The wind filled the silence, singing through the black stone.

Then the god laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, rich and almost human — and clapped Mara on the shoulder with a hand that weighed nothing and felt like the whole mountain resting there for half a heartbeat.

"Oh, *well done*," Wren said, delighted. "Well done indeed. Do you know how long it's been since anyone refused me to my face? Neither do I. That's how long." He stepped back, and the delight cooled into something older and sadder and infinitely more dangerous. "But I'll tell you a truth for free, guide, since you gave me one. The Hierophant is three days behind us with two hundred faithful and a cage built for the child. And ahead—" he nodded up the pass, toward the smoke-hazed skyline where the Burnt Vale waited, and beyond it, if the grimoire's opened pages told true, the Gate of Teeth. "Ahead is the Door. And the Door opens for one who gives up what they most want to keep. So consider, tonight, by your excellent fire: what will *you* give? Because I promise you the Court has already decided what they'll take."

He tipped his ridiculous hat. And between one gust of wind and the next, he was gone — no footstep, no farewell, just a man-shaped absence where a companion had been, and the faint smell of apples in the cold air.

Vane came back down the trail at a run, blade out, Pell clinging to her neck. "Where is he? What did he say?"

"He offered me everything I ever wanted," Mara said. He picked up his pack. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "And I said no. Ask me tomorrow whether I'm proud of it."

Above them, far up the pass, something answered the wind's singing — a low, vast note, like a throat clearing after four hundred years of silence.

The Gate of Teeth was close. And they were not the only ones walking toward it.
