## 16. The Gate of Teeth

Sera Vane had spent twenty years believing she understood courage. It was the thing inquisitors wrote reports about — the steady deacon who refused to recant, the magistrate who signed a death warrant with a shaking hand and signed it anyway. Courage, as the Court taught it, was obedience performed under pressure. She had believed that the way you believe a floor will hold.

Then she watched a mute child step in front of a crossbow bolt meant for a smuggler, and every definition she owned burned like the Court's own incense.

But that was later. First there was the gate.

* * *

They smelled it before they saw it: woodsmoke and hot iron and something sweeter underneath, like meat left too near a consecrated flame. The path climbed out of the birch scrub and opened onto a shelf of stone, and there — wedged into the throat of the mountain like a coin in a miser's fist — stood the Gate of Teeth.

Mara had named it, years ago, on some other smuggler's errand, and the name fit. Two walls of black rock jutted from either side of the pass, jagged as a snapped jaw, leaving a gap no wider than a hay cart. Beyond it the stone rose sheer for a hundred feet. There was no going around. There was only through.

And through was an army.

Not a large one. That was almost worse. The Ember Court's grand hosts had burned or scattered or starved over the long autumn; what remained was this — forty-odd soldiers in scorched tabards, camped in the jaws of the gate itself, braziers glowing along the rock like a row of patient orange eyes. And pacing before them, hands clasped behind his back, robes immaculate in a country made entirely of mud and ash, stood the Hierophant.

"He beat us here." Mara crouched beside her behind a wind-twisted pine, his voice flat in the way she had learned meant fear wearing competence's clothing. "Of course he did. He's had three weeks and we've had a blizzard, a mutiny, and a god walking beside us asking riddles."

"The god's gone," Vane said.

"That's my point. We lost our miracle and he kept his."

Vane counted braziers instead of answering. Eleven. Forty-one soldiers, maybe forty-three. Two ballistae, unstrung but present, which meant they could be strung, which meant the sky above the gate would become very unhealthy for anyone approaching on foot. She did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the High Shrine of Vess and arrived at the same sum: they could not win. They could only spend themselves wisely.

"Options," she said.

"Option one," Mara said, "we go home."

"You don't have a home."

"Exactly. It's a very short option." He rubbed his jaw. "Option two. Pell hums, the stones wake, half of them run. Option three—" He stopped. She watched him not say it.

"Say option three," she said.

"There isn't an option three."

"Mara."

"There isn't," he repeated, and looked at her, and she saw that option three was *I walk down there alone and give them what they want*, and that he had been carrying it since Frostbound like a stone in his boot, and that he would try to do it tonight if she let him.

So she didn't let him. She put her hand flat on his forearm — the first time she had touched him without intent to arrest — and said, "We go together or we don't go. Those are the only options I recognize anymore. I used to have a whole book of them. I burned it."

He laughed, quietly, horribly. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once guided a bride over the pass to a wedding where both parties were drunk."

Pell, sitting between them with knees drawn up, made a sound. It wasn't a word. It was the small humming note the child carried everywhere now, low as a hearth coals make, and the pebbles around Pell's feet shivered faintly in answer, rolling toward the child like dogs coming when called.

Vane looked at the child and felt the old machinery of judgment try to turn and find its gears stripped out. A month ago she would have written a report. *Subject displays unsanctioned resonance.* A month ago that sentence had ended lives. Now it just sounded like a description of weather.

"Pell," she said softly. The child looked up. Dark eyes, watchful, older than eight years had any right to be. "When we go down there — you stay behind me. Not beside me. Behind."

Pell shook her head once, slow and absolute.

"Yes," Vane said.

The child shook it again and then, deliberately, reached out and pressed one small palm against Vane's breastplate, right over the scar where the Court had torn away her insignia. The touch was light. It went through her like a nail.

"She says she doesn't hide behind people," Mara translated.

"How do you possibly know that?"

"I don't. But I've been guessing what she means for a month and I'm right about half the time, which is better odds than prayer."

Below, a brazier flared. One of the Hierophant's priests — Vane could see the white-and-gold now, the ember sigils stitched at every hem — lifted a horn to his lips, and the sound that came out was not a horn's sound. It was layered, doubled, as if two throats spoke through one instrument, and the note rolled up the mountainside and set Vane's teeth aching.

It was a summons. She knew the form. *Come down. Be judged. Burn cleanly.*

"They know we're here," she said.

"They've known since the vale," Mara said. "Since before the vale. He wants us to come down. That's the part I hate. Traps you can avoid. Invitations you have to answer."

Pell began to hum louder. The pebbles rose off the ground, all of them at once, hanging in the air like a held breath.

* * *

They answered at dusk, because Mara insisted dusk gave smugglers the advantage — "We're the ones who know where the rocks are, and in poor light so does nobody" — and because Vane insisted they wait until the last possible hour so that the Court's sentries would be cold, tired, and thinking about supper. Between the two of them they had constructed, without ever quite discussing it, a strategy that was half tactics and half spite.

It began well, which should have warned her.

Mara took them along a shelf he swore existed — "I carried salt across it at fourteen, if I fell then I'll fall now and it'll be fair" — a ledge no wider than a coffin, hugging the eastern wall of the gate. Below them, the Court's fires threw shadows upward, and the shadows hid them. Vane moved with her sword already loose in its scabbard, Pell strapped to her back in the woolen harness they'd rigged in the high shrine, the child's arms around her neck and her breath warm against Vane's ear, humming so softly only the stones heard.

The plan was simple: gain the western spur, come down behind the army's flank while Mara drew their eyes from the front — the oldest trick on any road, the smuggler's feint — and reach the gate itself. Not to fight through. To *reach*. Everything after the gate was the Door's business, not theirs.

Everything before it was hers.

She was thirty feet from the spur when the ballista fired.

She never saw the crew, only heard the crack of the arm and felt the air tear past her shoulder close enough to comb her hair, and then the bolt struck the ledge ahead of her and the ledge — old, frost-cracked, betrayed by a summer of thaw — simply decided it had held enough weight for enough centuries.

Stone slid. Vane dropped, wrapped herself around Pell, and rode six feet of collapsing mountainside down into the firelight, landing hard enough that the world went white and then red and then resolved, painfully, into the face of an Ember soldier standing over her with a spear.

Behind her, Mara shouted her name. Ahead, forty faces turned. Above, the Hierophant stepped forward into the light like a man arriving at a dinner he had personally catered.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said, warmly. Warmth, she thought, was the great lie of the age. Everything about the Court was warm. Its fire, its welcome, its mercy. "You've brought me the child. That saves such wear on everyone's boots."

Vane got one knee under herself. Her ribs reported grievances. Pell's grip on her neck loosened — the child sliding down, moving, moving *forward*, and Vane grabbed for her and caught wool and missed —

— because between one heartbeat and the next, Pell walked out in front of her, into the ring of spears, and stood there, small and straight-backed, humming.

A spearman lunged — not to kill, she'd understand later; the Hierophant wanted the child breathing — to seize. Pell's hum changed pitch. It was one note, rising, the note Vane had heard in caves and refugee camps and once, terribly, in a dream, and the standing stones of the gate — the black teeth themselves — lit from within like lanterns behind horn.

The spearman flew backward ten feet and did not get up quickly.

"Inquisitor," the Hierophant said, and for the first time the warmth thinned. "Control your animal."

"She's not mine," Vane said, drawing her sword, stepping forward to stand where Pell had decided she would stand, which was nowhere near *behind*. "Nothing is yours. That was the entire content of my heresy, if you'll recall the trial. You weren't listening. Nobody ever listens at trials."

She fought.

She had fought before — inquisition work was not gentle, and border garrisons bred brawls the way stables bred flies — but she had never fought like this, with nothing behind her blade but her own name. Before, every stroke had been the Crown's stroke, the Court's stroke, and even disgraced she had still swung with borrowed weight. Now the sword was only hers, and her body was only hers, and the woman fighting in the firelight at the Gate of Teeth was someone she had never met and recognized immediately.

Two spearmen down. A priest's chain-whip caught her pauldron and she tore free, losing the pauldron. Mara appeared out of the dark on the flank like a rumor, knife-work quick and graceless and effective, shouting nonsense — "Your left! No, *my* left!" — and she realized dimly he was drawing eyes, being loud, being bait, spending himself as a decoy because it was the only way he knew how to stand next to someone.

Then the second ballista fired, and it did not miss.

* * *

Later — much later, in a night with no bottom in it — Vane would take the moment apart and put it back together and never manage to make it smaller.

Mara had turned toward the gate. The bolt came out of the dark at his back, silent, honest, aimed by a crewman who deserved better employers. Vane saw it. Pell saw it — or knew it, or heard it arrive the way the child seemed to hear everything now — and crossed the space between them faster than sense allowed and put both hands up, palms out, the way you stop a door.

The bolt stopped.

For half a breath it hung in the air a foot from Pell's fingers, trembling, and the child's hum deepened to a note Vane felt in her molars, and she thought — absurdly, in the middle of a battle — *it's working, we're going to —*

The ballista crew fired again.

The second bolt took Pell high in the side, below the ribs, spinning the child around and down, and the hum cut off mid-note, and every stone within fifty yards went dark at once like blown candles.

What happened next, she did in order, and each item was simple, and together they were the whole of her:

She killed the ballista crew. Both bolts' worth of men, four of them, with a speed she would not remember choosing. She killed a priest trying to raise the alarm-horn. She reached Pell, gathered the child up — light, so dreadfully light, blood soaking through the wool harness — and carried her to the narrow shadow of the gate's western tooth, where the spears could not come three abreast.

And then she turned around and held the pass.

"Hold the gate!" she roared at Mara, and heard him scream something back — she'd learn later it was her name, and Pell's, and then nothing, because words ran out — and then the Hierophant's guard came at her, all of them, the last army of Thornfell funneled into the teeth like water into a millrace, and Sera Vane discovered what the old songs had been trying to tell her all along and the Court had spent a century editing out.

Courage was not obedience under pressure. Courage was a door held open for someone else to run through, held open with your body, knowing the door closes on you.

They broke on her in waves. She gave ground by inches because inches were all the tooth-shadow offered, and every inch she gave cost them three men of room to swing. Her sword arm went numb at the elbow. Something opened her cheek. A spearhead found the joint of her cuisse and she put a dagger through its owner's eye and kept the dagger. She was laughing at some point — she heard herself, appalled — some ragged, furious laugh, and she thought: *this is what the gods must feel like, everyone coming to you with their hunger at once,* and she thought: *no. Nothing like the gods. The difference is I intend to survive this, and if I don't, it will be the first thing I've ever done that was entirely my idea.*

The Hierophant watched from beyond the press, white-robed, unmoving, and she locked eyes with him across the killing floor and mouthed, clearly, so he could read it: *This is what mercy looks like.*

He raised one hand, and the guard withdrew — not defeated, she noted, withdrawn, regrouped, reformed into a crescent that sealed the pass's lower mouth. He was patient. Patience was his whole religion.

But the gate behind her stood empty. And Mara Joubert was through it, with the grimoire, climbing toward the Door.

* * *

She knelt in the tooth-shadow with Pell's head in her lap. The wound was bad. Not — she checked twice, three times, with hands that shook — not mortal, the angle merciful, the bleeding slowing, but bad, and the child's face was gray as ash, and the hum was gone, and the silence where it had been was the loudest thing on the mountain.

"Stay," Vane told her, pressing cloth to the wound. "Do you hear me? I've broken every law I ever served. I've burned my name, my office, my god, and my king's country, all of it, the whole stupid ledger. You are the only thing left on my books that isn't owed to somebody. Stay."

Pell's eyes opened. Found her. And the child — gray, pierced, silent for eleven years by every account — shaped one word with cracked lips, barely breath at all:

"Door."

"Yes," Vane said, and her voice failed and recovered. "Yes. He's there. He's doing it. So you hold on, because when this is over I am going to need someone to explain to me what just happened, and you are the only person alive who actually knows."

Above them, deeper in the mountain, the stone began to glow.

Not ember-orange. Green — the green of the pillar on the shepherd's shrine, the green Mara described in his sleep, the green of a thing waking or ending, she couldn't tell which, and the light came pouring up out of the earth beneath the Door like spring water finding a crack, and the whole pass trembled, and somewhere far below she heard a sound like a vast lock turning.

Pell's small hand found hers and gripped it.

The Hierophant, beyond the crescent of his guard, went to his knees — and began to pray.
