## 17. What Mara Wants to Keep

The Door did not look like a door.

Mara had spent his whole life around thresholds — the wobbly back gate of his mistress's hut, the smuggler's posterns cut into cliff faces, the low lintels of shrine doorways you had to bow through whether you meant to or not. Doors were things with hinges and intentions. This was a seam in the mountain, a crack of darkness in the living rock of Thornfell's heart, tall as two men and narrow as a coffin stood on end, and it had no frame because it needed none. The stone around it was smooth as river glass, worn by nothing anyone could name, and the darkness inside it was not the darkness of a cave. It was the darkness of a closed eye.

Pell stood before it with one hand pressed flat against the stone, humming.

The stone hummed back.

Mara felt it in his teeth — that low, patient note, like a bell struck once a very long time ago and still deciding whether to finish ringing. Vane had her sword drawn, though there was nothing left to fight; they had left the Court's last guard bleeding behind them in the Gate of Teeth, and the Hierophant himself had fallen somewhere in the smoke below. There should have been triumph. There was only this: three survivors, one wounded child, and a hole in the world that hummed when touched.

"Is it open?" Vane asked. Her voice was raw from shouting orders she'd never thought she'd give. "Can you tell?"

"It's listening," Mara said.

He didn't know how he knew that. He just knew. The way he'd always known which pass would hold snow and which would shed it, which innkeeper would sell him out and which would pour him an honest cup first. Some knowledge wasn't learned. It settled into you like silt in a riverbed, and you only noticed it when the water ran clear enough to see.

The grimoire hung against his chest inside his coat, and it had gone warm. Not the fever-heat of the sealed pages in the blizzard cave, when the book had all but burned itself open in his hands. A gentler warmth. The warmth of a hand resting on your shoulder.

*Well done,* it seemed to say. *You brought it home.*

"Stay back," Mara told them, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "Both of you. I don't know what it does when it — when it notices someone."

"Notices," Vane repeated, flat. "It's a door, Joubert. It doesn't notice."

But Pell nodded. Pell, who hadn't spoken since the ash choir, who had said exactly one word in all the weeks since — *Door* — looked up at Vane with those old-soul eyes and nodded, slow and certain, and Vane's certainty cracked down the middle like river ice in spring.

They waited. That was the thing nobody told you about the ends of things: mostly they were waiting. Mara sat down on the cold stone with his back against the mountain and let the silence gather, and Pell crept over and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and after a while Vane gave up on vigilance entirely and sat down too, sword across her knees, looking for all the world like a woman resting between chores.

"So," Vane said eventually. "What happens now?"

"Now," Mara said, "I think it asks me something."

"How do you—"

"The book." He pulled the grimoire out and set it on the stone between them. It fell open on its own, the way it had been doing more and more lately, pages moving under his fingers like the gills of a fish. "In the cave, when the blizzard had us. You remember what it said. What the Door costs."

Vane remembered. Everyone remembered. It was the kind of sentence that got into a person and stayed, like a splinter working toward the heart. *It opens only for one who gives up what they most want to keep.*

"I assumed that was a riddle," Vane said. "Priestly language. Give up your pride, give up your fear, some symbolic nonsense the old orders loved."

"You've seen what the gods do with symbolic nonsense?" Mara almost laughed. "They answer prayers with fire, Sera. They're literalists. Every one of them. If the Door says it wants what I most want to keep, then it wants the thing itself, whole and real, torn out of me root and branch."

"And what is that?" Vane asked. "What do you most want to keep?"

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Mara had been circling for days like a hawk circling a henhouse, unable to land: he knew the answer instantly. No hesitation. No weighing of options. The moment the question formed, the answer was simply *there*, the way his own name was there, the way the memory of salt on his lips was there.

He wanted out.

Not from Thornfell specifically. Not from danger, though there had been plenty. He wanted *out* — out from under, out from between, out from the whole suffocating architecture of a life that other people had built around him without asking. His mistress who had chosen him as a child and shaped him like a hedge row. The smugglers who needed his roads. The caravan masters who trusted him because he was useful and never once asked if he wanted to be. And now Vane, who was good and honest and brave, and who needed him anyway, needed his map and his knowledge and his stubborn survival instinct, needed him the way everyone had always needed him, and needing someone is just another word for owning a piece of them.

Beyond the mountains there was a country where nobody knew his name. He had dreamed of it since he was small enough to fit in a salt sack. Green country, soft country, a country without shrines, where a man could walk into a town and be nobody, absolutely nobody, and buy bread with coins and eat it on a wall in the sun and owe nothing to anyone. He had carried that dream through every winter pass and every betrayal and every night he'd spent memorizing a grimoire he'd stolen out of spite and kept out of something he refused to call destiny.

That was what he most wanted to keep. Not the dream itself — dreams were cheap, everyone had them. The *certainty* of it. The knowledge that it existed, that it waited, that someday, if he was quick enough and careful enough and selfish enough, he would reach it.

"I can't tell you," he said to Vane.

She looked at him sideways. "You don't have to."

"No. But I'm telling you I won't." He surprised himself again. "Because if I say it out loud, you'll try to talk me out of it. Or worse, you'll agree with it. And I need—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. "I need to find out what I am when nobody needs anything from me. Even if it costs everything."

* * *

The Door chose its moment the way a cat chooses a lap: without explanation, and irreversibly.

The humming changed. Pell felt it first — the child went rigid, hand still on the stone, and turned to look at Mara with an expression of such naked alarm that Mara was on his feet before his mind caught up. The seam of darkness brightened at its edges, not with fire but with a kind of attention, the way a pupil dilates. And then the darkness showed him something.

It showed him the green country.

He was standing in it. That was the horror and the glory of it — not watching, standing. Soft grass under boots he recognized, his good ones, the ones with the mended soles. Warm sun on his neck. A town below him, red-roofed and smoke-wreathed, with a market square where a man was selling apples off a cart and arguing cheerfully with a woman about the price, and neither of them was praying, and neither of them was burning, and no god was listening to either of them because there were no gods here, there had never been any gods here, and the silence of it was the most beautiful sound Mara had ever heard.

He walked down into the town. Nobody looked at him. That was the miracle. A woman carrying a basket passed within arm's reach and her eyes slid past him like water past a stone, and it wasn't invisibility, it was *irrelevance*, it was the sweetest thing he had ever been given. He bought an apple. He paid with coins he somehow had. He ate it sitting on a low wall in the sun, and the juice ran down his wrist, and he was nobody, he was nobody, he was nobody, and no one in the wide green world needed him for anything at all.

There was a house. He understood, without being told, that it was his. Small, whitewashed, a garden gone slightly wild in the way gardens go when their keeper likes them that way. Bees in the lavender. A chair by the window angled toward the evening light. On the table inside, a book — not the grimoire, something fat and foolish, a novel maybe, stories about people who never existed — and a cup of tea going cold because he'd forgotten it, because forgetting things was safe here, because nothing here punished forgetfulness with fire.

Years unspooled. He watched them happen to himself from inside himself, which was the strangest part, the dream moving at dream-speed while feeling more solid than any day he'd ever lived. Seasons turned over the garden. His hands got older. Somewhere in the middle years he stopped flinching at loud noises, and somewhere later than that he stopped checking exits when he entered rooms, and one morning he woke up and realized he couldn't remember the smell of the salt caravans anymore, couldn't picture his mistress's face, couldn't recall the exact words of the shepherd's dying prayer at the roadside shrine — and instead of grief he felt *lightness*, the lightness of a man setting down a load so heavy he'd forgotten it was a load.

Nobody knocked on his door. Nobody followed him. Nobody ever, ever needed him.

He was free. He was free. He was—

"Mara."

The voice came from very far away and very close, both at once, and it was Vane's voice, and it dragged him up out of the green country like a hook through the cheek.

He was on his knees in front of the Door. He didn't remember kneeling. The vision still clung to him — he could smell the apple, could feel the sun, could taste the freedom the way you taste a meal for hours after eating it — and the mountain around him was cold and dark and smelled of stone and blood from the battle below, and the contrast was so brutal it made his eyes sting.

"How long?" he croaked.

"A few breaths," Vane said. She had a hand on his shoulder, gripping hard. "You walked toward it. We pulled you back. Pell bit me."

Pell, standing behind Vane, looked entirely unrepentant.

"It shows you," Mara said. His voice came out strange, distant, like someone else reporting. "It doesn't tempt you with words. Words are cheap and it knows we know they're cheap. It shows you the thing itself. It lets you live inside it long enough to forget there was ever anywhere else."

"And then?"

"And then it takes it." He got up, slowly, joints aching like an old man's, though he was barely thirty. "That's the price. Not my life. My *want*. It takes the wanting itself. I'd stand in that green country forever and never reach it, because the part of me that reached would be gone. Do you understand? It doesn't kill the dream. It kills the dreamer's hunger for it. I'd be a man with a garden and bees and no reason in the world to want anything, ever again, including to be free."

Vane was quiet for a long moment. Then, in her flat inquisitor's voice, the one she used for verdicts: "Would that be so bad?"

"Yes." No hesitation. "Yes, Sera. Because I've met men with nothing they wanted. The Court is full of them. The Hierophant wanted nothing except the gods fed — that's why he was so dangerous, that's why he burned a kingdom. A man with no want isn't peaceful. He's a held breath. He's a room with the windows painted shut. Everything I hate about the Ember Court lives inside that vision, dressed in sunlight and apples."

"But you nearly took it." It wasn't a question. She'd seen his face.

"I nearly took it." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods forgive me, I *nearly took it*. I was on the wall with the apple and I thought, this is enough, this has always been enough, and every hard cold clever survival instinct I've sharpened for thirty years laid down and rolled over like a dog in the sun. That's what it does. That's what the old gods do. They don't threaten. They *offer*. And the offer is always true. That's the trap. Every prayer they answered with fire was answered truly. The shepherd wanted to live; the flame took him somewhere warm. The pilgrims in the burnt vale prayed too loudly and got exactly what they asked for. The Court farms them honestly. Nothing in this whole cursed country has lied to us except people."

Pell tugged his sleeve. Mara looked down.

The child pointed at the Door. Then pointed at Mara. Then made a shape with both hands — a small, deliberate gesture, like holding something fragile — and offered it to him.

"What is it?" Mara asked softly.

Pell frowned, frustrated, mute as ever, and tried again. Held out the invisible fragile thing. Then pointed at Mara's chest, where the grimoire hung. Then shook their head. Then pointed at the Door. Then made the fragile shape again, and this time crushed it, slowly, deliberately, watching Mara's face the whole time.

"You think it doesn't want the dream," Mara said slowly. "You think it wants something else. Something I'm holding that I'd have to break."

Pell nodded, fierce.

Vane crouched down to the child's level. "Pell. What does he keep? What does Mara keep?"

And Pell — who had spoken one word in all these weeks, whose voice had been taken by the gods' hunger and returned thin as thread — looked at Mara, and opened their mouth, and said, in a whisper that broke over the mountainside like a dropped cup:

"*Us.*"

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a year.

Or it lasted four seconds. Mara genuinely could not have said. He stood in front of the humming seam with a child's whispered word hanging in the air and the ghost of an apple's sweetness still on his tongue, and he understood, with the awful clarity that comes only once or twice in a life, that Pell was right.

The Door had shown him the green country because the green country was the truest thing he wanted. But it had not yet named its price, and when it did, it would not ask for the dream. Dreams were already half-given away; he'd been ready to trade his wanting since the blizzard cave. The Door was older and cleverer than that. It would ask for what he *kept*. Present tense. The thing he carried now, guarded now, would die without.

Two people. One wounded child who hummed to stones and had crossed a kingdom of fire on his shoulders and Vane's stubbornness and his own refusal to leave them behind. One disgraced inquisitor who had lost her law and her god and her name and had found, in the wreckage, something better than all three — and who needed him, yes, but who had also, somewhere on the frozen passes, stopped needing him and started *choosing* him, which was a different thing entirely, a thing nobody had ever done to Mara Joubert in his entire careful, borrowed, pre-written life.

If he walked through, they stayed. In a country full of waking gods and burning priests, with the Hierophant dead but the Court's faithful scattered like embers on wind, with Pell marked by something older than the Court and hunted by whatever survived of it. They stayed, and he would be beyond the mountains eating apples in the sun, and every day of his freedom would be purchased with their peril, and the dream would sour in his mouth by midsummer, and he would know it, and the knowing would be the price collected daily, with interest.

Unless.

Unless the Door could be closed from the other side. Unless the key — and the grimoire had grown hot against his chest again, insistent, urgent, *yes, yes, you see it now* — unless the key worked both ways. Unless a man could seal the passage behind him and starve the gods of their channel and end the hunger that had eaten Thornfell alive, and pay for it with the only coin the Door accepted: the thing he most wanted to keep.

His escape. His green country. His freedom, unnamed and unwitnessed and finally, finally his.

Or them. Them, standing in the cold with him, needing him, chosen by him, the first family he'd ever picked instead of inherited.

The Door hummed. The grimoire burned. And far below, down the mountain paths they had bled to climb, Mara heard — faint, impossible, wrong — the sound of singing. Many voices. Rising. Prayers, climbing the rock like ivy, climbing toward the Door the way water climbs toward a wound.

Something was coming up the mountain. Something that prayed.

"Oh no," Vane breathed, and drew her sword at nothing. "Oh no, no, no. He's dead. We saw him fall."

"He's dead," Mara agreed quietly. "But he died praying. And they answered."

The humming of the Door changed key.
