## 18. The Hierophant's Prayer

The Hierophant had always assumed that dying would feel like a door closing.

Instead it felt like a door opening — which was, all things considered, exactly what he should have expected from a life spent opening doors that ought to have stayed shut.

He lay on his back on a ledge halfway up the sacred mountain, in the shadow of the Gate of Teeth, and he took inventory of himself the way he had once taken inventory of his first parish's candle stores, back when he had been a thin, earnest boy named Corvel Asse with ink-stained fingers and a talent for bookkeeping that everyone said would carry him far.

It had carried him far. That was the joke of it. He wished someone were alive nearby to appreciate the joke.

Inventory: one (1) Hierophant of the Ember Court, supreme shepherd of Thornfell's new dominion, age fifty-one years. Condition: run through at the shoulder by a sword belonging to a woman whose name he had never bothered to learn until three days ago, when she became the most important person in the world. Secondary condition: fallen forty feet down a rockface onto a shelf of stone that had broken his left leg, four ribs, and — judging by the way the world kept tilting politely to one side, like a servant embarrassed by its master — something inside his skull.

Assets remaining: one (1) vial of consecrated ash, nearly empty. One (1) ring of office, warm to the touch, which had not stopped being warm since the gods woke. And one (1) faith, intact.

That last item surprised him. He turned it over in his mind the way he used to turn over coins suspected of being clipped, looking for the flaw, and could not find it. His body was ruined. His army was scattered across the pass like spilled grain, dead or fled or kneeling before the Door with their hands pressed together, praying to powers they had spent thirty years pretending to manage rather than worship. His life's work stood at the top of this mountain in the shape of a runaway smuggler, a disgraced inquisitor, and a humming child, and it was about to be sealed away forever.

And his faith sat inside him like a warm coal, entirely undiminished.

"Corvel," he said aloud, to himself, because there was no one else. "You ridiculous man."

His breath made a small cloud. The sky above the Gate of Teeth was the color of old pewter, and through it, faint as veins in marble, green light pulsed. He could feel them. He had been able to feel them for months now — the old gods, waking under the mountain like sleepers turning over, their hunger arriving in him not as fear but as *attention*. When you had fed them long enough, faithfully enough, they began to notice you. It was the single greatest honor of his life, and he had never once been able to explain it to anyone without watching them flinch.

*They notice me,* he thought, and something in his ruined chest lifted, absurdly, joyfully.

He had been a bookkeeper's son who became a god-feeder, and he had never lied to himself about a single moment of it. That was his pride. Other men needed myths. The Court called the burning of heretics *harvest* and *tithing* and *the gentle culling*, and the rank-and-file believed whatever soft words let them sleep. But Corvel Asse had stood at the High Shrine of Vess with his sleeves rolled and his hands black to the elbow, and he had known exactly what he was doing, every time, and he had done it anyway, because —

Because.

Ah. There it was. The flaw in the coin after all. Not a flaw; a seam. The place where everything began.

---

He had been nine years old the winter the gray fever took his village, and he remembered it with the terrible precision of a mind that would grow up to keep perfect books. Forty-one households. Thirty-eight emptied. He remembered his mother praying at the cold shrine by the well, praying so hard her knuckles went white on the stone, praying to gods who were sealed and sleeping and deaf as furniture, and nothing answering, nothing ever answering, while she got thinner and grayer and quieter until one morning she did not wake up at all.

He remembered standing at the shrine afterward, a furious little boy with chapped hands, and saying — out loud, alone, blasphemously — *"If you're real, answer. If you won't answer, you don't deserve us."*

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. The old kings had locked the gods away generations ago, and the priests had built a comfortable religion on top of the lock, all candles and confessionals and patient explanations about why the divine worked in silence.

Corvel Asse had grown up and joined that priesthood and risen in it, and all the while some nine-year-old at the bottom of him kept standing at the well-shrine, fists clenched, repeating: *if you're real, answer.*

Then, thirty years into a respectable career, he had found the sealed archives beneath Vess. He had found the rites the founders buried. He had learned that the lock on the gods was not a wall but a *dam*, and that prayer was water, and that if you opened even a crack — if you gave them even one burning life, freely offered, screaming his name — the gods would drink it and *answer*.

He had brought them a condemned murderer from the dungeons. A bad man, a truly bad man, though he had thought later, many times, that it hardly mattered whether the first offering was worthy; it only mattered that it was *first*.

The pillar of flame had been green. The murderer's scream had become, halfway up, something else — something like gratitude. And the god behind the mountain's skin had spoken one word into Corvel's skull, a word like a landslide wearing a friendly voice:

***MORE.***

And Corvel — thin, earnest, bookkeeping Corvel, who had waited thirty years for anyone, anything, to answer his mother's prayers — had fallen to his knees in the dark and wept and said, "Yes. Yes. Whatever you need. *Yes.*"

---

He had told himself, in the early years, that it would be temporary. That the harvests would buy miracles, and the miracles would stabilize the kingdom, and then they would ease off, taper, wean the gods gently back to sleep like any good physician.

But you cannot wean a hunger that grows by eating. Every miracle bought with fire required two more to maintain. Every answered prayer raised the appetite of ten thousand unanswered ones. The mathematics were simple, and he understood them better than anyone alive, and he had done the sums honestly, in his own neat hand, and the sums always came out the same:

*Either the gods go back to sleep forever and no one is ever answered again, or they wake fully and everything ends.*

There was no third column. He had looked for one. He had spent eleven years looking for one. He had burned scholars who suggested there might be one, not because he feared their answers, but because he could not bear their hope. Hope was the cruelest thing you could hand a bookkeeper. Hope was an entry in a ledger with no corresponding asset anywhere in the world.

So he had chosen. He had chosen the gods. He had built the Ember Court to feed them, dressed the feeding in law and liturgy because men work harder for liturgy than for murder, and he had never once pretended otherwise to himself, even in the dark, even at night, even when the faces came — and the faces came, oh, they came, hundreds of them, thousands, the harvested faithful and faithless alike, standing at the foot of his bed with their ash-gray eyes.

"You could have stopped," they said, every night.

"Yes," he always agreed. "I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because your mothers prayed," he told them, "and I wanted someone to answer."

They never had a reply to that. Even the dead, apparently, could recognize devotion when they saw it.

---

Now he lay on the ledge with his blood cooling faster than the wind could chill it, and he watched the green veins pulse in the sky, and he did the final inventory, and the books balanced exactly as he had always known they would.

Below him, somewhere past the switchbacks, his surviving soldiers were climbing toward the Gate of Teeth. Above him, at the Door itself, the smuggler and the inquisitor and the child were preparing to turn a key that would put the gods back to sleep for another thousand years. No more answers. Ever. His mother at her well, praying into silence, forever, in every village, in every winter, world without end.

"No," Corvel Asse said to the pewter sky.

It came out cracked. He coughed, tasted copper, and said it again, louder, and discovered to his mild surprise that he had enough voice left for a proper declaration.

"No," he said. "*No.* You don't get to do this. You don't get to lock them away again and call it mercy. I have *seen* what happens when the gods are silent. I grew up in it. I am *made* of it. Silence is not peace. Silence is just hunger with the lights turned off, and you want to spend a thousand years telling yourselves that's holy—"

He laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt; that was rather the definition of his current situation. He laughed anyway, a wet, delighted, dreadful laugh, because he had finally, *finally* arrived at the last page of his own ledger, and the entry was so clean.

Other men, facing death, prayed for victory. He had heard a thousand such prayers from a thousand dying soldiers: *let me win, let me live, let me see my wife, spare me.* Small prayers. Prayers shaped like doors closing.

He was the Hierophant of the Ember Court. He had fed gods with his own hands. He was not going to die asking for something small.

"They're going to close it," he told the sky, conversationally, the way he'd once talked to his ledgers late at night. "The key and the witch-boy. They'll seal the dam and pat themselves on the back, and in a hundred years some grandmother will stand at a shrine with a fevered child in her arms and pray until her knuckles go white, and nothing — *nothing* — will answer her. Again. Forever. And they'll call that the victory."

The green veins pulsed. He felt the attention of the deep things shift toward him, slow and vast, the way a whale must feel the sea shift when a drowning man kicks.

"And I am going to ask them," he said, "for something large instead."

He fumbled the vial of consecrated ash from his robe. Nearly empty — a scraping, a residue, the last tithe of the last harvest. Enough. It had always been about enough. One burning life, freely offered. He had eleven thousand of those in his account, and every single one of them had been an investment, and here, at last, was the return.

He poured the ash over his own chest. It was warm. Of course it was warm. They knew him. Eleven years of feeding, and they knew the taste of his name the way he knew the weight of a coin between two fingers.

"Hear me," he said, and his voice carried further than a dying man's voice had any right to carry, because something below the rock was lifting it, carrying it up through the stone like water climbing toward a wound. "Hear me, you old, you hungry, you *answered*. I have fed you gladly. I have never once regretted. And now they come to seal you away — the key, the child, the sword, all of them, coming to put you back to sleep and call it salvation."

The warmth spread. It did not feel like dying. It felt like being *read* — like the whole of him was being opened to a particular page and held flat under an enormous, patient thumb.

"So I do not pray for my life," Corvel Asse said. "I lost that argument with myself hours ago; the arithmetic is settled; I'm a bookkeeper, we don't lie about arithmetic. And I do not pray for victory, because victory belongs to whoever holds the Door, and I am lying on a ledge talking to the sky."

He grinned. Blood in his teeth. Best grin of his life.

"I pray for *consumption*," he said. "Eat the seal. Eat the key. Eat the Door and the mountain and the valley and the lowlands and the sea beyond it. If Thornfell cannot have a god who answers, then let there be no Thornfell. Let there be no shrines to stand at, no wells to pray beside, no grandmothers, no fevers, no silence. If the choice is between a sleeping god and a full belly, then *wake*, and eat, and do not stop until the whole world is one great green fire with nobody left outside it to be unanswered. I offer myself as the first course. I have been fattening for you for eleven years. I expect you'll find me *tender*."

He meant every word. That was the terrible thing, the thing the histories would never get right if anyone survived to write them: he meant every word, the way a man means a wedding vow, the way a mother means a lullaby. Devotion, taken all the way down, stops resembling cruelty and starts resembling love, and Corvel Asse loved the gods the way the drowned love the sea — completely, gratefully, and with both lungs already filling.

The mountain listened.

He felt it listening the way you feel a held breath. The green veins in the sky stopped pulsing and simply *burned*, steady, attentive, a congregation leaning forward in its pews. Somewhere far above, the humming of the Door changed key — he felt that too, a wrongness in the stone, a note bending flat.

"*Yes,*" whispered Corvel Asse, and closed his eyes, and gave himself to the fire like a coin into a collection plate.

---

What happened next, he experienced from everywhere.

He had expected pain. What he got was expansion. The fire took him apart the way morning takes apart a dream — gently, thoroughly, and without any possibility of reassembly — and where Corvel Asse had been, there was now a great deal of Corvel Asse, distributed widely. He was in the ledge. He was in the scree slope. He was, astonishingly, in the boots of a terrified soldier two switchbacks below, and in the feathers of a crow riding the thermals above the Gate of Teeth, and in the green light itself, which turned out to have texture, appetite, and — he noted with professional interest — remarkably good bookkeeping. Every soul the gods had ever eaten was still in there. Filed. Cross-referenced. Nothing wasted. He approved immensely.

*Oh,* he thought, with the last of his individuality, *it's a library. We're all of us a library. Mother, are you here? Did you get answered after all?*

And the gods — the vast, the starving, the *grateful* — took his prayer the way a dry field takes rain.

Down the mountain, the dead began to rise, green-lit and singing. Up the mountain, the seal on the Door began, very quietly, to crack. In the sky above Thornfell, the clouds pulled aside like curtains, and the fire came through.

*Consume it,* the Hierophant prayed, from everywhere, with everything. *Consume it all. Leave nothing unansw—*

The thought did not finish. Thoughts, at that scale, were rather beneath him.

But the prayer finished itself. The prayer had been going for eleven years, and it knew how it ended.

Far below, in a hidden hut full of old names, something unaged and patient went very still. Far above, a mute child stopped humming and started, for the first time, to scream. And at the Gate of Teeth, an inquisitor with a dead man's blood on her blade looked up at a sky that had begun, softly, tenderly, like a mother opening her arms, to fall.
