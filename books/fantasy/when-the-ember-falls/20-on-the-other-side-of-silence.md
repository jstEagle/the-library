## 20. On the Other Side of Silence

Sera Vane woke to the absence of sound.

Not quiet — she had known quiet all her life. Quiet was the hush before an interrogation, the held breath of a courtroom, the respectful silence of a crowd watching a pyre take. Quiet was full of listening. This was different. This was silence the way a room is empty after furniture has been carried out: the shape of everything that had been there, and nothing in it.

She lay on her back on a slope of blackened scree above the Door's threshold, staring at a sky so clean it looked scrubbed. No green flicker at the horizon. No pressure behind her teeth, that faint divine hum she had lived with for weeks without realizing until now, when it was gone, how much of her headache had been the gods.

"Pell," she said, and her voice came out cracked and small.

The child was beside her, exactly where she'd dragged them both behind the fallen pillar when the mountain began to scream. Pell's eyes were open, fixed on the sky, and their chest rose and fell — shallowly, but rising. The bandage Vane had bound around their ribs yesterday — or what felt like yesterday, or possibly a lifetime ago — was dark with old blood but not fresh. That was something. That was one thing.

"Hey," Vane said, and pushed herself up onto her elbow, and immediately regretted every decision that had led to her having ribs, arms, or a body generally. "Hey, small person. Can you hear me?"

Pell blinked. Turned their head. Looked at her.

And then Pell hummed.

It was the same little tune they always hummed, the wordless wandering melody that made stones glow and standing things remember themselves — except this time nothing happened. The note went out into the air and simply ended, like a coin dropped down a well that never struck water. Pell hummed again, harder, and pressed one small hand flat against the scree beneath them.

Nothing. The stone stayed stone. Grey, dead, ordinary as bread.

Vane watched the child's face do something complicated — confusion first, then fear, then a kind of bewildered grief, like someone who has whistled for a dog their whole life and just learned the dog is gone. Then Pell looked up at her, and their mouth worked, and they said — hoarse, cracked, the second word of their entire speaking life:

"Gone?"

"Yeah," Vane said. She found she couldn't make it gentler than that. "I think so. I think they're all gone."

* * *

Getting down from the scree took most of the morning, mostly because Vane refused to admit how badly she was hurt until her left leg simply stopped negotiating and folded under her on the third switchback. She sat down hard in the ash, swore with the fluency of fifteen years in the Inquisition — a vocabulary she had always claimed was rhetorical, purely professional, and which Pell now heard deployed at full ceremonial length — and then let the child help her up.

Pell was nine years old, half her size, stabbed through the side two days ago shielding a man who wasn't there anymore, and they bore her weight without complaint, humming softly out of habit and then stopping each time, surprised anew by the silence.

"You know," Vane said, leaning on the small shoulder, taking one careful step and then another, "in the Court's training manuals, there's an entire chapter on field triage. I memorized it. Distinctions between mortal wounds and grievous ones. Proper use of tourniquets. Not once — not *once* — did it cover 'lean on the wounded child.' I feel the manuals have failed me."

Pell made a sound that might, in a less silent world, have been a laugh.

The pass below the Door was unrecognizable. Where the Court's last army had been arrayed — banners, braziers, the Hierophant's guard in their ember-lacquered armor — there was now only a field of grey ash shaped vaguely like a battlefield, drifting and settling in the wind. Here and there a helmet. A spearhead fused to its shaft. A brazier overturned, its coals cold. No bodies. Whatever the Door had done when Mara turned the key from within, it had not left the Court's faithful so much as *unwritten* them.

Vane stood at the edge of it for a long time, Pell's hand in hers, and searched herself for triumph and found none. She had spent her whole life believing that justice wore a uniform and answered to a writ. She had burned the uniform on a mountainside two days ago, turned her blade on her own order, become an outlaw in truth — and now the law she'd betrayed was ash, and the gods she'd doubted were gone, and she was standing in the ruin of everything with a wounded child and a dead man's debt and no idea what any of it had been *for*, except that it had to be.

"Mara," Pell said.

"I know," Vane said. "We'll look."

They looked.

* * *

They looked for three days.

They walked the length of the burnt vale, past the still-warm corpses of pilgrims who had prayed too loudly — except now, touching them, Vane found them cold, finally cold, the last heat of the gods' appetite drained out of them overnight. She said words over them. Old words, Court words, and then, feeling foolish, plainer ones. *You deserved better than to be a harvest.* It wasn't a rite. It would have to do.

They climbed back to the Door itself, because Pell insisted, pulling at her sleeve with a stubbornness Vane recognized and resented, being well acquainted with it in mirrors.

The Door was closed.

That was the only way to describe it, though *closed* was generous. There was a place in the mountain's heart where a passage had been, and now there was rock — seamless, ancient-looking, older than the shrine-states, older than Thornfell, as if the Door had never existed and the mountain had merely remembered its own face. Vane put her palm against it. Cold stone. Nothing more.

"He sealed it from the inside," she said. Her voice echoed strangely off the rock, and then didn't echo at all, as if even echoes had grown tired. "That idiot. That brave, selfish, *idiot* smuggler."

Pell pressed both hands and then their forehead against the stone. Hummed. Nothing. They kept their head there a long moment, and when they pulled back their eyes were wet but their jaw was set in a way Vane had seen once before — on the day in the refugee column when soldiers of the Court had come for the god-touched child, and Pell had stepped forward instead of hiding.

"He knew," Pell said. Third word. Fourth. Words were coming slowly now, like a spring thaw, one stone at a time. "Before. He knew."

"Yes," Vane said. Because lying to a child who had felt the gods' hunger directly seemed worse than cruelty; it seemed blasphemy, in a world that had just run out of gods to blaspheme. "I think he knew for a long time. Maybe since he was your age. Maybe since before."

"What we do?" Pell asked.

And that, Sera Vane thought, was the question, wasn't it. What does an outlaw do when the law she broke no longer exists? What does an inquisitor do when there is nothing left to inquire into? What does anyone do in a country that officially stopped existing, whose borders were lines drawn in fire and whose fire had just gone out?

"We go down," she said. "Downhill is a direction. It's the one thing I'm sure of."

* * *

The first village they reached was called Harrow's Bend, though the sign had been burned and someone had nailed up a plank reading, in uneven charcoal letters, HARROWS BEND, NO SHRINE, NO COURT, NO LORD, BRING YOUR OWN BREAD.

"They've been busy," Vane said.

The village looked like every village in Thornfell looked now, she supposed: half its houses scorched, the shrine at its center toppled and its stones — she noticed this with a small, strange pang — carefully stacked rather than smashed. Someone had decided the gods' bones deserved respect even in death. A woman was in the square teaching a dozen children their letters using a slate and a piece of the toppled shrine as a chalk. A man was arguing cheerfully with his neighbor about whether the mill belonged to everyone now or just to whoever could fix it, and the argument had the comfortable rhythm of an argument that would be settled by shared work rather than decree.

No one wore colors. No one knelt. No one, Vane realized, had looked at her sword with anything but mild curiosity — and when the mill-argument man saw it, he said, "Blacksmith? Ours died in the burning. Could you spare it? We'd pay in bread."

"It's an inquisitor's blade," Vane said automatically, and then heard how it sounded, and added, "Formerly."

"Formerly's fine," the man said. "Everything's formerly now." He said it without bitterness, almost with relief, the way you'd say *the fever's broken*. "Name's Aldous. You'll want food, and your small one wants sleep, and you want someone to look at that leg, which you've been favoring since you crested the hill like a man pretending he isn't favoring a leg. We've a herb-wife. Well. We've a woman who knows herbs and has opinions. Close enough."

For the first time in longer than Vane could measure, someone fed her without asking what she believed.

* * *

She slept eleven hours and woke furious about it, the way soldiers do, and then lay in the straw of the herb-wife's back room and listened to the silence and let herself grieve.

Because that was the other thing nobody told you about the end of the world: it kept going afterward. There were meals to eat and legs to heal and children to feed, and grief did not get a private chamber with a door. It sat at the table with you. It passed you the bread.

Mara Joubert had been — what? She ran the ledger of him in her head while the morning light crawled across the rafters. A thief. A liar. A guide who charged fairly and trusted no one and had stolen a grimoire from the only person who ever taught him anything. A man who executed a betrayer on a frozen pass outside any law, and had come down from that pass with frostbite in his eyes. A man who had been chosen as a key before he could walk, whose whole life had been a preparation he never consented to, and who had spent every mile of their journey insisting — loudly, bitterly, hilariously — that he owed nothing to anyone and intended to die rich and anonymous beyond the mountains.

And who had walked into the dark anyway, and turned the key, and never once, not at the very end, asked her to tell anyone what he'd done.

"No grave," she said aloud to the empty room. "He doesn't even get a grave."

The herb-wife, whose name was Bess and whose opinions were as advertised, appeared in the doorway with a poultice and no sympathy whatsoever. "Graves are for people who stay put," Bess said. "Smugglers don't stay put. Stop moping and give me the leg."

"It's my leg."

"And I'm the one with the comfrey. Life is unfair. Hold still."

Pain, Vane reflected, was at least honest company. She held still.

* * *

They stayed at Harrow's Bend through the turning of the season, because Pell needed it and because Vane discovered, to her considerable alarm, that she was good at something new.

It started when the villagers began coming to her with disputes. Word traveled — word always traveled, even in a country that no longer existed, perhaps especially there — that there was a former inquisitor lodging at Bess's, one who had fought the Court, one who knew the old law and the shrine-law and the difference between them. And the disputes came: the mill question, unresolved and metastasizing. A boundary argument between two farms whose boundary-stones had glowed during the bad months and then stopped, leaving both farmers certain the stones had meant something. A widow whose late husband had sworn a prayer-debt to a neighbor and who wanted to know, in a world with no gods to collect it, whether debts to heaven transferred to earth.

"They used to settle this with a priest," the widow said helplessly.

"The priests are ash," Vane said.

"Well, yes. Hence."

So Vane sat in the square on a bench that someone had sawn level for the purpose, and listened, and weighed, and decided. She used the old forms where they were good — and some of them were good; fifteen years of Inquisition had taught her that the scaffolding of justice could hold up a house even when the roof of faith had burned away. She discarded the rest. No oaths by flame. No trials by ordeal. No prayers entered into evidence.

"Say it plainly," she told the two farmers, on the fourth day, when they tried to invoke their glowing boundary-stone as a witness. "The stones can't testify anymore. So it's just us, and the truth, and me. Say it plainly and I'll judge it plainly."

And they did. And it was — there was no other word for it — *human*. Slow, grumbling, imperfect, human justice, kept alive by hand like a fire in wet wood.

Pell sat beside her on the bench those days, healed enough now to be bored, and took to adjudicating the smaller cases — who owned the lost knife, whose turn it was at the well — with grave gestures and a vocabulary that grew by three or four words a week. The children adored them. The children had decided Pell was a hero, on account of the stabbing, and Pell bore the fame with the modesty of a cat accepting tribute.

One evening, working the bellows in Aldous's forge — they had given her the inquisitor's blade to reforge into a plow-blade, and she had watched it go into the fire with a feeling she declined to examine — Pell tugged her sleeve and pointed at the eastern hills.

"Door-way," Pell said. One of the compound words they were inventing lately, folding meanings together like a child folding paper.

Vane followed the small finger. On the ridge above the valley, where the old road climbed toward the pass, there was a figure. Small, at this distance. Standing very still at the crest, where the snow was beginning to fall — the first snow of the season, fat and slow and silent.

Her heart did something complicated and painful.

Then the figure moved, and she saw it was only a shepherd counting his flock home, and she let her breath out slowly, and told herself the tightness in her chest was smoke from the forge.

But Pell was still looking at the ridge, and Pell's head was tilted, the way it tilted when they listened to things other people couldn't hear. And after a moment the child hummed — that same little wandering tune, the one that had made stones glow and gods lean close — soft, almost absent, a habit more than a hope.

And far up on the ridge, faint as breath on glass, one of the boundary-stones of the old road flickered.

Just once. Just barely. Green as a memory of a flame.

Pell lowered the tune and looked up at Vane with wide eyes, and said, very quietly, in the gathering snow:

"He listens."

* * *

Vane stood in the falling snow for a long time, looking at a ridge where there was nothing but a shepherd and a stone and the dark coming on.

She thought about thresholds. About doors that open only for one who gives up what they most want to keep. About a man who had wanted, all his life, to be free and unnamed, walking out the other side of a mountain into exactly that — and what such a man might do with eternity if eternity happened to come with a view of every doorway in the world.

"He always did know every path through these mountains," she said to the snow, to the stone, to whoever was listening, which was no one, which was perhaps someone. "Of course he'd find the loophole."

Pell slipped a small cold hand into hers.

Together they turned back toward the lights of Harrow's Bend, where supper waited and the bench stood ready and the country, nameless and godless and free, was learning — one dispute, one loaf, one leveled bench at a time — how to begin again.
