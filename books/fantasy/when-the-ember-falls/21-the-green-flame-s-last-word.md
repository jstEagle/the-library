## 21. The Green Flame's Last Word

The first thing Mara learned about being dead — or whatever this was, this half-and-half existence, this being poured into the cracks of the world like mortar between stones — was that doors had opinions about him.

Not all doors. A barn door swung open for him readily enough, and a tavern door in the village of Lowmere, and once, memorably, the door of a privy that he very much wished had kept its opinions to itself. But thresholds — true thresholds, the liminal places where one thing ended and another began — those he could not cross. He could stand in them. He could stand in them forever, if he liked, which was occasionally useful when it rained. He could feel them humming under his feet like plucked harp strings, each one a small echo of the great Door at Thornfell's heart, the one he had turned from the inside, the one that had closed behind him into darkness and taken his mortality with it as payment.

He had known the price going in. That was the thing people never believed afterward, when he told it — and he told it rarely, because mostly there was no one to tell it *to*, and because the telling made his chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with lungs anymore. He had stood before the Door and it had shown him the escape he'd dreamed of since he was seven years old: salt roads south, a name no one knew, a life where nobody needed him for anything. And he had looked at that dream the way you look at a coat you've outgrown, and he had said, out loud, to a slab of mountain older than language:

"No. I'll keep something else."

And the Door, which had been waiting four hundred years for someone to say exactly that, had opened from the inside.

* * *

The shrine sat on a shoulder of the pass above Lowmere, small enough that most travelers mistook it for a shepherd's hut until they noticed the old stone basin and the soot-blackened niche where an idol used to stand. The idol was gone now. Mara had carried it down the mountain himself, three years ago, and left it in the millpond, where it could do no one any harm and where the trout seemed to enjoy it.

In its place he'd set nothing at all. An empty niche. An empty basin. A roof that didn't leak, because he'd re-thatched it himself, twice, learning each time that his hands worked fine but tired strangely, that hunger came to him only when he let himself remember what it felt like, and that snow fell *through* him if he wasn't paying attention, which was embarrassing more often than he cared to admit.

The sign over the lintel he had carved himself, in careful letters, because his mistress had beaten penmanship into him with a switch and some lessons outlive everything, even death:

**PRAY IF YOU LIKE. NOTHING IS LISTENING.**

Pilgrims hated it. Pilgrims loved it. There was no predicting which.

* * *

On a grey morning in late autumn, with the first real snow of the season dusting the pass like flour sifted over dark bread, a woman climbed up to the shrine carrying a basket of turnips and a grievance.

Mara heard her coming long before he saw her — he heard everything on the mountain now, every footfall and stream-splash and marmot whistle, the whole pass murmuring to itself like an old man reading aloud — and he had time to put the kettle on. The kettle was another thing he'd learned: fire still obeyed him, gently, the way a dog obeys a hand it trusts. He did not light fires the way the gods had lit their green pillars. He lit them small and orange and ordinary, and he was scrupulous about it, the way a reformed drunk is scrupulous about small beer.

The woman was Maren Ostrow, widow, turnip farmer, and the closest thing the mountain had to a mayor, mostly because she was louder than everyone else and owned two goats. She stamped snow off her boots, set the turnips on the shrine's little table with the air of a woman settling a debt, and squinted at him.

"You're thinner," she said.

"I'm always thinner."

"You're *always* thinner, yes, but today you're thinner than usual." She peered at him. "Have you been eating?"

"Maren, I don't eat."

"You sit at that table when folk bring food and you move food around your plate like a child hiding liver. I've seen you. Don't think I haven't."

"It's polite," Mara said. "It makes people comfortable. There's a difference between eating and seeming to eat, and civilization rests entirely on that difference."

She snorted, which from Maren was applause, and lowered herself onto the bench with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions too. "I've come to complain."

"About the weather?"

"About my neighbor. Tam Wicker. He's been telling folk that his boy's fever broke because Tam prayed at your shrine."

"His boy's fever broke because Tam finally agreed to fetch the herbwoman from Lowmere instead of praying over the child for three days," Mara said. "But I'm glad the boy's well."

"That's not the point. The point is Tam's going around saying the shrine *answered*. You should hear him. 'The empty god,' he calls it. Says it listens in its own way. Says silence is just a kind of listening." She folded her arms. "You want that? Because I remember what happened last time things listened. Half the country burned, and the other half got religion, which was worse."

Mara poured the tea — he drank none of it, but the steam rising off the cup made the morning feel like a morning instead of a vigil — and considered his answer.

"No," he said. "I don't want that."

"Then do something about it."

"What would you have me do? Post a notice?" He gestured at the carved lintel. "I already posted a notice."

"Your notice says nothing's listening. It doesn't say nothing's *answering*. Folk can't tell the difference anymore, Mara. They spent years watching prayers get answered with fire, and now they're so starved for a god that won't eat them that they'll invent one out of an empty stone niche and a man who doesn't age." She leaned forward. "You know what you are to them. Down in the valley. Have you heard what they call you?"

"I try not to."

"The Quiet Keeper. The Door-Warden. Old Mother Ostrow says—" she paused, with dignity, "—that is, *some* say you're the last saint of Thornfell, standing watch so the hungry ones stay shut away."

"And what do you say?"

Maren picked up her tea, blew on it, and looked out at the pass — at the white road winding down toward the lowlands, toward the villages where Vane was teaching law again and children were learning that stones were just stones, wonderful and enough. "I say you're a stubborn man who stole a grimoire and never gave it back, and that saints are mostly stubborn men the story got hold of afterward." She drank. "But I brought turnips, so draw your own conclusions."

* * *

After she left — taking two turnips back, because even generosity has limits — Mara swept the shrine, oiled the hinges of the gate, and did the small maintenance of eternity, which turned out to be mostly sweeping.

That was the joke of it, and he held onto the joke the way a drowning man holds driftwood: he had spent his whole life running from being needed, and now he was bound to a threshold on a cold mountain pass, needed by every traveler who wanted a safe place to sleep and every pilgrim who wanted to touch a stone that a saint had touched. The Door had asked him what he most wanted to keep, and he had thought he understood the question. What he wanted to keep was his freedom, his namelessness, his clean solitude.

What the Door had heard — what the Door, being older and wiser than any god, had always known — was that what he truly wanted to keep was *them*. Vane, scowling over her ledgers in Lowmere. Pell, humming to standing stones that hummed back, harmlessly, sweetly, like a tuning fork remembering music. The whole broken, reborn, godless country on the far side of the mountain he could see but never reach.

So the Door had kept it for him. All of it. Just — over there. Behind glass. Forever.

He had raged about it, the first year. He had stood at the edge of the pass looking down at the lights of Lowmere and cursed the Door in three languages and one invented on the spot. But rage, he discovered, does not keep. Like bread, it goes stale; unlike bread, there's no one to give the stale part to. And somewhere in the second year, sweeping snow off the shrine steps, he had caught himself humming — Pell's tune, the one the child had taught them in the cave, the one that made stones glow — and realized he was content, and been so startled he dropped the broom.

Bittersweet, his mistress would have called it. His mistress, who had waited unaged in her hidden hut for him, who had told him with terrible gentleness that his whole life had been preparation he never consented to. He wondered sometimes if she was still out there, sustained by nothing now, the gods gone quiet in their graves. He wondered if she had aged all at once, like a candle catching up, or simply stopped, like a clock. He had never gone to look. Some doors you leave closed on purpose.

* * *

Toward evening, a traveler came up the pass.

Mara felt him before he saw him — that was new, that was *wrong*, a wrongness like a cold draft under a door that should be sealed — a pressure in the threshold itself, a faint sour note struck on the harp-string under his feet. He set down the broom slowly and watched the figure climb through the snow: a big man, travel-worn, hooded, walking with the deliberate care of someone carrying something breakable.

Or someone who *was* something breakable.

The man stopped at the gate. Pushed back his hood. He had a plain face, forgettable, the kind of face you'd meet in a market and lose by the next stall — except for the eyes, which were the deep patient green of forest shade, of moss on old stone, of a flame that had once answered a dying shepherd's prayer on this very road.

"Well," Mara said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"I am everywhere," the god said, courteous, mild. Its voice was the sound wind makes deciding not to blow. "That is rather the difficulty. When you unmade the channel, you did not unmake us. We are... dispersed. Thinned. A taste of smoke after the fire's out." It looked past him at the shrine, at the empty niche, and something moved in its green eyes that might, in anything with a face less old, have been longing. "We are starving, Door-Warden. Slowly. Painlessly, compared to before. But starving."

"And you came here why? To beg? To bargain? I've had better offers than yours, and I turned down the Hierophant's."

"To ask a question." The god folded its hands, patient as winter. "One question. It is the question we were made from, in the beginning, before the priests and the pyres and the farming of us. Every god is a question that someone needed answered so badly it became a hunger." It tilted its head. "Ours is this: *when the ember falls, and nothing answers — what keeps the dark from being lonely?*"

Mara was quiet a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the thatch. Somewhere below, a cart creaked along the lowland road, and a dog barked at it, and a child laughed at the dog, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which was the whole point, which had cost everything.

"I don't know," he said at last. "People, I suppose. People answering each other. Badly, usually. Late, often. But answering."

The god considered this. Then it smiled — a small, tired, almost human smile, and Mara understood suddenly, with a lurch, that this was what his mistress had seen in them once, long ago, before the sealing: not monsters. Questions. Questions that had waited too long for answers and gone mad with waiting.

"Yes," the god said softly. "That is what we concluded as well. It is why we do not fight the closing of the Door." It stepped back from the threshold, and the sour note under Mara's feet eased, softened, resolved. "Keep your shrine, Door-Warden. Keep your unanswered prayers. They are safer than any answer we ever gave." The green eyes glimmered. "But be warned — we are not the only things that survived the Emberfall. Something else crossed the mountain ahead of me tonight. Something that was *bound* to us, the way you are bound to thresholds. It went down toward the valley. Toward the village where the inquisitor teaches law."

The cold that went through Mara then had nothing to do with the snow.

"It was asking directions," the god added, almost apologetically, "to a grave that isn't there. Yours."

It bowed — courteously, starving, old — and walked back down into the dusk, growing fainter with each step, until it was only a shadow among shadows, and then not even that.

Mara stood a long time at the edge of his threshold, looking down the pass toward Lowmere, where the lights were coming on one by one, warm and small and mortal, where Sera Vane was probably grading some poor apprentice's handwriting with a red pencil and a sigh.

Then he went inside, banked the fire, took down the grimoire — his mistress's grimoire, the theft that had started everything, its sealed pages open now to him alone — and began, for the first time in three years, to look for a way *out*.

The kettle, unnoticed, boiled over.
