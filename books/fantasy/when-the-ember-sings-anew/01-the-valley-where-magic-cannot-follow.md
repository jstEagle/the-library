## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The rain came down the valley like it always did in autumn — sideways, cold, smelling of wet stone and woodsmoke — and Jonah Iversen stood in the doorway of the Wayfarer's Rest and watched it hammer the mud of the market square into something that looked like porridge.

"Contract's still on the table," said Marta Voss, the guild clerk, not looking up from her ledger. She was a small woman with ink-stained fingers and the patience of someone who had spent thirty years telling armed men to wait their turn. "Wool caravan out of Highmere. Wolves have gotten bold since the fires up north. Two weeks' work, maybe less."

"Wolves," Jonah said.

"And whatever's been scaring the wolves." She turned a page. "Shepherds say something big moved through the high passes last month. Left tracks they didn't want to describe."

Jonah leaned against the doorframe and let the rain-wind touch his face. Inside the Rest, the fire crackled, and old Tam Osgood was already three cups deep into his morning ale and telling anyone who'd listen about the year the river froze solid enough to drive carts across. It was the same story he told every October. It got longer every year.

"Marta," Jonah said, "how many contracts have I taken for you?"

She looked up at that, eyes narrowing over her spectacles. "In eleven years? Forty-one. Forty-two if you count the thing with the mill pond, which I do, because you charged me for it."

"And how many were wolves?"

"Three." She smiled without warmth. "You're hoping this one isn't wolves either."

"I'm hoping this one is wolves." He pushed off the doorframe. "Wolves are honest. They want meat, they take meat, they die if you make them. Everything else wants something you can't put a blade in."

Marta wrote his name in the ledger — she'd started writing before he finished agreeing, which told him what she thought of his chances of saying no — and slid the contract across the table. He signed it. The ink was the cheap kind that bled, and his name came out looking slightly drowned.

*Jonah Iversen.* Thirty-four years old, or thereabouts; he'd been left at the valley shrine as an infant with nothing but a blanket and a name stitched into the corner, so even his age was somebody else's guess. Six foot two, built like the door he'd been leaning on, hands that had broken things professionally for half his life. And one oath, sworn at nineteen, that bound him to a god who hadn't spoken in six years.

He folded the contract into his coat and stepped out into the rain.

* * *

The shrine sat above the village where the terraced fields gave up and the pines took over — a low stone building older than any record, roofed in slate gone green with moss, its doorway worn smooth by centuries of shoulders. The Ember Valley held maybe four hundred souls across five villages, and every one of them passed that shrine on their way somewhere, and most of them nodded at it, and almost none of them went in.

Jonah went in.

It wasn't devotion, exactly. Or it was, but devotion the way a man keeps visiting a house where someone used to live. The interior was one room, bare except for a hearth that never went out — the ember-shrine itself, coals banked in a ring of black iron, glowing a dull orange that never brightened and never died. No idol. No icon. The god of the valley had never wanted one, or so the old stories said. Just fire, kept alive, tended by whoever swore the keeping.

For two hundred years, that had been one person at a time. For the last six years, it had been Jonah, tending a fire for a god who no longer answered.

He knelt at the hearth's edge and fed it two splits of pine, watching them catch. The ritual was simple: feed the fire, speak the greeting, wait.

*"Ember-father, hearth-mother, breath between the stones. Your keeper asks: are you there?"*

Silence. There was always silence. But the oath in his chest — a warm knot behind his sternum, a second heartbeat he'd carried for fifteen years — gave its usual faint pulse, like a sleeper turning over. Not an answer. Just proof the connection hadn't died entirely. Proof that somewhere, in whatever country gods lived in when they lived at all, the thing he'd sworn himself to was still *there*, the way a lamp behind a shuttered window is there.

Six years ago, the voice had simply stopped. Mid-conversation, almost. Jonah had been twenty-eight, coming back from a contract on the northern ridge, bleeding from a gash in his arm, and he'd asked the god to help him find the trail home in the fog — a small thing, a nothing-prayer, the kind of request a keeper made daily. And the presence that always leaned back against him, warm and vast and faintly amused, had just... withdrawn. Like a hand pulled off a shoulder.

The oath stayed. The strength stayed — that was the cruel part. Whatever the god had poured into him at nineteen stayed locked in his body: the endurance, the speed, the way steel felt light as wood in his grip. He could work from dawn to midnight and feel it the next morning like a man half his age. He could carry a loaded cart out of a flooded ford alone. The villagers called it his blessing and meant it kindly, and Jonah thanked them and never explained that a blessing with no giver attached starts to feel less like a gift and more like a debt being collected slowly.

He fed the fire another split and sat back on his heels.

"You know what Marta said today?" he told the coals. "Tracks nobody wanted to describe. In the high passes." The coals popped softly. "I'm going to go look at it tomorrow. If it's a bear, I'll skin it and bring you the fat for the lamps. If it's something else—" He stopped. Rubbed his jaw. "Well. You'd tell me if it were something else. Wouldn't you."

The fire burned. The silence held its shape, patient as stone.

That was the arrangement now, and had been for six years: he talked, the god listened or didn't, and Jonah kept the ember alive because the alternative — letting it gutter out, letting the last thread go slack — was unthinkable in a way he couldn't articulate even to himself. Breaking the oath would free him. He knew that. Every mercenary in the lowlands who'd sworn to a silent god faced the same choice eventually: keep serving an absence, or speak the words of release and be an ordinary man again.

He'd drafted the release-words in his head a hundred times. He'd never once said them aloud.

Jonah stood, brushed the ash from his knees, and let himself out into the rain.

* * *

The Rest filled up proper by sundown, the way it always did on contract-nights, because a man taking a job bought the first round and everyone in the valley had opinions about how he should do it. Jonah claimed his usual table — back corner, wall at his back, sightline to both doors, habits carved too deep to bother unlearning — and let the room happen around him.

Tam Osgood had migrated from the frozen-river story to the wolf problem, which suited the crowd fine. "Highmere shepherd swears the tracks were the size of dinner plates," Tam announced, holding his cup at the level of the alleged print. "Dinner plates! And spaced wrong, he says. Too far apart, like the beast was walking on stilts."

"Walking on stilts," repeated Bree Callow, the farrier, deadpan. "A stilt-wolf."

"You laugh. My grandfather saw the ridge-devil in '61 and he wasn't a man given to—"

"Your grandfather also swore he wrestled the ferryman's wife, and the ferryman was standing right there."

The room laughed, and Jonah drank his ale and let it wash past him. This was the texture of his life, and he knew it, and mostly he was grateful for it: these people, this noise, the reliable rhythm of contracts and seasons. They trusted him. That was worth more than coin, though the coin was decent too. When the Kellam boys' father had died in the rockslide, it was Jonah who'd hauled the beam off and carried the man down four switchbacks without stopping. When the flood took the lower mill, it was Jonah who stood chest-deep in the current holding the sluice-gate shut for six hours until the carpenters could brace it. Nobody asked him why he could do these things anymore. Eleven years will sand the wonder off anything.

"You'll want company on the high pass," Bree said, dropping into the chair across from him. She was fifty, forearms like ship cable, and the only person in the valley who spoke to him like he was a person rather than an institution. "Ridge trails in the wet season, solo? You'll break your neck and lie there polite as you please until spring."

"I don't break my neck."

"No. Funny, that." She studied him over her cup. "Eleven years of stupid risks and not a scar to show past the ones you walked in with. Most men would've lost a finger by now. Lost worse."

"Lucky," Jonah said.

"Mm." Bree had known him since he was a green recruit doing escort work for the wool trains, and she had never once believed a single convenient answer he'd given her, and she had never once pressed. It was one of the great quiet kindnesses of his life. "Lucky. Well. Take the mule track, not the goat path, lucky man. And come back and drink with us after, because Tam's starting a new story about the winter of '58 and someone has to keep count of the lies."

"I always keep count of the lies."

"That's why we keep you."

She clapped his shoulder and went back to the bar, and Jonah sat with his ale and watched the fire in the big hearth and felt the small warm pulse of his oath answering it, twin embers, and thought: *this is enough. This is a good life.*

He thought it deliberately, the way a man checks a lock he already knows is fastened. Because underneath the thought, in the hour after the crowd thinned and the benches emptied and Tam was snoring in his chair, there was the other thing. The true ledger. The one that read:

*No wife. No children. No kin. A god who doesn't speak. A house you built yourself because no one would build it with you. Friends who love you the way people love a bridge — gratefully, and from a distance, and without ever wondering what it's like to be crossed over all day and stand empty all night.*

Rootless. Marta had used the word once, years ago, not meaning cruelty — *you're reliable but rootless, Iversen, like a good post* — and he'd laughed, and he'd never forgotten it, because it was exactly right. A post. Something driven deep into the ground and useful and alone.

He finished his ale. Outside, the rain had softened to mist, and the valley lay dark under it, five villages' worth of banked hearths and sleeping livestock, and above it all the shrine ember he'd tend again at dawn, and the day after, and the day after that, until one day he wouldn't, and the valley would nod at the shrine a little longer and then hire some other luckless soul to keep the fire.

Jonah paid his tab, nodded to the barkeep, and walked home through the mist to the stone house he'd raised with his own impossible hands, and slept the dreamless sleep the oath granted him, which was the closest thing to peace he had.

* * *

He left for the high pass two days later, in the grey hour before dawn, with a week's provisions, a good axe, and the mule track under his boots. The first day was nothing — wet pines, grouse exploding from the bracken, the valley dropping away behind him in folds of green and slate. He made camp in a shepherd's hut, lit a fire, spoke the evening greeting to his silent god out of habit, and slept.

The second day, he found the tracks.

Bree had been right to send him up the mule track, because the goat path would have put him directly in front of them, and Jonah was glad — profoundly, unexpectedly glad — to have seen them first from forty feet above instead of standing in them. They ran along the streambed for a quarter mile and then cut uphill through the scree, and they were, as Tam had reported, the size of dinner plates. Four-toed. Spaced too wide for any natural stride. And here was the detail the shepherds hadn't managed to say out loud, the thing that had dried up in their throats:

There were no claw marks. None. Every predator in the world left claws. These prints were smooth as a man's boot, and around each one, the frost of the early morning had melted in a perfect circle, as if the ground itself remembered being warm.

Jonah crouched at the edge of the trail and felt his oath stir.

Not the usual drowsy pulse. Something else — a heat behind his sternum, faint but rising, like a coal breathed upon. In fifteen years of service, the god's power had answered exactly twice without his asking: once when a rockslide came down on the Kellam road, and once — he'd never told anyone — the night six years ago when the voice went silent, a single flare of alarm, like a shout cut off mid-word.

This was closer to that second one than the first.

"Well," Jonah said quietly, to the tracks, to the mountain, to the god behind his breastbone. "You could tell me what this is. Any time. Truly. I'm standing right here."

The heat pulsed once more — and then, unmistakably, *flickered*, the way a flame flickers when something passes between it and the wind. Not an answer. More like a warning shot from a long way off, or a hand pressed flat against the inside of a door.

Jonah stood, checked his axe, and followed the tracks uphill into the cloud.

They led toward the pass. Toward the valley's edge — the invisible line where the Ember Valley ended and magic, which could not follow, did not go. And the tracks, he noted with a cold settling in his gut, did not stop at that line.

They crossed straight over it, heading out into the world beyond.

Nothing crossed that line. Nothing ever had. That was the whole point of the valley, the covenant older than any kingdom: within the Ember's bounds, magic could not follow, and so the hunted and the haunted and the sworn came here to be ordinary, and the ordinary stayed, and the world outside kept its monsters to itself.

Jonah stood at the line as the mist thickened around him, one boot on valley stone and one on the naked rock beyond, feeling his oath burn like a struck match in his chest.

Behind him, far below, a shepherd's dog began to bark. Ahead, in the cloud, something enormous exhaled.

And then, thin and strange on the wind, drifting down from the pass where no road ran and no village stood — a sound he would spend the rest of his life trying to forget, and fail:

A child, crying.
