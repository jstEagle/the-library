## 2. A Knock at the Door

The storm came down off the northern crags an hour past sundown, the kind of storm that made even the Ember Valley feel like it had edges.

Jonah Iversen heard it coming while he was still mending a bootlace by the hearth — a long rolling pressure in his ears, like the valley clearing its throat. Twenty-two years living under the shadow of the ash cliffs had taught him the difference between weather that meant to pass through and weather that meant to stay. This one meant to stay.

He got up and did what he always did before a bad night: checked the shutters, banked the fire, set the iron poker within reach of the door instead of the hearth. Old habits from a life where doors got kicked in more often than they got knocked on. Then he sat back down, picked up the lace, and told himself the knot he was tying was for the boot and not for his hands.

Outside, the wind found every gap in the eaves and played them like a reed instrument. Rain hit the roof in sheets. Somewhere up-valley, a shutter banged itself to splinters against a wall, and a dog — Hesta's dog, probably — began a low, continuous complaint that would last until morning.

Jonah was on his second cup of barley tea and halfway through deciding whether tonight was a night for sleep or for sitting awake listening to the weather, when someone knocked at his door.

Three knocks. Small ones.

He went still.

Nobody walked to Jonah's house in weather like this unless they were desperate or drunk, and the drunk came up from the tavern loud enough to wake the dead. Nobody knocked three times, soft as a bird landing. And nobody — this was the part that put him on his feet with the poker in hand — nobody stood outside long enough to knock *twice*, because the first knock should have been enough to tell any sane person the house belonged to Jonah Iversen, who had once thrown a tax assessor over his own fence for raising his voice.

The third knock came anyway. Patient. Almost polite.

Jonah crossed the room, set his shoulder near the doorframe out of habit, and pulled the door open a hand's width.

The storm tried to come inside all at once — rain, wind, a fistful of leaves. And standing in the middle of it, on his doorstep, barefoot on the wet stone, was a child.

Perhaps eight years old. Small for eight, maybe. A scrap of a grey cloak, soaked through, hanging off narrow shoulders. Dark hair plastered flat to a thin face. The child looked up at him — had to look a long way up; Jonah was a big man and knew it — and did not flinch, did not shiver, did not do anything at all except look at him with eyes so dark brown they were nearly black, steady as well-water.

"Off with you," Jonah said over the wind. "Tavern's down the road. Hesta'll feed you."

The child didn't move. Rain ran off the little chin. Those black eyes stayed on his face the way a hawk watches a field — not afraid, exactly. *Assessing.*

"I said—"

The child spoke.

The words were nothing. That was Jonah's first thought: *nothing*, syllables shaped wrong, vowels in places no valley tongue put them, a rhythm that rose and fell like something sung rather than said. It wasn't Lowmark. It wasn't Highmark, or the river-trade cant, or the clipped border speech of the freeholds. It wasn't anything. It was language the way a knife is a tool — recognizably one, and entirely wrong for the hand holding it.

And underneath the nothing of it, deep down in some cellar of himself he hadn't opened in twenty years, something *answered*.

Jonah's grip tightened on the poker.

Because he understood — not the words, not quite, but the *shape* of them, the way a man who has spent his life around horses understands the difference between a whinny of pain and one of greeting without knowing how he knows. The sounds meant something like *door*. Something like *cold*, though the child's voice carried no complaint in it, only statement. And the last phrase — two syllables, falling, then rising — landed in Jonah's chest with a weight that had no business belonging to a stranger's words.

It sounded, God help him, like the opening line of his own oath.

"Say that again," Jonah said.

The child said it again. Same cadence. Same impossible grammar. And again that cold thread of familiarity ran through him, words surfacing in him like drowned things rising — not meanings, but *echoes*. He'd spent six years of his youth drilled in the liturgy of the Ember Oath, kneeling on cold stone before a shrine while old Brother Callow droned the binding-forms in the god's own tongue, the tongue no living mouth used anymore except in prayer. He'd memorized sounds he never understood. He'd assumed they were decoration. Dead ritual words, worn smooth by centuries of repetition, meaning nothing to anyone.

They hadn't been decoration. They'd been *related* to whatever this child was saying. Cousins, maybe. Or parent and child.

"How do you know that speech?" he asked. "Who taught you?"

The child cocked its head. Considered him. Then held up one small hand, palm out, fingers spread — and spoke again, slower, pointing at each finger as though counting off, and this time one word repeated itself twice, and Jonah felt the shape of it settle into place like a key finding a lock:

*Alone.*

His stomach dropped.

"No," he said. "No, absolutely not. Where's your mother? Your father? Someone walked you up this road."

The child shook its head once, a small economical motion, and pointed down the dark road toward the village — then drew the flat of the hand across the gesture, a negation so clean and deliberate it might have been a sign from a soldier's code. *Not from there.*

"Then from where?"

The child pointed up. North. Toward the crags. Toward the pass that hadn't been passable since the rockslide three winters ago, and beyond it, beyond the valley's edge entirely, toward everything the rest of the world insisted the Ember Valley was a myth about.

"You climbed down from the pass," Jonah said flatly. "In this rain. Barefoot."

The child looked down at its own feet as if noticing them for the first time. They were blue-white with cold, cut in two or three places, standing in a thin spreading pool of rainwater on his doorstep. The child seemed mildly interested in this, the way one might be interested in someone else's misfortune.

Something in Jonah's chest did a thing he didn't authorize.

"Get inside," he said. "Before you die on my step and I spend the rest of winter explaining it to Hesta."

The child stepped past him into the house without hesitation — walked straight in, small bare feet leaving dark prints on his floorboards, took in the single room with one slow sweep of those black eyes (hearth, bed, table, shrine in the corner, poker still in Jonah's fist), and then stood in the middle of the floor dripping, waiting, as if the house had been prepared for the arrival and Jonah were merely the staff.

Jonah shut the door on the storm and stood with his back against it for a moment, which was cowardly and he knew it, but it bought him three seconds to think.

*Children don't come down from the pass. Children don't speak dead tongues. Children don't look at armed men like they're measuring them for a coffin.* Every instinct he'd sharpened over twenty years of taking coin for violence was telling him the same thing: *this is not a lost lamb, Jonah. This is something running from something, and both halves of that sentence are trouble.*

But the feet were still blue. That was the problem. Whatever else the child was, the feet were blue, and the lips were going pale, and the shivering — delayed, strange, arriving only now that the wind was shut out — had started in earnest.

Jonah sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to a fate he could see coming from a long way off, hung the poker back on its peg, and knelt by the hearth.

"Boots off," he said. "Well. You haven't any." He dragged his spare blanket off the chest at the foot of the bed — coarse wool, smelled of cedar and old campaigns — and wrapped it around the child's shoulders. "Sit. Don't touch the shrine."

The child sat. Cross-legged, right in front of the fire, pulling the blanket tight. And Jonah fetched the basin, warmed water over the fire, and — feeling absurd, a mercenary with blood on his ledger playing nursemaid — washed the cuts on the small feet and bound them in clean linen strips while the child watched him work with that unnerving, unblinking attention.

"You're not afraid of me," Jonah observed. "Everyone's afraid of me at first. It's the size. Hesta says I have a face like a dropped anvil."

The child said something in the dead language. It was short. Given the faint upward curve at the corner of the small mouth, Jonah suspected it was not a compliment.

"Don't get clever with me. I don't know what you're saying."

*I know,* the tone of the reply seemed to say.

He finished the bandaging, set the basin aside, and made tea — proper tea, with the honey he saved, because apparently he was that man now — and the child drank it in small precise sips, holding the cup in both hands, watching the fire with an expression Jonah slowly recognized because he'd worn it himself, once, in another life: the expression of someone recalculating whether they were safe, and provisionally concluding yes, and hating how much they wanted that answer to be true.

The rain hammered on. The wind screamed down the chimney and made the flames duck. And in the corner of the room, the shrine — a plain stone bowl, an unlit candle, a carved mark of interlocking circles that no priest had ever explained to him because there had been no priests left to explain anything — sat silent as it had sat silent for twenty-two years.

The child's eyes drifted to it. Stayed there.

Then the child turned, looked at Jonah, and spoke — and this time the words were slow, deliberate, spaced out like a man choosing stepping stones across a river, and Jonah felt each one land somewhere under his breastbone:

Two syllables he knew from the oath-liturgy — *the god-titles*, Callow had called them, names of address. A word that had surfaced in him earlier, *alone*. And a final word, rising, questioning, that he did not know at all and that nevertheless made the hair stand up on his arms, because the child asked it while looking directly at the empty shrine, and there was only one question anyone had ever asked about that stone bowl in twenty years of silence.

*"Where is it?"*

Or maybe: *"Why doesn't it answer?"*

Or maybe — and this was the reading that made Jonah set down his own cup very carefully, so as not to crack the clay —

*"Where did it go?"*

"I don't know," Jonah said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "That's the whole story. Nobody knows. It stopped. Twenty-two years ago, midwinter, every oath-bearer in the valley woke up hollow, and it never spoke again. There were men who walked into the ash wastes looking for it. None came back. So we swept the shrines and got on with our lives, because that's what people here do. We get on with it."

The child listened to all of this — none of which it could have understood a word of — with the grave attention of a magistrate hearing testimony.

Then it reached out one small bandaged foot and touched the toe, gently, to the hem of Jonah's trouser leg. Just that. Contact, brief, deliberate. And it spoke one more time, softly, almost kindly, and the two syllables it used were ones Jonah knew, because he'd said them himself ten thousand times over twenty years of prayers that no one answered:

His own name. Not *Jonah* — the oath-form of it, the name as it appeared in the liturgy, *I-ver-sen, sworn-of-the-ember* — spoken correctly, in the god's own grammar, by a barefoot eight-year-old who had climbed down from an impossible pass in an impossible storm.

Jonah sat very still.

Outside, far off, half-drowned beneath the rain, something that might have been thunder rolled along the ridge — except the ridge faced away from the storm front, and thunder did not repeat itself four times, evenly spaced, moving closer.

The child heard it too. Jonah saw it happen: the stillness that came over the small face, the way the black eyes flicked to the window, the tiny hands drawing the blanket tighter. For the first time since the door had opened, the child looked — not afraid, never afraid — *braced*.

It looked at Jonah. It pointed at the floor. *Here.* Then it pressed the flat of its hand against its own chest, and said one word, urgent now, the same word it had counted on its fingers at the threshold:

*Alone.*

"Not alone," Jonah heard himself say. His mouth had apparently made decisions without consulting him. "Tonight, you're not alone. Whatever's out there comes through that door, it comes through me first."

The child studied him for a long moment, firelight moving on the dark eyes. Then it nodded, once, businesslike — a general accepting a report — and lay down on the hearthstones, curled into the warm wool, and closed its eyes as though the matter were settled and sleep were simply the next item on an agenda.

Jonah sat up all night with the poker across his knees and the storm screaming against the walls, listening to the small, even breathing beside his hearth and to the ridgeline, where the thing that was not thunder had stopped moving.

It hadn't gone away. It was waiting.

So, he realized somewhere in the smallest hours, was he.
