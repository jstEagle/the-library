## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The archivist lived at the top of the valley, where the river came down cold off the shoulders of the mountains and the last farm gave up trying. Her name was Maren Oduya, and she had been blind since before Jonah was born, which everyone in Ember Valley agreed was the only reason she'd stayed sane in a house built entirely out of books.

Jonah carried Wren up the switchback trail himself. The child still wouldn't ride a horse — had screamed the one time he'd tried to lift her into the saddle, a thin silent scream with no sound in it, hands clawing at his arms until he set her down and she pressed both palms flat against the dirt like she was checking that it was real. So they walked, three days from his cabin, and Jonah carried her on his back when the trail steepened, and she rode him the way other children rode ponies: without thanks, with total confidence.

She'd been with him six days now. Six days of porridge and silence and the strange half-dreams her language left in his head when she talked in her sleep. He'd asked every farmer between his place and the mill if they knew the speech. None did. Old Tam had squinted at Jonah's careful imitation of it and said it sounded like "a crow arguing with a landslide," which was helpful to no one.

But Maren Oduya had spent seventy years collecting everything the valley had ever forgotten. If anyone below the snowline knew what the child was saying, it was her.

The house appeared around the last bend the way it always did — too large for the valley, two stories of grey stone with papered windows and a porch stacked with crates nobody had ever unpacked. A bell rope hung by the door. Maren didn't hold with knocking; she said doors were for people who could see who was coming, and the rest of the world should announce itself politely.

Jonah set Wren down, took the rope, and rang.

The door opened before the third chime finished. Maren stood in it, small and straight-backed, white hair braided tight against her skull, eyes the pale washed-out blue of old ice. She wore an apron with ink stains going back years, layered like tree rings.

"You walk heavy," she said. "Iversen."

"Maren."

"And you've brought someone light." Her head tilted, tracking Wren with that uncanny accuracy the blind sometimes had, as though blindness were just another way of looking. "Small feet. Bare feet, I'd guess, from the way they're standing — toes curled against my threshold like the stone's cold." She crouched, slow, keeping her hands visible the way you'd approach a spooked animal. "Hello, little one. I'm told you talk funny."

Wren looked up at Jonah. It was the first time she'd ever checked with him before deciding something, and it landed somewhere under his ribs and stayed there.

"It's all right," he said.

Wren turned back to Maren and said something in that language — quick, low, the consonants clicking like flint.

Maren went very still.

Jonah had seen the woman calm through a rockslide year, through the fever that took half the valley's goats and two children, through the fire that ate the east granary while she catalogued what burned. Stillness was not new to her. But this was different. This was the stillness of a woman listening to a sound she had hoped never to hear again, coming out of a mouth too small for it.

"Say that again," Maren said quietly.

Wren glanced at Jonah, then repeated it. Slower this time. Almost gentle, the way you'd speak to someone frightened.

"Inside," Maren said. "Both of you. Now. And Iversen—" she was already turning, hand finding the wall, moving through her own house with the speed of long habit, "—shut the door. Shut it hard."

* * *

The main room was exactly as Jonah remembered: floor-to-ceiling shelves, most of them stuffed past capacity, scrolls bundled with string, codexes leaning on codexes, a few clay tablets from before the valley kept records sitting on the hearth like pampered cats. The air smelled of dust and lamp oil and dried herbs Maren chewed for her joints. She lit three lamps, which was unusual — she needed none of them — and Jonah understood it was for him. For the thing she wanted him to see.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. There's tea in the blue pot, pour it yourself, my hands are busy."

Her hands were not busy. But she went to a shelf near the ceiling — one Jonah had never seen her touch — and brought down a box. Not wood. Some dark material, dense as iron, worked with lines that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them. His oath stirred faintly at the sight of it, a low unease in the blood, the feeling he got near the valley's edge. Near anything that remembered magic.

Maren set the box on the table between them and rested her palms on it.

"Before I open this," she said, "I want you to tell me exactly what happened. From the beginning. The storm night. All of it. Leave nothing out because you think it's foolish."

So he told her. The knock. The bare feet in the snow, unmarked by cold. The way the child had walked in like she owned the place and eaten his stew like she'd been starving for a week. The language. And then — because Maren's face demanded it, because lying to a blind woman felt like lying to a stone — the part he hadn't told anyone.

"When she talks," he said, "I understand some of it. Not the meaning, exactly. More like... the shape of it. Words surface. Like hearing your name underwater." He rubbed his jaw. "My oath-training. Twenty years ago they drilled us in fragments of the god-tongue. Liturgy words. Binding formulas. I never learned enough to hold a conversation — nobody does, it's not a conversation-language. But when she speaks, those fragments come up. And last night she said something in her sleep, and I swear on the shrine stones I felt it in my chest. Physically. Like a struck bell."

Maren's hands had stopped moving on the box.

"Which words," she said. "In your sleep-fragment. Which words did she say?"

"I don't know the meaning. It sounded like—" he closed his eyes, reached back, and spoke it carefully, shaping sounds he'd only ever half-learned: "*Sethra-kai. Sethra-kai il voren.*"

The lamps flickered. All three of them, together, though there was no draft.

Maren sat down slowly, across from him, and put her hands flat on the table as if steadying it.

"Do you know what you just said?" she asked.

"No."

"Neither do I. Not truly. Nobody living does — not fully." She exhaled. "But I know its name. What the language is called. And I know why my hands are shaking, and I'd thank you not to remark on it."

Wren, curled in the big chair with her knees drawn up, watched them both with those dark steady eyes. Jonah realized she was following everything. Not the words — the weight of them.

"Open the box, Maren," he said.

* * *

What was inside were pages. Loose pages, dozens of them, written in an alphabet of vertical strokes and hooked curves that hurt to look at directly — not painful, exactly, but resistant, like each character was a small locked door. Beneath the script, in faded common-tongue ink, someone had made translations. Many were crossed out. Some were crossed out so violently the pen had torn the page.

"My grandmother's brother wrote these," Maren said. "He was a linguist, before that word meant what it means now. Before the Tower of Tongues made a trade of it." Her fingers moved over the pages without touching them, reading by texture and memory. "He collected fragments of this language for forty years. Songs, inscriptions, a funeral rite recorded from a woman who claimed to remember it from her grandmother. He called it First-Tongue. Because that's what the oldest sources call it — not a language people speak. A language things are spoken *to*."

"Things?"

"The world." She tapped one page. "This is a binding formula. We think. My great-uncle believed the First-Tongue doesn't describe reality — it instructs it. Say a true sentence in it, mean it completely, and reality has no choice but to comply. That's what magic is, Iversen. That's what magic has always been. Not power. Grammar."

The fire popped. Wren had gone very still in her chair, and Jonah saw her hand had crept to her own throat, resting there lightly, the way you'd touch something precious or dangerous.

"That's superstition," Jonah said. "Old stories."

"Probably." Maren smiled without any humor in it. "And your god answering your prayers is probably coincidence, and the fact that magic cannot cross the valley line is probably geology. We're very good at believing probable things in this valley. It's how we sleep." She turned a page toward him, the torn one, the one crossed out hardest. "But here's what isn't superstition. Here's what my great-uncle wrote in the margin of this page, in plain letters, the week before he left the valley and never came back."

She recited it from memory, her voice flat:

"*They are hunting the speakers. Not scholars — speakers. The crown collects them. Whatever the king is building needs mouths that can shape the First-Tongue, and shaped mouths are easier than taught ones. If a speaker comes to you, hide them or run with them, but never turn them away, and never let the riders take them alive.*"

The room was quiet. Somewhere outside, the river ran cold and indifferent over the stones.

"Hunted," Jonah said.

"Hunted." Maren folded her hands. "That was sixty years ago, and the hunts have never stopped, they've simply gotten quieter. Children especially. Speakers born, not made — they're rare, and they're worth more than gold to whoever holds them." Her blind eyes fixed on him with terrible accuracy. "Now tell me, mercenary. This child appears at your door in a storm, barefoot, unafraid, speaking the language of binding as her mother tongue. How far do you think she walked to get here? And why would a child walk that far?"

Jonah looked at Wren. She looked back, unblinking, and then she did something he'd never seen her do. She reached across the table, took one of the pages from the box — gently, the way you'd handle a wounded bird — and held it up. She pointed at the script with one small finger and read it aloud.

Fluently. Easily. The way a valley child reads a market sign.

Then she set it down, folded her hands in her lap, and waited, as if to say: *there. Now you know.*

"She's not lost," Jonah heard himself say. The words came out of him hollow and cold. "She's running."

"Yes," Maren said softly. "And whatever she's running from, it taught her that language before she could tie her own boots. Which means it wants her for something. Which means—" the archivist turned her face toward the shuttered window, toward the valley beyond, toward the world, "—it will come for her. Riders, agents, worse. Magic cannot follow into this valley, Iversen. But men can. Men always can."

Jonah sat with it. Six days of porridge and silence rearranged themselves in his chest into something else entirely — not an inconvenience, not a mystery, but a debt accruing interest. He had taken contracts to protect merchants' wagons and farmers' daughters. He had an oath older than all of it, sworn to a god who no longer answered, and the oath had never once specified whom it protected. Only that he protect.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

Maren's mouth twisted. "If the crown knows where she went — days. If they're searching blind — weeks. If they sent anyone competent—" she shrugged, "—they're already on the ridge."

Wren tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed at herself, then at the door, then made a walking motion with two fingers. Then she pointed at him, and pressed her small fist against her chest — *with me* — and her expression was not pleading. It was checking. Giving him the chance, one more time, to be someone who stayed.

Jonah thought about the shrine. About twenty years of prayers falling upward into silence. About the warm weight of a sleeping child on his back, three days of switchbacks, trusting him with her whole slight body.

"We'll need supplies," he said to Maren. "And you're going to teach me every word in that box."

The archivist smiled, and for the first time since they'd arrived it almost reached her eyes.

"Pack light," she said. "And Iversen — ring the bell twice if riders come. Once for tea. Twice for war."

It was three nights later, well past midnight, that the bell rang twice.
