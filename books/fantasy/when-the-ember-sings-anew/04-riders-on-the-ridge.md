## 4. Riders on the Ridge

The soldiers came on the ninth day after the archivist's warning, which meant Tam had been right, which meant Jonah owed him a drink and an apology he'd never actually say out loud.

Jonah saw them first from the roof of his own house, where he'd gone to patch a shingle that didn't need patching just to have something to do with his hands while Wren napped inside. He'd taken up the habit of watching the ridgeline the way other men watched weather — not because he expected anything, but because Tam's words had gotten under his skin like a splinter: *Speakers of the First-Tongue are hunted, mercenary. And hunters do not stop at borders. They stop at nothing.*

The ridge was a black seam against the morning sky, pine and granite, the valley's teeth. Nothing crossed it. That wasn't law or superstition; it was simply how the world worked. The Ember Valley was dead ground for magic, a scar where whatever force ran through the rest of creation had burned itself out centuries ago. Gods didn't reach here. Curses didn't reach here. And men who carried magic in their blood or their bones or their sworn oaths found it went quiet the moment they passed the ridge stones, like a voice swallowed by deep water.

So when Jonah counted eleven riders picking their way down the switchback trail from the pass, his first thought was not *danger*.

His first thought was *impossible*.

He stood very still on the roof, hammer loose in his hand, and made himself count again. Eleven. Armor under travel cloaks — he could see the dull gleam at shoulder and knee. Lances wrapped in oilcloth so they wouldn't flash. That was soldier craft, real soldier craft, not bandit theater. And they moved like professionals too, spaced wide, no chatter carrying down the wind.

Crown work. Everything about them said crown work.

But crown soldiers were bound men, most of them. Sworn to Aldric's line with oaths that sat in the chest like warm coals. Oaths were magic's oldest leash. Even the meanest conscript in the king's army carried a thread of it, enough that crossing into the valley should have felt to him like walking into cold water — the power draining out, the god-voice going mute, the whole architecture of what made a soldier more than a man with a spear dissolving at the border.

They should have turned back at the ridge stones. Every one of them should have felt it and turned back, because no commander alive ordered men past the boundary knowing their strength would bleed away on the far side.

Unless someone had found a way around it.

Unless someone wanted the child badly enough to spend soldiers like coins.

"Wren," Jonah said quietly, though she couldn't hear him through the walls. He climbed down fast, dropped the last six feet, and came through his own door breathing hard.

She was awake already. She was always awake before him, he'd learned; she slept like a cat, in shallow sips, and woke without the fog most children carried. She sat on the bench by the hearth with her legs folded under her, watching him with those dark, unblinking eyes, and when she saw his face she went still in a way that was not childlike at all.

"Riders," Jonah said, and then, uselessly, because she didn't speak Valley tongue and he didn't speak hers — except that wasn't quite true anymore, was it. Three days of her living under his roof, three days of him listening to that old language pour out of her in her sleep, and words had started surfacing in him like drowned things rising. Words from twenty years ago, from the oath-training, from the weeks the priests of the Ember Order had spent drilling sounds into him that were older than any kingdom.

He tried one now. A word he'd heard her say in dreams, soft and repeated, and he shaped it as best his blunt tongue allowed and pointed east, toward the ridge.

Her head snapped toward the door.

Then she was off the bench and moving — not to hide, he noticed. Not under the bed, not behind the woodpile, none of the places a frightened child runs. She went to the corner where her few things lived, all of two items: a strip of cloth she sometimes held like a talisman, and a smooth river stone she'd carried since the night she arrived. She picked up the stone and pressed it into his hand and looked up at him, and her mouth set in a line that said, as clearly as any language, *not me. Us.*

Something turned over in Jonah's chest.

"Right," he said. "Us."

* *

The village of Lowmark had maybe sixty souls, one road, a mill, and a shrine. It had never been worth an army's attention, and Jonah had chosen it for exactly that reason. So there was no plan for this. There was only what people did, which was come out of their houses and stand in the road and stare, because that was what you did when the impossible walked down your street.

By the time the riders reached the well at the center of the village, half of Lowmark was gathered along the fences and doorways. Old Marta with her hands still floured from bread. The miller's boys up on the fence rail, until their father hauled them down. Tam the archivist, standing at the edge of the crowd with his white eyes aimed at the riders' footsteps, reading the ground by sound alone, and Jonah saw his jaw go tight and knew he was counting armor-creaks and arriving at the same arithmetic Jonah had.

Eleven. Too many for a parley. Too few for an occupation. Exactly right for a retrieval.

The lead rider reined in at the well and pushed back his hood, and Jonah's stomach dropped, because he knew the face. Not personally — but by reputation, the way every mercenary in three provinces knew it. Captain Dain Vosse. The king's leash. The man they sent when a problem needed solving quietly and completely, the man whose company had burned the freehold at Carrowmere down to its foundations over a tax dispute nobody talked about anymore.

Vosse looked older than his reputation. Grey in the beard, lines around eyes the color of wet slate. He surveyed Lowmark the way a man surveys a room he intends to leave cleaner than he found it, meaning emptier.

"People of the valley," he said, and his voice carried easily, trained to carry. "I'll be brief, because I know you value your peace. We're looking for a child. Girl, eight years or so, dark-haired, mute. She came through the pass some days back. She is property of the Crown of Aldric, and her return is a matter of state."

Murmuring along the fences. Jonah stood at the front of the crowd, arms crossed, and said nothing yet.

Vosse's gaze swept the faces and snagged, inevitably, on Jonah — because Jonah was the biggest thing in the village that wasn't a building, and because Vosse had the look of a man cataloguing threats. His eyes went over Jonah's shoulders, his hands, the old sword-calluses, and something flickered there. Recognition of a kind.

"You," Vosse said. "You've been soldiered."

"Retired," Jonah said.

"Mercenary." Vosse said it without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "I know the build. I know the stance. Where's the child?"

"There's no child here."

"There is." Vosse dismounted, unhurried, and Jonah noticed the way the other ten spread slightly, casually, covering angles. Professionals. "Our trackers lost her scent at the ridge stones, which surprised us, but scents are scents and valleys are valleys. She came down this road. Sixty people, one road. Someone fed her. Someone sheltered her." He smiled thinly. "Valley folk are famous for their charity. It's practically a vice here."

"It's practically a virtue," Marta said from the fence, flour still on her knuckles, and a few nervous laughs went through the crowd, and Jonah loved every one of them for it.

Vosse let the laugh die on its own. Then he said, pleasantly, "Here's what happens next. I ask each household in turn. Anyone harboring the child surrenders her, and we ride out, and this stays a quiet day. Anyone who lies to me—" he paused, and the pleasantness drained out of his voice like water out of a cracked cup, "—I want you to understand something about why we can be here. Do you know why we can be here?"

Silence.

"Because we left our oaths at the border," Vosse said. "All eleven of us. Voluntarily. There's a rite. It's not gentle, and it doesn't grow back, and every man in my company gave up the strength of his sworn god to make this ride. Which tells you, I hope, exactly how much the Crown wants this child. We are eleven ordinary armed men in a valley where no one's magic works — including yours, mercenary, if you're carrying anything, which you are, I can see it in how you stand. So think carefully about the arithmetic."

And that was the moment Jonah understood the shape of the trap, and admired it, and hated it. Vosse hadn't stumbled across the valley's immunity. He'd used it. He'd brought men stripped of divine power precisely so the valley would be level ground — so that Jonah's oath, whatever heat it still carried beyond the ridge, would be worthless here. Whoever planned this had known about the mercenary at the valley's edge. Had known about the child's likely protector.

Had known, maybe, everything.

"I'll ask once more," Vosse said. "Where is the child?"

And Jonah, who had spent forty years learning when to fight and when to wait, who had survived every contract and every war by being the man who calculated first and committed second, heard himself say:

"She's not here. But even if she were — you couldn't have her."

The murmur along the fences stopped.

Vosse studied him for a long moment. "That's a costly sentence, friend."

"I've said costlier."

"Have you?" Vosse drew a breath, and Jonah braced for the order, for steel, for the whole ugly arithmetic of eleven against a village. But instead Vosse tilted his head, almost curious. "You know what she is. Don't you. You've heard her talk." Something sharpened in the slate-grey eyes. "You understood her. Our linguists said no one outside the Tower could possibly—"

"Captain," said one of his riders, a lean woman with a crossbow slung across her back, and she said it quietly, but Jonah caught the tension in it. "Movement. North field."

Everyone turned.

Wren stood at the edge of the barley, small and straight-backed, in the oversized shirt Jonah had given her to sleep in, barefoot in the stubble. She had slipped out the back while the village argued, and Jonah's heart seized — *no, girl, no* — but she wasn't fleeing. She was walking toward them, calm as Sunday, and she stopped perhaps thirty paces off and looked at Captain Vosse with an expression Jonah had never seen on a child's face.

It was the expression of someone who recognized a jailer.

"There she is," Vosse said softly. "Good. That saves time." He raised a hand, and two riders wheeled toward the field, and Jonah's body was moving before his mind finished the thought — three steps, four, putting himself between the riders and the child, and behind him he heard Marta shout something and Tam shout something else and the whole fragile morning tearing open—

And then two things happened at once.

The first: Wren spoke. One word, that old language, pitched low but carrying, and Jonah felt it land somewhere behind his sternum like a struck bell.

The second: his oath answered.

Twenty years of silence. Twenty years of praying into a well with no bottom, of tending a shrine to a god who had stopped answering before Jonah's beard came in grey, of carrying the ember-mark on his forearm like a brand from a fire long dead — and in the space of a single heartbeat, it *woke*. Heat poured up his arm, not the borrowed warmth of the valley's hearths but something vaster, older, furious, a current with a will in it, and Jonah staggered because his knees nearly buckled under the sheer weight of being *heard* for the first time in two decades.

The valley was dead ground. Magic could not follow here. He had staked his whole understanding of the world on it.

But the heat didn't care. The heat roared through him like a river through a dry channel, and it wasn't coming *from* him — he realized that with a lurch. It was coming *through* him, through the word Wren had spoken, as if the child were a door and his oath a draft blowing through it, as if his god had been pressing its mouth against the wall of its prison for twenty years and had finally, through this impossible child, found a crack.

One of the riders screamed. His horse reared, eyes rolling, and bolted — and Jonah would swear until his dying day that the animal shied from nothing visible, from nothing but the air around Jonah, which shimmered faintly, like heat over a forge.

Every rider in Lowmark had gone still. Vosse's hand was frozen halfway to his sword, and his face had done something complicated — the professional calm cracking to show the man underneath, and the man underneath was afraid.

"That's not possible," the crossbow woman said. "Nothing answers in the valley. Nothing—"

"Captain," another rider said, tight and high. "Captain, the mark. Look at his arm."

Jonah looked down. Through the sleeve of his shirt, the ember-mark was glowing. Not dimly. Like a coal blown to life.

Wren's eyes met his across the barley stubble. She looked as frightened as he felt — no, more, because she clearly understood what was happening in a way he didn't, and understanding made it worse. Her lips moved again, barely a breath, and the word she shaped was one Jonah knew, one every sworn man knew, the oldest word in the training liturgy, the word that meant *stay*.

The heat in his arm settled. Didn't die — settled, banked, waiting, a live coal cupped in bone.

Vosse lowered his hand from his sword very slowly. When he spoke, his voice had regained its polish, but Jonah could hear the effort holding it there, like ice creaking under weight.

"Well," Vosse said. "That changes the report I'll be writing." He backed toward his horse without turning around, which was the smartest thing Jonah had ever seen a proud man do. "We'll withdraw. For today. But understand me, mercenary — the Crown does not lose interest. It waits. It spends. And next time we won't come stripped. Next time we'll come with what the Tower sends, and you don't want to meet what the Tower sends."

He mounted. Eleven riders, minus one spooked horse, formed up in the road with drilled precision.

"The child comes with us eventually," Vosse said. "Everything does. That's what the Crown means, in the end. It means *eventually*."

Then they rode, back up the switchbacks, gone into the pines, and the sound of hooves faded and the village exhaled all at once, sixty voices breaking into fear and argument and prayer.

Jonah didn't hear any of it.

Wren was standing in front of him now, looking up, and her small hand was gripping two fingers of the hand that still glowed faintly through the cloth, and she was talking — fast, urgent, that ancient language pouring out of her — and for the first time Jonah didn't need the archivist or a translator to catch the shape of it, because the same heat that filled his arm seemed to carry the meanings up with it, like bubbles rising through dark water.

*They will come back. They will come back with worse. You cannot stay. I cannot stay. The wall between us and it is thinning, and when it breaks—*

"Slow down," Jonah said hoarsely. "Girl. Wren. Slow down."

She stopped. She looked at his glowing arm, then at his face, and asked him a question — one word, the simplest word in any language, the word children learn first everywhere in the world —

*Why?*

Why did the mark burn. Why did the silent god answer. Why her. Why him. Why now.

And Jonah, staring down at the ember-brand that had been dead stone for twenty years and was now a banked fire under his skin, realized he had only one honest answer, and it was the answer that ended his life in the valley as surely as Vosse's riders had.

"Because it was never gone," he said. "It was caged. And I think—" his voice caught, and he steadied it, "—I think it's been trying to reach me through you since the night you knocked on my door."

Wren's grip tightened on his fingers.

Above the ridge, far off, thunder rolled — though the sky was clear — and Jonah understood, with the calm certainty of a man watching the last bridge burn behind him, that they had days at most. Maybe hours. Whatever came next from the Tower of Tongues, they could not be here when it arrived.

"We leave tonight," he said. "Both of us. Past the ridge."

Wren nodded once, solemn as an oath.

And the ember-mark pulsed, once, like a heartbeat — like agreement — like something that had waited twenty years finally, finally being told *yes*.
