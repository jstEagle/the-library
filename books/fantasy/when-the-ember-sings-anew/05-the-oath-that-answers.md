## 5. The Oath That Answers

They left by the goat track behind the shrine, because Jonah could not bring himself to walk out past the stone he had tended for twenty years and say goodbye to it. The track was steep, slick with rain, and invisible to anyone who didn't know the valley the way he knew the lines of his own hands. He carried Wren on his back when the climb went vertical, and the child weighed almost nothing — a bundle of sticks and stubbornness with her arms locked around his neck and her breath fogging his ear.

Behind them, torchlight crawled along the ridge. The soldiers had not given up. Of course they hadn't. Jonah had broken a sergeant's nose and put two men in the millpond, and men like that did not report failure. They reported a name, and the name went up the chain, and eventually something worse than a sergeant came down it.

He had told the valley folk he'd be back in a week. Marta had pressed a sack of bread and hard cheese into his hands and said, *You're a liar, Jonah Iversen, but you're our liar,* and he had laughed, because it was easier than admitting she might be right in a way none of them wanted.

The rain came down in gray sheets. The valley's edge was a day's hard walk by the road, but the goat track cut it to half that, winding up through the scree to the saddle of stone where the world changed. Everyone in Ember Valley knew the feeling of crossing that line, though nobody talked about it much. Inside the valley, you were just yourself. Outside, the air had a texture to it, a faint pressure against the skin, like wading into a river that wasn't there. Magic lived out there. Magic, and everything that hunted it.

Jonah had crossed that line a hundred times on contracts. It had never frightened him before, because he'd always carried the oath with him — the old words in his chest, the banked heat of a god's promise, the certainty that whatever waited beyond the stones, he was not walking out there alone.

The certainty had been dead for years. He was about to find out how dead.

* * *

They made the saddle at moonrise, when the rain thinned to a cold mist. Wren slid off his back and stood on the boundary stone, bare feet on wet rock, looking out at the dark country below — black forest, blacker sky, and far off, a smudge of orange that might have been a town burning its night fires.

She looked back at him and said something in that old language. Three syllables, low and careful, the way she'd been saying it all day whenever the soldiers got close. Jonah had stopped trying to pretend he didn't half-hear it. The words sat in his ear like a key sitting in a lock it almost fit.

*I know,* he said, in valley speech. *I know. Stay close.*

She frowned at him. Tried again, slower, and this time she pointed — at his chest, at the place behind the breastbone where the oath lived, or had lived. Then at the dark country below. Then back at his chest.

"I don't understand," he said, and hated how the words came out.

She stamped her foot. For a moment she looked like any eight-year-old in the world, furious at a giant who wouldn't listen, and then her expression changed — sharpened, went old and deliberate — and she pressed her whole palm flat against his sternum.

The oath woke up.

Not the way it used to. Not the slow warm tide he remembered from his fighting years, the strength that let him hold a shield wall alone for an hour, the sureness in the dark. This was different. This was a wire pulled taut between his chest and the child's hand, humming, and beyond her — out there, past the stones, out in the magic-haunted dark — something *answered*.

Jonah staggered. Caught himself on the boundary stone. The humming climbed, and he felt it in his teeth, in the old scar on his ribs, in the place behind his eyes where he kept the memory of the day he'd sworn. Twenty years of silence. Twenty years of prayers that fell like stones down a well. And now, through the palm of a mute child, his god was touching him like a man tapping on the far side of a wall.

*Hello,* the touch said. *Hello. Hello. I am here. I have always been here.*

"No," Jonah said out loud, and his voice cracked in the middle of it.

Wren took her hand away and the wire went slack, the presence receding like a tide, and Jonah stood in the mist shaking, one hand pressed to his own chest as if he could hold the door shut.

Because he knew that feeling. He knew it the way you know your mother's voice. The oath had a signature — every oath did, the priests said, every god signed its covenant in a different hand — and this was his. This was *his god*, the god of the Ember Valley shrine, the god who had taken his oath at nineteen and gone silent at thirty-one, leaving him a man with a hollow chest and a habit of talking to an empty stone.

It was out there. Past the valley's edge. It had been out there the whole time.

* * *

He didn't speak for an hour. He walked, and Wren walked beside him, and the forest closed over them, wet and black and full of the ordinary noises of ordinary night things. His mind kept circling the same shape like a dog circling a wound.

Silent for years. Silent — and then, the moment a child who spoke the First-Tongue put her hand on him, loud as a bell.

The archivist's words came back to him. *The First-Tongue is the language of binding, Jonah. Not of making. Of holding.* She'd said it like a warning about the child. He was beginning to understand it was a warning about everything.

His god hadn't left. His god had been *caged* — and the cage was made of words, and the child could put her hand through the bars.

He thought about the soldiers. Crown soldiers, in the valley where magic could not follow. He'd told himself it was impossible, then told himself it was ambition, then told himself it was coincidence, the way men tell themselves stories so they can keep walking. But if the god was caged — if something had bound it and dragged it away — then the thing that bound it would want the valley empty of the god's faithful. Would want the oath-bearers scattered, dulled, forgotten. The valley wasn't a refuge. It was a *pen*. A place where the god's people were kept like seed grain, alive but useless, until someone needed them.

And now someone needed them. Someone with a crown.

"Jonah." He said his own name aloud, just to hear a voice that wasn't the wind. Wren looked up at him. "I'm talking to myself. Ignore me."

She didn't ignore him. She took his hand — small fingers, cold as river stones — and walked with him, and every few hundred paces she glanced up at his face with an expression he was starting to recognize as her version of a question. *Are you all right. Are we all right. Are you going to keep me.*

He didn't have answers. He had a road, a sack of bread, and a god tapping on the inside of a wall.

* * *

Near dawn they found a shepherd's hut, abandoned years ago by the look of it, roof half-collapsed but the stone hearth still sound. Jonah got a fire going with the last dry wood and the last of his flint, and Wren crouched as close to the flames as she could without catching, hands out, shivering finally now that she'd stopped moving. He cut bread, gave her the bigger half without thinking about it, and sat down across the fire from her.

"Talk to me," he said. "You can't. I know. But — the words you say. The three syllables. When the soldiers were close. What were you saying?"

She looked into the fire for a long time. Then she said them. Slowly. And this time she said them while looking at him, and she said them *at* him, and Jonah understood — the way you understand a thing in your body before your head catches up — that she was speaking to the oath in him. Calling it. The way you'd call a dog.

The wire hummed. Faint, this time, a thread instead of a rope, but there. His chest warmed. The old scar ached the way it ached before storms.

"Stop," he whispered, and she stopped, and the warmth drained away, and he put his face in his hands.

Because here was the thing he had not let himself think on the ridge, the thing that had been waiting for him in the quiet: he had spent nine years grieving. Nine years of tending that shrine, of leaving offerings, of kneeling on cold stone and saying *are you there* into a silence that never once broke. Nine years of deciding, over and over, that he had been abandoned — that the god had taken his oath, used him, and moved on to louder, more useful servants. Nine years of building a life on that grief the way you build a house on a foundation you refuse to inspect.

And the foundation was rotten. The god had never left. It had been taken. And all those years he'd knelt in the valley, praying across the boundary into a world where his god couldn't hear — or worse, could hear and couldn't answer — he'd been blaming the prisoner for the silence of the cell.

"Twenty years," he said to the fire. "Twenty years I served you, and nine years I cursed you, and you were in a cage the whole time." His throat tightened. "Did you even *want* me out there? Or did they keep the pen stocked?"

The wire stirred, faint and hurt, and he flinched from it.

"No. Don't. I'm not — " He stopped. Breathed. "I don't know what I am."

Wren moved around the fire. She knelt in front of him, took his big scarred hands in her small cold ones, and held them the way he had seen old women hold the hands of the dying. Then she said one word. Not the three syllables. A different one. Short, soft, shaped almost like a valley word, and the meaning arrived in him without translation, the way heat arrives before you see the flame:

*Stay.*

Or maybe *remain*. Or maybe — and this was the reading that undid him — *I am keeping you*. The way you keep a coal under ash so it doesn't die in the night.

"You're a child," he said roughly. "You're a child and they bred you for this, whatever this is, and I'm supposed to be the one protecting you, and instead you're — " He shook his head. "Who taught you comfort? Who taught you that?"

Her face did something complicated. Closed, then opened a crack, then closed again. She looked at the fire.

And Jonah understood that whatever the Tower of Tongues was, it had taught her this. Had made a child who knew how to steady a grown man's hands, because a tool that could steady hands was a more useful tool. The thought made him want to break something. There was nothing in the hut worth breaking, so he didn't, and the wanting sat in him like a swallowed coal.

"Here's what we're going to do," he said. "We're going to walk south, away from the road, until we find someone who can tell us what you're saying and what's been done to you and what that thing on the king's head wants with a child who speaks the First-Tongue. And then we're going to decide what to do about it. Together. That's the whole plan. Bread and walking and deciding."

She looked up. And she smiled — the first time he'd seen it, small and crooked and gone almost before it arrived, like a fish surfacing — and nodded.

* * *

They slept in shifts. Jonah took first watch, sitting with his back to the wall, sword across his knees, watching the door and the fire and the small curled shape of the child, and he let himself do the thing he'd been refusing to do all night.

He reached for the oath. Deliberately. The way he used to, before the silence — the old practice, the morning prayer of a working mercenary, hand on the breastbone, attention turned inward.

Nothing. The same nothing as nine years. The hollow chest, the dead hearth.

Then Wren stirred in her sleep, turned toward him, and in her sleep her hand drifted out across the stones and came to rest against his boot.

The wire woke. Gentle, this time — a sleeping touch, a presence half-dreaming, warm as banked coals. And through it, faint and far and muffled, he felt the god feel *him*. Felt the attention of something vast turn, slow and wondering, like an animal in a dark stall lifting its head at a familiar footstep. Felt — and this was the part that broke something open in his chest that he'd have sworn was scar tissue all the way through — felt the god *grieve*. A long, low, patient grief, the grief of a thing that had been listening to nine years of a man's angry prayers through a foot of stone and had never once been able to say *I'm here, I'm here, I never left, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry.*

Jonah put his hand over his eyes and wept, quietly, the way he did everything, while the child slept and the god mourned with him across the bars of its cage.

When the grief had burned down to something he could carry, he wiped his face and thought, coldly and clearly, the way he thought on contracts.

Someone had caged a god. Someone had built a valley to pen its faithful. Someone had bred children who spoke the binding tongue. Someone wore the cage as a crown. And that someone's soldiers had crossed into the one place they should never have been able to go, hunting an eight-year-old girl — which meant they knew what she was, and they knew what she could do, and they were not going to stop.

Which meant the pen was over. Which meant the whole shape of Jonah Iversen's life — the contracts, the shrine, the drink with Marta and the boys, the reliable-rootless man of Ember Valley — was a life that had ended three nights ago at his own front door.

He looked at Wren, asleep against his boot, her fingers curled around his ankle like she was holding on even in her dreams.

"All right," he said to the god, to the girl, to the fire, to nobody. "I hear you. I'm coming."

Far away, through the wire, through the stone, through whatever cage held a god of hearths and oaths, something that had been silent for nine years let out a sound that was almost a sob — and Jonah, who had crossed the boundary stone at last and was standing in the magic-haunted dark with a child and a sword and no plan worth the name, felt it settle over him like a hand on his head.

Then, from the ridge above the hut, a horn blew. Long, and low, and wrong — a sound with a texture to it, a pressure, like the First-Tongue made into noise.

Wren's eyes snapped open. She was on her feet before Jonah had his sword clear, one hand pressed flat against the wall, and her face in the firelight was old, and certain, and terrified.

She said one word, and this time he understood it perfectly:

*Found.*
