## 6. The Road Out of Ember

The giant's name was Jonah.

Wren learned it in pieces, the way she learned everything now — by watching, by listening, by keeping very still so that nothing would notice her and want her back.

He said it himself, on the first night after the ridge, when they were crouched in the hollow under a fallen pine with the horn's echo still crawling along Wren's spine. He'd put one huge hand over her mouth — gently, the way you cover a candle flame — and pressed his ear to the dark like a man listening for a heartbeat inside a wall. When the hoofbeats finally faded westward, he took his hand away and pointed at his own chest.

"Jonah," he whispered. Then he pointed at her, eyebrows up.

She didn't answer. In the Tower of Tongues you did not give them your name. Your name was the first thing they took, and the last thing you got back, if you ever got anything back at all. She had been vessel-seven-of-nine. She had been *little mouth*. She had been *it*. She had not been Wren since the day the gray-robed woman wrote the word down in a ledger and then crossed it out.

The giant waited. That was strange. The handlers in the Tower never waited. They had schedules carved into their forearms, inked lists of what a vessel should be able to do by what age, and waiting was not on any list.

After a while he shrugged — an enormous motion, shoulders rising like hills — and pointed at himself again. "Jonah." Then he made a shape with his hands: two wings folded, a small head tucked down, and he looked at her expectantly.

A bird. He wanted to know her name, or give her one, and he was doing it with his hands because he knew she wouldn't speak his language yet. Or maybe because speaking felt dangerous tonight. Both things were true, though she couldn't have explained how she knew which words were dangerous. Some knowledge lived in her body below thought, planted there before she could walk.

Wren looked at the folded-wing shape of his hands for a long time. Then she lifted her own hands and opened them slightly — not wings, not flight. A bird that had landed. A bird choosing to stay near a fire.

Something moved across the giant's face that she later learned was called a smile, though on him it was mostly weather, a shifting in cloud.

"Wren?" he tried.

Her whole body went cold and hot at once. She hadn't told him. She hadn't told anyone. But the word must have been written somewhere on her, the way the Tower wrote everything down — in the set of her shoulders, in the way she flinched from open doors, in the sound she almost made around fire. Maybe names leaked out of people who had stopped guarding them.

She nodded, once.

"Okay, Wren." He said it carefully, like setting something breakable down on stone. "Okay."

* * *

They walked by night and hid by day, which Wren understood without being told, because hiding was the first thing they taught you in the Tower and the last thing they let you forget.

By day they slept in culverts, in bracken, once in the rib cage of some enormous dead thing that had been a barn before the war the grown-ups sometimes mentioned. Jonah built no fires. He shared dried meat and hard bread from a pack he'd carried out of the valley, tearing each piece precisely in half before offering hers to her on his open palm, flat, low, the way you feed a horse you don't know yet.

He talked to her while they walked. Not questions — she could tell he'd figured out that questions made her go quiet and small — just words, thrown out loose like stones skipped across water. He named trees. He named birds. He named the mountains and gave them insults, calling one *the old liar* because its trail doubled back on itself, and Wren found, to her horror, that she laughed — a single bark of sound, immediately smothered — and the giant heard it and pretended very hard that he hadn't, which was its own kind of kindness.

His language came to her faster than it should have. She noticed this on the third night and did not mention it, even inside her own head. Words arrived in her the way water arrives in a well: you dropped a bucket and something came up. The gray-robes had called this *attunement*. They had charts for it. Vessel-seven-of-nine, comprehension quotient exceptional, retention total, affect blunted, suitable.

*Affect blunted.* She turned that phrase over sometimes, walking in the dark behind the giant's broad back. It meant they had measured her and found her wanting in feeling, and here she was, feeling too much — fear like a coat of wet wool, and underneath it, worse, a warm treacherous thing every time the giant tore the bread in half.

She was not supposed to feel warm things about people. People were surfaces. People were doors the words came out of. The First-Tongue did not care whether you loved the mouth that spoke it, only whether the mouth was *open*, and the gray-robe who ran the attunement floor had smiled her paper smile and said, *you, little mouth, are always open*.

Wren kept her mouth shut anyway. It was the one thing they couldn't take without breaking her, and she was beginning to suspect — from the way the riders' horns went silent whenever the valley fell behind, from the way Jonah glanced at her when thunder rolled — that whatever lived in her voice was the reason everyone wanted her back.

* * *

On the fourth day, hiding in a dry streambed while a patrol clattered along the ridge above, she saw the giant be afraid.

Not of the patrol. Of something smaller.

A hawk had stooped at a finch in the meadow across the stream, missed, risen. Ordinary. But Jonah had gone rigid as fence-post iron, staring at the spot where the hawk's shadow had crossed the grass, and his hand — the one not holding the sword — was pressed flat against his own chest, over the sternum, pressing hard, like a man holding a door shut from the inside.

Then he muttered something. It wasn't in his own language. It came out rough and half-formed but Wren's skin prickled, because the shape of it was *First-Tongue*, worn smooth by years of repetition the way a stair is worn by feet. A prayer. An oath-fragment. Something he'd said ten thousand times to a god that never answered.

Except —

Wren felt it answer.

It was faint, a warmth moving under the ground of the world, like a coal breathing under ash. It rose through her before she chose anything, the way breath rises, and the prayer finished itself in her chest, and for half a heartbeat the streambed smelled of embers and the cold in her fingers went away.

Jonah spun. He stared at her with an expression she had no word for — terror wearing awe's clothing — and she shrank back against the bank, hating herself, hating the thing in her that reached for power like a tongue reaching for a cracked tooth.

"I'm sorry," she said.

It was the first time she'd spoken his language aloud. Two words. Her voice came out rusty, a hinge unused for months, and the sound of it seemed to strike him harder than the magic had.

"You speak," he breathed.

"A little." She wrapped her arms around her knees, making herself small. "It — hears you. Through me. I don't know why." A lie, or half of one. She suspected she knew exactly why, and the suspicion was a stone she carried under her ribs and showed no one.

The Tower had nine vessels in the attunement hall. Seven of them were empty cups; the words poured through them and left no trace, and the gray-robes logged the results in red ink. But the eighth vessel — a boy called Tam who used to hum when he thought no one listened — had once made a lamp flame stand up straight when a binding was spoken nearby, and they had taken him downstairs, to the deep floors, and he had not come back up, and the next week they had started saying *eight* instead of *nine* in the counts.

Vessels that held something weren't vessels anymore. They were *vessels-plus*. And vessels-plus went downstairs.

If the god — and she was nearly sure that was whose warmth she'd felt, big and old and sad as a buried bell — if the god heard prayers through her, then she wasn't running from soldiers anymore. She was running from the people who built cages for things that hear.

"They'll take me back," she said quietly. "To the capital. To the Tower of Tongues." She watched his face do something complicated. "They make us hold words. Big ones. Ones that shouldn't fit in a person." She touched her own throat. "I ran away before they finished teaching me the last one. I think — I think the last one is for *him*. For the king. They're going to pour him full of something and use me to say it, and then it won't matter that I ran, because I'll be a door again, forever."

The giant sat very still. Above them, unseen, the patrol jingled past and faded east.

Then Jonah did something no handler, no gray-robe, no keeper of ledgers had ever done. He reached out slowly — telegraphing it, palm open, giving her all the time in the world to flinch — and set his hand, light as snowfall, on top of her head.

"No," he said.

One word. His accent mangled the First-Tongue roots under it, but the meaning came through clean as cut glass, and Wren discovered that her eyes were leaking, which was inefficient and humiliating and impossible to stop.

*No,* he'd said. Not *no, they won't catch you.* Not *no, we'll outrun them.* Just: no. As if the whole future were a door and he was standing in front of it, and he was very large, and he intended to stay there.

She cried for a while, silently, the trained way, shoulders barely moving. He stayed how he was, hand on her head, patient as furniture, until the sun crossed the stream and the shadows swung around, and neither of them moved, because moving would end it and neither of them wanted it ended.

* * *

They made better time after that, though Wren could not have said why. Perhaps because she stopped walking like prey. Perhaps because he stopped walking like a wall with a sword. Somewhere on the fifth night, crossing a moonlit field of stubble with the capital's far glow staining the horizon like a bruise, she caught herself matching her stride to his automatically, and realized she had stopped counting exits.

That frightened her more than the horns had.

In the Tower they taught that attachment was a flaw in a vessel, like a crack in a cup. *What you love can be leveraged,* the gray-robe said, tapping her pointer against the chart. *Therefore love nothing.* Wren had repeated it back perfectly, comprehension quotient exceptional, and had believed it the way you believe the floor is solid — right up until the floor turned out to be a trapdoor, which was the night she climbed out the laundry chute with someone else's name sewn into her collar.

Now here was a crack opening in her, wide as a valley, shaped exactly like a large clumsy mercenary who fed her first and insulted mountains.

She tested it the way you test ice. On the sixth morning, while he dozed against his pack, she practiced his oath-words under her breath, feeling for the buried-warmth answer, learning its edges. On the seventh evening she told him three true things about the Tower: that it had no windows on the lower floors, that the children counted days in knots on their sashes, that the gray-robes feared one word above all others and would not let it be spoken even in the deep rooms. She did not tell him what the word was. Some doors you keep shut even for giants. But she watched him listen — really listen, jaw tight, fists closing — and the crack got wider, and she let it.

On the eighth night, rain drove them into the mouth of an old shrine — a roadside thing, centuries abandoned, a stone alcove with a guttering niche where travelers had once left offerings. Jonah got a fire going despite the wet, muttering triumphantly at it, and Wren sat close enough to share the heat and far enough to bolt, out of habit.

Half-asleep, she heard him pray. The same worn fragment of First-Tongue, soft, automatic, a man talking to a silence out of pure stubbornness. *I'm still here,* the words meant, in the old grammar. *Where are you.*

And the buried warmth rose — not through her this time, but *toward* her, gathering, a tide finding its channel — and before she could decide whether to allow it, the fire in the niche flared tall and gold and threw their shadows huge up the stone, and for one instant the flames made a shape like a doorway, and something on the other side of the doorway leaned close.

It did not speak. It had been a long time silenced, and speech cost it dearly. But Wren felt it look at her — really look, the way no one at the Tower ever had, seeing the vessel and the girl both — and she felt it form one careful, ancient, unbearably gentle word against the inside of her mind, in the First-Tongue, in the language of binding:

*Daughter-of-the-open-door. Keep him walking east. What waits in Vell knows my name — and theirs.*

The fire sank back to ordinary. The presence withdrew like a tide going out, leaving her emptied and ringing.

Across the flames, Jonah had gone white. "You heard it too," he said hoarsely. "Don't lie to me, Wren. You heard it."

Wren pulled her knees up and looked at him over the top of them — this frightening, kind giant who had given her a name and stood in front of doors — and understood that the running was over. Whatever came next, they would walk into it together.

"It said," she began, and her voice did not shake, "that we're going the right way. East. Toward the bells." She swallowed. "And that someone in the free city already knows everything."

Far off, beyond the rain, a church-bell in some unseen town tolled midnight — and tolled it wrong, off-key, in a rhythm that was almost language, almost a word, almost a warning.
