## 7. The City of Brass Bells

The bells found them before the city did.

Jonah heard them first as a tremor in the ground, a faint brass shiver through the soles of his boots, and then the road bent around a shoulder of hill and there was Vell — sprawled across three hills and the river valley between them, its towers crowned with bells of every size, from hand-bells to monsters big enough to swallow a cart. The sun caught them and threw light in wild arcs across the rooftops, so the whole city seemed to shimmer, to ring silently even at this distance.

Wren stopped walking. Her small hand closed on two fingers of his left hand, the grip he'd learned over nine days of road meant *stop*, meant *look*, meant sometimes *I'm scared* and sometimes just *stay*.

"I know," Jonah said. "It's ugly."

It wasn't, quite. It was loud, was the thing. Even silent, it looked loud. After the Ember Valley, where the air lay flat and honest as a slate, the city looked like a shout held too long in the throat.

They had crossed out of the valley six nights ago, moving by back roads, sleeping in ditches and hay barns, avoiding the king's roads wherever the king's roads could be avoided. The riders on the ridge hadn't followed past the valley's edge — whatever power moved them had balked at that border like a horse at a fence — but Jonah didn't trust borders. Borders were promises made by people who weren't there when they got broken.

He'd come to Vell once before, eleven years ago, young enough to still believe contracts were worth the paper. He remembered the slave market — they called it something softer now, the Bonding Yard, as if a nicer name could sweeten the chains — and he remembered the bell-priests who rang the hours and took their tithe and asked no questions, because asking questions was bad for business.

He remembered thinking: *a free city*. Free for whom, exactly.

"Food first," he told Wren. "Then a translator."

She looked up at him with those eyes that were too old for her face, and said nothing, because she never said anything — not in any tongue he knew. But she squeezed his fingers twice, which had lately come to mean *yes*, and once more, which meant nothing he'd identified yet but which he suspected was Wren's own word for something she couldn't say aloud.

He'd tried teaching her valley-speech on the road. She watched his mouth with fierce concentration and could shape a few words now — *bread*, *no*, *Jonah*, which came out as *Jo-na* with a hard little click in the middle that he privately loved. But her own language kept leaking out of her, especially at night, especially in dreams: soft syllables that made the fire lean toward her, that made the dark feel thinner.

First-Tongue, the archivist had called it. The language of binding.

Which was why they needed a translator, because Jonah's oath-training had given him scraps — a soldier's phrasebook of a dead god's grammar — and scraps wouldn't unbind whatever was hunting her.

The road took them down through terraced orchards and into the river gate, where the guards of Vell — not crown soldiers, Vell prided itself on that, though everyone knew whose coin greased whose palm — waved them through after a bored glance. Cities didn't look at faces. That was the whole trick of cities, and Jonah had spent his whole life being grateful for it.

Inside the walls, the noise hit him like weather. Cart wheels, hawkers, a dozen languages braided together in the market streets, and above it all the bells, ringing the third hour, an avalanche of brass that made Wren flinch and press against his leg until he put a hand on her head and steered her into the shadow of an awning.

"Bells," he explained, uselessly. "They do this all day. You get used to it."

She did not look convinced.

He bought bread and hard cheese and honey pears from a fruit-seller who charged him double and gave him a rotten one anyway, and they ate standing in a doorway off the Coppermarket, watching the crowd flow past. Wren ate the way she did everything — carefully, completely, wasting nothing — and when a beggar child drifted close she broke her last pear in half without hesitating and held it out. The beggar stared at it, then at her, then fled as if offered a live snake.

"That was kind," Jonah said.

Wren shrugged, a gesture so perfectly human it made his chest hurt.

*Translator,* he thought. *Where does a man buy a translator in Vell.* He knew the answer before he finished the thought, and hated it. Scholars clustered around the Bellhouse — the great library-temple at the city's heart — and scholars talked, and talking cost money, and money spent loudly drew ears. Still. There were quieter scholars. There were always quieter scholars, the ones who'd fallen out of favor, who worked out of rented rooms above dyeshops and taught dangerous subjects for drinking money. He'd find one. He'd found harder things.

He was wrong about how easy that would be, but he didn't know it yet.

* * *

They found trouble before they found a translator, because trouble in Vell had a schedule and kept it punctually.

It happened in the square outside the Bonding Yard, which Jonah would have walked wide around if the route to the Bellhouse quarter hadn't cut straight through. The Yard sat behind iron gates and a colonnade of whitewashed pillars, and in front of it, on a scaffold platform draped in blue cloth, stood a magistrate in robes of office, reading names from a scroll while a clerk beside him marked each one off with a flourish of the quill.

Around the platform, in a pen of wooden rails, stood the merchandise.

Children. Eleven of them, maybe twelve, aged five to fourteen, scrubbed and dressed in gray smocks, arranged by height like bolts of cloth. Each wore a lead tag on a cord around the neck, and the magistrate read the tags aloud with the pleasant cadence of a man announcing festival prizes.

"—lot forty-one, male, seven years, sound of tooth and limb, *unbound*, certified by the Office of Resonance, opening bid four crowns—"

Unbound. The word snagged in Jonah's ear like a fishhook. He'd heard it before — muttered by the crown riders, shouted in the archivist's warnings — but here it had a price tag on it, and the price tag made it real in a way warnings never did.

He turned to Wren to move her along, and saw her face.

She wasn't afraid. That was what frightened him. She was looking at the children in the pen with a flat, terrible recognition, the way a man looks at a photograph of a house that burned down with people inside it. Her lips moved, shaping words in that older tongue, very quietly, and the nearest bell tower — Jonah would swear this until his dying day — went briefly, discordantly silent, as if the brass had flinched.

"Wren." He crouched, put himself between her and the platform. "Look at me. Not there. Me."

Her eyes came back to him slowly. In them was a question he couldn't read, and then, worse, a decision — some small cold arithmetic she performed and folded away.

"What is it?" he asked. "What are they? What does *unbound* mean?"

She touched her own throat. Touched the place where a collar would sit. Then she pointed at the children, and then at herself, and then she spread her hands open — empty palms, empty everything — and shook her head.

*Nothing inside us,* the gesture said. Or maybe: *nothing they can't take.*

Jonah stood up slowly, and something old and oath-shaped turned over in his chest.

On the platform, the bidding began. A fat wool merchant took lot forty-one for six crowns and looked pleased with himself, as well he might; a sound seven-year-old was cheap at twice the price. Then came lot forty-four, a girl of about ten with a burn scar down one arm, and the magistrate paused over her tag with theatrical regret.

"*Unbound*, certified defective — resonance-null, no vessel value. Sold as labor only. Opening bid one crown."

No vessel value. The words slid past the crowd like water past a stone. Nobody flinched. A tanner bought her for two crowns and a promise, and the girl was led off the platform by a handler with a stick, and she went, because where would she go, and the clerk struck her name from the scroll, and the bells rang the half hour as if the world required marking, and Jonah stood in the middle of the Coppermarket with a mute child's hand in his and discovered that he was shaking.

Not with fear. With something that had no proper name but felt like the oath waking up in the wrong direction.

"There's a law about this," he said, to nobody. To Wren, maybe. To the god who wasn't listening. "There has to be a law."

There was a law. Of course there was a law. He could see it working right in front of him — the robes, the scroll, the clerk, the certified tags, the Office of Resonance with its wax seals. Every cruelty on that platform had been stamped and countersigned. The law wasn't absent from the Bonding Yard. The law was the architect of it.

He had spent twenty years believing that his oath meant something because a god stood behind it, and gods, whatever else you said about them, were not men. They didn't take bids. Now he watched men in nice robes sell children by weight and certification, and he understood — with the slow, sick clarity of a floor giving way — that every institution he'd ever trusted to be better than a mercenary was built on the same scaffold with the same blue cloth.

A hand caught his elbow. He nearly broke the wrist on reflex, and stopped himself barely in time.

The man attached to the hand was old, stooped, dressed in the faded gray of a bell-priest without the sash that marked one in good standing. His eyes were quick and bloodshot and kind.

"You're staring, friend," the old priest said softly. "Staring gets people noticed. Noticed gets people sold. Walk with me."

"I don't—"

"The little one." The priest's gaze flicked down at Wren, and something in his face tightened. "Gray smock, resonance-tagged, unbound. You're walking a certified vessel through the Coppermarket with her face bare. Half the brokers here would pay fifty crowns on sight, and the other half would pay the crown's informer fee for turning her in. Which do you imagine pays faster?"

Jonah's arm went cold. "Who are you?"

"Brother Anselm. Defrocked, technically. I teach letters to yard-children who age out unwanted." He smiled, and it was a tired smile. "Walk with me. Please. I've seen that expression before, on men about to do something expensive."

* * *

Anselm's rooms were above a dyeshop that reeked of madder and urine, two narrow chambers stacked with books, most of them water-damaged, all of them precious to him. He set water and a plate of dried figs before them and sat with his hands wrapped around his own cup like a man warming them at a fire that wasn't there.

"You want a translator," he said. "For the First-Tongue. Don't bother denying it — I've seen two people in my life carry that particular silence, and both of them were Tower property."

Jonah said nothing. Anselm nodded as if he'd expected it.

"Every translator in this city who knows the First-Tongue works for the crown or fears it, and mostly those are the same people. The Bellhouse archives have a sealed section. You know what's in the sealed section?"

"Bindings," Jonah said.

"Bindings," Anselm agreed. "And the names of everyone who ever studied them, cross-referenced with the Office of Resonance. You walk into the Bellhouse asking your questions, and by evening there's a rider on the king's road carrying your description." He drank. "You need someone outside the walls. Someone disgraced, someone with nothing left to lose, someone who hates the Tower of Tongues enough to help you and knows enough to be useful."

"And you know such a person."

"I know *of* such a person." Anselm set down the cup. "Sefa. Formerly first philologist of the Tower of Tongues, formerly the finest living scholar of binding-language, currently a debt-bondsman of the Vell merchant guilds, indentured for — I believe the number is thirty-one years, of which she has served four. She translates shipping contracts now." His mouth twisted. "The Tower sells its failures to the guilds. It keeps the successes. Make of that what you will."

"How do I reach her?"

"You don't. She's bonded — her contract travels with her, her debtors hold her seal, and anyone who helps her escapes shares her crime." Anselm leaned forward. "Which brings me to why I stopped you in the square, friend. Because you were about to do something stupid on that platform, and I'd rather you did something stupid that helps."

"Name it."

"Her debt is purchasable. Guilds sell paper the same as the Yard sells children — it's all the same trade, just with bigger numbers. Buy her bond, and she's yours to direct. Legal, clean, deniable." He named the figure.

Jonah had fought eight years for wages that sounded heroic in taverns and came to almost exactly that figure, minus the years he'd drunk through. He had it. Nearly all of it. It was the nearly that stuck.

"And the rest?"

"Is why I stopped you." Anselm's eyes were steady and sorry. "There's a broker in the Coppermarket who buys odd lots. Resonance-nulls, defectives, the ones nobody bids on. He pays well for them — not as labor. The crown pays him for certified nulls, quiet-like, for purposes I don't ask about." He paused. "Your little one reads as null to any tag-wand in this city. I've watched you pass three checkpoints today. You're either lucky or warded, and luck runs out."

Jonah looked at Wren. She sat very still among the ruined books, fig untouched, watching them with the patience of someone accustomed to being discussed.

"She's not for sale," he said.

"No," Anselm agreed. "But the difference between her price and Sefa's debt is eleven crowns, and there's a woman on that platform tomorrow morning, lot sixty-two, a resonance-null, ten years old, who'll go to the broker if nobody bids. And the crown pays for nulls, friend, and the crown is looking for a runaway vessel, and a null-lot auction is exactly where its informers stand." He spread his hands. "That's the arithmetic. Justice outside the law always costs, and it always costs more than you budgeted. I'm sorry. I stopped selling comfort years ago."

Jonah sat with it. Outside, the bells rang the fifth hour, and the dyeshop stink rose through the floorboards, and Wren reached out and took a fig after all, and ate it slowly, watching him.

Twenty years of keeping his word to a god who'd gone quiet, and here was the sum of it: he could keep his word to a stranger-child he'd known nine days, or keep his purse intact, and the god who'd made keeping-word his whole spine had nothing to say about which.

"All right," he said finally. "The lot tomorrow. And then the broker's price comes out of what's left, and if that's not enough—" He stopped. Started again. "How much does a mercenary's sword-arm fetch, these days? Contract work, dirty end of the trade."

Anselm studied him a long moment. "You'd sign yourself bonded to free another bondsman. That's a rare arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic," Jonah said. "Arithmetic balances. This is just a hole I keep throwing money into."

Something like approval crossed the old priest's face. "The guildhall opens at dawn. Lot sixty-two goes on the block at midmorning. And Jonah—" Anselm hesitated, and for the first time looked genuinely uncertain. "When you buy Sefa's bond, read the contract clause by clause. The guild papers have teeth in them that aren't printed. And when you meet her — she's not grateful. People who've been owned don't do grateful. Remember that, and you may survive her."

* * *

They slept that night in a stable loft Anselm rented for them, in the smell of straw and horse, and Jonah lay awake listening to the bells mark the hours — sixth, seventh, eighth — and thinking about platforms.

In the morning, at midmorning, he stood in the auction crowd with eleven crowns in his belt and watched lot sixty-two climb the steps of the blue-draped platform. She was small and dark-haired and she held her chin up in a way that made his throat ache, because it was Wren's way too, the way of children who had decided dignity was the one possession no one could tag and sell.

The bidding opened at one crown. It climbed. Three. Four. The broker — a neat man with soft hands and a ledger-chain — raised a finger at five.

Jonah raised his hand at six.

The broker turned and looked at him, properly looked, the way a man looks at a rival rather than a customer, and smiled, and raised a finger again.

Seven.

Eight, from Jonah. Nine, from the broker. Ten.

Eleven was everything Jonah had — the Sefa-debt balance, the road money, the margin for disaster — and he stood there in the crowd with his hand half-raised and understood that Anselm had been right about the arithmetic, that justice outside the law always cost more than you budgeted, that somewhere behind this platform's blue cloth the whole machinery of lawful cruelty was humming along contentedly, stamped and certified and rung in by bells.

He raised his hand. "Twelve."

Silence. The broker studied him, wrote something in his ledger — Jonah would think about that ledger entry for days, wondering what note the man had made, *tall mercenary, pays over value, watch him* — and let the lot go.

Lot sixty-two came off the platform and stood in front of him, blinking in the ordinary daylight, waiting to be told what she was now.

Jonah crouched to her height. Up close he could see she was trembling, fine as a plucked string, and holding still anyway.

"You're not going to the broker," he said. "You're not going anywhere you don't choose. First choice: there's a bell-priest called Anselm who teaches letters, and he'll feed you and keep you safe, or I can find you family in the valley where I live, where no wand can tag you. Your pick."

She stared at him. "Both," she whispered.

"Both," Jonah agreed, and felt something crack open in his chest that he suspected would never fully close.

He handed her to Anselm's keeping with the rest of his silver, walked out of the Coppermarket lighter by everything he'd carried for eight years, and went to find the guildhall — where a debt-bond called Sefa waited to be purchased, where the clerk would want references, where Jonah Iversen, oath-sworn and near-broke, would discover that the hardest contract of his life had no god's signature anywhere on it.

Behind him, high on the towers, the bells of Vell rang the noon hour, bright and beautiful and completely indifferent, and for the first time in twenty years, Jonah did not bow his head when they sounded.
