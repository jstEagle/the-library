## 8. The Translator's Bargain

The debt-house on Tanner's Row kept its records in a room with no windows, which told Jonah everything he needed to know about the people who ran it. Men who wanted light in their ledgers built windows. Men who wanted their debtors to forget the sun built walls.

He'd left Wren at the tinker's yard off Copper Lane — a woman named Brannit who owed him from the Kettlebridge job and asked no questions when he paid her three days' wages to keep a mute child fed and out of sight. Wren hadn't liked it. She'd gripped his sleeve with one small hand and looked at him with those flat grey eyes that saw too much, and she hadn't let go until he'd crouched to her level and said, "I come back. Always." Which was a stupid thing to promise a child he'd known four days. He'd promised it anyway.

Now he stood in the windowless room while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs read aloud from a ledger like a priest reciting sins.

"Sefa Vael. Indebted to the House of Meric for the sum of two hundred forty crowns, incurred through purchase of her bond from the Collegium of Vell upon her dismissal. Interest compounded quarterly. Current term of bondage: seven years remaining."

"Where do I find her?" Jonah said.

The clerk looked up, mildly offended. "Find her? You buy her time like any other client. Copying runs eight coppers an hour, translation twelve—"

"I don't want her pen. I want the woman."

"Then you'd be wanting to redeem the bond outright. Two hundred forty crowns plus penalty of early redemption, forty percent."

Three hundred thirty-six crowns. Jonah had fought a war once for eleven. "No," he said. "I'll be taking her instead."

The clerk's face went through several expressions, none of them friendly. "That would be theft, master mercenary. The Watch is slow in Vell but not *that* slow."

Jonah put both hands flat on the ledger desk and leaned in until the clerk could smell the road on him. "Then I'd best be quick."

---

He found Sefa Vael in the copying room on the second floor, at the worst desk, under the worst light, surrounded by other bond-scribes who worked with the hunched silence of people being paid in years rather than coin. She was maybe fifty, thin as a winter branch, gray hair cut short and practical, with ink on her fingers to the second knuckle. She didn't look up when he stopped at her desk. She just kept copying whatever contract of indenture or deed of sale lay before her, her pen moving in small furious strokes.

"You're the one who asked the clerk for me," she said. Her voice was low and rough, like gravel poured slowly. "I heard your boots stop. Everyone stops at my desk first. Curiosity about the disgraced one."

"I need a translator."

"I'm not for hire. I'm for sale. Different thing entirely, whatever the law pretends." She turned a page. "Go find a Collegium man. They'll charge you less than I cost."

"The language I need is First-Tongue."

Her pen stopped.

For a long moment the only sound in the room was the scratch of nine other pens, and Jonah watched the woman go very still in a way that reminded him unpleasantly of Wren — the stillness of prey that has decided running is worse than waiting.

"There is no First-Tongue," she said quietly.

"There is. A child speaks it. I've heard it. Half the sounds are familiar to me — oath-words, the old forms my god's priests used for binding vows. I know enough to know I don't know nearly enough, and I know that if I say the wrong syllable in front of the wrong ears, something very bad happens."

Sefa finally looked up. Her eyes were pale brown, sharp, and tired in the way of someone who had been awake for years. "You have oath-training," she said. Not a question. "Which god?"

"That stopped mattering when it went silent."

"It never stops mattering." She set down her pen and folded her hands, and just like that she was a different person — not a scribe but a scholar, and beneath the scholar something harder, something that had survived a fall. "First-Tongue. In the Collegium we called it the Root Speech, because every tongue in the valley kingdoms carries its bones. The Church calls it the Language of Making. The Tower of Tongues calls it nothing at all, because officially it doesn't exist, and unofficially—" her mouth twisted, "—unofficially they breed children to speak it. So yes, mercenary. I believe you about your child. And now you understand why I'm sitting at this desk instead of a lectern."

"They dismissed you for studying it."

"They dismissed me for finding it. There's a difference. Plenty of fools chase dead languages; the Collegium finds that charming. But I traced the root forms back past the founding liturgies, past the oldest hymnals, into the binding-formulas — and then I made the mistake of saying a word aloud correctly." She held up her right hand. The fingers trembled, faintly, constantly, like a plucked string that never quite settled. "This is what a true word does to an unprepared speaker. It took my career. It nearly took more. The Collegium called it nerve damage. I call it the price of admission."

"And now you copy contracts."

"And now I copy contracts." She smiled without any warmth at all. "Seven years left on my bond. Ask your question, mercenary, and then leave me to my pen."

"I'm not asking. I'm offering." Jonah pulled out the chair across from her and sat, which made the chair complain and two neighboring scribes flinch. "I'm going to break you out of here. Tonight. In exchange, you teach me and the child everything you know about First-Tongue, starting with how to speak it without it eating us alive."

Sefa stared at him. Then she laughed — one dry bark, quickly smothered. "Break me out. Of the House of Meric. In Vell. Where the magistrates sell unbound children to the crown in open session and call it charity." She shook her head. "Do you know what happens to a redeemed debtor who flees? They're marked. No guild takes them, no house rents to them, no road-papers are issued. You wouldn't be freeing me, you'd be exiling me."

"You've been exiled already. This is just exile with a desk."

Something flickered across her face — pain, quickly buried, but real. "You're blunt."

"People tell me."

"Blunt men die fast in Vell." She glanced toward the door, toward the windowless room below where the clerk guarded his ledger of human arithmetic. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, and Jonah had to lean close to catch it. "If I did this — if I were fool enough — there are conditions. First: the child learns alongside you. If the Tower wants her, she needs the tongue more than you do, and unlike you she may actually survive speaking it."

"She will learn. That's why I came."

"Second: we leave tonight, before the evening count. Third—" she hesitated, and for a moment the scholar's armor slipped and Jonah saw the woman underneath, seven years deep in a debt she hadn't chosen, "—third, when this is done, whatever this is, you tell me what the child is fleeing. All of it. I sold my life to that language once. I'll not walk into its teeth blind again."

"Done," Jonah said.

She studied him for a long moment. "You swear it? On your oath? Whatever's left of it?"

And there it was — the strange cold weight behind his sternum, the ember of the vow that hadn't warmed in six years, stirring the way it had stirred against the crown soldiers on the ridge. He didn't understand it. He wasn't sure he trusted it. But he put his hand over his heart anyway, over the scarred place where the oath lived, and said, "On my oath. What's left of it, and what isn't."

Sefa Vael closed her eyes briefly, as if praying to a god she'd also lost. "Then tonight," she said, "you'll get your first lesson free. Here it is: in First-Tongue, there is no word for *promise*. Only *bind*. Remember that when you make them."

---

Breaking Sefa out of the House of Meric proved easier than Jonah had expected, mostly because nobody guards against the thing everyone knows is impossible. Debt-houses in Vell relied on paper, not iron — the belief that the mark of bondage was inescapable was itself the lock. Jonah simply walked Sefa out the front door during the evening shift change, walking beside her like a hired guard escorting a scribe to a client's estate, and when the door-warden glanced up, Jonah showed him the bored expression of a man being paid by the hour. The warden nodded them through.

They were three streets gone before Sefa's breathing steadied. "That's it," she said, half laughing, half shaking. "Seven years of my life, and the lock was *nothing*. Just fear wearing a uniform."

"Fear makes the best lock," Jonah said. "Cheapest to build, hardest to pick." He thought of a valley where magic couldn't follow, and a shrine he'd swept for twenty years, and said nothing else.

Brannit's yard was dark except for one lantern burning in the lean-to, and Wren was awake — of course she was awake, sitting cross-legged on a crate with her arms wrapped around her knees, watching the gate. When Jonah came through it she was on her feet and moving before he could kneel, and she collided with him at full speed, arms around his waist, forehead pressed hard against his ribs. Not a hug, exactly. An inspection. Confirming he was solid.

"I'm here," he said, and rested one hand on her head. "Told you."

Wren stepped back and looked past him at Sefa, and her whole body changed — went wary, coiled, the way she'd been the night she arrived on his doorstep. She looked at Sefa the way she'd looked at the crown riders' horses.

"This is Sefa," Jonah said. "She's going to teach us. She's like you."

"I am nothing like her," Sefa said softly, and then she crouched, slow and deliberate, bringing herself down to the child's height the way Jonah had learned to. "But I know what they did in the Tower of Tongues. Or some of it. Enough to hate it properly." She extended one ink-stained, trembling hand, palm up, and waited.

Wren stared at the trembling fingers. Then, carefully, she reached out and touched the palm with one fingertip — and her own hand began to shake in answer, a sympathetic resonance, like two strings tuned alike. Both of them snatched back at the same moment.

Sefa exhaled. "Well," she said. "There's our proof, if I needed any. Resonance like that doesn't happen between strangers. It happens between speakers." She looked up at Jonah, and her pale eyes had gone bright with something that might have been hope or hunger. "Get the lamp. We start now. I've waited seven years to use this tongue for something other than copying shipping manifests, and I find I'm impatient."

---

The first lesson happened on Brannit's floor, by lamplight, with a board of smooth pine and a piece of charcoal.

"Before words," Sefa said, drawing a line down the center of the board, "understand what this language *is*. Your valley tongues describe. They point at things and name them. First-Tongue doesn't describe. It *commands*. Every true sentence in it is a small act of making. When you speak it truly — and truly means with right breath, right intent, right understanding, all three together — reality leans toward the word." She tapped the charcoal line. "This is why the Tower breeds vessels. Most people can't hold a true word. Their bodies refuse it, like mine refused. But a vessel is made to carry it."

Wren watched the charcoal move with total attention, and Jonah watched Wren, and what he saw frightened him: she wasn't learning. She was *recognizing*. Her lips moved slightly, ahead of Sefa's chalk sometimes, shaping sounds before the demonstration came.

"Start simple," Jonah said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "A safe word. Something that can't hurt anyone."

"There are no safe words," Sefa said. "Only small ones. Very well." She wrote a shape on the board — not letters exactly, more like a gesture frozen mid-motion, three strokes that somehow implied a fourth. "*Sel.* In the root forms it means *warm*, but truly spoken it means *be warm*. To this stone, say." She picked up a river pebble from the yard floor, set it on the boards, and breathed once, slow, settling herself. Then she said:

"*Sel.*"

The word didn't sound like anything. That was the horror of it — it sounded almost like nothing, a soft exhale shaped around a vowel, the kind of sound a hearth makes settling. And the pebble grew warm. Jonah felt it from a foot away, a faint radiance like a hand held near a candle, though the pebble neither glowed nor smoked. It was simply warm, in a way stones are not, in a way the lamplight could not explain.

The hair rose on Jonah's arms.

"Twenty years," he said quietly. "I served a god of the hearth-flame for twenty years and I never once saw magic do anything that small and that *clean*."

"Because your god's power comes through oaths, and oaths are chains — strong, but heavy, and they pull both ways." Sefa wiped the warmth from the stone by laying her palm flat over it and murmuring something that made the air feel suddenly cool. "First-Tongue has no chain. Only the speaker's capacity. Which is why it devours the unready." She looked at Wren. "Your turn. Small word. *Sel*. Warmth only. Think of a fire you loved."

Wren picked up the pebble and cupped it in both hands, and Jonah braced himself for failure, for the shaking that had taken Sefa's hand, for—

"*Sel*," Wren said.

The word came out of her mouth rounded and perfect, and the pebble didn't grow warm. It grew *alive* — heat rolling off it in gentle waves, and above it, impossibly, a thread of flame no bigger than a grass stem stood up off the stone and swayed like something waking. Wren's eyes went wide. She looked at the little fire with an expression Jonah had never seen on her face, and it undid him: wonder. Pure, unguarded, eight-years-old wonder.

Then she flinched and crushed the flame out with her palm, and the wonder shut away behind her flat grey mask so fast it was like watching a door slam.

"No," Sefa said sharply. "Don't hide it. Look at me, child." Wren looked. "You spoke a true word on your first attempt with better form than I managed after a year of study. That is not a thing to be ashamed of. That is a weapon pointed at the people who made you — *if* you learn to aim it."

Wren's jaw set. Slowly, she opened her hand. The pebble sat there, ordinary again, still faintly warm. She looked up at Jonah, and though she said nothing — she never said anything in any tongue he knew — the look was unmistakable. *Is it safe? Are you safe? Is this safe?*

"It's safe here," Jonah said. "Tonight, in this room, with us — it's safe."

It was the second stupid promise he'd made in one day. He was starting a collection.

They worked until the lamp burned low. Sefa taught them breath — how a true word rode on a long exhale, how intent had to be held like a drawn bow. She taught them three more small words: *sel*, warmth; *dun*, stillness; *kel*, a word she translated as *open* but whose true meaning, she admitted, was closer to *become honest*. Each one Jonah spoke clumsily, and each one answered him weakly — the pebble warming barely past skin-heat, the boards going quiet under *dun*, his own chest loosening strangely under *kel*, as if something in him had unlocked one notch. But Wren's attempts rang through the room like struck bells, and each time, Sefa watched her with an expression that grew more grim and more fascinated in equal measure.

Near midnight, Wren fell asleep sitting up, mid-exercise, her head tipping sideways until it found Jonah's shoulder. He went rigid, then carefully did not move.

"She trusts you," Sefa murmured, gathering her charcoal and board. "That's rarer than the tongue itself, from a Tower child. Do you understand what she is yet? Truly?"

"A vessel. You said it yourself."

"I said what the Tower would say. I want to know what *you* think she is, because you've been watching her all night instead of watching the pebbles, and you had a look on your face I've seen before — on scholars, on priests, on men who've found something that rearranges them." She tied her board with a cord and slung it across her back. "I'll tell you what I think, mercenary. The Hollow Chorus wears the king's crown. The gods have gone silent one by one across twenty years. And now a vessel walks out of the capital barefoot, alone, straight to the one man in the Ember Valley sworn to a missing god. Vessels aren't made to escape. They're made to be *used*. So either your child fled her purpose—" Sefa paused, and in the low lamplight her face was unreadable, "—or she was sent toward yours. And I confess I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

Jonah looked down at the sleeping weight against his shoulder, small and breathing and warm as a banked coal.

"Doesn't matter," he said. "Either way, she's under my roof now. Whoever sent her can send a bill."

Sefa snorted. "Spoken like a man who's never seen the Chorus collect a debt." She stood, joints cracking, and moved to the door — then stopped, one hand on the frame, and looked back. "One more thing. Tomorrow I teach you the grammar of binding, and after that, names. And mercenary — when we reach the part where I show you the word for *unbind*..." Her trembling hand tightened on the doorframe until the wood creaked. "You should know that the last person I taught it to was taken by the Tower within the week. They always know when the unbinding-word moves. So whatever you're planning to do with what I give you, plan it fast."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the watch bell. Something thinner, stranger, a sound like a needle drawn across the night. Sefa went white.

"That's the Tower's calling-bell," she whispered. "They ring it when they lose something." Her pale eyes fixed on Wren, asleep against Jonah's shoulder. "Or when they've found where something's hiding."

Jonah eased out from under the child's weight, laid her gently on the pallet, and went to stand at the shuttered window, listening to the thin bell thread its way through Vell, street by street, closer and closer, like something patient counting doors.
