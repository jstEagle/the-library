## 9. What the Crown Remembers

Sefa had spent eleven years in the Tower of Tongues learning that every language was a weapon, and three years since her disgrace learning that the sharpest ones were kept in ledgers.

The archive beneath Vell's Bell Quarter smelled of mildew and tallow and old money — the kind of place where a disgraced linguist could rent a desk by the week if she paid in coin instead of reputation. She had a candle, two lamps she couldn't afford to light at once, and a stack of royal shipping manifests she'd bribed a customs clerk to forget he'd ever owned.

The manifests were boring. That was why they mattered.

Kings lied in proclamations. Kings lied in chronicles. But somewhere between the quartermaster who ordered candles for the renewal rite and the clerk who signed for them, somebody always told the truth by accident.

She spread the pages across the desk and weighted the corners with stones from the broken bell that gave the quarter its name. The bell had cracked forty years ago mid-peal, on the day of Aldric's first coronation, and no founder in Vell would touch it since. Sefa found that detail charming in the way she found all omens charming: as evidence that people noticed things and then chose not to understand them.

*Renewal rite, seventh year of the reign.* Candles: nine hundred. Choir: thirty voices, drawn from the capital. *Vessel stipend:* one line item, amount redacted, recipient listed only as *T.T.*

Tower of Tongues.

Her old tower. Her old masters, still drawing royal coin to ferry something — someone — to the rites. She ran her thumb along the redaction bar, black ink laid down thick enough to hide even the ghost of the letters beneath.

"You're up early."

Jonah filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. Behind him, half-hidden in the stairwell's shadow, Wren peered out with the particular alertness of a child pretending not to be interested. The girl had been with him four days now, and in those four days Sefa had watched a hardened mercenary reorganize his entire posture around a small mute child like iron filings around a lodestone.

"I'm up late," Sefa said. "There's a difference. Sit down. Both of you."

They sat. Wren climbed onto the bench beside Jonah and immediately began studying the ledger pages upside down. Sefa noted that — noted everything about the girl now — and turned her lamp toward the center of the desk.

"Before I show you this, I need you to understand what I am and am not saying." She held up one finger. "I am not saying I know what's on the king's head. I'm saying I know what the crown *costs*, and costs are a kind of confession."

Jonah said nothing. He was good at nothing; she'd give him that. Most men filled silence like a hole in a wall.

She dealt the manifests out like cards.

"Candle wax, nine hundred pounds per rite, renewed every seven years since the reign began. Incense from the salt coast — not for scent, for smoke density; there's a requisition note about visibility. Thirty singers, replaced each time, because—" she tapped a margin note, "—the choir complains of hearing loss after the second hour. Permanent hearing loss. From singing."

Wren made a small sound. Not a word. A click at the back of her throat, almost involuntary. Her eyes had gone fixed on the word *choir.*

Sefa pretended not to see. "And here. Every seventh year, the crown pays the Tower of Tongues a vessel stipend, and the Tower ships a person to the capital under seal. I was in that tower for eleven years, mercenary. I never saw the manifest line before because I was never meant to. But I remember the years the masters went quiet. I remember which students were taken from the dormitory and didn't come back." Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She let it. "I thought they were being promoted."

Jonah leaned forward. "Taken how?"

"By selection. By resonance, we called it. Certain children could hold a tone longer than others without breaking. The masters tested us for it yearly, the way a farrier tests hooves." She heard her own bitterness arrive late and unwelcome. "I scored high. I was kept. The ones who scored higher were shipped."

Wren's hand had closed around the edge of the bench. Her knuckles were white.

Sefa slid the last page across — not a manifest but a fragment, purchased at obscene cost from a burned monastery in the eastern hills, and worth every coin. It was a palimpsest: a hymnal scraped clean and written over with an accounting of tithes, except the scraping had been imperfect, and under lamplight the older text rose through the newer like drowned men surfacing.

"Read the ghost text," she said.

Jonah squinted. "It's not any script I—"

"It's First-Tongue rendered phonetically in Old Liturgical. Monks used to do it so they could chant bindings without understanding them. Which is exactly as wise as it sounds." She translated aloud, slowly, letting each word land where it had to land:

*"It came to the first god as hunger comes to a fasting man, and the god welcomed it, thinking it prayer. And it ate the prayer, and ate the god, and wore his name into the world, and none knew the god was gone, for the thing spoke with his voice and remembered with his memory and blessed with his hands. This is the Chorus. This is the Hollow Chorus, who sings with stolen throats."*

The lamp flame bent in a draft that hadn't existed a moment before. Sefa steadied it with a cupped hand and went on, because stopping was worse.

"The fragment lists names. Or rather—" she corrected herself, "—it lists *gaps* where names were. Six gods of the eastern hills. Their names are scratched out, and next to each scratch is a date going back six hundred years. One god every hundred years, give or take. Do you understand the arithmetic, mercenary?"

Jonah had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man doing sums he doesn't want finished.

"Six hundred years," he said finally. "One god a century. And my god went silent—" His jaw worked. "Four years ago."

"Which breaks the pattern," Sefa agreed. "Unless the century wasn't a schedule. Unless it was a *hunting interval*. Unless something changed four years ago that let the Chorus move faster." She pulled the final document from beneath the stones: a royal writ, sealed twice, authorizing the construction of a reliquary vault beneath the coronation hall. Dated five years ago. Built over the following year. Completed one year before Jonah's god went silent.

"They built it a cage," Jonah said quietly.

"They built it a pantry," Sefa said. "And then they stocked it."

* * *

Wren touched the palimpsest.

Sefa saw her do it and didn't stop her, mostly because the girl moved like water finding a crack — one instant sitting on the bench, the next standing over the desk with two fingers resting on the ghost text, her lips moving. No sound. Sefa had learned in their brief lessons that Wren shaped First-Tongue the way other children breathed: constantly, unconsciously, silently. The girl wasn't speaking. She was *reading aloud inside her own skull*, and whatever she read there made her face go through three expressions in the space of a breath — recognition, horror, and then a third thing, older than either, that Sefa recognized because she'd worn it herself for years in the tower dormitories.

The look of a child realizing the adults knew exactly what they were doing.

"Wren," Sefa said gently. "What does it say? The part your fingers stopped on."

The girl's mouth worked. She had no voice to give — Sefa had catalogued the scarring on her throat during that first desperate examination, the surgical precision of it, done young, done *deliberately* — but she had hands. She pointed at the ghost text, at the scratched-out names, then drew a circle in the air with one finger.

"Names," Jonah said.

She shook her head sharply. Drew the circle again. Then pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, hard, and looked at Sefa with an urgency that needed no translation.

"Not the names," Sefa said slowly. "What's *under* the names. What the names were covering." She bent close to the palimpsest, angling the lamp until the parchment's scars threw shadows. And there — she cursed, softly, comprehensively — there it was. Beneath each struck-out divine name, visible only where the scraper's blade had skipped, ran a smaller word. Repeated six times. The same word, six times, in six different grammatical cases, the way First-Tongue insisted on when describing something that existed in more than one place at once.

She wrote the root on scrap paper and pushed it to Jonah, whose oath-training had seeded fragments of the language in him like shrapnel too deep to remove.

He stared at it. His face did something complicated.

"*Chorus*," he said. "It means... no. It means both. It means the singer and the song and the space the song fills. One word for all of it." He looked up. "Why would six dead gods share a word?"

"Because they don't share it," Sefa said. "Because it isn't theirs. Because when the Chorus ate them, it filed their names away and left its own name in the empty slot — six times, because it wears six shapes, or wore them, once." She sat back, and the full shape of it settled onto her shoulders like wet wool. "It's not six gods, mercenary. It's one thing with six graves."

"And mine makes seven."

"Yours makes seven. Except yours isn't dead, is it?" She watched him flinch and pressed, because mercy could wait and truth couldn't. "You told me your oath went silent four years ago. Silent isn't eaten. Eaten leaves nothing — no warmth, no dreams, no borrowed strength when riders cross a valley that should have burned them. Something answered you on that ridge, Jonah Iversen. Something is answering through *her*." He glanced at Wren; Sefa didn't. "The Chorus eats gods. So ask yourself what a cage built for a god looks like from the inside, and ask yourself why the crown needs a child who can speak the binding tongue shipped to the capital every seven years."

The silence stretched long enough for the lamp to gutter and recover.

"A mouth," Jonah said at last, and his voice came out sanded flat. "The rite needs a speaker. Someone to say the words that keep the crown fed and the prisoner contained. And they've been breeding them in the Tower for—" He stopped. Looked at Wren. Looked away, which took visible effort. "How long?"

"Longer than me," Sefa said. "I was tested at six. The practice is older than the practice records." She gathered the manifests, squared their edges, aligned them with the compulsive neatness of a woman whose mind refused to sit still while it was afraid. "Here's the arithmetic that matters. The renewal rite is in ninety-one days. The Chorus has been eating your god for four years, slowly, the way it ate the hill gods over decades — but slow feeding requires a stable ritual cycle, and ritual cycles require vessels, and vessels require the Tower. Which means the crown's whole apparatus — the soldiers in your valley, the resonance trap they'll certainly build next, the procession, the pageantry — all of it exists to keep one feeding scheduled and one mouth available."

Wren had gone very quiet in the way that Sefa was beginning to understand was not peace but calculation. The girl looked at the palimpsest, then at the window, where the grey pre-dawn of the Bell Quarter was starting to show the rooftops, then at Jonah — measuring, Sefa realized, distances. Exits. Weights of bodies. She had seen that exact habit in tower children who survived.

Then Wren did something new. She picked up Sefa's scrap paper, took the charcoal stub, and drew.

Not words. A picture, crude but unmistakable: a crown. Inside the crown's circlet, a small figure of a man, arms crossed over his chest — drawn huddled, drawn *small*, the way you draw someone hiding. Around the outside of the crown, radiating lines, and at the end of each line a tiny mark that repeated down the page like a refrain.

"What's that?" Jonah asked.

Wren pointed at the marks. Then at her own ears. Then she covered her ears with her hands and screwed her eyes shut and shook — a small, controlled, terrible shudder, the whole performance compressed into two seconds, and Sefa felt the hair rise on her forearms because she understood before she wanted to.

"She's heard it," Sefa said. "In the Tower. They played recordings — resonances, captured tones, whatever the masters dredged out of the old sites." She knelt to bring her eyes level with the child's. "Wren. The marks. Is that what the Chorus sounds like?"

The girl nodded once. Then she added to her drawing: she took the little man inside the crown and drew a line from his open mouth to the marks, connecting them. The king was singing it. The king was the instrument it played.

Or — Sefa's stomach turned as the alternative assembled itself — the man was still in there, screaming along with the music, audible only to someone who'd been trained since infancy to hear what songs were made of.

"He's alive in there," she said aloud. "Aldric. Whatever's wearing him hasn't finished. Four years of feeding and it hasn't finished him, because finishing takes a rite, and the rite takes a mouth, and the mouth—" 

"—is sitting in my debt-bondage office eating my charcoal," Jonah finished grimly.

Wren looked up at him. And smiled. Small, crooked, entirely without fear — the smile of a child who had just heard herself described as the most dangerous object in a kingdom and found it preferable to being described as property.

* * *

After they'd gone — Jonah walking Wren home through streets that were waking, the girl's hand swallowed in his fist, the pair of them looking from behind like a mountain escorting a sparrow — Sefa stayed at the desk and did the thing she had sworn off doing for three years.

She thought about her mentor.

Magister Corvane, who had taught her prosody and etymology and the ethics of true speech, and who had signed the shipping orders. Who had held her shoulder at nineteen and said, *you have a fine ear, Sefa, but a finer mouth; the tower needs mouths that stay.* Who had chosen which children were instruments and which were furniture, and had gone to sleep afterward, presumably, without difficulty.

She had told herself for three years that he must have believed in something. Necessity. Order. Some architecture of justification tall enough to hide the dormitory windows from.

Now she had the manifests. Now she had six scratched-out names and a seventh god caged beneath a coronation hall and a king worn like a glove, and she understood that Corvane had never needed an architecture at all. He had needed only the ordinary human talent for not finishing thoughts. She had inherited that talent. She was using it right now, sitting in a mildewed archive, not thinking about the fact that every lesson she planned to teach Jonah and Wren would make them better weapons for a fight that would end, one way or another, with a child standing in front of the thing that had eaten six gods and was hungry on schedule.

Ninety-one days.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the palimpsest's ghost text seemed to linger on her retinas, and she caught herself mouthing the refrain — *this is the Chorus, this is the Hollow Chorus* — and stopped, appalled, halfway through the second clause. Eleven years in the tower had left habits in her mouth deeper than doctrine. You did not speak true words about true things without meaning them.

But the thought arrived anyway, unbidden, in the privacy of her own skull, and it arrived with the calm of a fact already decided:

If the choice came down to the girl or the god, she knew which one she would spend. She only prayed the spending wouldn't require her voice — her last possession, the one thing the tower hadn't managed to confiscate.

Somewhere across the city, the cracked bell of the Bell Quarter hung silent in its frame, as it had for forty years, waiting for someone brave enough to recast it.

Sefa put her head down on the manifests and slept for two hours, and dreamed of choirs.
