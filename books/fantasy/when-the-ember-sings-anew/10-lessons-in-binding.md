## 10. Lessons in Binding

The words came before Sefa finished teaching them.

That was the first thing Wren learned to hide: that there was nothing to learn. Sefa would chalk a glyph on the floor of the borrowed room above the dyer's shop — a spiral, a broken line, three strokes like bird tracks — and say, *this one means hold*, and Wren would nod slowly, frown at it, let her lips shape the sound wrong twice before she got it right. She had learned to do wrong things gracefully. In the Tower they called it calibration. The handlers wanted to see effort. Effort meant the vessel was filling properly.

She did not know what else to call herself. Vessel was the word they used, in their clipped Tower speech, when they thought she was asleep or drugged or too young to keep the words. *The vessel responds well. The vessel's resonance is clean.* They said it about her the way the farrier in Ember Valley talked about a good horse.

But Jonah didn't call her anything like that. Jonah called her *kid* and *sprout* once, badly, like he was testing whether she'd let him, and mostly he just called her by her name, which was the only name she had ever chosen herself. Wren. A small brown bird that lived in hedges and sang louder than its size should allow. She'd found it in a book in Sefa's crate of stolen pages, and she had pointed at the little inked drawing and then at herself, and Jonah had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Yeah. That fits."

So she gave them what they expected. She stumbled. She asked with her hands what a word meant. She let Sefa correct her grip on the speaking-stone, the smooth river pebble they used for practice because true First-Tongue spoken bare into a room could make the room listen.

And every night, after Jonah's breathing went slow and heavy in the next room, after Sefa banked the lamp, Wren lay awake and read the chalk glyphs on the floor in the dark and understood all of them.

* * *

Sefa taught the way water teaches stone: patiently, relentlessly, wearing grooves into everything.

"Again," she said, morning of the fourth day. Her voice came out sanded-down from years of disuse before Jonah broke her bonds; she spoke carefully, hoarding each word like a miser. "The binding-form. *Veth-kara-sel.* Say it slow."

Wren said it slow. Wrong. Then less wrong. Then right, and the candle flame between them stood up straight as a spear and burned blue-white for two heartbeats before collapsing back to ordinary orange.

Sefa sat back on her heels. There was chalk dust on her dark fingers and wonder fighting through the wariness on her face. "Good," she said. "Better than good. You have an ear for it."

*An ear for it.* Wren kept her face blank and pleasant the way the Tower had trained her, the small closed smile that made handlers mark *stable* in their ledgers. Inside, something cold turned over. She hadn't needed an ear. The word *veth-kara-sel* was not new to her. She had been saying it since before she could walk — not with her mouth but with the thing inside her chest the Tower had built, the hollow place shaped exactly like a keyhole. They had sung bindings into her in the crèche, syllable by syllable, year by year, so that when the time came she would not need to learn the opening words. She would simply be them.

The candle flame settled. Sefa watched her a moment too long, then turned to write in her journal.

Wren looked out the window. Down in the street, Jonah was arguing with the dyer's wife about the price of bread, his big hands spread in a gesture of wounded innocence that fooled nobody. He was very large and moved like something that could be dangerous and chose not to be, most hours. When he caught her watching he raised one hand, half wave, half salute, and mouthed something she couldn't hear through the glass.

She found, to her horror, that her eyes stung.

In the Tower there had been no window to be watched through. There had been white rooms and grey corridors and the humming of the walls, which she now knew was the Chorus, the god-eater, singing to itself in the deep crypts beneath the capital, and her small hollow keyhole-self resonating in answer every time it passed near. They had measured the resonance. They had written numbers about her longing.

She had escaped with nothing but the clothes she wore and one word held behind her teeth like a stone: *run*. The First-Tongue for *run*, spoken truly, opens doors and closes pursuit, and she had spoken it truly at age seven, alone, at night, and the great gate of the Tower of Tongues had opened for her like a mouth, and she had run until the world ran out of roads and became the Ember Valley, where magic could not follow, and where she had knocked on the door of the only lit house because the light looked like a hearthfire and she was so cold.

Jonah had opened the door. That was the whole of it. That was the entire reason she was alive and learning and lying to these people who were being kind to her.

Because if they knew what she was — what she was *for* — they might look at her the way the Tower looked at her. Or worse. They might look at her kindly anyway, but differently, the way you look at a knife someone handed you. Useful. Careful of the edge.

She wanted to be looked at like the wren in the book. Small and loud and hers.

* * *

The lessons changed shape as the days stacked up.

Sefa stopped teaching simple forms and began teaching structure — how bindings nested inside each other, how a true sentence in First-Tongue wasn't a sentence at all but a small machine with moving parts. "Language here isn't description," she said, pacing the narrow room, gesturing with a stub of chalk. "It's command. But command has grammar, and the grammar is mercy. Get one clause wrong and the binding refuses. The old speakers built refusals in deliberately. Do you understand? Even the language that binds the world was made by people who feared what it could do."

Wren nodded along. And privately, in the locked room of herself, she disagreed.

She had heard the oldest forms. Not the cleaned-up scholarly versions Sefa had reconstructed from stolen fragments — the real ones, spoken by the Archivist of Tongues in the vault beneath the Tower, where they took the older vessels to be tuned. The oldest forms had no mercy in them. The oldest forms had been built by people who wanted the world to obey and had removed every refusal they could find, and what refusals remained were accidents, cracks, flaws in the making. The Hollow Chorus had eaten gods through those flawless sentences. It was eating King Aldric through one now.

Sefa believed the language was a tool. The Tower had taught Wren it was a weapon. Both of them were wrong, Wren thought, watching chalk dust drift in the lamplight. It was neither. It was a throat. The question was only what spoke through it.

"You're drifting," Sefa said sharply.

Wren blinked, arranged the small apologetic smile. Sefa's eyes narrowed — she missed nothing, this woman, except the one enormous thing — but she let it go and wrote another note in her journal.

There was a page in that journal Wren had already read. She read things; that was another skill the Tower had ground into her, quick eyes, fast memory, the vessel's mind kept sharp because a dull vessel cracked under pressure. Two nights ago Sefa had left the journal open while she went down to argue with the dyer about rent, and Wren had read four pages standing in the middle of the room with her heart going like a trapped bird.

*Sefa's research. The crown-thing. A god-eater called the Hollow Chorus.* Words she knew. Names she had heard chanted in the tuning vaults, though the handlers used a different name, a reverent name, the name of the thing they served without naming it. And beneath the research notes, in Sefa's smaller handwriting, a line that had kept Wren awake since:

*If the vessel is the final component, then whoever holds the vessel holds the rite.*

Wren had put the journal back exactly as it lay, adjusted to the finger-width Sefa left it, and gone to bed, and stared at the ceiling beams, and understood with total clarity that she was the final component. She had always known. What was new was seeing it written in a friend's hand, in a friend's hope: not *the girl*, not *Wren*, but *the vessel*. A part. A piece. The keystone of a rite that would finish consuming a king and set a god-eater loose with a child's mouth to speak through.

They wanted to save her from that. Jonah had bled trying. She had seen the blood on the cobbles of Vell the day the crown agents took her — no. That memory was wrong-ordered; the taking hadn't happened yet. But she dreamed it anyway, the way she dreamed Tower things before they happened, resonance running ahead of time like water finding a low place. She dreamed cobbles and blood and Jonah's roar going thin and far away, and she woke with her fists clenched and did not tell anyone, because telling made dreams heavier, and these people carried enough.

What she could carry, she would carry alone. That was the vow she made to herself in the dark, night after night, a small hard seed of a vow: *they will never use me against them. Whatever they built me for, I will unbuild it from the inside.*

She just had to figure out how, before the dreaming came true.

* * *

On the ninth day, Sefa brought out the speaking-stone and did not teach.

She sat across from Wren with the stone flat on her palm between them, and her ruined voice was quieter than usual. "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to think before you answer, because I'll know if you lie. Everyone lies to me about this eventually. It's the nature of the work." She tapped the stone once. "When you speak a true form — really true, all the way down — what happens to you? Where does the power come from?"

Wren considered the question honestly, which was dangerous, so she considered the safe version of honest. "Warmth," she said, letting her voice come out uncertain. "Like... standing near a fire?"

Sefa watched her. The lamplight caught the scar tissue at the woman's throat where her voice had been taken the first time, long ago, by a mentor whose name she never said without spitting. "Hmm," Sefa said. "Fire. Interesting." She turned the stone over. "For me it was cold. Like reaching into deep water. Every speaker feels it differently, and how you feel it tells you something about what you are." She leaned forward. "You feel warmth. The old texts say warmth belongs to vessels — hollow things, made to hold power that isn't theirs. The power passes through and doesn't stay." Her eyes were very kind and very terrible. "Have you ever felt cold, Wren? When you speak?"

*A vessel.* She had said the word. She had looked straight at Wren and said it, and Wren's whole body went still with the effort of not reacting, of keeping the small smile fixed, of being a person and not a ledger entry.

"No," Wren said. "Just warm."

It was the first outright lie she had told either of them, and it tasted like ash and like freedom at once.

Sefa held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly and closed her hand around the stone. "All right," she said. "We'll go slower. There's no hurry." But there was hurry; they all felt it; the coronation renewal rite approached in the capital like weather coming over mountains. Sefa rose, gathered her chalk, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "whatever they told you you are — I've read the old texts, and I've read them wrong, and I've seen them proven wrong. Texts are just people talking. People get to surprise the texts." Then she left, and her footsteps went down the stairs, and Wren sat alone with the candle and shook, silently, all the way down.

*People get to surprise the texts.*

She held onto that like a rope over a drop. She repeated it to herself while she swept the chalk glyphs away, while she washed the cup Sefa drank from, while Jonah came stomping up the stairs in the blue hour with bread he'd won the argument over and a grin he aimed at her like a gift. "Evening, sprout. Sefa feed your head or eat it?" He set the loaf down, tore off the heel, offered it to her the way he always did, first and best piece, no ceremony, as if feeding her were simply what hands were for.

Wren took the bread. Warmth. Standing near a fire. That was what he was. That was what they both were, and she would burn down the whole Tower architecture inside herself before she let anyone — crown, Chorus, fate, even her own built purpose — turn her into a tool that cut them.

"I understand more than I say," she almost told him, later, watching him bank the hearth with the same unhurried care he gave everything he'd decided to protect. The words climbed her throat and she swallowed them back down. Not yet. Not until she knew how much truth they could hold without breaking.

Instead she said, in careful valley-speech, her third language now and getting better daily: "Teach me the song? The one you hum when you sharpen the sword."

Jonah blinked. Then he laughed, short and surprised, and patted the bench beside him. "That's not a song, sprout. That's an oath-hymn. Old marching thing my god's priests used—"

"Hum it," Wren said.

So he hummed it, gruff and half-remembered, and Wren listened with her whole self, and somewhere in the middle of the third verse she went cold all over, because she recognized the melody. The tuning vaults. The Archivist of Tongues, chanting over the younger vessels, over *her*, weaving that same tune beneath the words like a thread through cloth. The hymn of Jonah's silent god — the caged god, the prisoner — had been used as a lullaby for the things bred to feed the god-eater.

The Chorus hadn't just devoured gods. It had made them sing to their replacements.

She kept her face still. She hummed along, off-key, a child learning a song from a kind giant, and inside she was screaming, and inside the screaming a plan was beginning to take shape, small and sharp as a needle: *if they built me with his god's music, then his god's music can unbuild me.*

Three days later, the bells of Vell began to ring wrong — not their bright brass voices but a deep, resonant undertow beneath them, a vibration Wren felt in her hollow places like a hand closing — and Jonah burst through the door with his sword half-drawn and said the words she had been dreaming for weeks:

"That's royal trumpets, sprout. The king's procession just entered the city."
