## 11. The King's Procession

The bells of Vell started ringing an hour before dawn, and they did not stop.

Jonah stood at the shuttered window of Sefa's rented room above the dye-shop, watching the streets fill. They came out in their thousands — dyers and dockhands, fishwives with children on their hips, the whole gray tide of the free city pressed toward the Processional Way like water finding a channel. Someone had hung bunting overnight. It looked wrong somehow, festive colors strung over a city holding its breath.

"They love him," Wren said.

She was sitting cross-legged on the floor, working through her lesson slate. She'd taken to First-Tongue the way other children took to skipping stones — casually, effortlessly, in a way that made Jonah's teeth ache when he thought about why. Sefa had drilled her on the numerals that morning. Now she'd abandoned the slate to peer through the gap in the shutters.

"Love him," Jonah said. "Or fear what happens if they're caught not cheering."

"Both," Wren said, in the flat way she delivered most truths. "Sefa says both."

Sefa was at the table with her maps and her notes, ink-stained to the elbows, hair pinned up with whatever had been nearest — today, two paintbrushes and a fork. She didn't look up. "Both is correct. Now get away from that window before someone sees you looking nervous. Nothing marks a fugitive faster than a nervous face at a window."

Jonah let the shutter fall. Four days since the magistrate's men had come sniffing around the dye-shop asking after "a tall northerner traveling with a child." Four days since he'd watched a magistrate sell three unbound children to a crown factor with all the ceremony of a man selling surplus rope. The factor had paid in stamped silver. Nobody in the room had blinked.

He hadn't slept much since. When he did sleep, he dreamed of the shrine in Ember Valley, cold and empty, and woke with his hand half-raised in a gesture of offering that no one received.

"We should be gone already," he said. "We have what we need. The binding-words. The route. Why are we still here?"

"Because you can't fight what you don't understand." Sefa set down her pen at last. "And because leaving now means walking into the open country with crown riders on every road. The procession is our cover, Jonah. Ten thousand people on the wayside means ten thousand faces nobody's counting. We leave *with* them, mixed in, heading east while everyone else heads home." She tapped the map. "But first we watch him. I need to see the king. I need to see how far along it is."

"How far along what is?"

"The consumption." She said it the way a physician says *the rot*. "The Hollow Chorus doesn't devour in a day. It eats slowly, from the edges inward. Memory first, then will, then everything that made him a man. What's left wearing the crown by the end is just... a glove. A shape it moves its hand through." Her eyes flicked to Wren, then away. "I want to see how much of Aldric is left. Because if there's anything left, that changes what we're doing. That changes *everything* we're doing."

Jonah looked at the child on the floor. Wren was watching them both with that unnerving stillness of hers, the stillness of something bred to wait.

"And if there's nothing left?"

"Then we kill him," Sefa said simply, "and we burn the Chorus out of the corpse, and we pray the binding holds without a living anchor. And I would rather not find out the hard way which world we live in."

* * *

They went down into the crowd mid-morning, and the crowd swallowed them.

Jonah hated crowds on principle — too many angles, too many backs turned to him — but this morning the crush was armor. He kept Wren between himself and Sefa, one big hand resting on her shoulder where any observer would read a father walking his daughter to see the king. Wren wore a dyer's smock over her hair, hooded, small enough to disappear. She'd painted a smear of indigo across one cheek as disguise, then gotten so absorbed in painting it that Sefa had to take the brush away. For a moment afterward she had smiled — actually smiled, crooked and sudden — and Jonah had felt something turn over in his chest that he refused, for professional reasons, to examine.

The Processional Way ran from the Brass Gate to the harbor square, lined with the famous bells of Vell — bronze giants in their towers, silent for the moment because even Vell knew better than to ring while the crown passed beneath. The crowd packed eight deep against the barriers.

They found a spot near a fountain, close enough to see, far enough to move. Jonah bought roasted chestnuts from a vendor because a man standing still with nothing in his hands drew eyes. He handed some to Wren. She held hers in both palms like something precious and ate them one at a time, and he pretended not to notice that she'd saved three in her pocket. Saving food. He remembered doing that. You never stopped doing that, once you'd learned.

"Hood up," Sefa murmured, and then: "There. Look. The vanguard."

The sound reached them before the sight — hooves, many, in perfect rhythm. Then the vanguard swung around the corner of the Bellwrights' Row and the crowd roared, and Jonah got his first look at what the crown had become.

It wasn't soldiers, not exactly. The front rank rode in the old style, scarlet and gold, lances upright. But behind them came others — men in gray, unmarked, riding with the loose-legged ease of professionals, and Jonah knew professionals when he saw them. Tower men. And behind *them*, rolling slow on a wagon bed, came a thing wrapped in canvas and chained down with black iron, and the air around it made his oath-stirring ache deep in his sternum, the way a tooth aches before the bad night.

"What is that?" he asked.

"A resonance engine." Sefa's voice had gone thin. "For catching speakers. Like the trap they used on—" She stopped. She'd almost said Wren's name. "Like the one they used before."

Wren had gone very still beside him. Not frightened-still. Listening-still. Her head was cocked slightly, the way she did when she heard something no one else could hear, and her lips were moving without sound, shaping words in a language older than kingdoms.

"Wren." He crouched to her level, put his body between her and the road. "What do you hear?"

Her eyes focused on him slowly, like surfacing from deep water. "It's singing," she whispered. "The thing on the wagon. It's singing the counting-song. The one they sang in the Tower when they measured us." Her fingers found his sleeve and gripped. "Jonah. It's counting *speakers*. If it counts me—"

"Then we're not standing here when it does." He straightened. "Sefa. How long until the king passes?"

Minutes, maybe. The vanguard was still coming, the main column behind it. Too far to retreat the way they'd come; the crowd had packed solid behind them.

Sefa's face had paled under her freckles. "The side street. Behind the chandlery. There's an alley that runs down to the tanners' quarter —"

"Go. Now. Both of you. I'll follow."

"You'll—"

"I'm going to stand here like a man watching a parade," Jonah said, "because that's all I am right now, to anyone looking. Two people hurrying away draw eyes. One more gawker doesn't. Go."

Sefa hesitated — he could see her weighing it, hating it — then took Wren's hand and began easing backward through the press. Wren twisted once to look at him, indigo smear bright on her cheek, and the look she gave him was the one that had been undoing him since the storm night: *come back.*

He turned to the barriers. Chestnut in hand. Gawking.

* * *

The king came into view at the far end of Bellwrights' Row, and the sound the crowd made was not a roar. It was something lower and stranger — awe with fear inside it, the noise people make for a thing they cannot decide whether to worship or flee.

King Aldric rode a white horse, alone, twenty feet ahead of his honor guard. He sat perfectly straight. Perfectly still. His armor was ceremonial white chased with gold, and above his brow sat the crown, and even at eighty yards Jonah felt it.

His oath answered it. That was the horror of it. Twenty years of silence, and now, in the presence of the thing that had caged his god, the old warmth kindled in his chest like a traitor — heat crawling up his arms, strength humming in his bones, the god's power straining toward the crown-thing the way a dog strains toward the whip that beats it. Toward its master. Or its jailer. He couldn't tell anymore, and the not-telling made him sick.

*Away,* he told it silently. *Down. Be quiet.* The heat banked, sullen.

Then the horse brought the king closer, and Jonah forgot his own body entirely.

Because King Aldric's face — the face the coin-stamps showed, serene and remote — was not serene. Up close, at thirty yards, Jonah could see what the distance hid. The king's jaw was clenched hard enough to whiten at the hinge. Sweat stood on his temple despite the cool morning. His hands on the reins were wrong — not relaxed, not posed, but *gripping*, knuckles bloodless, as if the reins were a rail at the edge of a cliff. His eyes stared straight ahead with a fixed, terrible focus, the focus of a man reciting something over and over in his head to keep from screaming.

And then, as the horse carried him past the fountain, past Jonah — ten yards, eight, six — the king's gaze slid sideways.

It found Jonah's.

Jonah had been stared at by kings' men and killers and gods. He knew every flavor of it. This was none of them. This was the stare of a drowning man who has spotted a rope and knows, even as he reaches, that reaching will drag the rescuer under too.

The king's lips moved. No sound. Just a shape, formed and re-formed, three times, four times, as the horse walked on:

*Help me.*

Or maybe — Jonah would torture himself with this for days — maybe it was only the wind, or a trick of light, or his own guilt making patterns out of nothing. But then Aldric's right hand lifted from the reins, a tiny motion, a twitch no one else would read as anything, and the fingers curled twice against the palm.

Twice. Again twice.

The old mercenary signal. *Two friendlies. Two of ours. Hold position.*

Every soldier who'd ever served the valley companies knew it. Every soldier who'd ever fought beside Jonah Iversen knew it.

The king was signaling him. The king was *still in there*, buried alive in his own body under years of the Chorus, and he had seen one face in ten thousand — one scarred, oath-marked, valley-born face — and spent whatever scrap of will he'd clawed back to say *I know you, I know your kind, hold, help.*

The oath flared in Jonah's chest like a struck match, and this time he couldn't tell whose voice it burned with.

Then the gray men on their horses shifted. One of the Tower riders turned his head — unhurried, deliberate — and scanned the barrier line. His gaze crawled along the crowd the way the resonance engine's song crawled, methodical, tasting. It crossed Jonah, paused—

—and stopped.

The rider raised one hand. Made a small circling gesture. Farther back, near the wagon, men in gray dismounted and began to work along the barriers, unhurried, pleasant, checking papers, asking questions. Working toward him.

*They counted me.* Not Wren — they hadn't gotten close enough for her. Him. The oath-flare. His god's power had sung out like a struck bell, and the engine had heard it, and now the bell was being hunted through a crowd of ten thousand.

Jonah did the math in half a heartbeat. Thirty feet to the alley mouth. Forty gray men. One oath-burned mercenary who could not afford to use the very power that had betrayed him.

He dropped the chestnuts, pulled his hood low, and walked.

Not fast. Fast draws eyes. He walked with the shuffling patience of a man who'd seen what he came to see, drifting with the crowd's edge, angling toward the chandlery, and the gray men were twenty feet off and closing, and somewhere behind him the resonance engine's song climbed a note —

A hand closed on his arm.

He spun, fist cocked, and stopped himself barely in time. Sefa. Hood back, furious, terrified.

"The alley's blocked," she breathed. "Crown guards at both ends. Checking everyone. They're sweeping the whole district — they've sealed the tanners' quarter. Wren's inside the chandlery cellar with the dyer's wife, but Jonah, there are men at the mouth of the alley and one of them has a slate with names on it and I swear on every dead god I saw my own name."

Behind them, the gray men reached the spot where Jonah had been standing. He heard one of them ask the chestnut vendor a question. Heard the vendor shrug.

"Sefa." His voice came out very calm, the way it always did when calm was a lie he was telling with his whole body. "How well do you know the undercity?"

She blinked. "The undercity? The old flood-tunnels? Nobody goes down there. The guilds sealed them forty years ago after the collapse —"

"Forty years ago," Jonah said, turning to scan the street, "somebody unsealed them. Somebody always does. Smugglers, debt-runners, worse. Find me that somebody. Find me a door. Because in about two minutes that rider finishes describing my face to his friends, and then every gate out of Vell closes, and the next place we hide needs to be a place with no gates at all."

Sefa stared at him. Then, slowly, she grinned — a wild, desperate, scholarly grin, the grin of a woman who has just been handed a research problem shaped exactly like a death sentence.

"Bellwrights' Row," she said. "The old bell-foundry. It flooded in the collapse. The guild sealed the lower works, but the foundry master drinks, and debts, and I know for a fact he owes money to men who keep doors open that should stay shut. Give me a quarter hour."

"Take ten minutes," Jonah said. "And Sefa — get Wren. Wherever we go next, we go together, and we go *now*."

She squeezed his arm once and melted into the crowd.

* * *

The bell-foundry's lower works smelled of rust, river water, and decades of secrets.

The smuggler — a soft-spoken woman named Brann with a boatman's shoulders and a moneylender's eyes — took Sefa's silver without counting it and led them down a ladder into the dark with a lamp held high. Wren rode on Jonah's back, arms locked around his neck, her chin tucked against his shoulder. She hadn't spoken since the chandlery. She was trembling, finely, continuously, like a wire under tension, and he pretended not to feel it because pretending not to feel it was kinder than naming it.

At the bottom, Brann lifted the lamp, and the light fell across a vaulted tunnel running away into blackness — old Vell, drowned Vell, brick and stone older than any kingdom on the surface.

"Rules," Brann said. "You pay the toll each week. You don't bring light bigger than mine. You don't sing, whistle, hum, or make any sound the stone might carry. And you especially—" her eyes settled on Wren, and something in them flickered, some old recognition, some old grief — "you don't let anything down here hear the little one speak."

"Why?" Jonah asked.

Brann's mouth twisted. "Because things live in the deep tunnels that listen," she said. "And the last child who came through here talking to the dark..." She shook her head. "Ask me again when you've survived a week. Door's through there. Don't die loudly."

She was gone up the ladder, and the dark closed in, and Jonah set Wren down on the wet stone.

In the lamplight she finally looked up at him. Indigo still smeared on her cheek. Eyes enormous.

"He's still fighting," she signed. She'd learned signs from him, these past weeks — his own crude field-signals, expanded word by word, their private bridge across her silence. "The king. I heard him. Under the singing. All the way across the square. He was saying *help* in the old words. The true ones. Over and over. Nobody else heard it."

Jonah crouched. His knees cracked. He was getting old for this, he thought distantly. Old, and hunted, and standing in a drowned city with a child who could hear a king screaming through a crown.

"Then we help him," Jonah said.

"It's a trap," Wren signed, swift and certain. "It's *always* a trap. They let you see him struggling. They want you to hope. Hope makes people stupid. The Tower taught—" She stopped. Her hands stilled. Something moved behind her eyes, some memory surfacing that she pushed down hard, and Jonah watched her do it and understood, suddenly, that there were rooms inside this child he had never been shown, and some of them had locks on the inside.

"Wren," he said quietly. "What did the Tower teach you?"

She looked at him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Water dripped somewhere far off in the dark, patient as a clock.

"That hope is a door," she signed at last, slower now. "And doors open from both sides."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone and brick, the bells of Vell began to ring again — the great farewell peal, the procession departing, the king riding east toward his capital and his coronation renewal rite and the thing that wore him. The sound rolled down through the earth and made the water tremble and set the drowned old bricks humming, and Wren flinched at each note, and Jonah gathered her against his shoulder and held her there while the city above rang itself hollow.

When the last bell faded, he spoke into the dark, mostly to himself, mostly to keep from screaming:

"Hold on, Your Majesty. Two of ours. Hold position."

Somewhere far below them, deeper than Brann's rules, deeper than the drowned streets, something in the dark heard the words — and answered with a sound like a thousand voices breathing in.
