## 12. Beneath the Bell Tower

The bell tower of Vell had a shadow, and the shadow had a door.

Sefa found it the way she found everything — by being the kind of person nobody bothered to watch. While Jonah argued with a fishmonger about whether his coin was clipped and Wren stood very still, radiating that particular silence that made strangers look away, Sefa drifted down the alley behind the tower and counted stones. She had spent nine years in debt-bondage learning to count things other people missed. Mortar seams. Drain grates. A section of wall where the moss grew wrong, fed by warmth from below.

"There," she said, when Jonah came back with his fish and his bruised dignity.

He looked at the blank wall. "There's nothing there."

"There's heat there." She crouched, pressed her palm to the stone. "Something underneath is breathing. Ovens, or people, or both."

Wren touched the wall too, then looked up at Sefa and made a small sound — not a word, but a shape of one. The child had been making more sounds lately. Sefa had begun keeping notes on them, tucked into the false binding of a hymnal she'd stolen from a shrine out of spite. *Vell undercity,* she wrote now, mentally. *Wren approves.*

Jonah knocked. Of course he knocked. He was a man who knocked on walls because somewhere in twenty years of service to a silent god, someone had told him doors opened for the honest, and he'd believed it.

The wall said nothing back.

Then it said something better. A voice, muffled, dry as old paper: *"Who taught you to knock like a soldier?"*

"A soldier," Jonah said.

A pause. Then grinding stone, a section swinging inward on hinges older than the city above, and lamplight spilled out along with the smell of woodsmoke, tallow, and — Sefa catalogued it before she could stop herself — *stew. Real meat. Someone down here has money.*

The man in the doorway had a crossbow held low and easy, and the face of someone who had once been handsome before life got its teeth in. His eyes went over each of them: Jonah first, longest; Wren second, with an expression that flickered through recognition of something and settled on pity; Sefa last, barely at all.

"You're Iversen," he said.

"You know me?"

"Everyone who ever served knows your name. You're the one whose god never answered." He stepped aside. "Come in before someone sees you standing in a doorway that doesn't exist."

* * *

Below Vell was another Vell. Sefa had known this intellectually — every free city sat atop its own sediment, debtors and deserters and the disappeared — but knowing and walking were different things. They descended a spiral of stairs cut from the tower's original foundation, past niches where old bells had been buried rather than recast, until the stairs opened into a vaulted chamber lit by dozens of lamps and warmed by three iron stoves.

There were perhaps forty people down here. Sefa counted automatically. Most wore the loose, practical clothes of people who expected to move fast. Weapons hung everywhere, but casually — a sword used as a poker, a spear propping shutters, knives in bread. And everywhere, on wrists and throats and forearms, the same mark: faded brands, scarred oaths, the ghost-shapes of vows burned off or worn away.

Oath-breakers. Or oath-orphans. The distinction mattered, Sefa suspected, mostly to themselves.

"Forty-one," she murmured to Jonah. "Two are watching the entrances. So thirty-nine fighters, maybe fewer able-bodied."

"Stop counting," Jonah said, without heat.

"I can't. It's what I'm for."

A woman came toward them through the smoke, and Sefa watched Jonah's whole body change.

She was tall, grey-shot black hair braided back, a burn scar covering the left side of her jaw like a map of some country neither of them wanted to visit. She walked like a person whose knees had opinions. And Jonah — Jonah, who had faced crown riders and debt-collectors and Sefa's own worst moods without blinking — stopped walking entirely, as if the floor had reached up and held his boots.

"Kestrel," he said.

"Hello, Jonah." Her voice was rough and warm, gravel in honey. "You look terrible."

"You look—"

"Older? Yes. It happens when you stop dying for a living." She glanced at Wren, then at Sefa. "Yours?"

"He's mine," Sefa said, before she could think about it. "The girl is ours. Long story."

"Long stories are the only currency we take." Kestrel turned. "Marrow! Two more bowls. The big one eats like a siege."

They sat at a long table scarred by decades of knives and arguments, and stew arrived in bowls that were, Sefa noted with professional envy, actual silver, badly tarnished. Around them the room went on with its evening — a man teaching three children dice games that were definitely gambling lessons, a woman restringing a bow, two people arguing quietly over a map of the capital marked with red ink.

"Twenty years ago," Kestrel said, "you and I held the bridge at Sorrel Ford for the Ashen Company. You remember."

"Three days. No pay."

"No god, either. Mine went quiet the year before. Yours—" she tipped her bowl toward him, "—never spoke at all, if the stories are right. But we both felt it, didn't we? When it went. Like a tooth pulled. You don't miss the tooth, you miss the tongue finding the gap."

Sefa watched Jonah's hands. They had gone still around the bowl, and stillness on Jonah Iversen was louder than most men's shouting.

"When did yours go silent?" he asked.

"Fourteen years ago. Same as everyone's, near enough. Whole generation of sworn soldiers woke up one morning talking to air." Kestrel's scarred jaw tightened. "We called it the Quiet. Thought it was a plague. Some of us starved because our strength left with the vows. Some of us—" she gestured at the room, the brands, the bowstrings, "—came down here and decided that if the gods were done with us, we'd be done waiting."

"And the ones who didn't come down here?" Sefa asked.

"Became crown property. There's a bounty on unsworn oath-talents. The Tower of Tongues buys them alive." Kestrel's eyes moved to Wren, and this time the pity was unmistakable, and so was something harder underneath it. "You know that already. That's why you're running."

"We need to reach the capital," Jonah said. "Before the coronation renewal rite."

The room did not go silent — rooms full of veterans don't do dramatic silences — but it *slowed*. Conversations dropped half a register. The map-arguers stopped arguing. Kestrel set down her spoon with great care, the way people do when they're buying time to not say the first thing they thought.

"The renewal rite," she repeated. "Where the Hollow Chorus finishes eating King Aldric."

Sefa kept her face neutral, which took work, because *finish eating* was not phrasing she'd used yet, and this woman had just handed them a piece of the puzzle they hadn't earned. "You know what it is."

"We know what it takes." Kestrel leaned forward. "Every mercenary company in the last century lost its god to the Quiet. We assumed plague. Then one of ours — Brannic, you remember Brannic, Jonah, big fool with the lute — got captured at the border and dragged to the capital, and came back eleven months later with no tongue and a head full of what he'd seen in the Tower archives. He drew it for us before he died." She pulled a cord at her neck. From beneath her shirt came a wooden pendant, and she unfolded it — no, it *opened*, hinged like a locket, revealing a small square of vellum covered in tight, careful charcoal lines.

A crown. Drawn in sections, exploded outward like a diagram of a machine. And threaded through every section, filaments — roots, Sefa thought, or veins — all converging on a central mass rendered in dense scribbled darkness, with a single word beneath in First-Tongue.

Sefa read it aloud before she could stop herself, because reading First-Tongue aloud was a habit she should have died out of years ago:

"*That which sings in hollow places.*"

The word landed in the room like a dropped coin. Several heads turned. One woman rose halfway from her seat, hand on a knife, looking at Wren — no, at *Sefa*, at her mouth, as if the syllables had been visible coming out of it.

"You speak it," Kestrel said quietly.

"Fragments. Badly." Sefa closed her mouth and wished she'd closed it sooner. "I was Tower-trained. Before my fall. Before the debts."

"Then you know more than we do, and we know it kills kings." Kestrel turned the locket toward Wren. "And you, little one. Where did they keep you?"

Wren looked at the drawing for a long time. Her small finger rose and touched the dark central mass — not the crown, not the king, but the thing inside it — and she tapped it twice, deliberate, and then shook her head with a finality that needed no translation.

"She was there," Jonah said. "In the Tower. They bred her for it."

The room's temperature changed. Sefa felt it happen — forty veterans recalculating one small girl from *pitiable* to *significant* in a single breath, and hating themselves for it even as their eyes changed. She recognized the arithmetic because she'd done it herself, weeks ago, on a night road. Being useful was a kind of safety. It was also the trap Wren had been born into.

"She's not a tool," Jonah said, to the whole room, in the voice he probably used on battlefields. "Whatever you're thinking. Whatever use you imagine. She's a child who likes eel pie and hides Sefa's shoes and laughs at bad jokes. That comes first."

Silence, real this time.

Then Kestrel smiled — slow, crooked, the scar pulling white at her jaw — and said, "Still insufferable. Good. We have too few people who mean it." She stood. "Walk with me, Jonah. You especially. There's something you need to hear before you decide anything, and it's better said away from the stew and the children."

* * *

They walked the undercity's perimeter while Sefa stayed below with Wren — a decision Sefa resented and understood. Kestrel gave them privacy the way garrisons give privacy: none at all, but plenty of noise elsewhere. Sefa spread her stolen hymnal on the table and worked, and let Wren lean against her arm, and listened with the part of her mind that never stopped listening.

Underneath the ordinary sounds — the stoves ticking, the dice lesson gone wrong, someone singing badly — the undercity breathed with a rhythm Sefa slowly identified as drills. Footfalls in pattern. Weapon calls in low voices. These weren't refugees hiding. These were soldiers maintaining readiness in a hole in the ground, waiting for something.

Waiting, she realized, for exactly what Jonah was carrying through their door.

When the two of them came back, Jonah's face was doing something complicated. Sefa had begun, in recent weeks, to read him — a skill she'd once applied to dead languages and now applied to a mercenary's weather. What she saw now was a man who had been offered a drink after a long thirst and was deciding whether to trust the cup.

"Well?" she asked.

"They want to help us get into the capital," Jonah said. "Tunnels. Old smuggler ways under the Processional Road. Kestrel's people have been mapping them for six years."

"And the price?"

"There isn't one. That's what's wrong with it." He sat down heavily. "They're offering because I'm one of them. Because my god went quiet too. Because—" he rubbed his face, "—because Kestrel thinks the Chorus can be killed, and that whoever breaks it will need an army's worth of people who've already lost everything and aren't afraid to lose the rest."

"That's not an army. That's a funeral with structure."

"Probably both."

Wren tugged Sefa's sleeve. When Sefa looked down, the child pointed at Jonah, then pressed her small fist against her own chest, over the heart, then opened the hand outward — a gesture Sefa had seen her make only once before, on the night the valley's riders had come, the night the oath had stirred. An offering. A question.

*Kestrel's people. Are they like you? Can they be trusted?*

"I don't know," Sefa answered honestly, in plain speech the child couldn't follow, trusting tone to carry what words couldn't. "But I've spent nine years learning that institutions fail and individuals don't always. Let's find out which these are."

Kestrel returned with a slate and chalk, and laid out the tunnel route while Sefa copied it into memory and then into the hymnal's margin. Four days' travel underground to within a mile of the capital's outer wall. Supply caches every league, maintained by couriers who didn't know what they carried. Safe houses at either end.

"You'll want guides," Kestrel said. "Brannic's route has two collapses since his drawings. I'll send Tam and Orla — best crawlers I have."

"Why?" Jonah asked it plainly, finally, the question he'd been circling all evening. "Why spend six years mapping tunnels to a palace you can't storm? Why keep forty soldiers ready in a cellar? What were you waiting for?"

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. At Wren. At Sefa. At the faded brand on her own wrist, which she turned over slowly, like a card.

"For someone with a reason to walk in the front door," she said. "We have rage, Jonah. Rage digs tunnels fine but it can't speak to a god. You've got a child who talks in the language the Chorus wears, a Tower linguist with something to prove, and an oath twenty years old that hasn't finished with you yet." She snapped the slate case shut. "We were waiting for a key. Now eat your stew. We leave at second bell."

* * *

Second bell came faster than Sefa wanted. She'd hoped for a night of sleep; instead she got a pallet, four hours, and Wren curled against her side like a burr, breathing in the deep rhythm of a child who had learned to sleep anywhere because nowhere was safe.

Sefa lay awake and did the thing she did instead of praying: she reviewed.

The locket-diagram, reproduced from memory in the margin of the hymnal. The First-Tongue phrase — *that which sings in hollow places* — cross-referenced against three Tower texts she'd memorized during her disgrace and one she'd stolen from Sefa-the-debtor's own master's shelf. The Hollow Chorus. A god-eater. Crowned. Fed by a renewal rite that was, by every account, days away.

And beneath all of it, the thing she hadn't said aloud, not to Jonah, not to anyone: the binding-word that could unravel it would require a speaker willing to pay whatever price the language exacted. True speech always cost. She had watched masters lose fingers, voices, years. Whatever unbound the Chorus would take something commensurate.

She looked at Wren's sleeping face and felt the future arrange itself with horrible clarity.

No, she thought. Not the child. Whatever the cost was, the Tower had taken enough from this one.

Footsteps. Jonah, moving quietly through the lamplight with a pack on one shoulder, checking straps. He caught her awake and crouched beside her pallet, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because they'd developed the rare friendship of two people who didn't require it.

"Kestrel says you knew Brannic's name before she showed the drawing," he said finally. "You read the label upside down across the table."

"It's a short phrase."

"It's a phrase from a language three people in this room can read." He paused. "What else haven't you told me?"

Sefa weighed it. Nine years of debt teaches you to hoard information like coin. But this man had broken her chains with a hammer and a threat, and this child had fallen asleep trusting her ribs, and hoarding had gotten her exactly nowhere worth being.

"The unbinding will cost a voice," she said. "Maybe more. True words take payment, and the bigger the truth, the bigger the toll. I've known it for three weeks. I've been trying to find another way. There isn't one."

Jonah absorbed this the way he absorbed everything — slowly, completely, without flinching. Then he said, "Then we'll make sure it's not hers."

"Not yours either. You break an oath tomorrow-ish and you might lose everything the god gave you. Your memories. Your strength. Possibly your sense of direction, though frankly you never had one."

"That's fair."

"Jonah." She sat up, keeping her voice under the room's noise. "If it comes down to a choice between my voice and her freedom, I'll pay. I need you to not argue with me in front of her when the moment comes, because I'll need the seconds."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded, once, the way soldiers seal contracts, and Sefa understood she had just acquired something she hadn't possessed since the Tower: an ally who knew the full ledger.

Above them, faintly, the second bell began to ring.

All around the vault, forty oath-breakers rose in the dark without a lamp being lit, moving on muscle and memory, and the undercity that had seemed like refuge revealed itself as what it truly was — a drawn bow, six years at full draw, finally given something to aim at.

Kestrel appeared out of the gloom with two children-sized packs and a grim smile. "Tam and Orla meet us at the north cistern. From there it's tunnels all the way to the capital's feet." She tossed Sefa a hooded cloak. "Welcome to the company, linguist. Try not to get us all killed with a word."

"No promises," Sefa said, and woke Wren gently, and followed the ghosts of soldiers up into the dark.
