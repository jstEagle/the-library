## 13. The Price of the Broken Oath

The cell beneath the bell tower smelled of tallow and old stone, and it was fuller than Jonah had expected. Twelve oath-breakers sat around a scarred table that had been dragged down here piece by piece, judging by the mismatched legs. Candles guttered in bottles. Maps hung from nails driven into the mortar — maps of the capital, of the Tower of Tongues, of the procession routes through Vell, annotated in three different hands.

Maren stood at the head of the table. She hadn't changed much in nine years. Broader through the shoulders, maybe, a white scar splitting one eyebrow, but she still had the way of standing like a wall that had decided where it wanted to be. She'd fought beside him at Redford's Crossing, back when his god still answered, back when he'd been something more than a man with a sword.

"You look older," she said.

"You don't," Jonah lied.

She almost smiled. "Sit down, Iversen. You're going to want to be sitting for this."

He sat. Wren pressed close against his side on the bench, close enough that he could feel her breathing. Sefa took the chair across from him, her ink-stained fingers laced together, watching Maren with the flat attention of someone cataloguing everything for later use.

"Tell me what breaking an oath does," Jonah said. "All of it. Don't soften it."

Maren didn't soften it. He'd give her that.

* * *

"The oath isn't a contract," she began. "You've thought of it as a bargain — service for strength, prayer for power. That's how they teach it, because that's how men can stomach it. But it's not a bargain. It's a grafting."

She held up her own hand, turned it over. In the candlelight Jonah could see the faint silver tracery beneath her skin, running up from the wrist like frost on a window. His own arms carried the same marks; he'd stopped noticing them years ago.

"When your god answered you, it didn't lend you anything. It grew into you. Rooted. The strength in your shoulders, the speed, the years it's added to your life — those aren't gifts sitting in a purse you can hand back. They're grown through you. Bone-deep. Memory-deep."

"Memory," Sefa repeated sharply.

"Memory." Maren met Jonah's eyes. "The oath lives in the same place your life lives, Iversen. The oldest parts of it. When you tear the oath out, you don't get to choose what comes with it."

The candles flickered. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, Vell's bells rang the third hour, and the sound came down into the cell like rain falling on a roof very far away.

"How much?" Jonah asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later. "How much memory?"

Maren shrugged, and the shrug was worse than a number would have been. "Different for everyone. Torv broke his oath four years ago. He lost his left hand's worth of skill — couldn't hold a knife for a season, had to learn again like a child." She nodded at a heavyset man down the table who didn't look up. "Ilse lost a language. Woke up one morning and couldn't remember her mother's voice at all. And Dain—"

"Dain," said the woman beside Torv, quietly, "lost the valley."

Jonah went cold. "What valley?"

"Ember Valley," Maren said. "Where you're from. Where your shrine is. Where you've lived every day of your life since you were nineteen." She leaned forward, forearms on the table. "Dain served the same god you did, Iversen. Different region, same silence, same grafting. When she broke her oath to free herself of it, she woke up and knew she'd had a home once, and could not have told you its name or its shape or a single road out of it. It's coming back to her in pieces. A smell. A bend in a river. Four years, and maybe half of it."

Dain spoke without looking at him. "I keep a journal now. I wrote down everything before I did it. Every street. Every face. I read it every night like scripture." Her mouth twisted. "It doesn't help much. Reading about a place isn't remembering it."

Jonah looked at his hands. The silver tracery ran up past his wrists, past his elbows, and he had always assumed it faded somewhere under his sleeves. Now he wondered how far up it went. To the heart? The throat? Behind the eyes?

"Wren," he said, and stopped, because his throat had closed.

Wren's small hand found his under the table. She couldn't speak — had never spoken, not in any tongue the valley knew — but her grip said *I'm here* as clearly as words. She'd learned, these last weeks, all the ways of saying things without First-Tongue.

"If he loses the valley," Sefa said, "he loses the shrine. The route back. Possibly us, if we were only ever part of that country of his." She said it clinically, but Jonah heard the question underneath: *are we?*

"He won't lose people he made after the oath," Maren said. "The grafting is old. It holds the oldest ground. New growth tears free clean." She paused. "Usually."

"*Usually,*" Sefa repeated.

"Iversen asked me not to soften it."

* * *

They talked until the bells rang six. Maren laid it out the way she'd once laid out battle plans, terrain and cost and no illusions.

Breaking the oath required three things. A true speaking of the vow's reversal in First-Tongue — which meant Jonah had to learn enough of the language to mean it, not just recite it, because the tongue reshaped reality when spoken truly and a false heart produced nothing but a nosebleed. A witness bound by the same god, to see it done — Dain, as it happened, though she'd have to relearn her own broken fragments to serve. And a moment of the god's attention, however caged, however distant — which was why this had to happen in the capital, at the renewal rite, when the Hollow Chorus would pull its prisoner's power through the crown like water through a pipe.

"You'll feel it leave," Maren said. "Everything. All at once. Men have died of it — not the tearing, the *emptiness*. Twenty years of borrowed strength gone in a breath, and the body remembers what it actually is. You'll be fifty years old, Iversen, the morning after. Maybe older. Maybe you'll need help walking."

"And if the memories go," Jonah said, "they go for good?"

"No one's ever gotten them all back." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "But Dain's are returning. Slowly. Like a house being rebuilt from salvaged stone. If you write it down — everything, now, while it's still yours — then someday you might read your way home."

* * *

That night he climbed the bell tower stairs alone, to the little room the oath-breakers kept for guests, and sat at a desk with a candle and a blank book, and did not write anything for a long time.

Below him Wren slept in the next room. He could hear her breathing through the wall, that steady child's rhythm, and he found himself memorizing the sound of it, which frightened him more than anything Maren had said. You didn't memorize things you were sure of keeping.

He thought about the valley. Not the big things — the shrine, the ridge line, the forge road — but the small ones. The way the morning fog pooled in the low pasture so the sheep seemed to wade. Old Tam's habit of spitting before he said anything important. The exact weight of the shrine door, swollen with damp, needing a hip to open it. The smell of the ember-stone after rain, warm and mineral, like a banked fire that never quite died.

Twenty-six years of it. Since he was nineteen and kneeling at that shrine with blood on his hands from his first contract, swearing himself to a god whose name he'd barely learned. Twenty-six years of answered prayers and borrowed strength and, at the end, silence. Years of talking into a well with no echo.

And now they told him the well had never been empty. His god was down there, caged, wearing a king like a shackle, and the only way to reach it was to break the thing that connected them forever.

*To save it, destroy the bond. To free the prisoner, burn the rope.*

He picked up the pen.

*The fog pools in the low pasture first,* he wrote. *The sheep wade in it to their knees. Tam spits before anything important. The shrine door sticks; you open it with your hip, left side, always the left.*

It came easier after the first lines. He wrote until the candle burned down to a puddle, then lit another and wrote more. The roads out of the valley, all four. The names of the families who farmed the south terraces. What grief felt like at his father's funeral, the specific ache of it, the taste of the ale afterward. What the oath had felt like answering the first time — heat climbing his arm like sunlight through water.

Once, near dawn, he caught himself writing about Wren — the way she'd stood barefoot in his doorway, unafraid, rain streaming off her — and realized with a jolt that he didn't need to write that down. That wasn't grafted. That was new growth. That tore free clean.

He put down the pen and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and let himself grieve, quietly, in advance, for the valley he might already be losing. Then he blew out the candle, because grief was a luxury and there was work.

* * *

In the morning he found Sefa in the undercity's common room, surrounded by her papers, already teaching. She'd rigged a slate against the wall and covered it with First-Tongue characters, angular and strange, like lightning frozen mid-fall.

"Lesson one," she said, without looking up. "Sit."

He sat. Wren slid in beside him, wide awake, eyes already tracking the symbols with an ease that Sefa pretended not to notice and Jonah had stopped pretending not to see.

"We start with the word for *unbind*," Sefa said. "Not because it's first in any curriculum, but because you need to know what you're aiming at. Say it wrong, nothing happens. Say it right and mean it—" she tapped the slate, "—reality moves. So we drill it until your mouth knows it better than your fear does."

She pronounced it. The sound went through the room like a cold draft, and two oath-breakers playing cards at the next table flinched and swore.

"Now you."

Jonah said it. Wrong, the first dozen times. Wrong the first hundred. But somewhere around noon, saying it over and over until his jaw ached, he felt something shift — not in the word, in *him*. A loosening, deep down, like a root beginning to understand it might one day be pulled.

He kept drilling. And underneath the syllables, quiet and steady, he began building the list of things worth losing everything for: a mute child who'd walked out of a storm into his life. A king screaming inside his own crown. A god in a cage, silent for twenty years, still — somehow — listening.

By evening his voice was raw and the slate was full and Wren had fallen asleep against his shoulder, her hand curled around his calloused finger.

Sefa watched them both, then erased the slate and wrote one new character at the top.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we learn the word for *goodbye*."

Above them, far away, the bells of Vell rang out the hour — and beneath the ringing, so faint he might have imagined it, Jonah felt something answer. Not words. Not warmth. Just attention, brief and vast, like an eye opening at the bottom of a well.

His god, feeling him practice.

*Coming for you,* Jonah thought at it, and did not know if it was promise or farewell.
