## 14. Wren Is Taken

Wren knew the sound before she knew what it was.

It came through the floor of the safehouse in the undercity, a hum so low she felt it in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes, in the place behind her sternum where the Tower had put things she still didn't have names for. She was sitting on the windowsill counting raindrops on the glass — a habit from the nursery, where counting had been the only game allowed after lights-out — and when the hum started, every drop on the pane shivered at once.

She went very still.

In the Tower they had called it *tuning*. The tutors would strike a silver fork against the edge of her cot and watch how her body answered. *Good resonance today, vessel seven,* they'd say, writing numbers in their ledgers. Numbers that were about her but never for her. She had learned to lie with her body the way other children learned to lie with words: make your shoulders slack, make your breathing shallow, make yourself a dull bell that no one wants to ring.

The hum climbed. Somewhere below, iron was singing.

Jonah was downstairs with Sefa and the oath-breakers, arguing again about roads — the coast road, the mountain road, whether the capital's gates would already be watching for a big man with a scarred jaw and a child who didn't speak. Wren had stopped listening an hour ago. Adults always thought their arguments were walls. They were more like curtains; you could see through them if you stood at the right angle.

She slid off the sill without a sound. The Tower had taught her that too.

* * *

The safehouse had been a wine cellar once, back when this street had been respectable, and its stone throat ran down three levels to a cistern nobody used anymore. Wren crept along the upper gallery, barefoot on cold flagstones, and looked down through the railing into the lamplight where the grown-ups hunched over maps like priests over scripture.

Jonah stood with his back to her, arms crossed, and even from above she could read him the way she'd learned to read everything in the Tower — by shape and weight and the music underneath. He was afraid. He hid it well, folded it away under his shoulders the way he folded his sword under his cloak, but fear had a frequency too, and hers had been built to hear them all.

*—can't be coincidence,* one of the oath-breakers was saying. The woman with the burn-scarred cheek, Maren. *They swept the tanner's row at dawn. They're not searching Vell. They're narrowing.*

*Then we move tonight,* Jonah said. His voice did the thing it did when he'd decided something — flat, final, like a door closing. *Coast road. Sefa, can you walk it?*

*I can crawl it if I have to.* Sefa's voice came out cracked and small these days; she kept touching her throat as if checking that the words were still there. *But the rite is in nine days. The coast road is twelve.*

*Then we don't sleep.*

Wren's chest did the tight thing. Nine days. In nine days they would open the king up like a book and write the Chorus deeper into him, and they would use her mouth to do it — she'd heard the agents say it in the wagon, heard them call her *the new voice*, heard them discuss her lungs and her teeth and the pitch of her scream with the same bored precision the tutors had used. She had escaped that once. She had run barefoot through snow for eleven days because anything was better than being a mouth for a thing that ate gods.

And now the adults were going to argue about which road, and the road didn't matter, because the humming was getting louder.

She pressed her palms flat against the railing. Below her, the lamp flames had begun to lean — all of them, in different directions, toward the cellar door, like flowers finding a sun that wasn't there.

*Tuning,* said the memory, in a tutor's dry voice. *Vessel seven, please hold still.*

She held still. It was the only prayer she knew.

* * *

They came at the change of the watch, when even careful men are slowest.

Wren heard the door first — not forced, not broken, just *opened*, which was worse, which meant someone had bought a key or a man or both. Then boots, many boots, moving with the particular patience of people who already know how the night will go. And under it all, the hum, rising now into something almost sweet, almost like singing, and she recognized it with a lurch of sickness:

It was her own voice. Recorded, distilled, wound into brass and wire. A resonance trap tuned to vessel seven. They had made a lure out of her, and her own bones were answering it, pulling her toward the stairs whether she wanted to go or not.

She bit her hand. Hard. The pain cut through the pull enough for her to think.

*Jonah.* She couldn't say it — hadn't spoken since the valley, since the night her voice had first carried his god's power and scared them both — but she could throw things. She grabbed the clay cup off the sill and hurled it down into the lamplight.

It shattered at Jonah's feet. He looked up fast — he always looked up fast, like a man who'd spent years expecting knives from ceilings — and their eyes met across the gallery, and she pointed at the door.

He understood in half a heartbeat. That was the thing about Jonah that still frightened her, that she was slowly learning might be the best kind of frightening: he moved like the world owed him nothing and he intended to collect anyway.

*Upstairs,* he roared, and the cellar exploded into motion.

* * *

What happened next, Wren saw mostly in pieces, the way the Tower had taught her to see everything — fragments, angles, useful details — because whole pictures were a luxury for children who weren't vessels.

Maren going out the back with two of her oath-breakers, blades already drawn. Sefa pressing a scrap of paper into Wren's hand — a word written in First-Tongue, *hide-behind*, the binding for concealment — and mouthing *when you need it, only when you need it, it costs*. Jonah putting himself between the stairwell and her, which was ridiculous, he was enormous, he was a wall with a beard, and walls do not understand that some songs go through stone.

The front door came apart.

Not broken — *unmade*, hinges sighing into dust, and through it stepped four figures in plain gray, faces wrapped, and behind them a fifth figure carrying a frame of brass and wire that sang with Wren's stolen voice. The trap-bearer raised it like a lantern, and the song swelled, and Wren's knees buckled. Her body wanted to walk to it. Her blood wanted to walk to it. Some part of her that the Tower had built before she was old enough to have opinions wanted to walk to it more than it wanted anything else in the world.

*No,* she thought, in First-Tongue, because thinking in that language made thoughts heavier, harder to move. *I am not a bell. I am not a bell. I am—*

A name. She had a name now. Not vessel seven. Wren. Jonah had given it to her in the valley, testing it aloud like a man testing ice, and Sefa had written it in the dirt, and it was hers, it was the one thing the Tower had never had, and she dug her nails into it like a handhold on a cliff.

Jonah hit the first agent like a landslide.

She had seen him fight before — the ridge, the valley, quick brutal work — but never like this, never with the oath awake in him, heat rolling off his shoulders in waves she could feel from ten feet away. The gray men were good; it didn't matter. He went through the first two the way weather goes through a field. For one bright moment Wren actually believed.

Then the trap-bearer turned the frame toward Jonah and spoke a single word into it.

Her word. Her voice, pulled out of the Tower's ledgers, pitched to the exact frequency of the oath's channel — the channel that ran through *her*, that had been built into her, that connected her to the sleeping god the way a wire connects a lamp. The word struck Jonah's borrowed power and rang it like a cracked bell.

He staggered. Just a half-step. Just enough.

The third agent drove a hooked blade through the gap of his guard, into his side, and twisted.

Wren screamed. It tore out of her before she could stop it — her first true voice in weeks, raw and wordless — and the sound hit the room like a thrown torch. Every lamp flared blue. The trap-frame shrieked and split along its seams. One of the gray men clutched his ears and dropped.

For a moment, everyone froze, even Jonah, even bleeding, staring at her.

*There she is,* said the trap-bearer, muffled and satisfied, and Wren understood with terrible clarity that she had just done exactly what they wanted. Not the frame. *Her.* The frame had been a tuning fork. She was the instrument.

Hands closed on her arms — gloved, gentle, horribly practiced, the hands of people who had handled frightened children a thousand times. She fought. She was proud later, telling herself the story in the dark, that she fought: teeth, heels, the binding-word Sefa had given her gasped into the air to make herself smoke and slip. But the trap-bearer simply spoke her own recorded voice back at her — *calm, vessel seven, calm* — in the soft nursery cadence the tutors had used, and her body betrayed her, muscles unlocking, breath slowing, eight years old again in a cot with silver rails.

The last thing she saw clearly was Jonah.

He was on his knees, one hand pressed to his flank, blood between his fingers, and he was *crawling* toward her, dragging himself through the wreckage of the doorframe with his jaw set in a way she'd come to know meant *I have decided*, and his eyes found hers over the shoulders of the gray men, and he mouthed something. No sound. Just the shape of it.

*Alive. Stay alive.*

Then the hood came down, and the world became wool and the sweet awful song of her own voice, and the procession carried her away.

* * *

The wagon smelled of straw and old incense, and they chained her gently, which was somehow the cruelest part. Gentle chains. Gentle hands. A cup of water offered to her lips as if she were a guest.

Through a slit in the canvas she watched Vell fall away behind them, brass bells shrinking to toys, then to glitter, then to nothing. Ahead, on the far horizon, the capital sat under its permanent haze of woodsmoke and ceremony, and somewhere inside it the Tower of Tongues waited with her old cot and her old number and her old name.

The trap-bearer sat across from her, frame stowed, face unwrapped now — a tired woman with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who graded papers. A tutor. Of course.

*You spoke,* the woman said. Not unkindly. *At the house. You haven't spoken in weeks, according to the file.* She tapped a ledger on her knee — Wren's ledger, she realized, her whole life in columns. *That's significant. The Chorus will want to know why. What changed?*

Wren looked at her and said nothing. It was easier now. Silence had been her first language; the Tower had merely interrupted it.

*The big man,* the tutor tried. *The mercenary. He'll be dead or he won't. Either way he's finished. You should begin preparing yourself for the rite. It's better when the vessel doesn't resist — less damage to the instrument.*

Wren turned her face to the slit of sky and let the hood shadow her expression, and inside the hood, inside the silence, she did the thing they had never managed to train out of her, the thing that had made the senior tutors uneasy, the thing that had gotten her marked *deviant, observe closely* in her own ledger:

She counted.

Raindrops on glass. Steps between cells. Days until the rite. Nine days, minus one for the road. Eight.

And while she counted, she planned, because despair was a luxury and guilt was a weight she'd carry later — *he bled because of me, he crawled because of me* — and neither of them could ride in the wagon with her. Only this could: the memory of a word Sefa had pressed into her palm, *hide-behind*, and the fact that she had not used it. She had saved it. She had *saved* it, the way you save the last of the bread.

Eight days. A word in her pocket. A name in her chest that no ledger owned.

And somewhere behind her, bleeding in a ruined doorway in the undercity of Vell, a wall of a man who had decided — and Wren had learned enough about decided men to know that distance was not the same as done.

The wheels turned north. Wren counted the turns of them, one by one, all the way to the capital.
