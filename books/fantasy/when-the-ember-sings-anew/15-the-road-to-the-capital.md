## 15. The Road to the Capital

They left Vell before the bells finished ringing for dawn.

Jonah had argued for an hour of preparation, a night of sleep, anything. Kesrin had looked at him the way you look at a man who hasn't understood the shape of the knife yet. "The rite is in nine days," she'd said, tightening the strap on her pack with one hand and her sword belt with the other. "You want to spend one of them sleeping?"

So they rode out through the Ash Gate while the city was still gray and half-asleep, four horses under them and nothing behind them but debt. Sefa rode badly, hunched over her mare's neck like a woman reading a book only she could see, and every few miles she made Jonah stop so she could scratch notes into a wax tablet balanced on her knee.

"You'll wear the horse down," he told her.

"I'll wear *myself* down if I don't write this." She held up the tablet. The symbols meant nothing to him — angular, deliberate, like a fence built to keep something in. "The Chorus doesn't just speak First-Tongue. It *composes*. Every rite it performs adds a verse to itself. If we know what verses Aldric's coronation renewal will use, we know what it will be able to do by the time we reach the capital."

"And do you know what verses those are?"

"No." She said it flatly, without apology. "But Wren does."

The name landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall and never heard it hit bottom.

Kesrin's second oath-breaker was a young man called Doss, quiet as packed snow, who'd taken the rear guard without being asked and hadn't spoken since except to count bridges. Jonah didn't know his story yet. He suspected he would, eventually, the way you learn the stories of people you bleed beside. For now it was enough that Doss watched the road behind them like a man who expected the crown to come collect its property, because it had, once, and Doss had been the property.

Nine days. The capital at Corvane sat eleven days' hard ride north, through hill country that belonged to no crown and every bandit. They would arrive two days late unless they killed horses, and if they killed horses they'd arrive on foot, which was worse than late.

Jonah did the arithmetic anyway, the way he did everything: plainly, and without hope of liking the answer.

* * *

The first night they made camp in the ruin of a wayshrine — not one of Jonah's god's; those were all in the valley, all tended, all silent. This one belonged to some older devotion, its statue worn faceless by weather or by hands. Sefa examined the plinth and pronounced it pre-Imperial, then wrote three more lines on her tablet and refused to explain them.

Wren should have been there. That was the thought that kept finding him, in the gaps between tasks. When he gathered firewood, when he checked the horses' hooves, when he sat closest to the flame because the cold came off the hills like water. There should have been a small shape beside him, barefoot even now because shoes were a war he'd lost twice, humming sounds that weren't quite words, watching him with those dark eyes that saw too much and said nothing.

Instead there was a space, and the space had a shape, and the shape was small.

"She ate the last of the dried plums," Kesrin said, settling across the fire from him. "Before they took her. I remember thinking, good, someone in this company has sense."

Jonah said nothing.

"You're doing the thing," Kesrin went on. "Where you carry the guilt around like a sack of wet sand and won't put it down long enough to be useful."

"The trap used her own records." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Built from what the Tower knew about her. Her resonance. Her — her *pattern*. I stood ten feet away and I couldn't—"

"Couldn't what? Outshout a machine built by a hundred years of linguists to do exactly that?" Kesrin poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "I've broken oaths, Iversen. I know what it costs to fail. It costs more if you make it your whole weight. Put the sand down. Pick up a sword."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Is that how you got over yours?"

"I didn't get over mine. I got *around* it. Different direction entirely." She glanced toward Sefa, who had fallen asleep sitting up, tablet still in her lap, and lowered her voice. "Your linguist is going to burn herself out before Corvane. And you're going to let her, because you think grief is a plan."

"It's not grief."

"No. It's worse. It's hope wearing grief's coat so you won't notice it." Kesrin lay back on her bedroll, done with him. "Sleep, mercenary. Tomorrow we ride like the dead are chasing us. Because they are."

* * *

On the third day they cut through the Karst Hills, where the road narrowed to a shelf above a river gorge, and Doss finally spoke.

"Bridge ahead is watched," he said. It was the longest sentence Jonah had heard from him. "Two men. Crown colors under cloaks. They've been there since yesterday — I circled back at dusk."

Sefa looked up from her tablet. "Can we go around?"

"Not with the gorge. Not with your riding." Doss said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "We go through, or we go slow."

"Then we go through," Jonah said, and started planning it the way he planned everything: exits first, then weapons, then lies. Lies were best. Lies kept blood off the road, and blood drew more riders.

The lie was simple. Jonah rode ahead alone, hands visible, playing the hired escort reporting to his employers that the road was clear. The two soldiers bought it right up until Sefa trotted past behind him and one of them straightened in his saddle with the particular alertness of a man who has seen a wanted notice recently.

"That's her. The Tower woman. And that's—" The second soldier's eyes found Jonah's face, and whatever he saw there made him reach for his whistle instead of his sword.

Jonah reached faster.

He didn't kill them. He was past killing men who were only doing their jobs — or he told himself he was, and the distinction mattered to him, so he kept it. He broke the whistle-arm at the elbow and put the other man's face into his saddlebow hard enough to rearrange his week, then took their horses' lead ropes and dragged the animals off the shelf road, leaving the soldiers groaning in the dust with a long walk to anywhere.

"They'll report us," Sefa said, white-knuckled on her reins.

"Not soon. Not clearly." Jonah swung back into his saddle. His oath stirred faintly under his skin, that borrowed heat he'd felt once in the valley, warm and wrong, like a fever offering to help. He pushed it down. Every use of it now was a withdrawal from an account he'd sworn to empty. "Ride."

They rode.

* * *

It was that evening, making cold camp in a hollow because fires drew eyes, that Sefa told him about the mouth.

"The renewal rite isn't just maintenance," she said. She'd stopped writing; her tablet lay forgotten in the grass. "I've been working from Tower liturgies I memorized before they stripped my credentials. The Chorus consumed six gods before Aldric. Each consumption needed a vessel — a throat. A mouth. Someone bred to carry the god's last words while the Chorus digested them."

"Wren."

"Wren." Sefa's voice was careful, the way you're careful walking on ice over deep water. "She wasn't stolen from the Tower, Jonah. She was *harvested*. Raised for exactly this. The resonance trap, the timing, the rite — none of it is improvised. This has been the plan since before she could walk."

The hollow feeling in Jonah's chest expanded until he thought it might crack his ribs. "And the renewal? What happens to Aldric?"

"Aldric is nearly gone. What's left of him has been fighting for years — you saw him, you said, you saw the flicker. The renewal rite finishes it. The Chorus stops wearing him and starts *being* him, and Wren becomes its voice in the world. Every binding-word spoken through her mouth will be true forever, because she was made true." Sefa looked at the stars as if they owed her money. "There's a reason the final unbinding needs a willing speaker. The Chorus has spent a century making sure no one willing gets close enough."

Jonah sat with that. In the dark, Doss was cleaning his blade with the slow patience of a man who trusted the motion more than the outcome. Kesrin slept the way soldiers sleep, instantly and armed.

"We get there early," Jonah said finally. "Or we get there exactly on time. Either way, we're inside that hall before the rite completes."

"With four swords and a mute child's worth of knowledge?" Sefa shook her head. "You need more than momentum, mercenary. You need a door."

"Then tomorrow we find a door."

"There is one." She hesitated, and he realized she'd been carrying this since Vell, weighing whether to spend it. "The crypts beneath the capital. Under the old foundation stones. My mentor used to say the Tower was built on top of something it was afraid of. If any part of your god still exists — caged, buried, whatever the Chorus did with it — that's where."

Jonah stared into the dark where the fire wasn't. Twenty years of prayers thrown at silence. Twenty years of tending a shrine to something that never answered, and keeping faith anyway, because the faith was the only shape his life had.

If the god was down there, caged — then the silence had never been abandonment. It had been a scream with nowhere to echo.

"Then that's where we're going," he said.

* * *

They rode the next five days like men being paid by the mile. Through the hill country, past burned-out farmsteads that might have been tax collectors' work or bandits' or the crown's quiet pruning of places that asked questions. Sefa taught as they rode, shouting First-Tongue syllables against the wind, making Jonah repeat them until his throat was raw.

"Why me?" he shouted once. "Wren's the natural. You're the scholar."

"Because Wren isn't here, and I may lose my voice before this ends — the unbinding takes it, the old texts are clear about that. Which leaves you." She leaned close over the gap between their horses. "A binding-word spoken truly reshapes what it touches. But a word spoken by a man breaking his own oath — that's a force the Chorus has no category for. It's never met someone who *chose* to be powerless."

Jonah turned that over for a mile or so. "You're saying my weakness is a weapon."

"I'm saying the Chorus thinks in ledgers. Assets, debts, bindings. It cannot price a man giving away his strength for free." She almost smiled, and it was the first time he'd seen her do it. "That's our door."

* * *

On the eighth day, cresting the last ridge as the sun went down, they saw the capital.

Corvane sat in its valley like a stone in a fist — walls first, then the sprawl, then the Tower of Tongues rising pale and needle-thin at the city's heart, and beyond it the palace, crowned in scaffolding for the rite. Banners hung from every tower, gold on black. Even at this distance Jonah could hear the city: a low continuous hum, thousands of voices, and underneath it something else, something that made his teeth ache and the oath under his skin turn over in its sleep.

Doss reined in beside him. "Bells," he said quietly.

"What about them?"

"They're ringing the hour. Listen." He held up a hand. Far off, faint, the sound rolled across the hills — and beneath each bell-strike, Jonah heard it now, a second tone. A harmony that no bell had been cast to make. Like a choir practicing under the floorboards of the world.

Sefa's face had gone the color of ash. "It's already singing," she whispered. "The Chorus. It's rehearsing."

Kesrin drew her sword an inch and slid it back, a habit, a promise. "Two days late," she said. "So we don't sleep when we get there. We go straight down."

"Down?" Jonah asked.

"To the crypts. To your god." She kicked her horse forward. "And then up, through whatever floor we come out under, and we end this."

They descended toward the lights of Corvane, four riders and a silence between them that was fuller than any prayer Jonah had ever spoken. Behind them the road stretched back south, all the way to a valley where magic couldn't follow and a shrine stood untended and a garden waited to be planted.

Ahead, the bells rang, and something beneath them sang along.

Jonah rode toward it, and for the first time in twenty years, he was not alone.
