## 16. The Tower of Tongues

Sefa had spent eleven years telling herself the Tower was smaller than she remembered.

It wasn't. It rose out of the capital's oldest quarter like a needle pulled from a wound, white stone gone gray with incense smoke, its windows lit at every hour because the work of language never slept. She had been nine years old when she first climbed those steps, a debt-child from the river districts with a quick ear and no family worth naming. Master Corvane had put his hand on her head and said, *You will learn to speak what the world is made of.*

She had believed him. That was the worst of it. Not what she'd done afterward — what she'd done was terrible, but terrible things are easy to survive once you stop pretending they were accidents. What never stopped hurting was how completely she had believed.

"You're staring," Jonah said quietly beside her.

"I'm inventorying." She kept her eyes on the tower. "Servants' door on the east face, warded — I know the ward, I helped write it. Laundry goes in through the undercroft before dawn. The archive is on the sixth floor. Corvane's study is the seventh. And somewhere below both is the resonance vault, where they keep the records that let them build a trap for an eight-year-old girl."

Jonah shifted his weight. In the shadow of a dyer's awning he looked like exactly what he was: a large man trying not to be noticed by a building. "And we're going in through?"

"The laundry."

He almost smiled. "Of course we are."

Behind them, in the safehouse beneath the bell-tower, Wren was asleep with her back against Ashka's shoulder, and Sefa had spent an hour after midnight teaching her hand-signs for *run*, *hide*, *don't trust anyone in white*. Wren had learned them in minutes, which told Sefa everything about how many times the child had needed such words before.

"She'll be safer if we get the word tonight," Jonah said.

"Everyone's always safer if we do the brave thing quickly," Sefa said. "That's how they recruit you. Speed feels like mercy."

She pushed off the wall and started walking.

* * *

The laundry door opened to her old passphrase without hesitation, and that felt like an insult. Eleven years disgraced, expelled, name struck from the rolls, and the Tower still remembered her hands. The undercroft smelled of lye and hot copper, the same as it always had, and the same half-asleep bond-servants sorted linens by lamplight, children mostly, collared in soft gray wool so their necks wouldn't chafe. Sefa had worn one of those collars until she was fourteen and Corvane had cut it off himself, saying, *You've earned words instead.* She had thought it was kindness.

Two of the laundry children looked up as she passed. One of them had Wren's exact shade of brown eyes, and Sefa had to keep walking, because if she stopped she would start tearing collars off throats, and Jonah would have to fight his way out alone, and the word would stay buried.

The service stair was unguarded. Of course it was. The Tower had never feared its own corridors; why would it? Everything dangerous inside it was either bound or employed.

Sixth floor. The archive doors stood open, because at this hour the night-lexicographers were deep in transcription, bent over desks like monks, and none of them looked up. Sefa moved along the stacks she knew by heartbeat — grammar of the river tongues, third shelf; binding-forms, restricted case, iron hasp — and found what she'd come down a floor to find: the index of the resonance vault.

She ran her finger down the entries. *Vessel candidates, cohort nine. Vessel candidates, cohort ten.* And there, in Corvane's own cramped hand, a marginal note beside a name that had been scraped nearly to nothing:

*Subject retains full First-Tongue intuition despite suppression regimen. Do not attempt extraction by force. She must speak willingly or the bindings will invert.*

There it was again. *Willingly.* The same word, over and over, across three different documents, like a stone rolled into a shoe.

"Sefa." Jonah's voice was barely breath. He was looking up the stairwell, toward the seventh floor, where a single lamp burned behind frosted glass. "Someone's awake up there."

"Yes," she said. "I know whose lamp that is."

* * *

Master Corvane had not changed. That was the second insult of the night. His hair had gone from iron to ash and his hands had the tremor of age now, but he sat at his desk the way he always had, spine straight, lamp trimmed low, a pen moving across vellum with the patience of a man who knew the world obeyed grammar.

He looked up before she knocked. Of course he did. He'd taught her that trick too.

"You walk like your mother," he said. "She used to come up my stairs the same way. Angry and quiet about it." He set down the pen. "Eleven years, Sefa. You could have written. A letter. Anything."

"A letter to you?" She stepped into the lamplight and watched him see her — the scar at her throat where they'd taken her voice's register down a key during her discipline year, the debt-brand on her wrist, the eyes he'd once called *the finest instrument I ever trained*. "What would I have said? *Dear Master, today I taught a stolen child to swear in a dead god's language, and I enjoyed it.*"

Something moved behind his face. Not guilt. Recognition, maybe. Grief's poorer cousin.

"So it's true," he said. "They told me you were dead. Then they told me you were in Vell. Then last week they told me you were coming." He folded his hands. "I assumed all three were true at once. With you, they usually were."

"The crown knows we're here."

"The crown knows everything its records remember. Which brings us to the interesting question." He rose, slowly, joints crackling like green wood, and poured two cups of wine from the decanter he kept for donors and liars. He offered her one. She didn't take it. He drank his own anyway. "Why does a woman who hates this place break into it alone, at the hour when only fools and archivists are awake? What are you looking for, Sefa?"

"The final binding-word."

The silence lasted long enough for the lamp to gutter.

"I know it exists," she went on, before he could find his footing. "I've reconstructed the grammar around it. The Chorus wears Aldric through a lattice of spoken constraints — I have most of them, Sefa's notes, Wren's memory, the oath-breakers' fragments. But every lattice has a keystone, and yours has one too, because I found the shape of the hole in the resonance records. There's a word missing. One word. And I want to know why your own documents won't say it aloud."

Corvane set down his cup with exaggerated care, the way men do when their hands want to shake and they refuse to permit it.

"And what makes you think I'll tell you?"

"Because you left the archive open," she said. "Because your lamp is lit at the hour when only fools and archivists are awake. Because eleven years ago you argued against the vessel program in front of the Conclave and lost, and I read the minutes, Master, I read them years later in a banned stack, and you said — your exact words — *a binding that requires an unwilling mouth is not a binding, it is a bruise.*" Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "So either you lost that argument and surrendered, or you lost it and stayed to sabotage from inside. I've come to find out which. Because I can't afford to guess wrong."

For a moment the old man simply breathed. Then he laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering oil.

"You always did read past the margin." He turned to the shelf behind his desk, the one holding forty years of grammars in leather gone soft as skin, and pressed a stud hidden in the spine of a dictionary of extinct trade-languages. The shelf swung inward on a whisper of counterweights. "Come in, then. And bring your mercenary. If he's going to stand in my study radiating menace, he may as well do it where the wine is better."

* * *

The room beyond the shelf was small, windowless, and crammed with the opposite of treasure: loose pages, failed drafts, a decade of experiments that had not worked, arranged with the loving chaos of a mind that trusted itself to find anything. Jonah ducked under the lintel and stood by the door, arms crossed, taking up half the room the way large men do.

Corvane lit a second lamp. On the central table lay a single sheet of vellum under glass, and on it, in ink that shimmered faintly the way First-Tongue always shimmered when written truly, was a word.

Sefa leaned over it and felt the floor tilt.

Not because the word was strange. Because she recognized it. She had spoken it a thousand times in ordinary life, in marketplaces and kitchens and arguments — a common word, warm and small, a word people gave each other freely every day. Written here in true form, it glowed like a coal.

"That's—" she began.

"Yes," Corvane said. "Say it in the common tongue and it means what it has always meant. Speak it *willingly*, in true form, to the crowned one, and it is the keystone that holds every other constraint in place. The Hollow Chorus built its whole cage around a word that cannot be forced." He smiled without any joy at all. "Do you understand the elegance of it? They wanted a lock no torturer could open and no traitor could pick. So they made the key a gift. The word only binds if the speaker means it. Wren must say it herself. Willingly. Believing it, even for a moment."

"What is the word?" Jonah asked from the doorway. Flat. Dangerous.

"It doesn't matter what it *means*," Sefa said slowly, working it through, cold spreading through her chest. "It matters what it costs. If she says it believing it, the lattice locks forever. The Chorus finishes consuming Aldric. She becomes its mouth permanently." She looked up at Corvane. "That's the trap they built for her. Not chains. A sentence she'd have to *mean*."

"They've been raising her toward it since birth," Corvane said. "Every kindness in that tower was engineered. Every warmth she remembers was administered. Cohort ten was designed to produce a child who would say the word gladly." He rubbed his tremor out of his hand. "And then she escaped, and everything became urgent."

"There's a counter," Sefa said. "There has to be. A keystone can be replaced."

"A keystone can be *unseated*," Corvane corrected. "Which is why the word is also the answer — but inverted. Spoken unwillingly, falsely, in defiance of its own meaning, at the moment of crowning, it cracks the lattice instead of sealing it. But—" He held up a finger. "Here is the cruelty your reconstruction will already have guessed. A false speaking of a true word, in that chamber, in front of the Chorus, while it listens with the king's ears... the speaker pays. Language reshapes reality when spoken truly. Untrue speech spoken *into* truth tears the tongue that speaks it. Your voice, Sefa. Perhaps more than your voice. The Conclave's own trials documented it. Speakers who tried it came away mute. Some came away worse."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, a bell marked the hour, muffled by stone.

"So that's the choice," Sefa said. Her voice sounded very calm to her, the way water looks calm over a drop. "Wren says it truly and is destroyed. Or someone says it falsely and is destroyed in her place. Those are the options your brilliant colleagues wrote into the world."

"Those are the options the Chorus wrote into the world," Corvane said sharply. "Do not confuse the authors with the scribes. I have spent thirty years transcribing a monster's grammar, girl, because someone had to know how it worked before anyone could break it. Yes. I trained you. Yes, I taught in this tower while cohorts were bred downstairs like livestock, and I told myself knowledge was neutral, and I was wrong, and the minutes of the Conclave record me losing that argument and staying anyway." He met her eyes, and for the first time in her life she saw him entirely without armor, and he was smaller than she'd always believed. "You asked which I am. I am the man who stayed to sabotage and accomplished almost nothing for thirty years, and now an eight-year-old is going to pay for my cowardice unless you finish what I couldn't."

Sefa looked at the word under glass. Warm. Small. A word she had said to students, to friends, once to a lover on a bridge in Vell, meaning it every time.

"How does Wren avoid saying it?" she asked. "At the rite. They'll force the liturgy through her. She'll be surrounded."

"She slipped bindings into the procession liturgy in Vell," Jonah said from the door. "Ashka told us. She bought seconds. She's fast."

"She's eight," Corvane said. "Fast is not enough. Unless—" He paused, and Sefa watched the thought arrive on his face like weather. "Unless she doesn't go to the rite at all. Unless the crown takes her somewhere else first. Somewhere the Chorus keeps what it consumes."

Sefa's stomach dropped. "The crypts."

"The crypts," Corvane agreed. "Under the coronation hall. Where the god your mercenary swore to is caged in stone. The renewal rite requires proximity to the prisoner — that's not ritual theater, it's mechanics. The Chorus draws strength across the binding. Which means on coronation morning, the child, the king, the Chorus, and the god will all be within forty feet of one another, and every ward in that hall will be tuned outward, toward the crowd, expecting assassins with knives rather than linguists with words." He pulled the glass off the vellum, lifted the page, and held it out to her.

She didn't take it. "Why give it to me? You could sell me to the crown right now and buy your pension twice over."

"Because I argued in the Conclave that a binding requiring an unwilling mouth is a bruise," Corvane said, "and I have been waiting eleven years for someone to come up my stairs angry and quiet about it. Take the word, Sefa. Learn it. Teach the child to recognize it so they can't spring it on her blind. And then decide who says it wrongly." His hand trembled, and the page trembled, glowing like something alive. "Decide faster than I did."

She took it.

The moment her fingers closed on the vellum, every lamp in the study — both lamps, the corridor lamps, the lamps six floors down in the archive — flicked once, hard, like a caught breath. Jonah straightened off the door. Corvane's head snapped upward.

"No," the old man whispered. "No, they shouldn't be able to—"

Down in the bones of the Tower, something began to hum. Sefa felt it in her teeth, in the scar at her throat, in the debt-brand on her wrist: a rising tone, patient and enormous, the sound of a very large attention turning over in its sleep and beginning, with terrible slowness, to listen.

It had heard the word. Even unwritten, even unspoken — it had felt the shape of it pass between their hands.

"The resonance vault," Sefa breathed. "It's waking up. It knows its keystone just left the building."

Jonah was already moving, hauling the shelf shut behind them, voice low and certain. "Then we run now, and we pray the girl in the bell-tower learns the word before coronation morning — because something tells me the crown just decided to collect her early."
