## 18. Coronation of Ash

They dressed her in white.

Wren had learned, in the Tower of Tongues, that white was the color they used for things they meant to use up. Chalk. Candles. Paper. Vessels. The robe they put on her now was heavy with embroidery, silver thread spelling words she could read in three languages and two that had no name outside the Tower's deepest rooms, and every one of them meant the same thing: *this body belongs to the speaking.*

The dressing women did not look at her face. They had been instructed not to, she supposed. In the Tower, the attendants who worked with the vessels never looked at their faces either. It was easier to prepare a thing if you did not have to admit it had eyes.

Her wrists ached where the resonance cuffs sat. They had made them from her own records — she had felt it the moment they closed around her, the cold hum of her own voice folded back against her skin, a net woven from every word she'd ever been made to say. She could not speak a true word while they held her. She had tested it once, in the wagon, a single syllable of First-Tongue pressed small behind her teeth, and the cuffs had burned her like a stove-top and something in the dark of the wagon had *laughed*.

So she had stopped testing, and started counting.

Counting was a Tower skill too, but it was hers now. They had given it to her for their purposes — cadence, rhythm, the mathematics of liturgy — and she had kept it for her own. She counted the turns of the corridor as they walked her up through the palace's bones. She counted the guards. She counted the bells of the coronation hall, nine great bells of green bronze, each one tuned, she knew, to a frequency that made the air easier for the Chorus to wear.

And she counted seconds, because Jonah had told her once, on the road, in his slow careful way: *If it goes wrong, don't be brave. Be slow. Every second you steal is a second I can use.*

He had come for her at the crossroads inn and failed. She had seen him go down — the big man folding like a felled tree, blood on the cobbles, Sefa screaming his name — and she had let them take her anyway, had not screamed, had not fought, because the trap they'd built was tuned to her fear and the more afraid she was the tighter it closed. She had learned that in the wagon, curled around her burned wrists. *Be slow. Be quiet. Be a vessel until you are not.*

That had been eleven days ago. She had counted every one.

* * *

The hall smelled of hot metal and lilies.

Wren stood at the foot of the dais on a disc of pale stone that had not been there a week ago — she could see the fresh chisel-marks at its edge — and the whole court of Aldric's kingdom was arranged around her in tiers, lords and ladies and soldiers in dress uniform, and not one of them was looking at the throne.

They were looking at the king, the way you look at a fire. You cannot help it. You are ashamed and you cannot stop.

King Aldric sat beneath the nine bells in his coronation robes, and he was dying in slow motion, and everyone in the hall had agreed, in the wordless way of crowds, to pretend otherwise. The crown sat low on his brow, and where its metal touched his skin the flesh had gone smooth and gray as riverstone, and thin black lines ran from the crown's points down his temples like the roots of a tree that had decided, after long consideration, to grow *inward*. His eyes were open. That was the worst part. The eyes were open and they tracked, sometimes, and once — only once, when the herald read the first line of the rite — they had moved to Wren's face and something behind them had been *screaming*.

She knew that scream. She had made it herself, in the Tower, in the years before she learned that screaming changed nothing and silence changed everything.

The Chorus stood up inside the king's body and adjusted its robes, and the herald announced the renewal rite, and nine hundred people bowed to a thing wearing their king like a glove, and Wren counted the seconds until it would be her turn to speak.

* * *

They had taught her the opening words in the wagon. Of course they had. That was the whole purpose of her — the whole purpose of the Tower, the whole purpose of the breeding program whose records she had read by candlelight in Sefa's rented room while Jonah slept sitting up against the door. A vessel. A mouth. The Chorus was old and its own voice was a ruin, worn out by centuries of speaking through throats it had emptied, and a renewal rite required a *clean* voice, a voice that had never been spent, and there were perhaps three such voices in the world and Wren was the one they had caught.

The opening words were in First-Tongue. They had made her recite them forty times a day, and every recitation had been logged, and the cuffs drank each one down so that when the moment came they could force her mouth through the shapes if her will failed.

But here was the thing the Tower had never understood about her, the thing she had hidden the way you hide a coin in your cheek: she did not recite. She had never recited. Even at six years old, even in the white rooms with the resonance forks, when they made her speak, she *listened* first. First-Tongue was not a language you said. It was a language you *agreed* with, and every word carried its opposite coiled inside it like a seed, and a true speaker knew how to plant the seed without waking the fruit.

Sefa had taught her the word for it. *Vel-ketha.* The binding-under. The word hidden in the word.

They think I am a mouth, Wren thought, standing on the pale stone with the whole court watching and the Chorus's attention settling on her like a hand on the back of her neck. They have never once wondered what a mouth might swallow.

* * *

The rite began.

The herald's voice rang out in the court-tongue, and beneath it, so low that only Wren and the thing in the king could hear it, the Chorus began to speak the true words, the old ones, feeding them to her through the cuffs in a cold current against her skin. *Say. Say. Say.* It did not threaten. It did not need to. The cuffs would make her say them whether she willed it or not, and the Chorus knew it, and there was a terrible patience in the way it fed her the first line, like a nurse holding a spoon to a child's mouth.

Wren opened her mouth.

The first word of the renewal rite was *Asheneth* — *I open*. It was the door-word. Every binding in First-Tongue was a door, and this one opened the king's last defenses the way a key opens a lock, and once it was said, the rest would follow like water finding a hole, and the Chorus would finish what it had started nineteen years ago when it had first set itself on a young king's brow and begun to drink.

Wren said: *Asheneth.*

And under it, in the place where the seed sleeps, she said: *Asheneth-vel.*

*I open — and am not opened.*

The cuffs flared. The Chorus felt it — she felt it feel it, a cold shock through the resonance net, a moment of confusion, because the word had been spoken *correctly*, the shape of her mouth had been perfect, the court had heard the opening of the rite — and yet the door it had opened was not the door it expected. She had opened *herself*, yes, but she had opened herself *inward*, had made of her own voice a room with no exit, and the first thread of the Chorus's power that came pouring through her mouth found no path to the king. It pooled in her. It sat in her chest like swallowed ice.

*What are you,* said the Chorus, not in words, in pressure.

*A vessel,* Wren thought at it, sweetly. *You made me.*

The second line came. She spoke it true, and bound it under. The third. The fourth. Each word of the rite went through her mouth like a coin through a thief's fingers — the court saw gold, the Chorus felt gold pass, but nothing reached the treasury. She was stealing seconds now, whole fistfuls of them, and the liturgy that should have taken four minutes was stretching, stretching, because the Chorus kept *pausing*, kept *listening* to its own words come back wrong, and it could not stop the rite without admitting before nine hundred witnesses that its vessel was flawed, and pride, she thought, even the pride of a god-eater, is a kind of chain.

Sweat ran down her back inside the white robe. The ice in her chest was building. Every bound word she swallowed stayed swallowed — she was a cup filling with a god's overflow, and the cuffs burned her wrists in steady lines of pain, and somewhere past the fifth line her nose began to bleed, a thin warm thread over her lip, and the court murmured, and the Chorus *pressed*.

The sixth line of the rite was the keystone. *Veth-asheneth-kora.* *I open, and what I open I hold.* When she spoke it, the binding-under would fail — she knew this, had known it since the third line, because the keystone was built to lock the opening *to the speaker's will*, and her will was the one thing the cuffs would not carry. When she spoke the sixth line, the ice in her chest would pour into the king, and the Chorus would finish its feast, and everything she had stolen would have bought nothing but time.

She counted the time she had. Forty seconds, maybe fifty, before they reached the keystone.

*Jonah,* she thought, not a prayer, because she did not pray, she had been made in a white room and prayers were not installed in her. *Jonah, you said every second. You said.*

The hall's great doors were forty paces behind her. She could not turn her head. The rite forbade it, and the rite was the only law in the room the Chorus still honored.

She spoke the fifth line and bound it under, and blood dripped from her chin onto the white robe, a small red flower opening on all that silver thread, and in the tiers above her someone — a woman, a lord's wife, someone human — made a small sound of pity.

Good, Wren thought. Look at me. All of you, look at me. Look away from the doors.

* * *

The sixth line was in her mouth.

She felt the Chorus gathering itself behind it, the whole weight of the caged god leaning on the words, and she felt the cuffs tighten, and she understood with a strange calm that she was going to speak it, that her body was going to speak it no matter what she did, that the seed she had planted was a garden and not a fortress and the keystone would break it open — unless.

Unless the door the keystone opened was already broken. Unless someone had gotten there first.

The bells began to ring.

Not the nine green bells — those hung silent above the king, waiting for the rite's end. These were the tower bells of the palace itself, the alarm bells, and they were ringing *wrong*, all of them at once, no pattern, no order, the way bells ring when someone has climbed into the belfry and is swinging the ropes with both hands and no training at all.

The court's heads came up. The guards at the doors turned. And through the ringing, under it, Wren heard a sound she had been counting on for eleven days: the sound of the coronation hall's side door — the servants' door, the one Sefa had sworn existed because every palace Sefa had ever studied had one, *palaces are built by people who need to leave them* — opening.

Jonah came through it the way weather comes through a broken window.

He was not stealthy. He was not subtle. He was a big man in a stolen guard's coat with blood still crusted in his hair from the crossroads, and he had a bell-rope in one hand — that was what the bells were, that was the alarm, he had *rung them himself*, had climbed the tower with cracked ribs and rung the alarm with his own arms because he wanted every eye in the hall looking *up* — and he came down the aisle at a dead run and the guards at the dais turned to meet him and he went through them like a cart through a fence.

The hall exploded into screaming. Nine hundred people discovered all at once that they had never once believed the king was well.

And the Chorus did the thing Wren had known it would do, the only thing left to it. It abandoned patience. It stopped feeding her words through the cuffs and simply *took*, and the sixth line began to come out of her mouth in a voice that was not hers, deep and layered and wrong, six voices in one throat, *veth-asheneth-kora*, and the ice in her chest began to move toward the king like a flood toward a cracked dam—

—except.

Except that Wren had spent eleven days in a wagon learning the shape of the cuffs, and three years in the Tower learning the shape of her own voice, and one crucial hour on a road learning the shape of a big man's promise. The keystone needed a clean channel from her mouth to the king. And Wren's mouth was currently full of six borrowed voices, and a channel that is forced is not a channel that is clean, and she had *one* true word left in her, one word the cuffs had never logged because she had never said it aloud, had only ever shaped it silently against the roof of her mouth in the dark, over and over, like a coin in her cheek.

She said it.

*Vel.*

*Hold.*

Not the binding-under. Not the seed. The plain, naked, first word of First-Tongue, the word the Tower had beaten out of its vessels because a vessel that could *hold* was a vessel that could *refuse* — and it came out of her small and cracked and bleeding and it was the truest thing she had ever said, and the flood of the Chorus hit the word and *stopped*.

The hall went silent except for the bells and the screaming and Jonah's boots on the dais stairs.

The Chorus turned all of its attention on her then, truly turned it, and Wren stood on her pale stone in her bloodied white robe and held, the way a door holds, the way a dyke holds, the way a small thing holds when holding is all it has, and she felt the thing in the crown *understand* her for the first time. Not as a mouth. Not as a vessel.

As an opponent.

*You are a very small creature,* it said, in pressure, in cold, in the grinding of the gray stone of the king's face.

*Yes,* Wren thought. *And I am not alone.*

Jonah reached the dais. He was bleeding from somewhere new, and his eyes found hers across the last ten feet with a ferocity that was almost gentle, and behind him, limping through the servants' door with her bound wrists and her ruined throat, came Sefa — and behind *her*, two figures in oath-breaker gray, and the tallest of them was carrying something wrapped in oilcloth that hummed with a heat Wren recognized, a heat like a valley where magic cannot follow, a heat like a piece of a god.

Jonah put himself between Wren and the throne.

"Hi, sprout," he said, out of breath, blood on his teeth. "Sorry I'm late."

The crown of King Aldric began, very slowly, to glow.

And the king — the *man* — looked up at Jonah with the last of his own eyes and said, in a voice like a dry leaf, like a man speaking from the bottom of a well nineteen years deep:

"*Kill it.*"
