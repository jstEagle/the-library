## 19. The Unbinding

The crown glowed like a coal breathing.

Jonah had seen a lot of terrible things in twenty years of selling his sword, but nothing had ever looked at him the way the Hollow Chorus looked at him now — through Aldric's eyes, with Aldric's face, wearing the king's expression like a mask that fit too well. The gold filigree of the crown had gone molten-bright, and beneath it Jonah could see the seams: places where the thing had grown into the man's skull the way ivy grows into stone. Not sitting on the head. *Rooted* in it.

"*Kill it*," Aldric had said. And then the mouth closed, and something else opened its eyes behind the king's eyes, and the voice that came out was not one voice at all. It was many. It was a choir of dead gods singing through one ruined throat.

**YOU BROUGHT IT TO ME,** the Hollow Chorus said. **THE LAST SPEAKER. THE LAST VESSEL. I HAVE BEEN SO HUNGRY SINCE YOUR GOD STOPPED FIGHTING.**

Wren's hand found Jonah's. Small fingers. Cold. She didn't hide behind him — she stood beside him, chin up, staring at the thing on the throne with an expression he'd only seen once before, in the crypts, when she'd looked at the caged god's prison-stone. Recognition. And hatred.

"It doesn't get her," Jonah said.

**IT ALREADY HAS HER,** said the Chorus. **WE MADE HER. EVERY WORD IN HER IS OURS.**

"Yeah," Jonah said. "That's what you don't understand about words."

He spat blood onto the marble. Somewhere behind him, Sefa was moving — he heard her bare feet on the stone, heard the oath-breakers holding the doors against the crown guard, heard the whole great hall of the capital groaning around them like a ship in a storm. The Chorus's power pressed down on everything, a weight like deep water. Every mercenary instinct he had screamed at him to run.

Twenty years ago he'd knelt at a shrine in Ember Valley and sworn an oath to a god who never answered. He'd spent his whole life carrying that oath like a stone in his chest — the strength in his arms, the speed of his healing, the heat that rose in him when he defied the riders on the ridge. All of it borrowed. All of it *theirs*. And all of it, every drop, threaded through with the god's own tongue, the First-Tongue, stitched into him so deep he half-understood a mute child's speech on a storm night without knowing why.

The archivist had told him once: *A binding spoken truly cannot be undone by force. Only by a greater truth.*

Jonah looked at Wren. Wren looked back.

"You remember what I told you," he said quietly. "On the road. About oaths."

Her eyes were wet but steady. She signed it, fast, the way they'd built together over months of the road — *you keep your word even when it costs.* Then she hesitated, and added the sign they'd never needed before. The one that meant *come home after.*

"I will," Jonah lied gently, because some promises you make anyway.

Then he stepped forward, alone, toward the throne, and began to speak.

Not in the trade-tongue. Not in the valley dialect he'd grown up with. In First-Tongue — the language older than any kingdom, the language of binding, the syllables Sefa had drilled into them in a debt-bonded garret in Vell until his mouth bled from shapes it wasn't made for. He spoke the words of his oath. Every word of it. Aloud. In full. For the first time since the shrine.

And the hall *heard* it.

The Chorus screamed. Not in rage — in recognition. Because an oath is a binding, and a binding spoken aloud can be *unbound*, and the thing fused to Aldric's crown understood, in the last instant, exactly what Jonah was doing. His power wasn't his. It was a thread running from his heart to the caged god beneath the capital, and the Chorus had fed on that thread for nineteen years, sipping strength through a straw it thought Jonah would never find.

Jonah found it. And he pulled.

It hurt the way being born must hurt. The strength went out of him in a flood — twenty years of borrowed heat, borrowed speed, borrowed iron in the arm — and he spoke the unbinding as it left, each word costing more than the last, his voice cracking, his knees buckling, the marble rising up to meet him. He felt memories go with it, torn loose like roots from soil: his mother's kitchen, the smell of the valley in autumn, the name of the innkeeper who always poured him the dark beer. He let them go. He let *all* of it go, because the thread had to be severed clean or the Chorus would climb down it into the god's prison and devour the last of it forever.

The last word came out as a whisper.

The thread snapped.

Jonah hit the floor as an ordinary man. Weak. Human. Empty. And across the hall, the crown of King Aldric *cracked* — a single hairline fracture running through the glowing gold, wailing like a kettle, like a choir losing its note.

**WHAT HAVE YOU DONE,** the Chorus said, and for the first time it was only one voice, and it was afraid.

"Nothing," Jonah rasped into the marble. "She has."

Because Sefa was already standing at the foot of the dais, and she had been waiting her whole disgraced life for this moment, and she did not hesitate.

She spoke the unbinding.

The true one. The final binding-word her mentor had hidden in the Tower's deepest archive, the word that could only be spoken by a willing speaker, the word that unraveled a god-eater stitch by stitch. Her voice rang through the hall — clear, precise, the voice of the finest linguist of her generation — and with every syllable, something left her. Jonah watched it happen. Watched her voice fray like rope under load, watched the words come out thinner, rougher, watched her press a hand to her throat and keep speaking anyway, because she had chosen this price in advance and paid debts her whole life and this was simply the last one.

The crown shattered.

Not all at once — in layers, like ice going out of a lake in spring, each crack releasing a sound that was almost a voice, almost a name, gods long devoured exhaling their last through the ruin of the Hollow Chorus. Gold rained from Aldric's head. Black threads of the thing's substance whipped through the air, screaming in a hundred stolen tongues, seeking anything to bind to —

— and found nothing, because Jonah had cut his thread, and Sefa was cutting the rest, and Wren stood at the center of it all with her small fists clenched, and when a tendril of the dying thing lunged for her, she said one word.

One word of First-Tongue. True. Hers.

The tendril stopped. Hung in the air. And came apart like smoke in wind.

The Hollow Chorus unraveled. There was no better word for it. It came apart the way a lie comes apart — all at once, and then slowly, and then it was gone, and the great hall of the capital was very quiet, and very bright, and smelled of ozone and old gold.

King Aldric woke screaming.

They were the screams of a man surfacing from nineteen years underwater — raw, ragged, human. He clawed at his own scalp where the roots had been, weeping, laughing, cursing, all three at once. The crown guard froze in the doorway, weapons half-raised, watching their king collapse off his broken throne and crawl across the marble like any mad beggar, alive, *his own*, for the first time in two decades.

Jonah couldn't move. He lay where he'd fallen, an ordinary man in a hall full of armed soldiers, and watched Wren run to him — not to the king, to *him* — and drop to her knees and put both hands on his face and check him over with the fierce, frantic competence of someone who had done this before, on a road, in the rain, for a giant who kept getting hurt keeping her safe.

"I'm fine," he croaked. It was mostly true. He'd never felt less fine. He'd never felt more *himself*.

Sefa sat down hard beside them. When she tried to speak, nothing came. She touched her throat again, and this time her face did something complicated — grief and relief tangled together — and then she laughed, silently, shoulders shaking, and signed to Wren: *worth it.*

Aldric stopped screaming eventually. He knelt in the wreckage of his crown, an old man in a young body, and looked at Jonah across the marble.

"You," the king said hoarsely. "I know you. From inside. You prayed to it for twenty years and it never answered you, and you came anyway."

"Yeah, well." Jonah's vision swam. "I had a kid waiting on me."

Wren took his hand. And held on.

* * *

They carried him out through corridors that smelled of ash and celebration beginning somewhere far away. Bells were ringing — the city's brass bells, all of them, wild and unscheduled, the sound of a capital realizing its king was awake. Soldiers knelt as they passed. Someone had started a fire in a brazier that hadn't been lit in nineteen years.

Jonah drifted in and out. Once he woke to find Wren asleep against his side, her hand still fisted in his shirt, and Sefa watching them both with an expression he was learning to read without her voice: *look what we made. Look what survived.*

Once he woke to Aldric sitting beside his cot, alone, unguarded, looking older than any king should.

"The Tower," the king said quietly. "There are children in the Tower of Tongues. Like her. I signed the orders. I don't even remember signing them, but my hand did it, so they're mine." His jaw worked. "What do you want? Name it. A title. Land. Anything."

Jonah looked at the ceiling. Thought about the valley. Thought about a garden that didn't exist yet, over an empty shrine.

"Not yet," he said. "But I'll think of something. And you're going to hate it."

Aldric almost smiled. It was a terrible, broken, human smile. "Good," the king said. "I owe it a try."

Outside the window, the bells kept ringing, and beneath them — faint, so faint Jonah might have imagined it, except Wren stirred in her sleep and smiled — something warm and small and old answered.
