## 20. What the King Owes

The throne room of the capital smelled of ash and old incense, and Jonah stood in it with blood still crusted under his fingernails that wasn't his.

Three days since the unbinding. Three days since Sefa's voice had gone out like a candle pinched between wet fingers, since Aldric had woken screaming on the steps of his own dais, since the Hollow Chorus had come apart in the air like a rope of smoke cut by a knife. Three days of the palace not knowing what to do with itself — the guards saluting a king who could barely stand, the stewards sweeping up cinders that kept falling from nowhere, the courtiers whispering in the corridors about whether the man on the throne was still the man at all.

Jonah knew he was. He'd seen the flicker of him back in Vell, one instant of a trapped man looking out through eyes that weren't entirely his own. And he'd seen him now, properly, in the small hours when the palace slept: Aldric sitting alone in a side chamber with a ledger open on his knees, reading the records of his own reign, weeping without sound.

That was the thing nobody told you about freeing a man from a god. The god took the blame with it. What was left was just a person, holding the bill.

* * *

They came for Jonah on the third morning. Two chamberlains and a captain of the household guard, all of them very polite, none of them meeting his eye for longer than a breath.

"His Majesty requests your presence in the Hall of Reckoning," the captain said. "Alone."

"And if I'd rather not come alone?"

The captain's jaw worked. "He asked us to tell you — alone, or not at all."

So it was alone. Jonah followed them through corridors hung with banners of a dynasty he'd never sworn to, past windows where the city spread out below in its winter grime and chimney smoke, ordinary and alive, full of people who would never know how close they'd come to having a god-eater for a king. His body felt strange without the oath in it. Lighter, somehow, and weaker, like a house with a wall knocked out. Twenty years of borrowed strength gone, and what remained was just a mercenary's frame, scarred and stiff in the cold, aching in the shoulder where a crown agent's blade had opened him outside the procession grounds.

He missed the strength. That was the honest truth of it, and he let himself feel it fully as he walked, because he'd decided somewhere on the road to the capital that he was done lying to himself about what things cost. He missed it the way you miss a limb. And he would not have taken it back for anything in the world.

Wren had made him promise. Before they went into the crypts, before the coronation hall, before any of it, she had taken his hand in both of hers — her hands so small around his one — and said, in First-Tongue, the words Sefa had taught them both: *What is given can be taken. What is chosen cannot.*

He hadn't understood then, not really. He understood now.

* * *

The Hall of Reckoning was smaller than the throne room, older, built by some earlier dynasty for exactly the purpose its name implied. No dais. No crown displayed on velvet. Just a plain stone table, two chairs, and a window high on the eastern wall through which the morning light fell in one long blade across the floor.

Aldric stood at the table. Not sat — stood, gripping its edge with both hands, and Jonah saw immediately why: the king's arms shook. Not with fear. With the effort of standing upright in a body that had been worn like a glove for eleven years, every muscle remembering all at once what it was for.

"You came," Aldric said. His voice was rough, half-ruined from the screaming. "I thought you might not."

"You're the king. When the king asks—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of him harder than he'd meant it. He closed his eyes, steadied himself. "Please don't do that. I've had eleven years of people saying *your majesty* at me while something else wore my face and did — " He stopped. Looked down at the table. On it lay the ledger Jonah had seen him reading in the night, and beside it a shorter document, sealed with black wax.

Jonah didn't touch either. "You read it," he said. "All of it?"

"Every page. Twice." Aldric laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "Do you know what I did while I was crowned? I mean — do you know? Because I'm only just learning. The Chorus kept my memories, most of them, but it kept them the way a glove keeps the shape of a hand. I remember watching myself sign things. I remember watching myself speak. Eleven years of standing behind glass inside my own skull while that thing used my mouth to — " His breath went ragged. "The Tower of Tongues. It expanded the breeding program. Fourteen children a year. I signed the writs. My hand signed them."

"The Chorus's hand."

"My hand." Aldric looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "That's the whole of it, isn't it? That's what no one will say to me. It was my hand. I was weak enough to be taken, vain enough to accept a crown that whispered, and everything after — every child, every binding, every family broken so the Tower could harvest their daughters — flows from the day I put that thing on my head because the priests told me it was an honor." He pushed off the table and stood straighter, shaking, refusing to fall. "I am not asking you to absolve me, mercenary. I'm telling you so you understand what I owe, and to whom."

Jonay leaned against the wall and waited. He'd learned patience in twenty years of praying to a silence; three days of a king's guilt was nothing against that.

Aldric slid the sealed document across the table toward him.

"A writ of reward," the king said. "Land in the Ember Valley — yes, I know what the valley is, the Chorus raged about it often enough, the one place it couldn't reach. A title. A place at court. Gold enough that your grandchildren would never carry a sword again. Whatever you want, within reason, it's yours. You freed a kingdom. More than a kingdom." His voice dropped. "You freed *me*. Name it."

Jonah looked at the seal. Black wax, the royal crest pressed deep, the paper heavy and fine. Everything he'd ever been promised by anyone in power fit on documents like this, and none of those promises had ever survived contact with the people who made them.

"I want to ask you something first," Jonah said. "And I want you to answer it the way you answered the question about your hand. Honestly, even if it costs you."

Aldric's chin lifted slightly. Wariness, but also respect — the look of a man who had spent eleven years being managed and had developed a taste for bluntness.

"Ask."

"The Tower of Tongues. Is it still standing?"

Something flickered across the king's face. "It is."

"Are the children still in it?"

"They are — " Aldric stopped. Closed his mouth. Opened it again. "Forty-one children remain in the Tower's custody. The writs dissolving the institution have been drafted, but the council — " He faltered. "The council advises caution. The Tower holds the kingdom's entire apparatus of translation, diplomacy, treaty-craft. Its scholars argue that dismantling it suddenly would leave the realm mute among its neighbors. There are also questions of law. The children are wards of the crown. Their custody is governed by statutes older than my reign, and the council's lawyers maintain that a king recovering from — from what I have suffered — lacks the standing to overturn settled law by decree."

Jonah let that sit in the air for a moment. Then he said, quietly: "Say the last part plainly. Say what the lawyers actually told you."

Aldric's jaw tightened. For a long moment the only sound was wind worrying at the high window.

"They told me," he said finally, "that a king who served an alien power for eleven years may be judged incompetent retroactively. That if I move too fast, too strangely, in my first weeks, the council has grounds to declare a regency. That the safest course — for me, for the succession, for stability — is to leave the Tower intact until my position is secure, and address the children afterward. In a year. Perhaps two." He met Jonah's eyes. "There. Plainly. They told me to trade forty-one children for my own throne, and they dressed it in language about treaties and statutes so that no one would ever have to say it aloud."

"In a year or two," Jonah said, "some of those children turn twelve. That's when the Tower starts the vessel-work. I know because Wren told me, and she knows because she lived it." He pushed off the wall and crossed to the table, and set his palm flat on the black wax seal, and did not pick up the writ. "I don't want this."

Aldric blinked. "You don't — the land, the title, whatever you—"

"No." Jonah turned the document over, so the seal faced down, and left it lying there like something dead. "Here's what I want instead. And I'll warn you now, it's going to be harder than gold."

* * *

He laid it out the way he'd lay out terms for a contract, because that was the only language he trusted, and because he'd learned on the road — Sefa teaching, Wren correcting, the oath-breakers nodding along — that precision was a form of mercy. Vague promises were how institutions ate people.

"One. The Tower of Tongues is dissolved. Not reformed, not reviewed, not placed under new management. Dissolved. Its charter burned, its funding ended, its buildings sealed pending a full accounting of what happened inside them."

"Sealed buildings become forgotten buildings," Aldric murmured. "Forgotten buildings keep their secrets."

"Then: sealed, inventoried, and opened to public record within the year. Every register, every breeding log, every name. The families whose children were taken get to read what was done, in the kingdom's own handwriting. You owed them that for eleven years. The debt doesn't die with the Chorus."

Aldric had gone pale, but he nodded. "Go on."

"Two. The forty-one children are released into guardianship — real guardianship, chosen by them where they're old enough to choose, vetted by people who aren't Tower scholars. Funded by the crown. Not as charity. As restitution. And the ones who were bred as vessels need healers who understand what was done to them, which means the crown pays for the best, wherever they are, however long it takes."

"Three," Jonah continued, before the king could speak, "the scholars and wardens of the Tower who ran the vessel program answer for it. Publicly. Not purged in the night, not disappeared — tried, in the open, with the evidence from the registers. If the law can't reach what they did, then the law gets bent until it can, and everyone watches it bend, so no one can pretend afterward that justice was quiet."

"That will tear the council apart," Aldric said. "Half of them took Tower money. Two of them have nieces in the Tower's service."

"Good," Jonah said. "Let it tear. A council that can be torn apart by forty-one children's names was already rotten through, and better it breaks now, in daylight, than later, in the dark, the way the Chorus broke everything else."

The king stared at him. "You're asking me to spend my fragile, contested, possibly-regency-doomed authority on — on the least powerful people in my kingdom, at the exact moment the lawyers say I should hoard it."

"Yes."

"And in exchange you take nothing. No land, no title, no gold."

"I take the thing I asked for. That's the exchange."

Aldric laughed again — different this time, startled out of him, almost human. "Who taught you to negotiate? You're terrible at it. You're supposed to extract concessions for yourself. That's how power works. Everyone in this building would tell you the same."

"Everyone in this building," Jonah said, "wore the same crown's shadow for eleven years and called it order. Maybe it's time someone in it learned a different way to count."

Silence. The blade of morning light had crept across the floor and now touched the table, lit the dead seal, climbed the king's shaking hands. Aldric looked down at them for a long time.

"When I was crowned," he said at last, "there was a moment — seconds, maybe less — when I felt it settle onto me. The Chorus. Like cold water finding every seam of a coat. And some part of me, some small coward part, decided not to fight, because fighting hurt and yielding didn't, not right away. I have spent eleven years telling myself I was overpowered. I was not overpowered. I was *comfortable*. That's the truth of how gods eat kings." He straightened, and though his arms still trembled, his voice did not. "You refused one, didn't you. In the crypts. It offered you everything back and you said no."

"It offered me twenty years of prayers answered. I said no."

"Why?" And it wasn't a king's question, or a negotiator's. It was a man's, raw and personal, the question of someone standing at the foot of his own ruin trying to learn how it was survivable.

Jonah thought about lying, and didn't. "Because I'd finally found something worth more than being strong. Took me long enough to find it. A mute kid who speaks the oldest language alive, a scholar who gave her voice for the rest of ours, a handful of broken mercenaries who became — " He stopped, surprised by the word even as he said it. "*Family.* The god couldn't give me that. It could only give me power, and power was always the price, dressed up as a gift. So. No."

Aldric nodded slowly. Then he drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, and a pen, and began to write — slowly, his hand still unsteady, the letters large and deliberate. Jonah watched him write for a while before he realized what it was: not a decree. A declaration of intent, in the king's own words, addressed to the council, beginning *I, Aldric, having been for eleven years unworthy of this crown, do hereby—*

"You'll have your dissolution," Aldric said as he wrote. "Your restitution. Your trials. I'll spend every coin of my authority on it, and when the lawyers come at me with their regency threats, I'll let them come. A king who trades children for a throne deserves to lose it." He paused, pen lifted. "But hear me, Iversen. I will fail at parts of this. Councils are slow, courts are corrupt, and I am one shaking man learning to govern a body and a kingdom at the same time. When I fail, I'd rather it be witnessed by someone who'll call it what it is. Stay. Advise me. Be the conscience I spent eleven years outsourcing to a god."

"No," Jonah said, and gentled it. "I'm going home. There's a valley that needs me, and a kid who needs a door she can knock on, and a shrine that ought to become a garden." He tapped the dead writ once. "But I'll leave you something better than my advice. Her name is Sefa. She can't speak anymore — she spent her voice unbinding the thing that owned you — but she can write faster than any ten lawyers, and she knows exactly how the Tower worked from the inside, because she trained in it. She's disgraced, indebted, and the most dangerous mind in your kingdom. Hire her. Pay her debts first. Listen to her even when she's making you furious. Especially then."

Aldric considered. "And the child? Wren?"

"She goes where she goes. Nobody decides for her ever again — that's the whole point of everything." Jonah headed for the door, then stopped with his hand on the iron ring. "One more thing. Free, not owed. When the trials start, and the families come to read the registers — be in the room. Not on a throne. In the room, standing, letting them look at you. Some of them will curse you. Let them. It's the cheapest payment you'll ever make on that debt, and the only one they'll actually want."

He pulled the door open. Morning air moved through the hall, carrying chimney smoke and bell-noise and the ordinary sound of a city that didn't know yet what had been decided for it.

"Iversen." Aldric's voice stopped him. The king was looking at the black-sealed writ still lying face-down on the table. "This. What should I do with it?"

Jonah smiled for the first time in three days. "Burn it. You're a king. You should practice deciding that some things aren't for sale."

* * *

He found Wren in the palace kitchens, which was where he'd known he'd find her — she'd discovered on the second day that the kitchen staff fed anyone who helped, and that a former Tower vessel could learn to knead bread like it was a form of prayer. She was flour to the elbows, sitting on a barrel beside the ovens, and Sefa was with her, the two of them bent over a slate, Sefa writing in quick sure strokes, Wren answering in First-Tongue characters that glowed faintly warm and faded.

Sefa looked up as Jonah came in. She couldn't smile widely — the unbinding had left her face tired, her throat scarred-looking — but her eyes did the smiling for her, and she raised the slate: *Well?*

Jonah sat down on the barrel next to Wren. "Tower's coming down. Kids go free. Trials in the open. Sefa, congratulations, you're employed — the king's paying your debts, and you're going to run the reckoning, because nobody else in this palace knows where all the bodies are written down."

Sefa stared. Then she wrote, fast: *They'll never agree.*

"They already started not agreeing. Aldric sent the declaration to the council an hour ago." He shrugged. "Two chancellors resigned on the spot. One of them threw a decanter. It's going to be a war, and it's going to be slow, and it's going to work — because a king with nothing left to lose is the most dangerous kind, and this one just figured out what he owes."

Wren tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed at herself, then at the door, then made a shape in the air — a valley, a curve of hills, unmistakable even in flour-white hands.

"Yeah," Jonah said, and his chest did something complicated and warm. "Home. Soon as the roads clear. There's a garden waiting to be planted over an empty shrine, and I hear the soil down there grows anything."

Wren grinned — and then stopped, her head tilting, listening to something Jonah couldn't hear. The ovens ticked. The firelight shivered.

And in the hearth, small and clear, in a voice like a coal settling, something answered her.

Sefa's chalk froze mid-word. Wren's eyes went wide, and she grabbed Jonah's arm with both floured hands, and the voice came again — warmer this time, closer, a thread of sound rising out of the ashes of a dead god's silence:

It was singing.
