## 21. The Silence After Gods

Sefa had spent her whole life believing that words were the only thing that mattered, and now she had none of them.

She sat in the window of the healer's house in Vell — they had carried her back down the mountain rather than let her travel farther on a broken ankle and an empty throat — and she watched the city go about its morning as if the sky hadn't torn open nine days ago. Bells rang from the brass towers. Fishmongers shouted prices. A dog chased a cart wheel's shadow up the Processional and lost it at the fountain. The world, it turned out, did not require the Hollow Chorus to be dead in order to keep turning. It had never required anything of the kind.

Her throat was a closed door. She could feel the shape of every word behind it, stacked like books in a flooded library. *Unbind. Unmake. Go home.* She had said them once, truly, in the old tongue, at the cost of everything else she would ever say, and the language had taken its price the way it always took its price: completely, without malice, without apology.

First-Tongue was honest that way. She had taught that to Jonah in a borrowed room above a dye-shop, chalk dust on her fingers, and he had looked at her like she'd handed him a live coal. *It doesn't lie,* she'd told him. *That's what makes it dangerous.*

Now she was the proof.

A knock at the doorframe. Wren stood there with a slate in one hand and a basket of pears in the other, hair braided crookedly because braiding was still a two-person job and Jonah's hands were better suited to sword grips than ribbons. The girl had appointed herself Sefa's translator within a day of the unbinding, with the grave efficiency of a child who had been made into a tool and was determined, on her own terms, to become useful again.

*Jonah says you have to eat,* Wren signed, then spoke aloud out of habit before catching herself and flushing. She still did that — spoke first, remembered second, as though eight years of having a voice couldn't be undone by one winter of learning silence. Sefa found it oddly comforting. Somewhere under all that careful signing, Wren was still a girl who babbled.

*I'm eating,* Sefa signed back, lifting the pear she had not touched.

Wren gave her the flat look of a customs inspector. Then she set the basket down, climbed onto the window seat without asking, and leaned her head against Sefa's shoulder the way she'd started doing sometime during the road to the capital, when fear had worn them both smooth enough to fit together.

For a while they watched the city.

Then Wren signed, slowly, choosing each hand-shape with care: *Are you sad?*

Sefa considered lying. She was, after all, very good with words, even the ones she no longer owned.

She settled instead for the truth in pieces. *I am mourning,* she signed. *Not him. Not the god. I'm mourning my own voice. It's allowed to be both small and enormous.*

Wren frowned at the signs, working through them. Then, with the terrible directness of children: *You can still talk. You just do it different now.* She tapped Sefa's hands. *This is talking. I understand you fine.*

And that — that was the thing Sefa could not have said aloud if she'd had a thousand throats, so it was fortunate she didn't have to say it at all. She put her arm around the girl and held on, and outside, the bells of Vell rang the hour, and nobody rang them for a god.

* * *

The god came on the eleventh day.

Sefa knew it was coming before anyone told her, because the birds went quiet along the whole street at once, the way they had gone quiet in the crypt beneath the capital when she and Jonah had walked down into the dark to find the thing that had once answered twenty years of a mercenary's prayers. Silence, she was learning, came in flavors. There was the silence of absence. And there was the silence of presence — something so vast holding itself so carefully still that the air around it forgot how to move.

She was in the garden behind the healer's house, relearning the world by touch, when the light changed. Not dimmed — *changed*, the way candlelight changes when someone carries it into the room. She turned, and there he was in the gateway: a man of middling height with grey in his beard and dust on his boots, dressed like a pilgrim, looking like nothing on earth, because he wasn't.

Diminished. That was the word the oath-breakers used, whispered, half in awe and half in grief. The Hollow Chorus had eaten gods whole for centuries, but it had only caged this one, and a cage preserves. When Sefa and the bindings had unraveled the Chorus thread by thread, the prisoner had spilled out into the world with nowhere to hold itself — and so it had done the only thing left. It had poured itself into flesh. Mortal-bound. One body. One heartbeat. One pair of boots, currently crusted with the mud of the long walk from the capital.

He looked tired the way mountains look tired. He looked like a man who had been carrying the sky and had set it down and discovered his shoulders remembered nothing else.

Jonah was already there. Of course he was; Sefa had heard him cross the yard a minute earlier, drawn by the same wrongness in the birdsong, moving with the easy lethal grace that had been his since before either of them met — though not, she noted, quite as easily as before. The oath-breaking had cost him. The strength the god had lent him across twenty years of service was gone, and what remained was a big man in his forties with old scars and new ones, who winced slightly when he thought no one was watching.

Wren materialized at Jonah's side the way she always did, close enough to touch, not quite touching. Watching the god with eyes that had been bred in the Tower of Tongues to watch exactly this kind of thing.

The god spoke first. His voice was ordinary. That was somehow the worst part.

"Jonah Iversen."

"Your — " Jonah stopped. Sefa saw him wrestle with it, the old reflexes of reverence fighting the newer, harder-won thing underneath. "You're smaller," he finished, which was not what any liturgy would have suggested, and which Sefa privately scored as excellent.

"I am one man's worth of myself," the god agreed. "It is more than I expected to be. I had thought to pour out entirely." He looked around the garden — at the pear tree, at the laundry line, at Sefa sitting in the dirt among the herb rows — and something moved across his face that might, on a human face, have been wonder. "Everything is so *close* now. I had forgotten."

"Why are you here?" Jonah asked. No heat in it. Just the question, laid out flat, the way he laid out everything.

"You know why."

"I want to hear you say it."

The god folded his hands. For a moment he was silent, and Sefa, watching, understood that the silence was not hesitation. It was the weight of a being who had spent eons being answered trying to remember how to ask.

"The Chorus is dead. Its bindings are ash. But the old oaths remain written in the world — yours among them. You swore yourself to me, Jonah Iversen, and the oath was never dissolved, only abandoned. I am asking you to take it up again. Serve me as you served before. With your strength returned, and more besides." A pause. "I will not pretend the offer is small. I am small, and I need hands. The world has a hole in it where I used to stand, and things will move into that hole if no one holds the border. I can offer you purpose. I can offer you power. I can offer you—" and here his gaze flicked, briefly, to Wren, "—protection for those you love, such as a diminished god can still provide."

The garden was very quiet. Sefa realized she had stopped breathing.

She watched Jonah's face the way she had once watched ancient texts, reading the marginalia, the corrections, the places where a scribe's hand trembled. Twenty years, she thought. Twenty years of praying into a silence that never answered, of keeping faith with a god who had been screaming inside a cage the whole time, of building his entire self around an abandonment that had never actually happened. And here was the answer, standing in a garden in worn boots, offering him everything back.

Any other man would have wept. Any other man would have knelt.

Jonah looked at the god for a long moment. Then he looked down at Wren, who had taken his hand without appearing to notice she'd done it, her small fingers laced through his scarred ones as naturally as breathing.

"No," Jonah said.

Sefa's heart turned over.

The god did not flinch. "No?"

"No." Jonah's voice was steady, and Sefa heard in it the echo of the crypt, of the moment he had refused restoration at the source, and knew this was not impulse. This was a decision he had already made, alone, in whatever country existed inside him, and today he was merely announcing it. "I spent twenty years serving something I couldn't hear. I built my whole life around a silence. I told myself it was devotion." He shook his head slowly. "It was loneliness wearing devotion's clothes. I don't want that back. I don't want to kneel to anything ever again — not because I'm proud, but because kneeling kept me from seeing what was right in front of me." His hand tightened around Wren's. "I've got a kid to raise. A friend who can't talk and a house with an empty shrine and a garden that needs planting. That's my congregation now. That's the whole of it."

"You would refuse a god," the god said. Not angry. Almost gentle. "Most men never get the chance. Fewer survive taking it."

"Yeah, well." The ghost of a grin. "I've survived worse odds with worse company."

Something happened to the god's face then that Sefa would spend the rest of her life trying to describe, and failing, because she no longer had words and perhaps there had never been adequate ones anyway. It was not disappointment. It was recognition — the look of a being meeting, for the first time in its long existence, a man who wanted nothing from it, and finding the meeting clean.

"Then I release the oath," the god said formally, and the air shivered the way it does before rain. "Unwritten. Unowed. You are free, Jonah Iversen, wholly, and I — " he paused, and the pause had centuries in it, " — I find I envy you."

"What will you do?" Wren asked. Her voice, small and clear. She alone in the garden seemed unafraid, and Sefa wondered — not for the first time — whether the Tower had bred fear out of her or simply never bred it in.

The god considered her. "Walk," he said at last. "There is a great deal of world and I have never seen it from ground level. Perhaps I will learn to plant things." His eyes crinkled. "I am told the soil in Ember Valley grows anything."

Jonah stared at him. "You're not serious."

"I am told," the god repeated, with dignity, "that there is a house there with an empty shrine, and that the shrine's keeper is a man who makes excellent stew, and that the child speaks First-Tongue to hearthfires and gets answers. I have been a god for a very long time, Jonah Iversen. I would like, for a while, to be a guest."

Silence. Wren was grinning. Sefa realized her own mouth had curved too, helplessly, the first true smile since the unbinding.

"We'll need another chair," Jonah said finally, in the tone of a man losing an argument he'd already lost. "And you're chopping wood. Gods or not, everybody chops wood."

* * *

They left three days later, when the roads cleared and the king's new writ arrived — Aldric's seal, dissolving the Tower of Tongues by name, ordering every vessel-child released to kin or guardians, and, appended in the king's own shaking hand, a single line addressed to *the mercenary Iversen and his companions*: *The crown owes you a debt it cannot pay. I intend to spend my life trying anyway. — A.*

Jonah read it twice and used the back of it to note down seed prices.

Sefa rode in the cart because of her ankle, wrapped in blankets, watching Vell shrink behind them. The god walked beside the oxen, learning the rhythm of it, stumbling once on a rut and laughing — actually laughing — at his own clumsiness, and Wren laughed with him, and Jonah pretended not to smile and failed completely.

In the evenings at camp, Sefa taught. She had no voice, so she taught with sticks in the dirt, with hand-signs Wren translated, with the fierce economy of a woman who had lost her instrument and refused to lose her music. The god sat at the edge of the firelight and listened to lessons in his own ancient tongue delivered by a mute woman through a child's mouth, and if it wounded him to hear the First-Tongue reduced to scratches in cold ash, he never showed it. Once, deep in the night, Sefa woke and found him standing over the dying fire, shaping a word silently with his lips — some old word of keeping or blessing, meant for the sleeping forms around him — and then letting it fall unspoken, unfinished, because he no longer had the right to speak it into the world unasked.

She signed to him from her blanket: *Say it anyway. We won't break.*

He looked at her for a long time. Then he shook his head, almost smiling. *When we reach the valley,* he signed back, clumsily, a god's fingers learning a child's grammar. *There is a hearth there. I am told things sung to it get answers.*

On the last morning, with the Ember Valley's rim rising red-gold ahead of them and the air already tasting of home, Wren climbed up onto the cart seat beside Sefa and tugged her sleeve.

*Sefa.* Her hands were quick, excited. *Last night the fire sang again. While everyone slept. It sang a song I've never heard — old, older than the Tower songs — and it was singing about a valley, and a garden, and — * she hesitated, and her eyes went wide, and she grabbed Sefa's wrist hard enough to leave marks —

*— and it was singing about you. It says your voice isn't gone. It says the fire kept it. It says when we get home, if you speak to the hearth, it will give the words back.*
