## 22. When the Ember Sings Anew

The valley smelled the same.

That was the first thing Wren noticed, standing at the ridge where the road dipped down between two shoulders of grey stone — that the air still carried woodsmoke and cold pine and something mineral underneath, like licked iron. The Tower had smelled of ink and lye and fear. Vell had smelled of brass polish and fish and too many bodies in too little space. The capital had smelled of ash, even before the coronation hall came apart, even before Sefa opened her mouth and unmade a god with a sentence.

But this place smelled like nothing had happened anywhere at all.

Wren looked up at Jonah, who had stopped walking when she stopped, because he always did that now, like her feet were tied to his by an invisible thread he'd never asked for but never once complained about.

"You lived here," she said. Not a question. She knew it the way she knew the shape of her own hands.

"Twenty-nine years," Jonah said. He shifted the pack on his shoulder. It held seed packets and a spade and three loaves of bread wrapped in cloth, and nothing else, because everything else they owned fit in one cart pulled by a mule named Turnip who was currently eating a thistle with tremendous commitment. "Then I left to go save a king and break a god, and now I'm back, and the shrine's empty, and I don't—" He stopped. Rubbed his jaw. Jonah talked like a man crossing a river on stepping stones, one flat word at a time, and sometimes he ran out of stones mid-sentence.

"You don't know if it's home," Wren finished for him.

He looked down at her with that expression she'd spent months learning to read — the one that wasn't surprise exactly, more like the small ache of being seen. "Yeah," he said. "That."

Behind them, Sefa coughed — her new cough, the wet rattle that lived in her throat now where her voice used to be — and made a quick gesture: *walk*. Her hands spoke fluently these days. She'd invented half the vocabulary herself in the first weeks after the unbinding, furious and precise, refusing to be slow about it. *Walk,* she signed again, then pointed at the sky, where clouds were stacking up over the western peaks like plates on a merchant's arm.

They walked.

* * *

The shrine sat in a fold of the hillside above the village, a low stone building with a slate roof and a door that no longer quite closed. Inside there was a hearth, cold for years, and an altar stone, bare since the day the god went silent, and dust thick enough to write in. Wren wrote in it. She drew a wobbly sun and a stick figure with a very large sword, which she felt was accurate.

Jonah stood in the doorway holding the spade like he didn't know what it was for.

"I used to sweep this floor every morning," he said. "Before contracts. Before anything. I'd light the fire even though it never answered, and I'd say the morning words, and I'd leave." A pause. "I thought that was devotion. Turns out it was just talking to myself in a nice room."

Sefa set her hand on his arm. Then she pointed at the hearth, and at the floor, and made a sweeping motion, and raised one eyebrow in a way that managed, without a single sound, to say *well? Are you going to stand there feeling sorry for yourself or are you going to work?*

Jonah huffed out something that was almost a laugh. "Yes, ma'am."

They worked all afternoon. They pried up the flagstones around the shrine's south wall, where the ground lay fallow and dark, and turned the soil until their backs ached. Sefa directed from a stool with the imperious calm of a woman who had spent thirty years telling students what to do and saw no reason to stop just because she could no longer tell them *out loud.* Jonah dug. Wren carried water from the stream in a bucket nearly as big as she was, slopping most of it, refilling, carrying, slopping again.

And they planted.

Turnip seeds and beans. Squash and onions. A row of marigolds along the wall because Sefa insisted gods liked flowers even when they were gone, or maybe especially then, and because Wren liked the way the seeds looked like tiny curled-up dragons. The soil went over the old altar path, over the worn places where twenty years of a mercenary's boots had polished the stone smooth. By the time the light went amber and long, the garden was in, and the shrine looked less like a tomb and more like the front yard of somebody's house.

"Somebody's house," Wren said out loud, testing it.

Jonah wiped his forehead with his sleeve. "Whose?"

"Ours." She said it fast, before she could lose her nerve, the way you jump into cold water. "Ours. If you want."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Wren counted them the way the Tower had taught her to count everything — breaths, seconds, syllables, the exact weight of a word before it left your mouth. She'd been bred to weigh words. She was still learning to spend them freely.

Then Jonah crouched down so his eyes were level with hers, which he always did when it mattered, even though it must have hurt his knees, which were older than kingdoms as far as Wren could tell.

"Wren," he said. "I broke my oath. You know what that cost me. Half my strength. Some of my memories — I still can't remember my mother's face, only that I had one, and that she hummed." His voice caught on that, just slightly, and smoothed itself over. "I'm not a sworn man anymore. I'm not a god-touched anything. I'm just a big scarred fellow with a bad knee and a mule."

"And a garden," Wren said.

"And a garden," he agreed. "And a deaf— " he glanced at Sefa, corrected himself, "—a *quiet* friend who'll kill us both if we burn the bread. And a kid who speaks the language the world was made in." He put his huge hand gently on top of her head. "Kid, you don't have to ask. You've been mine since the night you showed up dripping on my doorstep looking at me like I was the strange one."

"You *were* the strange one," Wren said. "You're enormous."

"So I'm told."

Sefa tapped her knuckle against the doorframe twice — her sign for *enough sentiment, get inside before the rain* — and right on cue the first drops came down, fat and warm-smelling, and the three of them and the mule crowded into the little stone shrine that had been empty for years and was suddenly, absurdly, full.

* * *

The hearth was the problem.

It hadn't been lit since the god went quiet. Jonah said so while he knelt at it, arranging kindling with the same care he gave to sword maintenance. "I lit it every day for six years after the silence started. Every day. Nothing. Not even smoke would sit right — it would curl away from the center like the fire itself was avoiding the spot." He struck flint. Sparks jumped and died. "I figured whatever was wrong with the god was wrong with the fire too."

"The god isn't gone," Wren said.

She hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way true things sometimes did with her — sideways, unbidden, in the pause after someone else stops talking. Jonah turned. Even Sefa went still, her hands frozen mid-sign.

"What do you mean?" Jonah asked carefully.

Wren hugged her knees. She'd learned things in the capital's crypts, kneeling in front of the diminished, mortal-bound remnant of the thing that had once been Jonah's god — the thing that had offered him restoration, power, everything back, and been refused. She'd heard its voice through the stone. Everyone assumed that when the Hollow Chorus unraveled, the prisoner god had simply... ended. Gone out like a lamp.

But Wren had been built to hear languages the way other people heard music. And underneath the god's last words — *he refused me, and it was the first honest prayer I ever received* — she had heard something else. Something faint. Something that sounded less like a voice and more like an ember settling in a grate.

"It didn't die," she said. "It got *small.* Smaller than small. The Chorus ate everything it had except the part that couldn't be eaten, and the part that couldn't be eaten is..." She searched for the word in Common, found it wanting, and reached past it into First-Tongue, where the concept lived whole: *the-warmth-that-remembers-being-warm.*

"When the Chorus came apart," she said, "that part fell. Like a spark off a log. And sparks land somewhere."

Jonah stared at her. Rain ticked on the slate roof. Somewhere under the floor, under the foundation, under the hill itself, Wren felt it — the way you feel someone watching you from another room, the way you know a kettle is about to sing a half-second before it sings.

"Where did it land?" Jonah whispered.

Wren looked at the hearth.

* * *

She knelt where Jonah had knelt for twenty years, saying morning words to a silence that never answered. She arranged the kindling the way he'd shown her — small sticks first, breathing room between them, patience. Sefa lowered herself onto the bench nearby, hands folded, watching with the fierce attention she usually saved for rare manuscripts. Jonah stayed standing, arms crossed, jaw tight, a man trying very hard not to hope in case hope was a load-bearing wall and it collapsed on him.

Wren took a breath.

First-Tongue wasn't hard for her. That was the terrible secret of her whole life — the Tower hadn't taught her the language, they'd grown her *in* it, stitched it into her before she had words for milk or mother. Speaking it truly reshaped reality; speaking it falsely did nothing. So she had learned, in the crypts and the coronation hall and the long road home, to speak only what was true.

She looked at the cold black hearthstone and told it the truth.

"*Little one,*" she said, and the syllables came out round and warm, filling the small stone room like water fills a cup. "*You fell far. You fell long. But falling ends where the ground begins, and the ground remembers how to hold things.*"

Nothing happened.

She kept going anyway, because the second truth was this: "*You are not a god anymore. Nobody kneels here. There's just a house, and a fire needs lighting, and we're asking if you'd like to be it.*"

"*Not worshipped,*" she said. "*Kept.*"

The rain came harder. The kindling waited. Jonah's breath had gone shallow and careful beside her, and Wren thought: *this is what he sounded like, all those years, every morning, talking to a room.* Except he'd spoken Common, the language of asking. She was speaking First-Tongue, the language of binding — but she wasn't binding. She was doing the opposite of binding. She was doing the thing nobody in the Tower had ever thought to do with the oldest words in the world.

She was inviting.

"*Come be warm where we are warm,*" she finished, softly. "*Come be small where it's safe to be small. Come home.*"

For a long moment, nothing. The rain. The mule shifting in the lean-to outside. Sefa's breath.

Then the kindling cracked.

Not the way wood cracks when fire takes it — the way wood cracks when it *decides*, a single clean pop like a knuckle, like a door unlatching. A thread of smoke rose from the smallest stick, thin and silver, and curled — and instead of drifting toward the draft, it curled *inward*, down toward the heart of the hearth, the way smoke had never once curled in all the years Jonah had begged it to.

A spark woke in the ash. Small. Orange. Stubborn.

It caught the birchbark. The bark flared blue-gold, and the flame that followed wasn't a normal flame — it was smaller than it should have been, and warmer than it should have been, and it leaned toward Wren's hand the way a cat leans toward a familiar lap. Heat washed over her face, gentle as a blanket. And in the crackle of the burning wood she heard it, faint and unmistakable, the way you hear your own name across a noisy room:

Something warm and small, humming.

The tune was wordless. It didn't need words. Wren knew it instantly, knew it the way she knew First-Tongue, bone-deep and pre-verbal, and she looked up at Jonah with tears running down her face and no memory of starting them.

"It's your mother's song," she said. "The humming. It's — Jonah, it kept it. All those years you prayed, it was listening, it kept *everything*, it kept the one thing you lost—"

Jonah made a sound she'd never heard him make. This enormous man, this scarred mountain of a mercenary who had broken an oath and unmade a god and carried her bleeding out of a burning city, sat down on the hearthstones like his legs had been cut out from under him, and put his face in his hands, and cried in front of the fire like a child.

Sefa's hand settled on his back. Her other hand moved — slow, deliberate, eloquent: *Welcome home.*

The fire hummed. The rain sang on the roof. Outside, in the dark, the garden they'd planted was already drinking, and under the soil a hundred tiny curled-up dragons waited for spring.

* * *

Later — much later, when Sefa had fallen asleep on the bench with a manuscript of blank pages open on her lap, ready for the dictionary she planned to build, the great bridge between tongues that would make sure no child was ever again bred to be a lock — Wren sat by the hearth with her feet almost touching the warm stones.

Jonah sat beside her. He'd stopped crying hours ago, but something in his face had rearranged itself permanently, some knot behind his eyes finally untied.

"It won't ever be what it was," he said quietly. "A god. It'll just be... this. Firelight. Warmth. Maybe it hears us, maybe it doesn't, really. Maybe it's just a fire that likes us."

"That's better," Wren said.

"Yeah," Jonah said. "I think maybe it is."

Wren held her palms out to the flames. The fire leaned into them, humming its borrowed lullaby, and she let her eyes close, and in the drowsy borderland between waking and sleep she listened — really listened, the way only she could — to the small warm thing that had fallen so far and landed, at last, in the one hearth in the world that had never stopped being swept.

And that was when she heard it. Beneath the humming. Faint as a heartbeat under a floorboard, coming not from the fire but from everywhere — from the hills, the stream, the sleeping garden, the marrow of the valley itself:

Other voices. Tiny ones. Scattered ones. Sparks, fallen all across the world when the Chorus came apart, lying dormant in stones and wells and hearths, waiting — all this time waiting — for someone who could speak to them.

Waiting for someone to invite them home.

Wren opened her eyes. The fire flickered, attentive, like a dog that has heard its master's footsteps at the end of a long road.

"Jonah," she whispered. "There are more of them."
