## 1. The Quiet Trade

The knife knew the bone better than I did. That was the trick of it—let the blade lead, let the grain of the femur tell you where the curve wanted to go, and never, ever listen to what came out of your own mouth while you worked. In Verrance, a carver who murmured to her materials was a witch. A carver who hummed was merely eccentric.

I hummed.

The morning light came through the shutters in slats, striping my workbench gold and gray, and the little charm under my knife—a knuckle-bone for a fisherman's wife who'd lost two sons to the winter sea—was nearly finished. I'd carved a spiral into its face, shallow as a fingerprint, and around the spiral a ring of notches that meant nothing to anyone but me. *Come home safe,* the notches said. *Or if you can't come home, don't be cold.*

The charm wouldn't do anything. That was the point. It was bone and patience and a fair price, and when Mrs. Ovel held it in her chapped hands she would feel comforted, because people feel comforted by things made carefully, and that was the whole honest trade.

The other trade happened after dark.

* * *

By night, the dead of Verrance found their way to the narrow room above my stall, drawn up through the floorboards like damp. They didn't rattle chains or moan at windows—the city's ghosts were mostly tired, mostly polite, mostly grateful. They came because Grandmother had taught me the listening, before the fever took her, and the listening was a kind of door. You didn't open it so much as stand beside it and let them lean on the frame.

The first ghost tonight was a lamplighter named Corren, dead eleven years, who only wanted someone to know he'd never once dropped a flame onto a rooftop, no matter what his old crew chief said. He told me the story three times. I told him each time that it mattered, that skill like that deserved remembering, and each time he brightened a little more, like coaxing a coal.

"You're the only one," he said, drifting toward the ceiling where the moonlight pooled. "Twelve years you've been here, girl, and you're still the only one who hears us."

"I'm nobody," I said, which was the litany, which was survival. Nobody heard ghosts. Nobody carved whispers into charms. Nobody existed, officially, in the register of the city wardens.

"Nobody," Corren repeated fondly. "You carve like somebody."

He faded with the deepening night, satisfied, and the next one came—a child, this time, small and shy of the candlelight, asking me to pass a message to her mother on Tanner's Row: *the blue shawl is under the loose stair board, Mama, I hid it there so no one would take it.* Messages like that were the dangerous part of the work. I delivered them wrapped in ordinary words, left on doorsteps, tucked into market baskets, deniable. But every note was a thread, and threads pull taut.

Because three weeks ago, the criers had come through the Ringmarket with the new edict nailed to every post: *By order of King Aldric, all who traffic with the unquiet dead, who hear the voices of the departed, who practice the listening arts—these are enemies of the crown. Let them be sought, tried, and burned.*

Burned. The word sat in the edict plain as a butcher's mark, no velvet on it at all. And beneath the proclamation, the seal of a king whom no one had seen smile in six years—not since the plague winter, not since whatever had hollowed him out behind those grey eyes. The city whispered that grief had made him cruel. The city whispered a lot of things. I kept my head down and my hands busy and my ears, most days, firmly closed.

Most days.

* * *

She came at dusk, near closing, when the Ringmarket shadows went long and blue. I knew her by her walk before her face—a slow, deliberate shuffle, the walk of a woman rationing her last strength. Old Maren, who sold dried lavender two stalls down from where Tam the tinker clattered at his copper pots. Maren had bought charms from me for years, always the harmless kind, always with the same joke: *"For luck, Lena. Not that luck ever listened."*

Today she didn't joke. She set a folded cloth on my counter and unwrapped it, and inside lay a whistle carved from yellowed bone, worn smooth as riverstone, threaded on a leather cord gone black with age.

"My grandmother's," Maren said. "And hers before that." She pushed it across the counter. "It's yours now."

"Maren, I can't take—"

"You can. You will." Her voice dropped, and something in it made me go still. "You'll want it, girl, when the trouble comes. It hums. Did you know that? Only ever hums at one name."

My mouth had gone dry. "What name?"

"Say it and see." Her eyes, milky at the edges, fixed on mine with terrible gentleness. "Go on."

I glanced down the market row—Tam bent over his soldering, the fishwives arguing over eels, a pair of crown wardens loitering by the well with their new white armbands—and then I lifted the whistle to my lips, though I hadn't blown, hadn't intended anything, just held it there. And as my breath crossed the cold bone, I said, barely aloud:

"Aldric?"

The whistle hummed. Low, sweet, wrong—a note that seemed to rise up out of the bone itself rather than out of my breath, a note that vibrated in my back teeth and in the floorboards and somewhere, impossibly, far away, in the direction of the palace on its hill. It lasted three heartbeats. Then silence rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

Maren nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a debt. "Six years since it started humming. Six years, and my grandmother told me before her: *when it sings, find the listener. There's always a listener. The gift skips and hides, but it doesn't die.*" She laid her hand over mine on the counter, and her skin was paper and ice. "You're the listener, aren't you, Lena Joubert."

It wasn't a question, and lying to a dying woman felt like a sin too heavy even for me. So I did the next best thing. I said nothing, and my silence confessed everything.

"Listen to me." Her grip tightened. "That whistle belonged to women who served kings. Court listeners, once—before the burning times, before everyone forgot why the crown ever needed them. It knows the ember. Do you understand? It *knows the ember*, and it's been humming at that palace for six years because something in that palace is calling for help." She coughed, wet and long, and when she straightened, her face had gone the color of tallow. "The king is not well, girl. And I don't mean sick."

"Everyone says grief changed him."

"Grief doesn't make a man's shadow flicker like a candle in draft." She released my hand and stepped back, gathering her shawl. "Take the whistle. Keep carving. Keep quiet. But when the humming gets louder—" she smiled then, tired and knowing, "—you follow it."

She died four days later, in her sleep, in her room above the lavender stall. I stood at her funeral in the rain with forty other sellers from the Ringmarket, the whistle hidden against my breastbone under my coat, warm as a swallowed coal.

Tam stood beside me, big hands folded, watching the gravediggers with sad eyes. He couldn't hear the eulogy—he never could—but he read the priest's lips and shook his head at the platitudes, and when we walked home together through the wet streets he tapped my shoulder twice, our old signal, and signed: *She knew about you?*

"She knew," I signed back.

*Trouble,* he signed, and frowned.

"No," I lied, and squeezed his arm. "Just an old woman's fancy."

But that night, alone in my room above the stall, I took out the whistle and turned it in the candlelight, and it hummed—faintly, without being touched, without any name spoken at all. Hummed toward the hill. Toward the palace. Toward the king.

And beneath the hum, so faint I might have dreamed it, I heard something else. A voice. Thin and frayed and desperate, threading itself through the bone like smoke through a keyhole.

*Please. Whoever hears me—please. I am still in here. I am still—*

The candle guttered. The voice cut off.

I sat very still for a long time, the whistle cold against my palms, twelve years of careful silence pressing down on me like snowfall on a roof.

Then, three streets away, I heard the wardens' boots.
