## 2. Ashes on the Register

The bell above the market gate rang wrong that morning.

Lena heard it before she saw the reason—a flat, cracked note, like a coin bitten by a counterfeiter—and her hands went still over the half-carved sparrow in her lap. Bone always told the truth. So did bells, if you knew how to listen. This one was announcing something other than the hour.

She looked up from her stall. The morning crowd of Verrance's Saltmarket moved the way crowds always did: merchants shouting prices, fishwives arguing over eels, children darting between cart wheels with stolen apples. Ordinary noise, ordinary life. But threaded through it now, thin as wire, came the sound of boots. Too many boots, moving in step.

Witch-hunters wore ash-grey cloaks and carried long tongs for handling what they caught. Lena had seen them twice since the edict was nailed to every church door—*Let none hear the dead; let none speak with spirits; let such souls be named upon the register*—and both times she had become suddenly fascinated by her own fingernails, by the grain of her own table, by anything but them.

Today they came down the row like a slow tide, pausing at each stall, unrolling a ledger. The register. Names, trades, sins. A woman ahead of her was weeping quietly while a hunter wrote something down.

Lena's fingers found the bone sparrow. She could feel the ghost inside it—not a dead person's ghost, just an echo, a warmth she'd pressed into the carving the way her grandmother had taught her—but she kept her face empty. Empty faces survived. She had learned that in twelve years of being careful.

"Name?" The hunter who reached her smelled of tallow and cold iron. He didn't look up from his ledger.

"Lena Joubert."

"Trade?"

"Charm-carver. Trinkets only. Birds, fish, luck-knots." She turned the sparrow so he could see it: a pretty nothing, wings folded, eyes blank. "Souvenirs."

His pen scratched. He moved on.

She let out a breath she'd been holding since midwinter.

It was almost noon when Mrs. Corvel came to buy a charm against cradle-death—the old fear, though there had been no baby in Corvel's house for thirty years. She was a broad, respectable woman with a voice like wet gravel, and Lena carved for her every season because Mrs. Corvel paid well and asked no questions. Or so Lena had believed.

"The usual," Mrs. Corvel said, not meeting her eyes. Her hands were trembling.

Lena chose a smooth piece of river-bone and began to shape it, letting her gift sink into the grain the way she always did—just a whisper, just enough warmth to make it *hold*, to make it work. The bone hummed faintly against her thumb. To anyone else it was silent. To Lena it said, in its wordless way, *safe, safe, safe*.

Mrs. Corvel flinched.

That was the first sign. The second was how quickly she paid, coins pressed into Lena's palm without counting, and how she walked away too fast, shoulders hunched like a woman carrying something heavy and shameful.

Lena watched her go straight to the hunters' tent at the end of the row.

She didn't run. Running was death. Instead she sat very still and thought about Tam.

Tam worked two stalls down, a deaf tinker with quick clever hands and a smile that made even the fishwives soften. He couldn't hear the ghosts, couldn't hear anything at all, and he was the safest person Lena had ever known—the only one who'd never once asked why she sometimes paused mid-sentence, head tilted, listening to a silence no one else could feel. When customers grew curious about her carvings, Tam would appear beside her with a broken kettle and an enormous grin, all elbows and pantomime, and steer the conversation somewhere harmless. Last winter, when fever took his sister, Lena had carved him a small bone bell that held the sound of his sister's laugh. He kept it on a cord around his neck and touched it when he missed her. He never asked how it worked. He only ever signed *thank you* with both hands, slow and solemn, like laying flowers.

The hunters were walking toward him now.

"No," Lena breathed. "No, no—"

Tam saw them coming. Of course he did—he read the world through his skin, through vibration, through the way shadows fell. He looked at Lena across three stalls of market noise, and he did not look afraid. He looked at her the way you look at someone you're saying goodbye to, and then he deliberately, slowly, picked up his mallet and went back to work, putting his body between the hunters and her stall.

They burned everything anyway. Both stalls. The hunters were thorough that way—edict said the tools of sorcery must be cleansed by fire, and apparently a deaf man's hammers counted. Lena stood in the press of shouting onlookers and watched eleven years of careful, quiet work curl into black smoke: the sparrows, the luck-knots, the little boats for sailors' wives. Beside them, Tam's kettles and clocks went up too, and Tam himself was dragged away in irons, signing desperately to the crowd—not *help me*, Lena realized. He was signing *go, go, get out*.

To her.

She ran. It wasn't brave. It was the only thing left to do.

* * *

They found her before dark, of course. A hunter's net, a hood, a cart that smelled of pig-muck and fear. They took her not to any common jail but upward—to the palace itself, its white towers needling a bruised sunset sky—and down again, down into dungeons cut from living rock where the air tasted of salt and old iron.

They put her in a chair in a round stone room lit by a single brazier. Two guards. One clerk. One witch-hunter with a face like a closed door, who opened the register across the table and said, "Lena Joubert. Charm-carver. Denounced by one Maren Corvel for the practice of spirit-whispering. Is this true?"

"I carve trinkets," Lena said.

The hunter turned the register toward her. On the page, under her name, someone had written a single line in a fine, precise hand: *Confirmed. The bones speak to her.*

"That's not my writing."

"It's His Majesty's," the hunter said. "The king inspects the register personally these days. He marked you himself."

Lena stared at the line. The handwriting was beautiful—slanted, elegant, the hand of a scholar—and it trembled, very slightly, at the end of the last word. As if the writer's hand had been shaking. As if the writer had been fighting something.

"You'll confess," the hunter continued, "or you'll burn. Simple as arithmetic. We can do it tonight or—"

The guards laughed at something. Lena never learned what—one of them had elbowed the other and snickered about a girl in a tavern—and the laughter filled the round room, bouncing off the stone.

And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat under a floor, she heard a second sound.

A voice. Male, worn, cultured—the voice of a man who had spent his life giving orders and had forgotten how to ask for anything. It came from nowhere. From everywhere. From the brazier, maybe, or from the stone itself.

*Please.*

Lena's whole body went cold.

*Please. If anyone can hear me. Please.*

It was pleading. It was ragged and exhausted and so quiet it barely existed, and it was layered beneath the guards' laughter like a drowning man beneath ice.

*I know you're there,* the voice said. *I can smell the listening. Girl—the charm-carver—they wrote your name in my hand because I wrote it, I held the pen, I—* A sound like a sob strangled at birth. *I am still in here. Whatever wears my crown, whatever signs my orders—I am still in here, and I have been screaming for— I don't know how long. Years. Do you understand? The king is begging you.*

One of the guards glanced at her. "Something funny, witch?"

Lena arranged her face into frightened blankness, which required no effort at all. Inside, her mind was racing, tumbling, catching on the impossible thing and turning it over and over. The king inspected the register. The king marked her name. And the king—the *real* king—was buried alive inside his own body, calling out through stone and fire to the one person in Verrance who might possibly hear him.

The hunter-king. The hunted king.

*Please,* said the voice again, thinner now, fraying. *I felt you hear me. Don't pretend you didn't. They will kill you either way—burning or worse—so listen to me, girl. There is a servant, Wren, grey livery, third door past the kitchens. Tell her the ember sings at night. She will know. She has been waiting eleven years for someone who can hear it.*

"Why would I trust you?" Lena whispered, so softly it was only breath.

Because the voice answered anyway, as if breath were enough, as if her listening alone were a doorway:

*Because I am the only other person in this palace who is also going to die here. And because—" the voice cracked, and steadied, and became terribly gentle— "*because whatever happens to me, save yourself first. Promise me that much. You remind me of someone I failed."

The brazier flared. In its light, for half a heartbeat, Lena saw a shadow fall across the wall that belonged to no one in the room—tall, crowned, its edges flickering like coals—and then it was gone.

The witch-hunter closed the register. "Tomorrow," he said. "Confess or burn."

As they marched her back to her cell, chains whispering, Lena passed a torch mounted on the dungeon wall, and the flame leaned toward her—not blown by any draft, but leaning, deliberate, attentive, the way a dog leans toward a hand it knows.

And in the crackle of the burning pitch she heard it, soft and hungry and patient, humming a tune she almost recognized:

*a name, a name, sung low and sweet—*

Her grandmother's warning surfaced from twelve years down: *Never answer a fire that knows your name, child.*

Lena walked faster. Somewhere above her, a king was screaming behind his own eyes, and a servant named Wren was waiting by a grey door, and Tam was in a cell no better than hers because he'd tried to protect a secret that had never been hers alone.

Fear was still there, cold in her stomach where fear lived.

But beneath it, kindling, something new had caught and begun to burn.

Resolve.

* * *

They left her alone that night. That was their mistake.

In the dark of her cell, Lena drew the bone whistle—the dying woman's payment—from inside her sleeve, where the hunters had somehow never thought to search. She held it to her lips but didn't blow. She only spoke the king's name into its hollow, the way the old woman had shown her with her last hoarse breath.

*Aldric.*

Deep in the bone, something stirred and began to hum.

And far above her, through floors of rock and mortar and gold, something else hummed back.
