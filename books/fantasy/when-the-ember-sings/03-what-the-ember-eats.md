## 3. What the Ember Eats

They brought her up through the palace's spine—a servant's stair so narrow the walls brushed both shoulders, lit by tallow stubs that guttered when she passed. The woman leading her wore a kitchen apron over chainmail and moved like water finding its way downhill. She had not given a name. She had only looked at Lena's bound wrists, at the bone whistle hidden in Lena's sleeve—the guards had missed it, or pretended to—and said, *You heard him. So did I. Come.*

The king's chambers were behind three doors, and each door had fewer guards than it should have. That was the first wrongness. The second was the smell: warm ash and something sweeter beneath it, like sugar left too long in a fire.

The third was King Aldric himself.

He stood at the window with his back to her, and even from across the room Lena could hear it—that voice under his voice, the pleading one she'd caught in the dungeons, worn down now to a threadbare whisper. *Please. Please. Please.* A king begging in his own skull, and no one the wiser.

"Close the door," Aldric said without turning. His voice was gravel and command. Then, softer—softer than any king should speak—"I'm sorry. For the fright you'll have. It's easier if you don't run."

Lena did not run. Twelve years of practice held her feet to the floor. But when he turned, she saw what lived behind his eyes, and every ghost she'd ever listened to went silent at once, as if even the dead were holding their breath.

His eyes burned. Not metaphorically. Deep in each iris a coal-colored light pulsed, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, and where the light touched, she could see the edges of him fraying—the fine gold stitching of memory unraveling from a man who had once been whole.

"You're dying," she said.

"I'm being *eaten*," the king answered—and then his face changed, the jaw slackening, the shoulders dropping, and the voice that came out was younger, rawer, cracked with exhaustion. "It eats what I am. Twenty years of it. My mother's face. My wedding day. The name of my first horse." He laughed, and there was nothing kingly in it. "I keep lists now. Hidden. Things I refuse to forget. But the lists are getting shorter."

The ember-spirit surfaced again like a whale coming up for air, and Aldric's posture reassembled itself into majesty. "You carve bone," it said. "You listen. The dead tell you their stories, and stories are just memories wearing different clothes." The coal-eyes brightened with something like appetite. "You smell delicious."

Lena's mouth went dry, but her hands—her hands were steady. They always were when she worked. "Then why haven't you taken mine?"

"Because stolen memories sour," the spirit said, and for a moment genuine irritation flickered across the king's borrowed face. "A memory ripped away turns to ash on the tongue. I have gorged myself on this kingdom's fear for twenty years—every witch burned, every charm smashed, every screaming confession. Ash, all of it. Ash keeps me alive the way salt water keeps a sailor alive." The light in its eyes dimmed, and beneath the dimming Lena glimpsed something shocking: hunger that had curdled into longing. "But a memory *given*. Willingly. Freely. That is a meal. That is a feast. And this fool"—the king's hand struck his own chest—"won't give me a single one. He holds on with teeth and nails. Stubborn little candle of a man."

"He's still in there," Lena said.

"He is the wick," the spirit agreed. "I am the flame. And when the wick is gone—" It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was a terrible smile. "I find another. There is always another crown."

* * *

They met in the undercroft below the royal chapel, seven of them around a table scarred by two hundred years of careless elbows. Maren the cook who had smuggled Lena up; Brother Ede, a defrocked priest who'd been exiled for preaching that ghosts deserved last rites; Sefa, a night-soil collector who heard voices in wells; old Wren, blind since birth, who saw more than anyone; Tam.

Tam sat beside Lena, his hands still trembling. They'd pulled him from his cell that morning, and the first thing he'd done was sign to her, rapid and fierce: *Never doubted. Never.* Then, gentler: *Your stall. I'm sorry about your stall.*

She signed back: *Stalls can be rebuilt. You can't.* And then, because some things needed saying aloud even if he couldn't hear them: "I'm glad you're here."

Brother Ede laid out the theory they'd stitched together from forbidden texts and Wren's visions and Sefa's well-whispers. "It feeds on fear-taken memories. Given ones, it cannot digest properly—they pass through it like stones. Enough willing offerings, and we don't kill it. We starve it into dormancy. Sleep. The kind it slept before someone woke it and fed it a kingdom."

"And the cost?" Lena asked, though she already knew. Her charms whispered truths whether she wanted them or not.

Ede wouldn't meet her eyes. Wren did, her milky gaze unnervingly precise. "Each giver loses what they offer. Entirely. As if it never happened." She turned her sightless eyes toward the ceiling. "And it must be something precious, or it won't take hold. Cheap memories slide off. It wants the warm things. The load-bearing walls of a life."

Silence around the table. Sefa spoke first, her voice steady as her hands were not. "My daughter died eight years ago. The well-voices helped me survive it. I'll give the memory of her funeral—not her face, never her face—but the day I buried her. The worst day. Let it feed the fire that ate my girl's name from the town records." She looked up, daring anyone to call it small. "Some memories are worth losing twice."

Maren wiped her hands on her apron. "The bread I baked the morning my sons left for the war. They never came back. I've baked it every year since. I'll give the baking. Keep the boys." Her chin lifted. "If the price is grief, I've paid worse for less."

Tam signed something long and deliberate, and Lena translated, her throat tight: "The day he lost his hearing. Fever, age nine. He says—he says silence took everything from him, so let silence pay something back."

One by one they offered. Not eagerly. Not bravely, either—there was nothing brave in the way Wren described giving up her mother's lullaby, or how Ede's hand shook as he named the ordination day that had defined his whole ruined life. They offered the way people pay ransom: because the alternative was worse.

Lena watched them and understood, with a cold clarity, what the ember had meant. *Willingly given.* It couldn't be coerced, couldn't be tricked. Love had to be real or it wasn't food at all.

That night, alone in a narrow guest cell that smelled of beeswax and stone, Lena unwrapped the bone whistle and held it to her ear. It hummed faintly, the way it had since the dying woman pressed it into her hands—*when the king's name is spoken*, the old woman had wheezed, *it sings. It sang for my mother too. We were waiting for the listener.*

Lena thought of her grandmother's kitchen. Woodsmoke and rosemary. Small hands guided over a carving knife. *Listen with your fingers, little bird. The bone remembers what people forget.*

Twelve years of those evenings. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands.

She pressed the whistle to her lips but did not blow. Not yet. First they would try the others' offerings, and pray it was enough.

In the dark, very softly, the whistle hummed a note she'd never heard it make before—low, urgent, almost pleading—and Lena felt the hair rise on her arms, because she recognized the tune.

It was her grandmother's lullaby.

The one she had never told anyone.

The bone knew. Something in the palace knew. And tomorrow, when the circle gathered to feed the fire their most precious things, Lena understood with terrible certainty whose offering the ember was truly waiting for.

Her hands began to shake.
