## 4. The Last Warm Thing

The throne room at midnight smelled of cold ash and old incense, and the ember-spirit wore the king like a borrowed coat that no longer fit.

It sat on the throne with Aldric's body slumped sideways, chin on chest, and let the shape of him sag while it looked at us through eyes gone the color of banked coals. Around me stood my strange, impossible family: Tam with his ink-stained fingers and his deaf, stubborn silence; Maren the gravedigger who heard her own dead son in every rainfall; Old Sefa, who had once fed a memory of her wedding day to the thing to buy us an hour; and Joss, the hunter's son who had turned his father's ledgers over to us and not slept since.

The circle was drawn. The bone charms hummed. And I was out of time.

"It knows what we're doing," Maren whispered. "Sefa's memory bought us nothing but its attention."

"Then we stop feeding it scraps," I said, though my hands were shaking. "We give it everything."

* * *

We had learned the truth in the king's own chambers three nights past, when Aldric—Aldric himself, the drowned remnant beneath the fire—had pressed a shaking hand against mine through the bars of his possession and mouthed two words: *starve it.*

Willing memories were poison to the ember. That much the bone whistle had sung true, humming against my palm whenever I spoke the king's name, guiding us like a compass needle toward the one wound the spirit could not cauterize. But unwilling memories—memories taken by force, as the ember took them from Aldric year after year—those only made it fatter. It had grown strong on theft. Only gift could kill it.

So we offered.

Maren gave the sound of her son's first laugh, and wept without sound for a full hour after, because she could no longer remember what she'd given away, only that something warm was missing from her chest. Joss surrendered his memory of his father teaching him to ride, so that the man he'd betrayed became a stranger in his own history. Tam—dear, fierce Tam—pressed both hands flat against the circle's edge and gave up the face of his mother. He couldn't hear her voice anyway, he signed afterward, bitter and brave. He hadn't thought losing her face would hurt this much.

Each offering struck the ember like a stone dropped into a furnace. It flared, shrieked through Aldric's throat in a voice like splitting timber, and dimmed—and then it clung harder to its host, digging into what remained of him, hoarding.

"He'll be empty before it starves," Old Sefa said, watching the king twitch on his cot. "There's barely anything left in there. Look at him. It's eating the walls of the house now."

She was right. Aldric had stopped speaking days ago. His eyes, when they opened, didn't seem to find our faces. Whatever the ember left behind, it would be a man-shaped absence, breathing out of habit.

Unless someone gave it the one thing it couldn't digest fast enough—the whole of something, all at once, willingly, completely. A feast so large it choked.

I knew what I had. I had known since the first night.

I just hadn't been able to say it aloud.

* * *

Now, in the throne room, with the circle burning pale gold around us and the ember watching, I knelt at the center and set down my carving knife, my bag of charms, and the little bone whistle that had started everything.

"Lena." Tam caught my wrist. He couldn't shout, so he shook me instead, signing fast with his free hand: *No. Not you too. We've lost enough.*

"I'm the only one who has enough," I said. "You all gave pieces. I have twelve years. She taught me every song, every charm, every way of listening to the dead. Twelve years of her voice, her hands, her kitchen smelling of rosemary and smoke. That's not a scrap. That's a banquet." My throat closed. "And it's *mine*. Freely given. That's the whole point, isn't it? It can't take what I refuse to clutch."

He signed something else, slower. *Will you remember that you loved her?*

"No," I said. "That's the cost."

His jaw worked. Then he did the last thing I expected: he kissed my forehead, the way family does, and stepped back into the circle's edge, and raised both fists—not in protest, but in salute.

Old Sefa began the binding-song, low and cracked. Maren joined. Joss, tone-deaf and undeterred, joined anyway. Tam kept rhythm by striking his palm against the floor, a heartbeat for those of us who needed one.

I closed my eyes and reached for my grandmother.

She came easily, the way she always did. Marta Joubert, with flour on her knuckles and a bone needle behind her ear, standing in the doorway of a kitchen twelve years and a lifetime away. *Listen with your ribs, child, not your ears,* she was saying. *Bones remember what minds forget.* She was teaching me my first charm. She was scolding me for carving on the good table. She was dying, and holding my hand, and telling me not to be afraid of the quiet, because the quiet was full of everyone who had ever loved me.

I gathered it all—all of it, every year, every hour—and I held it up in my mind like a lantern, and I opened my hands.

*I give this freely. Take it. Choke on it.*

The ember came for the offering like a starving dog.

I felt it rip through me—not pain, exactly, but subtraction. Rooms closing one by one inside my head. Rosemary smoke: gone. Her laugh: gone. The shape of her hands: going, going. I sang as it devoured, because singing was how we bound things in my grandmother's line, and even as I lost the reason for the song I kept singing it, note after note, the melody living somewhere deeper than memory, in the marrow, in the bones that remember what minds forget.

The ember swelled. Flared white. Screamed—a sound that cracked the throne room windows and sent soot cascading down like black snow—and then, gorged past bearing on twelve years of willing love, it collapsed inward, folding smaller and smaller and smaller until it was nothing but a coal, a spark, an ember in truth, sinking down through Aldric's chest into some deep cellar of him where, at last, it slept.

The king fell forward off the throne.

Tam caught him before his head hit the stone.

* * *

Aldric woke at dawn, diminished and human and weeping without knowing why.

He remembered almost nothing of the nine years the spirit had ridden him. He remembered his mother's face and not his father's. He remembered being a boy in the eastern provinces, before the crown, before any of it. He looked at the four of us standing over him—gravedigger, tinker, turncoat, hag—and then at me, and asked, "Did I burn them? Tell me plainly. Did I burn them?"

"Some," I said. "Not all. And you begged for help, near the end. However empty the rest of you got, that part never went out."

He stared at the ceiling for a long time. "Then I owe a debt the treasury can't pay," he said finally. "Tell me what you want. Anything."

What I wanted was my grandmother, and no decree could carve her back into me. So I asked for something else instead.

When the court assembled that afternoon—shocked, whispering, half-convinced their king had risen from death or madness—I stood before the throne with Tam on my left and Maren, Sefa, and Joss on my right, and King Aldric, hollow-eyed and steady-voiced, declared before God and Verrance that these five were kin: one household, one blood, bound by choice and witnessed by law. Tam's hand gripped my shoulder hard enough to bruise. Maren cried. Joss pretended he didn't. Sefa cackled and told the assembled nobles they should be so lucky as to have family this good.

The edict was torn up before sundown. The witch-hunters' pyres went cold.

* * *

That evening I sat alone in my rebuilt stall—it was Tam's rebuilding, really; he'd worked all day without stopping—and I turned a fresh piece of bone in my fingers.

I still reached for her. That was the cruelest arithmetic of grief: knowing there had been a grandmother, a kitchen, twelve years, and finding only smooth blankness where it all had been. The others had given hours. I had given a childhood.

But my hands remembered. When I picked up the knife, they moved with a surety I hadn't taught myself, cutting a spiral, then leaves, then a pattern I didn't know and recognized all the same—hers, hers, hers. The knowledge lived below the loss, like roots under winter ground.

I carved a charm unlike any I'd ever sold. No whisper in it. No ghost, no name, no memory. Just an open spiral, a door left unlatched. A place where new things could settle and stay.

I hung it around my neck, and it held nothing but hope, and hope was enough to sleep on.

Near midnight, a knock at the stall's shutter. Tam stood outside in the lamplight, snow gathering on his shoulders, holding up two cups of mulled wine and wearing the expression of a brother who refuses to let his sister grieve alone. Behind him, faintly, Maren's laugh, Sefa's cackle, the warm noise of a house full of people who had chosen each other.

I flipped the sign on my stall to *closed*, and went home to my family.

And far beneath the palace, in the dark cellar of a sleeping king, something ancient dreamed—and for the first time in nine years, the dream was not hungry.
