## 1. The Sixth Dish

The stew arrived in a silver tureen, and Mirabel Villalobos knew before the lid came off.

Not by sight — though the steam did curl strangely, catching the candlelight with an oily sheen that honest broth never wore. Not even by smell, not yet. It was something older than either of those senses, something that lived in the back of her throat like a second tongue: a faint metallic whisper, the ghost of a flavor she had spent six years learning to read the way scholars read dead languages. *Bitterroot and ash-of-iron,* she thought, ladling a portion into Lord Aldery's bowl with hands that did not tremble. *A quarter grain, maybe less. Enough to stop a heart by morning, disguised under venison and winter turnips.*

She had tasted four hundred dishes in this court. This was the sixth poisoned one, and the first meant for someone beneath her notice — which was, in its own quiet way, the most alarming thing about it.

Lord Aldery Vess of the Marchlands was a minor lord in every sense that mattered. Minor lands, minor revenues, minor ambitions. He sat three seats down from the salt on the usurper's long winter table, close enough to be seen, far enough to be forgotten. He had done nothing, as far as Mirabel knew, except exist in the wrong place at the wrong time while wearing the wrong face — a face that some said still carried a trace of the old dynasty's look about the jaw.

*Ah,* Mirabel thought, and lifted her own spoon.

The hall of Winterhold roared around her. Forty nobles at table, their voices bouncing off vaulted stone hung with the usurper's banners — black and gold, the crossed hammers of House Corvane, hammered into every surface like nails into a coffin lid. Braziers fought the December cold and lost. The wine was watered, the music was indifferent, and everyone smiled the way people smile when they are being watched, which in this hall they always were.

Mirabel put the spoonful in her mouth and held it there.

Venison, browned in butter. Turnip. A thread of rosemary. And underneath, delicate as a spider's leg across the palate: bitterroot, cut with ash-of-iron to blunt its telltale bitterness, finished with something floral — larkspur, perhaps, or its cheaper cousin — to give the death a gentle entrance. It was elegant work. It was *expensive* work. No kitchen poisoner in the city worked this cleanly; the guild alchemists mixed for the crown alone, and they charged accordingly.

She swallowed. The poison slid down her throat and settled into her blood like a guest arriving at a house prepared for it, finding every door already barred, every window shuttered. Her body had been built against this, dish by dish, year by year — small doses taken deliberately since she was nine years old, when her mother had first pressed a crumb of bitterroot between her lips and said, *This will hurt you a little now so it cannot kill you later.* Six years in Corvane's service had refined the architecture of her survival to something approaching art. She could take enough larkspur to fell a horse and feel only a pleasant warmth behind the eyes.

What she could not survive was being *known*.

So she did what she had rehearsed in the dark of her narrow room above the kitchens, the way other women rehearsed dances. She let the spoon waver. She set it down — not too carefully; carelessness was the whole trick — and pressed two fingers to her lips as though tasting something she couldn't name. Then she stood, swayed once, beautifully, and caught the edge of the table.

"My lord." Her voice came out thin, pitched just above the din, and the din began to die around her the way it always did, curiosity strangling conversation seat by seat. "Forgive me. I—"

She retched. Dry, theatrical, convincing — she had learned from watching actual poisonings, the way the body folds itself around its own betrayal. Several lords recoiled. Lady Sarelle of the river provinces made a sound of disgust and drew her skirts aside as if illness were contagious through silk.

"Water," Mirabel whispered, and then, because the moment demanded it, because a taster who collapsed silently was merely sick but a taster who *named* her killer was valuable: "There is something in the stew."

* * *

They cleared the hall within the hour.

It was done efficiently, without panic, which told Mirabel more than any confession could have. Guards moved through the ranks of guests with the unhurried certainty of men following orders written before dinner began. The tureen was carried out under cloth. Lord Aldery was escorted — gently, almost tenderly — to "guest quarters," which everyone understood to mean a locked room, and which Lord Aldery himself seemed to understand too, if the grayness of his face meant anything. Two of the under-stewards were taken as well. One of them wept. The other looked, Mirabel thought, less frightened than resigned, and she filed that away with the rest.

She herself was given a chair by the fire in the steward's anteroom, a blanket, and a cup of hot wine that she pretended to sip. Word of the taster who had saved a lord's life would travel the court by morning, as such words always did, burnishing her value, deepening her usefulness. That was the currency she traded in: indispensability worn lightly, luck worn heavily. *Lucky Mirabel*, the servants called her, half-affectionate, half-superstitious. She had survived six years on the strength of that word. *Luck* explained everything and revealed nothing.

Except that tonight, for the first time, she wasn't sure luck would hold.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not stop performing, sitting in the firelight with her untouched wine: six poisoned dishes in six years, and every one of them different. A rival's envy. A merchant's grudge. A spurned lover's spite. Ordinary poisons, ordinary motives, the ordinary weather of court life. But this — this was crown work. Crown alchemists, crown budget, crown signature. And the target had been Lord Aldery Vess, who owned nothing worth poisoning him for.

Which left only one explanation, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone:

The crown was testing its own table. Seeding its own dishes. Watching who sickened, who died, and — most importantly — who *didn't*.

An immunity was a secret you could keep for years behind a mask of luck. But you could not keep it against a hundred seeded meals, against a systematic sweep of the court, against hunters who understood exactly what they were looking for. Sooner or later, the sieve would catch everyone whose body refused to die on schedule. And the usurper — clever, patient Corvane, who had taken a throne in blood and held it for eight years by knowing where every body was buried, including several he'd buried himself — would want those people found. An immune servant was useful. An immune stranger was a threat. Either way, they would be registered, catalogued, owned.

Or worse. There were rumors already, whispers in the servants' corridors that Mirabel had dismissed as kitchen gossip: that the crown was collecting gifts the way misers collected coin. Tasters, mages, hedge-witches, anyone whose flesh broke the ordinary rules. That somewhere below Winterhold there were rooms where such people were studied. That no one sent down came back up.

She had told herself the rumors were the usual court rot. Tonight she stopped telling herself that.

* * *

Near midnight, the anteroom door opened, and Master Corven Hale came in.

He was the Undermaster of the Household, her handler, the man who had placed her in this position six years ago with a handshake and a warning: *Be lucky, girl, and be nothing else.* He was sixty, dry as old rope, with a face designed by nature to be forgotten and a memory that missed nothing. He carried two cups of mulled wine and set one beside her untouched cup, which was either kindness or a test, and with Hale it was impossible to say which.

"You'll be rewarded," he said, settling into the opposite chair. "The Lord Protector is pleased. Aldery Vess lives, and Vess has cousins in the Marchlands who remember things like this."

"I'm glad to be of use." Her voice was steady. Six years of practice.

"Mm." Hale looked into his wine. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, the great hall was being scrubbed of the evening, the tureen's silver scoured, the story already hardening into legend: the loyal taster, struck down by her devotion, saved by her constitution. "Strange thing, though."

"Is it?"

"Six dishes in six years, and you've never truly sickened. Ill, yes. Frightened, certainly. But you've never once needed the physician." He said it mildly, the way a man remarks on weather. "Tonight I watched you vomit nothing. Dry as a sermon. Very convincing." He raised his eyes to hers at last, and they were not unkind, which was somehow the worst thing about them. "I've known for a long time, Mirabel. Since the third year. The quail at midsummer, do you remember? Larkspur in the glaze. You tasted it and your pupils never changed, and a body that takes poison shows it in the eyes before anywhere else. I've been waiting ever since to see what you'd do with the knowledge that I know."

The fire popped. Mirabel discovered that her heart was very loud and her face, thanks to six years of practice, was very quiet.

"Why wait?" she asked.

"Because you were careful. Because you were good at your work. And because—" he paused, choosing, and she realized with a cold sliding feeling that whatever he chose to say next had been chosen many times over in private, "—because until this month, being immune was simply rare. As of this week, it is contraband." He leaned forward. "There is to be a proclamation. Day after tomorrow, in the great square. Every soul in the Protector's service will swear a census oath — name, birth, and gift, if any. Refusal is confession. Confession is..." He waved a hand, delicately. "Downstairs."

Mirabel looked at the fire and thought, absurdly, of her mother's kitchen, of the crumb of bitterroot, of the long slow construction of a body that could not be killed by the ordinary means — all of it built toward survival, and now survival itself was the crime.

"You're telling me this," she said slowly, "why?"

Hale stood, straightening his coat, and for a moment the dry old rope of him looked tired all the way through.

"Because twenty-two years ago you pulled my brother out of a flooded mill-pond when you were a child yourself, and I have owed your family a debt since before you could write your name." He walked to the door, then paused with his hand on the latch. "The roads east of the city are watched after dawn. The mountain passes are watched badly. And the Thornfell caravans leave from the tannery gate at moonrise, if a person were foolish enough to ask after them." He did not turn around. "One night, Mirabel. That's what I can buy you. Spend it well."

The door closed behind him.

Mirabel sat alone by the fire with two cups of cooling wine, listening to the wind come off the mountains — the same wind, she thought, that was even now moving through the high passes of Thornfell, over roads she had never walked, toward a life she had not yet begun to lose.

One night. She had one night to become no one.

She drank both cups.
