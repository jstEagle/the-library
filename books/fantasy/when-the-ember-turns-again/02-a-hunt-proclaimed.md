## 2. A Hunt Proclaimed

The proclamation was read from the Marble Stair on the morning of the Feast of Ashes, and Mirabel Villalobos heard it standing in the third row of the kitchen servants, her hands folded, her face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a woman thinking about bread.

She had gotten very good at that face. Six years of practice. A taster who looked frightened was a taster with something to hide; a taster who looked bored was furniture. She had chosen furniture a long time ago.

"By order of His Radiant Majesty Aldric the Second," intoned the herald, a thin man whose voice carried the particular flatness of someone reading words he did not like, "let it be known throughout the realm and its dominions that the gift of immunity — that corruption of the body which permits poison to pass through it unharmed — is hereby declared a matter of crown concern."

*Matter of crown concern.* The phrase had a bureaucratic smoothness to it, the kind of language that arrived just before the knives did.

"All persons possessing this gift shall present themselves for examination within forty days. Those who register willingly shall be fed, housed, and honored in the service of the throne. Those discovered unregistered—" the herald paused, wet his lips, "—shall be subject to exposure, seizure, and conscription by whatever means the Office of Gifts deems necessary."

The courtyard was very quiet. Somewhere above, a gull screamed at the winter sky. Mirabel watched the minor lords and ladies in their furs exchange glances — some puzzled, some amused, and here and there, one or two faces going carefully still in a way she recognized because she had spent six years teaching her own face to do exactly that.

They were hunting us, she thought. All this time, and they were hunting *us*.

The stew three nights ago made new sense now. The alchemists' signature in the broth — the layered bitterness, the careful architecture of it — had not been an assassination attempt at all. It had been a net cast into the court's own waters, to see what rose. Lord Emmerich Vane had eaten his portion and lived, and somewhere a clerk had written down: *Vane, unaffected. Observe.* And if Vane had doubled over vomiting as Mirabel had so artfully pretended beside him, the clerk would have written something else entirely. She had saved him without knowing it. Or perhaps she had only saved herself, and he had simply been lucky.

Forty days. She began counting before the herald had finished speaking.

* * *

Her handler found her that evening in the scullery, where she was legitimately employed scrubbing copper pots until they shone like small moons. Serah Ondile had been Master of the Tasting Office for nine years, a narrow woman with iron-gray hair pulled back so tightly it seemed to stretch her features into permanent skepticism. She had hired Mirabel off a dock in Corvane Harbor on the strength of a lie about references, and Mirabel had always suspected — the way one suspects weather coming — that Serah had known the references were false and hired her anyway, for reasons of her own.

"You heard it," Serah said. It wasn't a question.

"I heard it."

"The Office of Gifts opens next week. They'll begin with the kitchens." Serah picked up a pot, examined her reflection in its curve, set it down again. "Blood work first. Then the trials. They have a new assay now — some alchemist's trick, drops that turn black on an immune tongue. Can't fake your way past chemistry, girl. I'm sorry to be blunt, but you've never once fooled me and I see no reason to start being polite now."

Mirabel kept scrubbing. The copper sang under her hand. "I don't know what you mean, mistress."

"No," Serah agreed. "You never have." She was quiet a moment. In the corner, the fire ticked and settled. "Six years. Do you know how many tasters I've gone through in six years? Eleven. Eleven girls and boys, and every one of them sick or dead inside two years, because the court poisons its own food supply out of paranoia and calls it security. And then there was you. Never ill. Not once. Not even the winter fever that took half my staff. You'd taste a dish with enough widow's-bane in it to kill a horse and ask if there was more."

"You pay me to have a strong stomach."

"I pay you to have a *lucky* stomach, and luck doesn't run six years straight." Serah set the pot down with a click. "I've written four reports about you in six years. All four said the same thing: *competent, unremarkable, no gift indicated.* I wrote them knowing they were lies. I've been waiting to find out what you were saving yourself for."

Mirabel finally stopped scrubbing. Her hands were red from the hot water. "And what am I saving myself for, mistress?"

"That," said Serah, "is not my business. But this is." From inside her coat she drew a folded paper and laid it on the bench between them. Mirabel didn't touch it. She knew what it was before she looked — the weight of good vellum, the smell of proper sealing wax. A travel pass. "Caravan road north, through Thornfell, merchant's warrant under the name Maren Ostrova. It's clean. I paid a man who owes me, and he paid a man who owes him, and none of them know your face."

"Why?" Mirabel asked. It was the only question left. "Why would you do this?"

Serah's skepticism cracked, just at the edges, and underneath it Mirabel saw something old and tired and strangely gentle. "Because thirty years ago a taster named Odile Voss covered for me when I drank something I shouldn't have, and I never got the chance to repay her. She died registered. Fed, housed, honored. They worked her until her body gave out and then they buried her in the Office plot with a nice stone." She straightened her coat. "You have until dawn. After dawn, I report to the Office of Gifts, and I will do my duty, and I will describe you accurately, because if I don't they'll send someone worse than me looking. One night, Mirabel. That's the debt. Don't waste it crying over pots."

She left. Mirabel stood alone in the scullery for a long moment, hands still dripping, the fire burning down, and understood that she had just been given her life by a woman who would spend the morning helping to hunt it.

* * *

She took almost nothing. That was the strange lesson of the night — how little a life amounted to when you had to carry it. Six years in the usurper's court, and everything she owned fit into a satchel: two dresses, a knife, a pouch of coin, a small tin of bitterroot she'd hoarded against a day she'd never let herself imagine. She stood in her narrow room above the laundry and looked at the rest of it — the quilt she'd bought secondhand, the chipped cup she liked, the dried flowers in a jar on the sill — and felt grief arrive quietly, without drama, the way frost arrives on glass.

It wasn't the objects. It was the shape of the life. Six years of small compromises, each one survivable, each one reasonable: take the tasting post, don't ask questions, laugh at the right jokes, look away from the right things. She had built herself a house out of compromises and called it safety, and now she could feel the whole structure dissolving around her like sugar in warm water, and beneath it was nothing — just a woman with a dangerous body and a forged name, walking out into the dark.

She paused at the door of the Tasting Office on her way through the service corridors. Through the window she could see the long table where she had knelt so many mornings, silver spoons laid out in rows, the great ledger open to her page. *Villalobos, M. Competent. Unremarkable.* She pressed two fingers to the cold glass, briefly, like touching a grave, and walked on.

The city gates opened at the fourth bell for the market carts, and she went through them in the gray before-dawn among turnip wagons and fish barrels, wearing the face of a merchant's clerk named Maren Ostrova, who had never tasted poison in her life and hoped she never would. Behind her the bells of the usurper's court rang the hour, and ahead the mountains stood up black against the last stars, the Thornfell passes still holding their snows, and somewhere in that wilderness — so the caravan brokers whispered, for a price — smuggler roads ran north to the free cities, to anywhere, to nowhere.

She had forty days. She had one night's head start. She had her palate, and a pass, and nothing else in the world.

At the crossroads below the walls, where the cart tracks split toward the lowland towns, a line of wagons waited in the freezing mist, lanterns hooded, mules stamping. A man with a leather patch over one eye stood at the head of them checking seals by lamplight, and when Mirabel approached with her forged warrant held out, he looked at the paper, then at her, with the single eye he had left.

"Ostrova," he read. "Going north?"

"As far as north goes."

He grunted, handed the warrant back, and jerked his thumb at the third wagon. "Fare's double for people traveling light. Light means running, and running costs me sleep." He turned away, then paused. "Name's Ferrow. Dax Ferrow. Rule one on my road: don't ask what's in the other wagons."

Mirabel climbed up onto the spice wagon's bench, the forged pass safe in her coat, the court bells fading behind her in the mist. Rule one suited her fine. She had spent six years not asking what was in the dishes; she could certainly manage not asking what was in the wagons.

The caravan lurched forward into the dark, toward the mountains, and behind them — faint, patient, already beginning — came the sound of the hunt waking up.
