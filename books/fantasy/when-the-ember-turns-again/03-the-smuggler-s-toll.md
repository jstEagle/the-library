## 3. The Smuggler's Toll

The pass road out of Thornfell's southern skirts was a scar of mud and old cart-iron, and Mirabel walked it with a forged pass in her bodice and six years of careful living burning off her like morning fog.

She had learned, in the usurper's court, to read men the way she read food. A cupbearer's tremor. A steward's over-sweet courtesy. The way a lord's eyes went flat when he lied about his wife. So she read the caravan master at the toll-camp below Greywater Stair — a broad man with a leather patch over one eye and the other eye the color of river ice, cold and quick and missing nothing — and she knew before he spoke that he would charge her double.

"Passage to the lowlands," he said, not looking up from his ledger. "Twelve silver."

"The posted rate is six."

"The posted rate is for people carrying nothing worth taking." He turned the page. "You're carrying something."

"Myself."

"That's what I said." He glanced up then, and the one eye held her the way a jeweler holds a stone to the light. "Dax Ferrow. My caravan, my rules, my prices. You want cheap, there's a pilgrim train leaving tomorrow that'll sell you a plank spot between two weeping widows. You want fast and quiet, you pay me."

Mirabel counted out twelve silver without haggling. Haggling told a man you had time; she did not. But she paid it with a small internal wince, because twelve silver was nearly everything the night's flight had left her, and because Dax Ferrow watched her count it like a man memorizing how much she had left.

"You're running," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I'm traveling."

"Mm." He made a mark in the ledger. "Caravan leaves at first light. Rule one: nobody asks anybody anything on my wagons. Rule two: if I ask *you* something, you answer true or you walk. Rule three—" he finally looked at her fully, and something in the set of his mouth suggested he was enjoying himself, "—secrets cost double. Whatever yours is, I'll find it eventually, and I'd rather be paid up front."

"And if I have no secrets?"

The eye crinkled. "Then you're the first person who ever bought passage from me who didn't, and I'll refund you the difference out of sheer astonishment."

* * *

The caravan was eleven wagons, forty souls, and a smell of pepper and cardamom so thick it seemed to precede them down the mountain like a herald. Mirabel was given a place on the salt wagon, wedged between a taciturn woman named Osla who mended nets even while walking, and a boy of perhaps fourteen whose job appeared to be feeding dried fish to a bad-tempered mule.

No one asked her name. No one asked where she'd come from. It was, she realized somewhere in the second day, the most restful company she had kept in six years. At court, silence was a weapon everyone aimed at everyone else. Here it was simply furniture — something you sat among, unremarkable.

She began, cautiously, to sleep.

It lasted until the fourth morning, when the road narrowed into a defile called the Kettle — walls of grey stone rising on either side, the sky reduced to a bright ribbon overhead — and Dax Ferrow rode back along the line of wagons with his face gone carefully blank.

"Hunters," he said quietly, to no one and everyone. "Two riders behind us, closing slow. Hounds."

Mirabel's spine went cold.

Everyone knew about the sniffer-hounds. They were the crown's ugliest cleverness: dogs bred in kennels beneath the alchemists' quarter, fed trace poisons from birth until their noses could catch a taster's gift in the air itself. The court physicians swore the animals smelled not poison but *resistance* — some faint signature of a body that had learned to drink death and stay standing. Six years, Mirabel had masked herself with luck and misdirection, and never once had she stood downwind of a hound.

"Wagons hold," Dax called. "Nobody runs. Running is a confession."

The hunters came up the defile at an unhurried walk, as though they had all the time the mountains allowed. There were four of them, not two — the rear pair had been hidden by a bend — and they wore the plain grey of the gift-hunt's new livery, unadorned, official, anonymous. Between the lead pair trotted two hounds on silver chains: lean, pale-eyed animals with the exaggerated skull of breeding and nostrils like wet black plums.

The lead hunter reined in beside Dax. He was young, with the soft hands of someone raised to paperwork rather than riding, and he smiled the smile of a man delivering good news.

"Routine inspection," he said. "New writ. All travelers subject to scenting."

"On my own toll-road?" Dax spread his hands. "This is smugglers' ground, friend. Old treaties."

"Treaties are being renewed." The young hunter dismounted. The hounds pressed against their chains, pale eyes rolling, and one of them turned its head — slowly, deliberately — toward the line of wagons.

Toward the middle of the line.

Toward the spice wagon.

Mirabel saw it and felt the world tilt. She had assumed the hunt was for people like her — tasters, immunes, the crown flushing out its hidden assets. But the hound wasn't casting about. It was *pointing*, rigid as a compass needle, and its partner swung to match it, both animals straining toward the same wagon with a certainty that had nothing to do with random inspection.

Something in that wagon mattered more than she did.

The second hunter, older, grey-stubbled, came down the line with a censer on a chain, swinging it lazily — burning something acrid, some alchemical reagent meant to make the hounds' noses sharper. He stopped at the salt wagon. He looked at Mirabel.

"Lady travels alone?"

"Trader," she said, in the flat accent she'd worn since the border. "Spices, southbound."

"Open your pack."

There was a moment — brief, weightless — in which Mirabel considered the knife in her boot, the drop behind the wagon, the odds. Then she thought of the hounds, and of the fact that whatever they were pointing at, it wasn't her, and she understood that her survival now depended on keeping every eye fixed elsewhere.

So instead she did the thing she was actually, genuinely gifted at.

"Bitterroot," she said suddenly, loudly, to Osla. "Your chest salve — the bitterroot one. Now, please. The whole pot."

Osla stared at her. "I don't—"

"*Now.*"

And Mirabel moved. She pulled the little clay brazier from under the salt wagon's bench — every wagon kept one for the cold nights — tipped in a fistful of charcoal, and struck fire to it with hands that did not shake, because she had spent six years training her hands never to shake. When Osla thrust the earthenware pot at her, she dug two fingers into the dark green paste and slathered it across the brazier's coals.

Bitterroot smoke was a taster's oldest trick, older than the crown, older than the kennels. It was what kitchen-tasters burned under their windows in the old kingdom to blind their own palates between meals, so that no enemy could season their food toward tolerance. To a nose, it was a wall. To a hound bred on precision, it was agony.

The smoke rose thin and bitter, sharp enough to sting the eyes at ten paces.

"What is that?" the young hunter demanded.

"Chest salve," Mirabel said. "Half this caravan has the mountain cough. You want to inspect us, friend, breathe deep." She picked up the pot and walked — calmly, insolently — down the line of wagons, and at each one she knocked, and at each one a door opened, and a hand took a pinch of the paste and added it to a waiting brazier. She had not planned it. She had not asked. But wagon after wagon, the caravan understood, and the bitter smoke climbed into the defile air until the Kettle filled with it like a bowl filling with fog.

The hounds went mad.

Not pointing-mad — *wretched*-mad, sneezing, pawing their snouts, backing against their handlers' legs with their pale eyes streaming. The older hunter cursed and dragged his censer close to his face, and even through it Mirabel saw him blinking hard. The young one retreated three steps up the slope, coughing.

"This is obstruction," he managed.

"This is medicine," Mirabel said. "But if you'd like to explain to your writ-writers why four trained hounds died of smoke inhalation on a smuggler's road, do write it down. I'll wait."

For a long moment nobody moved except the wind, pushing the bitter smoke uphill in slow coils.

Then Dax Ferrow laughed — one short bark, genuine, surprised out of him — and said, "You heard the lady. Medicine. Move along, friend. Road's too sickly for inspection today."

The hunters withdrew. It took them the better part of an hour to get the hounds back up the trail, and the whole caravan stood among their smoking braziers and watched them go, and when the last grey rider rounded the bend, forty people exhaled at once.

* * *

That evening, camped above the tree line under a sky crowded with stars, Mirabel sat alone at the fire's edge until Dax Ferrow dropped onto the rock beside her, uninvited, and handed her a tin cup of wine that tasted, she noted professionally, of nothing worse than cheap plums and a careless pour.

"That was quick thinking," he said. "Or practiced thinking. With you it's hard to tell which is worse."

"Rule one," Mirabel said. "Nobody asks anybody anything."

"Rule two," Dax said. "*I'm* asking." He drank, watching the fire. "Those hounds weren't hunting you. You noticed. Everyone else was too busy coughing."

Mirabel said nothing.

"They pointed at my spice wagon, lady. My wagon. Which means whatever the crown wants badly enough to send hounds into the Kettle, it's been riding with me for a while, and I've been too busy counting silver to ask what." He turned the one eye on her, and for once there was no humor in it at all. "So here's what happens next. Tomorrow, I open that wagon. And either you tell me tonight why a woman who carries bitterroot paste like a soldier carries a knife just saved my entire caravan for free — or I decide you're part of whatever this is, and rule two stops applying to you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, the mule complained about something at length.

Mirabel looked into her wine and understood that she had crossed some line in the smoke without noticing — that she had spent six years being useful only to herself, and one afternoon being useful to forty strangers, and that the second thing had felt, terrifyingly, like relief.

"I don't know what's in your wagon," she said at last. "But I know what those hounds were smelling. And I think you should open it tonight, not tomorrow."

Dax Ferrow studied her for a long moment. Then he stood, drained his cup, and said, "Bring your paste. Something tells me we'll want the smoke."

He led her through the sleeping camp to the spice wagon, and together they pulled the boards from the false floor — and what lay beneath was not spices at all.
