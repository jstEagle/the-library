## 4. What the Wagon Carries

The boy in the false floor was not what Dax Ferrow had promised his people, and he knew it.

He had told them cargo. He had told them a sick passenger, a feeble cousin of a merchant house, too frail to travel by common road. He had told them the spice wagon's weight was saffron and cardamom, that the smell alone would cover any coughing. And they had believed him — Brannic with his bad knee, the twins who drove the mules, old Sefa who cooked for all of them — because Dax Ferrow had never once lied to his caravan in eleven years on the Thornfell roads, and a man's whole trade rests on the weight of his word.

Now the word was ash, and the boy lay blinking up at the lantern light like something dug out of a grave.

He was nineteen years old, though you'd have taken him for younger. Three years in a box the length of a coffin and twice as wide will hollow anyone. His skin had the translucence of paper held to a flame; his hair, what there was of it, grew fine and colorless as spider silk. He wore a plain wool coat three sizes too large, and beneath it, where the collar gaped, Dax could see the faded ghost of an old scar — not from a knife or a fall, but deliberate, surgical, the kind of mark left by men who wanted to be sure no one mistook who this child had been.

Emeric. Last grandson of the Ember Throne. The name Dax had carried in his mouth like a hot coal for three years and never once spoken aloud.

"Is it night?" the boy asked. His voice was soft and rusted from disuse. "I heard the dogs."

"The dogs are gone," Dax said. "Thanks to her." He tipped his head toward Mirabel Villalobos, who stood at the edge of the lantern's reach with her arms crossed and her face doing nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

Dax made them sit — himself on a crate, the boy propped against the wagon wheel wrapped in two blankets, Mirabel standing because she seemed like a woman who preferred standing — and he told it plainly, because there was no other way left to tell it.

"You've all been carrying a king," he said. "Or near enough. That's what the hounds were smelling. Not contraband, not poison, not anything I could smoke our way out of. Him. They've been hunting his blood-scent since the day the usurper put the old royal family against a wall, and tonight they caught it, and if that taster hadn't masked us with bitterroot we'd all be in chains by dawn, or worse."

He looked at his hands. They were steady, which felt like a betrayal; they should have shaken.

"I lied to you," he said. "All of you. Three years I've hauled him up and down these mountains telling you he was a merchant's invalid son. I took your trust and spent it without asking, because I thought it was mine to spend. That's not how a caravan works. That's not how *anything* works. So say what you'll say, and if any of you want off at the next crossroads with your full share, I'll pay it and wish you well."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere down the slope a night bird called, once, and gave up.

It was Brannic, finally, who broke the silence, working his bad knee as he always did when thinking. "You fed him same as us?"

"Same pot. Same portions."

"Then I've been cooking for royalty three years and nobody told me to use the good plates." Old Sefa snorted, and the tension cracked just enough to breathe through. "He eats like a bird anyway. Some king."

"He's not a king," Dax said quietly. "That's rather the problem. He's the last thing standing between the usurper and a finished job, and right now he can't walk a mile without resting, and every gift-hunter between here and the sea can taste his name in the air."

Mirabel had not moved. Now she did — one step closer, into the light, and Dax watched her look at the boy the way she'd looked at the poisoned stew back at court: cataloguing, weighing, finding the poison in the situation and naming its maker.

"You knew," she said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse — it was a question she already knew the answer to. "When you charged me double at the toll. You knew what those hounds were going to find, or you feared it, and you let me buy passage onto a dragnet anyway."

"I didn't know you," Dax said. "A stranger walks out of the usurper's own winter court with a forged pass and asks for the deep roads? For all I knew you were the hunt wearing a woman's face."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen you stand in front of sniffer-hounds with nothing but smoke and nerve." He shook his head slowly. "I've run these roads eleven years, mistress. I know every kind of courage there is on them. The loud kind, the drunk kind, the desperate kind. What you did tonight wasn't any of those. You calculated the exact weight of what you could survive and then spent it to save people who owed you nothing. I don't know whether to thank you or be afraid of you."

"Both is traditional," Mirabel said, and for the first time since he'd met her, something moved behind her eyes that might have been humor, worn thin as the boy's coat.

* * *

They talked until the moon went down behind the eastern crags.

Dax laid out the truth of their position the way he'd lay out a map, because maps were honest even when he wasn't. The free cities were three days' travel below them, but word traveled faster than wagons, and a dragnet this size meant gold had changed hands ahead of them. Every gate would have a bounty notice and a hound-handler by now. The high passes south were snowed in till spring. East lay the usurper's heartland, which needed saying only to be dismissed.

"That leaves the old smuggler roads," he said, "and I'll be honest with you, taster — I don't know them all. There are ways through Thornfell older than any kingdom now on a map. My grandfather ran them. Half of them I couldn't find sober, and the other half I'm not sure exist anymore."

"They exist," Emeric said.

They all turned. The boy had pulled the blanket tighter around his shoulders, and in the lantern light his pale face had something new in it — not strength, exactly, but the memory of it, like a hearthstone that holds warmth after the fire dies.

"My nurse taught me the old names," he said. "Before. She said a road only exists for people who remember what it was called. The Shepherd's Stair. The Hollow Under Raven Rock. The King's Mile, though it hasn't been a king's anything for six years." His voice caught on the last words, and he steadied it with visible effort. "I learned them the way other children learn prayers. In case."

"In case of what?" Mirabel asked gently.

"In case someone ever needed to take me home."

The silence that followed had weight to it. Dax found he couldn't look at the boy directly; guilt is easier to carry sideways. Three years in a spice box, learning dead names by lantern light, trusting a one-eyed smuggler who kept promising *soon* — soon to the coast, soon to allies, soon to safety — while the world outside closed like a fist.

"I'm sorry," Dax said. It came out rougher than he intended. "For the box. For the lies. For the *soon*. A man should never promise what he can't deliver, and I've been delivering nothing but promises for three years."

Emeric looked at him with eyes the gray-green of old copper, and what Dax saw in them frightened him more than the hounds had: forgiveness, freely given, from a boy who had nothing left to give but that.

"You kept me alive," Emeric said simply. "The rest we can argue about later."

* * *

Near dawn, Mirabel found Dax checking the mules' harness by feel, an old habit that kept his hands busy while his mind worked.

"The handler," she said quietly, so the others wouldn't hear. "The one who runs the hunt. Vessic Corane. He was my superior at court for four years. He's suspected what I am almost since the day I arrived — I've seen him set traps for me that I barely walked around. Tonight he had us cold, hounds and crossbows and a burned waystation to funnel us into, and he let a bitterroot smoke-screen end it." She paused. "One-eyed men don't miss much, Ferrow. Neither do handlers. He let us go for a reason, and I need to know you understand that reason won't hold forever."

Dax tightened a buckle that didn't need tightening. "What reason?"

"A debt. An old one. He called it in tonight — one night's head start instead of my arrest." Her voice stayed flat, but Dax heard the cost underneath it, the way you hear water under ice. "Which means somewhere behind us is a man who knows exactly what I am, exactly what you're carrying, and exactly which direction grief points us. He spared me because he chose to, not because he failed. That's the most dangerous kind of mercy there is."

"Then we don't waste it." Dax straightened and looked east, where the sky was going the color of dirty pewter above the black teeth of the peaks. "We move at first light, off the trade road entirely. The boy says there are roads that remember their names. Fine. We go find one."

"And after the passes?" Mirabel asked. "If the cities are sealed and the south is snow — where does a dead kingdom's last heir actually *go*, Ferrow? Have you thought that far? Or do you just drive until the road ends?"

Dax opened his mouth to give the easy answer, the smuggler's answer — *there's always another road* — and found he couldn't. Because the truth was sitting in a spice wagon wrapped in blankets, learning dead names in case someone needed taking home, and the truth was that some doors close so completely that no amount of road-knowledge will ever find the hinges.

"I don't know," he admitted. It tasted like copper. "But I know a legend my grandfather used to tell, about a door in the high mountains that the old dynasty sealed when it fell. A door into the country that officially doesn't exist anymore. I always took it for a story to keep children from wandering the high snows." He glanced back at the wagon, at the pale boy sleeping upright against the wheel. "Lately I'm less sure what's a story and what isn't."

Mirabel followed his gaze, and Dax watched her expression change — watched the taster read something in the boy's sleeping face the way she read poison in a dish, quick and certain and unwelcome.

"Every door has a price," she said softly, almost to herself. "That's the first thing they teach you in the kitchens. Nothing opens for free."

Above them, on the ridge line, a single bird rose against the graying sky — and Dax, who had spent eleven years learning to read the mountains the way merchants read ledgers, saw that it was flying *away* from the pass behind them, low and fast, the way birds fly when something on the ground has disturbed their roosting.

The hunt hadn't stopped. It had only changed hands.

"We break camp now," Dax said, reaching for the bell to wake his people. "And taster — whatever price that door wants, pray it isn't paid in blood. Because I've got a caravan full of liars and a king in a spice box, and I'm fresh out of things I'm willing to lose."
